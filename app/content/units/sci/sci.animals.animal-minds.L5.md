# Do other animals feel? Bee dances, elephant rumbles and the question of consciousness
`sci.animals.animal-minds.L5`

**Summary.** A hermit crab in a good shell puts up with a small electric shock, but one in a poor shell climbs out. What does that tell us about what the crab feels? In this lesson you will read a bee's dance, test a prize against an ice cube on yourself, and watch one animal.

**Intro.** *Someone taps just below your kneecap and your leg kicks. Someone offers you the last piece of mysore pak and you stop to think about your sister. Both are things your body did. What is the difference between them?* The kick happens the same way every time, whatever else is going on. The sweet is different. You weigh how much you want it against how your sister will feel. Scientists use this difference to study other animals. They cannot ask a bee or a crab how they feel. But they can watch whether the animal weighs one thing against another. In this lesson you will run that test on yourself. You will also learn two ways animals send detailed messages: the dance of the honeybee and the low rumble of the elephant.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain how a honeybee's waggle dance gives the direction and distance of food, and how scientists checked that other bees use it.
1. I can describe how elephants keep in touch with rumbles too low for us to hear, through the air and through the ground.
2. I can tell an emotion, which can be measured from outside, from a feeling, which is what the emotion is like from inside.
3. I can run a fair trade-off test, and explain what it shows that watching a reflex does not show.
4. I can name four kinds of evidence scientists look for when they ask whether an animal feels pain or pleasure.
5. I can explain the main views in the debate about animal consciousness, and what evidence each view asks for.
6. I can write what an animal did separately from my guess about what they felt.
7. I can explain why the answer to 'who feels?' changes laws and the way people treat other animals.

## Terms
- **Signal**: A sound, movement, smell or touch that one animal makes and that changes what another animal does. *e.g.* A crow's loud call makes other crows look up.
- **Waggle dance**: A dance a honeybee does on the comb after she finds food. The direction of her run and how long she waggles tell other bees where to fly. *e.g.* A bee runs straight up the comb, waggling for two seconds.
- **Symbol**: Something that stands for something else by a rule. The symbol does not look like the thing it stands for. *e.g.* 'Up' on the comb stands for 'towards the sun'.
- **Infrasound**: Sound that is too low in pitch for human ears. People hear down to about 20 vibrations each second, and infrasound is lower than that. *e.g.* Part of an elephant's rumble is infrasound.
- **Rumble**: A long, very low call that elephants make. We hear only the higher part of it, as a soft growl. *e.g.* A mother elephant rumbles and her calf walks back to her.
- **Seismic signal**: A message that travels as a tiny shaking of the ground. *e.g.* An elephant's rumble also shakes the soil a little, and that shaking spreads outwards.
- **Emotion**: A state of the whole body and brain that gets an animal ready to act, such as fear or joy. It can be measured from outside: the heart, the muscles, the chemicals in the blood and what the animal does. *e.g.* A frightened deer has a fast heart and tight muscles, and runs.
- **Feeling**: What an emotion or a sensation is like from the inside, for the one who has it. *e.g.* Only you know what your fear feels like.
- **Sentience**: Being able to feel things as good or bad, such as pain, comfort, hunger or pleasure. *e.g.* A law in Britain names crabs and octopuses as sentient animals.
- **Consciousness**: Having any experience at all. If there is something it is like to be you, you are conscious. Scientists and thinkers do not agree on one full definition. *e.g.* There is something it is like to taste rasam.
- **Reflex**: A fast reaction of the body that happens the same way each time, before any weighing of what else matters. *e.g.* You blink when a puff of air hits your eye.
- **Nociception**: The body detecting damage, or something that could cause damage. Special nerve endings called nociceptors do this job. *e.g.* Nerve endings in your finger fire when you touch a thorn.
- **Trade-off**: Giving up some of one thing that matters to get more of another. *e.g.* You stand in the hot sun a little longer to finish the cricket over.
- **Judgement bias test**: A test of mood. The animal learns that one signal means something good and another means something bad. Then scientists give an in-between signal and see which way the animal takes it. *e.g.* Bees who had just been shaken treated an in-between smell as bad.
- **Signalling chemical**: A chemical that one cell releases to pass a message to other cells. Serotonin and dopamine are two of them. *e.g.* Dopamine is released in a brain when something turns out better than expected.
- **Preference test**: A test that lets an animal choose between two things, to find out which one they want and how much. *e.g.* Hens offered a wire floor or a floor of loose litter chose the litter.
- **Anthropomorphism**: Giving another animal human thoughts and feelings that the evidence does not show. *e.g.* Saying a gecko on the wall 'is bored of us'.

## Explanations
### An emotion and a feeling are two different things · *defined*
**Definition:** An emotion is a state of the body and brain that prepares an animal to act, and it can be measured from outside. A feeling is what that state is like from inside.

When a street dog hears a cracker burst, her heart speeds up and her muscles tighten. She tucks her tail and runs. All of that can be measured. Scientists call it an emotion, here the emotion of fear. What the fear is like for the dog is a different matter. That inside part is called a feeling, and no instrument measures it. This is true for people too. A doctor can count your pulse, but she cannot feel your fear. So scientists study emotions directly. They work out feelings from evidence, in other animals and in other people. Animals feel. The hard part is showing it in a way that convinces someone who doubts it.
- e.g. A dog's fast heart after a cracker bursts is part of the emotion. What that moment is like for her is the feeling.
- e.g. A friend says 'my stomach hurts'. You believe her, though you have never felt her stomach.
- e.g. A hen's body temperature changes when she is handled. That is a measurement, not yet a feeling.
### The waggle dance: a message that stands for a place · *defined*
**Definition:** The waggle dance is a honeybee's signal for where food is. The angle of the waggle run from straight up gives the direction from the sun. The length of the waggle gives the distance.

A honeybee who has found good flowers comes home and dances on the comb, usually in the dark. She runs in a straight line while shaking her body from side to side, then circles back and runs again. The direction of the run is a code. Straight up the comb means 'fly towards the sun'. A run 60 degrees to the right of straight up means 'fly 60 degrees to the right of the sun'. The longer she waggles, the farther the food. Why does this count as more than a reaction? Because the dance is about a place that is far away and out of sight. 'Up' does not look like the sun. It stands for the sun by a rule that all the bees share. Karl von Frisch worked the code out in the 1940s. The other bees follow the dancer closely and touch her with their antennae, their feelers, because they cannot see her in the dark.
- e.g. A run straight down the comb means the food lies directly away from the sun.
- e.g. A bee who waggles for one second sends her sisters a shorter way than a bee who waggles for three.
- e.g. The sun moves across the sky, so a bee who dances for a long time slowly changes her angle to match.
### How a bee measures distance · *defined*
**Definition:** A flying honeybee measures distance by optic flow, which is how much the scene moves across her eyes during the flight.

How does the dancer know how far she flew? In 2000, Mandyam Srinivasan, a scientist born in Bengaluru, and his team tested this. They made bees fly to sugar through a narrow tunnel only a few metres long. The walls had a busy pattern. When the bees came home, they danced as if they had flown hundreds of metres. In the narrow tunnel, the pattern rushed past the bees' eyes very fast. So a bee measures distance by how much scenery streams past her eyes as she flies. She does not measure the energy she used or the time she took. This also shows that the dance reports what the bee experienced on her flight. It is her measurement, with the errors her way of measuring brings.
- e.g. From a moving auto, nearby shops rush past and far hills hardly move.
- e.g. Bees who flew a few metres through a narrow patterned tunnel danced for a far place.
- e.g. A bee flying over a plain lake sees little streaming past and may dance for too short a distance.
### Do the other bees really use the dance? · *debated*
For years, some scientists doubted the dance. Adrian Wenner and Patrick Wells showed in the 1960s that bees can find food by smell alone. They said the dance might not be needed at all. That was a fair challenge. In 1975, James Gould made dancers point to a place they had never visited, and the followers flew to that place. In 2005, Joe Riley's team stuck tiny radar aerials on bees who had followed a dance. Then they carried those bees to a new starting spot. The bees flew the direction and distance of the dance from the new spot, and so arrived at the wrong place. Smell could not explain that. Today most scientists accept that the dance sends bees to the right area, and that smell and sight then find the flowers. Whether to call the dance a language is still argued over.
- e.g. Followers moved to a new starting spot still flew the danced direction and distance.
- e.g. Near the flowers, a bee uses the smell she picked up from the dancer's body.
- e.g. A dance has a symbol for direction, but bees do not make new 'sentences' with it.
- **Karl von Frisch, James Gould and Joe Riley:** The dance carries direction and distance, and other bees use that information. The radar tracks show bees flying the danced course even from the wrong starting place.
- **Adrian Wenner and Patrick Wells:** Smell is enough for bees to find food. They pushed others to test the dance properly. Most scientists now think they were right that smell matters, and wrong that the dance does not.
- **Many scientists who study language:** The dance uses symbols and is about things that are not present, and both are rare among signals. But it covers only a few subjects and has no grammar, so they do not call it a language.
### India's honeybees dance in different dialects · *defined*
**Definition:** A dance dialect is one kind of bee's own scale for how much waggling stands for how much distance.

India has several kinds of honeybee, and each dances a little differently. The dwarf honeybee builds one small comb in the open, around a twig. She dances on the flat top of the comb and points her run straight at the food. She needs no code for direction. The rock bee builds the huge combs that hang under flyovers, water tanks and tall branches. She dances on the upright comb and uses the 'up means sun' code. The Asian hive bee lives in dark hollows and uses the same code. In 2020, a team working in Bengaluru, which included Patrick Kohl, Neethu Thulasi, Ebi Antony George and Axel Brockmann, timed the dances of all three. For the same distance, the Asian hive bee waggled longer than the rock bee. The Asian hive bee feeds close to home and the rock bee flies far. So each kind of bee has a scale that suits the distances she flies.
- e.g. A dwarf honeybee's run on the flat comb top points like a finger at the flowers.
- e.g. One second of waggling means a longer flight for a rock bee than for an Asian hive bee.
- e.g. A map of a town and a map of India use different scales for the same reason.
### Elephant rumbles: calls too low for us to hear · *defined*
**Definition:** A rumble is a long, very low elephant call. Its lowest part is infrasound, which is sound below the lowest pitch people can hear.

In 1984, Katy Payne sat beside the Asian elephants at a zoo in Portland, in the United States. She felt a faint throbbing in the air, though she heard nothing. It reminded her of standing near the biggest pipes of a church organ. She came back with recorders that pick up very low sound. When the tapes were played fast, the low calls became high enough to hear. The elephants had been calling all along. A rumble's lowest part is about 14 to 24 vibrations each second, and human ears stop at about 20. Why would an elephant call so low? Low sounds lose less of their energy to the air, and they bend around trees and hills, so they travel much farther than high sounds. A herd spread over a forest can stay in touch. In tests in Africa, elephants answered recorded rumbles played from two kilometres away. At Mudumalai in Tamil Nadu, Smita Nair and her colleagues sorted the calls of wild Asian elephants into four kinds: trumpets, roars, chirps and rumbles.
- e.g. At a temple festival you feel the big drum in your chest from far away, but the small bells fade quickly.
- e.g. A mother elephant rumbles and her family, out of sight among the trees, turns towards her.
- e.g. When an elephant rumbles, the skin on her forehead flutters.
### Messages through the ground · *idea*
A rumble also shakes the ground a little, and that shaking spreads outwards through the soil. Caitlin O'Connell-Rodwell tested whether elephants notice it. In Namibia her team played recorded alarm rumbles into the ground only, with nothing in the air. Elephant families at a waterhole bunched closer together and turned to face the source. So elephants can pick up a message through the ground. How do they sense it? An elephant's foot has a thick cushion of fat and many nerve endings that sense pressure, and so does the tip of her trunk. The shaking may also travel up her leg bones to her ears. Scientists are still working out which path matters most, and how much wild elephants depend on ground messages in daily life.
- e.g. Elephants at a waterhole went still and bunched together when alarm rumbles were played through the soil.
- e.g. A listening elephant may lean forward and put more weight on her front feet.
- e.g. You can hear a soft thump better with your ear on the floor than standing up.
- **Caitlin O'Connell-Rodwell and her team:** Elephants respond to rumbles sent through the ground alone, so they can use the ground as a second channel when the air is noisy or the wind is wrong.
- **Cautious scientists:** The tests show elephants can detect ground signals. They do not yet show how often wild elephants rely on them, or from how far.
### Names, comfort and the dead: what elephants do for each other · *debated*
In 2024, Michael Pardo and his team studied hundreds of rumbles recorded in Kenya. A computer could often work out which elephant a rumble was meant for. When the team played a rumble back, the elephant it was first meant for answered faster than she answered other rumbles. So elephants may call each other by something like a name. The result is new and needs repeating. In 2014, Joshua Plotnik and Frans de Waal watched Asian elephants in Thailand. When one elephant was upset, others walked over, touched her face with their trunks and chirped. In 2022, Sanjeeta Sharma Pokharel, Nachiketha Sharma and Raman Sukumar gathered videos of Asian elephants around their dead. Some mothers carried a dead calf for days. Elephants feel, and these are the acts we would expect from an animal who cares about others. Scientists agree about what the elephants did. They disagree about what name to give it.
- e.g. A calf squeals, and an older cousin walks over and puts her trunk in the calf's mouth.
- e.g. A mother carries her dead calf on her tusks for several days.
- e.g. An elephant lifts her head and answers one recorded rumble much faster than another.
- **Frans de Waal, Joyce Poole and Raman Sukumar's group:** Comforting, name-like calls and staying with the dead are best explained by elephants having emotions such as distress and attachment, as we do.
- **Cautious scientists:** The acts are real and carefully recorded. But 'grief' is a word for a human feeling. We should describe exactly what the elephants do, and keep testing other explanations.
- **Lisa Feldman Barrett, a psychologist:** Words like 'grief' and 'anger' are sorted partly by human language and culture. Other animals surely have pleasant and unpleasant states, but those states may not divide up the way our words do.
### How to test for a feeling: the trade-off · *defined*
**Definition:** A trade-off test changes what an animal can gain, and checks whether that changes how much harm the animal accepts. A reflex does not change with the reward. A weighed choice does.

A reflex happens the same way whatever else is going on. A feeling can be weighed against other things that matter. That gives scientists something to test. In 2009, Robert Elwood and Mirjam Appel gave hermit crabs a small electric shock inside their shells. Hermit crabs live in empty sea shells and care a great deal which kind they get. Crabs in the kind of shell they like less left it more readily. Crabs in a good shell stayed through the same shock. In 2022, Matilda Gibbons and her team gave bumblebees a choice between a hot feeder and a cool one. When the hot feeder held much sweeter sugar water, more bees put up with the heat. Both animals weighed harm against reward, as you do when you hold on to something uncomfortable for a reason. Weighing does not prove a feeling. It does show that a simple reflex is not the whole explanation.
- e.g. A hermit crab in a poor shell leaves at a shock that a crab in a good shell sits through.
- e.g. A bumblebee walks onto a hot feeder only when the sugar there is much sweeter.
- e.g. You keep holding a hot dosa plate because the table is two steps away.
### Moods in small bodies: the pessimistic bee and the anxious crayfish · *debated*
A person in a low mood expects the worst from an unclear sign. In 2011, Melissa Bateson, Geraldine Wright and their team tested this in honeybees. The bees learned that one smell meant sugar and another meant a bitter drink. Then half the bees were shaken for a minute, as if an animal were attacking the hive. Shaken bees more often treated in-between smells as the bitter one. The levels of several signalling chemicals in their bodies had dropped too. In 2014, Pascal Fossat's team gave crayfish mild electric shocks. Afterwards the crayfish kept to the dark arms of a tank and avoided the lit ones. A medicine that calms anxious people made them explore the light again. So a bee and a crayfish can be put into a lasting state that works like a mood.
- e.g. Shaken bees pulled back from a smell that calm bees were willing to try.
- e.g. Stressed crayfish stayed in the dark, and went back to the light after a calming medicine.
- e.g. After a bad morning, you may expect an unclear message from a friend to be bad news.
- **Melissa Bateson and Lars Chittka:** These animals show the same pattern that we use as a sign of mood in rats, dogs and people. The fair conclusion is that they have states like emotions.
- **Cautious scientists:** The shaking changed how the bees responded. That shows an emotion-like state of the body. It does not show what the state is like for the bee, or whether it is like anything.
### The same signalling chemicals in a bee, a crayfish and you · *defined*
**Definition:** A signalling chemical is released by one cell to pass a message to another. Many of the signalling chemicals in human brains are also found in insects, crabs, worms and even plants.

Why did a human medicine calm a crayfish? Because the crayfish's nerves use some of the same signalling chemicals as yours. Serotonin and dopamine carry messages between nerve cells in people, crayfish, bees and worms. People, crayfish and bees share ancestors who lived more than 500 million years ago. Those ancestors already used these chemicals, and their descendants kept them. Some of the chemicals are far older than nerves. Serotonin is also made by plants and fungi. This does not prove that a crayfish's fear feels like yours. The same chemical can do different jobs in different bodies. But it does mean that emotions were not invented by humans. Our emotions run on parts that were in use long before there were any humans.
- e.g. Dopamine is involved when a bumblebee finds an unexpected drop of sugar, and when you get a surprise treat.
- e.g. A tomato plant makes serotonin too.
- e.g. Bees have a chemical called octopamine that does a job like adrenaline in your body.
### Who is conscious? The debate · *debated*
Animals feel. A crab, a bee and an elephant each have experiences of their own. Not everyone has thought so. In 1637, Rene Descartes wrote that other animals were like clockwork, moving with nothing felt inside. In 1871, Charles Darwin answered that the minds of humans and other animals differ in amount, not in kind. For much of the 1900s, many scientists refused to talk about animal feelings at all, because feelings cannot be measured. That has changed. In 2012, a group of brain scientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness. In 2024, hundreds of scientists and philosophers signed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness. It says the evidence for conscious experience is strong in mammals and birds. It says there is at least a realistic chance of it in all animals with backbones, and in insects, crabs and octopuses. The argument now is about how far it goes and how sure we can be.
- e.g. A law passed in Britain in 2022 treats crabs, lobsters and octopuses as animals who can feel.
- e.g. The philosopher Thomas Nagel asked in 1974 what it is like to be a bat, who finds her way by echoes.
- e.g. You have never felt another person's pain either. You work it out from what they do and say.
- **Charles Darwin, and the signers of the New York Declaration (2024):** Bodies, brains and behaviour change little by little from one kind of animal to the next. So it would be strange if feeling switched on suddenly in humans. Where the evidence is unsure, we should take the chance of feeling seriously.
- **Joseph LeDoux, a brain scientist:** The brain circuits that make a rat freeze work without awareness, even in people. A felt emotion may need extra brain parts that only some animals have. Behaviour alone cannot tell us.
- **Jain teachers, the scientist J. C. Bose, and philosophers such as Philip Goff:** Feeling does not begin with brains. Jain texts count the senses of every living being, down to those with one sense. Bose measured responses in plants and metals. Goff argues that experience is a basic feature of all matter.
### Feeling without a brain like ours · *idea*
Some people argue that pain needs the wrinkled outer layer of the human brain, called the cortex. Fish, crabs and bees have no such layer. But many animals do the same job with different parts. Birds have no layered cortex, and yet crows plan and solve problems. An octopus has most of her nerve cells in her arms. The insect scientists Andrew Barron and Colin Klein argued in 2016 that the centre of an insect's brain does what the human midbrain does. It puts together the animal's senses, body and needs into one picture to act on. They think that is enough for a simple kind of experience. Under all of this lies a harder puzzle. Nobody can yet explain how the activity of any nerve cells goes with a feeling, even in a human brain. The philosopher David Chalmers named this the 'hard problem' in 1995.
- e.g. A crow's brain has no layered cortex, but she can make a hook to fish out food.
- e.g. An octopus's arms do much of their own steering, with little help from her central brain.
- e.g. A honeybee's brain is about the size of a sesame seed, and she still learns, remembers and decides.
- **Andrew Barron and Colin Klein:** Experience needs a brain centre that joins the animal's senses and needs into one picture. Insects have one, so insects probably have simple experiences.
- **Brian Key, a brain scientist:** Pain as we feel it depends on the layered cortex. Fish have none, so we should not assume that fish feel pain.
- **David Chalmers, a philosopher:** Even a full map of a brain's activity does not explain why that activity is felt. Until that is solved, no brain test can settle who is conscious.
### Why it matters: asking animals what they want · *defined*
**Definition:** A preference test lets an animal choose between two things, and sometimes pay a cost for one of them. It shows what the animal wants and how much.

If other animals feel, then how we keep them matters to them. In the 1970s, Marian Stamp Dawkins found a way to ask. She let hens choose between a cage and a run with grass, and between a wire floor and loose litter to scratch in. Then she made the choice cost something, to see how much each thing mattered to the hens. Tests like hers are now used to design farms, zoos and laboratories. Vets give fish painkillers after an operation. In 2021, a team led by Jonathan Birch reviewed more than 300 studies for the British government. The law passed the next year treats crabs, lobsters and octopuses as animals who can feel. Many people also argue for a simple rule of caution. When we are unsure whether an animal feels, we should act as if they do.
- e.g. A zoo hides food in a log so that a bear has something to work out.
- e.g. Hens given the choice walk to the pen with loose litter.
- e.g. A fish gets a painkiller in her tank water after an operation.

## Mostly agreed
- Honeybees who follow a waggle dance use the direction and distance it gives. *How we know:* Joe Riley's team tracked followers by radar in 2005. Bees released from a new spot flew the danced course from there and missed the food, so they were not following smell.
- Elephants make calls that are partly too low for human ears, and other elephants answer them from far away. *How we know:* Katy Payne's team recorded the calls in 1984. Later, elephants in Africa turned and walked towards recorded rumbles played from two kilometres away.
- Emotions, the measurable states of body and brain, are shared widely among animals. *How we know:* Mammals, birds and fish release similar stress chemicals when frightened. Bees and crayfish change how they treat unclear signals after a bad experience.
- Crabs, bees and fish do more than react to harm. They guard a hurt place, learn to avoid what hurt them, and weigh harm against reward. *How we know:* Hermit crabs give up poor shells sooner than good ones at the same shock. Bumblebees accept heat for sweeter sugar. Hurt trout stop feeding for a while and rub the sore place.
- Nobody can measure a feeling directly, in another animal or in another person. *How we know:* Every instrument measures something outside the feeling: a pulse, a chemical, a brain signal or an act. The feeling is worked out from these.
- The evidence for conscious experience is strong for mammals and birds. *How we know:* Hundreds of scientists and philosophers signed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness in 2024, after decades of tests on brains and behaviour.

## How we found out
**Lynne Sneddon, with Victoria Braithwaite and Michael Gentle, 2003.** Until about twenty years ago, many people who fished, and many scientists, held that fish felt no pain. A fish has no face that shows hurt and makes no cry. Nobody had found nerve endings for injury in a fish. In 2003, Lynne Sneddon, working in Scotland with Victoria Braithwaite and Michael Gentle, looked for them. She tested the skin on the faces of rainbow trout and found 58 nerve endings, and 22 of them fired only at harm, such as strong pressure, heat or acid. Then she put a little bee venom or vinegar into the lips of some trout, and harmless salt water into others. The trout with venom or vinegar breathed fast. They rocked from side to side, rubbed their lips on the gravel, and took much longer to start feeding again. A painkiller reduced all of this. So the old idea was wrong on its main point: fish do have the nerves, and their response is much more than a flinch. A few scientists still argue that a fish's brain cannot produce the feeling of pain.  
*What people believed before:* Fish have no nerve endings for injury and only flinch, so nothing is felt when a fish is hurt. Sneddon found the nerve endings, and showed that hurt trout stopped feeding, rubbed the sore place and were eased by a painkiller.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Other animals are like clockwork. They move, but nothing is felt inside.** (Rene Descartes, a French thinker, in 1637.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many acts of the body, such as a blink, do run without any weighing. Machines can be built that react to the world.  
  Against: Animals learn, weigh harm against reward and comfort each other. They share our nerves and signalling chemicals. A clock does none of this.
- **Fish feel no pain, because they have no cortex.** (James Rose in 2002 and Brian Key in 2016.; *minority*)  
  For: In people, the cortex is active when pain is felt. Fish have no layered cortex.  
  Against: Lynne Sneddon found nerve endings for injury in trout in 2003. Hurt trout stopped feeding and rubbed their lips, and a painkiller reduced this. Birds also lack a layered cortex.
- **Insects have feelings.** (Lars Chittka, in his book The Mind of a Bee (2022), and others.; *debated*)  
  For: Bees show mood-like changes, accept heat for a better reward and tend a hurt antenna. Bumblebees roll balls with no reward.  
  Against: A bee has about a million nerve cells and a very different brain. Some scientists say these results show flexible behaviour, and that this is not yet evidence of experience.
- **Saying that an elephant grieves is anthropomorphism.** (Many careful scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: Grief is a human word. We could be reading our own feelings into an animal whose experience we have not measured.  
  Against: Frans de Waal called the opposite mistake anthropodenial: refusing to see a likeness that the evidence supports. Elephants and people are both mammals with similar brain parts for emotion.
- **The bee dance is not needed. Bees find food by smell.** (Adrian Wenner and Patrick Wells, from the 1960s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Bees can be led to food by smell alone, and near the flowers they do use smell.  
  Against: Radar tracks from 2005 show bees flying the danced direction and distance, even when it takes them to the wrong place.
- **The more an animal looks like us, the more that animal feels.** (A common idea, though few people say it aloud.; *debated*)  
  For: Animals close to us on the family tree, such as apes, share the most brain parts with us.  
  Against: Octopuses and bees are very unlike us, and still pass several tests for emotion-like states. Looking like us is not evidence either way.

## Roots
In the Ice and Prize Test you found out what a prize changes and what it leaves alone. You also met bees who dance directions and elephants who call below our hearing. Now we ask what this tells us about feeling, in you and in everyone else.

**Deep truth.** In the Ice and Prize Test, most children hold the ice longer when the prize is bigger. The blink at a puff of air stays almost the same. A hermit crab also gives up a poor shell at a shock, but stays in a good one. So weighing a hurt against a want is a sign of feeling that anyone can see. An animal needs no cry and no face like ours to show it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We look for feelings in faces and cries, because that is how humans show them. So a crab, a fish or a bee, who has no face we can read and makes no cry we can hear, gets treated as if nothing were felt. This mixes up how a feeling is shown with whether the feeling is there.  
*Evidence:* In the Ice and Prize Test, the prize changed how long you held the ice, but hardly changed your blink. Hermit crabs left poor shells more readily than good ones at the same shock. Bumblebees accepted a hot feeder only when the sugar there was much sweeter.

- *who am i:* You are an animal who weighs a hurt against a want many times a day. A crab does that too, and so does a bee.
- *what is life:* For anyone alive, some things are good for them and some are bad. Feelings are how that difference is known from the inside.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of messages that were not sent to us, such as dances in dark hives and rumbles below our hearing. Each kind of animal lives among their own.

Every animal alive today comes from the same early ancestors. Those ancestors already used chemicals such as serotonin to pass signals around their bodies. From that one beginning came many kinds of body: six legs and a brain the size of a sesame seed, a trunk and feet that listen, two hands and a voice. Feeling runs through all of them, and each body shows it in a different way. So when you watch a bee or an elephant weigh a choice, you are watching a relative do what you do.

- **Dhammapada, verse 129:** “Sabbe tasanti daṇḍassa, / sabbe bhāyanti maccuno; / Attānaṁ upamaṁ katvā, / na haneyya na ghātaye.” As paraphrased: all beings tremble at violence, and all fear death. Put yourself in the place of another, and do not harm or cause harm. (The verse asks you to work out another's feeling from your own. Scientists do the same when they compare a crab's trade-off with a person's. The verse simply does not wait for the experiment.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra, chapter 2 (sutras 22 to 24 in most editions), a Jain text by Umasvati:** “पृथिव्यप्तेजोवायुवनस्पतयः स्थावराः / वनस्पत्यन्तानामेकम्” Living beings are grouped by how many senses they have. Plants have one sense, touch. Worms have two, ants three, bees four, and humans and other large animals five. (This old list draws no line between beings who feel and beings who do not. It says they differ in how many ways they sense. Today's scientists also find that feeling is hard to switch off at any one kind of animal.)
- **Jeremy Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), chapter 17, in a footnote:** On how we should treat other animals, he wrote: 'The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?' (Bentham said that what matters is feeling, and that cleverness and speech are beside the point. The trade-off tests and mood tests in this lesson are ways of putting his question to a crab or a bee.)
- Science (settled): Crabs, bees and fish weigh harm against reward, which a simple reflex does not do.
- Science (debated): Whether insects and crabs have conscious experience is argued over. The New York Declaration of 2024 calls it a realistic possibility.
- Science (open): Nobody can explain how the activity of nerve cells goes with a feeling, even in a human brain. David Chalmers named this the hard problem in 1995.
- Other view, Joseph LeDoux, a brain scientist who has studied fear in rats for decades: The brain circuits that make an animal freeze or flee work without awareness, even in people. LeDoux thinks a felt emotion needs further brain parts that build a picture of oneself. So he says a rat who freezes may or may not feel afraid, and that behaviour cannot tell us which. This is a careful view from someone who knows the evidence well.
- Practice, Two pulls at once (3 min): Sit still and breathe slowly. Think of something you want to do right now, such as scratch, get up or have a snack. Stay still for one minute anyway. Notice where in your body the wanting sits. Notice that something in you is weighing the want against staying still. Now look at any animal you can see, such as a crow, an ant or a person. Ask yourself quietly what two things they might be weighing right now.
- Ask: When you weigh a hurt against a want, who or what is doing the weighing?
- Ask: If a bee weighs heat against sugar, what do you and the bee have in common at that moment?
- Ask: Could there be a feeling that never shows in anything an animal does? How would anyone know?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* You let go of the ice when it hurt enough. Was that a choice or a reaction? Where is the line between the two? (hint: Think about whether the prize changed the moment you let go, and what that tells you.)
- *deep:* A bee's dance stands for a place she cannot see from the hive. Is that a word? What makes something a word? (hint: Think about what 'up' means on the comb, and whether the bees could have agreed on a different rule.)
- *big:* You have never felt a friend's pain. Why are you sure she feels it? Does the same reason work for an elephant, a fish and a bee? (hint: List your reasons for believing your friend. Then check each one against each animal.)
- *deep:* A crab weighs a shock against a good shell. Is the weighing the feeling, or only a sign of it? (hint: Think about whether a machine could be built to weigh two numbers, and whether that would settle it.)
- *big:* If we are not sure whether a crab feels pain, how should people treat crabs while we are unsure? (hint: Think about what is lost if we are careful and wrong, and what is lost if we are careless and wrong.)
- *big:* Elephants send rumbles that human ears miss. What other messages might be passing around you right now? (hint: Think of smells, very high sounds, the shaking of the ground, and light that our eyes do not pick up.)
- *deep:* Serotonin is in you, in a crayfish and in a tomato plant. What does sharing a chemical tell us, and what does it leave open? (hint: Think about whether the same tool can be used for different jobs.)

## Story: The Tumbler That Shivered
*A made-up story. Selvi, her mother Rani, Kannagi and Mr Joseph are imagined. Valparai in the Anamalai hills of Tamil Nadu is real. So are the phone messages that warn tea estate workers there when elephants are near, which the Nature Conservation Foundation began. Elephant calves do fall into trenches and tanks, and forest staff free them by breaking one side into a slope. Elephants do call in rumbles too low for people to hear, and Katy Payne first noticed them as a throbbing in the air. The water shivering in a tumbler is imagined.*

The message reached Amma's phone at half past five. 'Elephants near field number seven. Please stay indoors.' In the workers' lines above Valparai, everyone got the same message, and everyone knew what to do. You brought the washing in. You called the children. You waited, and in ten minutes the herd would cross the road and go down to the stream.

That evening they did not cross.

Selvi ate her dinner at the window, because the window was the best seat in the house. Below her the road bent round the tea bushes. On the bend stood the elephants, dark and close together. Their trunks hung still. An hour went by, and they had not moved the length of a lorry.

People began to grumble from their doorways. The night shift at the factory started at eight. The ration van was stuck on the far side. Mr Joseph, the field supervisor, walked up and down with his torch. 'They are being stubborn,' he said. 'If they are not gone by seven, I will light crackers.'

'You will not,' said Amma. 'You know what the forest people say about crackers.'

Selvi put her steel tumbler on the window sill and reached for more rice. That was when she saw the water.

The top of the water shivered. Tiny rings ran from the edge to the middle and were gone. She looked for a lorry on the road. There was none. Nobody was walking in the room. She held her breath and listened. She heard crickets, a pressure cooker next door, and nothing else.

The water shivered again.

This time she felt something as well. It was very faint, low in her chest, like a flutter. She knew that feeling. At the temple festival last year she had stood beside the big drum. Before she heard each beat, she felt it under her ribs.

Selvi looked at the elephants. The biggest one, an old female, stood at the front. The skin of her forehead was trembling. When it trembled, the water in the tumbler shivered. Then the forehead went still and the water went still. All the elephants spread their ears wide and did not move at all. One of them rested the tip of her trunk on the ground.

'They are doing something,' Selvi said. Nobody heard her.

She watched it happen four more times. The forehead trembled and the water shivered. Then came the stillness, with the ears held wide. She thought it was like someone calling down a well and waiting.

People who are calling are calling to somebody, Selvi thought.

She counted the herd. There were six. She took Amma's phone from the shelf and scrolled back to yesterday's message. 'Herd of seven with one calf, near the check dam.' She counted again. There were six elephants, and none of them was small.

Then she noticed which way they were facing. It was not towards the houses or the road. Every head pointed downhill, past the last row of tea, towards the old water trench.

She ran out of the door. A motorbike had just stopped by the lines. It was Kannagi akka, the forest watcher, still in her khaki jacket. Mr Joseph was already talking at her about crackers.

'Akka,' said Selvi. 'They are not blocking us. They are calling someone. There were seven yesterday.'

'They have not made a sound in an hour,' said Mr Joseph.

'They have,' said Selvi. 'It is too low to hear. You can see it in a tumbler of water. And the big one's forehead shakes every time.'

Mr Joseph opened his mouth, but Kannagi akka held up her hand. She looked at the herd for a long moment, and then down the slope where they were looking.

'I have felt that in my chest,' she said quietly. 'Twice, in the forest. I did not know what it was.' She unhooked the radio from her belt. 'Nobody lights anything. I am going round by the lower path, well away from them. Selvi, go back inside.'

From the window Selvi watched three torches move along the bottom of the field. They stopped at the trench. Then one torch swung quickly from side to side.

The calf had been in the trench since the afternoon. The cement sides were as high as her back, and too smooth to climb. Every time the herd called, she had answered, and nobody on the hill had heard her either.

It took the forest staff forty minutes with crowbars and spades. They broke down one end of the trench and packed the earth into a slope. The calf scrambled up it on her knees and ran.

Everyone heard what came next. The herd roared and trumpeted, and made small chirping squeaks that Selvi had never heard an elephant make. The elephants closed round the calf until Selvi could not see her. Trunks touched her back, her ears and her mouth. Then the old female turned, and the whole herd went down to the stream with the calf in the middle.

The road was empty by eight. The night shift went to work.

Selvi came back to the window. The water in her tumbler was flat and still. For an hour the road outside had been full of calling, and the whole hill had heard only crickets. She drank the water and wondered what else was being said out there.

**For the parent:** The elephants were calling the whole time, in a voice too low for human ears. Selvi worked it out from what she could see and feel: the trembling forehead, the stillness afterwards, the direction they faced. A message can be there even when we hear nothing.

- Ask: Why might a very low call be useful to animals who live spread out in a forest?
- Ask: What can you learn from which way an animal is facing and how still they are?
- Ask: Name one message that might be passing near you now that your ears and eyes miss.
- Ask: How could you test whether a sound too low to hear is really there?
- Ask: When an animal's act looks stubborn or strange, what should you check before deciding why they did it?

## Games
### g1 · Stay or Leave? The hermit crab game (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the cards. Shuffle the shell cards and the pinch cards into two piles, with the worth and strength sides face down. Print them on two colours of paper if you can, so the piles do not get mixed.; Each player takes 12 dry beans or chana. These are your strength. Each player draws one shell card and turns it over. That is the shell you live in. A shell worth 5 is roomy and strong. A shell worth 1 is cramped and cracked.; Each round, turn over the top pinch card. The same pinch hits every player.; Now each player chooses. To stay, pay as many beans as the strength of the pinch. To leave, pay 2 beans, put your shell at the bottom of the shell pile and draw a new shell. Leaving costs beans because a crab with no shell is in danger.; If you do not have enough beans to stay, you must leave. If you have fewer than 2 beans, you lose the beans you have and keep your shell.; Play six rounds. Then score: multiply your shell's worth by 4, and add the beans you have left.; The highest score wins. If you play alone, try to beat 16 points.
- **Printable cards: 15 shell cards, each with the shell's worth on the back (three shells each worth 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5):** Shell A (back: Worth 1); Shell B (back: Worth 2); Shell C (back: Worth 3); Shell D (back: Worth 4); Shell E (back: Worth 5); Shell F (back: Worth 1); Shell G (back: Worth 2); Shell H (back: Worth 3); Shell I (back: Worth 4); Shell J (back: Worth 5); Shell K (back: Worth 1); Shell L (back: Worth 2); Shell M (back: Worth 3); Shell N (back: Worth 4); Shell O (back: Worth 5)
- **Printable cards: 16 pinch cards, each with the pinch's strength on the back (from 0 to 4):** Pinch 1 (back: Strength 0); Pinch 2 (back: Strength 2); Pinch 3 (back: Strength 1); Pinch 4 (back: Strength 3); Pinch 5 (back: Strength 1); Pinch 6 (back: Strength 4); Pinch 7 (back: Strength 2); Pinch 8 (back: Strength 0); Pinch 9 (back: Strength 1); Pinch 10 (back: Strength 3); Pinch 11 (back: Strength 2); Pinch 12 (back: Strength 4); Pinch 13 (back: Strength 1); Pinch 14 (back: Strength 2); Pinch 15 (back: Strength 0); Pinch 16 (back: Strength 3)
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet for one player: six rounds:** Round; My shell's worth; Pinch strength; Stay or leave?; Beans left
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 15 shell cards and 16 pinch cards. Shuffle each pile, with the worth and strength sides down. Give every player 12 beans, a score sheet and one shell card.  
  *Parent says:* Cut out the cards and shuffle the two piles. Take 12 beans and one shell each. Turn your shell over and read what it is worth.  
  *Child mode:* Shuffle both piles. Take 12 beans and one shell. Turn your shell over.
  - Note: Dry beans and chana can choke a child under five, so keep them away from small brothers and sisters. If you have no printer, write the cards on slips of paper. The backs are listed in the printables.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play rounds 1 to 3. Each round, turn over one pinch card. Each player says 'stay' or 'leave' and pays. After each round, each player says in one sentence why they chose that.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the pinch. Will you stay and pay, or leave, pay 2 and take a new shell? Tell me why.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the pinch and your shell. Stay or leave? Say why.
  - Ask: What two things did you look at before you chose? (good: How strong the pinch was and how good my shell was.; My shell's worth and my beans.; Only the pinch. I did not think about the shell.)
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play rounds 4 to 6. Then each player multiplies their shell's worth by 4 and adds their beans. Write the scores down.  
  *Parent says:* Three more rounds. Then multiply your shell's worth by 4 and add your beans.  
  *Child mode:* Play three more rounds. Then work out your score.
  - Note: A player who always stays usually ends near 14 points. A player who always leaves ends near 12. A player who weighs the shell against the pinch usually scores about 19 or 20.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask each player when they stayed and when they left. Then show the check question, which tells what real hermit crabs did in a test.  
  *Parent says:* When did you stay through a strong pinch? When did you leave at a weak one?  
  *Child mode:* When did you stay? When did you leave?
  - Check: A crab in a good shell stays through a shock. A crab in a poor shell leaves at the same shock. What does this show? → **The crab weighs the shock against the shell**
  - Think: Would a reaction that works like a knee kick ever depend on how good the shell is?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Ice and Prize Test: what does a prize change? (35 min)
- **question:** Does a bigger prize change how long you choose to hold an ice cube? Does a prize change whether you blink at a puff of air?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. Will you hold the ice longer for a big prize than for no prize? Will a prize help you not to blink when air is puffed at your eye? Say why you think so for each one.
- **fair test:** Every ice cube comes from the same tray and goes straight from the freezer into the hand. You hold each cube the same way, in a closed fist over a bowl. You change hands each round and rest for three minutes, so a cold hand does not spoil the next round. The prize is said aloud before each round. The first and last rounds both have no prize. If those two times are close, the order of the rounds did not matter much. No round goes past 90 seconds.
- **safety:** Stop any round at 90 seconds, even if the child wants to go on. The child may let go at any moment, and letting go early is a useful result. Do not do the ice rounds with a child whose skin swells or itches in the cold, or whose fingers turn white in the cold. Puff the air gently from the side of the eye, from a hand's width away, and never push the straw towards the eye.
- **record:** Write the seconds held in each round. Under the table, write how many times you blinked in five puffs with no prize, and in five puffs with a prize. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Round | Prize for every 10 seconds | Which hand | Seconds held | Tokens won
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the question aloud. Ask your child for both guesses and the reason for each. Write the guesses above the table. Do not say whether you agree.  
  *Parent says:* You will hold ice for no prize, a small prize and a big prize. Will the prize change how long you hold on? Why? Then I will puff air at your eye. Will a prize help you not to blink? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make two guesses, one for the ice and one for the blink. Say why for each.
  - Ask: Which do you think a prize can change more, holding the ice or blinking? Why? (good: Holding, because I can decide to hold on.; Blinking just happens by itself.; Both, if the prize is big enough. We can test it.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is a good start. Many children are sure a big prize will stop the blink.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Agree what the tokens buy, and keep to it. For example, every 3 tokens buy one minute of a game your child chooses. Explain the rules. They may let go at any time, and no round goes past 90 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Each token is worth something real. Let us agree what. You can let go whenever you want. This is not a test of how brave you are. It is a test of what a prize changes.  
  *Child mode:* Agree what the tokens buy. You can let go any time. It is not a bravery test.
  - Note: The prize must matter to your child, or the test shows nothing. Choose something you will really give. The most a child can win in the whole test is 46 tokens.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Round 1 has no prize. Put one ice cube in your child's left hand. They close the fist over the bowl. Start the timer. Stop it when they let go, or at 90 seconds. Write the seconds. Then they dry and warm the hand for three minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Round 1. There is no prize this time. Hold the ice for as long as you choose. Let go when you want to.  
  *Child mode:* Round 1 has no prize. Hold the ice as long as you choose. Then warm your hand.
  - Note: Say nothing while your child holds the ice. Do not count aloud or cheer, because that is a kind of prize too. During the rest, they can rub the hand on the towel or hold it under an arm.
- **s4 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Round 2 uses the right hand. Say the prize first: 1 token for every full 10 seconds. Time it, write it and pay the tokens. Rest three minutes. Round 3 uses the left hand. The prize is 3 tokens for every full 10 seconds. Time it, write it, pay and rest.  
  *Parent says:* Round 2. You win 1 token for every 10 seconds you hold on. Go. Now rest. Round 3. This time you win 3 tokens for every 10 seconds.  
  *Child mode:* Round 2 pays 1 token for every 10 seconds. Round 3 pays 3 tokens. Let go when you choose.
  - Note: If your child reaches 90 seconds, stop the round and write '90, stopped'. Pay only for full 10 seconds held.
  - Check: Why do we rest and change hands between rounds? → **So a cold, numb hand does not change the next result**
- **s5 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Round 4 uses the right hand and has no prize, the same as round 1. Time it and write it. Then compare rounds 1 and 4.  
  *Parent says:* Last ice round. There is no prize again. Hold on for as long as you choose.  
  *Child mode:* Round 4 has no prize again. Hold the ice as long as you choose.
  - Note: If rounds 1 and 4 are close, then practice and cold hands did not change much. Any big difference in rounds 2 and 3 came from the prize. If round 4 is much longer than round 1, your child got used to the ice, and the test is less clear.
- **s6 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Now the puff test. Your child keeps both eyes open. Puff gently through the straw at the outer corner of one eye, five times. Count the blinks. Then offer 2 tokens for every puff with no blink. Puff five more times and count again.  
  *Parent says:* Keep your eyes open. I will puff air five times. Now the prize: 2 tokens for every puff where you do not blink. Try your hardest.  
  *Child mode:* Keep your eyes open while air is puffed. Then try not to blink for a prize.
  - Note: Hold the straw a hand's width from the side of the eye and blow one short, gentle puff. Most children blink at nearly every puff, with or without the prize. A few manage to hold back once or twice. Write down exactly what happened.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child what the prize changed and what it did not. They write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why. Give the explanation in the parent note only after they have finished.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your numbers. What did the prize change? What did it leave almost the same? Write what you think, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your numbers. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.
  - Ask: What did the prize change, and what did it hardly change? (good: I held the ice longer for the big prize.; I blinked almost every time, even for the prize.; The two no-prize rounds were close, so the prize made the difference.)
  - Ask: Scientists gave hermit crabs a small shock in a good shell or a poor shell. Which is that more like, the ice or the puff? Why? (good: The ice, because the crab's choice changed with how good the shell was.; The ice, because both weigh a hurt against something they want.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain. A blink at a puff of air is a reflex, a fast reaction that happens the same way each time. Letting go of the ice is different, because how long you held on depended on what you could gain. Scientists use the same idea with other animals. If your child held to 90 seconds in every round, the limit hid the result, and that is worth saying. Do not make the limit longer. If your child held back some blinks, say so honestly. A reflex can sometimes be held back a little, but far less than a choice can be changed.
  - Think: A bumblebee walks onto a hot feeder only when the sugar there is much sweeter. What might be going on in the bee?
### e2 · The Floor Message Test: can a message travel through the ground? (25 min)
- **question:** Can a message of soft thumps reach a listener through the floor when it is too soft to hear through the air? Which works best: ears in the air, an ear on the floor, or bare feet?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. Which way of listening will get the most messages right, and which the fewest? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same person sends every message, with the pad of one finger, at the same soft strength. The listener is always three metres away with eyes shut. The room is quiet, with the fan and the television off. A die chooses each message, so the sender cannot fall into a pattern. Each way of listening gets six messages. Only the way of listening changes.
- **safety:** Lie down away from doors that someone might open. If people live below you, keep the thumps as soft as the test asks.
- **record:** For each message, write the thumps sent, the thumps the listener said, and right or wrong. Count the right answers out of six for each way of listening. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Way of listening | Thumps sent | Thumps the listener said | Right or wrong
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the question. Ask your child which way of listening will work best and worst, and why. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will send soft thumps along the floor. You will listen standing, with an ear on the floor, and with bare feet. Which will work best? Which worst? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Which way of listening will work best? Which worst? Say why.
  - Ask: Why might a sound travel differently through a floor than through air? (good: The floor is hard, so the shaking passes along it.; Air is loose, so a soft sound dies away.; I am not sure. We can test it.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the right thump. The listener stands three metres away with eyes shut. The sender thumps the floor with one finger pad, softer each time. Stop when the standing listener is no longer sure whether there was a thump. Use that strength for the whole test.  
  *Parent says:* Shut your eyes. Tell me each time you hear a thump. I will make them softer and softer.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Say when you hear a thump. They will get softer.
  - Note: This step matters most. If the thump is easy to hear standing up, every way of listening will score six and the test shows nothing. Use the soft pad of the finger, not the nail or the knuckle. Switch off the fan.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The listener stands with eyes shut. Roll the die. For 1 or 2, send one thump. For 3 or 4, send two. For 5 or 6, send three. Leave one second between thumps. The listener says the number. Write it down. Do this six times.  
  *Parent says:* Stand still with your eyes shut. I will send one, two or three thumps. Tell me how many. If you are not sure, guess.  
  *Child mode:* Stand with your eyes shut. How many thumps were there? Guess if you are not sure.
  - Note: Do not say whether each answer is right until all six are done. A listener who is only guessing gets about two right out of six.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The listener lies on the towel with one ear pressed to the floor. They cover the other ear with a hand. Send six more messages chosen by the die. Write each answer.  
  *Parent says:* Now lie down and press one ear to the floor. Cover your other ear. How many thumps this time?  
  *Child mode:* Lie down. Press one ear to the floor. Cover the other ear. How many thumps?
  - Check: Why does a die choose the number of thumps? → **So the listener cannot guess from a pattern**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The listener sits on a chair with bare feet flat on the floor and both hands pressed over the ears. Send six more messages chosen by the die. Write each answer.  
  *Parent says:* Now sit with your bare feet flat on the floor. Press your hands over your ears. Can your feet tell how many thumps?  
  *Child mode:* Sit with bare feet flat. Cover both ears. Can your feet feel the thumps?
  - Note: Most people feel little or nothing through their feet at this strength. That is a real result. Write down what your child says.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the right answers for each way. Ask your child what the numbers show, and why they think it happened. They write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why. Then read them the parent note.  
  *Parent says:* Count the right answers in each row. Which way of listening worked best? Was any way no better than guessing? Why do you think that happened?  
  *Child mode:* Count your right answers for each way. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.
  - Ask: A listener who only guesses gets about 2 right out of 6. Which of your rows could be just guessing? (good: Standing, because I got 2.; My feet row, because 3 is close to 2.; Not the ear on the floor, because I got all 6.)
  - Ask: Your feet felt little. An elephant's feet pick up rumbles from far away. What might be different about her feet? (good: They are much bigger.; She may have more nerve endings for shaking.; She is very heavy, so her feet press hard on the ground.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain. A soft thump shakes the floor, and a hard floor passes that shaking along well. Air passes on far less of it. With an ear on the floor, the shaking goes through the skull bones to the inner ear. An elephant's foot has a thick cushion of fat and many nerve endings for pressure. Scientists think the shaking may also travel up her leg bones to her ears. Our feet have far fewer of those nerve endings.
  - Think: When might the ground be a better way to send a message than the air?

### a1 · Read the Dance: where did the bee send her sisters? (12 min)

- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw card B with a wavy line straight down, marked '3 seconds'. Say that this food was directly away from the sun, about 3 kilometres off. Then draw card C with a line pointing to the right, marked '1 second'. Ask where bee C's food is.  
  *Parent says:* Bee B ran straight down for 3 seconds. Her food was away from the sun, about 3 kilometres off. Bee C ran to the right for 1 second. Where is her food? Tell me your rule.  
  *Child mode:* Bee B ran down for 3 seconds. Her food was away from the sun. Where is bee C's food?
  - Ask: What is the rule for direction, and the rule for distance? (good: Up on the comb means towards the sun, and the same turn from up means the same turn from the sun.; Each second of waggling is about one kilometre.)
  - Note: Bee C's food is to the right of the sun's direction, a quarter turn, about 1 kilometre away. Let your child say a rule before you confirm it. One second for one kilometre is a rough figure for the honeybees Karl von Frisch studied.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the map: a hive in the middle and the sun at the top edge. On this map 3 centimetres stand for 1 kilometre. Your child marks the flowers of bees A, B and C with a ruler. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the hive, and the sun is this way. On our map, 3 centimetres is 1 kilometre. Mark where each bee's flowers are.  
  *Child mode:* Mark each bee's flowers on the map. Use 3 centimetres for each kilometre.
  - Note: Bee A's flowers go 6 centimetres towards the sun. Bee B's go 9 centimetres directly away from it. Bee C's go 3 centimetres to the right. Honeybees do this in the dark, by touch.
  - Check: A dancer runs to the left of straight up, a quarter turn, for 2 seconds. Where is her food? → **A quarter turn left of the sun, about 2 kilometres away**
  - Think: 'Up' does not look like the sun. How might bees have come to share the same rule?
### a2 · Saw or Guessed? Ten minutes with one animal (17 min)
- **safety:** Watch street dogs and monkeys only from a window or balcony. Do not touch or feed the animal during the ten minutes.
- **record:** Keep the two-column page. Circle one guess, and write under it another explanation and what you would watch for next.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rule a page into two columns. Head them 'What I saw' and 'What I guess'. Choose one animal you can see now. Start a ten-minute timer.  
  *Parent says:* Pick one animal to watch. In the first column write only what a camera would record. In the second, write what you guess they felt or wanted.  
  *Child mode:* Pick one animal. Write what you see on the left. Write your guesses on the right.
  - Note: If no other animal is about, your child can watch a person in the family who agrees to be watched. Humans are animals too, and the same two columns work.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Watch for ten minutes. Each time the animal does something new, your child writes it on the left. Then they write a guess on the right, if they have one. Stay quiet and still.  
  *Parent says:* Watch quietly. Each time they do something new, write it down. If you have a guess about why, write that in the other column.  
  *Child mode:* Watch quietly for ten minutes. Write each new thing they do. Add your guess beside it.
  - Note: Look for words such as 'angry', 'happy', 'bored' or 'wants' in the left column. Those are guesses. Ask what your child saw that made them think so, and move the word across.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child circles one guess. Under it they write one other explanation for the same act. Then they write what they would watch for next to decide between the two.  
  *Parent says:* Circle one of your guesses. What else could explain what you saw? What would you look for next time to find out which is right?  
  *Child mode:* Circle one guess. Write one other explanation. What would you watch for next?
  - Check: Which of these is something seen, and not a guess? → **The crow dropped the bread and flew off when the cat came**
  - Think: Your guesses about a friend's feelings are guesses too. What makes you trust them?
### a3 · The Middle Note: how a mood test for bees works (15 min)
- **safety:** Check that your child can safely eat the raisins and the methi seeds. If you prefer, the bitter note can cost one raisin from their pile, with nothing bitter to eat.
- **record:** Write what you did each time the middle note sounded: reached or held back. Write one sentence about what you think decided it.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill one tumbler to the top, half fill the second and leave the third empty. Strike each on the rim with the spoon. Your child listens and says which note is highest and which is lowest. Put the half-full tumbler out of sight.  
  *Parent says:* Listen to the three tumblers. Which note is highest? Which is lowest? Is the half-full one in between?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the three tumblers. Which note is highest? Which is lowest?
  - Note: A struck tumbler gives a lower note when it holds more water. Check that the half-full note sounds in between. If the notes are hard to tell apart, use bigger tumblers or glasses.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child turns away. Strike the full tumbler. If they hold out a hand, they get a raisin. Strike the empty tumbler. If they hold out a hand, they get a methi seed to chew. Mix the two notes eight times.  
  *Parent says:* Turn round. When you hear a note, you may hold out your hand or not. One note brings a raisin. The other brings a bitter seed. Work out which is which.  
  *Child mode:* Turn round. One note brings a raisin. The other brings a bitter seed. Hold out your hand, or not.
  - Note: Most children stop reaching for the bitter note within four or five tries. Carry on until they get two of each right in a row.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Carry on mixing the two notes. Among them, strike the hidden half-full tumbler three times. Give nothing for that note. Write down whether your child reached or held back each time.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. Hold out your hand or not, as you choose.  
  *Child mode:* Keep going. Hold out your hand, or not. You choose.
  - Note: Do not warn your child that a new note is coming. Their first reaction to it is the result.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the third tumbler. Ask your child what they did at the middle note and why. Then tell them about the honeybee test.  
  *Parent says:* There was a third note in between. What did you do when you heard it? Why? Scientists did this with honeybees and smells. Bees who had just been shaken held back more at the in-between smell.  
  *Child mode:* There was a middle note. What did you do at that note? Why?
  - Note: Your child's three tries say nothing about their mood. Scientists compare many animals in each group. The activity only shows how the test works.
  - Check: Shaken bees treated an in-between smell as bitter more often than calm bees. What does this suggest? → **A bad experience left the bees expecting worse**
  - Think: What kind of day might make you take an unclear message as bad news?
### a4 · The Evidence Checklist: reflex, or more than a reflex? (15 min)
- **record:** Keep the table of six findings and four signs, with your ticks. Under it, write which finding you think is the strongest evidence and why.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rule a table with four columns. Head them 'guards the hurt place', 'learns to avoid', 'weighs it against a reward' and 'eased by a painkiller'. Ask your child why each sign might matter.  
  *Parent says:* Here are four signs scientists look for. Why might each one tell us more than a simple flinch does?  
  *Child mode:* Here are four signs. Why might each one tell us more than a flinch?
  - Ask: Why would a painkiller making a difference be a clue? (good: If the medicine that eases our pain also changes what the animal does, something similar may be going on.; A flinch would still happen with a painkiller.)
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the six findings in the parent note aloud, one at a time. For each, your child writes a short name in a row and ticks every sign it shows.  
  *Parent says:* Listen to each finding. Tick each sign that it shows. Some will get one tick, and some will get more.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to each finding. Tick every sign it shows.
  - Note: Read these out. 1. A trout with a sore lip rubs it on the gravel, and feeds again sooner after a painkiller. 2. A hermit crab leaves a poor shell at a shock, but stays in a good one. 3. An octopus with a sore arm strokes it with her beak, avoids the room where it happened, and chooses a room where she got a painkiller. 4. A bumblebee walks onto a hot feeder only when the sugar there is much sweeter. 5. Your leg kicks when the knee is tapped. 6. A shore crab stops using a dark shelter where they got shocks.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the ticks in each row. Ask your child which finding is the strongest evidence, and which tells us least. Ask what test they would add for the animal with fewest ticks.  
  *Parent says:* Which finding has the most ticks? For an animal with few ticks, what would you test next?  
  *Child mode:* Which finding is the strongest? What would you test next?
  - Note: Likely ticks: the trout guards and is eased by a painkiller. The hermit crab weighs. The octopus guards, learns and is eased. The bumblebee weighs. The knee kick has none. The shore crab learns. Few ticks often mean that nobody has run that test yet. They do not mean the animal failed it.
  - Check: Which finding shows only a reflex, with none of the four signs? → **The leg kicks when the knee is tapped**
  - Think: No list of signs can be complete. What sign would you add?

## Challenges
### c1 · Ask Without Words: design a test of what an animal wants (30 min)
- **problem:** Pigeons or crows visit your window ledge. You want to find out which of two plain foods they prefer, and how strongly. You cannot ask them. Design a fair test in which the birds answer by what they do.
- **your turn:** Design a test of what someone wants for a different animal, such as ants, a pet, or a baby cousin who cannot talk yet. Say what the two choices are, how you make one of them cost more, and how many tries you need.
- **constraints:** Use only plain foods that are safe for birds, such as uncooked rice, bajra or ragi grains, or unsalted puffed rice. No fried, salted or sweet food.; The test must show how much the birds want a food, as well as which food they choose.; The side each food is on must be chosen by a coin, so that the side cannot explain the result.; Plan at least 10 tries, and feed only a spoonful each time.
- **general:** Put two bowls side by side, one food in each, and count which bowl the birds visit first.; Swap the sides with a coin toss before every try.; Count how many of 10 tries each food wins.
- **creative:** Make the favourite food cost more. Move that bowl closer to where you sit, and see how near the birds will come for it.; Cover the favourite food with a paper lid that the bird has to push off, and see whether they still choose it.; Offer a big pile of the second-choice food against a few grains of the favourite, and see where the choice flips.; Run the test in the morning and in the afternoon, to see whether hunger changes the answer.
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child how a bird could show which food they prefer. Then ask what else, besides liking the food, could make a bird go to one bowl. Write the list.  
  *Parent says:* How could a bird tell you which food they like, without words? What else might make them pick one bowl, apart from the food in it?  
  *Child mode:* How could a bird show which food they like, without using words?
  - Ask: What could make a bird choose a bowl, apart from the food? (good: It is nearer to where they landed.; It is farther from me.; They went there last time.; The pile looks bigger.)
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child makes a plan that deals with each thing on the list. They can draw it, or set out the bowls and coin and explain it aloud. Then they add a cost. They choose a way to make the favourite food harder to get, in three steps.  
  *Parent says:* Show me your test, in a drawing or with the bowls. How will you stop the side from deciding it? Now make the favourite food cost more. How far will they go for it?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your test, or set out the bowls and tell it. Fix each problem on your list. Then make the favourite food harder to get.
  - Note: A good plan has the same bowls, a coin for the sides and equal piles. The cost can be distance from a person, a paper lid to push off, or a smaller pile. The idea of a cost comes from this lesson's trade-off tests.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* A family member plays the bird. They secretly choose a favourite and how much they want it. Your child runs six tries of the plan and works out the favourite and how strong it is.  
  *Parent says:* I will be the bird. I have a favourite food, and I will not tell you. Run your test and find out what I want, and how much.  
  *Child mode:* A family member plays the bird. Run your test. Find out what they want, and how much.
  - Check: A bird picks ragi over rice when both are near. She picks rice when the ragi is close to a person. What does this show? → **She prefers ragi, but not enough to come near a person**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child fixes one weak point that the practice run showed. They write the final plan in five lines. If birds do visit your ledge, they may run it over the next few days.  
  *Parent says:* What went wrong in the practice run? Fix that one thing. Then write your final plan in five lines.  
  *Child mode:* Fix one thing that went wrong. Write your final plan in five lines.
  - Note: If your child runs the real test, keep to a spoonful of grain a day, so that the birds do not come to depend on it. Wash hands after handling the bowls.

## Worksheets
### Dances, Rumbles and Feelings (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) On the comb, a honeybee runs straight up while waggling. Where is the food?  
   Options: In the direction of the sun / At the top of the hive / Directly away from the sun  
   **Answer:** In the direction of the sun. *Why:* Straight up on the comb stands for 'towards the sun'. The dance is about a place outside, so the second answer mixes up the comb with the world. Straight down would mean away from the sun.
2. (mcq) What does a longer waggle run tell the bees who follow the dancer?  
   Options: The food is sweeter / The dancer is older / The food is farther away  
   **Answer:** The food is farther away. *Why:* The length of the waggle stands for distance. A bee who flew farther waggles for longer on each run.
3. (truefalse) Honeybees usually dance in the dark, so the followers read the dance by touch.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Inside a hive or a tree hollow there is almost no light. The followers stay close to the dancer and touch her with their antennae.
4. (fill) Sound that is too low for human ears is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['infrasound']. *Why:* Infrasound is lower than about 20 vibrations each second. The lowest part of an elephant's rumble is infrasound.
5. (mcq) Why can a very low rumble keep a spread-out elephant family in touch?  
   Options: Low sounds travel farther than high sounds / Low sounds are always louder / Only elephants can make low sounds  
   **Answer:** Low sounds travel farther than high sounds. *Why:* Low sounds lose less energy to the air and bend around trees and hills. So they carry much farther than high sounds of the same loudness.
6. (truefalse) If people nearby hear nothing, the elephants are not calling.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Much of a rumble is below human hearing. Katy Payne first noticed the calls as a throbbing in the air, and special recorders confirmed them.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Emotion → a body and brain state that can be measured; Feeling → what that state is like from inside; Reflex → a fast reaction that is the same each time; Trade-off → giving up one thing to get more of another. *Why:* An emotion can be measured from outside. A feeling is known from inside. A reflex does not change with what else matters, and a trade-off does.
8. (sort) Sort each one: can it be measured from outside, or is it known only from inside?  
   **Answer:** a fast heartbeat → Measured from outside; running away → Measured from outside; a stress chemical in the blood → Measured from outside; what fear is like → Known only from inside; how sweet jaggery tastes to you → Known only from inside. *Why:* Heartbeats, acts and chemicals can be measured by someone else. What an experience is like is known directly only by the one who has it.
9. (mcq) In a test, you hold ice longer for a big prize than for no prize. What does that show?  
   Options: Letting go was weighed against the prize / The ice was warmer in the big-prize round / Holding ice is a reflex  
   **Answer:** Letting go was weighed against the prize. *Why:* The ice was the same. Only the prize changed, and your time changed with it. So letting go was a choice that weighed discomfort against gain. A reflex would not change with the prize.
10. (truefalse) A blink at a puff of air changes a lot when a prize is offered.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The blink is a reflex. It happens almost every time, prize or no prize. That is what makes it different from a weighed choice.
11. (fill) A crab leaves a poor shell at a shock, but stays in a good one. This weighing is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['trade-off', 'trade off', 'tradeoff']. *Why:* The crab weighs the shock against the worth of the shell. That weighing is called a trade-off.
12. (write) Explain why scientists do not count a flinch alone as evidence that an animal feels pain.  
   **Answer:** A flinch can be a reflex, which happens the same way whatever else is going on. Scientists look for more, such as guarding the hurt place, learning to avoid the cause, or weighing the hurt against a reward.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a reflex needs no weighing, and names at least one further sign that scientists look for.
13. (mcq) Which is something you saw, and not a guess about a feeling?  
   Options: The dog lowered her tail and backed away / The dog was ashamed / The dog wanted to say sorry  
   **Answer:** The dog lowered her tail and backed away. *Why:* A camera could record the tail and the backing away. 'Ashamed' and 'wanted to say sorry' are guesses about what went on inside her.
14. (write) A pigeon flies off the ledge when you open the window. Write what you saw. Then write two possible explanations.  
   **Answer:** Seen: the pigeon flew off just as the window opened. Explanations: the sudden noise or movement frightened the pigeon, or the pigeon was about to leave anyway.. *Why:* A strong answer keeps the act separate from the guesses, and gives two different explanations for the same act.
15. (draw) Draw a waggle dance that sends bees a quarter turn to the right of the sun, far away. Label the run.  
   **Answer:** A comb with 'up' marked, and a long wavy run pointing to the right, a quarter turn from up. The label says the run is long because the food is far.. *Why:* A strong drawing points the run to the right of 'up' by a quarter turn, and makes it long to show a far place.
### Reflex or Weighed? (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Bees flew a few metres through a narrow patterned tunnel, then danced for a far place. Why?  
   Options: The tunnel made them tired / The pattern rushed past their eyes / They forgot where they had been  
   **Answer:** The pattern rushed past their eyes. *Why:* A bee measures distance by how much scenery streams past her eyes. In a narrow patterned tunnel a lot streams past in a short flight, so she measures 'far'.
2. (truefalse) In India, the dwarf honeybee dances on the flat top of her comb and points straight at the food.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Her comb is in the open, so she can dance on a flat surface and aim her run at the food. Bees who dance on an upright comb use the 'up means sun' code.
3. (order) Put in order how scientists showed that bees use the danced direction.  
   **Answer:** ['Bees followed a dance in the hive', 'Scientists fixed tiny radar aerials on the followers', 'They carried the followers to a new starting spot', 'The bees flew the danced direction and distance from there', 'The bees arrived at the wrong place, so smell was not guiding them']. *Why:* Moving the bees separated the dance message from the smell of the real flowers. The bees followed the message.
4. (mcq) Recorded rumbles were played into the ground only. Elephants bunched together and faced the source. What does this show?  
   Options: Elephants can pick up signals through the ground / Elephants see sound / The ground makes rumbles louder  
   **Answer:** Elephants can pick up signals through the ground. *Why:* Nothing was played through the air, and the elephants still responded. So the message reached them through the ground.
5. (count) A listener gets 2 of 6 messages right standing, and 6 of 6 with an ear on the floor. How many more?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 6 take away 2 is 4. With three possible messages, guessing alone gives about 2 right out of 6. So the standing score is no better than guessing.
6. (sort) Sort each one: a reflex, or a weighed choice?  
   **Answer:** a knee kick when the knee is tapped → Reflex; a blink at a puff of air → Reflex; a crab leaving a poor shell but not a good one → Weighed choice; a bee accepting heat for sweeter sugar → Weighed choice; holding ice longer for a bigger prize → Weighed choice. *Why:* The reflexes happen the same way each time. The weighed choices change when the reward changes.
7. (count) A child holds ice 20 seconds for no prize and 60 seconds for a big prize. How many times as long?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 60 divided by 20 is 3. The child held on three times as long for the big prize. The ice did not change, so the prize made the difference.
8. (mcq) Why does the ice test start and end with a no-prize round?  
   Options: To check whether the order of rounds mattered / To give the child more practice / To use up the ice  
   **Answer:** To check whether the order of rounds mattered. *Why:* If the first and last rounds give close times, then cold hands and practice did not change much. Any big change in the middle rounds came from the prize.
9. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Guards a sore place. Learns to avoid harm. Flinches. Accepts harm for reward.  
   **Answer:** Flinches. A flinch can be a reflex. The other three need more than a reflex.. *Why:* Guarding, learning and weighing all change with the situation. A flinch happens the same way each time.
10. (truefalse) Shaken honeybees could no longer tell the sweet smell from the bitter smell.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They still told the two apart. What changed was how they treated in-between smells. They took those as bitter more often.
11. (write) A crayfish who got shocks stays in the dark. A calming medicine sends them back to the light. What does this suggest?  
   **Answer:** The shocks left the crayfish in a lasting state like anxiety. The medicine that eases anxiety in people changed it, so the state may work through similar signalling chemicals.. *Why:* A strong answer says the state lasted after the shocks, and links the medicine to shared signalling chemicals. It does not claim to know what the crayfish felt.
12. (mcq) Serotonin is found in people, crayfish and bees. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: They all eat the same food / They inherited it from shared ancestors / Scientists gave it to them  
   **Answer:** They inherited it from shared ancestors. *Why:* These animals share ancestors who lived more than 500 million years ago and already used serotonin. Their descendants kept it.
13. (write) Explain why 'the elephant touched the calf with her trunk' and 'the elephant comforted the calf' are different kinds of sentence.  
   **Answer:** The first says what was seen. The second adds a guess about why she did it and what the calf felt. The guess may be right, but it needs evidence.. *Why:* A strong answer says one is an observation and the other is an explanation of it.
14. (perspective) Elephants stayed for days beside a dead calf.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side with a reason, and names something to check, such as stress chemicals, or how they treat dead elephants who are not family.. *Why:* Both views accept what the elephants did. They differ on how far the evidence lets us go in naming a feeling.
15. (draw) Draw a fair test of whether hens prefer a wire floor or loose litter. Label what you keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Two pens side by side, the same size and light, one with wire and one with litter, a hen at the entrance, and a coin to choose the sides.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps size, light, food and water the same, changes only the floor, and swaps the sides between tries.
### Who Is Conscious? (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What does it mean to say there is 'something it is like' to be a bat?  
   Options: The bat has experiences of her own / The bat looks like something / Bats are like people  
   **Answer:** The bat has experiences of her own. *Why:* The philosopher Thomas Nagel used this phrase in 1974 for consciousness. It means the bat has an inside view, whatever that view is like.
2. (truefalse) Scientists can measure another person's feeling directly with a brain scanner.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A scanner measures brain activity. The feeling is still worked out from that activity and from what the person says and does.
3. (match) Match each thinker to their view about other animals.  
   **Answer:** Rene Descartes, 1637 → they move like clockwork, with nothing felt; Charles Darwin, 1871 → their minds differ from ours in amount, not in kind; Joseph LeDoux → freezing in danger does not prove felt fear; Jain teachers → every living being senses, down to one sense. *Why:* These four views run from denying feeling in other animals, through caution, to finding it in every living being.
4. (mcq) The New York Declaration of 2024 says conscious experience in insects is what?  
   Options: A realistic possibility / Proven beyond doubt / Impossible  
   **Answer:** A realistic possibility. *Why:* The declaration says the evidence is strong for mammals and birds. For insects, crabs and octopuses it says there is at least a realistic possibility.
5. (mcq) Some say pain needs a cortex, so fish feel none. Which fact weakens this argument most?  
   Options: Fish live in water / Birds lack a layered cortex but solve hard problems / Fish are smaller than people  
   **Answer:** Birds lack a layered cortex but solve hard problems. *Why:* Birds show that a brain without a layered cortex can do jobs that the cortex does in mammals. So lacking a cortex does not show that a job is not done.
6. (write) What is the 'hard problem' of consciousness? Explain it in your own words.  
   **Answer:** Nobody can yet explain how the activity of nerve cells goes with a feeling. We can map which cells are active, but that does not say why the activity is felt.. *Why:* A strong answer separates describing brain activity from explaining why there is an experience at all.
7. (count) A bee's brain has about 1 million nerve cells. A human brain has about 86 billion. How many times as many?  
   **Answer:** About 86,000 times. *Why:* 86 billion divided by 1 million is 86,000. Yet a bee learns, remembers and weighs choices, so the number of cells does not settle who feels.
8. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Dopamine. Serotonin. Octopamine. Infrasound.  
   **Answer:** Infrasound. It is a kind of sound. The other three are signalling chemicals.. *Why:* Dopamine, serotonin and octopamine carry messages between cells. Infrasound is sound too low for human ears.
9. (truefalse) Because a bee and a person both use dopamine, we know that a bee's joy feels like a person's joy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A shared chemical shows shared ancestry and similar machinery. It does not show that the experience is the same, because one chemical can do different jobs.
10. (write) Is the waggle dance a language? Give one reason for yes and one reason for no.  
   **Answer:** For yes: the dance uses a symbol, because 'up' stands for the sun, and it is about a place that is out of sight. For no: it covers only a few subjects and has no grammar for making new sentences.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a real feature of the dance on each side, and may say that it depends on how 'language' is defined.
11. (perspective) A restaurant wants to drop live crabs into boiling water.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer uses evidence from crab tests, and says what is lost if each person turns out to be wrong.. *Why:* The evidence does not give certainty either way. The two views differ on who should carry the risk of being wrong.
12. (mcq) Hens paid a cost to reach loose litter. What does paying a cost add to a choice test?  
   Options: It shows hens can count / It makes the test shorter / It shows how much the hens want it  
   **Answer:** It shows how much the hens want it. *Why:* A free choice shows which thing an animal prefers. A choice with a cost shows how strongly they prefer it.
13. (write) Design a test of whether a snail weighs harm against reward. Say what you would change and measure.  
   **Answer:** Offer food across a mildly unpleasant surface, such as a slightly rough or dry strip. Change how good the food is. Measure whether the snail crosses more often for better food.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the reward, keeps the unpleasant thing the same and harmless, and measures a clear act over many tries.
14. (perspective) A friend says her fish 'gets lonely' when she is at school.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer changes one thing, for example someone else brings the food, or the friend comes without food, and watches what the fish does.. *Why:* Both explanations fit what was seen. A test that separates the person from the food would help to tell them apart.
15. (draw) Draw a rumble travelling from one elephant to another by two paths. Label each path and what picks it up.  
   **Answer:** Wavy lines through the air reaching the second elephant's ears, and ripples through the ground reaching her feet and trunk tip.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the air path and the ground path, and labels the ears for one and the feet or trunk for the other.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A honeybee waggles straight down the comb. Which way should her sisters fly?  
   Options: Towards the sun / Directly away from the sun / Down to the ground  
   **Answer:** Directly away from the sun. *Why:* Up stands for towards the sun, so down stands for directly away from the sun. If you chose 'down to the ground', remember that the dance is a code for a direction outside.
2. (mcq) How did radar tracking show that bees use the dance and not only smell?  
   Options: Bees flew faster with radar / Moved bees flew the danced course to the wrong place / Bees stopped dancing  
   **Answer:** Moved bees flew the danced course to the wrong place. *Why:* Bees released from a new spot flew the danced direction and distance from there. Smell would have led them to the real flowers.
3. (mcq) Why do elephants use such low calls to stay in touch?  
   Options: High sounds hurt their ears / Low sounds carry farther through forest / Low sounds use no energy  
   **Answer:** Low sounds carry farther through forest. *Why:* Low sounds lose less energy to the air and bend around trees and hills, so they reach family members who are far away.
4. (truefalse) An elephant can pick up another elephant's rumble through the ground.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Elephants responded when recorded rumbles were played into the soil alone. Their feet and trunk tips have many nerve endings for pressure.
5. (mcq) Which of these is an emotion as scientists use the word, and not a feeling?  
   Options: A racing heart and tight muscles in danger / What being scared is like / What a mango tastes like to you  
   **Answer:** A racing heart and tight muscles in danger. *Why:* An emotion is the measurable state of body and brain. What that state is like from inside is the feeling.
6. (mcq) A bumblebee walks onto a hot feeder only when its sugar is much sweeter. What is she doing?  
   Options: Weighing harm against reward / Showing a reflex / Failing to sense the heat  
   **Answer:** Weighing harm against reward. *Why:* Her choice changed when the reward changed, so heat was weighed against sugar. A reflex would pull her off the heat every time.
7. (mcq) Which result would suggest that letting go of ice is a weighed choice?  
   Options: Hold times are the same in every round / Hold times rise as the prize rises / The ice melts faster each round  
   **Answer:** Hold times rise as the prize rises. *Why:* If the time changes with the prize while the ice stays the same, the prize is being weighed against the discomfort.
8. (mcq) Which is the strongest evidence that an animal's response to harm is more than a reflex?  
   Options: She pulls away quickly / She pulls away every time / She gives up a reward to avoid the harm  
   **Answer:** She gives up a reward to avoid the harm. *Why:* Pulling away quickly, every time, is what a reflex looks like. Giving up something she wants shows that the harm is being weighed.
9. (truefalse) Scientists all agree that insects have conscious experiences.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Scientists agree on what bees do in the tests. They disagree on whether that shows experience. The 2024 declaration calls it a realistic possibility.
10. (mcq) You write: 'The crow cawed three times and flew to the wire.' What kind of note is this?  
   Options: A guess about a feeling / What was seen / An explanation  
   **Answer:** What was seen. *Why:* It records acts that a camera could capture. A guess would add what the crow felt or wanted.
11. (mcq) Why did Britain's 2022 law add crabs and octopuses to the animals it protects?  
   Options: A review found strong evidence that they can feel / They are endangered / They are clever at puzzles  
   **Answer:** A review found strong evidence that they can feel. *Why:* A review of more than 300 studies found evidence such as guarding wounds, learning to avoid harm and making trade-offs. The law is about feeling, not rarity or cleverness.

## Applications
- Farms, zoos and laboratories use preference tests to decide what space, flooring and company the animals in their care need.
- In the Anamalai hills, phone messages and warning lights tell tea estate workers when elephants are near, so that people and elephants keep apart.
- Scientists place recorders in forests to count elephants by their rumbles, and to pick up gunshots from poachers.
- Vets now give painkillers and anaesthetics to fish, and some aquariums do the same for octopuses.
- Britain's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act of 2022 requires the government to consider how its decisions affect animals who can feel, including crabs and octopuses.
- Engineers have copied the way honeybee colonies share news of good flower patches, to write computer methods that search for good answers.
- Nurses and doctors judge pain in babies, who cannot speak, from the face, the cry, the heartbeat and how the baby moves. They work out a feeling from outside, as this lesson does for other animals.

## Open questions
- **Is there something it is like to be a bee?** Bees pass several tests for emotion-like states. But every test measures what a bee does, and nobody has found a way to check the experience itself.
- **Do elephants really have names for each other?** One study in 2024 found name-like calls in African elephants. It has not yet been repeated, and nobody has tested Asian elephants in the same way.
- **How does the activity of nerve cells go with a feeling?** Scientists can find which cells are active during a feeling. No theory yet explains why that activity is felt, even in people.
- **What does a bee who follows a dance actually pick up from the dancer?** Followers touch the dancer and sense her movements, the air she stirs and her smell. Scientists are still working out which of these carries the direction and distance.
- **Do other animals have feelings that humans have no word for?** Our words for feelings were made for human lives. An animal who senses by echoes or by smell may have states that none of our words fit.

## Connected maths
- Meenakshi held the ice for 20 seconds with no prize, 35 seconds for a small prize and 60 seconds for a big prize. How many times as long did she hold on for the big prize as for no prize? What changed between those two rounds? **3 times as long. Only the prize changed.**. 60 divided by 20 is 3. The ice cube, the hand rule and the rest time were the same in both rounds. Only the prize was different. So the prize explains why she held on three times as long. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Kavya's four rounds were 22 seconds with no prize, 41 with a small prize, 68 with a big prize and 25 with no prize again. Her brother says she only improved with practice. Use the numbers to answer him. **The two no-prize rounds differ by only 3 seconds, so practice changed very little. The big-prize round was 43 seconds longer than the last no-prize round.**. 25 take away 22 is 3 seconds. That is the most that practice can explain. 68 take away 25 is 43 seconds. Round 4 came after round 3, so if practice were the cause, round 4 would be the longest. It was not. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- For one kind of honeybee, 1 second of waggling means about 1 kilometre. A dancer waggles for 2.5 seconds. A honeybee flies about 25 kilometres in an hour. About how many minutes will a follower take to reach the flowers? **About 6 minutes.**. 2.5 seconds means about 2.5 kilometres. At 25 kilometres an hour, a bee covers 2.5 kilometres in one tenth of an hour. One tenth of 60 minutes is 6 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- The sun is in the east. A dancer's waggle run points a quarter turn to the right of straight up. In which compass direction is the food? **South.**. Straight up stands for the sun's direction, which is east. A quarter turn to the right is 90 degrees clockwise. Starting from east and turning 90 degrees clockwise brings you to south. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L5`)
- In the Floor Message Test, each message is 1, 2 or 3 thumps, chosen by a die. Arjun gets 2 right out of 6 standing and 6 out of 6 with an ear on the floor. Which score could be luck alone? Why? **The standing score could be luck. Guessing among three messages gives about 2 right out of 6.**. With three equally likely messages, a guess is right about 1 time in 3. One third of 6 is 2. So 2 out of 6 is what guessing gives. Getting all 6 right by guessing would happen less than once in 700 tests. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- In the hermit crab game, you have a shell worth 3 and 5 beans. The pinch is strength 3. Staying costs 3 beans. Leaving costs 2 beans, and a new shell is worth 3 on average. Which choice leaves you with more points, on average? **Leaving, by 1 point on average: 15 points against 14.**. Your score is the shell's worth times 4, plus your beans. If you stay, that is 3 times 4, plus 2 beans, which makes 14. If you leave, a new shell is worth 3 on average. That is 3 times 4, plus 3 beans, which makes 15. With a shell worth 5, staying would win. (queues `math.logic.if-then.L5`)
