# Wet but thirsty: how a root drinks, and why salty soil dries a plant
`sci.plants.water-transport.L4`

**Summary.** A seedling standing in a flooded field can droop like a plant in the desert. Water is all around her roots, and she still cannot drink it. Water moves toward the place where it is held more tightly, and salt holds water tightly. Your child sprouts moong seeds in water with different amounts of salt, and finds where the sprouting stops. They then follow water along its whole path, from soil to root to leaf to air, and see that dry air does most of the pulling. They also meet the mangroves of the Sundarbans, which live in sea water.

**Intro.** *Pour a glass of sea water on a potted plant. Within two days she droops, though her soil is soaking wet. How can a plant die of thirst while she stands in water?* Try this at the table. Cut a cucumber into slices as thin as a coin. Lay ten slices on a plate and sprinkle a good pinch of salt over them. Come back in 20 minutes. There is a puddle on the plate, and the slices have gone floppy. The salt did not make that water. It came out of the cucumber. Whatever the salt does to a cucumber slice, it can also do to the roots of a plant.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that water moves toward the place where it is held more tightly, and that salt and thin films of water hold it tightly.
1. I can explain why a plant can wilt in soil that is wet with salty water.
2. I can describe how a root takes water in through root hairs, and why the root's cells must hold water tighter than the soil does.
3. I can describe the path of water through a plant as a slope from soil to leaf to dry air, and say where most of the drop happens.
4. I can explain how the holes in a leaf open and shut by the same movement of water.
5. I can plan a fair test with one change, and use the results to say how salty is too salty for a sprout.
6. I can tell the journey of water in the xylem apart from the journey of sugar in the phloem.
7. I can give two ways that plants such as mangroves live in salty or dry places.

## Terms
- **Pull number**: A number for how tightly water is held. A bigger pull number means a tighter hold. Scientists call the real thing water potential and write it with a minus sign. *e.g.* Water in a glass has a pull number of 0. Sea water is about 2.5.
- **Osmosis**: The movement of water through a thin skin toward the side where water is held more tightly. *e.g.* Salt on a cucumber slice pulls water out of the slice by osmosis.
- **Membrane**: A very thin skin around every cell. It lets water through easily and holds back many dissolved things. *e.g.* The membrane around a root cell lets water in but keeps the sugars inside.
- **Dissolved**: Mixed into water so well that you cannot see it any more, like salt in a glass of water. *e.g.* The salt in sea water is dissolved. You taste it but you cannot see it.
- **Root hair**: A very thin tube that grows out of one cell on the outside of a young root. It takes in water. *e.g.* A moong sprout's root has a fuzz of white root hairs.
- **Turgor**: The firm push of a cell full of water against its own wall, like air pushing inside a tyre. *e.g.* Turgor keeps a fresh palak leaf stiff.
- **Wilting**: Drooping, because the plant's cells have lost water and lost their firm push. *e.g.* Coriander left on a counter wilts in an hour or two.
- **Transpiration**: The loss of water as vapour from a plant, mostly through tiny holes in the leaves. *e.g.* A bag tied over a leaf fills with tiny drops of transpired water.
- **Stoma**: A tiny hole in a leaf, shaped like a mouth, that lets air in and water vapour out. The plural is stomata. *e.g.* A tulsi leaf has thousands of stomata on its underside.
- **Guard cell**: One of the two bean-shaped cells around a stoma. They swell to open it and shrink to close it. *e.g.* In bright morning light, the guard cells of a leaf swell and the stomata open.
- **Xylem**: The tubes that carry water and dissolved minerals from the roots up to the leaves. *e.g.* The strings in a celery stalk are bundles of xylem.
- **Phloem**: The tubes that carry sweet sap, made in the leaves, to the roots, fruits and growing tips. *e.g.* Sweet neera from a cut coconut flower stalk is phloem sap.
- **Salinity**: How salty water or soil is. *e.g.* Sea water has a salinity of about 35 grams of salt in every litre.
- **Mangrove**: A tree or shrub that grows in salty water along coasts and river mouths. *e.g.* The Sundarbans in West Bengal is the biggest mangrove forest in the world.

## Explanations
### Water has a grip: some water is held tightly · *defined*
**Definition:** Water is held by the things around it. Some water is held loosely and moves easily. Some is held tightly and is very hard to pull away. In this unit, a pull number says how tightly water is held. A bigger pull number means a tighter hold.

Plain water in a glass is held very loosely. Its pull number is 0. Water in the thin film around a grain of soil is held more tightly, because the grain attracts it. Water with salt or sugar dissolved in it is held more tightly too, because the dissolved bits attract water. Scientists call this idea water potential. They measure it in megapascals, written MPa, and put a minus sign in front. We leave the minus sign out, so a bigger number is a tighter grip. Here are some typical numbers. Wet soil is about 0.1. A leaf cell is about 1 to 1.5. Sea water is about 2.5. Water always moves from the place with the smaller pull number to the place with the bigger one. So 'wet' does not tell you if a root can drink. The grip does.
- e.g. Plain tap water has a pull number of about 0, so it is easy to take.
- e.g. A handful of damp soil that you squeeze still holds water you cannot squeeze out. That water is held tightly.
- e.g. Sea water is about 2.5, which is tighter than the 1 to 1.5 inside most leaf cells.
### Osmosis: water crosses a thin skin toward the tighter grip · *defined*
**Definition:** Osmosis is the movement of water through a thin skin, called a membrane, from the side where water is held more loosely to the side where it is held more tightly. The membrane lets water through but holds back many dissolved things.

Every plant cell has a membrane, and a stiff wall outside it. Inside the cell is a sap with sugars and salts dissolved in it. If the water outside holds water more loosely than the sap does, water flows in. The cell swells and pushes on its wall, like air in a tyre. That firm push is called turgor, and it keeps a leaf standing up. If the outside holds water more tightly, as with salt on a cucumber slice, water flows out. The cell shrinks and goes soft. Many soft cells together make a plant wilt. The wall pushes back as the cell fills. The flow stops when the push-back is as strong as the pull. Then the cell is full and firm.
- e.g. Salt on cucumber slices pulls water out of the slices, so a puddle forms and the slices go limp.
- e.g. A wilted coriander sprig stood in water takes water in through its cut end and cells, and goes firm again.
- e.g. A dry moong seed swells in water because its cells hold water tightly and pull it in.
### How a root takes water in · *defined*
**Definition:** A root takes in water through thousands of thin root hairs. Water flows in when the root's cells hold water more tightly than the soil does.

Behind the tip of each young root, single cells grow out into root hairs. They are so thin that they slip between soil grains, and they make the root's surface huge. One rye plant, grown in a box of soil and carefully measured in 1937, had about 14 billion root hairs. Laid end to end, they would be over 10,000 kilometres long. The root's cells keep their sap thick with sugars and minerals, so they hold water tightly. The plant spends energy to pump minerals into her root cells, and water follows the minerals in. When the soil dries, a plant makes her root sap even thicker, so she can still win water from soil that holds it tightly. The water crosses the root to the xylem. A waxy band around the xylem stops water sneaking past between the cells, so every drop must go through a cell skin. That is how the root decides what gets in.
- e.g. A moong sprout grown on a wet cloth shows a white fuzz of root hairs under a magnifying glass.
- e.g. Roots of a potted tulsi that dries out grow thicker sap, which is why a plant that has dried a little can drink from soil that looked too dry.
- e.g. Pulling a weed leaves the root hairs behind in the soil, which is why a transplanted seedling needs shade and extra water for a few days.
### Fungus helpers: reaching water a root cannot reach · *defined*
**Definition:** Mycorrhiza is a partnership between a plant's roots and a fungus. The fungus grows threads, thinner than root hairs, out into the soil and brings back water and minerals. The plant gives the fungus sugar.

About 7 or 8 in every 10 kinds of land plants have fungus partners. The fungal threads reach into tiny gaps that even a root hair cannot enter, and they spread far from the root. In return, the plant sends part of the sugar she makes down to the fungus. Water and minerals travel one way, and sugar travels the other. Scientists are still finding how much water the fungus really brings in different soils.
- e.g. Most trees in a forest share fungal threads under the soil.
- e.g. Many crop plants, such as maize and finger millet, grow better with fungus partners in poor soil.
### The slope from soil to sky · *defined*
**Definition:** Water in a plant moves along a slope of pull numbers. It starts in wet soil, where the number is small. It passes through the root, the stem and the leaf, and ends in the air outside, where the number is large.

Here are rounded example numbers. Wet soil is 0.1. A root cell is 0.5. The xylem in the stem is 1. A leaf cell is 1.5. Moist air at 90 percent humidity is about 14. Dry air at 50 percent humidity is about 94. Water flows down this slope because each step has a slightly bigger number. Look at the last step. The drop from the leaf to dry air is about 60 times bigger than all the steps in the plant put together. So the air does nearly all the pulling. Lifting water against gravity costs only about 0.1 for every 10 metres of height. Even for a tree 100 metres tall, that is small next to what dry air offers. In fog, the air is about 99 percent humid and its number is only about 1.4. Then the slope almost vanishes, and a plant loses almost no water. Some tall trees even take in a little water from fog through their leaves.
- e.g. Washing dries faster on a dry, windy day because dry air pulls water out harder.
- e.g. A plant loses much less water during a humid monsoon day than on a dry summer afternoon.
- e.g. A tree 50 metres tall needs only about 0.5 of pull to lift water to the top, which is tiny next to dry air.
### Leaves let water out: transpiration · *defined*
**Definition:** Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mostly through the stomata, the tiny holes in a leaf. More than 95 of every 100 drops that enter the roots leave this way.

A leaf must open its stomata to let in carbon dioxide, the gas it needs to make food. But when the holes are open, water vapour escapes. Many leaves have 100 to 300 stomata on every square millimetre. Only 1 or 2 drops in every 100 that a plant takes in are used to build her body. The rest passes through the plant and goes into the air. On a hot day, a field of crops sends up about 5 litres of water from each square metre of ground. This is not waste. The water on its way up carries minerals from the soil to the leaves, and its evaporation cools the leaf in the sun, like sweat cools your skin.
- e.g. A leaf in a closed bag soon has droplets on the inside of the bag.
- e.g. A grown banyan tree can send hundreds of litres of water into the air on a hot day.
- e.g. A field of rice needs about 2,500 litres of water for each kilogram of rice, and most of that water passes through the plants.
### Guard cells: a hole that opens by drinking · *defined*
**Definition:** Each stoma has two guard cells beside it. When water flows into the guard cells, they swell and bend apart, and the hole opens. When water flows out, they straighten and the hole shuts.

In morning light, the guard cells pump potassium, a mineral, into themselves. Their sap gets thicker, so water flows in by osmosis. They swell, and the thin inner wall of each guard cell is bent open like a bow. When the plant is short of water, her roots and leaves make a signal chemical called abscisic acid. It makes the guard cells let their potassium out. Water follows out, the guard cells go soft, and the hole shuts. At night most stomata shut. A plant must balance two needs: let in carbon dioxide, and not lose too much water. How a leaf weighs that balance, minute by minute, is still being studied.
- e.g. On a hot afternoon, a bean plant closes many of her stomata to save water.
- e.g. In bright light and moist soil, the stomata of a leaf are mostly open.
- e.g. When a cut leaf wilts quickly, its guard cells have lost water and the holes have closed.
### Why salt makes plants thirsty · *defined*
**Definition:** Salty soil water has a big pull number. If it is bigger than the pull number of the root's cells, a root cannot take water in, and water flows out of the root instead.

Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in every litre. Its pull number is about 2.5. A leaf cell of most crop plants is about 1 to 1.5, and a root cell is lower still. So sea water holds its water tighter than the plant does. Water flows out of the roots, and the plant wilts in the middle of a flood. The same thing happens in two other ways. Too much fertiliser is a kind of salt, and it can 'burn' a pot plant. And in dry places, water from canals or wells has a little salt in it. When that water evaporates from a field, the salt stays behind and slowly builds up in the soil. Salt also does a second harm. Some of it gets into the plant and damages her cells over days and weeks. Farmers fix salty soil by washing the salt down with fresh water, by building drains, and by growing crops that tolerate salt.
- e.g. A pot plant given a spoon of fertiliser, instead of a pinch, droops the next day.
- e.g. After a cyclone pushes sea water over a field, the paddy droops even when the field is wet.
- e.g. A white crust on the soil of a dry field is salt left behind after water evaporated.
### Winning the contest: mangroves and other salt-tolerant plants · *defined*
**Definition:** A salt-tolerant plant has a way to hold water tighter than salty water does, and a way to deal with the salt. Mangroves do this in sea water. Each kind has its own method.

Mangroves live where rivers meet the sea, as in the Sundarbans and at Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu. Their leaf and root cells hold water far tighter than 2.5, so they can pull water out of sea water. Most Rhizophora mangroves keep out more than 90 of every 100 grains of salt at the root. The water in their xylem is nearly fresh. Avicennia mangroves let some salt in and push it out through salt glands on their leaves. You can see white crystals on the leaf surface. Some mangroves store salt in old leaves, which then fall off. Their roots stand up out of the mud, because mud has little air. Other salt-tolerant plants include the coconut palm and the Pokkali rice of Kerala, which grows in fields that sea water enters.
- e.g. Avicennia leaves can have a white dusting of salt that tastes salty.
- e.g. Pokkali rice in Kerala is grown in salty paddies and fish are farmed in the same fields after harvest.
- e.g. A coconut palm grows on a beach where most other trees cannot.
### The second river: sugar travels in the phloem · *defined*
**Definition:** The xylem carries water up. The phloem is a second set of tubes that carries sugary sap from the leaves to every part of the plant that needs food. Phloem and xylem lie side by side in the stem.

In the 1670s, the Italian scientist Marcello Malpighi cut a ring of bark off a branch. Bark holds the phloem. The leaves above the ring stayed green, because the xylem inside the wood still brought water up. But the bark swelled above the ring, because sugar was stuck there. This shows two pipes. A leaf makes sugar and pumps it into the phloem. Water follows the sugar in by osmosis, so the pressure at the leaf rises and pushes the sap along. The sap flows toward a root, a fruit or a growing tip, where the sugar is taken out and used or stored. The water then goes back into the xylem. The sweet neera that drips from a cut coconut or palmyra flower stalk is phloem sap. An idea of this kind was first written down by Ernst Münch in 1930, and it is still tested today.
- e.g. Neera from a cut coconut flower stalk is sweet because it is phloem sap.
- e.g. A tree with a ring of bark cut right round the trunk slowly dies from the roots, because sugar cannot get down to them.
- e.g. Sugarcane stores its sweet sap in the stem.

## Mostly agreed
- Water moves through a plant cell's skin toward the side where water is held more tightly. This is called osmosis. *How we know:* Scientists in the 1800s sealed sugar solutions in membrane bags and watched water flow in. Wilhelm Pfeffer measured the pressure in 1877. Plant cells shrink in strong salt and swell in plain water under the microscope.
- More than 95 of every 100 drops of water that enter a plant leave through the leaves as vapour. *How we know:* Stephen Hales weighed plants and their water in the 1720s. Modern gas meters on single leaves give the same answer.
- Salty soil makes it hard or impossible for most crop plants to take in water. *How we know:* Seeds sprout less and less as salt rises in field and lab trials. Salt-affected farmland has lower yields in India and across the world.
- The holes in a leaf open and close because the guard cells around them swell and shrink with water. *How we know:* Under a microscope, guard cells swell and shut in a few minutes when light, dryness or a plant signal chemical changes.
- Dry air can pull on a leaf's water far harder than is needed to lift water to the top of the tallest trees. *How we know:* The pull in dry air at 50 percent humidity works out to about 94 on our scale. Lifting water 100 metres needs about 1.
- Water goes up in the xylem, and sugar travels in the phloem, which is a different set of tubes. *How we know:* Cutting a ring of bark stops sugar but not water. Aphid feeding and radioactive-labelled sugars show where the sugar flows.

## How we found out
**Per Scholander (1905 to 1980), a Norwegian-born scientist working in the United States, building on Henry Dixon and John Joly of Dublin, 1894 (the idea), and the early 1960s, with results published in 1965 (the measurement).** In 1894, Henry Dixon and John Joly, two scientists in Dublin, said that a tree pulls her water up from the top, and that the water holds together in an unbroken thread under strain. Many scientists did not believe it. A pulled thread of water, they said, should snap. And nobody could measure the pull inside a living tree. In the early 1960s, Per Scholander, a Norwegian-born scientist working in the United States, found a way. He cut a leafy twig and sealed it in a metal chamber, with the cut end poking out. Then he slowly raised the gas pressure around the leaves. When a drop of sap just showed at the cut end, the gas pressure matched how hard the twig had been pulling its water. He measured pulls many times stronger than the pressure of the air around us. The thread of water had not snapped. Scientists still argue about some details.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought a thread of water pulled so hard must snap, so the pull idea could not be right. Scholander measured the pull and found that plants hold water under strong pull without it breaking.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Water always flows from the wetter place to the drier place.** (Most people, in everyday life.; *rejected*)  
  For: Water runs downhill and soaks into dry cloth, so it seems to go from wet to dry.  
  Against: Across a plant cell's skin, water flows toward the side where it is held more tightly, even if that side already has salt. 'Wet' hides the grip.
- **Salt kills a plant because salt is a poison.** (Many gardeners and some textbooks.; *debated*)  
  For: Some salt does get in and harms cells over time, and a plant with too much salt in her leaves burns at the edges.  
  Against: Seeds stop sprouting in salty water before much salt enters. A harmless dissolved chemical that is not salt can have the same effect. The first harm is thirst. The poison comes later.
- **All mangroves get rid of salt in the same way.** (Some books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many mangrove pictures show white salt crystals on leaves.  
  Against: Rhizophora mangroves keep most salt out at the root. Avicennia mangroves push salt out through their leaves. Others store salt in old leaves. Each kind has its own method.
- **The roots are the engine that pushes water up to the leaves.** (Many students and some older books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Roots really can push a little. At night, sap can ooze from a cut stem, which is called root pressure.  
  Against: A cut stem with no roots still lifts water to its leaves. Root pressure is far too weak to lift water to the top of a tall tree. The pull from the leaves does the lifting.
- **A flooded field kills plants only because the roots drown for lack of air.** (Many farmers.; *debated*)  
  For: Water-filled soil has little air, and most roots need oxygen.  
  Against: Rice survives fresh water floods for days. Sea water floods kill rice much faster, which shows that the salt matters as well as the missing air.

## Roots
We have tested salt, seeds and leaves, and followed water from the soil to the sky. Now we ask what this tells us about thirst, about wet and dry, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Water does not go where there is the most water, but where it is held most tightly. A root can drink only if her cells hold water tighter than the soil does. Salty soil holds water very tightly, so a plant can stand in a flooded field and still die of thirst.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'wet' and 'dry' as if they only measured how much water there is, and as if more water always means more to drink. But water has a grip. Water in salty mud, or in a thin film on soil grains, is held so tightly that a root cannot pull it free.  
*Evidence:* In the sprout test, every cup got the same damp towel and the same amount of water. The seeds sprouted in plain water, sprouted fewer and slower with a little salt, and not at all in strong salt water. Salt on cucumber slices pulled water out of them in the same way.

- *who am i:* Your own cells are salty inside, and that is how they keep their water. If you drink sea water, you lose water and get thirstier. The same rule that dries a seedling in the sea is working in you.
- *what is life:* A mangrove keeps salt out at her roots. A sea fish drinks sea water and pushes the salt out through her gills. Different living things found different ways to win the same contest over water.
- *what is this world:* Rain, soil, a root, a leaf and dry air are not five kinds of water. It is one water, moving down one long slope from the ground to the sky.

A drop of water in wet soil, in a root, in a leaf and in the air is the same drop on a long journey. The drop goes toward wherever it is held more tightly, step by step, and the biggest step of all is the last one, from the leaf into dry air. A plant cannot stop this slope. She can only change the grip, by thickening her sap, shutting a leaf hole or keeping salt out at the root. So each plant is a place where the water's long slope from the sea, to the cloud, to the soil, to the sky passes through a living body.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to put a lump of salt in water at night, and to bring it back in the morning. Shvetaketu cannot find it. Uddalaka tells him to sip from the top, the middle and the bottom. It is salty everywhere. The salt is there even though no one can see it. (Dissolved salt cannot be seen, yet it decides where the water goes. In a cucumber, a seed or a root, what you cannot see still controls what you can.)
- **Rig Veda 10.9.1 to 10.9.3 (the hymn to the Waters):** The Waters are asked to share their kindest sap, as loving mothers share with their children, and are praised as givers of strength and healing. (Water brings its sap, the minerals and sugars, to every part of a plant. The hymn saw water as the thing that carries life from one being to another.)
- Science (settled): Water moves from higher to lower water potential in every living thing, in plants, fungi, bacteria and animals. The same rule governs a root, a mushroom and a kidney.
- Science (open): Whether a plant thickening her root sap in dry soil is making a decision is a question scientists describe in different words, and it is still being asked.
- Other view, Many plant physiologists: When the soil dries, a root's cells fill with more sugars and salts because chemical signals switch genes on. That is chemistry running by itself. Saying the plant 'decides' is only a way of talking.
- Practice, The water you cannot squeeze out (3 min): Wet a clean cloth and hold it in both hands. Squeeze it gently and let the water drip into a bowl. Then squeeze as hard as you can. Notice that the cloth is still damp. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out slowly, three times. Think of the damp that stays in the cloth. Some water stays held, however hard you squeeze.
- Ask: If a root cannot drink from wet soil because the soil holds on too tightly, was there water there or not?
- Ask: A mangrove and the sea both hold on to water. Is the contest fair? Who is winning, and does anyone lose?
- Ask: A plant thickens her sap when the soil dries. Is she deciding, or is it just happening? How could you ever tell the difference?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A plant standing in a flooded, salty field can die of thirst. Is that soil wet or dry? (hint: Think about the words 'wet' and 'available to drink'. Are they the same?)
- *deep:* Water moves to where it is held more tightly. Who is doing the pulling, the water or the thing that holds it? (hint: Think about a wet sponge and a wet cloth. Which gives up its water more easily?)
- *deep:* Dry air pulls water out of leaves far harder than the tallest tree needs. So is a tree lifting her water, or is the air lifting it? (hint: Imagine a tree on a day with no wind and fog. What happens to the water in her tubes?)
- *big:* A person who drinks sea water gets thirstier, but a mangrove grows in it. Is sea water good or bad for life? (hint: Think about who is drinking. Does 'good' belong to the water, or to the one who meets it?)
- *big:* In dry places, farmers water their fields for years and the soil slowly turns white with salt. Where does the salt come from, and who is to blame? (hint: Think about what stays behind when water evaporates.)
- *big:* Rain that fell on the Western Ghats may pass through many plants before it reaches the sea. Whose water is it? (hint: Follow one drop from a cloud, to soil, to a root, to a leaf, and back to the air.)
- *deep:* Nobody can see the pull inside a tree. How could you measure it, and how would you know your measurement was right? (hint: Think about how you measure a pull on a spring.)
- *deep:* When soil dries, a plant makes her root sap thicker so she can keep drinking. Is that a decision? How would you find out? (hint: Think about what you do when you feel thirsty, and what a root does.)

## Story: Water Everywhere, and Not a Drop
*A made-up story. Moushumi, her family and the village are imagined. The science in it is real: sea water floods do leave rice seedlings wilting, fresh rain does wash salt down and out, and Nona is a real traditional salt-tolerant rice of the Bengal coast.*

On Gosaba island in the Sundarbans, the storm came in the night. Moushumi lay on the floor of the school building with fifty other people and listened to the roof shake. In the morning the wind had gone. But the sea had come over the mud wall that kept the fields dry, and it had left itself behind. Brown water lay across the whole island, up to a grown-up's knees.

It took two days to drain away. On the third day, Moushumi walked to the nursery bed behind her house. This was where her family kept rice seedlings, ready to plant out when the rains came. When she left, they had been bright green. Now every seedling was bent over, with yellow tips, like seedlings in the worst heat of April. The mud around them was wet. She pushed a finger in, and water came up around it.

Her uncle, Bhaskar Kaka, was already there with a bucket. 'They are thirsty,' he said. He dipped the bucket in the pond at the edge of the field and poured it over the bed. The next morning, the seedlings were worse. He poured again.

Moushumi did not understand. The bed was soaked, and her uncle was giving it more water. How could a plant standing in wet mud be thirsty? 'Maybe they drowned,' said her cousin Rupa. But the bed had drained, and drowned plants do not look like plants in a drought.

That evening, her grandmother, Thakuma, sat on the step with a heap of bitter gourd. She cut it into thin rounds, threw handfuls of salt over it and left it in a steel plate. 'Why salt?' Moushumi asked. 'To bring out the bitter water,' said Thakuma. 'My mother did it, and hers.' When Moushumi came back after a while, a green puddle had gathered under the rounds, and the slices had gone soft.

She looked at the puddle for a long time. In class, Ranu Didi had done the same with cucumber. Salt does not make water, Didi had said. Salt holds water, so tightly that the cucumber lets go. Moushumi licked her finger, dipped it in the pond water, and licked it again. Salt. The sea had filled that pond.

She thought of the roots in the bed, sitting in mud soaked with the sea. If salt could pull water out of a gourd, it could pull water out of a root. The mud was wet, and still it held its water too tightly to give any away. The seedlings stood in water and could not drink a drop. And every bucket from the pond made the grip tighter.

She wanted to be sure before she spoke, because who listens to a girl of ten? So she tested it. She dug up six seedlings and washed their roots. She stood three in a cup of pond water and three in a cup of rain water from the drum on the roof. Rupa kept watch. By dusk, the three in the pond water were lying over the rim. The three in the rain water still stood straight.

She carried both cups into the courtyard. Her uncle looked at the cups. He looked at the brown pond. Then he sat down on his heels. 'I have been giving them the sea,' he said quietly.

There was a long argument that night. Where would fresh water come from, when the pond was salty and the tube-well was far away? Thakuma settled it. 'The rain is coming,' she said. 'Rain is the one water with no salt in it. Let it fall on the bed and run off, and it will carry the salt away.' The women of the lane dug a cut in the bank so the water could drain out, and then they waited.

The rain came three days later and fell all night. In the morning, the bed was running with muddy water through the cut, and when Moushumi touched the mud to her tongue, it tasted of nothing. A week later, new leaves pushed up. Many of the old seedlings never recovered. So Thakuma took down a rusty tin from the shelf. Inside were seeds of nona, the old salty-field rice her own mother had grown. 'This kind has met the sea before,' she said. They sowed it in the bed.

On the way to school, Moushumi crossed the creek. The mangroves along its bank stood in the dark water, as green as ever. The storm had passed right over them. The sea had stood around their roots for two days, and not one leaf drooped. She stopped on the bridge. Whatever those trees knew, they were drinking from the very water that had hurt her seedlings.

'How?' she said aloud. A fish jumped, and the ripples spread out, and the tide began to turn.

**For the parent:** A root drinks only when her cells hold water more tightly than the soil does. Salty mud holds water so tightly that seedlings in a wet bed were still thirsty, and pouring on salty pond water made the grip even tighter. Fresh rain washed the salt away and loosened the grip.

- Ask: Why did the seedlings droop even though the mud was wet?
- Ask: Why did pouring on more pond water make them worse?
- Ask: How could Moushumi's test with the two cups be made fairer, so that nobody could argue with it?

## Experiments
### e1 · Salt and the Sprouts: how salty is too salty for a seed? (55 min, 4 days)
- **question:** How much salt can there be in the water before moong seeds stop sprouting?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which cups will have the most sprouts? Which will have none? Say why you think so, and how you will decide when a seed has sprouted.
- **fair test:** Every cup gets 10 moong seeds from the same packet, soaked for the same time, on the same kind of paper towel. Every cup gets the same amount of water each day, and sits in the same shady place. Only the salt in the water is different.
- **safety:** Salt water is safe to touch but it is not for drinking. Wash hands after handling seeds. Keep small seeds away from children under five.
- **record:** Fill in the table at each check. On day 4, draw the four cups side by side and write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Cup | Salt in 200 millilitres of water | Sprouted after 1 day (out of 10) | Sprouted after 2 days | Sprouted after 3 days | Longest root after 3 days (millimetres)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Make four kinds of salt water. Set up the four cups of seeds. (25 min); day 2: Count the sprouts. Give each cup 2 tablespoons of its own water. (7 min); day 3: Count again. Give each cup 2 tablespoons of its own water. (7 min); day 4: Count, measure the longest root, and say what the results show. (14 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the four tumblers, the salt and the soaked moong. Say that each cup will get a different water. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We have four cups. Cup A gets plain water. B, C and D get a little, more, and most salt. Which cups will grow sprouts? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four cups, four kinds of water. Which will grow sprouts? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Which cup do you think will have the fewest sprouts? (good: The saltiest one.; D.)
  - Ask: How will we know a seed has sprouted? (good: A small white root pokes out.; The seed splits and a tail comes out.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write the guesses at the top of the page, so you can look back at them on day 4. A seed counts as sprouted when a white root is at least 2 millimetres long.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Label four tumblers A, B, C and D. Pour 200 millilitres of plain water into each. Leave A plain. Add a level quarter teaspoon of salt to B, a level half teaspoon to C and a level teaspoon to D. Stir each one until the salt is gone.  
  *Parent says:* Label the tumblers A, B, C and D. Each gets the same plain water. Now add salt. A gets none. B gets a quarter spoon, C gets a half, and D gets a full teaspoon. Stir until you cannot see the salt.  
  *Child mode:* Make four waters. A has no salt. B, C and D have more and more. Stir them.
  - Note: Level spoons, not heaped ones. Cup D is about as salty as sea water. Keep the tumblers: you will use the same water again on days 2 and 3. Cover each one with a saucer so the water does not evaporate.
  - Check: Why does cup A get plain water with no salt? → **So we have something to compare the salty cups with**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fold a paper towel in four and lay it in a katori. Pour 2 tablespoons of water A onto the towel. Spread 10 soaked moong on top, not touching. Do the same for B, C and D, using their own water. Cover each katori with a plate.  
  *Parent says:* Each katori gets a wet towel and 10 seeds. Use water A for the first one, B for the second, and so on. Do not mix them up. Then cover each one with a plate.  
  *Child mode:* Wet a towel in each katori. Spread 10 seeds on it. Use the right water for each.
  - Note: Pick out 40 good-looking seeds first and leave out any that are split or shrivelled. Count them out in tens. The towel should be wet but not floating. If you see water pooling in the katori, tip a little off.
  - Check: What is the ONE thing that is different between the four cups? → **The salt in the water**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the four katoris side by side in a shady, warm place, out of direct sun. Draw the results table in your notebook. Draw what you think each cup will look like in 3 days.  
  *Parent says:* Put the cups somewhere shady and warm. Now draw what you think each cup will look like in three days. We will check them tomorrow.  
  *Child mode:* Put the cups in a shady spot. Draw what you think you will see in 3 days.
  - Note: Warm and shady is best for moong, for example a kitchen shelf. Do not put the cups in a fridge or in direct sun.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at each cup. Count the seeds with a white root of at least 2 millimetres. Write the numbers in the table. Pour 2 tablespoons of each cup's own water onto its towel. Cover the cups again.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the cups one by one. How many seeds have a white root? Write the number in the table. Then give each cup 2 spoons of its own water.  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouts in each cup. Write the numbers. Give each cup its own water.
  - Ask: Which cup has the most sprouts so far? (good: A.; The plain one.)
  - Ask: Is the order what you guessed? (good: Yes.; No, a surprise.)
  - Note: Water with the right tumbler: A's water on A's cup. If a seed has a mouldy smell or look, take that seed out and note it. Do not make the numbers look better than they are.
  - Wait: Leave the cups for one day, about 24 hours.
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count again. Write the numbers in the table. Give each cup 2 tablespoons of its own water. Cover them again.  
  *Parent says:* Count the sprouts again, in the same way as yesterday. What has changed since the first count? Then give each cup its own water.  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouts again. Write the numbers. Give each cup its own water.
  - Ask: Which cup changed the most since yesterday? (good: A.; B.; The one with a little salt.)
  - Ask: Is any cup still without sprouts? (good: Yes, D.; C and D.)
  - Think: Does a salty cup stop the seeds from sprouting, or does it just make them sprout later?
  - Wait: Leave the cups for one more day.
- **s7 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count a third time. Measure the longest root in each cup with a ruler in millimetres. Write the numbers. Look at the roots with a magnifying glass if you have one. Draw what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Count the sprouts one last time. Then measure the longest root in each cup. Look for the fuzz of root hairs. Write everything in the table.  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouts. Measure the longest root. Look for the fuzzy root hairs.
  - Ask: What do the roots in the saltier cups look like compared with cup A? (good: Shorter.; Fewer.; Thinner and browner.)
  - Note: If cup A also has very few sprouts, the seeds are probably old. Repeat the experiment with a fresh packet. Results will not match every family's exactly. A pattern of fewer and shorter roots as salt rises is the thing to look for.
  - Wait: Leave the cups for one more day.
- **s8 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why. Then compare with the guess you made on day 1.  
  *Parent says:* Now say three things. One, what I think. Two, what I saw, with numbers. Three, why I think it happened. Then look at your first guess. Were you right?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why. Check your first guess.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like: 'I think seeds stop sprouting in a lot of salt. In D, none sprouted, and in A, nine did. The salt must make it hard for the seed to drink.' Do not give the idea before your child has tried to say it. If they say salt is a poison, you can say that is partly true, and that the first harm is thirst. The poison comes later.
  - Check: In cup D, the seeds stayed in water but did not sprout. What is the best reason? → **The salt water held its water tighter than the seeds did, so they could not drink**
### e2 · Fan, Still Air or Bag: which sprig loses the most water? (40 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Does a leaf lose water faster in moving dry air, in still air, or inside a bag of damp air?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which bottle will lose the most water, and which the least? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Use three identical bottles, three sprigs of the same kind and size, the same amount of water and oil, and the same light. Only the air around the leaves is different: moving under a fan, still, or inside a bag.
- **safety:** Keep fingers out of the fan. Put the bottle with the fan well away from the edge of a table.
- **record:** Fill in the table at each check. Draw the leaves and the bag at the 3-hour check. Look for drops inside the bag.
- **results table:** Bottle | Air around the leaves | Water level at the start (mm) | Water level after 3 hours (mm) | Water level next morning (mm) | Water lost (mm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Fill the bottles. Add the sprigs. Bag one. Place them. Check after 3 hours. (30 min); day 2: Measure the next morning and say what the results show. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three bottles and the three sprigs. Explain that one will stand under a fan, one in still air, and one inside a bag. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Three sprigs, three bottles. A is under the fan. B is in still air. C has a bag over the leaves. Which sprig will lose the most water? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three sprigs. One under a fan, one in still air, one in a bag. Which loses most water?
  - Ask: Which bottle do you think will lose the most water? (good: The fan one.; A.)
  - Ask: What do you think will happen inside the bag? (good: It will get wet.; Drops will form.; It will get foggy.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write the guesses down. The idea is that moving, dry air pulls water out of leaves faster, but let your child get there by seeing.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the three bottles with water right up into the neck. Add half a teaspoon of oil to each, so it floats on top of the water. Stick a strip of tape on each neck. Mark the top of the oil on the tape with the marker. Write A, B and C on the bottles.  
  *Parent says:* Fill each bottle up into its neck. Float a little oil on top. The oil stops water from drifting into the air. Mark the oil level on a strip of tape. Write A, B and C.  
  *Child mode:* Fill three bottles into the neck. Add a little oil on top. Mark the level with tape.
  - Note: Narrow necks make a small loss easy to see. If the oil level is hard to see, put a drop of food colour in the water. The three bottles should be the same shape.
  - Check: Why do we float oil on top of the water? → **So water can only leave through the leaves, not from the surface**
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Cut the end of each sprig fresh, under a little water in a bowl. Push one sprig into each bottle neck, so the cut end is in the water and the leaves are outside. Tie a bag loosely around the leaves of bottle C and the bottle neck with a rubber band.  
  *Parent says:* Cut each stem under water, so no air gets into the tubes. Put a sprig in each bottle. Now put a bag over the leaves of bottle C. Tie it round the neck.  
  *Child mode:* Put a sprig in each bottle. Tie a bag over the leaves of bottle C.
  - Note: A grown-up cuts the stems. Make sure the cut ends stay in the water. A bag that is tied tight round the neck will keep the air inside damp. Leave the neck of the bag a little loose, so the sprig is not crushed.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put bottle A on a shelf under a ceiling fan, or in front of a table fan on low, 1 metre away. Put bottles B and C in the same room, in still air, behind a standing book. Set the fan going. Write the time.  
  *Parent says:* A goes where the breeze reaches it. B and C go behind a book, out of the breeze. Switch the fan on and write down the time.  
  *Child mode:* Put A in the breeze. Put B and C in still air. Write the time.
  - Note: Keep all three in the same light, at the same height. A table fan on low is enough. Leave them for at least 3 hours.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 3 hours, look at the bag on bottle C. Are there drops inside? Measure how far the oil has dropped on each bottle with the ruler. Write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* It has been three hours. Look inside bag C. What do you see? Then measure how far each oil level has dropped.  
  *Child mode:* Look inside the bag. Measure how far each level has dropped.
  - Ask: What can you see inside the bag? (good: Tiny drops.; It is misty.)
  - Ask: Where do you think those drops came from? (good: From the leaves.; From the sprig's water.)
  - Note: The drop may be small, only 1 or 2 millimetres, in 3 hours. That is normal. If you have a kitchen scale, you can weigh each bottle at the start and now. One gram lost is one millilitre of water.
  - Wait: Wait 3 hours.
- **s6 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The next morning, measure the oil level in each bottle again. Fill in the table. Work out how much water each bottle lost. Look at the leaves. Which ones look tired?  
  *Parent says:* Now it is morning. Measure again. How many millimetres did each bottle lose? Which bottle lost the most? Which the least?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the levels again. Which lost the most? Which lost the least?
  - Ask: In which bottle did the water go down the most? (good: A.; The fan one.)
  - Ask: Did the bagged sprig drink anything? (good: Very little.; Almost nothing.)
  - Note: Usually the fan bottle loses most and the bagged one loses least, but real results can be messy. A sprig that wilts early will drink less. If the results are all small, repeat with more leaves.
  - Wait: Leave the bottles overnight.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why.  
  *Parent says:* Say three things. What do you think? What did you see, with numbers? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like: 'I think moving air makes leaves lose more water. The fan bottle went down 4 millimetres and the bag bottle went down 0. The bag air got damp, so it could not pull.' Look back at the first guess together.
  - Check: The bagged sprig lost almost no water. What is the best reason? → **The air inside the bag became damp, so it pulled very little water out of the leaves**

### a2 · Leave the Salt Behind: a paper strip that grows a crust (25 min, 2 days)

### Wet but thirsty (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A rice seedling stands in a field that was flooded with sea water. The mud is wet, but she droops. What is the best reason?  
   Options: The salty water holds its water tighter than her roots can pull, so water leaves her roots / The roots have too little air and the water has nothing to do with it / Rice only drinks through the leaves  
   **Answer:** The salty water holds its water tighter than her roots can pull, so water leaves her roots. *Why:* Salty water has a bigger pull number than a root cell, so water flows out of the root. If you chose the second answer, you are thinking of drowning, which does happen in floods. But sea water kills seedlings faster than fresh water does, so the salt matters.
2. (fill) Water always moves toward the place with the ____ pull number.  
   **Answer:** ['bigger', 'larger', 'higher', 'greater']. *Why:* A bigger pull number means water is held more tightly there. Water moves toward the tighter hold.
3. (truefalse) Salt sprinkled on a cucumber slice pushes water into the slice.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The salt holds water tighter than the cucumber cells do, so water leaves the slice and makes a puddle.
4. (match) Match each place to its pull number.  
   **Answer:** Plain tap water → about 0; Wet soil → about 0.1; A leaf cell → about 1 to 1.5; Sea water → about 2.5; Dry air → about 94. *Why:* The tighter the hold, the bigger the number. Tap water holds its water very loosely. Dry air pulls water away more than anything else on the list.
5. (sort) Sort each situation by what happens to the water in the root.  
   **Answer:** soil that has just had fresh water → Water goes into the root; soil flooded with sea water → Water leaves the root; soil after a good flush of fresh rain → Water goes into the root; a pot with far too much fertiliser → Water leaves the root; soil dried to a pull number of 1.5 around a bean root at 1.2 → Water leaves the root; damp garden soil after a short shower → Water goes into the root. *Why:* Water goes into the root when the root holds water tighter than the soil does. It leaves when the soil holds water tighter. Salt, fertiliser and very dry soil all make the soil's hold tighter.

   Options: Used up to build the plant's body / Let out into the air through the leaves as vapour / Kept in the stem  
   **Answer:** Let out into the air through the leaves as vapour. *Why:* Only 1 or 2 drops in every 100 are used to build the plant. Nearly all the rest travels through and leaves as vapour through the stomata.

   **Answer:** ['Wet soil', 'A root hair', 'A root cell', 'The xylem in the stem', 'A leaf cell', 'The air outside']. *Why:* The pull number rises at each step, so water flows from the soil, through the root, up the stem, into the leaf, and out into the air.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Water flows into the guard cells, they swell, and the swelling bends them open. When they lose water, they go flat and the hole shuts.

If the seeds droop in the same way, thirst is the first harm.. *Why:* Real scientists support the thirst-first idea with exactly this kind of test, and also accept that salt does harm the plant later. Both views have a part of the truth.
13. (truefalse) Water in the xylem of a tall tree is pushed up by a pump in the roots.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Water is pulled up, mostly by dry air pulling on the leaves. The xylem has no pump. Dead xylem tubes still carry water.
14. (mcq) A student sprouts seeds in strong salt water, and none sprout. She says, 'Salt kills seeds.' Which extra test would show whether the seeds in the salt water were still alive?  
   Options: Rinse them in plain water and see if they sprout / Boil them and taste them / Dry them in the sun  
   **Answer:** Rinse them in plain water and see if they sprout. *Why:* If the seeds sprout after a rinse, the salt only stopped them from drinking. They were not dead. This is how scientists tell thirst from poison.
15. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? A mangrove, a coconut palm, Pokkali rice, ordinary wheat.  
   **Answer:** Ordinary wheat, because she cannot grow in salty soil. The other three can.. *Why:* Mangroves, coconut palms and Pokkali rice all have ways of holding water tighter than salty water does. Ordinary wheat does not.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which place holds its water the tightest?  
   Options: Plain tap water in a glass / Damp garden soil / Sea water  
   **Answer:** Sea water. *Why:* Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in each litre. Dissolved salt holds water tightly, so sea water has the biggest pull number of the three.
2. (mcq) Water moves through the skin of a plant cell toward the side that...  
   Options: holds water more tightly / holds water more loosely / is warmer  
   **Answer:** holds water more tightly. *Why:* This movement is called osmosis. The side with the bigger pull number wins the water. Warmth is not the reason.
3. (mcq) Salt is sprinkled on slices of raw mango and a puddle forms. Where did the puddle come from?  
   Options: From inside the mango cells / Out of the salt / Out of the air  
   **Answer:** From inside the mango cells. *Why:* Salt has no water to give. The mango cells are mostly water. The salt held water tighter than they did, so water left them.
4. (mcq) What does a root hair do for a plant?  
   Options: It gives a huge surface for taking in water and slips between soil grains / It carries sugar to the roots / It lets water vapour out  
   **Answer:** It gives a huge surface for taking in water and slips between soil grains. *Why:* Root hairs are thin tubes that grow out of root cells. They are so thin and so many that they make the root's surface very large.
5. (mcq) Why must a root cell hold water tighter than the soil does?  
   Options: Because water only moves toward the tighter hold / Because roots like to be thirsty / Because the soil is always dry  
   **Answer:** Because water only moves toward the tighter hold. *Why:* If the soil held its water tighter, water would flow out of the root. So a root must keep her sap thick enough to win.
6. (mcq) Along a plant's path from soil to sky, where does the biggest drop in pull number happen?  
   Options: Between the leaf and the dry air / Between the soil and the root / Between the root and the stem  
   **Answer:** Between the leaf and the dry air. *Why:* The leaf is about 1.5 and dry air is about 94. The drop inside the plant is tiny compared with this.
7. (mcq) What happens to a stoma when its guard cells lose water?  
   Options: It shuts / It opens wider / It turns into a root hair  
   **Answer:** It shuts. *Why:* Guard cells hold the hole open only when they are plump with water. When they lose it they go flat and the hole shuts.
8. (truefalse) A rice seedling in a field flooded with sea water has plenty of water to drink.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The water is there, but it is held tightly by the salt. The seedling cannot pull it into her roots.
9. (mcq) A ring of bark is cut off a branch. The leaves above the ring stay green for weeks. What does this show?  
   Options: Water goes up in the wood, which the ring did not cut / Leaves do not need water / Sugar goes up the wood  
   **Answer:** Water goes up in the wood, which the ring did not cut. *Why:* The xylem is inside the wood, so it kept bringing water up. The ring cut the phloem in the bark, which carries sugar down.
10. (mcq) Which of these helps a mangrove live in sea water?  
   Options: Roots that keep most of the salt out / Leaves that stay white / Roots that never touch water  
   **Answer:** Roots that keep most of the salt out. *Why:* Different mangroves use different methods. Some keep salt out at the root, some push it out through leaf glands, and some store it in old leaves.
11. (mcq) Why do wet clothes dry slowly on a damp monsoon day?  
   Options: Damp air has a small pull number, so it pulls less water from the cloth / Rain adds more water to the clothes / Clothes dry only at night  
   **Answer:** Damp air has a small pull number, so it pulls less water from the cloth. *Why:* Water leaves cloth in the direction of the bigger number. Damp air has a smaller number than dry air, so the pull is weaker and drying is slow.
12. (mcq) A student tests how salt affects sprouting with four cups of seeds. What must stay the same in all four cups?  
   Options: The seeds, the towel, the amount of water and the place / The amount of salt / The number of sprouts  
   **Answer:** The seeds, the towel, the amount of water and the place. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. Here that is the salt. Everything else must be the same, so that salt is the only possible cause of any difference.

## Applications
- Choosing salt-tolerant crops, such as Pokkali rice in Kerala, for coastal fields that sea water reaches.
- Washing salt out of farmland with fresh water, and building drains so it can leave.
- Planting mangrove belts along coasts to slow cyclone waves and shelter villages, as in the Sundarbans.
- Preserving food in salt or sugar, such as achaar, dried fish and murabba, by drawing water out of food and out of the microbes in it.
- Giving the right amount of fertiliser, because too much works like salt and dries the roots.
- Drip irrigation, which gives a plant water slowly and keeps salt from building up in one place.
- Oral rehydration solution (ORS), which uses salt and sugar so that the gut takes in water.
- Growing plants in tubs of water with carefully measured dissolved minerals, called hydroponics.

## Open questions
- **How does a plant sense that the soil is starting to dry, before her leaves have started to wilt?** Scientists have found chemical signals from roots to leaves, but exactly how roots detect dryness is still being worked out.
- **How exactly do Rhizophora mangrove roots keep out the salt while letting water in?** Scientists know the roots block most of the salt. They are still studying which proteins and root layers do the job.
- **How does a leaf weigh letting carbon dioxide in against keeping water, second by second?** The guard cell parts are known. How a leaf balances all the signals together is not fully understood.
- **Is the pressure-flow idea enough to explain how sugar moves along the phloem of a 100-metre tree?** Tests support the idea in short plants. Tall trees are hard to test, and some scientists think extra helpers may be needed.
- **Can we breed a salt-tolerant rice that gives as much grain as ordinary rice?** Salt-tolerant types such as Pokkali give less grain. Plant breeders are trying to move the tolerance into high-yielding types.
- **How much water do the fungus partners of a plant really bring to her?** In some soils the fungus partner seems to bring a lot. In others, scientists find little. The answer depends on the plant, the fungus and the soil.

## Connected maths
- In a sprouting test, 9 of 10 seeds sprouted in cup A, 7 in cup B, 3 in cup C and 0 in cup D. What fraction of all 40 seeds sprouted? **19/40**. 9 plus 7 plus 3 plus 0 is 19. 19 seeds sprouted out of 40, so the fraction is 19 over 40. It cannot be made simpler. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- On the string staircase, a pull number of 0.1 is 1 millimetre. A leaf cell is 1.5 and dry air is 94. How many millimetres is the dry-air strip, and about how many times longer is it than the leaf strip? **940 millimetres, about 63 times longer**. Multiply each pull number by 10. The leaf is 15 millimetres. Dry air is 940 millimetres. 940 divided by 15 is about 63. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L4`)
- Cup D had 6 grams of salt in 200 millilitres of water. Sea water has about 35 grams in a litre. Is cup D saltier or less salty than sea water, and by how much per litre? **Less salty, by 5 grams per litre**. A litre is 5 times 200 millilitres. 5 times 6 grams is 30 grams per litre. 35 take away 30 is 5. So cup D is a little less salty than sea water. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L4`)
- It takes about 2,500 litres of water to grow 1 kilogram of rice, and most of it passes through the plants. How much for a 100 gram serving? How many 10 litre buckets is that? **250 litres, which is 25 buckets**. 100 grams is one tenth of a kilogram. A tenth of 2,500 is 250. 250 divided by 10 is 25 buckets. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- After 3 days, the number of sprouts in cups with 0, a quarter, a half and 1 teaspoon of salt was 9, 7, 3 and 0. Between which two neighbouring cups did the number fall the most? What pattern do you see? **Between the quarter-spoon cup and the half-spoon cup, a fall of 4. The more salt, the fewer the sprouts.**. The falls are 9 to 7, which is 2, then 7 to 3, which is 4, then 3 to 0, which is 3. The biggest fall is 4. Adding salt always lowers the sprouts, and the middle step hurt the most. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- A sprig under a fan lost 6 millimetres of water, and a sprig in still air lost 4 millimetres. The sprig in a bag lost 0 millimetres. How many times as much did the fan sprig lose as the still one? Can you say how many times as much it lost as the bagged one? Why or why not? **1.5 times as much. No, you cannot divide by 0.**. 6 divided by 4 is 1.5. The bagged sprig lost nothing, and dividing by 0 does not give a number. We can only say the fan sprig lost 6 millimetres more. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A leaf loses 2 millilitres of water an hour for the 12 hours of daylight, and 0.2 millilitres an hour for the 12 hours of night. How much does it lose in a whole day? What fraction of that is lost at night? **26.4 millilitres. At night it is 1/11.**. Day: 2 times 12 is 24. Night: 0.2 times 12 is 2.4. Together that is 26.4. Night's share is 2.4 out of 26.4, which is 1 out of 11. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Lifting water costs 1 millimetre of strip for every 10 metres of tree height. A tree is 35 metres tall. How long is the strip for lifting? A student says, 'So dry air hardly matters.' Is the student right? **3.5 millimetres. No, the student is wrong.**. 35 metres is 3.5 groups of 10 metres, so 3.5 millimetres. The dry-air strip is 940 millimetres. Dry air matters a great deal, because it does nearly all the pulling. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
