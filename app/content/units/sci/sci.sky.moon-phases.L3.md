# Why the Moon seems to change shape, and how eclipses happen
`sci.sky.moon-phases.L3`

**Summary.** The Moon makes no light of its own, but the Sun always lights half of it. As the Moon goes round the Earth each month, we see more or less of that lit half, so its shape seems to change. The Moon spins once for each trip round us, so we always see the same face. When the Sun, the Earth and the Moon line up exactly, a shadow falls and we get an eclipse. Indian calendars count the Moon's days, called tithis, and many festivals still follow them.

**Intro.** *The Moon is a round ball of rock. So why does it look like a thin curve on some nights, and a full circle on others?* People everywhere have watched the Moon grow from a thin curve to a full circle and back again, about once a month. Farmers and fishing families planned their work by it, and Indian calendars still count its days. The word 'month' even comes from the word 'Moon'. But the Moon makes no light, and it never changes its size. So what changes? In this unit you will make every Moon shape yourself, with a ball and a lamp in a dark room, and find out.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can show with a ball and a lamp why the Moon seems to change shape, and name its phases in order.
1. I can keep a Moon diary: I draw the Moon's shape, the date, the time and where it is, and I spot the pattern.
2. I can explain why we always see the same face of the Moon: it spins once for each trip round the Earth, because the Earth's pull slowed it down.
3. I can explain solar and lunar eclipses using shadows, and say why they do not happen every month.
4. I can say how Indian calendars count the Moon's days, the tithis and the pakshas, and name festivals set by the Moon.
5. I can tell the main idea of how the Moon was made, and what scientists still argue about.
6. I can say what Aryabhata, Wang Zhenyi and others found about the Moon, and give examples of living things that keep time by it.

## Terms
- **Phase of the Moon**: The shape of the lit part of the Moon that we see on a night. The Moon goes through all its phases once a month. *e.g.* A thin crescent, a half Moon and a full Moon are three phases.
- **New Moon (Amavasya)**: The night when we cannot see the Moon. Its lit half faces away from us, towards the Sun. *e.g.* Diwali falls on an Amavasya, so the sky is dark and the lamps shine bright.
- **Full Moon (Purnima)**: The night when we see the whole lit half of the Moon, a full round circle. *e.g.* Guru Purnima and Buddha Purnima fall on full Moon nights.
- **Crescent**: A thin curved Moon, shaped like a fingernail clipping. *e.g.* Two or three days after Amavasya, a thin crescent Moon shows low in the west just after sunset.
- **Half Moon (quarter Moon)**: A Moon that looks like half a circle. It is called a quarter Moon because it comes a quarter of the way through the month. *e.g.* About a week after Amavasya you see a half Moon high in the sky at sunset.
- **Gibbous Moon**: A Moon that is more than half lit but not yet full. It looks like a slightly squashed circle. *e.g.* Two or three nights before Purnima, the Moon is gibbous.
- **Waxing**: Growing. Each night, we see a little more of the Moon's lit half. *e.g.* From Amavasya to Purnima the Moon is waxing. This is the bright half of the month, Shukla Paksha.
- **Waning**: Shrinking. Each night, we see a little less of the Moon's lit half. *e.g.* From Purnima to Amavasya the Moon is waning. This is the dark half of the month, Krishna Paksha.
- **Lunar month**: The time from one new Moon to the next. It is about 29 and a half days. *e.g.* From one Diwali Amavasya to the next Amavasya is one lunar month.
- **Tithi**: A Moon day in the Indian calendar. There are 30 tithis in each lunar month. A tithi is usually close to one day long, but it can be a few hours shorter or longer. *e.g.* Vinayaka Chaturthi (Vinayaka Chaturthi) falls on the fourth tithi, chaturthi, of the bright half of the month.
- **Paksha**: Half of a lunar month, about 15 tithis. Shukla Paksha is the bright half, when the Moon grows. Krishna Paksha is the dark half, when it shrinks. *e.g.* Navratri begins in Shukla Paksha, as the Moon starts to grow.
- **Orbit**: The path one thing takes as it goes round another. *e.g.* The Moon's orbit takes it all the way round the Earth.
- **Tidal locking**: When a moon spins exactly once for each trip round its planet, so it always shows the planet the same face. The planet's pull slowed its spin until it fell into step. *e.g.* Our Moon is tidally locked to the Earth. So are the four big moons of Jupiter.
- **Far side of the Moon**: The half of the Moon that always faces away from the Earth. We never see it from here. It gets just as much sunlight as the side we see. *e.g.* A Chinese spacecraft, Chang'e 4, was the first to land on the far side, in 2019.
- **Earthshine**: Sunlight that bounces off the Earth, lands on the dark part of the Moon, and bounces back to us. It makes the dark part of a crescent Moon glow faintly. *e.g.* Look at a thin crescent Moon just after sunset. You can often see the whole round Moon, faintly grey.
- **Eclipse**: When one thing in space moves into another's shadow, or blocks its light from us. *e.g.* In a lunar eclipse, the Moon moves into the Earth's shadow.
- **Solar eclipse**: When the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth, and blocks the Sun. It can only happen at new Moon. *e.g.* On 22 July 2009, just after sunrise, the Sun went completely dark for a few minutes over Varanasi and Patna.
- **Lunar eclipse**: When the Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, and the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. It can only happen at full Moon. *e.g.* In a total lunar eclipse the Moon often turns a coppery red.
- **Shadow**: A dark area where light cannot reach, because something is in the way. *e.g.* Your own shadow on the ground at noon. The Earth's shadow stretches far out into space.
- **Rahu and Ketu**: In Indian astronomy, the names of the two points where the Moon's path crosses the Sun's path in the sky. Eclipses can only happen when the Moon is near one of them. *e.g.* Old stories said Rahu swallowed the Sun. Astronomers like Aryabhata kept the names, but used them for these two crossing points.
- **Giant impact**: The main idea of how the Moon was made. A planet about the size of Mars crashed into the young Earth, and the Moon formed from the hot rock thrown out. *e.g.* Scientists sometimes call the crashing planet Theia.
- **Adhik maas**: An extra month that Indian calendars add about once every three years. It keeps the Moon months in step with the seasons. *e.g.* Without adhik maas, Diwali would slowly drift from autumn into summer.
- **Moon illusion**: The rising Moon looks much bigger near the horizon than when it is high in the sky. It is really the same size. The difference is made by our brains. *e.g.* A full Moon rising behind a water tank looks huge. Three hours later, high up, it looks small.

## Explanations
### Half of the Moon is always lit · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon makes no light of its own. The Sun always lights exactly half of it, the half facing the Sun. The other half is in the Moon's own shadow.

You may already know that moonlight is sunlight bouncing off the Moon. Here is what follows from that. At every moment, one half of the Moon is in daylight and one half is in night, just like the Earth. The Moon never grows or shrinks. What changes is how much of its daylight half is turned towards us. When the lit half faces us, we see a full Moon. When it faces away, we see nothing. In between, we see a slice of it.
- e.g. Hold an orange near a lamp in a dark room. One half is always bright. Walk round it, and the bright part you see changes shape.
- e.g. The Earth has phases too, seen from the Moon. Astronauts going round the Moon photographed a half-lit Earth rising over its edge.
### The phases in order, and why the month is 29 and a half days · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon's phases always come in the same order: new, crescent, half, gibbous, full, gibbous, half, crescent, and new again. One full round takes about 29 and a half days.

The Moon goes round the Earth. When it is between us and the Sun, its lit half faces away, and we see a new Moon. A quarter of the way round, we see half of the lit half: a half Moon. Halfway round, on the far side of us from the Sun, we see all of the lit half: a full Moon. Then the shapes shrink again in the opposite order. In India, a growing Moon is lit on its right side, the side towards the setting Sun. A shrinking Moon is lit on its left. Here is a surprise. The Moon goes once round the Earth, measured against the stars, in about 27 and a third days. But a new Moon to the next new Moon takes about 29 and a half. While the Moon goes round, the Earth also moves along its own path round the Sun. So the Moon needs about two more days to catch up and line up with the Sun again.
- e.g. From one Amavasya to the next is about 29 and a half days. So calendars give lunar months 29 or 30 days.
- e.g. The bright curved edge of the Moon always points towards the Sun. In the evening, that is the place where the Sun has just set.
### Each phase rises at its own time · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day, on average. So each phase is up at a different time of day and night.

The Moon moves a little further round the Earth every day. So the spinning Earth needs a little extra time to turn us back to face it. That extra time is about 50 minutes, though it varies. A new Moon rises and sets with the Sun, hidden in its glare. A growing half Moon rises around noon and is high in the sky at sunset. A full Moon rises as the Sun sets and is up all night. A shrinking half Moon rises around midnight and is still up in the morning. So the Moon is not only a night thing. For about half of each month you can see it in the daytime, if you know where to look.
- e.g. On Purnima, look east at sunset. The full Moon rises as the Sun goes down in the west.
- e.g. A few days after Purnima, look west in the morning on the way to school. The Moon is often still there, pale against the blue sky.
### Why we always see the same face: tidal locking · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon spins once on its own axis for every trip it makes round the Earth. So the same side always faces us. This is called tidal locking.

Many people think the Moon does not spin at all. It does, very slowly, once every 27 and a third days. That is exactly the time it takes to go round us once. So it keeps its face towards us, just as you do when you walk round a chair while always facing it. It was not always like this. Long ago, the young Moon spun faster. The Earth's gravity pulled on the Moon's rock and stretched it into a very slight bulge, pointing at the Earth. As the Moon spun, the bulge was carried away, and the Earth's pull kept tugging it back. That tug worked like a brake. Over millions of years it slowed the Moon's spin until the bulge stayed pointing at the Earth. Then the spin and the trip matched, and they have stayed matched ever since. The Moon does the same to the Earth. Its pull on our oceans and rocks is slowly slowing the Earth's spin too. One day, very far in the future, the Earth might always show the Moon one face. That would take so long that the Sun will change first.
- e.g. Walk round a chair while always looking at it. When you get back to the start, you have faced every wall of the room once. You turned round once, just like the Moon.
- e.g. Most big moons in the solar system are tidally locked, like Jupiter's moons Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto.
- e.g. Pluto and its moon Charon are locked to each other. Each always shows the other the same face.
### The far side is not dark, and the Moon has two different faces · *debated*
People sometimes call the far side of the Moon 'the dark side'. That is wrong. It gets just as much sunlight as the side we see. On the night of Amavasya, when we see no Moon, the far side is in full daylight. Nobody saw it until 1959, when a Soviet spacecraft, Luna 3, flew round and photographed it. Scientists got a surprise. Our side has big dark patches, called seas. They are not water. They are huge plains of old, dark lava that flooded out long ago. The far side has almost none. Its crust is also thicker. In 2019, China's Chang'e 4 became the first spacecraft to land on the far side. In 2024, Chang'e 6 brought rock from the far side back to Earth for the first time. Why the two faces are so different is still argued about.
- e.g. The dark patches on our side make the 'face' or the 'hare' that people see in the Moon.
- e.g. A rover on the far side cannot talk to Earth directly, because the whole Moon is in the way. China put a relay satellite, Queqiao, beyond the Moon to pass messages along.
- **Many scientists:** The side facing the young Earth stayed hotter, because the newborn Earth was glowing hot nearby. So the crust on that side stayed thin, and lava could break through it easily. On the cooler far side, the crust grew thicker.
- **Other scientists:** A second, smaller moon may once have crashed gently into the far side, piling up the thick crust there. Others think a giant impact on the far side shook the whole Moon. Nobody knows yet which idea is right.
### Earthshine: the Earth lights up the Moon's night · *defined*
**Definition:** Earthshine is sunlight that bounces off the Earth onto the dark part of the Moon, and back to our eyes. It makes the dark part of a thin crescent glow faintly.

Look at a thin crescent Moon two or three evenings after Amavasya. Very often you can see the rest of the Moon's circle too, glowing a soft grey. That faint light has made a long trip. It left the Sun, bounced off the Earth's clouds, seas and land, travelled to the Moon, bounced again and came back to you. Leonardo da Vinci worked this out about 500 years ago and wrote it in his notebooks. When we see a thin crescent Moon, someone standing on the Moon would see a nearly full Earth, shining big and bright. The Earth reflects sunlight much better than the Moon does, because of its white clouds.
- e.g. Earthshine is easiest to see on a crescent in the evening sky, or a crescent in the early morning before Amavasya.
- e.g. Scientists measure earthshine to find out how much sunlight the whole Earth bounces back, which helps them study its clouds.
### How was the Moon made? The detective story · *debated*
Most scientists think the Moon was born in a giant crash, about four and a half billion years ago. A young planet about the size of Mars, which they call Theia, hit the young Earth. Hot rock was flung into space and gathered into the Moon. How do we know anything at all about something nobody saw? Like detectives, scientists use clues. The rocks that astronauts brought back are almost exactly like the rock of the Earth's outer layers. The Moon has only a tiny iron centre, while the Earth has a big one. A crash that splashed out the Earth's outer rock would explain both. Computers let scientists run the crash again and again with different sizes and speeds. In 2001, Robin Canup and Erik Asphaug showed that a glancing blow from a Mars-sized planet could make a Moon like ours. But there is a puzzle. In many of these computer crashes, the Moon ends up made mostly of Theia. Then why are its rocks such a close match to the Earth's?
- e.g. Astronauts brought back about 382 kilograms of Moon rock between 1969 and 1972.
- e.g. Tiny crystals in a Moon rock show the Moon was already solid about 4.46 billion years ago.
- **Most scientists, following William Hartmann and Donald Davis (1975), and Robin Canup (2001):** One giant glancing crash with a Mars-sized planet threw out rock that slowly gathered into the Moon.
- **Jacob Kegerreis and his team (2022):** Their more detailed computer crashes suggest the Moon may have formed in just a few hours, flung out in one piece, not slowly gathered over years.
- **Raluca Rufu, Oded Aharonson and Hagai Perets (2017):** Maybe there was no single giant crash. Many smaller crashes each made a small moon, and the small moons joined into one.
- **Qian Yuan and his team (2023):** Two huge blobs of unusual rock deep inside the Earth, near its core, might be the leftovers of Theia. This is an idea, still being tested.
### Eclipses are shadows in space · *defined*
**Definition:** An eclipse happens when the Sun, the Earth and the Moon line up. In a solar eclipse, the Moon blocks the Sun from part of the Earth. In a lunar eclipse, the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

Everything lit by the Sun throws a shadow behind it, far out into space. Usually the Moon's shadow and the Earth's shadow fall on nothing. A solar eclipse can only happen at new Moon, when the Moon is between us and the Sun. The Moon is small, so its dark shadow only touches a small patch of the Earth. Only people in that patch see the Sun fully covered. A lunar eclipse can only happen at full Moon, when the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon. The Earth's shadow is much bigger than the Moon, so it can swallow the whole Moon. Everyone on the night side of the Earth can see it. During a total lunar eclipse the Moon turns coppery red. A little sunlight bends through the Earth's air and reaches it. The air lets the red light through and scatters the blue away, the same way it makes sunsets red. So the red Moon is lit by all the sunrises and sunsets on Earth at that moment.
- e.g. On the night of 7 September 2025, people all over India watched a total lunar eclipse turn the Moon red.
- e.g. A solar eclipse is the only kind that is dangerous to watch, because people may look at the Sun. Never look at the Sun directly, eclipse or not. A lunar eclipse is safe to watch with bare eyes.
### Why there is not an eclipse every month: Rahu and Ketu · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon's path round the Earth is tilted a little, about 5 degrees, compared with the Earth's path round the Sun. So at most new and full Moons, the shadows miss. Eclipses only happen when a new or full Moon falls close to one of the two crossing points of the paths.

If the paths were flat, there would be a solar eclipse every Amavasya and a lunar eclipse every Purnima. But the Moon usually passes a little above or a little below the line between the Sun and the Earth. Its path crosses the Sun's path in the sky at only two points. Indian astronomers named these two points Rahu and Ketu. Long ago, a story said a demon called Rahu swallowed the Sun or the Moon. The astronomers kept the old names but used them for something they could calculate. An eclipse can only happen when the new or full Moon is near Rahu or Ketu. That happens roughly every six months. So eclipses come in 'seasons', about half a year apart.
- e.g. Every year has at least two solar eclipses somewhere on the Earth. Many of them are only partial, with part of the Sun left showing.
- e.g. About 2,500 years ago, astronomers in Babylon noticed that eclipses repeat in a pattern every 18 years and 11 days. They used it to predict them.
### Aryabhata explains the Moon, 499 CE · *defined*
**Definition:** Aryabhata, an Indian astronomer, wrote in 499 CE that the Moon is lit by the Sun, and that eclipses are caused by shadows, not by a demon.

Aryabhata lived in Kusumapura, near today's Patna. In his book, the Aryabhatiya, in the part called the Golapada, he wrote two things clearly. Half of the Earth, of each planet and of the Moon is dark from its own shadow, and the half facing the Sun is bright. And in a solar eclipse the Moon covers the Sun, while in a lunar eclipse the great shadow of the Earth covers the Moon. He also gave ways to calculate when eclipses would happen. Later Indian astronomers, like Varahamihira and Lalla, agreed that eclipses are shadows. The great mathematician Brahmagupta argued against Aryabhata on several points. Much later, about the year 1030, a Persian scholar called Al-Biruni studied Indian astronomy. He thought Brahmagupta knew the shadow explanation was right, and spoke up for the old Rahu story to keep peace with religious tradition.
- e.g. Anaxagoras in Greece, about 2,450 years ago, also said the Moon shines by the Sun's light and that eclipses are shadows.
- e.g. Aristotle noticed that the Earth's shadow on the Moon during a lunar eclipse always has a curved edge. Only a round ball always throws a round shadow, so the Earth must be round.
### Wang Zhenyi's garden eclipse · *defined*
**Definition:** Wang Zhenyi, a young Chinese astronomer who lived from 1768 to 1797, explained lunar eclipses with a model made from a lamp, a round table and a mirror.

In Wang Zhenyi's time in China, girls were not supposed to study. Her grandfather had a big library, and she read his books on astronomy. She taught herself mathematics. Many people then believed a heavenly dog ate the Moon during an eclipse, and they banged drums and gongs to scare the dog away. Wang Zhenyi wanted to show what really happened. In a garden pavilion, she hung a lamp from the roof as the Sun. A round table was the Earth. A round mirror was the Moon. She moved them until the table's shadow fell on the mirror, just as the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. She wrote about it in an essay explaining the lunar eclipse. She wrote about a dozen books on astronomy and mathematics before she died, aged only 29. She also wrote poems arguing that daughters can be as heroic as sons.
- e.g. You can make her model at home with a lamp, a big ball and a small ball.
- e.g. A crater on the planet Venus is named after Wang Zhenyi.
### What eclipses taught science · *defined*
**Definition:** Eclipses let scientists study things the Sun's glare usually hides. A new element, helium, was found in an eclipse seen from India in 1868. An eclipse in 1919 helped test Einstein's idea that gravity bends light.

On 18 August 1868, a French astronomer, Jules Janssen, watched a total solar eclipse from Guntur, in today's Andhra Pradesh. He split the light from the Sun's glowing edge into its colours. He saw a bright yellow line that matched no known substance. An English astronomer, Norman Lockyer, said it came from a new element. He named it helium, after Helios, the Greek word for the Sun. Helium was found on the Sun before anyone found it on Earth. In 1919, astronomers led by Arthur Eddington photographed stars near the Sun during an eclipse. The stars seemed slightly shifted from their usual places. That fitted Einstein's idea that the Sun's gravity bends the paths of light passing near it. In Einstein's idea, gravity bends space and time, and light follows those bends.
- e.g. Helium is the gas in party balloons that float. Its name comes from the Sun, where it was first found.
- e.g. Light from far galaxies is bent by gravity too. Scientists call this gravitational lensing.
### The Indian Moon calendar · *defined*
**Definition:** Indian calendars count months by the Moon. Each lunar month has 30 tithis, split into a bright half, Shukla Paksha, and a dark half, Krishna Paksha. An extra month is added about once every three years to keep in step with the seasons.

A tithi is the time the Moon takes to move a set step further from the Sun in the sky. Because the Moon's speed changes, a tithi can be a few hours shorter or longer than a day. In much of North India, a month ends on Purnima. In much of South and West India, a month ends on Amavasya. Twelve Moon months make about 354 days. That is 11 days short of a Sun year of 365 days. So every three years or so, an extra month, the adhik maas, is slipped in. That keeps Diwali in autumn and Ugadi in spring. The Islamic calendar is a pure Moon calendar with no extra month. So Eid and Ramzan come about 11 days earlier each year, and slowly move through every season. Chinese, Jewish and Buddhist calendars also follow the Moon. Easter falls on the Sunday after the first full Moon of spring.
- e.g. Diwali falls on Amavasya. Holi, Karthigai Deepam, Guru Purnima, Buddha Purnima and Sharad Purnima fall on full Moons. Ugadi comes just after a new Moon. Eid begins when the new crescent is seen.
- e.g. Sun festivals stay on nearly the same date each year. Makar Sankranti and Pongal fall around 14 January, and Vishu around 14 April.
### Living things that keep Moon time · *debated*
Many living things keep time by the Moon, for their own reasons, not ours. On the Great Barrier Reef in Australia, many corals release their eggs together on a few nights after a full Moon in late spring. Each coral polyp seems to sense the moonlight. Dung beetles in Africa can roll their dung balls in a straight line on moonlit nights. They steer by the way moonlight is spread across the sky. Lions hunt most successfully on dark nights, so the people who live near them are in most danger in the days after the full Moon. Do people keep Moon time too? That is argued about.
- e.g. Spring tides, the highest high tides, come at new Moon and full Moon. Many sea creatures time their egg-laying to the tides.
- e.g. Night animals like owls, bats and mice change how they behave on bright full-Moon nights.
- **Leandro Casiraghi, Horacio de la Iglesia and their team (2021):** People in villages without electricity in Argentina, and students in the city of Seattle, went to sleep later and slept less on the nights before a full Moon.
- **Many other sleep scientists:** Other studies found little or no link between the Moon and human sleep. The question is still open.
### The Earth and the Moon go round each other · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon does not simply go round a still Earth. The Earth and the Moon both go round a shared point between them. Because the Earth is so much heavier, that point lies inside the Earth, about 4,700 kilometres from its centre.

Think of a heavy grown-up and a small child holding hands and spinning round. The child swings in a big circle. The grown-up wobbles in a small circle. Both turn round a point between them, close to the grown-up. The Earth and the Moon are like that. Each month, the Earth wobbles in a small circle as the Moon swings round. They also change each other in other ways. The Earth's pull locked the Moon's face. The Moon's pull makes our tides, and slowly slows the Earth's spin. As it does, the Moon drifts about 3.8 centimetres further away every year. Scientists measure this by bouncing laser light off mirrors left on the Moon by astronauts.
- e.g. Astronomers find planets round other stars partly this way. A planet makes its star wobble a little, just as the Moon makes the Earth wobble.
- e.g. Some scientists think the Moon helps keep the Earth's tilt steady, and so keeps our seasons steady. Others think the Earth would manage without it.
### The perfect eclipse will not last for ever · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, and also about 400 times further away. So they look the same size in our sky, and the Moon can just cover the Sun. Because the Moon is drifting away, this will not always be true.

In a total solar eclipse, the Moon covers the Sun almost exactly. That lets people see the Sun's faint outer glow, the corona, which is usually hidden by glare. This perfect fit is a coincidence of our time. The Moon moves about 3.8 centimetres further away every year. Long ago, it was closer and looked bigger, so it covered the Sun with room to spare. In several hundred million years, it will look too small to cover the Sun completely. Then there will be no more total solar eclipses on Earth, only ones where a ring of Sun is left showing.
- e.g. When the Moon is a little further away than usual, it cannot cover the whole Sun. A ring of Sun shows round it. This is called an annular eclipse. South India saw one on 26 December 2019.
- e.g. Hold a coin at arm's length. Move it until it just covers a far-away lamp. Further away it is too small, nearer it is too big.

## Mostly agreed
- The Moon makes no light. Half of it is always lit by the Sun, and its phases are how much of that lit half we see. *How we know:* A ball and a lamp make every phase in the right order. The lit side of the Moon always points towards the Sun in the sky. Astronauts and spacecraft near the Moon have photographed the Earth half lit, so the Earth has phases too.
- The Moon spins once for each trip round the Earth, so we always see the same face. *How we know:* Every photograph of the Moon from Earth, for as long as there have been cameras, shows the same seas and craters. Spacecraft have mapped the far side, which looks different.
- Solar eclipses happen only at new Moon, and lunar eclipses only at full Moon, when the Sun, the Earth and the Moon line up. *How we know:* Astronomers predict eclipses to the minute, hundreds of years ahead. They use these rules, the tilt of the Moon's path and careful measurements of how the Moon and the Earth move.
- The Moon is slowly moving away from the Earth, and the Earth's spin is slowly slowing down. *How we know:* Laser light bounced off mirrors left on the Moon by astronauts shows it moves about 3.8 centimetres further away each year. Ancient records of eclipses, from Babylon and China, also show the Earth's spin has slowed.
- The Moon is about four and a half billion years old, and was probably made in a giant crash. *How we know:* Moon rocks have been dated in laboratories. They are made of rock very like the Earth's outer layers, and the Moon has only a tiny iron centre. Exactly how the crash happened is still argued about.

## How we found out
**Anaxagoras (Greece, about 450 BCE) and Aryabhata (India, 499 CE), About 450 BCE to 499 CE.** Long ago, many people thought the Moon made its own light. Old stories said it really wasted away and grew back each month. In India, a story said an eclipse happened when the demon Rahu swallowed the Sun or the Moon. Careful sky-watchers noticed something those ideas could not explain. The bright side of the Moon always points towards the Sun. A thin crescent is always close to the Sun in the sky, and a full Moon is always opposite it. About 2,450 years ago in Greece, Anaxagoras said the Moon shines with the Sun's light. In 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote in the Aryabhatiya that the half of the Moon facing the Sun is bright, and the other half is dark. He wrote that in a solar eclipse the Moon covers the Sun, and in a lunar eclipse the Earth's shadow covers the Moon. With this idea, astronomers could work out the day and time of an eclipse long before it came. A demon could not be predicted, but shadows could be calculated. Indian astronomers kept the names Rahu and Ketu, but used them for the two points in the sky where eclipses can happen.  
*What people believed before:* The Moon makes its own light and really grows and shrinks, and a demon swallows it in an eclipse. The lit side always facing the Sun, and eclipses worked out in advance from shadows, showed these ideas were wrong.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Moon changes shape because the Earth's shadow covers part of it.** (Many children and grown-ups. It is one of the most common ideas about the Moon.; *rejected*)  
  For: Shadows make things dark, and the Earth does throw a shadow into space.  
  Against: The Earth's shadow points away from the Sun. So it could only fall on the Moon when the Moon is opposite the Sun, and that is exactly when we see a full Moon. A crescent shows when the Moon is near the Sun in the sky, where the Earth's shadow can never reach. The Earth's shadow touches the Moon only in a lunar eclipse. The ball and lamp test makes every phase without any shadow falling on the ball.
- **The far side of the Moon is always dark.** (Many people. Even some songs and films call it 'the dark side of the Moon'.; *rejected*)  
  For: We never see the far side, so it seems hidden in darkness.  
  Against: The far side gets just as much sunlight as the near side. It is lit when our side is dark. On Amavasya, the far side is in full daylight. 'Far side' is the right name, not 'dark side'.
- **The Moon does not spin at all.** (Many people, because the Moon always shows us the same face.; *rejected*)  
  For: From the Earth, the Moon's face never seems to turn.  
  Against: Walk round a chair while always looking at it. By the end you have faced every wall of the room. You turned once. The Moon does the same. Measured against the stars, it spins once every 27 and a third days.
- **Eclipses are dangerous for food, water and people, so everyone must stay inside.** (Old customs in many places, including parts of India. Some families still fast, or throw away cooked food, during an eclipse.; *rejected*)  
  For: Eclipses are rare and strange, and people long ago did not know what caused them. Customs often hold a family's memories and feelings, and many people value them for that.  
  Against: An eclipse is a shadow. It sends nothing harmful down onto food, water or people. The one real danger is looking straight at the Sun during a solar eclipse, which can burn the eyes. Scientists and families all over the world watch eclipses safely, with eclipse glasses for the Sun and bare eyes for the Moon.
- **The full Moon makes people and animals behave strangely.** (A very old belief. The English word 'lunatic' comes from 'luna', the Latin word for the Moon.; *minority*)  
  For: Many nurses and police officers say full-Moon nights feel busier. Some animals really do change what they do when the Moon is bright.  
  Against: Large studies of hospitals, police records and births have found no real link between the full Moon and strange behaviour in people. We remember the busy full-Moon nights and forget the busy other nights. Some newer studies suggest people sleep a little less before a full Moon, but others found no effect.
- **Brahmagupta argued against Aryabhata's shadow explanation of eclipses only to please religious leaders.** (Al-Biruni, a Persian scholar who studied Indian astronomy, about the year 1030; *debated*)  
  For: Brahmagupta was a brilliant mathematician. He calculated eclipses using shadows himself, so he must have known the shadow explanation worked.  
  Against: We cannot know what Brahmagupta really thought. He may have honestly believed both could be true at once, or had other reasons. Historians still discuss it.

## Roots
We have been learning why the Moon changes shape, why it shows us one face, and how shadows make eclipses. Now we ask what the Moon's shapes tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the lamp test, the ball was always half lit, and only its place changed. From different places you saw a dark ball, a crescent, a half and a full circle. The real Moon is always half lit by the Sun too. So the shape we see each night shows where the Moon is, compared with the Sun and us.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say the Moon 'grows' and 'shrinks', and call it 'new' or 'full', as if the Moon itself changed. It never does. The names describe where the Moon is, compared with the Sun and with us.  
*Evidence:* In the lamp test, one ball that never changed gave every Moon shape, just because you turned on the spot. On any night, everyone on Earth sees the same phase, because the phase is set by where the Sun, the Earth and the Moon are.

- *who am i:* A Moon phase is how the Moon looks from where you stand, on the Earth. Your place is part of every shape you see.
- *what is life:* Corals time their egg-laying by the Moon, and dung beetles steer by moonlight. Life on Earth has grown up with the Moon's monthly rhythm.
- *what is this world:* The Moon is always half lit by the Sun. The shape we see changes with where the Moon is, compared with the Sun and the Earth.

Moonlight is sunlight that has bounced off the Moon. So every phase you see is made by the Sun, the Moon and the Earth together, at that moment. The Moon itself was probably made from the young Earth's rock, in a giant crash. The two still go round a shared point, and each has changed the other's spin. The Sun, the Earth and the Moon work as one system, and each Moon shape is one moment of it.

- **Isha Upanishad, opening verse (also Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.1.1):** That is whole, this is whole. From the whole, the whole comes out. Take the whole from the whole, and the whole remains. (Purnima comes from purna, whole. The Moon is whole on every night of the month, even on Amavasya. Only the part we see grows and shrinks.)
- **Dogen, Genjokoan (1233), in the Shobogenzo:** Waking up is like the Moon reflected in water. The Moon does not get wet, and the water is not broken. The whole Moon, and the whole sky, shine even in one drop of dew. (One Moon shows in every puddle, pot of water and sea at the same time. In the same way, everyone on Earth sees the same Moon phase on the same night.)
- **Jataka tale 316, the Sasa Jataka (the story of the hare):** A hare had nothing to give a hungry traveller, so he offered himself. To remember its kindness, the hare's shape was drawn on the Moon for everyone to see. (Many people in India and East Asia see a hare in the Moon's dark lava seas. We see the same hare every night because the Moon always shows us the same face.)
- Science (settled): The Earth and the Moon both go round a shared point inside the Earth. Each has changed the other: the Earth locked the Moon's spin, and the Moon slows the Earth's.
- Science (debated): The Moon was probably made from the young Earth and another planet, mixed together in a giant crash. Exactly how is still being worked out.
- Other view, Many realist philosophers: The Moon is what it is by itself: a round ball of rock, half lit. Its phases are only how it looks to us. So they tell us about our place, and nothing about what the Moon really is.
- Practice, Find the Sun from the Moon (3 min): If the Moon is up, let's look at it from the window. If not, look at a picture of it. Breathe in slowly while I count to four. Breathe out while I count to four. Now look at the lit side of the Moon and point the way it faces. The Sun is that way, even if it has set. Picture the Sun, the Moon and us, in a line or at an angle. Then think of everyone on Earth who sees this same shape tonight. Sit quietly together for one minute.
- Ask: Everyone on Earth sees the same Moon phase tonight. Is the crescent in the Moon, or in the way we see it from here?
- Ask: The Earth and the Moon were made from the same rock. Are they two things, or one?
- Ask: Does the Moon matter only because we look at it, or for itself?

## Story: The Girl Who Fed the Heavenly Dog
*Wang Zhenyi is real. She lived from 1768 to 1797, read her grandfather's books on astronomy, wrote an essay explaining lunar eclipses, and is remembered for a model with a lamp, a round table and a mirror. Banging drums and gongs to scare off the heavenly dog during an eclipse was a real custom in China. Her cousin Lan, the cook, the night of this eclipse and the conversations are made up.*

On the night the heavenly dog came to eat the Moon, my cousin Zhenyi was not frightened at all. She was annoyed. 'It is late,' she said, frowning at the sky. 'It should have started already.'

I was seven that autumn. Zhenyi was sixteen, and the cleverest person in our house, though nobody was supposed to say so. Girls were meant to sew and to be quiet. Zhenyi sewed badly and was almost never quiet. She spent her afternoons in Grandfather's library, among his tall cases of books, reading about the stars.

Down in the courtyard, everyone was ready. Old Mei the cook had her biggest pan and a ladle. My uncles had the gong from the gatehouse. My mother had a little drum, and her hands were shaking. Above the roofs hung the full Moon, round and white as a steamed bun.

'When the dog bites,' my mother told me, 'we make so much noise that the dog spits the Moon out again. It has always worked.'

That was true. Every time anyone could remember, the dog had bitten, the people had banged their pans, and the Moon had come back.

Then I saw it. A small dark bite, on the left edge of the Moon. My breath stuck in my throat. The courtyard exploded. The gong boomed. The pan clanged. My mother's drum rattled like rain on a roof. I clapped my hands over my ears and pressed myself against Zhenyi.

She was not banging anything. She was looking at the bite through a little rolled-up tube of paper. 'Lan,' she said, close to my ear, so I could hear her over the noise. 'Look at the edge of the bite. What shape is it?'

I did not want to look. But Zhenyi always asked questions as if your answer truly mattered, so I looked. The dark part was not jagged, like teeth marks. Its edge was a smooth curve, part of a circle. A very big circle, much bigger than the Moon.

'It is round,' I said.

'Remember that,' said Zhenyi. Then she took my hand and pulled me out of the courtyard, through the moon gate, into the dark garden.

In the middle of the garden stood the little pavilion where Grandmother used to drink tea. Zhenyi had been busy there for a week. Nobody had asked what she was doing. Nobody ever asked Zhenyi anything. From the roof beam hung a lamp, burning steadily. On the floor stood the round wooden table from the kitchen, which Old Mei had been looking for all week. Beside it, on a stand, was Grandmother's round bronze mirror.

'The lamp is the Sun,' Zhenyi said. 'The table is the Earth, where we live. The mirror is the Moon. The Moon has no light of its own, Lan. It only shines because the Sun shines on it. See?' She tilted the mirror until it flashed the lamp's light onto my face, like a little Moon.

'Now, tonight is a full Moon. Where must the Moon be, for us to see its whole bright face?' She did not wait for me to answer. 'On the far side of the Earth from the Sun. Like this.' She carried the mirror round the table and set it down on the other side, away from the lamp.

The table threw a long, dark shadow across the floor of the pavilion. Zhenyi slid the mirror slowly along, until its edge touched the shadow. A dark curve crept across the shining bronze. It was a smooth curve. Part of a very big circle.

I stared. 'It's the same bite,' I whispered.

'It is the same bite,' said Zhenyi. 'There is no dog, little cousin. There never was. Tonight the Sun, the Earth and the Moon stand in a straight line. The Earth is in the middle. So the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. And the Earth is round, so its shadow has a round edge.'

'But we see the full Moon every month,' I said. 'Why doesn't the shadow bite it every month?'

Zhenyi looked at me as if I had given her a present. She lifted the mirror a hand's width higher on its stand. Now the shadow slid underneath it, and the mirror shone, whole and bright. 'Because the Moon's road is a little tilted,' she said. 'Most months it passes just above the shadow, or just below. Only sometimes does it go straight through.'

Far away in the courtyard, the gong was still booming.

'Should we tell them?' I asked.

Zhenyi was quiet for a long moment. 'I will write it down,' she said at last. 'I will write it down so clearly that anyone who can read will understand. Even someone who has been told she is not supposed to.'

We went back to the courtyard. By then the Moon was almost swallowed, and the part inside the shadow had turned a deep, dusty red, like a lantern seen through silk. My mother cried out. But I was not afraid any more. I knew that somewhere a lamp was still burning, and a round table was still throwing its shadow, and that the shadow would slide away.

I took Old Mei's ladle and banged her pan as hard as I could, and laughed. Zhenyi raised an eyebrow at me.

'It is not for the dog,' I told her. 'It is for the Moon. It's being brave.'

Slowly, the shadow slid away, exactly as it had slid across the bronze mirror in the garden. The Moon came back, round and white. In the courtyard, everyone cheered and said the noise had worked again. In the pavilion, the lamp burned all night. In the morning, Old Mei found her table, and Zhenyi began to write.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the 'bite' out of the Moon during a lunar eclipse is the Earth's own shadow. It can only happen at full Moon, when the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon. Its curved edge is a clue that the Earth is round. And it does not happen every month because the Moon's path is tilted, so it usually passes above or below the shadow. The story also asks who is allowed to ask questions, and what we owe to people who are frightened.

- Ask: What shape was the edge of the 'bite' on the Moon? Why was that an important clue?
- Ask: In Zhenyi's model, what was the lamp, what was the table and what was the mirror?
- Ask: Why did the lunar eclipse happen on a full Moon night, and not on a half Moon night?
- Ask: Why doesn't an eclipse happen at every full Moon? What did Zhenyi do to the mirror to show it?
- Ask: Lan banged the pan at the end, even though she knew there was no dog. Why do you think she did?
- Ask: Zhenyi was not supposed to study because she was a girl. What would have been lost if she had obeyed?

### c1 · Keep Diwali in Autumn: design a Moon calendar (30 min)

   **Answer:** On Amavasya the Moon is still in the sky, but we cannot see it at all. *Why:* A strong answer uses the clue: a bulb glows on every side, all the time.    **Answer:** A full Moon night. *Why:* In a lunar eclipse, the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. That needs the Earth to be between the Sun and the Moon, which only happens at full Moon. Picking 'new Moon' means mixing it up with a solar eclipse, which happens at new Moon.

### Moon Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) If you stood on the Moon on the night of Amavasya, what would the Earth look like?  
   Options: Nearly full and bright / Completely dark / A thin crescent  
   **Answer:** Nearly full and bright. *Why:* On Amavasya, the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun. So the side of the Earth facing the Moon is in daylight. From the Moon, you would see a nearly full Earth. Picking 'completely dark' means thinking the Earth and Moon always have the same phase. In fact their phases are opposite.
2. (mcq) The Moon goes once round the Earth, measured against the stars, in about 27 and a third days. Why does new Moon to new Moon take about 29 and a half days?  
   Options: The Earth moves along its path round the Sun, so the Moon needs about two more days to line up again / The Moon slows down near the Sun / Calendars round the number up  
   **Answer:** The Earth moves along its path round the Sun, so the Moon needs about two more days to line up again. *Why:* While the Moon goes round us, we also move round the Sun. So the Moon must go a little further to get back between us and the Sun. Picking 'calendars round it up' is a fair guess, but the difference is real: it is about two whole days.
3. (write) Astronauts left mirrors on the Moon. Scientists on Earth fire laser light at them and time how long the light takes to come back. Explain how this tells them the Moon is moving away from us.  
   **Answer:** Light always travels at the same speed. So the longer the light takes to come back, the further away the Moon is. Each year the light takes a tiny bit longer, so the Moon must be moving away. It moves about 3.8 centimetres a year.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the time tells the distance, because light's speed never changes, and that a growing time means a growing distance. Scientists have measured this for more than 50 years.
4. (mcq) Moon rocks brought back by astronauts are very like the rock of the Earth's outer layers. Which idea about the Moon's birth does this fit best?  
   Options: A giant crash splashed out rock from the young Earth / The Moon was a wandering planet that the Earth caught / The Moon formed far away and drifted here  
   **Answer:** A giant crash splashed out rock from the young Earth. *Why:* A crash that splashed out the Earth's outer rock would make a Moon of very similar rock. A captured wanderer would probably be made of different rock. The puzzle scientists still argue about is why the match is so close.
5. (perspective) Nobody saw the Moon being born, about four and a half billion years ago. Scientists use clues and computer models, and they still argue.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks an idea and backs it up with a clue: for example, Moon rocks are very like the Earth's outer rock, which fits any crash that splashed out the Earth's own rock. It admits that the other ideas fit that clue too. It suggests what would help: more Moon rocks from new places, or better computer models.. *Why:* All three ideas agree that crashes made the Moon. They disagree on how many and how fast. The close match between Moon rock and Earth rock is the puzzle every idea must explain.

   **Answer:** There can be a lunar eclipse. *Why:* Near those points, the Sun, the Earth and the Moon can line up exactly. At full Moon, the Earth's shadow then falls on the Moon. The old names from the Rahu story were kept for something astronomers could calculate.
7. (write) Many people think the Moon is just something pretty to look at, and that it does nothing to the Earth. Give two ways the Moon changes things on Earth.  
   **Answer:** The Moon's pull makes the sea rise and fall in tides, twice a day. The Moon's pull is also slowly slowing the Earth's spin, so our days are getting very slightly longer. 4. (mcq) In your Moon diary, you draw the Moon at the same time every second evening. What do you notice about where it is?  
   Options: It is further east each time / It is always in exactly the same place / It jumps about at random  
   **Answer:** It is further east each time. *Why:* The growing Moon moves further round its orbit each day, so at the same time each evening it is further east, further from where the Sun set. Picking 'the same place' usually means only looking once or twice, or at different times.
5. (mcq) You see the full Moon rising in the east. Where is the Sun?  
   Options: Setting in the west, on the opposite side of the sky / High overhead / Rising next to the Moon  
   **Answer:** Setting in the west, on the opposite side of the sky. *Why:* The full Moon is opposite the Sun. So as it rises in the east, the Sun is setting in the west. Picking 'rising next to the Moon' describes the new Moon, which we cannot see.
6. (mcq) Imagine the Moon spun round twice for every trip round the Earth. What would we see over one month?  
   Options: Every side of the Moon, including the side we never see now / Always the same face, just as now / No Moon at all  
   **Answer:** Every side of the Moon, including the side we never see now. *Why:* We see one face only because the Moon spins exactly once for each trip round us. Spinning twice, it would turn all its sides towards us in a month. Picking 'the same face' forgets that the match between spin and trip is what keeps one face towards us.
7. (mcq) Is anything still being slowed down by the pull between the Earth and the Moon today?  
   Options: Yes. The Moon's pull is slowly slowing the Earth's spin, so our days get very slightly longer / No. Everything stopped changing long ago / Yes. The Moon will soon stop spinning  
   **Answer:** Yes. The Moon's pull is slowly slowing the Earth's spin, so our days get very slightly longer. *Why:* The Earth's pull already slowed the Moon until one face stayed towards us, so that has finished. But the Moon's pull on our seas acts like a gentle brake on the Earth's spin. Days get longer by about 2 thousandths of a second every hundred years.
8. (mcq) A solar eclipse can only happen at…  
   Options: New Moon / Full Moon / Half Moon  
   **Answer:** New Moon. *Why:* In a solar eclipse the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth. That is where it is at new Moon. Picking 'full Moon' mixes it up with a lunar eclipse.
9. (mcq) A total lunar eclipse can last more than an hour. A total solar eclipse lasts only a few minutes at any one place. Why?  
   Options: The Earth's shadow is big, so the Moon takes a long time to cross it. The Moon's shadow is small, so it passes over one place quickly / The Sun moves faster than the Moon / Solar eclipses happen at night  
   **Answer:** The Earth's shadow is big, so the Moon takes a long time to cross it. The Moon's shadow is small, so it passes over one place quickly. *Why:* In the shadow experiment, the football's big shadow could swallow the whole lime, but the lime made only a small spot. The Moon needs a long time to pass through the Earth's wide shadow. The Moon's small shadow sweeps across the Earth, so it covers one town for only minutes.
10. (mcq) Meera says, 'There is no lunar eclipse most months because the Earth's shadow is too short to reach the Moon.' What is wrong with her idea?  
   Options: The shadow does reach that far. The Moon's tilted path usually takes it just above or below the shadow / Nothing. She is right / The Earth has no shadow  
   **Answer:** The shadow does reach that far. The Moon's tilted path usually takes it just above or below the shadow. *Why:* The Earth's shadow stretches far past the Moon, so it is long enough. What usually happens is a near miss: the Moon's path is tilted about 5 degrees. Only when a full Moon falls where the paths cross does it pass through the shadow.
11. (mcq) How many tithis are there in one lunar month?  
   Options: 30 / 7 / 365  
   **Answer:** 30. *Why:* Each lunar month has 30 tithis, 15 in the bright half and 15 in the dark half. Picking 7 mixes it up with the days of a week.
12. (mcq) Eid al-Fitr starts when people see something in the evening sky. What do they look for?  
   Options: The first thin crescent Moon after the new Moon / A full Moon / A lunar eclipse  
   **Answer:** The first thin crescent Moon after the new Moon. *Why:* Eid begins a new Moon month, and the Islamic calendar starts each month with the first sighting of the new crescent just after sunset. That is why Eid's date moves about 11 days earlier each year. Picking 'full Moon' mixes it up with Purnima festivals.
13. (mcq) The Earth has a big iron centre. The Moon has only a tiny one. How does the giant crash idea explain this?  
   Options: The crash splashed out mostly the Earth's outer rock, and the iron stayed deep inside the Earth / The Moon's iron melted away / The Moon was made of iron, and it rusted  
   **Answer:** The crash splashed out mostly the Earth's outer rock, and the iron stayed deep inside the Earth. *Why:* Iron is heavy, so in the young Earth it had sunk to the centre. A crash that splashed out the outer rock would make a Moon with very little iron. A captured Moon from somewhere else would have no reason to be so poor in iron.
14. (mcq) What do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Whether the Moon formed in years or in hours, from one crash or many / Whether the Moon goes round the Earth / Whether moonlight is sunlight  
   **Answer:** Whether the Moon formed in years or in hours, from one crash or many. *Why:* The Moon going round us, and moonlight being sunlight, are settled. The details of the Moon's birth are still argued about, using computer models and rock clues.
15. (mcq) In 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote that an eclipse is caused by what?  
   Options: A shadow / A demon swallowing the Sun or Moon / Clouds  
   **Answer:** A shadow. *Why:* Aryabhata wrote in the Aryabhatiya that in a solar eclipse the Moon covers the Sun, and in a lunar eclipse the Earth's shadow covers the Moon. The demon was Rahu, from an old story.
16. (mcq) Wang Zhenyi explained lunar eclipses with a model. What did she use for the Earth?  
   Options: A round table / A mirror / A lamp  
   **Answer:** A round table. *Why:* The round table was the Earth, the lamp was the Sun and the mirror was the Moon. The table's round shadow fell on the mirror, as the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.
17. (mcq) Which of these living beings really time their lives by the Moon?  
   Options: Red crabs on Christmas Island, who release their eggs into the sea at a set phase of the Moon / Mango trees, who flower on every full Moon / Sunflowers, who turn to face the Moon at night  
   **Answer:** Red crabs on Christmas Island, who release their eggs into the sea at a set phase of the Moon. *Why:* Millions of red crabs walk to the sea and release their eggs when the Moon is in its last quarter, when the tides change least. Many corals also release eggs together after a full Moon. Mango trees and sunflowers do not follow the Moon in these ways.

## Applications
- Calendars in India and many other places count months by the Moon, and set festival dates by its tithis.
- Fishing families and port officers use tide tables. The biggest tides, spring tides, come at new Moon and full Moon.
- Astronomers predict eclipses to the minute, hundreds of years ahead, so scientists and families can travel to see them.
- Eclipses let scientists study the Sun's faint outer glow, the corona, and once revealed a new element, helium.
- Lasers bounced off mirrors on the Moon measure its distance to within a few millimetres, and test ideas about gravity.
- Relay satellites, like China's Queqiao, let rovers on the far side of the Moon talk to the Earth.
- Scientists study earthshine to measure how much sunlight the whole Earth reflects, which helps them study its clouds.
- The way the Moon makes the Earth wobble is like the way planets make their stars wobble. Astronomers use those wobbles to find planets round other stars.
- India's Chandrayaan missions study the Moon's south pole, where ice may one day give water to astronauts.
- Night-time wildlife surveys plan around the Moon, because many animals behave differently on bright nights.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how was the Moon made?** Most scientists think a giant crash made it. But they argue about whether it was one crash or many, and whether the Moon formed in hours or over years. Computer models give different answers.
- **Why are Moon rocks such a close match for the Earth's rocks?** In many computer crashes, the Moon ends up made mostly of the other planet, Theia. Yet its rocks match the Earth's almost perfectly. Nobody has fully explained this.
- **Why are the near side and the far side of the Moon so different?** The near side has big dark lava seas, and the far side has almost none and a thicker crust. Scientists have several ideas, and rock from the far side, brought back in 2024, is being studied.
- **Are the leftovers of Theia hidden deep inside the Earth?** Two huge blobs of unusual rock sit deep in the Earth, near its core. Some scientists think they could be pieces of Theia. It is an idea still being tested.
- **Why does the rising Moon look so much bigger?** People have argued about the Moon illusion for more than 2,000 years. Ideas involve how the brain judges distance and size. Nobody has a full answer.
- **Does the Moon affect how people sleep?** A few studies found people sleep later and less before a full Moon. Others found no effect. More careful studies are needed.
- **Would there be life on Earth without the Moon?** Some scientists think the Moon keeps the Earth's tilt steady, which keeps our seasons gentle. Others think the Earth would have stayed fairly steady anyway.
- **How much water ice is hidden at the Moon's poles?** Spacecraft, including India's Chandrayaan-1, found signs of water. How much ice there is in the dark craters, and how easy it is to reach, is not yet known.

## Connected maths
- Your Moon diary counts days from Amavasya, day 0. Purnima comes about 15 days later. If Amavasya is on 2 November, on about which date will Purnima be? Use a calendar to count. **About 17 November.**. Start at 2 November and count on 15 days: 3, 4, 5 and so on up to 17. So Purnima is about 17 November. The real date can be a day different, because tithis are not exactly one day long. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- The full Moon rose at 6:00 in the evening on Purnima. The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day. At about what time will it rise 3 days later? **At about 8:30 in the evening.**. Each day adds about 50 minutes. Three days add 50 + 50 + 50 = 150 minutes. 150 minutes is 2 hours and 30 minutes, because 120 minutes make 2 hours. 6:00 plus 2 hours and 30 minutes is 8:30. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In the ball and lamp experiment, half the ball was lit. At half Moon, you could see half of that lit half. What fraction of the whole ball could you see lit? **A quarter.**. The lamp lights half the ball. At half Moon, you see only half of that half. Half of a half is a quarter. The name 'quarter Moon' comes from something else: it comes a quarter of the way through the month. But this fraction is an easy way to remember the name. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- Look at this pattern of Moon shapes: new, crescent, half, gibbous, full, gibbous, half, crescent, new, crescent… What are the next three shapes? **Half, gibbous, full.**. The pattern repeats every 8 shapes: new, crescent, half, gibbous, full, gibbous, half, crescent. After 'new, crescent', it starts again, so the next shapes are half, gibbous, full. (queues `math.patterns.repeating-patterns.L2`)
- Twelve Moon months are about 354 days. A Sun year is 365 days. How many days short is a Moon year? How many days short after 3 years? **11 days in one year, and 33 days after 3 years.**. 365 take away 354 is 11. So each Moon year is 11 days short. After 3 years: 11 + 11 + 11 = 33 days. That is more than one Moon month of about 29 and a half days. So adding one extra month about every 3 years brings the calendar back in step. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In Eclipse Hunters, 2 of the 10 tilt cards say ON THE LINE. Each time your Moon passes full Moon, is an eclipse likely or unlikely? **Unlikely. Only 2 cards out of 10 give an eclipse, and 8 out of 10 do not.**. There are 10 cards. 2 give an eclipse and 8 do not. 8 is much more than 2. So most times your Moon passes full Moon, there is no eclipse. The real sky is like this too. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L3`)
