# Trillions of cells: your own cells, and the microbes who live with them
`sci.what-is-life.cells.L5`

**Summary.** Brush your teeth until they are clean, and eat nothing all night. By morning a soft coat is back on them. The coat is made of living bacteria, who divided again and again while you slept. In this unit your child stains that coat to see it, and counts how many doublings turn one cell into the 17 trillion cells of a ten-year-old. They learn how scientists count cells that nobody can count one by one, and how a wrong number, 'ten microbes for every human cell', lasted forty years. A body carries about as many microbe cells as cells of its own, and scientists still argue about whether that makes you one living being or many.

**Intro.** *Tomorrow morning, before you brush, run your tongue slowly over your front teeth. They feel a little furry. Last night, after brushing, they felt smooth. You ate nothing while you slept. So what is the furry coat, and where did it come from?* The coat is alive. It is made of bacteria, and each bacterium is one single cell. A few stayed on your teeth after brushing, and each of them divided into two, again and again, all night. Your own body is built in the same way, from cells who divide. It has about 17 trillion cells of its own, and about as many microbe cells live on it and in it. In this unit you make the coat on your teeth show up in colour, and you find out how anyone can count to a trillion.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say about how many cells are in my body, and explain how scientists estimate a number that is too big to count.
1. I can give evidence that each cell in my body is alive on her own.
2. I can explain that a body grows mainly by making more cells, and work out how doubling gets from one cell to trillions.
3. I can explain why a grown-up's body stays the same size while making billions of new cells every day.
4. I can say where the microbes on and in my body live, about how many there are, and what they and my body do for each other.
5. I can run a fair staining test on my own teeth, and explain what the result shows about living bacteria.
6. I can give two views on the question 'Am I one living being or many?', with evidence for each.
7. I can explain how the 'ten to one' number was shown to be wrong, and why a sample must be checked.

## Terms
- **Cell**: A cell is the smallest part of a living thing that is alive on her own. She takes in food, uses energy, senses what is around her and can make new cells. *e.g.* A red blood cell, a skin cell and a bacterium are each one cell.
- **Trillion**: A trillion is a million millions. It is written as 1 followed by 12 zeros. In the Indian system it is one lakh crore. *e.g.* A ten-year-old child is made of about 17 trillion cells, which is 17 lakh crore.
- **Estimate**: An estimate is a careful answer that you work out when you cannot count or measure exactly. A good estimate says how it was worked out. *e.g.* 'About 17 trillion cells' is an estimate. Nobody counted them one by one.
- **Sample**: A sample is a small part that you count or measure, so that you can work out the whole. *e.g.* You count the chana in one spoonful. That spoonful is your sample of the whole tumbler.
- **Red blood cell**: A red blood cell is a small disc-shaped cell that carries oxygen in your blood. A grown red blood cell has no nucleus. *e.g.* More than 8 out of every 10 of your own cells are red blood cells.
- **Cell division**: Cell division is how one cell becomes two. The cell copies her instructions and then splits into two cells. *e.g.* You grew from one cell to trillions of cells by cell division.
- **Doubling**: Doubling means becoming twice as many. When every cell in a group divides once, the number of cells doubles. *e.g.* 1 cell, then 2, then 4, then 8, then 16. That is four doublings.
- **Cell turnover**: Cell turnover is the body making new cells to take the place of cells that wear out and die. *e.g.* Your body makes about two and a half million new red blood cells every second, and loses about as many.
- **Capillary**: A capillary is the thinnest kind of blood tube. Its wall is one cell thick, so food and oxygen can pass through to the cells nearby. *e.g.* Press your fingernail and it goes pale, because you pushed the blood out of the capillaries under it.
- **Microbe**: A microbe is a living being too small to see with your eyes alone. Most microbes are a single cell. *e.g.* Bacteria and yeasts are microbes.
- **Bacterium**: A bacterium is a single-celled living being with no nucleus. Many are called bacteria. Most bacteria are about ten times smaller across than your own cells. *e.g.* The bacteria in curd turn milk sour and thick.
- **Microbiome**: Your microbiome is all the microbes who live on your body and in it: bacteria, and also some fungi, viruses and other tiny beings. *e.g.* The bacteria in your gut, on your skin and on your teeth are all part of your microbiome.
- **Large intestine**: The large intestine is the wide last part of your food tube. Food that your own body could not digest ends up there. Most of your microbes live in it. *e.g.* The fibre from a banana or from sambar vegetables reaches your large intestine, and bacteria there feed on it.
- **Fibre**: Fibre is the part of plant food that your own body has no way to break down. Many gut bacteria can break it down. *e.g.* Whole ragi, dal, banana, guava and vegetables are rich in fibre.
- **Plaque**: Plaque is the soft, sticky coat that grows on teeth. It is made of living bacteria and the glue they make. *e.g.* The furry feeling on your teeth in the morning is plaque.
- **Biofilm**: A biofilm is a layer of microbes who stick to a surface and to each other, inside a slime that they make themselves. *e.g.* Plaque on teeth and the slippery layer inside a water bottle that is not washed are both biofilms.
- **Germ-free**: A germ-free animal is one that scientists have raised in a sealed, clean chamber with no microbes at all. *e.g.* Germ-free mice help scientists find out what microbes do for a body.
- **Micrometre**: A micrometre is one thousandth of a millimetre. Scientists use it to measure cells. *e.g.* A red blood cell is about 8 micrometres across. A bacterium is about 1 or 2.

## Explanations
### How many cells are in you · *defined*
**Definition:** A ten-year-old child is made of about 17 trillion cells. A grown-up is made of about 30 to 36 trillion. These numbers are estimates, not counts.

Nobody can count a trillion of anything one by one. If you counted one cell every second without stopping, 17 trillion cells would take more than 500,000 years. So scientists use samples. They take one kind of tissue, such as blood, liver or fat. They measure how much of it a body has, and how many cells are in one small measured piece. Then they multiply, and add up all the tissues. In 2013 Eva Bianconi and her team in Italy did this organ by organ and got about 37 trillion for a grown-up. In 2023 a team led by Ian Hatton used over 400 kinds of cell and got about 36 trillion for a man of 70 kilograms, 28 trillion for a woman of 60 kilograms and 17 trillion for a child of ten. The numbers differ because bodies differ in size, and because every sample has some error. That is normal for an estimate, and honest scientists say so.
- e.g. One drop of blood, the size of a pinhead, holds about 5 million red blood cells. Labs count a tiny measured sample and multiply.
- e.g. In Count Without Counting, you count the chana in one spoonful and work out the tumbler. Scientists count cells in the same way.
- e.g. 17 trillion is 17,000,000,000,000. In the Indian system that is 17 lakh crore.
### Each of your cells is alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Every cell in your body is a living being. She takes in food and oxygen, releases energy, gets rid of waste, senses signals and answers them.

It is easy to think of cells as bricks, because pictures show them packed in rows. But a brick does nothing, and a cell is busy every second. How do we know each one is alive on her own? One piece of evidence is that cells taken from a body can go on living in a dish, if they are kept warm and fed. Heart muscle cells in a dish keep beating. Another piece of evidence is that some cells travel alone. A white blood cell called a neutrophil crawls between other cells. When she senses chemicals that bacteria give off, she turns and follows them, and swallows the bacterium when she catches up. David Rogers filmed this under a microscope in the 1950s. Cells sense and decide. Some scientists prefer to say that a cell only reacts to chemicals, and that 'decide' is too strong a word. Because each cell is alive, each one needs food and oxygen all the time. That is why thin blood tubes called capillaries run within about a hair's width of nearly every cell.
- e.g. Press on your thumbnail. The pink goes pale and comes back in about two seconds. That pink is blood on the way to feed living cells.
- e.g. Sit on your foot for too long and it goes numb. Its nerve cells were squeezed and short of blood, and they stopped sending signals properly.
- e.g. Skin cells from one person can be grown in a dish into sheets of new skin for burns.
- **Most cell biologists:** A cell senses chemicals with special parts on her skin, and those parts switch other parts on and off. The cell changes direction as a result. They describe this as a response, and many would not call it a decision.
- **Scientists who study how cells behave, such as Dennis Bray:** A cell takes in many signals at once, weighs them and acts. That is what deciding means, even when it is done with chemicals and not with a brain.
### The kinds of cells in you, by number · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body has several hundred kinds of cell. By number, most of them are red blood cells.

Ask most people which cells they have the most of, and they say skin or muscle. In fact about 84 out of every 100 of your own cells are red blood cells. They are very small, and there are about 5 million in a drop of blood the size of a pinhead. About 5 more in every 100 are platelets, which help blood to clot. Platelets are small pieces of a larger cell, with no nucleus, so some scientists do not count them as full cells. All your muscle cells, fat cells, nerve cells, skin cells and bone cells together make up only a few in every 100. Yet muscle and fat make up most of your weight, because each muscle cell or fat cell is thousands of times bigger than a red blood cell. Old books say there are about 200 kinds of cell. Newer studies read which instructions each cell is using, and they find many more kinds. Scientists do not yet agree on how different two cells must be to count as two kinds.
- e.g. A red blood cell is about 8 micrometres across. About 125 of them in a row would measure one millimetre.
- e.g. A nerve cell that runs from the bottom of your spine to your big toe is one single cell, about as long as your leg.
- e.g. A human egg cell is about a tenth of a millimetre across. It is one of the few human cells that sharp eyes can just see.
### A body grows by making more cells · *defined*
**Definition:** A living body grows bigger mainly by making more cells through cell division. The cells themselves stay about the same size.

You began as one cell, a fertilised egg. That cell divided into 2, the 2 into 4, the 4 into 8. This is called doubling. Doubling grows very fast. After 10 doublings there are about a thousand cells, after 20 about a million, after 30 about a billion, and after 40 about a trillion. Just 44 doublings would be enough to make 17 trillion. So why does growing up take years and not 44 days? Because real cells do not all keep dividing. Most cells take up a job, such as nerve or muscle, and then divide rarely or never. Many cells die and are replaced on the way. Each division also needs food to build the new cell from. How do we know that growing means more cells, and not bigger cells? Compare animals. A cell from a mouse, a human and an elephant are close to the same size. The elephant is bigger because she has far more cells. There are exceptions. Fat cells swell when they store more fat, and muscle cells grow thicker with exercise.
- e.g. A newborn baby has roughly one to two trillion cells, going by body weight. A ten-year-old has about 17 trillion.
- e.g. In Count the Doublings, you double on a calculator until you pass 17 trillion, and count the steps.
- e.g. A banyan grows from a seed smaller than a mustard seed to a tree that covers a field, by adding cells.
### Staying the same size: new cells in, old cells out · *defined*
**Definition:** A grown body makes about 330 billion new cells every day and loses about the same number. So it stays the same size.

In 2021 Ron Sender and Ron Milo added up how fast each kind of cell is replaced. Their total was about 330 billion cells a day, or nearly 4 million every second. Most of these are blood cells and cells that line the gut. Why those? Red blood cells are squeezed through thin capillaries for about four months, with no nucleus to help them repair themselves, and they wear out. Gut lining cells are rubbed by food and bathed in strong juices, and they last only a few days. A growing child makes a little more than they lose, so the number of cells goes up. In a grown-up the two are equal. If a group of cells makes more than it should, the result is a lump, and some lumps are cancers. If the body makes too few red blood cells, the person becomes pale and tired. This is called anaemia, and it is common in India when food has too little iron. So a steady body is a balance that is kept up every second.
- e.g. The dust on a shelf at home is partly flakes of dead skin cells that people have shed.
- e.g. A person who gives blood makes the red blood cells again within a few weeks.
- e.g. Your fingernail grows about 3 millimetres a month, because cells at its root keep dividing.
### The cells in you that are not yours · *defined*
**Definition:** A grown-up body carries about 38 trillion bacteria, which is about as many as its own cells. Together they weigh only about 200 grams.

These bacteria are not an illness. They live on every healthy person. Nearly all of them live in the large intestine, the last wide part of the food tube. The rest live on the skin, in the mouth and nose, and in a few other places. Blood, muscles and the brain normally have none. How can 38 trillion beings weigh only as much as a glass of water? Because a bacterium is about ten times smaller across than one of your cells. That makes her about a thousand times smaller in volume. Bacteria are not the only residents. Yeasts and other fungi live on you too, and so do other single-celled beings called archaea. There are also many viruses, most of which live on your bacteria and not on you. Tiny eight-legged mites live in the hair roots on almost every grown-up's face. The number of bacteria changes through the day. Each time a person passes stool, they lose a large share of their gut bacteria, and the rest multiply again.
- e.g. A piece of plaque the size of a pinhead holds about 100 million bacteria.
- e.g. One teaspoon of fresh curd holds many millions of living bacteria. They pass through your gut, and a few kinds may stay for a while.
- e.g. The smell of a sweaty shirt is made by skin bacteria feeding on sweat. Fresh sweat has almost no smell.
### What your microbes do for you, and what you do for them · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body gives its microbes warmth, water and food. In return many of them digest food you cannot, make some vitamins, and keep harmful microbes out.

Your own cells make no tools for breaking down most plant fibre. Bacteria in the large intestine do. They break fibre into small molecules called short-chain fatty acids. The cells that line your large intestine take most of their energy from one of these. Gut bacteria also make part of the vitamin K that helps your blood to clot. On the skin and in the gut, the resident microbes take up the space and the food. A harmful microbe who arrives finds it hard to settle. How do we know all this? From germ-free mice, raised with no microbes at all. Such mice must eat more food to keep the same weight. Their defences against infection develop poorly, and they need extra vitamin K in their feed. The microbes are not doing us a kindness. They are feeding themselves, and what they leave over is useful to us. Some of the same bacteria cause illness if they get into the blood through a wound. So where a microbe is matters as much as who she is.
- e.g. A mother's milk contains special sugars that her baby has no way to digest. They feed helpful bacteria in the baby's gut.
- e.g. After a course of antibiotic medicine, some people get a loose stomach, because many gut bacteria were killed along with the harmful ones.
- e.g. Idli, dosa, curd, kanji and dhokla are made by microbes, and each brings living microbes or the things they made into your gut.
### Where your microbes came from · *defined*
**Definition:** A baby has almost no microbes before birth. Microbes arrive during birth and in the first years, from the mother, the family, food and the places the child lives in.

The first microbes come from the mother during birth, from her skin and from her milk. More come from every person who holds the baby, from food, soil, pets and other children. By about three years old, a child's gut community looks much like a grown-up's. No two people carry exactly the same set. In 2010 Noah Fierer and his team swabbed computer keyboards. They could tell which person used which keyboard by matching the bacteria on the keys to the bacteria on the fingers. What you eat changes your community too. In 2019 a team at IISER Bhopal compared people in Bhopal and people in Kerala. The two groups ate different food, and they carried different gut bacteria. People who live in one home share more microbes with each other than with strangers. Whether a baby has any microbes at all before birth has been argued about. Most scientists now think that a healthy womb has none.
- e.g. Twins who grow up in one home have gut microbes that are more alike than those of two strangers, but they are never identical.
- e.g. A dog who lives in a flat shares skin bacteria with the people there.
- e.g. A child who moves from Chennai to Shillong and eats different food will slowly carry a somewhat different gut community.
### The coat on your teeth is a city of bacteria · *defined*
**Definition:** Plaque is a biofilm: layers of living bacteria stuck to the tooth and to each other, inside a glue that they make.

Within minutes of brushing, a thin film from your saliva covers each tooth. Within hours, bacteria from your saliva and tongue stick to that film. They divide, and each one's family spreads. Other kinds then stick to the first ones. By the next day there is a soft layer you can scrape off with a fingernail. If it stays for a week or two, minerals from saliva harden it into tartar, which only a dentist can remove. Where does the coat grow thickest? Along the gums and between the teeth, where the tongue, the cheeks and the brush reach least. Most plaque bacteria live quietly. The trouble begins when the coat is thick and gets sugar. Some of the bacteria turn sugar into acid, and the acid slowly dissolves the tooth under them. Brushing does not make a mouth free of bacteria, and it does not need to. It keeps the coat thin. In 1683 Antonie van Leeuwenhoek scraped the coat from his own teeth and looked at it through his lens. He saw tiny living beings moving in it, and wrote to the Royal Society in London about them. They were bacteria.
- e.g. In the Morning Coat test, the colour sticks to plaque and shows you where the coat has grown back.
- e.g. The slippery layer inside a steel water bottle that is only rinsed is a biofilm too.
- e.g. A neem or babool twig, a brush and a finger with tooth powder all work in the same way, by wiping the coat off.
### Are you one living being or many? · *debated*
You are built from cells who each live, and you carry about as many microbe cells as your own. So is a person one living being, or a community? Scientists and philosophers give different answers, and the evidence does not settle it.
- e.g. A lichen on a tree trunk looks like one being. It is a fungus and an alga, and often a yeast as well, living as one body.
- e.g. A coral is an animal with algae living inside her cells. Without the algae she turns white and may starve.
- e.g. A termite has no way to digest wood without the microbes in her gut.
- **Lynn Margulis, and biologists who use her word 'holobiont':** A body and all its microbes live, eat and fall ill together, so they should be studied as one unit. Even your own cells are a team, because the mitochondria in them began as bacteria.
- **Biologists such as Nancy Moran and Daniel Sloan:** You are one organism who has many residents. Your own cells all carry the same instructions and come from one fertilised egg. The microbes have their own instructions and their own interests, and some harm you when they get the chance.
- **Many doctors who study the body's defences:** The body treats its microbes as neighbours to be managed. It feeds them, keeps them on the surfaces, and attacks any that cross into the blood. That looks like a border between two sides who mostly get on.
### Down to atoms, and out to the whole Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** One of your cells is built from about a hundred trillion atoms. On Earth as a whole, most living beings are single cells.

There are more atoms in one of your cells than there are cells in your whole body. Those atoms do not stay. The water, the food and the air you take in bring new atoms, and old ones leave in your breath, sweat and urine. So a cell is more like a pattern that is kept up than a fixed lump of matter. Now look outwards. Your body has about a hundred times as many cells as there are stars in our galaxy, the Milky Way. And you are one animal among many. Scientists estimate that the Earth holds about a million trillion trillion bacteria, which is 1 followed by 30 zeros. In 2018 Yinon Bar-On, Rob Phillips and Ron Milo worked out the weight of all life. All the bacteria on Earth together weigh about 35 times as much as all the animals together. Plants weigh the most of all. Life on Earth has been single cells for most of its history, and by number it still is.
- e.g. A teaspoon of good garden soil holds more bacteria than there are people in India.
- e.g. A carbon atom in a cell of your fingertip may have been in a rice plant in Thanjavur last year, and in the air before that.
- e.g. Bacteria live in hot springs, in Antarctic ice, in clouds and in rock deep under the sea floor.
### Do your gut microbes change how you feel? · *idea*
This is a new field, and scientists only have early ideas. Gut bacteria make chemicals, and some of these act on nerves. A long nerve, the vagus nerve, carries signals from the gut to the brain. In experiments, germ-free mice behave differently from ordinary mice, and they change when they are given microbes. In people, the evidence is much weaker. Some studies find that people with low mood carry a different mix of gut bacteria. But a low mood also changes what a person eats, so nobody can yet say which comes first. It is honest to say this: gut microbes may affect mood a little, and nobody knows how much.
- e.g. Many people feel their stomach tighten before an exam. That shows the brain signals the gut. The question is how much the gut and its microbes signal back.
- e.g. Shops sell 'probiotic' drinks that promise a better mood. The evidence for such promises is weak so far.

## Mostly agreed
- A human body is made of tens of trillions of cells, and every one of them came by division from one fertilised egg. *How we know:* Estimates made in different ways, by Bianconi in 2013, Sender in 2016 and Hatton in 2023, all land between 28 and 37 trillion for a grown-up. Cell division has been watched directly since the 1850s.
- Each cell is alive on her own. *How we know:* Cells taken from a body go on feeding, moving and dividing in a dish. White blood cells have been filmed chasing bacteria.
- By number, most of your own cells are red blood cells. *How we know:* A measured drop of blood can be counted under a microscope or by a machine. Multiplying by the 5 litres of blood in a grown-up gives about 25 trillion.
- A healthy grown-up carries about as many bacterial cells as human cells. *How we know:* Sender, Fuchs and Milo (2016) multiplied the bacteria in a gram of gut contents by the measured amount the large intestine holds. They got about 38 trillion bacteria, beside 30 trillion human cells.
- Gut microbes digest fibre that our own cells cannot, and animals with no microbes are less healthy. *How we know:* Germ-free mice need more food to keep their weight, need added vitamin K and have weak defences against infection.
- Plaque is made of living bacteria who multiply on the teeth. *How we know:* Leeuwenhoek saw them moving in 1683. Dyes that stick to plaque show it growing back within a day of brushing.

## How we found out
**Thomas Luckey and Dwayne Savage, who made and spread the first estimate. Eva Bianconi and her team, who counted human cells organ by organ. Judah Rosner, who questioned the number. Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo, who worked it out again., The first estimate was made in 1972 and became 'ten to one' in 1977. It was corrected in 2016..** In 1972 an American scientist, Thomas Luckey, wanted to know how many bacteria live in a person. He could not count them, so he estimated. One gram of gut contents holds about 100 billion bacteria. He took the gut to hold about 1,000 grams. That gave 100 trillion bacteria. In 1977 Dwayne Savage set this beside a rough figure of 10 trillion human cells. So people said that microbes outnumber our own cells ten to one. The number was printed in books and shown in museums for about forty years. Hardly anyone checked where it came from. In 2014 Judah Rosner wrote that the number was very rough. In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo in Israel worked it out again. Only the large intestine is that crowded, and it holds about 400 grams, not 1,000. They also counted human cells again and got about 30 trillion. Eva Bianconi's team in Italy had got 37 trillion in 2013. The new answer was about 38 trillion bacteria to 30 trillion human cells. That is close to one to one.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that a body has ten microbe cells for every cell of its own. The sample was fine, but it was multiplied by the wrong amount. When the true size of the large intestine was used, the answer fell to about one to one.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Microbe cells outnumber your own cells ten to one.** (Many books, museums and news reports from 1977 to 2016; *rejected*)  
  For: It came from a real calculation by Thomas Luckey in 1972, and it was repeated by many respected scientists.  
  Against: The calculation treated the whole gut as if it were as crowded as the large intestine. When Sender, Fuchs and Milo used the measured size of the large intestine in 2016, the answer fell to about 38 trillion. That is close to one to one.
- **A clean body is a body with no bacteria on it.** (Many soap and mouthwash advertisements; *rejected*)  
  For: Some bacteria do cause illness, and washing hands with soap before eating prevents a great deal of it.  
  Against: No washing removes all bacteria, and the ones who live on healthy skin and in a healthy gut help to keep harmful ones out. Germ-free mice are less healthy than ordinary mice.
- **Cells are the building blocks of the body, like bricks in a wall.** (Many school science books; *minority*)  
  For: Cells are small units, and bodies are built up from them. Under a microscope, onion cells do look like a wall of bricks.  
  Against: A brick is not alive and does nothing. A cell feeds, senses, answers signals and makes new cells. A body is closer to a town of living beings than to a wall.
- **Every cell in your body is replaced every seven years.** (A popular saying; *rejected*)  
  For: Many cells are replaced often. Gut lining cells last days, skin cells weeks and red blood cells about four months.  
  Against: Jonas Frisen's team in Sweden, and others, dated cells using carbon atoms from atom bomb tests. Most nerve cells in the outer brain and the cells in the middle of the eye lens are as old as the person. Heart muscle cells are replaced very slowly.
- **A person and their microbes are one single living unit, a 'holobiont'.** (Lynn Margulis and later biologists; *debated*)  
  For: The body and its microbes feed each other and fall ill together. Some animals, such as corals and termites, die without their microbes.  
  Against: Human gut microbes can live in other people, and many are picked up from outside in each new life. They do not pass from parent to child as reliably as a body's own cells do. So other biologists say they are residents and not parts.
- **Gut microbes control your mood.** (Some popular books and probiotic advertisements; *debated*)  
  For: Gut bacteria make chemicals that act on nerves, and germ-free mice behave differently from ordinary mice.  
  Against: In people the studies are small and often disagree. Mood also changes eating, so it is hard to tell cause from effect. 'Control' is far stronger than the evidence.

## Roots
In the Morning Coat test, the bacteria on your teeth grew back in one night. You have learned that your body carries about as many microbe cells as cells of its own. Now we ask what that tells us about who 'you' are.

**Deep truth.** In the Morning Coat test, you brushed your teeth clean and the bacteria coat was back by morning. A grown-up carries about 38 trillion bacteria beside 30 trillion human cells. Mice raised with no microbes at all are weaker, not healthier. So your microbes are not dirt sitting on your body. They are part of how a healthy body works.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'my body' as if it were made only of our own cells. We call the microbes on it germs or dirt, as if a perfectly clean body would have none. The science shows that a body with no microbes works worse.  
*Evidence:* In the Morning Coat test the coat came back in one night, though you ate nothing. Sender, Fuchs and Milo counted about as many bacterial cells as human cells in a grown-up. Germ-free mice need more food, need added vitamin K and fight infection poorly. If your teeth showed no colour in the morning, write that down as it is and test again with stronger colour.

- *who am i:* You are about 17 trillion living cells who all came from one cell. You are also home to trillions of smaller living beings who joined you after you were born. When you say 'I', all of them are included.
- *what is life:* A living being is hardly ever alone in her body. Almost every animal and plant is also a home and a food supply for other beings, and many would not survive without them.
- *what is this world:* Your microbes came from your mother, your family, your food and the soil. So some of the world outside you now lives inside you, and does part of your body's work.

You began as one cell. That cell became many cells, and the many became different from each other: blood, bone, nerve and skin. Then other beings moved in, and your body became a community. Go back far enough and your cells and your bacteria are relatives. All life on Earth comes from the same first cells, more than three and a half billion years ago. One line of their children became bacteria, and another line, much later, became animals. In you, the two lines live together again. This is what the One becoming many looks like in a body: one cell becomes many cells, and one family of life becomes many kinds who still depend on each other.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.9:** Uddalaka tells his son that bees gather nectar from many different trees and make it into one honey. In the honey, no drop says 'I am from this tree' or 'I am from that tree'. (Your body is made by trillions of cells of your own and trillions of bacteria. Their work ends up as one living, breathing child, and you cannot point to which part each one did.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra 5.21:** The Jain teacher Umasvati wrote 'parasparopagraho jivanam'. It means that living beings support one another. (Your gut feeds bacteria with the fibre you cannot digest, and the bacteria feed the cells of your gut wall with what they make from it.)
- **Walt Whitman, Song of Myself, section 51:** The poet writes: 'I am large, I contain multitudes.' He meant that one person holds many different selves. (Science found that this is true of the body in a plain way. One person holds trillions of living beings of thousands of kinds.)
- Science (settled): A healthy grown-up carries about as many bacterial cells as human cells.
- Science (settled): The mitochondria inside your own cells began as free-living bacteria, so even one human cell is a partnership.
- Science (debated): Some biologists say a body and its microbes should count as one living unit, a holobiont. Others say they are one host and many residents.

## Story: The Jelly That Stayed Firm
*A true story, with imagined details. Fanny Angelina Hesse (1850 to 1934), called Lina, was real. She worked without pay as her husband Walther's laboratory helper and drew his bacteria for his papers. In 1881 she suggested agar, which she knew from making jellies, for growing bacteria. She had learned of it from a Dutch neighbour in New York who had lived in Java. Robert Koch mentioned agar in one line of his 1882 paper and did not name her. Her grandson Wolfgang Hesse told the story later. The words spoken, the two plates on the window sill and the exact day are imagined. Accounts differ on whether the Hesses were in Berlin or at home in Saxony that summer.*

In the summer of 1881, Lina Hesse worked in two kitchens. One was the family kitchen, where she cooked for her husband and their children. The other was her husband's laboratory, where she cooked for bacteria.

Walther Hesse was a doctor. He wanted to know what floats in the air of a schoolroom, a bedroom or a mine. He did not mean dust. He meant the living things in the air that are too small to see.

Lina's work in the laboratory had no name and no pay. She boiled the beef broth that the bacteria fed on. She washed the glass. And she drew what Walther saw through the microscope, with a pen fine enough to draw one bacterium at a time.

The trouble with bacteria was that nobody could count them. One bacterium is far too small to see. A thousand of them in a spoon of broth are still too small to see. The broth only turns cloudy when there are many millions, and by then every kind is mixed up with every other kind.

That winter, Walther had learned a new way from Dr Koch in Berlin. You stir gelatin into the warm broth and pour it onto a glass plate. It sets like a pudding. Then you leave the plate open to the air for a while, cover it, and wait.

'Each bacterium lands in one place,' Walther had told her. 'She cannot swim away, because the jelly holds her. So she divides where she is. One becomes two, two become four. In two days her family is a heap so big that you can see it. A dot.'

'One dot for each bacterium that landed,' said Lina.

'Yes. So we count the dots.'

Lina had liked that very much. You could not see one bacterium. But you could keep her in one place and wait, and she would grow into something you could see.

That was in winter. Now it was July, and the gelatin would not stay set.

By noon each day the laboratory was hot, and the plates began to run. The dots slid into each other. Walther held one up to the window. Yesterday he had counted forty dots on it. Today it was one grey puddle.

'And look at this one,' he said. 'It is not even warm here in the corner. But some of them eat the gelatin. They turn it to water all round themselves.'

Lina looked at the puddle for some time.

'Each dot was one family,' she said slowly, 'only because nobody could move. When the jelly melts, the families run together. Then you cannot tell one cell's children from another's. The firm ground is the whole trick.'

Walther nodded. He had known that already, and it did not help him.

Lina went back to the other kitchen to see to lunch. On the shelf by the window stood a row of her fruit jellies, red and gold, made the week before. The sun had been on them all morning.

She stopped with the spoon in her hand. The jellies stood up straight. They had not run.

She had made jellies and puddings this way since she was a girl in New York. A Dutch neighbour had shown her family how. The neighbour had lived for years in Java, where it is hot every day of the year, and where cooks set their sweets with a dried seaweed called agar-agar. Gelatin jelly would be soup by noon in Java. Agar jelly stands firm.

Lina carried a dish of red jelly into the laboratory and put it on the window sill, in full sun, next to a plate of gelatin.

'Wait an hour,' she said.

In an hour the gelatin had run to the edge of its plate. The red jelly had not changed at all.

'It sets firm, and it stays firm in the heat,' said Lina. 'And nothing has ever spoiled it by eating it, not that I have seen. Boil it into your broth in place of the gelatin.'

They tried it that afternoon. The agar melted only when the broth was near boiling. Then it set into a smooth, firm sheet as it cooled. They left the plates open to the air of the room for a quarter of an hour, and covered them.

Two days later, in the worst heat of the week, the plates were still firm. On each one sat small round dots, white and yellow and one pale pink. Every dot was separate from the others. Every dot stood where its first cell had landed.

Walther counted them. Lina drew them.

Walther wrote to Dr Koch about the new jelly. The next year, Koch printed his famous paper on the bacterium that causes tuberculosis. One short line in it says that agar can be used in place of gelatin. It does not say whose idea that was.

Lina never asked for her name to be added, and she was never paid. She went on drawing bacteria for Walther's papers for many years.

Today, laboratories all over the world still use her jelly. When a doctor in Chennai or Kochi wants to know which bacteria are living in a child's sore throat, a swab from the throat is wiped across a plate of agar. The next day there are dots. Each dot began as one living cell who stayed where she landed, and divided.

**For the parent:** One bacterium is far too small to see. But she is alive, and a living cell divides. If she is held in one place, with food, her family piles up around her until it makes a dot that anyone can see. Each dot stands for one cell who landed there. That is how people count living things that are too small to see: they keep each one still, and let her multiply. It only works on ground that stays firm, and Lina Hesse knew a jelly that did.

- Ask: Why does one dot on a firm jelly tell you that one living cell landed there?
- Ask: Why would the counting fail if the cells could drift about in a liquid?
- Ask: A grain of sand on the same jelly would never grow into a dot. What does that tell you about the difference between a grain and a cell?
- Ask: The idea that solved the problem came from a kitchen, not from a laboratory. Where else might useful ideas for science come from?
- Ask: Many hands help to make a discovery. Who do you think should be named?

### c1 · Feed Every Cell: design the blood tubes for a patch of body (35 min)
- **problem:** On a grid of 9 squares by 9 squares, colour some squares red as blood tubes. Every other square is a cell, and each cell must share a side with a red square. Use as few red squares as you can.
- **your turn:** Now design your own network. Can you feed all the cells with fewer than 31 red squares? Then block one red square and see how many cells lose their supply. Which design would you want in your own body, the smallest one or the safest one?
- **constraints:** All the red squares must join into one network, side to side, starting from the 'heart' square in the middle of the left edge.; A cell that only touches a red square at a corner is not fed.; Count your red squares. Fewer is better, because more squares are left to be working cells.
- **general:** Stripes: colour rows 2, 5 and 8 red, and join them down the left edge. This feeds every cell and uses 31 red squares.; A comb: one red column down the middle, with red rows branching off it.; A spiral that winds in from the edge.
- **creative:** Branch like a tree, as real blood tubes do: one thick trunk that splits into thinner and thinner twigs.; Add a few loops, so that there are two routes to each part. Real capillaries form a net with many loops.; Leave the tubes thin at the far ends and count how far each cell is from the heart. In a real body, no cell can be far along a blocked road.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a 9 by 9 grid and mark the heart square. 2. Colour rows 2, 5 and 8 red. 3. Join them with red squares on the left edge. 4. Check that every white square shares a side with a red one. 5. Count the red squares.  
  *Parent says:* Each white square is a living cell. Each one must touch a blood tube along a side. Let's try stripes first, and count what the tubes cost us.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a 9 by 9 grid. Colour rows 2, 5 and 8 red. Join them at the left. Count the red squares.
  - Note: The stripes design uses 31 red squares and leaves 50 working cells. Check a few white squares together, to be sure of the side-touching rule.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws a fresh grid. 2. They design their own network and say why it should need fewer squares. 3. They check every white square. 4. They count the red squares and try again.  
  *Parent says:* Now your own design. What will you change, and why should it need fewer red squares? Check every cell before you count.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a new grid. Design your own tubes. Check every cell is fed. Count the red squares.
  - Ask: Why can a body not simply fill itself with blood tubes? (good: Then there would be no room for the cells that do the work.; Tubes cost space and material, so a body builds just enough.)
  - Note: We have not found the smallest possible number for this grid. If your child gets below 31, check every white square together, and check that all the red squares join up.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On the best design, cross out one red square as a blockage. 2. Your child finds the red squares now cut off from the heart. 3. They count the cells that have lost their supply. 4. They add a loop to the design and test the same blockage again.

  *Child mode:* Cross out one red square. Find the tubes cut off from the heart. Count the hungry cells. Add a loop.
  - Note: A design with no loops loses everything beyond the block. A loop costs extra red squares but keeps the cells fed. Real bodies pay that cost in most places.
  - Check: Why do the thinnest blood tubes pass close to nearly every cell in your body? → **Because every cell is alive and must be fed**

   **Answer:** The true number is near those values. *Why:* Estimates made in different ways never agree exactly. When they land close together, we can trust the rough size. Choosing 'a mistake' treats an estimate as if it were an exact count.
3. (truefalse) True or false: most of your weight comes from the kind of cell you have most of.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most of your cells are red blood cells, but most of your weight is muscle and fat. One muscle cell is thousands of times bigger than a red blood cell.
4. (fill) One human cell is built from about a hundred trillion ____.  
   **Answer:** ['atoms']. *Why:* Atoms. One cell has more atoms than your body has cells.
5. (write) An elephant is far heavier than a person. Does she have bigger cells or more cells? How could you check?  
   **Answer:** More cells. You could look at a sample of her cells, such as blood, under a microscope and measure them. They are close to the size of ours.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a way to test it, and does not only state the fact.
6. (order) Put these in order, from the smallest number to the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['People on Earth', 'Cells in a ten-year-old', 'Bacteria in one grown-up', 'Bacteria on the whole Earth']. *Why:* About 8 billion people, 17 trillion cells, 38 trillion bacteria in one grown-up, and about 1 followed by 30 zeros bacteria on Earth.
7. (mcq) Mice raised with no microbes at all need more food to keep their weight. What does that suggest?  
   Options: Microbes steal food from ordinary mice / Microbes help to get energy from food / Germ-free mice run about more / Food is worse in clean cages  
   **Answer:** Microbes help to get energy from food. *Why:* Gut microbes break down fibre that the mouse cannot digest, and the mouse takes in what they make. Without them, some of the food is wasted.
8. (truefalse) True or false: gut microbes help us because they want to be kind to us.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False, as far as anyone can tell. They feed themselves, and what they leave over is useful to us. Both sides gain, and neither has to intend it.
9. (perspective) Two biologists disagree about what a person is.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence. For the first: germ-free animals are less healthy, and mitochondria were once bacteria. For the second: all our own cells share one set of instructions, and gut bacteria cause illness if they reach the blood.. *Why:* Scientists have not settled this. The marks are for using evidence and for being fair to the other view.
10. (write) Mother's milk has sugars that only a baby's gut bacteria can use. What does that tell you?  
   **Answer:** A mother's body makes food for her baby's microbes as well as for her baby. So bodies have depended on their microbes for a very long time.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that the body spends effort on feeding microbes, which only makes sense if they matter to the baby's health.
11. (match) Match each person to what they did.  
   **Answer:** Antonie van Leeuwenhoek → saw living bacteria from his own teeth; Lina Hesse → suggested a firm jelly for growing bacteria; Eva Bianconi → led a count of human cells, organ by organ; Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo → showed that 'ten to one' was wrong. *Why:* Each of them found a way to see or count living things that are too small or too many to count directly.
12. (sort) Sort each statement: is it settled, or still argued about?  
   **Answer:** A grown-up carries about as many bacteria as human cells → Settled; Gut microbes change a person's mood → Still argued about; Cells come from other cells by division → Settled; A person and their microbes are one living unit → Still argued about; Germ-free mice are less healthy → Settled; How many kinds of cell a body has → Still argued about. *Why:* A claim is settled when different careful tests agree. The others have some evidence, but studies still disagree.
13. (draw) Draw one dot of bacteria on a jelly plate. Beside it, draw what it was two days earlier.  
   **Answer:** A round dot labelled 'millions of cells', and beside it a single tiny cell labelled 'one cell, too small to see'. An arrow joins them, marked 'divided again and again'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the visible dot is the family of one cell who stayed in one place and divided.
14. (fill) The 'ten to one' estimate went wrong because a good sample was multiplied by the wrong ____.  
   **Answer:** ['amount', 'size', 'volume', 'weight']. *Why:* The amount. The whole gut was treated as if it were as crowded as the large intestine, which holds much less.
15. (mcq) A number is printed in many good books. What is the best reason to trust it?  
   Options: Many books repeat it / A famous person said it / You can trace how it was measured / It is easy to remember  
   **Answer:** You can trace how it was measured. *Why:* A number can be trusted when you can see how it was worked out and the working has been checked. 'Ten to one' was in many books and was still wrong.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) How do scientists know roughly how many cells are in a body?  
   Options: They count every cell / They count samples and multiply / They weigh one cell / They guess  
   **Answer:** They count samples and multiply. *Why:* They measure the cells in small samples of each tissue and multiply by how much tissue there is. Choosing 'guess' mixes up a guess and an estimate.
2. (mcq) Which is the best evidence that one cell is alive on her own?  
   Options: Cells are very small / Cells have a skin / A cell can live and divide in a dish / Cells are packed in rows  
   **Answer:** A cell can live and divide in a dish. *Why:* A cell that feeds and divides away from the body is clearly alive on her own. Being small or having a skin is true of some non-living things too.
3. (mcq) You have about 17 trillion cells. About how many doublings from one cell would make that many?  
   Options: 44 / 440 / 17,000 / 17 trillion  
   **Answer:** 44. *Why:* 44 doublings give about 17.6 trillion. Doubling grows very fast, so the number of doublings is small.
4. (truefalse) True or false: an elephant is bigger than a mouse mainly because her cells are bigger.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Their cells are close to the same size. The elephant has far more of them.
5. (mcq) A grown-up's body makes billions of new cells every day. Which cells make up most of them?  
   Options: Brain cells / Blood cells and gut lining cells / Bone cells / Heart cells  
   **Answer:** Blood cells and gut lining cells. *Why:* Blood cells and gut lining cells wear out fastest, so they are replaced most. Most brain and heart cells stay for many years.
6. (mcq) About how many bacteria does a grown-up carry, compared with their own cells?  
   Options: Almost none / About as many / Ten times as many / A thousand times as many  
   **Answer:** About as many. *Why:* About 38 trillion bacteria beside about 30 trillion human cells. 'Ten times as many' was believed for forty years and was shown wrong in 2016.
7. (mcq) What do gut bacteria feed on that your own cells cannot digest?  
   Options: Fibre / Water / Sugar / Salt  
   **Answer:** Fibre. *Why:* Fibre from plants. The bacteria break it into small molecules, and some of these feed the cells of your gut wall.
8. (mcq) Clean teeth took colour again after one night with no food. Why?  
   Options: Bacteria in the mouth divided / The colour came back by itself / Teeth grow a new layer nightly / Water left a stain  
   **Answer:** Bacteria in the mouth divided. *Why:* A few bacteria stay after any brushing. Each divides into two again and again, and by morning their families make a coat that takes the colour.
9. (truefalse) True or false: a body with no microbes at all would be healthier.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Germ-free mice need more food, need added vitamin K and fight infection poorly.
10. (mcq) Which is a fair reason to say you are one organism and not a community?  
   Options: Your microbes weigh very little / Your own cells share one set of instructions / Microbes are too small to matter / You were born alone  
   **Answer:** Your own cells share one set of instructions. *Why:* All your own cells came from one fertilised egg and carry the same instructions, and the microbes carry their own. This is evidence for one side of a real debate.
11. (mcq) Why did the 'ten to one' number last for forty years?  
   Options: It was correct until 2016 / People repeated it without checking the working / Nobody was interested / Bodies have changed since  
   **Answer:** People repeated it without checking the working. *Why:* The number was copied from book to book. When the working was checked, a wrong size for the gut was found. A number is only as good as its working.

## Applications
- A blood test counts the cells in a tiny measured sample of blood and multiplies. A low red cell count shows anaemia, which is common in India.
- Doctors grow bacteria from a throat swab on a plate of agar jelly, to find out which kind is causing an illness.
- Dentists use a dye to show children where the coat on their teeth is thickest, so they know where to brush.
- Sheets of skin grown from a person's own cells are used to treat large burns.
- Doctors give antibiotic medicines with care, because they kill helpful gut bacteria along with harmful ones.
- Makers of curd, idli batter and kanji depend on bacteria who multiply by dividing, just as cells in a body do.
- Wildlife scientists count tigers, trees and fish by sampling, with the same reasoning as counting cells.

## Spotted around us
- The furry feel of your teeth before the morning brush is a coat of living bacteria.
- A blood test report from a lab in your town lists red blood cells as millions in a tiny measured drop.
- A scraped knee oozes a clear liquid and then a yellowish crust. White blood cells have crawled there.
- The sour smell of a shirt after a football game is made by skin bacteria feeding on sweat.
- Dosa batter left overnight in a warm kitchen rises to the brim of the vessel, because the microbes in it multiplied.
- The slippery layer inside a water bottle that was only rinsed is a biofilm, like the coat on teeth.
- Dust on a shelf in a flat is partly flakes of skin cells shed by the people who live there.
- A neem twig sold outside a temple or a bus stand is a brush for wiping the bacteria coat off teeth.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how many kinds of cell are in a human body?** New tools read which instructions each cell is using, and they keep finding new kinds. Scientists do not agree on how different two cells must be to count as two kinds.
- **Is a person with their microbes one living being, or a host with many residents?** The evidence fits both descriptions. It depends on what we decide the word 'individual' should mean, and scientists and philosophers disagree.
- **Do gut microbes change how people feel and think?** They do in mice. In people the studies are small and often disagree, and mood also changes what people eat.
- **How does a body 'know' its right size, so that making cells and losing cells stay in balance?** Cells signal to their neighbours, and some of the signals are known. Nobody can yet explain how trillions of cells keep the total so steady.
- **Why do fingernails grow about twice as fast as toenails?** Blood supply and how much the nail is knocked about may both matter. No test has settled it.
- **Which of the thousands of kinds of microbe in us matter most for health?** Most kinds have never been grown on their own in a laboratory, so nobody has studied what each one does.

## Connected maths
- In Count the Doublings, 10 doublings turned 1 cell into 1,024 cells. Without doubling ten more times, work out about how many cells there are after 20 doublings. **About a million (1,048,576).**. Ten doublings multiply the number by 1,024. Ten more multiply it by 1,024 again. 1,024 x 1,024 = 1,048,576, which is about a million, or 10 lakh. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- A ten-year-old has about 17,000,000,000,000 cells. Write this number in words in the Indian system, using lakh and crore. **Seventeen lakh crore.**. One crore is 1,00,00,000, which has 7 zeros. One lakh crore is 1 followed by 12 zeros, and that is a trillion. So 17 trillion is 17 lakh crore. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- In Count Without Counting, Kavya's three sample spoons held 26, 29 and 26 chana. 13 spoons filled the tumbler. Her true count was 338. How far out was her estimate, and is that within one tenth of the true count? **Her estimate was 351. It was 13 too many, which is well within one tenth.**. The mean of 26, 29 and 26 is 81 ÷ 3 = 27. The estimate is 27 x 13 = 351. The difference is 351 - 338 = 13. One tenth of 338 is about 34, and 13 is less than 34. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L5`)
- A grown-up's body makes about 4 million new cells every second. About how many is that in one minute? Give the answer in crore. **About 240 million, which is 24 crore.**. 4 million x 60 seconds = 240 million. One crore is 10 million, so 240 million is 24 crore. A grown-up makes more new cells in one minute than there are people in most countries. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- A human cell is about 10 micrometres across. A bacterium is about 1 micrometre across. Think of both as small cubes. How many bacteria would fill the space of one human cell? **About 1,000.**. Ten bacteria fit along one edge of the cell. Ten rows fit across, and ten layers fit upwards. 10 x 10 x 10 = 1,000. This is why 38 trillion bacteria weigh only about 200 grams. (queues `math.area-perimeter.volume.L5`)
- In the Morning Coat test, Meenakshi's total score was 0 at the clean start and 12 after 8 hours of sleep. If the coat kept growing at the same speed, what would her score be after 24 hours? Why can the real score never pass 36? **36 by this pattern. It can never pass 36, because 12 teeth with a top score of 3 each make 36.**. 12 points in 8 hours is 12 points for each 8 hours. 24 hours is three lots of 8 hours, so 3 x 12 = 36. The scale stops at 36. A real coat also slows down as it runs out of free tooth surface, so the pattern will not hold exactly. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the Nail March, a line on Arjun's thumbnail moved 2 millimetres in 21 days. His whole thumbnail is 12 millimetres long. About how long does it take to grow a whole new thumbnail? **About 126 days, which is about four months.**. 2 millimetres take 21 days. 12 millimetres is 6 times as much, so it takes 6 x 21 = 126 days. That is a little over four months. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
