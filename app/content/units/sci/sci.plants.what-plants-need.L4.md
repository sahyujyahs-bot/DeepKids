# What plants need underground: air, minerals and helpers in the soil
`sci.plants.what-plants-need.L4`

**Summary.** A brinjal plant in a big pot starts to droop and turn yellow. The family waters them more and adds fertiliser, and the plant gets worse. What is wrong? Your child pours water onto dry soil and sees bubbles of air rush out, and finds that between a third and a half of soil is gaps. Roots need that air as much as they need water. Then comes the rest of the underground story: about a dozen minerals in tiny amounts, the shortest supply that sets the limit for everything else, bacteria that make fertiliser inside bean roots, and fungi that trade with trees. Some of it is well known, and some of it scientists still argue about.

**Intro.** *You water a drooping plant, and they droop more. You water again, and they get worse. How can more water make a thirsty-looking plant sicker?* A plant that droops looks as if they are dry, so most of us reach for the watering can. But the drooping can also come from too much water. Roots are alive. They need food, water, and air to breathe, just as you do. In this unit you pour water onto dry soil and find out how much air hides between the grains. Then you look at the other things a plant needs from the soil, in small amounts but never none, and at the bacteria and fungi that help them get it.

## Terms
- **Mineral nutrient**: A simple substance from the soil, such as nitrate or potassium, that a plant takes in through the roots, dissolved in water. A plant needs only small amounts, but cannot grow without them. *e.g.* Nitrate is a mineral nutrient that a plant uses to make chlorophyll and proteins.
- **Nitrogen**: An element that plants need to make chlorophyll and proteins. About 78 parts in every 100 of the air is nitrogen gas, but a plant cannot use the gas. They take it in as nitrate or ammonium from the soil. *e.g.* A plant short of nitrogen turns pale yellow-green, starting with the oldest leaves.
- **Nitrate**: A mineral that holds nitrogen in a form that dissolves in water. Roots take it in easily. Bacteria in the soil make nitrate from dead plants and animals. *e.g.* Compost slowly releases nitrate that a vegetable plant can use.
- **Phosphorus**: An element that plants use to build their energy carriers, their DNA and their roots and flowers. They take it in from the soil as phosphate. *e.g.* A plant short of phosphorus grows slowly and some leaves turn dark green or purple.
- **Potassium**: An element that helps the stomata open and close, and helps the plant manage water. It also helps move sugar around. *e.g.* A plant short of potassium often has yellow or brown edges on the older leaves.
- **Fertiliser**: A substance added to soil to give plants minerals. It can be made from natural things, such as cow dung, or made in a factory. A plant takes up the same simple minerals from either. *e.g.* A farmer spreads urea on a paddy field to give nitrogen.
- **Compost**: Plant waste, such as vegetable peels and dry leaves, that has been rotted by bacteria, fungi and worms into a dark, crumbly material that feeds plants. *e.g.* A heap of kitchen peels and dry leaves turns into compost in a few weeks to months.
- **Humus**: The dark, spongy part of soil made from well-rotted plants and animals. It holds water and minerals like a sponge. *e.g.* The dark top layer of a forest floor is rich in humus.
- **Soil air**: The air that fills the gaps between the grains of soil. Roots and soil living things use the oxygen in it to respire. *e.g.* When water is poured onto dry soil, soil air escapes as bubbles.
- **Waterlogged**: Soaked so full of water that the gaps in the soil have no air left. A waterlogged soil is a danger for the roots of most plants. *e.g.* A pot with a blocked hole becomes waterlogged after heavy rain.
- **Root hair**: A tiny hair on a young root, thinner than a thread. Root hairs take in water and minerals, and spread the root out between the grains of soil. *e.g.* A moong sprout has a fuzz of white root hairs, which you can see with a magnifying glass.
- **Water culture**: Growing plants with their roots in water that holds dissolved minerals, and no soil. It is also called hydroponics. *e.g.* Lettuce can be grown in trays of mineral water on a rooftop farm, with no soil at all.
- **Nutrient solution**: Water with minerals dissolved in it in the right amounts for a plant to take in. *e.g.* A few drops of liquid plant fertiliser in a litre of water make a nutrient solution.
- **Legume**: A plant of the bean family, which grows seeds in pods. Dals, groundnuts, peas, beans and tamarind are legumes. They can get nitrogen with the help of bacteria in their roots. *e.g.* Moong, chana, tur and groundnut are all legumes.
- **Root nodule**: A small lump on the root of a legume, where helpful bacteria live. Inside, the bacteria turn nitrogen gas from the air into a form the plant can use. *e.g.* A groundnut plant pulled from the field has dozens of pale lumps on the roots. When you cut one, it is pink inside.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form that plants can use. Some bacteria do it. Lightning does a little. Factories do it too, to make fertiliser. *e.g.* A bean plant's root bacteria fix nitrogen. A fertiliser factory also fixes nitrogen, using high heat and pressure.
- **Mycorrhiza**: A partnership between a fungus and a plant root. The fungus spreads thin threads through the soil and brings minerals and water to the plant. The plant gives the fungus sugar. The word means 'fungus root'. *e.g.* Most trees in a forest have fungi living on and inside their roots.
- **Limiting nutrient**: The mineral that is in shortest supply for a plant, which sets how well the plant can grow. Adding more of another mineral does not help until this one is supplied. *e.g.* If a soil has plenty of potassium but very little nitrogen, adding potassium will not help the crop. Nitrogen is the limiting nutrient.
- **Deficiency**: Not having enough of something. A plant with a mineral deficiency shows signs, such as yellow or purple leaves, that point to what is missing. *e.g.* Pale yellow older leaves are often a sign of a nitrogen deficiency.
- **Crop rotation**: Growing different crops in the same field in turns, season after season. Growing a legume in between helps put nitrogen back into the soil. *e.g.* A farmer grows rice in the wet season and a dal such as black gram in the dry season on the same field.
- **Azolla**: A tiny floating water fern, with bacteria living in the leaves that fix nitrogen. Farmers in Asia have grown it in paddy fields to feed the rice. *e.g.* A green mat of azolla can cover a paddy field in a few weeks.
- **Aerenchyma**: Long air channels inside the stems and roots of plants that grow in mud and water. They carry air down from the leaves to the roots. The word is said 'air-EN-kim-ah'. *e.g.* A slice of lotus stem has big round holes in it. They are air channels.

## Explanations
### Roots need air: the gaps in soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil is not solid. It is made of grains with gaps between them. A good soil has between a third and a half of its volume as gaps. The gaps hold air and water, and roots breathe the air.

Pick up a handful of soil, and it seems like one solid thing. Pour water onto it and bubbles of air rush out. That air was in the gaps between the grains. In a loose garden soil, the gaps can be nearly half of the volume. Roots grow into these gaps. A root is made of living cells, and every living cell respires. The root cells use oxygen to break down sugar that the leaves sent down, and they give out carbon dioxide. The oxygen comes from the air in the gaps. When the gaps are full of water, or squashed shut by trampling, the oxygen runs out. The root cells cannot get energy, and they begin to die. A plant can then droop and turn yellow even though there is plenty of water. The grains hold the plant up, but the gaps are where the roots live and breathe.
- e.g. A garden path that people walk on every day has packed, hard soil with few gaps, so few plants grow there.
- e.g. A loose soil with lots of dry leaves in it has many gaps, and earthworms keep making more.
- e.g. Digging the soil in a pot gently opens up gaps for the roots to breathe.
### Drowning roots: why too much water kills · *defined*
**Definition:** In a waterlogged soil, water fills the gaps and pushes out the air. Oxygen moves through water thousands of times more slowly than through air, so the roots run out of oxygen and die.

When you water a plant, water fills the gaps, and the extra drains out through the bottom. If the pot has no hole, or the hole is blocked, or the saucer stays full, the water stays. Oxygen can still dissolve into the water, but it spreads through water thousands of times more slowly than through air. The roots and the soil bacteria use up the little oxygen there is. Soon the roots have none. Roots without oxygen cannot take in water or minerals, so the leaves droop, as if the plant were thirsty. Bacteria that do not need oxygen start to grow and make sour, rotten-egg smells, and the roots turn brown and soft. This is called root rot. Some plants have found ways around it. Rice, lotus and mangroves have long air channels in their stems and roots. They carry air down from the leaves to the roots in the mud. Mangroves in the Sundarbans also push up pencil-like roots above the mud that act like snorkels.
- e.g. A potted plant left in a saucer of water during the northeast monsoon often turns yellow and droops.
- e.g. A paddy plant grows well in a flooded field because air flows down inside the stem to the roots.
- e.g. A brinjal or chilli plant dies in a flooded field in a few days, since they have no air channels.
### More than water and light: the mineral nutrients · *defined*
**Definition:** Besides water, air and light, a plant needs about a dozen or more minerals from the soil. Three are needed in the biggest amounts: nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. Others, such as iron, are needed in tiny amounts.

A plant builds their body from carbon dioxide and water, but that is not enough. Chlorophyll needs nitrogen to be made, and a magnesium atom sits at the middle of every chlorophyll molecule. The plant also needs iron to make chlorophyll. Proteins need nitrogen and sulphur. The energy carriers in a cell, and the DNA, need phosphorus. Potassium helps the guard cells open and close the stomata. Calcium helps hold the cell walls together. Plants take these minerals from the soil water through their roots, as tiny dissolved bits called ions. The three biggest needs, nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, are often called N, P and K. They are the three numbers on a bag of fertiliser. A plant needs very small amounts of others, such as zinc, copper, boron and manganese. But a tiny amount is still a necessary amount. Without any one of them, the plant cannot complete their life.
- e.g. The three numbers on a bag of fertiliser, such as 20-10-10, tell how much nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium it holds.
- e.g. A mineral needed in tiny amounts, like iron, is still essential. Without it, new leaves come out yellow.
- e.g. Spinach is rich in iron, which it took from the soil.
### Reading the leaves: clues to what is missing · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant short of a mineral shows it in their leaves. Where the sign appears, and what it looks like, are clues to what the plant is missing.

Some minerals can be moved around inside a plant, and some cannot. Nitrogen can move. When there is too little, the plant pulls nitrogen out of the oldest leaves to feed the new growth, so the old leaves turn pale yellow first. Potassium also moves, so a shortage often shows as yellow or brown edges on the older leaves. Phosphorus shortage can make older leaves dark green or purple. Iron cannot move around once it has been put in a leaf. So with too little iron, the newest leaves turn yellow while the veins stay green. These patterns are only clues, not proof. Several different problems can make a leaf yellow, including too much water, too little light and root rot. A good plant doctor looks at which leaves are affected, then checks the roots and the soil before deciding.
- e.g. Old, lower leaves of a tomato plant turn pale yellow first. This is a clue for nitrogen.
- e.g. New leaves on a lemon plant turn yellow with green veins. This is a clue for iron.
- e.g. Yellow leaves from the bottom of a potted plant in a full saucer may be root rot instead.
### The shortest plank: the limiting nutrient · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant's growth is limited by the mineral that is in the shortest supply. Adding more of the other minerals does not help until the shortest one is supplied.

Imagine a wooden barrel made of planks of different heights. You can only fill the barrel as high as the shortest plank, because water spills over it. A plant is like that. Suppose the soil has lots of phosphorus and potassium but very little nitrogen. The plant grows only as well as the nitrogen allows. Pouring on more phosphorus does nothing. This idea is called the law of the minimum. A German agricultural scientist named Carl Sprengel worked it out in 1828, and the chemist Justus von Liebig made it famous in the 1840s. Today it is often called Liebig's law, though Sprengel was first. Farmers use it. They test the soil, find the shortest supply and add only that. This saves money, and it saves the soil and rivers from extra minerals that the crop cannot use.
- e.g. A farmer's soil test shows plenty of potassium and phosphorus but low nitrogen. She adds a nitrogen fertiliser, and the crop improves.
- e.g. A house plant with pale leaves does not need more water. They may need a few drops of fertiliser with nitrogen.
### Growing without soil: the water culture test · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant can grow with no soil at all, if their roots are in water that holds the right minerals and enough air. This shows that the plant's food is made in the leaves, and from the soil they need only minerals and support.

Around 1860, the German plant scientists Julius Sachs and Wilhelm Knop grew plants in glass jars, with their roots in water. They put into the water only a few simple minerals: salts of nitrogen, potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, sulphur and iron. There was no soil and no rotting humus. The plants grew well and made seeds. This showed that plants do not need humus as food, as many farmers then believed. It also showed that soil provides only water, minerals and support. Today, this idea is used in water culture farming, also called hydroponics. Lettuce, tomatoes and herbs are grown on trays and pipes with their roots in a flowing mineral solution. A water culture grower must pump air into the water, or leave an air gap, because roots in water still need to breathe.
- e.g. A tray of lettuce on a rooftop, with roots hanging in flowing mineral water.
- e.g. A money plant that lives in a glass of water for a year, taking its tiny minerals from the water.
### Nitrogen: plenty in the air, but hard to use · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is about 78 parts in 100 nitrogen gas, but plants cannot use the gas. Nitrogen must be 'fixed', that is, joined to other atoms, before roots can take it in. Bacteria, lightning and factories fix nitrogen.

Nitrogen gas is made of two nitrogen atoms joined by a very strong bond. Plants and animals cannot break it. So a plant can be surrounded by nitrogen and still starve for it. Nitrogen has to be fixed first, into forms such as nitrate and ammonium. There are three ways it happens. Lightning does a little, because its heat joins nitrogen and oxygen, and rain carries the result to the soil. Bacteria do much more. Some live free in the soil, some live in lumps on the roots of legumes, and some live inside the tiny water fern azolla. And since the early 1900s, factories do it too. Two German chemists, Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch, worked out how to make ammonia from nitrogen gas and hydrogen at high heat and pressure. Fertiliser made this way is estimated to feed roughly half of all people alive today. The same chemistry was also used to make explosives, and Haber's later wartime work with poison gas was a terrible use of science.
- e.g. Urea in a bag of fertiliser is made from factory nitrogen.
- e.g. Rain after a thunderstorm carries a little fixed nitrogen to the soil.
- e.g. Dead leaves and dung rot into nitrate that roots can take in.
### Beans and their bacteria: root nodules · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants of the bean family, called legumes, grow lumps on their roots called nodules. Helpful bacteria called rhizobia live in the nodules. The bacteria fix nitrogen for the plant, and the plant gives them sugar.

Dig up a groundnut or a pea plant with their roots, and you will see small round lumps on the roots. These are nodules. Inside each nodule live millions of bacteria, called rhizobia. The plant gives them sugar, which they get from photosynthesis. In return, the bacteria turn nitrogen gas from the air into ammonia that the plant can use. A healthy nodule is pink inside, because it holds a red substance that carries oxygen, a bit like the red in blood. This is a trade, and both sides gain. It was worked out in the 1880s by two German scientists, Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth, and the bacteria were first grown in a dish by a Dutch scientist named Martinus Beijerinck. Farmers in India have long grown dals such as tur, moong, urad and chana in turns with rice and wheat, which use up nitrogen. The legume leaves some nitrogen behind in the soil for the next crop.
- e.g. A groundnut plant pulled from a field has dozens of nodules. When you cut one open, it is pink.
- e.g. A farmer grows black gram after rice, so the next rice crop needs less fertiliser.
- e.g. A green manure plant such as dhaincha is ploughed into the soil to put nitrogen back.
### Fungus roots: the underground trade · *defined*
**Definition:** Most land plants have fungi living on or inside their roots. The fungus spreads thin threads through the soil and brings the plant water and minerals, especially phosphorus. The plant gives the fungus sugar. This partnership is called mycorrhiza.

A root is thick compared with a fungus thread, which is much finer than a root hair. A fungus can reach into tiny gaps between grains that a root cannot, and can spread far beyond the root. Phosphorus does not move easily through soil, and a root soon uses up all the phosphorus close to it. The fungus brings more from farther away. In exchange, the plant sends the fungus a share of the sugar from their leaves. Scientists estimate that most land plants, perhaps eight or nine in every ten kinds, have fungus partners like this. The partnership was named mycorrhiza in 1885 by a German scientist named Albert Bernhard Frank. Some early land plants that lived hundreds of millions of years ago already had fungus partners, which suggests that plants may have needed fungi to move onto land at all. Mushrooms you see on the ground are only the fruit of fungi whose threads spread far underground.
- e.g. Mushrooms appearing under a tree after rain are the fruits of fungi linked to the tree's roots.
- e.g. Plants grown in sterilised soil, with no fungi, often grow smaller and take up less phosphorus.
### Compost and humus: dead things feed the living · *defined*
**Definition:** Compost is plant and animal waste rotted by bacteria, fungi and worms. It turns into humus and minerals that plants can take up, and it opens up the soil so that it holds air and water.

Nothing in nature is waste. A fallen leaf, a vegetable peel or cow dung is food for decomposers: bacteria, fungi, worms, beetles and tiny soil creatures. They break the dead material down. They use some of it for their own bodies and energy, and leave the rest as simple minerals that roots can take in, such as nitrate and phosphate. What remains is a dark, spongy material called humus. Humus does three jobs. It holds minerals so that rain does not wash them away. It holds water like a sponge. And it makes the soil crumbly, with lots of gaps for air. This is why gardeners add compost, and why farmers in India have used cow dung manure and vermicompost for centuries. A heap of compost can get warm, because the decomposers are respiring fast and giving off heat. Rotting is not the end of a leaf. It is the leaf's next job.
- e.g. Kitchen peels and dry leaves turn into dark, earthy-smelling compost in a few weeks to months.
- e.g. Vermicompost is made by earthworms eating waste and leaving rich castings behind.
- e.g. A forest has no farmer and no fertiliser bag, and trees still grow, because the leaf litter is recycled.
### Too much of a good thing: when fertiliser harms · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant needs minerals, but too much fertiliser can harm them. Strong fertiliser salts can pull water out of the roots and scorch the leaves, and extra minerals that the crop cannot use wash into rivers and lakes.

Fertiliser is made of salts. When there is a lot of salt in the soil water, the roots find it hard to take in water, and may even lose water. The leaf edges turn brown and crispy. Farmers call this fertiliser burn. It is common in pots, where a strong dose has nowhere to go. A small dose, often, works better than a big one at once. Another problem happens downstream. Rain can wash extra nitrate and phosphate off fields and into ponds and rivers. There they can feed a huge growth of algae, called a bloom, which later rots and uses up the oxygen so that fish die. Careful farmers use only what the crop needs, and place it where the roots are. Compost and manure release minerals slowly. The Class 4 unit on photosynthesis tells how an algae bloom can use up the oxygen in a pond.
- e.g. A potted plant gets brown leaf edges after a double dose of liquid fertiliser.
- e.g. Green, smelly water at the mouth of a river after heavy rain can come from fertiliser washed off the fields.
### A handful of soil is alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Healthy soil is full of living things: bacteria, fungi, worms, mites and tiny animals. They recycle dead material, make minerals available to roots, and keep the soil loose with gaps.

A teaspoon of healthy soil can hold hundreds of millions of bacteria and long threads of fungi. Larger animals live there too: earthworms, ants, beetles, termites and tiny mites. They are the soil's workforce. Earthworms eat soil and dead leaves, and their burrows let air and water down to the roots. Charles Darwin spent about forty years watching earthworms, and in 1881 he published a book about them. He worked out that worms bring many tonnes of soil to the surface of a field each year, and that they slowly bury old stones and even ruined walls. Many people laughed at the idea of a book about worms, but it sold very well. Bacteria and fungi do the rest. They turn dead matter into minerals and humus. When the soil is poisoned with too much chemical, or packed hard by machines, this life dies, and the soil becomes tired. A farmer's soil is a herd of living things, as well as a heap of grains.
- e.g. Little heaps of soil on the ground after rain are worm casts, soil that earthworms have passed through their bodies.
- e.g. A rotting log on a forest floor is crawling with beetles, fungi and bacteria.
### Do roots decide where to grow? · *debated*
Roots do not grow at random. They grow towards water. They grow thicker where there are minerals. They bend away from salt and from poisons, and they steer round rocks. A root tip senses gravity, moisture, touch, chemicals and even the roots of other plants, and then decides which way to grow. In 1880, Charles Darwin and his son Francis wrote that the tip of a root seems to act like the brain of a simple animal, directing the root's movements. Today, scientists agree that roots sense and respond. They argue about what to call it.
- e.g. A root grows more branches into a patch of soil rich in minerals than into a poor patch.
- e.g. A root that meets a stone turns and grows around it.
- **Plant scientists such as Stefano Mancuso, Frantisek Baluska and others:** Roots gather information, compare it, remember it and choose. This is a kind of thinking. Roots and shoots are intelligent, in their own way, and have no need of a brain.
- **Other plant scientists, such as Lincoln Taiz and colleagues:** Roots do respond in clever ways, but these are chemical responses to signals, like an automatic system. There is no reason to call it thinking or choosing, and no evidence for awareness.
### Do trees share food through fungus networks? · *debated*
Because many trees in a forest share the same fungus, some scientists have asked if the fungus threads form a network between trees. Could the network pass sugar, water or warning signals from one tree to another? In 1997, a team led by the Canadian forest scientist Suzanne Simard reported that carbon moved between young trees of two different kinds in a forest, and that fungi might be the link. The idea was called the wood wide web, and it became famous in books and films. The picture of old 'mother trees' feeding their children through the network moved many people. Other scientists now say that some of these claims have gone further than the evidence.
- e.g. In experiments, carbon marked with a tracer was found to move between nearby trees in a forest.
- e.g. Seedlings that share a fungus with an old tree sometimes grow better than seedlings that do not.
- **Suzanne Simard and scientists who agree with her:** Fungus networks link trees and move carbon, water and signals between them. Older trees can help seedlings. A forest is more like a community than a crowd of single competing trees.
- **Justine Karst, Melanie Jones, Jason Hoeksema and other scientists, in 2023:** Fungus threads do link roots, and some carbon does move. But there is not enough evidence that the movement is big enough to help seedlings survive, or that trees do this on purpose to help their kin. Some popular claims have run ahead of the evidence.
### The Green Revolution: more food, and new costs · *debated*
In the 1960s, India had a food shortage and relied on grain from other countries. Scientists, including Norman Borlaug in Mexico and M. S. Swaminathan in India, helped bring in new short-stalked varieties of wheat and rice. These plants put more of their energy into grain, and did not fall over when given a lot of fertiliser. With fertiliser, irrigation and new seed, harvests rose steeply, and India became much less dependent on imported grain. This was called the Green Revolution. It also brought costs. Farmers bought seed, fertiliser and pumps, and many took on debts. In some regions, the water in the ground fell, and soils became tired. Extra fertiliser flowed into rivers. Scientists and farmers still disagree about the balance.
- e.g. In Punjab and Haryana, wheat and rice harvests rose steeply, and the water table in many places has fallen.
- e.g. Newer ideas, such as growing dal in turns with cereals and adding compost, aim to keep the high yields while caring for the soil.
- **Supporters of the Green Revolution:** It saved many people from hunger. Without new varieties and fertiliser, there could not have been enough food for India's growing population.
- **Critics of the Green Revolution:** It made farmers depend on costly seed and fertiliser, drained the groundwater, tired the soil and pushed aside older crops such as millets. The benefits were not shared equally.

## Mostly agreed
- Roots need oxygen to respire. Waterlogged soil, in which the gaps are full of water, kills the roots of most plants. *How we know:* Roots in water without oxygen stop growing and die within days. Plants with air channels, such as rice and lotus, survive floods because the channels carry air down to the roots.
- Plants need at least a dozen minerals from the soil, in small amounts. Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are needed in the largest amounts. *How we know:* Plants grown in water culture grow well with the right minerals and fail when any one is left out. Plant ash contains the same minerals.
- Plants do not need humus or soil as food. They need minerals, water, air and light, and make their own food by photosynthesis. *How we know:* Since about 1860, plants have been grown in water with only mineral salts, and have made leaves, flowers and seeds.
- A plant's growth is held back by the mineral that is in shortest supply. *How we know:* Farmers' trials, in which they add one mineral at a time to a soil, show the crop rises only when the shortest mineral is added.
- Bacteria in the root nodules of legumes fix nitrogen for the plant, and the plant gives them sugar. *How we know:* Legumes grown in sterile soil make no nodules and grow poorly without nitrogen. When the right bacteria are added, nodules form and the plants thrive. Radioactive and heavy kinds of nitrogen can be followed from the air into the plant.
- Most land plants have fungi on or in their roots that bring them minerals, especially phosphorus, in exchange for sugar. *How we know:* Under a microscope, fungus threads can be seen entering root cells. Tracers show phosphorus moving from fungus to plant and carbon moving from plant to fungus.
- Too much fertiliser can burn plants and cause algae blooms and fish deaths in water downstream. *How we know:* Scorched leaves are seen on plants given strong doses. Dead zones in lakes and at river mouths have been traced to fertiliser and sewage flowing in.
- Soil is full of living things, whose work keeps it loose, rich and full of air. *How we know:* Sterilised soil grows plants poorly. Counts of soil life show hundreds of millions of bacteria in a teaspoon of healthy soil.

## How we found out
**Julius Sachs and Wilhelm Knop, German plant scientists, building on Carl Sprengel and Justus von Liebig, About 1860.** In the early 1800s, many farm scientists believed in the humus theory: that plants feed on humus, the dark rotting stuff in soil. In 1840, the chemist Justus von Liebig argued that plants take only simple minerals from the soil, and carbon dioxide from the air. A German scientist, Carl Sprengel, had written something similar in 1828, but few listened. Around 1860, two German scientists, Julius Sachs and Wilhelm Knop, tested the idea. They grew plants in glass jars with their roots in water. The water held only a few simple mineral salts, and there was no soil and no humus. The plants grew well, flowered and made seeds. So humus was not the plant's food. The soil gives minerals, water and a place to hold on. The plant makes their own food in their leaves.  
*What people believed before:* Many farm scientists believed that a plant feeds on humus, the dark rotting matter in soil. Plants grown in water with only mineral salts, and no humus, grew well and made seeds.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Plants eat soil, or eat the rotting humus in it.** (A belief held for centuries, and taught by respected farm scientists in the early 1800s as the humus theory; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants grow better in dark, rich soil. Farmers who add manure get better crops. It seems natural that the plant eats what is dark and rich.  
  Against: Plants grown in water with only mineral salts do well. The food of a plant is sugar, made in the leaves. The soil gives water, minerals and support, and manure helps by releasing minerals and by opening the soil.
- **If a drooping plant needs water, more water is always better.** (A habit among many gardeners and families; *rejected*)  
  For: A dry plant really does droop, and a good watering brings them back.  
  Against: A plant in soaked soil droops too, because the roots have no air. Giving more water makes it worse. Feel the soil before you water.
- **Air is mostly nitrogen, so plants get all the nitrogen they need from the air.** (A reasonable guess that many people make; *rejected*)  
  For: Nitrogen is by far the largest part of the air, and plants are surrounded by it.  
  Against: The nitrogen in air is nitrogen gas, whose atoms are locked together very tightly. A plant cannot use it directly. Only fixed forms in the soil, such as nitrate, can be taken in by roots.
- **Organic fertiliser is always better than factory fertiliser, or the other way round.** (Different groups of farmers and consumers; *debated*)  
  For: Organic manure and compost improve the soil's structure, hold water and feed soil life as well as the plant. Factory fertiliser is cheap, exact and quick, and feeds many people.  
  Against: A plant takes in the same simple minerals from either kind. Both can be misused. Too much of either can wash into rivers. Manure is slow and may not supply enough nitrogen for very high yields. Factory fertiliser alone, over many years, can leave the soil tired.
- **Trees in a forest use fungus networks to look after their own young.** (A popular idea from books and films, based partly on scientific work; *debated*)  
  For: Carbon has been shown to move between trees linked by fungi, and some seedlings near old trees grow better.  
  Against: Researchers who reviewed the studies in 2023 found that the evidence for many of these claims is weak. Moving a tiny amount of carbon does not show that trees help each other on purpose.
- **Carl Sprengel's idea of the shortest plank is Liebig's law.** (Most textbooks, which call it Liebig's law of the minimum; *debated*)  
  For: Justus von Liebig was famous, and he put the idea into his best-selling book of 1840.  
  Against: Carl Sprengel, a less famous German scientist, had written about the idea in 1828. Liebig took the idea and made it famous. Some scientists now say it should be called the Sprengel-Liebig law.

## Roots
We have found that half of soil is air, that roots breathe, and that plants trade with bacteria and fungi. Now we ask what this says about empty space and about who we depend on.

**Deep truth.** Water poured on dry soil pushed out bubbles of air. Between a third and a half of each soil was gaps. Roots grow into these gaps and breathe there. So the empty part of soil is as much the plant's home as the grains.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of soil as the solid stuff, and of the space between the grains as nothing. For a root, the gaps are where the air is, so the nothing is half of the home.  
*Evidence:* In the Soil Breath test, 100 millilitres of dry soil took in about 35 to 50 millilitres of water before the water reached the top, and bubbles of air rushed out. Packed soil took in less. A pot with all its gaps full of water drowns the roots. If your own numbers differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your lungs also work by having space inside them, full of air. Like soil, a body is partly grains and partly gaps, and the gaps are where the breathing happens.
- *what is life:* No living being works alone. A bean has bacteria in her roots, a tree has fungi, and the soil has millions of tiny helpers. Life is a web of trades.
- *what is this world:* The soil under our feet is half empty space, and that space is full of air and life. What looks solid and still is full of room and movement.

Soil looks like one solid thing. Pour water on it and half of it is revealed to be air. The roots live in the gaps, and the bacteria and fungi do too. The gaps are not left over. They are where the work happens. Empty space and solid matter turn out to be two parts of one working soil, just as the breathing and the body are two parts of one living plant. The plant also depends on partners they cannot see, who live in the same gaps. Take away the gaps, or the partners, and the plant cannot live.

- **Atharva Veda, Prithvi Sukta (12.1):** The Earth is called a mother, and the verse says, 'The Earth is my mother, and I am her child.' (A plant's body is built from air, water and a pinch of the Earth's minerals. We eat plants, so we, too, are built from the Earth's soil.)
- **Thirukkural 1037, Thiruvalluvar:** The Tamil poet Thiruvalluvar wrote that if the farmer ploughs so well that the soil dries down to a quarter of its weight, the crop will grow even without a handful of manure. (Ploughing opens the soil and lets air into the gaps. Thiruvalluvar saw, long before roots were studied, that air in the soil matters.)
- Science (settled): Between a third and a half of a good loose soil is gaps, and roots need the oxygen in them.
- Science (settled): Fungi on roots bring plants minerals and water, and bacteria in nodules fix nitrogen. Both trade with the plant for sugar.
- Science (debated): How much carbon and how many signals move between trees through fungus networks is still argued about.
- Science (open): Whether a cereal crop could be given nodules and fix its own nitrogen is unknown.
- Other view, Many soil physicists: The gaps in soil are a physical property called porosity, which can be measured. The gaps are not a home for anything. They are only empty volume between grains. Saying that the empty space is part of the plant's home is a way of speaking, and does not add to the science.
- Practice, Hold a handful of gaps (3 min): Hold a handful of dry soil in your cupped hands. Remember that nearly half of it is air. Breathe in slowly three times. Feel your chest make space for the air. Think of the roots doing the same.
- Ask: If half of soil is air, is the air part of the soil, or something that is only visiting?
- Ask: A bean and her root bacteria each need the other. Where does the bean end and the bacteria begin?
- Ask: What else looks solid but is really mostly gaps, and what lives in the gaps?

## Story: Rani on the Balcony
*Revathi, Paati, Appa, Salma Aunty and the plant Rani are made up. The science in the story is real. A plant in soaked soil can droop and yellow, and the roots rot from the bottom. Leaves that have turned yellow do not turn green again. The new leaves are the sign of recovery. A real plant may take longer to recover, or may not recover.*

Revathi lived with her family in a flat on the third floor in Chennai. The best part of the flat was Paati's balcony. Paati was Revathi's grandmother, and for forty years she had grown things in pots: a tulsi, a curry leaf, a jasmine, and a row of tiny green chillies. But her favourite was a brinjal plant in a big blue pot. She had grown the plant from a seed. She called her Rani, which means queen.

In the summer, Rani had given them eleven purple brinjals, shiny as polished stones. Paati had counted each one. She still talked about the sambar they made from the first one.

Then came October, and the northeast monsoon. The rain came sideways onto the balcony for days. And one morning, Revathi found Rani's leaves turning yellow, from the bottom up. By afternoon they were drooping, as if the plant were tired.

'She is thirsty,' said Paati at once. She filled a steel mug at the tap and gave Rani a good drink. In the evening, she gave her another.

The next day Rani was worse. Appa looked at her over his coffee. 'She is hungry,' he said. He went to the shop at the corner and came back with a packet of plant food. He measured two big spoons of it, double what the packet said, because Rani looked so unwell. 'Strong medicine for a strong queen,' he said.

By the third day, the edges of Rani's leaves had turned brown and crispy. Salma Aunty from the floor below came up to look. 'Too little sun,' she said. 'Move her to the corner.' Paati shook her head. 'She has always been in that spot.'

Revathi did not say anything. She was nine, and her lessons were at home, at the kitchen table. Last week, her mother had given her a clear glass of dry garden soil and a jug of water. 'Pour slowly,' Amma had said. 'Watch.' Revathi had poured, and silver bubbles had streamed out of the soil. 'That is air,' Amma had said. 'Soil is full of gaps, and the gaps are full of air. Roots need to breathe.' They had measured it. Nearly half of the soil had been air.

Now Revathi knelt on the wet balcony tiles and looked at Rani's pot. She pushed a finger into the soil. It was cold, and soaked right down to her knuckle. It had not rained on the pot for hours. Why was it so wet? She sniffed her finger. It smelled sour, like a drain.

She tried to lift the pot. It was heavy, and it sloshed. Under it, the saucer was full of green water, with a tiny mosquito wriggler going up and down. And the hole in the bottom of the pot, which was supposed to let the water out, was plugged tight with a flat piece of broken tile. Appa had put it there, months ago, to stop the soil falling out.

'Paati,' said Revathi. 'Rani is not thirsty.' Paati sighed. 'Kanna, look how she droops. Of course she wants water.'

'When I poured water on the dry soil,' said Revathi, 'bubbles of air came out. The soil has gaps, and the gaps are full of air, and the roots breathe it. Now look. Rani's pot has no gaps with air. They are all full of water. The roots cannot breathe. A plant droops from too little water, and also when the roots are drowning.'

'How do you know?' said Appa. It was a fair question. Revathi thought. 'We can look,' she said. 'If the roots are white and firm, I am wrong. If they are brown and soft, I am right.'

Together, they tipped Rani out onto newspaper. Appa held the pot and Paati held the stem. Revathi took a deep breath. The roots at the top were pale and strong. But the lower part of the root ball was dark brown and slimy, and it smelled terrible.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Paati said slowly, 'Forty years, I have kept plants. I always thought the roots only drank.'

'They drink and they breathe,' said Revathi. 'Through the same gaps.'

Appa trimmed the brown roots away with kitchen scissors, snip by snip, until only the white ones were left. Revathi mixed new soil in a bucket: garden soil, some sand and a few handfuls of coconut husk, so it would stay loose with plenty of gaps. They put it into a clean pot with three big holes in the bottom, and no tile. Under the pot they put two small bricks, so that the saucer would never touch the water. 'And no plant food for two weeks,' said Revathi. 'Drowned roots cannot take it in anyway.'

Then she did something Paati had never done in forty years. She pushed a finger into the soil, two knuckles deep. 'Dry here,' she said. 'Now we can water.' She gave Rani just half a mug.

Ten days later, a small green leaf, the colour of a new parrot, opened at the top of Rani's stem. The old yellow leaves stayed yellow, but Paati did not mind. She snipped them off and put them in the compost.

Salma Aunty came up to see. 'My tulsi is going yellow,' she said. 'Can you look at it, Revathi?'

Paati stood at the balcony with her arms folded. 'Check the finger,' she told her friend, 'not the clock.' She winked at her granddaughter.

**For the parent:** For the parent: roots are alive and breathe. They get oxygen from the air in the gaps between soil grains. When the gaps fill with water, the air is pushed out, and the roots drown. Drooping can mean too much water, and not only too little. In the story, Revathi's clue is the bubbles of air from the soil in a glass, and the proof is the brown, slimy roots. Strong fertiliser on drowned roots makes things worse, because roots with no oxygen cannot take in minerals.

- Ask: Why can a plant droop from too much water as well as from too little?
- Ask: How could you tell, without any machine, if a drooping plant is dry or drowning?
- Ask: Why would giving more fertiliser to a plant with drowned roots not help?
- Ask: Why does a pot need a hole, and why does a saucer full of water cause trouble?
- Ask: Soil looks solid. What shows you that it is full of gaps?

### e1 · Soil Breath: how much air is hiding in soil? (30 min)

### e2 · Seedlings Without Soil: do plants need minerals? (40 min, 18 days)
- **question:** A seedling can grow with no soil at all. Does a bean seedling need minerals in the water? And is a lot of fertiliser better than a little?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After two weeks, which jar will have the tallest, greenest seedlings? Which will have the smallest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three jars are the same size, hold the same amount of the same water, and get the same light. Each has three seedlings of the same age and kind. Only the fertiliser changes: none in jar A, a small dose in jar B and a big dose in jar C. So any difference comes from the fertiliser.
- **safety:** A grown-up reads the label and measures the fertiliser, because it can irritate eyes and skin. Keep it away from the mouth, and wash hands after touching the jars. Do not drink the water. Raw rajma seeds and sprouts are poisonous if eaten, so do not taste them, and cook none of this batch for food.
- **record:** On days 4, 8, 12 and 18, measure the tallest shoot in each jar, match the leaf colour to a crayon, and note any brown or soft roots. Draw the three jars at the end.
- **results table:** Day | Jar A (plain water): height and leaf colour | Jar B (a small dose): height and leaf colour | Jar C (a big dose): height and leaf colour
- **schedule:** day 1: Soak the seeds and wrap them in a damp cloth. Keep the cloth damp. (10 min); day 4: Make the three jars and set up the sprouts. Measure the starting height. (25 min); day 8: Measure the shoots and match the leaf colours. (5 min); day 12: Measure again and match the colours. Change the water. (5 min); day 18: Measure, check the roots, draw the jars and write the claim, evidence and reasoning. (15 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the seeds, the jars and the fertiliser. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will grow beans with their roots in water, with no soil. One jar has plain water. One has a little fertiliser. One has a lot. Which will grow best? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Beans in water, no soil. Plain, a little fertiliser, or a lot. Which grows best? Why?
  - Ask: Do you think a bean can grow in plain water? For how long? (good: Yes, because the seed has food stored.; Only for a few days.; No, because plants need soil.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A bean has a food store in the seed, so the seedling can grow for a while in plain water. The question is for how long.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Soak the rajma seeds for 6 hours. Wrap them in a damp cloth. Keep the cloth damp for 3 days, until each seed has a white root 2 to 3 centimetres long.  
  *Parent says:* Soak the seeds. Wrap them in a damp cloth. Keep the cloth damp. After three days, you will see white roots.  
  *Child mode:* Soak the beans. Wrap them in a damp cloth. Wait three days.
  - Note: Choose nine seeds with matching roots. Rinse the cloth once a day with clean water. If a seed turns soft or smells sour, throw that seed away. Make a few more than nine, in case some do not sprout.
  - Wait: Wait 3 days for the roots
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Fill each jar with 300 millilitres of water. A grown-up reads the fertiliser label. Add half the label amount to jar B. Add double the label amount to jar C. Leave jar A plain.  
  *Parent says:* Jar A gets plain water. Jar B gets half the dose on the label. Jar C gets double the dose. A grown-up measures. Mark the jars.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up measures the fertiliser. Mark the jars A, B and C.
  - Note: The labels say how much to use per litre or per 300 millilitres. Measure carefully, because the point of the test is the difference in dose. Tape the card over each jar and push the sprouts through, so that the seed rests on the card and only the roots reach the water. Leave a small air gap between the water and the card, because roots need air too. Top up with the same solution when the water drops.
  - Check: Why do we leave a small air gap between the water and the card? → **Roots in water still need air to breathe**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 4, 8 and 12, measure the tallest shoot in each jar and match the leaf colour. Write the results. Top up the water.  
  *Parent says:* Measure the tallest shoot in each jar. Match the leaf colour to a crayon. What is changing? What is the same?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the tallest shoot in each jar. Match the leaf colour.
  - Ask: Which jar is doing best so far? Which is doing worst? (good: Jar B is tallest and greenest.; Jar A is pale.; Jar C has brown tips.)
  - Note: Seeds have their own store, so the three jars may look alike for the first week or so. Differences usually appear after the second week. Change all the water and the solutions every 5 days to keep them fresh, with the same doses.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 18, measure and match the colours again. Check the roots. Draw the three jars. Write a claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Compare the three jars. Which seedlings look healthiest? What happened to the roots in jar C? What does this show?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the three jars. Check the roots. Which grew best?
  - Ask: Why might a big dose of fertiliser be worse than a small dose? (good: Strong fertiliser can burn the roots.; Too many salts make it hard for roots to take in water.; More is not always better.)
  - Note: If all three jars still look alike on day 18, the seeds' store may have lasted. Leave them a few more days, or repeat with smaller seeds such as moong. That is also a real result. If the dose in jar C was too weak to harm, a stronger dose would be needed. Do not make it much stronger for a child to use alone.
  - Check: Seedlings in plain water are pale and small after two weeks, though the seeds were healthy. What is the best explanation? → **Plain water has almost no minerals, and the seeds' store has run out**
  - Think: A plant in a field gets minerals from soil that has been rotting leaves for years. What does the soil give that plain water does not?
### e3 · Roots Need to Breathe: cuttings in boiled water with and without air (35 min, 10 days)
- **question:** A cutting grows new roots in water. Does the water need to have air in it? What happens if you take the air out of the water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After ten days, which jar will have more and longer roots: the one with air in the water, or the one with the air shut out? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both jars hold the same boiled and cooled water, the same number of cuttings of the same plant, in the same light. Boiling drives most of the dissolved air out of the water. In jar A, a thin layer of oil on top keeps new air from getting in. In jar B, there is no oil, and you stir the water each day so that air mixes in. Only the air in the water changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water and lets it cool completely before it is poured. Take care with oil, which makes floors slippery. Do not eat the cuttings from the test.
- **record:** On days 3, 6 and 10, count the new roots and measure the longest root in each jar. Draw one cutting from each jar.
- **results table:** Jar | Air in the water | Number of new roots at day 10 | Length of the longest root (centimetres) | Colour and feel of the roots
- **schedule:** day 1: A grown-up boils the water. Let it cool. Set up the two jars with cuttings, and add the oil to jar A. (27 min); day 3: Stir jar B. Count and measure the roots. (5 min); day 6: Stir jar B. Count and measure the roots. (5 min); day 10: Final count, measurement, drawing and the claim, evidence and reasoning. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the cuttings and the jars. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* Boiling water drives out its air. Oil on top keeps air out. Will roots grow well in water with no air? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Water with no air, and water with air. Where do roots grow best? Why?
  - Ask: Do you think roots need air? How could we find out? (good: Yes, because roots are alive and breathe.; We could grow roots with and without air.; No, because roots are in water, not air.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up boils 600 millilitres of water for 5 minutes. Put a lid on the pot and let it cool completely. This takes about 30 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up boils the water. Put a lid on the pot. Let the water cool all the way. Boiling drives out the air.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up boils the water. Let it cool with a lid on.
  - Note: Dissolved air comes out of water when it is heated. If you pour the water into the jars while it is still hot, you risk cracking glass and burning a child. Cool it to room temperature, then use it. Do not pour it from a great height, which mixes air back in.
  - Check: Why do we boil the water for this test? → **Boiling drives out the dissolved air**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put four cuttings in each jar. Pour in the cooled water gently. Add a thin layer of oil on the top of jar A. Leave both jars in the same bright place.  
  *Parent says:* Put four cuttings in each jar. Pour in the water gently. Add the oil to jar A only. Put both jars side by side.  
  *Child mode:* Four cuttings in each jar. Add oil to jar A only.
  - Note: Cut each cutting just below a joint, and remove the bottom leaves so that none sit in the water. Pour the water slowly down the side of the jar to keep air out. Stir jar B gently with a clean spoon each day for about 30 seconds, to let air in. Do not touch jar A.
  - Check: What is the oil layer for? → **It stops new air from dissolving into the water**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 3, 6 and 10, count the new roots and measure the longest in each jar. Stir jar B each day. Write the results.  
  *Parent says:* Count the new roots. Measure the longest. Compare the two jars. Do you see a difference?  
  *Child mode:* Count the roots in each jar. Measure the longest.
  - Ask: Which jar has more roots so far? Is that what you expected? (good: Jar B has more roots.; There is no difference yet.; Jar A has fewer and they look brown.)
  - Note: Roots may take 5 to 7 days to show. A cutting can carry some air down inside its stem, so the difference may be small. That is a real result. If the roots in jar A look brown or soft, or the water smells sour, that is a sign of too little air.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 10, make the final count and measure. Draw one cutting from each jar. Write the claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Which jar grew more and longer roots? What does that tell us about roots and air?  
  *Child mode:* Which jar has more roots? What does that tell you about air?
  - Ask: What might a rice plant or a lotus do that a mint cutting cannot? (good: They carry air down to their roots through channels in the stem.; They have air tubes.; They live in mud where there is no air.)
  - Check: Cuttings in water with air grew more roots than cuttings in water with the air kept out. What does this show? → **Roots grow better when they can get oxygen**
  - Think: A paddy plant grows in water for months. How might the plant get air to the roots?

   **Answer:** Old trees care for their own young on purpose. *Why:* The first three statements describe what was measured. The fourth claim says why the carbon moved. Moving a small amount of carbon does not show that a tree acts on purpose to help their young.
16. (truefalse) Roots grow evenly in all directions, whatever is in the soil.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Roots grow towards water and mineral-rich patches, branch more where food is plentiful, and bend away from stones and salt.
17. (mcq) Some scientists say roots think and choose. Others say roots only respond automatically. What do the two groups disagree about?  
   Options: What to call the roots' clever responses, not whether the roots respond / Whether roots grow at all / Whether roots need water / Whether roots exist  
   **Answer:** What to call the roots' clever responses, not whether the roots respond. *Why:* Both groups agree that roots sense and respond in sophisticated ways. They disagree about whether this deserves words such as thinking and choosing.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) About what share of a good, loose soil is gaps that hold air and water?  
   Options: Between a third and a half / Almost none / All of it / One in every hundred parts  
   **Answer:** Between a third and a half. *Why:* Soil is grains with gaps between them. In loose garden soil, the gaps can be nearly half of the volume. Packed soil has less.
2. (mcq) A plant in a pot droops. The soil is soaked and smells sour. What is the most likely problem?  
   Options: The roots are drowning for lack of air / The plant needs more water / The plant needs more fertiliser / The plant needs less light  
   **Answer:** The roots are drowning for lack of air. *Why:* Soaked soil has no air in its gaps. Roots without oxygen cannot work, and begin to rot, which gives the sour smell. More water or fertiliser would make this worse.
3. (mcq) Which three minerals do plants need in the largest amounts?  
   Options: Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium / Iron, zinc and copper / Sodium, chlorine and calcium / Carbon, oxygen and hydrogen  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. *Why:* Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are the three numbers on a bag of fertiliser. Iron, zinc and copper are needed in tiny amounts. Carbon, oxygen and hydrogen come from air and water.
4. (mcq) A soil has plenty of phosphorus and potassium but very little nitrogen. What limits the crop?  
   Options: Nitrogen, the shortest supply / Phosphorus, the most plentiful / Potassium, the most plentiful / Nothing limits it  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen, the shortest supply. *Why:* Growth is held back by the shortest supply. The plentiful minerals do not help until the shortest one is added.
5. (mcq) Plants grown with their roots in water that holds a few mineral salts, and no soil, grew well and made seeds. What did this show?  
   Options: Soil gives minerals, water and support, but the plant makes their own food / Plants eat soil / Plants do not need minerals / Plants need humus  
   **Answer:** Soil gives minerals, water and support, but the plant makes their own food. *Why:* The plants had no soil and no humus, and still grew. So the plant's food is made in the leaves. The soil supplies minerals, water and a place to hold on.
6. (mcq) What do bacteria in a bean's root nodules give the plant, and what do they get from the plant?  
   Options: They give nitrogen and get sugar / They give sugar and get nitrogen / They give water and get light / They give nothing and get a home  
   **Answer:** They give nitrogen and get sugar. *Why:* The bacteria fix nitrogen from the air into a form the plant can use. The plant gives them sugar from photosynthesis. Both sides gain.
7. (mcq) Why can a plant not use the nitrogen gas that makes up most of the air?  
   Options: Its atoms are locked together tightly, and it must be fixed first / It is too heavy / It is poisonous / It is the wrong colour  
   **Answer:** Its atoms are locked together tightly, and it must be fixed first. *Why:* The two atoms in a nitrogen molecule are joined by a very strong bond. Only bacteria, lightning and factories can break it and join the nitrogen to other atoms.
8. (mcq) What does a fungus on a tree's roots mainly bring to the tree?  
   Options: Water and minerals such as phosphorus, in return for sugar / Light / Carbon dioxide / Sugar, in return for water  
   **Answer:** Water and minerals such as phosphorus, in return for sugar. *Why:* Fungus threads reach farther into the soil than roots, and gather minerals and water. The tree pays with sugar from photosynthesis.
9. (truefalse) Scientists all agree that trees use fungus networks to care for their young on purpose.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Carbon does move between trees linked by fungi, but scientists disagree about how much, and whether the trees do it on purpose. This is still being tested.
10. (mcq) A day after a big dose of fertiliser, the leaf edges of a pot plant turn brown and crispy. What is the most likely cause?  
   Options: Fertiliser salts have burned the roots and leaf edges / The plant needs more fertiliser / The plant has too little water in the saucer / The plant needs less air  
   **Answer:** Fertiliser salts have burned the roots and leaf edges. *Why:* Strong fertiliser salts draw water out of roots and scorch the leaf edges. The fix is to flush the pot with plenty of water and use a smaller dose next time.
11. (mcq) Which statement about a plant's needs in the soil is correct?  
   Options: Roots need air as well as water / Roots need only water / Roots need only minerals / Roots need no air because they are underground  
   **Answer:** Roots need air as well as water. *Why:* Roots are alive and respire, so they need oxygen. The gaps in soil hold the air they breathe. Waterlogged soil has no air.
12. (mcq) In 1828, Carl Sprengel described the shortest plank idea, but it became known as Liebig's law. What does this show about science?  
   Options: Credit does not always go to the first person who found something / The first person always gets the credit / Only famous people can make discoveries / Ideas cannot be named after anyone  
   **Answer:** Credit does not always go to the first person who found something. *Why:* Liebig was famous and wrote a best-selling book, so his name stuck. Sprengel had the idea first. Looking back at who found what helps give credit where it is due.

## Applications
- Gardeners and farmers checking drainage, soil structure and air, as well as water.
- Farmers testing their soil to find the limiting mineral, and adding only what is needed.
- Growing dals and green manure plants in turns with rice, wheat and maize.
- Composting kitchen and farm waste to return minerals to soil.
- Hydroponic and rooftop farms growing vegetables in mineral water, with air pumped in.
- Making fertiliser from the nitrogen in the air, and using it carefully.
- Planting mangroves and paddy that survive in mud because of air channels in their roots.
- Adding helpful fungi and bacteria to the soil so that crops use less fertiliser.
- Reading leaf colours to spot a plant's problems early.
- Planning fields and pots so that water drains and roots can breathe.

## Open questions
- **How much food and water do trees really share through fungus networks?** Some studies find carbon moving between trees. Others say the amounts are too small to matter. More careful tests in real forests are needed.
- **Could wheat, rice and maize be given nodules that fix their own nitrogen?** Scientists are working on it, but a nodule needs many genes working together in the right order. Nobody has made it work in a cereal crop.
- **How many kinds of tiny living things live in a teaspoon of soil?** Most soil microbes have never been grown in a dish or given a name. New methods read their genes directly, and keep finding kinds that nobody knew.
- **How does a root tip combine so many signals, such as water, minerals, touch and gravity, into one decision?** Scientists know many of the signals and the chemicals involved. How the root tip puts them all together is still being worked out.
- **Did the first plants on land need fungi to survive?** Fossils about 400 million years old show fungi inside early plant roots. Whether the first land plants could have lived without them is not known.
- **How can farms keep high harvests while rebuilding soil and saving groundwater?** Many ideas exist, such as crop rotation, compost and less ploughing. Which mix works best in each place is still being tested.

## Connected maths
- A big pot holds 2,000 millilitres of loose garden soil. In the Soil Breath test, about 45 out of every 100 millilitres of that soil were gaps. About how many millilitres of the pot are gaps? **About 900 millilitres.**. 2,000 millilitres is 20 lots of 100 millilitres. 20 times 45 is 900. So about 900 millilitres of the pot are gaps, and 1,100 millilitres are grains. When the pot is waterlogged, those 900 millilitres are full of water instead of air. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- Loose soil took in 46 millilitres of water for every 100 millilitres of soil. Packed soil took in 34 millilitres. How many millilitres fewer was that? About what fraction of the loose soil's gaps was lost by packing? **12 millilitres fewer, about a quarter of the gaps.**. 46 take away 34 is 12. 12 out of 46 is a bit more than 12 out of 48, and 12 out of 48 is one quarter. So packing squeezed out about a quarter of the gaps. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the Pot That Never Drowns challenge, 300 millilitres of water were poured in and 190 millilitres drained into the tray. How many millilitres stayed in the soil? Is that more or less than a half of the water poured in? **110 millilitres stayed. That is less than half.**. 300 take away 190 is 110. Half of 300 is 150. 110 is less than 150, so less than half of the water stayed. The rest drained out, and left room for air. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- A 50-kilogram bag of fertiliser is labelled 20-10-10. The numbers are percentages of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. How many kilograms of each are in the bag? **10 kilograms of nitrogen, 5 kilograms of phosphorus and 5 kilograms of potassium.**. 20 percent of 50 is 10, because 50 divided by 5 is 10. 10 percent of 50 is 5, because 50 divided by 10 is 5. So the bag has 10, 5 and 5 kilograms. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A made-up price: Bag A is 50 kilograms of 20-10-10 fertiliser and costs 1,000 rupees. Bag B is 50 kilograms of 10-10-10 and costs 700 rupees. Which bag costs less for each kilogram of nitrogen? **Bag A. It costs 100 rupees for each kilogram of nitrogen, and bag B costs 140 rupees.**. Bag A holds 10 kilograms of nitrogen. 1,000 divided by 10 is 100 rupees for each kilogram. Bag B holds 5 kilograms of nitrogen. 700 divided by 5 is 140 rupees for each kilogram. A is cheaper for nitrogen, even though the whole bag costs more. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- In the Seedlings Without Soil test, the tallest shoot in jar B was 3 centimetres on day 4, 8 centimetres on day 8, 14 centimetres on day 12 and 22 centimetres on day 18. How much did it grow between each check? Between which two checks did it grow fastest each day? **It grew 5, 6 and 8 centimetres. It grew fastest each day between day 8 and day 12.**. 8 take away 3 is 5. 14 take away 8 is 6. 22 take away 14 is 8. The gaps are 4 days, 4 days and 6 days. 5 over 4 days is 1.25 centimetres a day. 6 over 4 days is 1.5. 8 over 6 days is about 1.3. So the fastest was between day 8 and day 12. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In the Rotation Plan challenge, a field starts with 5 nitrogen points. The farmer grows rice, which uses 2, then black gram, which adds 2, then maize, which uses 3. How many nitrogen points are left? Could she grow maize again straight away? **2 nitrogen points are left. She could not grow maize again, because it needs 3.**. 5 take away 2 is 3. 3 add 2 is 5. 5 take away 3 is 2. Maize uses 3, and there are only 2 left, so she needs a dal or fertiliser first. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- A fertiliser label says to use 5 millilitres for every litre of water. How many millilitres should go into a 300 millilitre jar for the full dose? What about for half of the dose, and for double the dose? **1.5 millilitres for the full dose, 0.75 for half and 3 for double.**. 300 millilitres is 0.3 of a litre. 5 times 0.3 is 1.5. Half of 1.5 is 0.75, and double 1.5 is 3. So the three jars get 0, 0.75 and 3 millilitres in the test, or in proportion. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L4`)
- In the Soil in a Jar activity, the layers were sand 3 centimetres, silt 2 centimetres and clay 1 centimetre. What fraction of the mineral layers is sand? Which layer is the thinnest, and what could that mean? **Half of the mineral layers are sand. Clay is the thinnest.**. The layers add up to 3 plus 2 plus 1, which is 6 centimetres. 3 out of 6 is one half. Clay is only 1 out of 6, so this soil has little clay. A soil with a lot of clay would hold water tightly and have smaller gaps. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
