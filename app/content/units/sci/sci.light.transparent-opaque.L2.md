# Transparent, translucent and opaque: why more layers let less light through
`sci.light.transparent-opaque.L2`

**Summary.** One sheet of paper over a drawing still lets you see it, but a few sheets hide it. Even then, a torch still glows through the pile. Your child sorts home things into three kinds: transparent things we can see through, translucent things that let light through but no clear picture, and opaque things that block the light. Then they count how many layers of polythene, tissue and paper it takes to hide a drawing. They find that each layer stops some of the light, so how thick a thing is matters as much as what it is made of.

**Intro.** *Hold one paper napkin up to a window, and light shines through it. Hold up a whole packet of napkins, and it looks dark. Where did the light go?* Some things let light through, and some things block it. But the same paper can do both. One sheet glows in the light. A thick pile looks dark. In this unit you find out which things let light through, and you count how many layers it takes to stop it.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what transparent, translucent and opaque mean, and give a home example of each.
1. By the end I can sort things into the three kinds by holding them up to the light.
2. By the end I can show, by counting layers in a fair test, that more layers of a material let less light through.
3. By the end I can explain that a pile of paper can hide a picture and still let light through.
4. By the end I can say that a thin slice of almost anything lets some light through, and a thick enough piece of anything stops it.
5. By the end I can explain why the deep sea is dark, although a glass of water is clear.

## Terms
- **Transparent**: A transparent material lets light go straight through it. You can see clearly through it. *e.g.* Window glass, clean water and a clear polythene bag are transparent.
- **Translucent**: A translucent material lets some light through, but you cannot see a clear picture through it. It glows when light is behind it. *e.g.* A paper napkin, a thin cotton dupatta and the skin of an onion are translucent.
- **Opaque**: An opaque material does not let light through. You cannot see anything through it, and it does not glow. *e.g.* A steel plate, a wooden door and a brick wall are opaque.
- **Layer**: A layer is one sheet or one thickness of something. Layers can be piled on top of each other. *e.g.* Ten paper squares piled up make ten layers.
- **Material**: A material is what a thing is made of. *e.g.* A tumbler can be made of glass, steel or plastic. Those are three materials.
- **Fair test**: A fair test is a test where we change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then we know what made the difference. *e.g.* In the Layer Test, we change only the material. The drawing, the light and the size of the squares stay the same.

## Explanations
### Three ways a material can treat light · *defined*
**Definition:** A transparent material lets light go straight through. A translucent material lets some light through, but spreads it in all directions, so you see no clear picture. An opaque material lets no light through.

Remember the three plates, where you held things up to the window? Glass and clear plastic showed you the window clearly. They are transparent. A napkin and an onion skin glowed, but you could not see the window through them. They are translucent. A steel plate was dark, with no glow at all. It is opaque.
- e.g. Transparent: a glass tumbler, a clear polythene bag, clean water.
- e.g. Translucent: a paper napkin, a dry onion skin, a thin cotton dupatta.
- e.g. Opaque: a steel plate, a wooden spoon, a brick wall.
### Each layer stops some of the light · *defined*
**Definition:** When light goes through a layer of a material, some of the light is stopped and the rest goes on. Each new layer stops some more. So many layers let through much less light than one.

Remember the Layer Test, where you piled squares on a drawing? One square of paper let you see the drawing. With every square you added, the drawing got fainter, until it was gone. Each layer sent some light back and soaked up some more. Only the rest went on to the next layer. Polythene stopped very little light, so the drawing still showed through many layers.
- e.g. In the Layer Test, the drawing got fainter with each paper square.
- e.g. One napkin glows at a window, but a whole packet of napkins looks dark.
- e.g. A thin cotton curtain lets light into a room. Two or three curtains, one behind the other, make the room much darker.
### Hidden is not the same as dark · *defined*
**Definition:** A pile of translucent layers can hide a picture completely and still let light through.

In the Layer Test, a few paper squares hid your drawing. Then you shone a torch behind the same pile, and it glowed. So light was still getting through. Each layer spreads the light out a bit more, so the picture gets mixed up. After a few layers, no picture is left, but some light still comes out.
- e.g. The paper pile that hid the drawing still glowed with the torch behind it.
- e.g. A closed bathroom window of frosted glass lets in daylight, but you cannot see out.
- e.g. Your eyelids let in a red glow when you face a bright window, but you see no picture.
### Thin enough, almost anything lets light through · *defined*
**Definition:** A thin enough slice of almost any material lets some light through. A thick enough piece of any material stops nearly all light.

A whole cucumber is opaque. A paper-thin slice of the same cucumber glows at the window. Gold hammered into very thin gold leaf lets greenish light through, but a gold bangle does not. Scientists who study rocks grind them into slices thinner than paper, and most rocks then let light through. So whether something is opaque often depends on how thick it is.
- e.g. A thin cucumber slice glows. A thick piece looks dark.
- e.g. Thin gold leaf lets green light through. A gold coin does not.
- e.g. Rock ground thinner than paper lets light through, so scientists can study it under a microscope.
### Why the deep sea is dark · *defined*
**Definition:** Water is transparent, but each metre of water stops a little light. Below about 1,000 metres, no sunlight reaches at all.

A glass of water is clear. But sunlight going down into the sea passes through more and more water. Each metre stops a little of the light. About 200 metres down, it is very dim. Below about 1,000 metres, there is no sunlight left, and the water is completely dark. Many animals live there, and lots of them make their own light.
- e.g. Water in a glass is clear.
- e.g. In the milk test, half a glass of cloudy water let the coin show, but a full glass hid it.
- e.g. Deep-sea fish such as anglerfish carry their own glowing lights.

## Mostly agreed
- Some materials are transparent, some are translucent and some are opaque. *How we know:* Anyone can hold glass, a napkin and a steel plate up to a window and see the difference.
- More layers of a material let less light through. *How we know:* In the Layer Test, the drawing got fainter with each square added. Light meters show the same thing very exactly.
- Thin slices of most rocks let light through. *How we know:* Since the 1850s, scientists have ground rocks into slices thinner than paper and looked at them with light shining through, under a microscope.
- Below about 1,000 metres in the sea, no sunlight reaches. *How we know:* Scientists have lowered light meters from ships and gone down in deep-sea vehicles, and measured the light fading away.

## How we found out
**William Nicol, a Scottish scientist; Henry Clifton Sorby, an English scientist; Florence Bascom, an American geologist, About 1830 to the 1890s.** For a long time, people thought rocks were simply opaque. Light could not get through them, so the only way to study a rock was to look at its outside. In the 1830s, William Nicol in Scotland found a way to grind fossil wood so thin that light shone through it. Around 1850, Henry Clifton Sorby, a scientist in Sheffield, England, did the same with rocks. He glued a chip of rock to glass and ground it down until it was thinner than paper. Held up to the light, most of the rock was not opaque at all. It glowed, and under a microscope he could see the tiny grains inside it. His method spread. In the 1890s, Florence Bascom, one of the first women geologists in the USA, became an expert at reading rock slices like these. Geologists all over the world, in India too, still study thin rock slices today.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a rock could only be studied from the outside, because rock is opaque. Sorby ground rocks thinner than paper and showed that most of them let light through.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Metal and rock can never let light through.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A coin, a bangle, a steel plate and a stone all block light completely.  
  Against: Gold leaf, hammered very thin, lets green light through. Rock ground thinner than paper lets light through too. They block light only when they are thick.
- **If you cannot see through something, no light is getting through.** (A common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: A thick pile of paper hides a drawing completely.  
  Against: In the Layer Test, the pile that hid the drawing still glowed with a torch behind it. Light was getting through, but scattered, so there was no picture.
- **Clear water lets all the light through, however deep it is.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A glass of water, a bucket of water and the shallow sea at a beach all look clear.  
  Against: The deep sea is completely dark below about 1,000 metres, because so much water above stops the sunlight. In the milk test, a full glass of cloudy water hid the coin, but half a glass let it show.

## Roots
We have piled up squares until a drawing disappeared, and watched a torch glow through the pile. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** One sheet of paper over your drawing still let you see it. A few more sheets hid it, but the torch still glowed through them. Each sheet stops some of the light and lets the rest pass on. So whether paper is translucent or opaque depends on how many sheets there are.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort materials into fixed groups, as if paper is always translucent and rock is always opaque. But the group depends on the piece: one sheet of paper glows, a thick notebook can be dark, and rock ground thinner than paper lets light through.  
*Evidence:* In the Layer Test, the drawing faded with every paper square until it was gone, yet the pile still glowed with a torch behind it. A thin cucumber slice glowed at the window, but a thick chunk was dark.

- *who am i:* Your eyelids are thin layers of skin. Facing a bright window with your eyes shut, you still see a warm glow, because some light gets through them.
- *what is life:* Seaweeds and corals that need sunlight live near the top of the sea, where the water above is thin enough to let light through. Deep down, many animals make their own light.
- *what is this world:* Light meets everything, and everything lets through a little or a lot of it. Whether light gets through depends on what a thing is made of and how thick it is.

One torch sends out light. Each layer it meets takes a share of the light and passes on the rest. So the light fades little by little, until there is none left to pass on. Paper, water, a leaf and a rock all meet the same light, each in its own measure. Everything is aware in its own way, the paper and the water too. Many scientists disagree, and say only living beings with nerves are aware.

- **Bhagavad Gita 3.38:** As fire is covered by smoke, as a mirror is covered by dust, and as a baby is covered by the womb, so wisdom is covered by desire. (The Gita gives three coverings, from thin to thick. Smoke dims a fire, but its light still shows. Dust hides a mirror's picture. The womb hides the baby completely. In your Layer Test, too, each layer covered a little more.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra, chapter 8 (Jain):** Jain thinkers teach that every soul can know all things, but kinds of karma cover that knowledge. The first kind they name is jnanavaraniya, which means 'knowledge-covering'. As the covering thins, more knowledge shines through. (In your Layer Test, taking squares off the pile let more light through. The Jain teaching uses the same picture of layers for how much we can know.)
- Science (settled): Each layer of a material stops some of the light that reaches it, so light fades bit by bit through thicker pieces. Below about 1,000 metres, the sea has no sunlight at all.
- Science (settled): Most rocks let light through when they are ground thinner than paper, and very thin gold leaf lets green light through.
- Other view, The Buddha, in the Phena Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 22.95): The Buddha compared what we call a self to the trunk of a banana plant. Someone cuts it open looking for a hard core and peels away layer after layer, but finds no core inside. In this view, there is no hidden self under the layers waiting to shine through. There are only the layers.
- Practice, Light through your eyelids (3 min): Sit facing a bright window, but never look at the Sun. Close your eyes gently. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out slowly, three times. What colour do you see with your eyes shut? Now cup your hands over your closed eyes. What happened to the glow? Take your hands away slowly. Where does the glow come from?
- Ask: Your eyelids let light in even when they are shut. Is the glow you see inside you, or outside you?
- Ask: The light faded layer by layer in your test. Was there ever one moment when it stopped?
- Ask: If everything lets through a little light when it is thin enough, is anything truly opaque?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* One tissue lets light through, and a whole roll of tissue does not. At which layer does the roll become opaque? (hint: Did the light stop all at once in your test, or fade bit by bit? Is there one exact layer?)
- *deep:* When light runs out inside a pile of paper, where does it go? (hint: Look at the front of the pile when the torch is behind it. Does some light come back towards the torch? Does the paper warm up a tiny bit?)
- *big:* Your eyelids let in a red glow even when they are shut. Are you ever in complete darkness? (hint: Try it in a bright room and in a dark room at night. When is there no glow at all?)
- *big:* Fish deep in the sea live where sunlight never reaches. Is their world dark, or lit in a different way? (hint: Many deep-sea animals make their own light. Who would you see if you went down there?)

## Story: The Dosa That Let the Light In
*Ajji means grandmother in Kannada and Tulu. Neer dosa is a very thin rice dosa from the coast of Karnataka. Mangaluru is a city by the sea in Karnataka. Nila, Varun, Amma and Ajji are made up.*

Nila was seven. She lived on the sixth floor of a block of flats in Mangaluru, with her Amma and her Ajji. That week her cousin Varun was staying too. He was five, and he asked a question about everything.

It was a rainy Sunday in July. The rain drummed on the kitchen window. Ajji was making neer dosa for breakfast. Neer means water, and the batter was thin and white, like milk. Ajji poured it on to the hot pan and swirled it round. In a moment it set into a dosa so thin that it was full of tiny holes, like lace.

Ajji lifted the first dosa off the pan and put it on a steel plate. Nila picked it up by one edge and held it up to the window. "Look, Varun!" she said. "The light is coming through it."

The dosa glowed. Nila could see the bright square of the window behind it, but only as a pale patch. She could not see the rain or the coconut tree outside.

"It's see-through, like glass!" said Varun.

"Not quite," said Ajji, turning the next dosa. "Through glass, you can see the coconut tree. Can you see the tree through the dosa?"

Varun squinted. "No. Only light."

"Then the dosa is translucent," said Ajji. "It lets light through, but not a clear picture. The window glass is transparent. You see straight through it."

Ajji folded the dosa in half, and then in half again. Now it was four layers thick. Nila held it up to the window again. It still glowed, but it was much dimmer.

Varun ran to get his steel tumbler. He held it up to the window. "This one is dark!" he said. "No light at all."

"The steel is opaque," said Ajji. "It does not let light through."

By now there was a pile of folded dosas on the plate, six of them, one on top of another. Nila lifted the whole pile carefully and held it up. It was very dark. Almost no glow came through.

Nila frowned. "So is a dosa translucent, or opaque?"

"What do you think?" asked Ajji.

Nila thought about it. One dosa glowed brightly. Four layers glowed less. Six folded dosas, which made many layers, did not glow at all. "It depends how many there are," she said slowly. "Every layer stops some of the light. If there are enough layers, all the light is stopped."

Ajji smiled. "That is true for almost everything."

After breakfast the rain stopped, and the sun came out over the sea. From the balcony they could see the Arabian Sea, grey-blue and wide.

"When I was a girl," said Ajji, "I swam at Panambur beach. When I scooped the sea water up in my hands, it was as clear as tap water. But if I stood where the water came up to my chest, I could not see my own feet."

"Why not?" asked Varun.

"Think of the dosas," said Nila. "A little water is like one dosa. Lots of water is like the whole pile."

"Yes," said Ajji. "The sea also carries sand and tiny living things, which make it cloudy. Far out, where the sea is very deep, there is so much water above that no sunlight reaches the bottom at all. It is dark down there, all day and all night. Many fish and other animals there make their own light, to see and to be seen."

Varun's eyes went wide. "I want to see a fish with a light!"

That night, the power went off. Amma lit a small torch and gave it to Nila. Nila pulled her thin cotton bedsheet over her head and shone the torch. The sheet glowed all around her like a tent.

"Varun," she whispered, "this sheet is one layer. Get me the thick quilt."

Varun dragged the quilt over. Nila pulled it over the sheet. Now the glow was gone. It was dark and warm under there, like the bottom of the sea.

"Now we're deep-sea fish," said Varun, and switched on his own little light, which was the torch.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Nila works out that whether a dosa is translucent or opaque depends on how many layers there are. Each layer stops some of the light, and enough layers stop it all. Ajji then shows that the same is true of sea water: a handful is clear, but deep water is dark.

- Ask: Why could Nila see light through one dosa, but not the coconut tree?
- Ask: What happened to the glow when Ajji folded the dosa into four layers?
- Ask: Why could Ajji see the water in her hands clearly, but not her feet in the sea?
- Ask: Under the bedsheet the torch glowed, but under the quilt it was dark. Why?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Layer Test: how many layers hide the drawing? (25 min)
- **question:** How many layers of clear polythene, tissue and white paper does it take to hide a drawing? When the drawing is hidden, does light still get through?
- **predict:** Which material will hide the drawing with the fewest layers? Which will need the most? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same drawing, the same window light and squares of the same size every time. We add one layer at a time and look from the same place. We change only the material.
- **safety:** Don't shine the torch into anyone's eyes. Keep plastic bags and squares away from babies and toddlers.
- **record:** Fill in the table (a grown-up can write the words, and your child writes the numbers). Then draw a tower of squares for each material, as tall as the number of layers it took.
- **results table:** Material | My guess: how many layers to hide the drawing? | How many layers it really took | With the torch behind that pile: glow or dark?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws a sun with a thick black sketch pen on white paper. 2. You cut ten squares of each material. 3. Your child holds one square of each up to the window.  
  *Parent says:* Here are three materials: clear polythene, tissue and white paper. Hold one square of each up to the window. What do you see through each one?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a big, thick sun. Hold one square of each up to the window. What can you see?
  - Note: Never let your child look at the Sun itself, even through the squares. A window with daylight, or a bright tube light, is enough.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess how many layers of each material will hide the sun. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will pile squares on the sun, one at a time. How many layers of polythene will hide it? How many of tissue, and of paper? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many layers of each will hide the sun. Why?
  - Ask: Why did you guess that the paper will need fewer layers than the polythene? (good: Paper is thicker.; I can see through polythene, but not really through paper.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Write it down and don't say what will happen.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the drawing on a table near the window. 2. Your child lays polythene squares on it one at a time, flat. 3. After each one, they look from where they sit. 4. Count the layers until the sun is gone. 5. Do the same with tissue, then paper. 6. Last, lay the steel plate on it.  
  *Parent says:* Lay one square flat on the sun. Can you still see it? Add another. Keep counting. Tell me the moment the sun is gone.  
  *Child mode:* Add one square at a time. Count them. Stop when the sun is gone.
  - Ask: What happened to the sun as you added paper squares? (good: It got fainter and fainter.; It went grey, then it was gone.)
  - Note: Paper usually hides a thick drawing in about 3 to 6 layers. Tissue often needs more, because each layer is thinner. The sun usually still shows through all 10 polythene squares, a little greyer. If a material still shows the sun at 10, write 'more than 10'. Your numbers may be different from these. What matters is what you see.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the curtains or go to a dim room. 2. Lay the torch on the table with its light pointing up. A phone lies face down, with its torch pointing up. 3. Switch it on. 4. Lay the paper pile that hid the sun over the light, and look down at it. 5. Do the same with the tissue pile and the steel plate.  
  *Parent says:* This paper pile hid the sun completely. Now I put the torch behind it. Do you think any light will get through? Why?  
  *Child mode:* The paper pile hid the sun. Will the torch glow through it?
  - Ask: Did the paper pile glow? (good: Yes, it glowed.; Light came through, but I still can't see the sun.)
  - Ask: Did the steel plate glow? (good: No. It stayed dark.; Only the edges were bright, around the plate.)
  - Note: The paper and tissue piles glow, even though they hid the drawing. The steel plate gives no glow. If you have time, try a whole closed notebook over the torch. A thick notebook often stops the glow, or lets only a very faint glow through.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which material hid the sun with the fewest layers? The paper pile hid the sun, but did it stop all the light? What does each layer do to the light?  
  *Child mode:* Which hid the sun fastest? Did the paper pile stop all the light? How do you know?
  - Ask: What does each layer do to the light? (good: It stops a little bit.; Some light goes through, and some is stopped.)
  - Ask: Is the pile of paper translucent or opaque? (good: Translucent, because the torch glowed through it.; It hides the picture, but light gets through, so translucent.)
  - Check: Malini, a girl in Chennai, piles 5 paper squares on her drawing. The drawing is hidden. Then she shines a torch behind the pile. What will she see? → **The pile glows**

## Applications
- Curtain makers sell thick or double-layered curtains, called blackout curtains, to keep a bedroom dark in the day.
- Scientists who study rocks grind them into slices thinner than paper, so light can shine through and they can study the rock under a microscope.
- A pulse oximeter, the small clip a doctor puts on your finger, shines light through your fingertip. The light that gets through tells it how much oxygen your blood carries.
- Paper lanterns and lamp shades use thin paper or cloth, so the light glows through softly.
- Seaweeds and corals that need sunlight live in the upper part of the sea, where enough light still reaches.

## Open questions
- **How little light can deep-sea animals see?** Below about 1,000 metres, sunlight is too faint for most eyes. But some deep-sea fish have very sensitive eyes. Scientists are still finding out how faint a light they can sense.
- **Could wood be made see-through and used for windows?** Scientists have made thin wood that lets light through, by taking out part of the wood and filling it with clear plastic. Nobody knows yet if it can be made cheap and strong enough to replace glass in homes.
- **Is there anything that no thickness can stop?** Tiny particles called neutrinos pass right through the whole Earth, almost always without being stopped. Scientists are still finding out what neutrinos are like, for example exactly how heavy they are.

## Connected maths
- In the Layer Test, Harini's paper hid the sun at 4 layers. Her tissue hid it at 9 layers. How many more tissue layers did she need? **5 more.**. Count on from 4 to 9: 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. That is 5. So tissue needed 5 more layers than paper. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)

- A napkin glows at the window. Is it transparent, translucent or opaque? → Translucent.
- Is a steel plate transparent, translucent or opaque? → Opaque.
- Do more layers let more light through, or less? → Less.
- Can a pile of paper hide a picture and still glow? → Yes.
- Why is the deep sea dark? → So much water above stops all the sunlight.

- Light is attenuated exponentially with thickness in an absorbing or scattering medium (the Beer-Lambert law for absorption). In clear ocean water, the sunlit zone reaches about 200 metres and essentially no sunlight remains below about 1,000 metres.
- Thin sections for petrography are ground to about 0.03 mm. William Nicol made thin sections of fossil wood around 1830. Henry Clifton Sorby applied the method to rocks from about 1849 and published 'On the microscopical structure of crystals' in 1858.
- Florence Bascom (1862 to 1945) earned a PhD at Johns Hopkins in 1893 on old volcanic rocks of South Mountain, Pennsylvania, studied in thin section, and became the first woman geologist hired by the US Geological Survey in 1896.
- Michael Faraday, Bakerian Lecture 1857, 'Experimental Relations of Gold (and other Metals) to Light': thin gold leaf transmits green light.
- Milk is about 87 percent water. Its fat globules and casein micelles scatter light, which makes it white and opaque.
- Pulse oximeters measure the red and infrared light transmitted through a fingertip.
- Transparent wood: lignin removed and replaced with a clear polymer (Li and colleagues, KTH, 2016; Zhu and colleagues, University of Maryland, 2016).
