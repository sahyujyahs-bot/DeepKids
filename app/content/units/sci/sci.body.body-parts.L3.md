# The outside of your body: what each part does and how to care for it
`sci.body.body-parts.L3`

**Summary.** Your two thumbs grew from the same genes, yet their fingerprints are different. Your skin, hair, nails, eyes, ears, nose, mouth, hands, feet and private parts are external organs: body parts you can see, each with its own job. Some of their guards, such as earwax and nose mucus, look like dirt but protect you. The top layer of your skin is dead cells, and new skin keeps growing underneath to replace it. Each part needs its own care, and washing hands with soap is one of the most important habits of all.

**Intro.** *Rub your thumb on the side of your nose, to pick up a little oil. Press it onto a clean glass or a steel tumbler. Now breathe on that spot. A swirly pattern appears. That is your thumbprint. Nobody has ever been found with the same one, not even an identical twin.* Your skin left that mark without you trying. Sweat comes out of tiny holes along the ridges of your fingertips. Your fingers also pick up a little oil from your face and hair. So everything you touch keeps a faint copy of your ridges. Every outside part of your body has a job like this. Some parts have guards that most people think are dirt. And every part needs its own kind of care.

## Terms
- **Organ**: A part of the body that does a special job. *e.g.* Your eyes are organs for seeing. Your skin is an organ that covers and protects you.
- **External organ**: An organ on the outside of the body, which you can see. *e.g.* Your nose, ears, hands and skin are external organs.
- **Internal organ**: An organ inside the body, which you cannot see. *e.g.* Your heart and lungs are internal organs. They are inside your chest.
- **Skin**: The stretchy covering of the whole body. It is the biggest organ you have. *e.g.* Skin keeps water in and dirt out. It lets you feel a hot cup.
- **Cell**: The tiny living building block that bodies are made of. It is far too small to see with your eyes. *e.g.* Your skin is made of millions of cells, packed together like bricks in a wall.
- **Keratin**: A tough material that hair, nails and the top layer of skin are made of. *e.g.* Your nails, a cow's horns and a bird's feathers are all made of keratin.
- **Pores**: Tiny holes in the skin. Sweat comes out through them. *e.g.* On a hot day, sweat comes out of your pores and makes your forehead wet.
- **Sweat**: Salty water that comes out of your skin. It cools you down as it dries. *e.g.* After playing in the sun, your shirt gets wet with sweat.
- **Eyelid and eyelashes**: The eyelid is the flap of skin that closes over the eye. Eyelashes are the short hairs on its edge. They keep dust out. *e.g.* When dust blows, you blink and your lashes catch it.
- **Tears**: Salty water that keeps your eyes wet and washes dust away. *e.g.* When smoke gets in your eyes, they fill with tears.
- **Earwax**: A sticky wax made inside the ear, usually yellow-brown. It traps dust and tiny germs, and slowly moves out by itself. *e.g.* A little wax at the opening of your ear is normal. Wipe it with a cloth.
- **Eardrum**: A thin skin stretched across the inside of the ear. Sound makes it shake, so you can hear. *e.g.* A cotton bud or a hairpin pushed into the ear can tear the eardrum.
- **Mucus**: The slippery, sticky liquid inside your nose. It traps dust and germs. *e.g.* When you have a cold, your nose makes extra mucus.
- **Enamel**: The hard, shiny outside layer of a tooth. It is the hardest material in your body. *e.g.* Enamel lets your teeth crunch a raw carrot or a peanut.
- **Milk teeth**: Your first set of teeth. There are 20 of them. They fall out one by one, and bigger teeth grow in their place. *e.g.* A wobbly front tooth at age 6 or 7 is usually a milk tooth getting ready to fall out.
- **Fingerprint**: The pattern of tiny ridges on the tip of each finger. Every finger has its own pattern, and no two people have been found to share one. *e.g.* Your thumb leaves a fingerprint on a shiny steel tumbler.
- **Genes**: Instructions inside almost every cell of your body, passed down from your parents. They guide how your body grows. *e.g.* Genes help decide the colour of your eyes and whether your hair is curly or straight.
- **Germs**: Tiny living things, too small to see, that can make us ill. *e.g.* Germs on dirty hands can get onto food, and then into your tummy.
- **Soap**: A cleaner that grabs onto oil and grease. Water then washes the oil, and the germs stuck in it, away. *e.g.* Plain water does not wash oily hands clean. Soap does.
- **Hygiene**: The habits that keep our body clean, so that we stay healthy. *e.g.* Washing hands before eating is good hygiene.
- **Private parts**: The parts of the body covered by underwear. They are used to pass urine and stool. They are private: they belong to you. *e.g.* Boys have a penis. Girls have a vulva. These are their correct names, like elbow or knee.
- **Trusted adult**: A grown-up you know well, who listens to you and keeps you safe. *e.g.* A parent, a grandparent, an aunt or a teacher can be a trusted adult.
- **Lice**: Tiny insects that live in hair and lay eggs called nits. They crawl from head to head. *e.g.* Lice make your head itchy. A fine comb and special shampoo get rid of them.

## Explanations
### External organs and internal organs · *defined*
**Definition:** An organ is a body part with its own job. External organs are on the outside of the body, where we can see them. Internal organs are inside.

Look in a mirror. Everything you can see is on the outside of you. Your skin, hair, nails, eyes, ears, nose, mouth, teeth, hands, feet and private parts are your external organs. Each one does a job. Under your skin are the internal organs, like the heart, lungs, stomach and brain. They have their own unit. The outside parts and the inside parts work together all the time. Your nose takes in air, and your lungs inside use it.
- e.g. Your eyes see a ball coming. Your hands catch it. Your feet run to it.
- e.g. Your nose smells food cooking. Your mouth starts to make spit, ready to eat.
- e.g. Your skin feels cold. You put on a sweater.
### Skin: the biggest organ · *defined*
**Definition:** Skin is the stretchy covering of the whole body. It is the biggest organ of the body.

Spread out flat, a grown-up's skin would cover about one door. Skin has many jobs. It keeps water inside you, so you do not dry up. It keeps dirt and most germs out. It lets you feel touch, heat, cold and pain. When you are hot, sweat comes out through tiny holes called pores, and you cool down as it dries. In sunlight, skin also makes vitamin D, which your bones need. To care for skin, bathe every day, dry the folds well, and cover cuts. Stay in the shade in the hottest part of the day.
- e.g. A bandage on a cut keeps dirt out while the skin mends itself.
- e.g. Your fingertips can feel a single grain of rice in your palm with your eyes shut.
- e.g. After a bath, drying between your toes and under your arms stops itchy rashes.
### Your outside is always renewing · *defined*
**Definition:** The top layer of skin is made of flat, dead cells. New cells are always being made underneath. They push up, and the old ones flake off.

You cannot feel it, but you are shedding skin all the time. Tiny flakes fall off every day. Some of the dust in a house is made of these flakes. New skin is always growing underneath, so the outer layer is replaced about every month or two. This is why a scraped knee heals and a sunburn peels. Bathing washes away the old flakes, with sweat and dirt. Scrubbing hard does not help. It can hurt the new skin underneath.
- e.g. Clear sticky tape pressed on your arm picks up tiny white flakes. Those are dead skin cells.
- e.g. A scraped knee grows new skin over a week or two.
- e.g. After a sunburn, the top skin peels off in sheets.
### Hair and nails: dead, yet growing · *defined*
**Definition:** Hair and nails are made of keratin, a tough material made by living cells. The part you can see is dead. Only the root, under the skin, is alive.

Why does a haircut not hurt? Because the hair you can see has no nerves. Living cells at the root, under the skin, keep making new hair and push the old hair out. Hair grows about 1 centimetre a month. Fingernails grow about 3 millimetres a month. Toenails grow about half as fast. Hair keeps your head warm and shades it from the sun. Eyebrows stop sweat dripping into your eyes. Nails protect your fingertips and help you pick up small things. To care for them, wash and comb your hair. Cut nails short and straight, and keep them clean, because dirt and germs hide under long nails.
- e.g. Your hair grows back after a haircut, but the cut never hurts.
- e.g. A cut fingernail grows long again in a few weeks.
- e.g. Biting nails puts the germs from under them straight into your mouth.
### Eyes and their guards · *defined*
**Definition:** The eyes are the organs of sight. Eyelids, eyelashes, eyebrows and tears are their guards.

Your eyes are soft and easy to hurt, so they have many guards. Your eyebrows stop sweat and rain running into them. Your eyelashes catch dust. Your eyelids blink shut in a flash when something comes close. Each blink spreads tears over the eye, keeping it wet and washing dust away. To care for your eyes, never rub them hard. If something gets in, blink and wash with clean water. Read in good light, and look far away often. Never look at the sun, not even for a moment.
- e.g. When a ball flies at your face, your eyes shut before you decide to shut them.
- e.g. Onion juice in the air makes your eyes fill with tears.
- e.g. Playing outdoors and looking far away is good for children's eyes.
### Ears and the wax that cleans them · *defined*
**Definition:** The ears are the organs of hearing. Earwax traps dust and germs in the ear canal, and slowly carries them out.

The outer ear is shaped like a funnel to catch sounds. Sounds travel down a short tube, the ear canal, to the eardrum. The eardrum is a thin skin, a little like the skin of a drum. Sound makes it shake, and that is how you hear. The ear canal makes wax. The wax traps dust and tiny germs. Then it slowly moves outwards by itself, helped by your jaw moving when you chew and talk. So ears mostly clean themselves. Doctors say never put cotton buds, hairpins or matchsticks into your ears. They push wax deeper and can tear the eardrum. Don't pour oil in either, unless a doctor tells you to. Just wipe the outside with a cloth. Keep away from very loud sounds.
- e.g. A little wax at the opening of the ear is normal. Wipe it off with a towel after a bath.
- e.g. Chewing a dosa moves your jaw, which helps the wax move out.
- e.g. Loud music through earphones for a long time can hurt hearing.
### Nose: air, smell and a dust trap · *defined*
**Definition:** The nose is the organ for breathing in air and for smelling. Hairs and sticky mucus inside it trap dust and germs.

Air comes into your body through your nose. Inside, short hairs and sticky mucus catch dust, pollen and germs, so less of it gets deep inside you. The nose also warms and wets the air. And it smells. Smell tells you when food is cooking or when milk has gone bad. To care for your nose, blow it gently into a handkerchief or tissue, one side at a time. Do not pick it, because fingers carry germs in. When you sneeze, sneeze into your elbow or a handkerchief, so your germs don't fly onto other people.
- e.g. After a dusty day, the mucus you blow out is dark. Your nose caught that dust.
- e.g. When you have a cold, food tastes dull, because you can't smell it well.
- e.g. Sneezing into your elbow keeps germs off your hands.
### Mouth and teeth · *defined*
**Definition:** The mouth takes in food and water. Teeth bite and chew food. The tongue tastes, moves food and helps us talk.

Children have 20 milk teeth. From about age 6, they fall out one by one, and bigger teeth grow in. A full grown-up set has 32 teeth. Front teeth cut and bite. Back teeth grind food, like a grinding stone. Teeth are covered in enamel, the hardest material in your body. But tiny germs in your mouth feed on sugar and make acid. The acid slowly eats holes in the enamel. To care for your teeth, brush twice a day, for about two minutes. Brushing at night matters most, because your mouth makes less spit while you sleep. Spit helps protect teeth. Rinse your mouth with water after eating, and keep sweet sticky food for mealtimes.
- e.g. A toffee stuck between your teeth feeds mouth germs for hours.
- e.g. Front teeth cut a guava. Back teeth grind it.
- e.g. Saying 'ta ta ta' needs your tongue to tap behind your top teeth.
### Hands, fingertips and handwashing · *defined*
**Definition:** The hands hold, feel and make things. Fingertips have ridges that make our fingerprints. Hands touch everything, so they carry germs.

Your thumb can turn and touch the tip of every finger. This lets you hold a pencil, a needle or a single grain of rice. Your fingertips are packed with nerves, so they feel very well. On their skin are tiny curving ridges. Sweat comes out of tiny holes along the ridges, and your fingers pick up oil from your face and hair. So you leave fingerprints on what you touch. Hands also pick up germs from everything they touch. So wash them with soap before eating, after the toilet, after playing outside and after touching animals. Rub every part for at least 20 seconds, then rinse. Hands carry a thin layer of oil and grease, and germs stick in it. Soap grabs that oil, and water carries it away.
- e.g. You leave fingerprints on a glass, a mirror and a phone screen.
- e.g. Germs from a door handle can get onto your idli if you don't wash your hands.
- e.g. A blind reader feels the raised dots of Braille with the fingertips.
### Feet · *defined*
**Definition:** The feet carry the whole weight of the body. They help us stand, balance, walk and run.

The bottom of each foot curves up a little in the middle. This curve is called the arch. It works like a spring when you walk and run. Feet sweat a lot, especially inside shoes. Wet, warm skin between the toes can get itchy rashes. Some tiny worms in damp soil can get in through bare skin. To care for your feet, wash them every day and dry well between the toes. Wear chappals or shoes outside, especially near drains or wet soil. Cut toenails straight across.
- e.g. A wet footprint on a dry floor shows the arch. The middle of the print is thin.
- e.g. Shoes worn all day without socks often smell, because of sweat and germs.
- e.g. Chappals protect your feet from hot roads, glass and germs in mud.
### Private parts: clean, private and yours · *defined*
**Definition:** Private parts are the parts of the body covered by underwear. Boys have a penis and girls have a vulva. Both have a bottom, with an opening called the anus. They are used to pass urine and stool.

These parts have correct names, just like elbow or knee. Using the right names is not rude. It helps you tell a doctor or a grown-up clearly if something hurts. To keep them clean, wash them every day when you bathe. After the toilet, clean yourself with water or toilet paper, from front to back. Then wash your hands with soap. Wear clean, dry underwear every day. Private parts are also private. Your body belongs to you. Nobody should touch your private parts, or ask to see them, or ask you to touch theirs. The only exceptions are a parent helping to keep you clean, or a doctor checking you, with your trusted grown-up there. If any touch feels wrong or confusing, it is never your fault. Say no, get away and tell a trusted adult. Secrets about touching should never be kept.
- e.g. A doctor checks a child's tummy pain while the child's mother stays in the room.
- e.g. A child tells their aunt about a touch that felt wrong, and the aunt listens and helps.
- e.g. A clean pair of underwear every morning stops itching and rashes.
### Little guards that look like dirt · *defined*
**Definition:** Earwax, nose mucus, eyelashes, eyebrows and tears are guards. They trap dirt, wash it away or keep it out.

Many people think earwax and mucus are just dirt to dig out. In fact, they are traps. Dust and germs stick to them, and the body moves them out. Eyelashes and eyebrows look like decoration, but they guard the eyes. Even the tiny ridges on your fingertips may help you grip and feel. Good care means helping the guards, not fighting them. Wipe the outside. Never poke in.
- e.g. Earwax moves out of the ear by itself, like a slow conveyor belt.
- e.g. Mucus traps dust, and you blow it out.
- e.g. Tears wash a speck of dust to the corner of your eye.
### What are fingertip ridges for? · *debated*
Everyone agrees that fingertips have ridges, and that every person's pattern is different, even an identical twin's. Each of your ten fingers has its own pattern too, so even your two thumbs differ. The ridges form before you are born, between about 10 and 16 weeks in the womb. They grow from a deeper layer of skin, so after a scrape the same pattern grows back. But what are the ridges for? Scientists still argue.
- e.g. On a wet steel plate, your fingertips can still feel and grip.
- e.g. Running your fingertip over cloth, you can tell cotton from silk with your eyes shut.
- **Grip idea:** Many people thought ridges help us grip, like the grooves on a tyre. In 2009, a test in Britain found that on smooth, dry surfaces, ridges actually grip a little less. They may still help grip on rough or wet things, by letting water escape.
- **Feeling idea:** In 2009, scientists in France built a fake fingertip with ridges. When it slid over a surface, the ridges made tiny shakes that helped it sense fine textures. So ridges may help us feel.
- **Both, or more:** Some scientists think ridges do several jobs at once. Some think they help skin stretch without tearing. Nobody is sure yet.
### Can we be too clean? · *debated*
Washing hands with soap stops many illnesses. Everyone agrees on that. But millions of tiny living things, too small to see, live on our skin all the time, and many of them help us. Some scientists ask whether children who meet very few germs grow up with more allergies. This is still being studied.
- e.g. Washing hands before eating stops many tummy illnesses. Doctors agree on this.
- e.g. Children who grow up on farms, around animals and soil, seem to get some allergies less often.
- **David Strachan, a British doctor, 1989:** He noticed that children with more older brothers and sisters got hay fever less often. He wondered if catching more germs early helped. This became known as the hygiene hypothesis.
- **Graham Rook, a British scientist, 2003:** He argued that what matters is not catching colds, but meeting the 'old friends': harmless microbes from soil, animals and other people that humans have always lived with. Washing hands before eating does not take these away.
- **Most doctors today:** Keep washing hands with soap at the important times. Also play outdoors, in soil and around plants and animals. The two go together.

## Mostly agreed
- Each external organ has its own job, and the organs work together. *How we know:* When one part is hurt, its job suffers. A blocked nose makes food taste dull, and cold, numb fingers fumble with buttons. People who cannot see often learn to read Braille with their fingertips, so another part takes on some of the work.
- The outer skin keeps renewing itself, and hair and nails grow from living roots. *How we know:* Sticky tape picks up dead skin flakes from anyone's arm. Cut hair and nails grow back, while the cut part never hurts.
- Washing hands with soap stops many germs spreading. *How we know:* In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis made doctors in a Vienna hospital wash their hands in a chlorine solution, and far fewer mothers died. Many studies since have shown handwashing with soap cuts tummy and chest illnesses.
- Sugar helps mouth germs make acid that damages teeth, and brushing with fluoride toothpaste protects them. *How we know:* Children who eat sweet snacks often get more holes in their teeth. Dentists see fewer holes in children who brush twice a day.
- Every finger has its own print, and fingerprints form before birth. *How we know:* Police have compared millions of fingerprints for over 100 years. No two have been found to match, not even those of identical twins, or the two thumbs of one person.

## How we found out
**Ignaz Semmelweis and the midwives and doctors of the Vienna General Hospital, and later Louis Pasteur and Joseph Lister, 1847 (handwashing in Vienna), and the 1860s and 1870s (Pasteur and Lister's work on germs).** In 1847, in a big hospital in Vienna, many mothers were dying of a fever soon after giving birth. The hospital had two wards for births. In the ward run by doctors and medical students, about one mother in ten died. In the ward run by midwives, far fewer died. Most doctors blamed bad air. A young doctor, Ignaz Semmelweis, noticed a difference. The doctors often came straight from cutting open dead bodies to help with births. The midwives did not. Then his friend, Dr Jakob Kolletschka, was cut by a student's knife while examining a dead body. He died of an illness very like the mothers' fever. Semmelweis guessed that something unseen on the doctors' hands was carrying it. He made every doctor wash their hands in a chlorine solution before touching a mother. Within months, deaths in the doctors' ward fell to about one or two in a hundred. Many doctors still refused to believe him. They did not like being told their own hands carried disease. In the 1860s and 1870s, Louis Pasteur and Joseph Lister showed that germs too small to see cause illness. Semmelweis had been right.  
*What people believed before:* Most doctors thought the fever came from bad air, and that their own hands could not carry illness. When Semmelweis made them wash in a chlorine solution, deaths in the doctors' ward fell from about 1 in 10 to 1 or 2 in 100.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Earwax is dirt, so ears should be cleaned out with cotton buds.** (Many people believe this. Cotton buds are sold everywhere.; *rejected*)  
  For: Wax looks dirty. Taking it out feels clean.  
  Against: Ear doctors say wax protects the ear and moves out by itself. Cotton buds push wax deeper and can hurt the eardrum. Only a doctor should remove wax that is stuck.
- **Shaving or cutting hair makes it grow back thicker and darker.** (A common belief in many homes, including about shaving a baby's head.; *rejected*)  
  For: Short new hair feels rough and looks dark against the skin.  
  Against: Cutting only removes the dead part. The living root under the skin does not change. Mildred Trotter, an American scientist, tested this in 1928. She and later scientists found that shaved hair grows back the same.
- **Reading in dim light ruins your eyes for good.** (Many parents and grandparents have said it.; *rejected*)  
  For: Reading in dim light makes your eyes tired and sore.  
  Against: Eye doctors say dim light makes eyes tired, but it does not cause lasting harm. Studies suggest children who spend more time outdoors are less likely to become short-sighted.
- **No two fingerprints in the world can ever be the same.** (Police and courts have often treated this as certain.; *debated*)  
  For: No two people have ever been found with the same prints, even twins.  
  Against: Nobody can check every person. Also, smudged prints can be matched wrongly. In 2004, the FBI wrongly matched a man's fingerprint to a bombing in Spain, and later said sorry.
- **The more you scrub and wash, the healthier you are.** (Many soap and cleaner advertisements suggest this.; *debated*)  
  For: Handwashing really does stop many illnesses.  
  Against: Scrubbing skin hard can hurt it. Many helpful microbes live on skin. Some scientists think children also need to meet harmless microbes from soil, animals and people.
- **Old Indian ways of cleaning teeth, with neem twigs and a tongue scraper, are just old habits.** (Some people think only toothpaste and brushes work.; *debated*)  
  For: Toothpaste with fluoride is very well tested for protecting teeth.  
  Against: Ancient books like the Charaka Samhita describe cleaning teeth with twigs and scraping the tongue. Some studies found neem twigs do reduce mouth germs. Many dentists say a twig used well cleans, but fluoride toothpaste protects enamel best.

## Roots
We have been learning about the outside of the body, from skin and hair to the ridges on your fingertips. Now we ask what your own two thumbprints tell you about how a body is made.

**Deep truth.** Your two thumbs grew from the same genes, yet their prints had ridge forks and ends in different places. Each pattern formed before birth, shaped by tiny differences in how each fingertip grew. So your body's fine details come from how you grew, as well as from your genes.  
*Habit it breaks:* People often speak of genes as a complete plan of a body, drawn down to the last line. Your two thumbs carry the same genes and still have different prints, so the genes cannot hold every detail.  
*Evidence:* In Fingerprint Detective, your left and right thumbprints did not match, though they grew from the same genes in the same womb. Identical twins share their genes, and their fingerprints differ too. Ridges start at a few spots on each fingertip before birth and spread across it, and where they meet sets the pattern.

- *who am i:* You are one of a kind, right down to your fingertips. Part of what makes you is the way you happened to grow.
- *what is life:* A living body builds itself. Its cells grow and divide, and the exact shape of each part comes out of that growing.
- *what is this world:* Your skin meets the world all day. It keeps out dirt and germs, feels every touch, and leaves your fingerprints on what you hold.

Your whole body grew from one first cell. That cell divided again and again, and almost every new cell carried the same genes. Yet those cells became skin, hair, nails, eyes and ten different fingerprints. One cell with one set of genes grew into many different parts. That is one becoming many, inside your own body.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to break open a tiny banyan seed. Inside, Shvetaketu can see nothing at all. Uddalaka tells him that the whole great banyan tree grows out of that nothing he cannot see. (The first cell of your body held no tiny fingerprint you could have seen. The ridges appeared only as your fingertips grew, before you were born.)
- **Plato, Cratylus 402a, reporting Heraclitus:** Heraclitus, a Greek thinker, said you cannot step into the same river twice. The water is always new, yet it is still the river. (Your outer skin is replaced every month or two, yet the same fingerprint grows back each time, because its pattern is held in a deeper layer of skin.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on 'interbeing':** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in it. Nothing exists all by itself. (Your new skin cells are built from the sambar, rice and water you took in. Look deeply at your skin, and you can see the farms and the rain that grew your food.)
- Science (settled): The top layer of skin is made of dead cells that flake off, while new cells grow underneath. The outer skin is replaced about every month or two.
- Science (settled): Fingerprint ridges form between about 10 and 16 weeks in the womb. Genes affect what kind of pattern a finger has, but every finger's exact print is different, even between identical twins.
- Other view, Many scientists who study genes: Genes matter more than this makes it sound. Identical twins' prints are often the same kind and look alike, and the kind of pattern runs in families. They would say the differences are small details, and the genes did the main work. That is an honest view.
- Practice, Read your fingertips (3 min): Sit somewhere with good light. Hold up both hands, palms towards you. Look closely at the tip of one thumb. Follow one ridge with your eyes, from one side to the other. Now look at your other thumb in the same way. These lines grew while you were growing inside your mother. Rest your hands on your knees and take three slow breaths.
- Ask: Your two thumbs have the same genes. What else could make their prints different?
- Ask: Your outer skin keeps being replaced, yet your fingerprint grows back the same. What keeps it the same?
- Ask: Is the fingerprint you left on a cup still a part of you?
- Ask: Your hair is dead, but it is yours. What makes something part of you?

## Story: Ruksana's Ink Thumbs
*Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose were real. They worked in Calcutta in the 1890s for Edward Henry, the head of the Bengal police, on a way to sort fingerprints. Haque worked out much of its counting. The system was taken up in British India in 1897 and later in Britain, and it was named after Henry. Ruksana, her family and everything they say and do are imagined.*

In the city of Calcutta, in the year 1896, a girl called Ruksana had the dirtiest thumbs in her lane.

It was not her fault. Her uncle, Azizul Chacha, brought work home. Every evening he came up the stairs with a bundle of cards tied in string. Each card had ten black smudges on it, one for each finger of some person far away. The smudges were made with ink. And the ink got everywhere.

"Ruksana!" her mother would call. "Why is there a thumb on the roti?"

There was. A small black whirl, right in the middle of the roti, like a tiny storm.

"It's mine," Ruksana said proudly. "I can tell."

"You can tell because there is ink on your thumb," said her mother. "Go and wash. With soap, not just water."

Ruksana went to the tap in the courtyard. She rubbed the soap until it foamed grey. The water alone had only made the ink shine. The soap lifted it. She held her thumb up to the evening light. Under the last grey streak, she could see fine lines on her skin, curving round and round like water going down a drain.

Every night Azizul Chacha sat at the low table with his cards and a lamp. He was a police sub-inspector. But he was also, everyone said, very good at numbers. His boss at the police office was an Englishman called Mr Henry. Mr Henry wanted to catch thieves by their fingerprints. A thief might change his name, or grow a beard. But he could not change the lines on his fingers.

"There is one problem," Chacha told Ruksana one night. He rubbed his eyes. "We have thousands of cards. Soon we will have lakhs. When a man is caught, we take his prints. Then we must find out if his card is already in our cupboards. How do we find one card among a lakh? A clerk would be looking until he was an old man."

Ruksana looked at the cards spread out under the lamp. Some fingers had whirls, like her right thumb. Some had loops that came in from one side, curved round like a hairpin, and went back out the same side. A few had plain little hills, rising and falling, like the arch of a bridge.

Nani was sitting by the lamp, picking stones out of the rice for the morning. "Nani," Ruksana asked, "why do we have lines on our fingers at all?"

Nani smiled. "Feel this," she said. She put a single grain of rice on the table. "Shut your eyes. Find it with your finger."

She found it at once. She could feel it roll.

"Now find it with your elbow."

She could not. She giggled and pressed her elbow all over the table, and the grain stayed hidden.

"Your fingertips are your best feelers," said Nani. "Maybe the lines help them feel, and help you hold things. Nobody knows for sure. What I do know is this. Along each line are tiny holes, too small to see. A little sweat comes out of them. Your fingers pick up a little oil from your face and hair too. So whenever you touch something, you leave your pattern behind, even without ink. You have been leaving yourself all over this house since you were born."

Ruksana looked around the room. The door. The water pot. Her slate. Her baby brother Faiz's cheek. She had touched them all. She was everywhere.

That night she could not sleep. She lay on her mat and thought about whirls, loops and hills. In the morning she made a game. She drew a big square on her slate and split it in two. On the left she wrote 'whirl'. On the right she wrote 'no whirl'. Then she went round the whole family, looking at right thumbs.

Her mother had a whirl. Her father had a loop. Faiz had a loop, though he tried to eat the slate. Old Nani's fingertips were so worn from forty years of grinding spices on the stone that Ruksana could hardly see the lines at all.

"They come back a little," said Nani, "when I rest my hands for a while." She laughed. "Even my skin knows when to take a holiday."

Ruksana showed her uncle the slate that evening. "I only asked one question," she said. "Whirl or no whirl? And it split everyone into two piles."

Azizul Chacha did not say anything for a long time. He looked at the slate. Then he looked at his own ten fingers, spread on the table.

"One question splits the cards into two piles," he said slowly. "But a person has ten fingers. Ask the same question for each finger. Whirl or no whirl, finger by finger. Then two piles become four. Four become eight. Eight become sixteen." He was already writing numbers down the side of a card. "Ten fingers. One thousand and twenty-four boxes. Not one great heap, but a thousand small ones. And each box has a number, worked out from the fingers themselves."

He looked up. His eyes were shining like a boy's.

"Did I help?" asked Ruksana.

"You asked a good question," he said. "Now I have to do a great deal of very boring arithmetic."

He did. For months he worked on it. He found ways to split the big boxes into smaller ones, by counting ridges and noticing tiny shapes. He worked with another clever man in the office, Hem Chandra Bose. In 1897, the government took up their system for all of India. A few years later, the police in London took it up too.

It was called the Henry System, after Mr Henry.

"Doesn't it make you cross?" Ruksana asked, when she heard the name. "It was your counting."

Azizul Chacha was quiet. Then he took her hand and turned it over, so her palm faced up. He pressed her fingertip, very gently, onto the clean glass of the lamp. When he lifted it, a faint whirl was there, made only of the sweat of her skin.

"You see that?" he said. "Nobody wrote your name on it. But anyone who looks carefully will know it is yours."

Ruksana grew up. She married, and moved to Dacca, and had children of her own. Their thumbs were often dirty too. Before every meal she sent them to the tap, with soap, and while they washed she made them hold their fingertips up to the light.

"Look," she would say. "That is you. You leave it on everything you touch. So be careful what you touch, and wash it off before you eat."

Many years later, the government gave her uncle a reward and an honour for his work, and Hem Chandra Bose too. By then, Ruksana had long known what she knew. On the lamp glass, on the water pot, on Faiz's cheek, she had seen it with her own eyes. The lines had been there before anyone gave them a name.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the fingertip is an external organ, and every part of it has a job. Its ridges help us feel, and perhaps grip. Tiny sweat pores along the ridges make us leave fingerprints on everything, which is why washing with soap matters before eating: soap lifts the oily layer that plain water cannot. The skin also wears and renews itself, as Nani's worn fingertips show. Underneath, the story asks who gets the credit for great work, through Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, whose names were left off the system they helped build.

- Ask: Why could Ruksana find the rice grain with her finger but not with her elbow?
- Ask: How did Ruksana leave her pattern on things, even without ink?
- Ask: Why did plain water not wash the ink off, but soap did?
- Ask: Why do you think Nani's fingertip lines had almost worn away?
- Ask: The system was named after Mr Henry. Who do you think should get the credit? Why?

### g1 · Guard Match (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 30 cards. Shuffle them and lay them all face down in rows.; On your turn, turn over three cards and read them out loud.; If you have a PART, its GUARD and its CARE card, you win the set. Put it in that part's box on the mat.; If not, turn them back face down in the same places. Try to remember where they are.; When you win a set, explain how the guard protects the part. A good explanation wins a bonus point.; When all ten sets are won, the player with the most points wins.
- **Printable cards: 30 cards to cut out: 10 parts, 10 guards, 10 care cards:** PART: Eyes; GUARD: Eyelashes, eyelids and tears; CARE: Never rub hard. Wash with clean water. Never look at the sun.; PART: Ears; GUARD: Earwax that moves out by itself; CARE: Wipe the outside only. Never poke anything in.; PART: Nose; GUARD: Nose hairs and sticky mucus; CARE: Blow gently into a handkerchief. Sneeze into your elbow.; PART: Teeth; GUARD: Hard enamel and spit; CARE: Brush twice a day, most of all at night.; PART: Skin; GUARD: A top layer of dead cells that renews itself; CARE: Bathe every day. Dry the folds. Cover cuts.; PART: Hair; GUARD: It keeps the head warm and shaded; CARE: Wash and comb. Don't share combs.; PART: Nails; GUARD: Hard keratin over the fingertips; CARE: Cut short and straight. Don't bite them.; PART: Hands; GUARD: Skin and a sense of touch that warns of heat; CARE: Wash with soap for 20 seconds.; PART: Feet; GUARD: Thick skin on the soles; CARE: Wash and dry between the toes. Wear chappals outside.; PART: Private parts; GUARD: Clothes, and the rule that your body belongs to you; CARE: Wash every day, wipe front to back, and tell a trusted adult if a touch feels wrong.
- **Printable board: A body outline mat:** A life-size or A3 outline of a child, with a box next to each of the 10 parts
- **Printable score sheet: Sets collected:** Player name; Sets of three; Bonus: guard explained
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 30 cards. 2. Lay them face down in rows. 3. Put the body outline mat beside them.  
  *Parent says:* Every part of your outside has a guard and a way we care for it. In this game, you find all three for each part.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the cards face down in rows. Each part has a guard and a care card. Find all three to win a set.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play a practice round. Turn over the cards for Ears, Earwax and 'Wipe the outside only' face up.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a full set. The part is the ear. The guard is earwax. The care is to wipe the outside only. Why don't we poke inside?  
  *Child mode:* Practice set: ears, earwax, wipe the outside only. Why not poke inside? Tap your answer.
  - Ask: Why don't we poke cotton buds into our ears? (good: It pushes the wax deeper.; It can hurt the eardrum.; The wax comes out by itself.)
  - Check: Earwax is… → **a guard that traps dust and moves out by itself**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take turns turning over three cards. 2. Keep a set if it matches. 3. Explain the guard for a bonus point. 4. Mark points on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* When you win a set, tell us how that guard protects its part.  
  *Child mode:* Take turns. Turn over three cards. A full set goes on the body mat. Explain the guard to win a bonus.
  - Note: Good explanations: eyelashes catch dust, and tears wash it away. Nose hairs and mucus trap dust. Enamel is hard, so teeth can chew, and spit washes them. The top layer of skin is dead, so it can rub off without hurting. Thick soles protect feet. For private parts, a good answer is 'my body belongs to me, and I can tell a trusted adult'.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When all sets are won, count the points. Then point to each part on the body mat and say its care rule together.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish by saying every care rule together, from head to toe.  
  *Child mode:* Count the points. Then touch each part on the mat. Say its care rule out loud.
  - Check: Game moment: you just won the Nose set. Which is the guard card? → **Nose hairs and sticky mucus**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bread Test: what do unwashed hands leave behind? (35 min, 6 days)
- **question:** Do washed hands leave fewer germs on bread than unwashed hands? Does soap make a difference?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which slice will grow the most spots: touched by dirty hands, by hands washed in water only, or by hands washed with soap? Which will grow the fewest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use slices from the same loaf, touch each one in the same way for the same time, and keep all the bags in the same dark cupboard. Only the hands change. Keep one slice untouched, so you can see what bread does on its own.
- **safety:** Never open the bags once they are sealed. Mould spores can upset breathing. At the end, throw the bags away still sealed. Wash hands after handling the bags.
- **record:** Fill in the table on days 2, 4 and 6. On day 6, draw each slice. Then draw a bar for each slice, as tall as its number of spots.
- **results table:** Slice | Day 2: spots | Day 4: spots | Day 6: spots | What it looks like
- **schedule:** day 1: Touch the slices, seal and label the bags, and put them in a dark cupboard. (20 min); day 2: Look at the bags without opening them. Count any spots. (3 min); day 4: Look again and count spots. (3 min); day 6: Count spots a last time, draw the slices, fill in the table, and say what you found. (9 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Line up four slices and four bags. 2. Label the bags: Dirty hands, Water only, Soap, Untouched. 3. Ask your child to guess which slice will grow most spots.  
  *Parent says:* Our hands touch everything, and they pick up tiny living things too small to see. Let's find out what they leave on food.  
  *Child mode:* Label four bags. Dirty hands. Water only. Soap. Untouched. Which slice will grow the most spots? Guess first.
  - Note: Bread from a local bakery, or pav, grows mould faster than packet bread, which often has preservatives in it. Sprinkle 5 drops of water on every slice before it goes in its bag, because mould grows best on damp bread. Keep the bags somewhere warm and dark.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child plays outside or touches door handles, shoes and the floor for two minutes. 2. They rub one slice all over with those hands, and seal it in the Dirty hands bag.  
  *Parent says:* First, the dirty hands. Touch slice 1 all over, on both sides, then seal it in.  
  *Child mode:* Touch door handles and your shoes. Then rub slice 1 all over. Seal it in its bag.
  - Note: Help your child seal the bag well. Squeeze out most of the air first. Do not use hands that have a cut on them.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rinses hands under water only, for 20 seconds, and shakes them dry. 2. They touch slice 2 the same way and seal it. 3. Now they wash with soap for 20 seconds, rinse and shake them dry again. 4. They touch slice 3 and seal it.  
  *Parent says:* Now water only. Then soap. Touch each slice exactly the same way, so it's fair.  
  *Child mode:* Wash with water only. Touch slice 2. Seal it. Now wash with soap for 20 seconds. Touch slice 3. Seal it.
  - Check: Why do we touch each slice in the same way, for the same time? → **So the test is fair, and only the washing is different**
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Use a clean spoon to slide slice 4 into its bag, without touching it. Seal it. Put all four bags in a dark cupboard.  
  *Parent says:* This last slice is untouched. It shows what bread does all by itself.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to slide slice 4 in with a spoon. Don't touch it. Put all bags in a dark cupboard.
  - Note: The untouched slice is the 'control'. It may grow a spot or two from the air. That is useful too: it shows germs are everywhere, and hands add more.
- **s5 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* On days 2, 4 and 6, look at the bags without opening them. Count the spots on each slice. Write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Don't open the bags. Just look and count.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the bags. Do not open them. Count the spots on each slice. Write the numbers down.
  - Note: Spots often show first on day 3 or 4. In a cool room or with packet bread, it can take longer. If there are no spots by day 6, keep looking every two days until day 10. Results vary, so trust what you see, and talk about it if the slices look alike.
  - Check: On day 6, the slice touched by dirty hands has the most spots. What does that show? → **Hands carry tiny living things we cannot see**
  - Wait: Look on day 2, day 4 and day 6.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the slices. Which grew the most? The fewest? Did water alone work as well as soap?  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess right? What did soap do that water alone didn't?  
  *Child mode:* Was your guess right? Which slice is cleanest? Did soap beat water?
  - Note: Plain water washes off some germs. But many stick in the thin layer of oil and grease on hands. Soap grabs the oil, so water can carry it, and the germs, away. Fuzzy mould spots are fungi, and the colours show different kinds. Small shiny spots may be bacteria.
  - Think: Before which jobs in a day should you always wash with soap?
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things about the bread test. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: washing with soap takes away the most germs. What I saw: the dirty-hands slice had 11 spots, water only had 6, and soap had 2. Why: soap lifts the oily layer that germs stick to.

### e3 · The Egg That Brushed Its Teeth (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Can toothpaste protect a hard shell from acid?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After a night in vinegar, which egg will have the softer, rougher shell: the plain one, or the one covered in toothpaste? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use two eggs of the same kind, two same-sized glasses, and the same amount of vinegar. Only the toothpaste changes. Leave both for the same time.
- **safety:** A grown-up handles the raw eggs. Wash hands after touching raw egg. Do not eat the eggs afterwards. Vinegar stings eyes, so keep it away from faces.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both eggs on day 2, and label which was smooth and which was rough.
- **results table:** Egg | Bubbles? (none, a few, lots) | Shell after one night: smooth or rough? | Shell after one night: hard or soft?
- **schedule:** day 1: Cover one egg in toothpaste and leave it for an hour. Then put both eggs in vinegar and watch the bubbles. (15 min); day 2: Take the eggs out with a spoon, rinse them, feel the shells and say what you found. (10 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two eggs. Tap them gently. Say that eggshell is a hard mineral, a little like tooth enamel.  
  *Parent says:* Your teeth have a hard coat called enamel. Mouth germs make acid from sugar, and acid eats enamel. Vinegar is an acid too. Let's see what it does to a hard shell.  
  *Child mode:* Eggshell is hard, a bit like your teeth. Vinegar is an acid. What will it do?
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Cover one egg completely in toothpaste and leave it on a saucer for one hour. Then wipe off most of the paste gently with a tissue.  
  *Parent says:* This egg is brushing its teeth. We give the toothpaste time to soak into the shell.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up covers one egg in toothpaste. Wait one hour. Then wipe it gently.
  - Wait: Leave it for one hour.
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put each egg in its own glass. Pour vinegar over both until they are covered. Watch for one or two minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely at the shells. What is coming off them?  
  *Child mode:* Put both eggs in vinegar. Watch. What do you see on the shells?
  - Note: The toothpaste egg may bubble less, or start later. Results vary with the toothpaste. That is fine: talk about what you see.
  - Check: Tiny bubbles come off the plain egg's shell. What is happening? → **The acid is eating the shell and giving off a gas**
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Next day, lift each egg out with a spoon and rinse it. Let your child feel both shells.  
  *Parent says:* Feel both shells. Which is rougher? Which is softer?  
  *Child mode:* Lift out the eggs with a spoon. Rinse them. Feel both shells. Which is rough?
  - Wait: Leave both eggs in the vinegar overnight.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things about the egg test. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: toothpaste helps protect a shell from acid. What I saw: the plain egg bubbled a lot and its shell went rough, but the toothpaste egg stayed smoother. Why: the toothpaste left a thin coat on the shell that slowed the acid down. Fluoride in toothpaste helps tooth enamel stand up to acid in a similar way. If both eggs look the same, that is a real result too. Talk about why it might have happened.

### Outside-Me Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, says, 'My heart is an organ, but my skin is just a cover.' What is wrong with her idea?

   **Answer:** Eyes → see; Ears → hear; Nose → breathe in air and smell; Skin → keep germs out and feel touch; Teeth → bite and chew food; Feet → carry the body's weight. *Why:* Each external organ has a job. Some do more than one: the nose breathes and smells, and the skin guards and feels.

   **Answer:** earwax → Guard; mucus in the nose → Guard; tears → Guard; eyelashes → Guard; mud on your knees → Dirt to wash off; oil on your hands after eating vada → Dirt to wash off. *Why:* Wax, mucus, tears and lashes are made by the body to trap or wash away dust and germs.    **Answer:** Blink a lot, and rinse with clean water if the dust stays. *Why:* Blinking spreads tears, and tears wash dust to the corner of the eye. Clean water helps if it stays. Rubbing hard can scratch the eye, and only a doctor should choose eye drops.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Germs and mould spores are far too small to see. In the bread test, hands that looked clean still left spots on the bread. Looking is not enough. Washing with soap is the safe way.

9. (write) In the eyebrow test, the water dripped above your eyebrow ran off to the side. Why do you think eyebrows are curved? What might happen on a hot, sweaty day without them?  
   **Answer:** The curve works like a little roof gutter. It sends sweat and rain round the side of the eye. Without eyebrows, sweat would run straight into my eyes and sting, and I could not see well while playing.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what the child saw in the test and explains the shape by its job.
10. (odd) Three of these habits protect a body part. One harms it. Which one is the odd one out?

   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Short nails leave no gap for germs. Wiping only the outside lets earwax move out by itself. Brushing at night protects teeth when there is less spit. Rubbing hard can scratch the eye and push in germs, so it is the odd one out. Blink, or rinse with clean water, instead.
11. (perspective) People have argued for a long time about what the tiny ridges on our fingertips are for.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks an idea and gives evidence, such as 'I could feel a single grain of rice with my fingertip but not with my elbow'. It then admits a fair point from another side, for example that ridges may help grip on wet or rough things.. *Why:* Scientists still argue about this. Ridges may help us feel, may help grip on wet or rough surfaces, and may do several jobs at once.
12. (write) Nails grow about 3 millimetres a month. How often do you think you should cut them? Why do germs collect under long nails?  
   **Answer:** About every one or two weeks, so they never grow long. Under a long nail there is a gap where dirt and food collect. Soap and water can't reach in well, so germs stay there and get onto food.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the growth speed to choose how often, and explains the gap under the nail.
13. (mcq) Anjali walks barefoot on wet soil near a drain. What is the main health reason she should wear chappals?

   **Answer:** Tiny hookworms in damp, dirty soil can get in through bare skin. *Why:* Young hookworms live in damp, dirty soil and can get in through the skin of bare feet. Chappals also protect from glass and hot roads.
14. (draw) Draw yourself after running on a hot day. Show where the sweat comes out of your skin. Draw arrows to show what happens to your body's heat as the sweat dries.  
   **Answer:** Drops of sweat on the forehead, face and arms, coming out of tiny holes called pores. Arrows show heat leaving the skin as the sweat dries into the air.. *Why:* Sweating is one of the skin's jobs. Drying sweat takes heat away with it, so the skin cools. A strong drawing shows sweat coming from the skin itself and heat leaving as it dries.
15. (write) In the trusted hand activity, you drew five grown-ups you trust. Why is it better to have five trusted grown-ups, not just one?  
   **Answer:** If one grown-up is busy, away or does not listen, I still have others to tell. Also, if the person doing wrong is someone I know, I need someone else to go to.. *Why:* A strong answer knows that a child must keep telling until someone helps.
16. (fill) Along each fingertip ridge are tiny holes that let out sweat. So whenever we touch something, we leave our ____ behind.  
   **Answer:** ['fingerprints', 'fingerprint', 'prints']. *Why:* Sweat, and oil picked up from the face and hair, leave the pattern of the ridges on whatever we touch. That is also how germs pass from hands to things.
### Thinking Further (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (perspective) Can a child be too clean? Three people give different answers.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and uses evidence, such as 'the dirty-hands slice grew the most spots, so hands really carry germs'. It then answers back fairly, for example that playing in soil and washing before meals can go together.. *Why:* This is still argued about. Doctors agree that washing at the important times stops illness. Many also think children need to meet harmless microbes outdoors. The views fit together more than they first seem to.
2. (write) Plan a fair test to find out if warm water washes oily hands cleaner than cold water. What would you change? What would you keep the same? How would you tell which worked better?  
   **Answer:** Change only the water: warm or cold. Keep the same oil and turmeric, the same soap, and the same 20 seconds of rubbing. Then look at how much turmeric is left on each hand, or photograph both.. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. A strong answer names what stays the same and how the result will be seen.
3. (mcq) Identical twins have the same genes. Their fingerprints form before birth. Why do the twins still have different fingerprints?  
   Options: Tiny differences in the womb, such as how each baby lies and how fast each finger grows, shape the exact ridges / Twins have different genes after all / Fingerprints change every year / One twin always has whorls and the other loops  
   **Answer:** Tiny differences in the womb, such as how each baby lies and how fast each finger grows, shape the exact ridges. *Why:* Genes give the general pattern, but small differences in the womb shape the exact ridges. Picking 'change every year' is wrong. Your fingerprints keep their pattern all your life.
4. (write) Your outer skin replaces itself every month or two. Some of the dust in your house is your old skin, and you leave fingerprints on everything you touch. Where do you think you end and the world begins?  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer. For example: I thought I ended at my skin. But my old skin flakes off and becomes dust, and I leave fingerprints on everything I touch. So it is hard to say exactly where I stop.. *Why:* This is a big question with no single answer. A strong answer uses what the child saw in the sticky tape test.
5. (write) In 1847, a doctor called Ignaz Semmelweis made doctors in his hospital wash their hands in chlorine water. Deaths of mothers in his ward fell from about 1 in 10 to 1 or 2 in 100. Many doctors still refused to believe him for years. Why do you think they found it hard to believe?  
   **Answer:** Germs are too small to see, so the doctors could not see anything on their hands. They also did not like being told that their own hands had made mothers ill. But the fall in deaths was big and clear evidence.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason people resist an idea, and says what evidence should have changed their minds.
6. (mcq) Lakshmi does a new bread test. She touches one slice after washing with soap for 2 seconds, and another after washing with soap for 20 seconds. What do you predict?  
   Options: The 20-second slice grows fewer spots, because longer rubbing loosens more oil and germs / Both grow the same, because soap is soap / The 2-second slice grows fewer spots / Neither grows any spots  
   **Answer:** The 20-second slice grows fewer spots, because longer rubbing loosens more oil and germs. *Why:* Soap needs time and rubbing to grab the oil that germs stick in, all over the hand. A quick splash misses most of it. That is why we rub for about 20 seconds.
7. (write) In the blink counter, you counted a grown-up's blinks in one minute while chatting. Why might a long time staring at a screen make your eyes dry and tired? How could you test it?  
   **Answer:** When we stare at a screen we blink much less. Each blink spreads tears over the eye, so fewer blinks means drier eyes. To test it, I would count blinks for one minute while chatting and one minute while watching a phone.. *Why:* A strong answer links blinking to tears, and plans a test that changes only what the person is doing.
8. (draw) Design a new guard for one body part. Label what it does and how it works.  
   **Answer:** Any creative design with a clear job. For example: ear flaps that close in loud places, like a hippo's ears closing under water.. *Why:* Good designs copy what real guards do: trap, steer away, wash out or shut.
9. (write) Some old Indian books describe cleaning teeth with neem twigs. Fluoride toothpaste is newer. Some people say old ways are always best, and some say new ways are always best. How could you find out which cleans better?  
   **Answer:** A fair test: some people use twigs and some use fluoride toothpaste for weeks, with everything else the same, and a dentist counts holes and germs. At home, I could coat one eggshell with neem paste and one with toothpaste, and put both in vinegar. That only tests protection from acid, not from germs.. *Why:* Scientists test old and new ways fairly, instead of trusting what is old or what is new. A strong answer changes only one thing, and says what its test can and cannot show.
10. (write) Your aunt's friend visits and wants a hug. You don't feel like hugging. Your mother says you can say no politely and wave instead. Why is it good that you get to choose?  
   **Answer:** My body belongs to me, even when I am small. Choosing a wave instead of a hug is polite and still keeps my body mine. Practising small 'no's makes it easier to say no to a touch that feels wrong.. *Why:* A strong answer knows that a child's body belongs to the child, and that saying no politely is not rude.
11. (mcq) After a sunburn, skin often peels a few days later. Why does this happen?  
   Options: The damaged top cells die and flake off, while new cells push up from underneath / The skin melts in the sun / Germs eat the skin / Skin peels so it can grow thicker  
   **Answer:** The damaged top cells die and flake off, while new cells push up from underneath. *Why:* The outer skin is always being replaced from below. Sunburn kills many top cells at once, so they come off in sheets instead of tiny flakes. This is the same renewing you saw on the sticky tape, only faster.
12. (odd) Which one is the odd one out? A haircut, cutting your nails, peeling sticky tape off your arm, a cut on your finger.  
   Options: A haircut / Cutting your nails / Peeling sticky tape off your arm / A cut on your finger  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The first three take away only dead cells, so they do not hurt or bleed. A cut on the finger goes down to living cells, nerves and blood, so it hurts and bleeds.
13. (truefalse) Soap that says 'antibacterial' on the box cleans hands much better at home than ordinary soap.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Many people believe this because of advertisements. Studies found ordinary soap, rubbed for 20 seconds, works just as well for everyday handwashing. What matters most is the rubbing and rinsing.
14. (mcq) When Kavya has a cold, her nose runs much more than usual. Why does her nose make so much extra mucus?  
   Options: To trap the cold germs and wash them out / Because the nose is full of dirt / Because the brain is leaking / Because she drank too much water  
   **Answer:** To trap the cold germs and wash them out. *Why:* Mucus is one of the nose's guards. When germs get in, the nose makes more of it to trap them and carry them out. That is why we blow the nose into a tissue and then wash hands.
15. (sort) Sort these into what SCIENTISTS AGREE ON and what SCIENTISTS STILL ARGUE ABOUT.  
   **Answer:** skin is an organ → Scientists agree; soap removes germs better than water alone → Scientists agree; shaving does not make hair thicker → Scientists agree; what fingertip ridges are mainly for → Scientists still argue; how much meeting germs early protects children from allergies → Scientists still argue; whether any two people in the world could have the same fingerprint → Scientists still argue. *Why:* Some things have been tested many times and always come out the same. Others are still being tested, and good scientists say plainly that they are not sure yet.
16. (mcq) Police treat fingerprints as proof of who touched something. In 2004, a fingerprint in Spain was matched to the wrong man. What is the most likely way a match like that goes wrong?  
   Options: The print is smudged or shows only part of a finger, so two people's part-prints can look alike / Fingerprints change every year / Everyone in Spain has the same fingerprints / Nothing can ever go wrong with a fingerprint match  
   **Answer:** The print is smudged or shows only part of a finger, so two people's part-prints can look alike. *Why:* A whole print has many ridge forks and endings, so it is very hard to mistake. A smudged part-print shows only a few, and those few can look like someone else's. That is why careful experts compare many details, ask a second expert to check, and look for other evidence too.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Kavya looks at herself in the mirror. Which organ can she see?  
   Options: Skin / Heart / Lungs / Stomach  
   **Answer:** Skin. *Why:* Skin is an external organ, on the outside, and it is the biggest one. The heart, lungs and stomach are internal organs, inside the body.
2. (mcq) Anjali has a cold, and her nose is blocked. Her favourite dosa suddenly tastes dull. Why?  
   Options: Much of what we call taste is really smell, and her blocked nose can't smell the food / Colds make the dosa go bad / Her tongue has stopped working for ever / Dosa only tastes good on sunny days  
   **Answer:** Much of what we call taste is really smell, and her blocked nose can't smell the food. *Why:* The tongue tastes only sweet, salty, sour, bitter and savoury. The nose picks up all the rest of the flavour. External organs work as a team, so when one is blocked, another seems to stop working too.
3. (mcq) Revathi bathes every day, but her ears still make yellow wax. What does that show?  
   Options: Wax is a guard the ear makes on purpose, not a sign of dirt / Her ears are very dirty / She is not washing properly / Wax only grows in dirty ears  
   **Answer:** Wax is a guard the ear makes on purpose, not a sign of dirt. *Why:* Clean ears make wax too. Wax traps dust and germs and moves out by itself. Picking 'very dirty' is the most common idea, and it is wrong.
4. (mcq) What do eyelashes and eyebrows have in common?  
   Options: Both guard the eyes, by catching dust or steering sweat away / Both help us see colours / Both make tears / Both are only for looks  
   **Answer:** Both guard the eyes, by catching dust or steering sweat away. *Why:* Lashes catch dust before it reaches the eye. Brows steer sweat and rain round the side. They look nice too, but they are guards.
5. (mcq) A haircut does not hurt, but pulling out one hair does. Why?  
   Options: The hair we see is dead, but the root is alive and has nerves around it / Pulling is always harder than cutting / The barber is more careful / Hair is made of bone  
   **Answer:** The hair we see is dead, but the root is alive and has nerves around it. *Why:* The part we cut is dead keratin. The root sits in living skin with nerves, so pulling it hurts.
6. (mcq) Meenakshi scrapes her knee. A week later, new skin covers it. Where did the new skin come from?  
   Options: Living cells underneath, which keep making new skin cells / The scab turned into skin / Skin from the other knee moved over / The plaster made it  
   **Answer:** Living cells underneath, which keep making new skin cells. *Why:* Living cells under the top layer keep dividing and pushing up new cells. That is how the outer skin renews itself, and how a scrape heals.
7. (mcq) In the bread test, why was one slice slid into its bag with a spoon and never touched?  
   Options: To compare: it shows how much the bread grows with no hands at all / Because there were not enough hands / To make it go bad faster / It was the slice for eating  
   **Answer:** To compare: it shows how much the bread grows with no hands at all. *Why:* The untouched slice is the comparison. If the hand slices grew more spots than it, the extra spots came from the hands.

   **Answer:** Soap took away far more germs than water alone, but not quite every one. *Why:* The soap slice had 2 spots, much closer to the untouched slice than the water-only slice with 6. So soap removes most germs and spores. It does not remove every single one, and nothing needs to.
9. (mcq) Why is brushing at night most important?  
   Options: Your mouth makes less spit at night, so germs can make acid for hours / Teeth grow at night / It helps you sleep / Germs only come out at night  
   **Answer:** Your mouth makes less spit at night, so germs can make acid for hours. *Why:* Spit washes and protects teeth. At night there is less of it. Picking 'germs only come out at night' is a mix-up: germs are there all day.
10. (mcq) In the egg test, what does the vinegar stand in for?  
   Options: The acid that germs in the mouth make from sugar / Spit / Toothpaste / Water we drink  
   **Answer:** The acid that germs in the mouth make from sugar. *Why:* Vinegar is an acid, and it eats the hard shell. Germs in the mouth turn sugar into acid that eats tooth enamel in the same way.
11. (mcq) If any touch feels wrong or confusing, what should you do?  
   Options: Say no, get away and tell a trusted grown-up / Keep it a secret / Think it is your fault / Wait and see  
   **Answer:** Say no, get away and tell a trusted grown-up. *Why:* Say no, get away, tell. It is never the child's fault, and secrets about touching are never kept.
12. (mcq) You tell a trusted grown-up about a touch that felt wrong, but they don't listen. What should you do next?  
   Options: Tell another trusted grown-up, and keep telling until someone helps / Stop telling anyone / Think you made a mistake / Keep it a secret  
   **Answer:** Tell another trusted grown-up, and keep telling until someone helps. *Why:* This is why children choose more than one trusted grown-up. Keep telling until someone listens and helps.
13. (mcq) What do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: What fingertip ridges are for / Whether skin is an organ / Whether hair grows / Whether teeth need brushing  
   **Answer:** What fingertip ridges are for. *Why:* Scientists agree skin is an organ, hair grows and brushing helps. They still argue whether ridges are mainly for grip, feeling or both.
14. (mcq) Your left and right thumbs grew from the same genes. What are their prints like?  
   Options: Different: the ridge forks and ends are in different places / Exactly the same, because the genes are the same / Different, because one thumb is older / They have no pattern at all  
   **Answer:** Different: the ridge forks and ends are in different places. *Why:* Every finger has its own print. The ridges formed before birth, and tiny differences in how each fingertip grew set the details. Picking 'exactly the same' is the common idea that genes decide everything. Both thumbs are the same age, so 'older' cannot be the reason.
15. (mcq) Fingerprints form before birth. What happens to them as you grow up?

   **Answer:** They get bigger, but the pattern stays the same all your life. *Why:* The ridges grow bigger as the fingers grow, but the pattern of loops, whorls and forks stays the same. That is why police can use them.

## Open questions
- **What are fingertip ridges really for?** Tests suggest they help us feel fine textures, and perhaps grip rough or wet things. But they may grip less on smooth dry things. Scientists still argue.
- **Exactly how does each person's fingerprint pattern form in the womb?** Ridges start at a few spots on each fingertip and spread out across it. Genes and the way the skin grows both shape where they meet. Scientists are still working out the details.
- **How clean is clean enough?** Handwashing at key times clearly helps. But we may also need to meet harmless microbes. Scientists are still studying how much, and which ones.
- **Why do some people make dry, flaky earwax and others wet, sticky earwax?** A difference in one gene is linked to it. Scientists are not sure why both kinds have lasted over human history.
- **Why do humans have so little body hair compared with other apes?** Some think it helped us cool down by sweating when running in the heat. Others have different ideas. Nobody knows for sure.
- **Why do we have eyebrows that move so much?** They guard the eyes, and they also show feelings. Some scientists think the feelings job helped humans live in groups. It is still an idea.
- **Exactly how many tiny living things live on your skin, and what do they all do?** There are many kinds, different in different places on the body. Scientists are still finding out what most of them do.

## Connected maths
- A thumbnail grew 2 millimetres in two weeks. About how much will it grow in eight weeks? **About 8 millimetres.**. Eight weeks is four lots of two weeks. In each two weeks, the nail grows 2 millimetres. 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8, or 4 × 2 = 8. So about 8 millimetres. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- A child has 20 milk teeth. A grown-up usually has 32 teeth. How many more teeth does the grown-up have? **12 more teeth.**. 32 take away 20 is 12. You can check: 20 + 12 = 32. So a grown-up has 12 more teeth than a child. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Ten family members gave their right thumbprints. 6 were loops, 3 were whorls and 1 was an arch. Draw a bar chart. Which kind was most common? **Loops were most common, with 6 out of 10.**. Draw one bar for each kind. The loop bar is 6 tall, the whorl bar is 3 tall and the arch bar is 1 tall. The loop bar is tallest, so loops were most common. 6 + 3 + 1 = 10, so every print is counted. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- A grown-up blinked 15 times in one minute. How many times would that be in 10 minutes? **150 times.**. One minute has 15 blinks. Ten minutes is ten lots of 15. 15 × 10 = 150. So about 150 blinks in 10 minutes. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- In the bread test, the dirty-hands slice had 12 spots and the soap slice had 3 spots. How many times as many spots did the dirty-hands slice have? **4 times as many.**. 12 divided by 3 is 4. You can check: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. So the dirty-hands slice grew 4 times as many spots as the soap slice. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
