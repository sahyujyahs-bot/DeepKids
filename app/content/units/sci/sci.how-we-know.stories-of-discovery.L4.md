# From 'I saw it' to 'we know it': how a discovery wins belief
`sci.how-we-know.stories-of-discovery.L4`

**Summary.** In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller wrote to the great scientists of London that a single drop of water was full of tiny living beings. Nobody believed him. Nobody else had a lens strong enough to look. How does a claim that nobody can check become something everybody knows? Your child makes a lens from one drop of water, finds out what it shows in a newspaper photograph, and then hands the steps to someone else to see if they get the same result. They also set up the three jars of a famous fly test, and meet people whose ideas were doubted for years before they were believed.

**Intro.** *Imagine you tell your class that one drop of water holds living beings too small to see. You are the only person with a lens strong enough to look. Would your class believe you? Should they?* In the 1670s this really happened. Antoni van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch cloth seller, made tiny lenses and looked at water. The Royal Society in London, the most famous group of scientists of the time, did not believe what he wrote. A discovery is not finished when one person sees something. In this unit you make your own water-drop lens, find out if a stranger can follow your steps and see what you saw, and meet people whose ideas took years to be believed.

## Terms
- **Method**: The steps you follow to find something out, written clearly enough that someone else could follow them. *e.g.* A recipe for idli batter is a method. Anyone who follows it can make idli.
- **Repeat**: To do a test again by following the same steps, to see if you get the same result. Scientists also call this replicating. *e.g.* A friend follows your written steps and gets the same answer as you.
- **Lens**: A curved piece of clear material, such as glass or water, that bends light. *e.g.* The glass in a pair of spectacles is a lens.
- **Refraction**: The bending of light when it passes from one clear material into another, such as from air into water. *e.g.* A straw in a tumbler of water looks bent where it meets the water.
- **Magnify**: To make something look bigger than it really is. *e.g.* A lens magnifies small print so you can read it.
- **Microscope**: A tool with one or more lenses that makes tiny things big enough to see. *e.g.* A school microscope shows the cells of an onion skin.
- **Doubt**: Not believing a claim yet, and asking for it to be checked. Good doubt asks to see the evidence. It is not rude. *e.g.* "Can I try it myself?" is a polite way to doubt.
- **Royal Society**: A group of scientists in London, started in 1660, who met to share and test new discoveries. Its motto means: take nobody's word for it. *e.g.* The Royal Society asked for Leeuwenhoek's claim to be checked before they believed it.
- **Spontaneous generation**: The old idea that living things, such as maggots, can appear on their own from non-living things, such as rotting meat or mud. *e.g.* Many people once thought flies were made by rotting meat.
- **Gauze**: A thin, loose cloth with tiny holes. It lets air and smells through but keeps out flies. *e.g.* A thin muslin cloth tied over a jar is gauze.
- **Mechanism**: How something works: the push or process that makes it happen. *e.g.* A pulley is the mechanism that lifts a bucket from a well.
- **Fossil**: The remains or print of a plant or animal from long ago, kept in rock. *e.g.* A fossil fern leaf can be found in coal.
- **Continental drift**: The idea, put forward by Alfred Wegener in 1912, that the continents very slowly move across the Earth. *e.g.* Wegener said South America and Africa were once joined and have moved apart.
- **Plate tectonics**: The modern idea that Earth's outer shell is broken into big pieces, called plates, that move slowly and carry the continents with them. *e.g.* India sits on a plate that is pushing into Asia, and this helps lift the Himalayas.
- **Instrument**: A tool for observing or measuring, such as a thermometer, a rain gauge or a lens. *e.g.* A rain gauge is an instrument that measures how much rain has fallen.
- **Animalcule**: A word that Leeuwenhoek used for the tiny living things he saw in water. It means little animals. Today we call many of them bacteria or single-celled beings. *e.g.* The animalcules in pond water swim, turn and feed, though each is too small to see without a lens.

## Explanations
### A discovery is finished when others can repeat it · *defined*
**Definition:** A discovery is not complete when one person sees something. It is complete when other people, following the same steps, can see it too.

Suppose you see something nobody else has seen. Maybe you are right. But how would anyone know? You might have made a mistake, or been fooled by your own eyes, or been the only one with the right tool. So scientists ask for the steps. If other people follow your steps and get the same result, it stops being only yours and becomes shared knowledge. This is called repeating a test. In 1989 two chemists announced a way to make energy in a jar of water at room temperature. Labs around the world followed their steps and could not get the same result, and the claim was dropped.
- e.g. Anjana sees dots in a newspaper photograph. Her cousin follows her steps and sees them too.
- e.g. A friend gets a different answer from yours when following your steps. This tells you a step is unclear.
- e.g. A result that nobody else can repeat is set aside until someone can.
### A method: steps that a stranger can follow · *defined*
**Definition:** A method is a list of steps written so well that a stranger, without you in the room, can follow them and get the same kind of result.

Writing steps sounds easy, but most first tries are not clear enough. 'Make a hole' leaves out the size of the hole, the tool, and which side is up. 'Hold it close' leaves out how close. A clear step names the amount, the tool, the place and the time. For example: 'Press the tip of a sharp pencil once into the foil, on a folded towel, then twist.' A good method also says what you should see, so a reader knows if they are on track, and what often goes wrong. The best test of your steps is to watch a stranger follow them while you say nothing.
- e.g. 'Add some water' is unclear. 'Add one drop of water from a toothpick tip' is clear.
- e.g. 'Look closely' is unclear. 'Put your eye as close as one finger-width from the foil' is clear.
- e.g. A recipe that gives the amount of salt, not just 'some', works for any cook.
### How a drop of water becomes a lens · *defined*
**Definition:** Light bends when it passes from air into water. A round drop of water bends the light from a small thing so that it spreads wider, and the thing looks bigger.

Light travels slower in water than in air, so when it crosses the surface at a slant, it bends. This is called refraction. A flat surface bends all the light the same way. A curved surface bends the light more at the edge than at the middle. A drop of water sitting in a tiny hole is held in a round shape because water particles pull on each other. The pull is called surface tension. The round drop acts as a lens. A smaller, rounder drop bends light more than a larger, flatter one, so it magnifies more. Glass lenses, spectacles, cameras and the lens in your own eye all work by bending light in a similar way.
- e.g. A straw in a tumbler of water looks bent, because light bends at the water's surface.
- e.g. A drop of water on a newspaper makes the print under it look bigger.
- e.g. A glass of water with a flat side acts like a weak lens, and a round glass bowl acts like a stronger one.
### Tools open new worlds · *defined*
**Definition:** An instrument extends what our senses can reach. Each new tool shows us a layer of the world that we did not know was there.

Our eyes cannot see things that are too small, too far away or too faint. In 1665 an English scientist, Robert Hooke, published drawings of a flea, a louse and the cells of cork, seen through a microscope with several lenses. He gave cells their name. Leeuwenhoek used a different design with just one very small and very good lens, strong enough to show living beings in water. Telescopes opened the sky in the same way. A new tool does not just add facts. It can show that something we thought empty, like a drop of water, is full. It also has limits. Every tool shows only what it was built to show, and it can add its own distortions, so people must learn how to read it.
- e.g. A microscope shows bacteria and single-celled beings in a drop of pond water.
- e.g. A telescope shows moons around Jupiter.
- e.g. A thermometer shows a temperature that your fingers cannot judge exactly.
### Why scientists doubted Leeuwenhoek, and why doubt is part of the work · *defined*
**Definition:** Doubt means asking for a claim to be checked before believing it. It is a normal part of science, and the motto of the Royal Society says it: take nobody's word for it.

Leeuwenhoek had no university degree. He wrote in Dutch, and he kept the way he made his lenses secret. In the 1670s he told the Royal Society in London that a drop of water held thousands of tiny moving beings. The members had never seen such a thing, and their own microscopes were not strong enough. So they doubted him. Doubting him was not rude. It was what the Royal Society's motto asked for. After some effort, Robert Hooke and others in London made strong lenses of their own, followed Leeuwenhoek's description, and saw the same creatures in 1677. Leeuwenhoek was then respected, and in 1680 he was made a member of the Society. Doubt, followed by checking, turned his claim into knowledge.
- e.g. A friend says she saw a shooting star. You ask where and when, and look yourself.
- e.g. A claim that no one can check gets set aside until someone can.
- e.g. A polite doubter says: 'Can I try it myself?' and does not say 'You are wrong.'
### An old idea that was wrong: life from rotting meat · *defined*
**Definition:** Spontaneous generation was the idea that some living things appear from non-living things. Francesco Redi tested it for flies in 1668 and showed it was wrong.

For thousands of years, many people believed that maggots were made by rotting meat. They saw maggots on meat again and again, so the idea seemed sensible. Redi, an Italian doctor, wondered if flies were laying eggs that people did not see. He put pieces of meat in several jars. Some jars stayed open. Some were sealed. Some were covered with a thin cloth that let the smell through but not the flies. Maggots appeared in the open jars. They did not appear on the meat in the sealed jars. On the cloth, flies laid eggs, but the maggots could not reach the meat. Redi's test was easy to repeat, and others did. A different version of the question, about microbes, was not settled until the 1860s, when Louis Pasteur showed that microbes in broth come from microbes in the air.
- e.g. Maggots on open meat come from fly eggs, not from the meat.
- e.g. Mould on bread grows from tiny spores in the air, not from the bread itself.
- e.g. A closed jar of boiled broth stays clear until a microbe gets in.
### Why good ideas are sometimes rejected: drifting continents · *defined*
**Definition:** A good idea can be rejected for years if it has no explanation for how it could work, and if it goes against what most experts believe.

In 1912, Alfred Wegener, a German weather scientist, said the continents were once joined and have drifted apart. He had evidence. South America and Africa fit like puzzle pieces. The same kinds of fossils, such as the fern Glossopteris and a freshwater reptile called Mesosaurus, are found on coasts now thousands of kilometres apart. Rock layers match across the ocean. But he could not name a force strong enough to move continents, and he was not a geologist. Most geologists rejected the idea. Wegener died in 1930 on an expedition in Greenland. In the 1960s, maps of the ocean floor and the magnetism in rocks showed that the sea floor spreads, and the continents ride on moving plates. Wegener's idea was accepted, in a new form. Geologists named the old southern continent Gondwana, after a region of central India.
- e.g. India was once part of Gondwana, and it moves north by about 5 centimetres a year.
- e.g. Glossopteris fossils are found in India, Africa, South America, Australia and Antarctica.
- e.g. An idea with strong evidence but no mechanism can wait decades.
### The woman who mapped the sea floor: Marie Tharp · *defined*
**Definition:** Many discoveries rest on people who collect measurements and draw them, not only on the person who gets the name. Marie Tharp is one example.

In the 1950s, Marie Tharp worked at a research centre in New York. For years, women were not allowed on research ships, so she could not collect depth readings herself. She drew the maps from the numbers that others brought back. Along the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, she noticed a deep valley in the ridge. Her colleague Bruce Heezen at first thought she must be wrong, because a valley like that would fit the idea of moving continents, which he did not believe. Then he found that earthquakes lined up along the same valley. Together they made maps of the ocean floor that helped to convince scientists that the sea floor is spreading. Her story shows how a pattern can be seen by the person who looks at all the numbers, and how it can take time for even a colleague to believe it.
- e.g. A person who draws a map from thousands of depth numbers sees a pattern that none of the numbers show.
- e.g. A colleague can doubt you and then change their mind when new evidence arrives.
- e.g. Credit often goes to the person who explains the pattern, and less to the person who drew it.
### Why wrong ideas are kept · *idea*
Wrong ideas do not stay for no reason. They can seem sensible, as with maggots on meat. They may be taught by respected people, so doubting them feels rude. A good story can feel more satisfying than a plain fact. And sometimes nobody has done the simple test. Thinkers still argue about how much of this is human nature and how much is how groups of people work. It may be that we cannot avoid the first three, but we can build habits against them, such as asking 'who tested this?' and trying the test ourselves.
- e.g. A teacher repeats a saying that nobody has ever tested.
- e.g. A nice story about a famous scientist spreads, though parts of it were added later.
- e.g. A simple test, like Redi's jars, is not tried for many years.
### The hands behind discoveries · *defined*
**Definition:** Behind many discoveries stand the people who observe carefully, draw, build instruments and keep records. They are often left out of the story.

Maria Sibylla Merian, a German artist and naturalist born in 1647, began raising silkworms as a girl. At that time many people believed insects came from mud. She raised caterpillars at home, watched each change, and drew the whole life of each insect, from egg to caterpillar to chrysalis to moth or butterfly, with the plant the insect ate. Her books of paintings appeared in 1679 and later, and in 1705 she published a famous book on the insects of Suriname. Anna Mani, born in 1918 in Peermade in Kerala, studied physics, and worked for the India Meteorological Department in Pune. She designed and tested instruments for measuring sunshine, wind and ozone, so that weather scientists in India could measure the sky properly. C. V. Raman won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930 for the Raman effect. His student K. S. Krishnan worked beside him on the experiments and was not named in the prize.
- e.g. A picture of the life of an insect is a record that others can check.
- e.g. An instrument that gives the same reading for everyone makes weather science possible.
- e.g. A student who runs the experiment may not be named when the prize is given.
### Some skills cannot be fully written down · *idea*
Even the best steps cannot say everything. Making a good water drop needs a feel in the fingers that comes from trying. Michael Polanyi, a scientist and philosopher, wrote that we know more than we can tell. People learn some skills by watching and doing, not by reading. This does not mean steps are useless. It means that sometimes the best way to repeat a test is to learn it from someone who has done it, and then to write the steps better. Scientists still argue about how much of knowledge can be written down.
- e.g. A cook can describe how soft the dough should feel, but a child learns it only by touching dough.
- e.g. A pupil learns to ride a bicycle by trying, not by reading steps.
- e.g. A tailor can feel when cloth is right before a measuring tape says it.
### Lone geniuses, or times that were ready? · *debated*
Do discoveries come from rare, brilliant people, or from the times being ready? Thinkers disagree. Both views have examples. Many discoveries were made by several people at nearly the same time.
- e.g. Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace each reached the idea of natural selection in the 1850s.
- e.g. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz each invented calculus, working apart.
- **The 'great person' view:** A few people have unusual insight, and without them a discovery would come much later, or not at all.
- **The 'time was ready' view:** When the tools, the questions and the knowledge are ready, someone will find the answer. That is why so many discoveries happen twice.
- **Many historians:** Both matter. The time makes a discovery possible, and a person's care and courage decide who finds it and how well it is shared.

## Mostly agreed
- Light bends when it passes between air and water, and a curved water surface can make small things look bigger. *How we know:* Anyone can make a water-drop lens and see print grow. Measurements of how light bends at surfaces match the same rule for glass, water and the lens of the eye.
- The tiny living beings that Leeuwenhoek saw in water are real. *How we know:* Hooke and others saw them in 1677, and microscopes since then have shown bacteria and single-celled beings in water everywhere.
- Flies come from eggs laid by flies, not from rotting meat. *How we know:* Redi's open, sealed and cloth-covered jars, repeated by many people, give maggots only where flies could lay eggs.
- The continents move very slowly on large plates, a few centimetres each year. *How we know:* Satellite measurements can now show the movement directly. Maps of the sea floor, matching rocks and fossils on separate coasts, and magnetism in sea-floor rocks all agree.
- A result that only one person can get is weaker than one that many people can get from the same written steps. *How we know:* In 1989 two chemists announced a way to make energy in a jar of water at room temperature, which was called cold fusion. Labs that followed their steps did not find it.
- Doubting a new claim is part of how science works, and good doubt asks for a check and does not insult. *How we know:* The Royal Society, started in 1660, chose the motto 'take nobody's word for it'. Leeuwenhoek's claim became accepted because others checked.

## How we found out
**Francesco Redi, an Italian doctor and naturalist in Florence, 1668.** For thousands of years, many people believed that rotting meat makes maggots by itself. It seemed obvious, because maggots always appear on old meat. In 1668, an Italian doctor named Francesco Redi, living in Florence, wondered if the maggots came from tiny eggs laid by flies. He put pieces of meat in jars. He left some jars open. He sealed some, and he covered some with a thin cloth. Maggots appeared in the open jars, where flies could land. They did not appear on the meat in the sealed jars. On the cloth, flies laid eggs, but the maggots could not reach the meat. Redi wrote down the test so others could repeat it. Other people did, and the idea that flies come from meat was set aside. The same question about microbes was settled much later, in the 1860s, by Louis Pasteur.  
*What people believed before:* Many people believed that maggots were made by rotting meat itself, an idea called spontaneous generation. Redi's three kinds of jar showed that maggots appear only where flies can lay eggs on the meat.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Discoveries are made by lone geniuses.** (A popular idea, found in many old books; *rejected*)  
  For: Some people have unusual insight, and some discoveries were made by one person working alone for years.  
  Against: Leeuwenhoek's claim needed Hooke and others to repeat it. Wegener's idea needed sea-floor maps drawn by Tharp and others. Many discoveries were made twice at the same time. Discoveries are built by many hands.
- **If most experts reject an idea, it must be wrong.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: Most ideas that experts reject are wrong, and experts are right far more often than not.  
  Against: Wegener's idea of drifting continents was rejected by most geologists for about fifty years, and it was right. What decides is the evidence, not how many people agree.
- **A discovery counts as soon as someone first sees it.** (Some textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: Someone had to be the first, and their seeing was real.  
  Against: If nobody else can repeat what the person saw, nobody can tell a discovery from a mistake. Leeuwenhoek was believed only after Hooke repeated the look in 1677.
- **People who held wrong ideas in the past were foolish.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: We know more now than people did then.  
  Against: The idea that maggots come from meat was sensible, because people saw it again and again. It took a clever test to separate the two stories. People in the past used the evidence they had, as we do.
- **Written steps can always carry a skill from one person to another.** (Some scientists who like clear methods; *debated*)  
  For: Clear steps let strangers repeat many tests, which is how science spreads.  
  Against: Some skills, like making a good water drop or kneading dough, pass best by watching and doing. Michael Polanyi argued that we know more than we can say.

## Roots
We found that Anjana's dots became knowledge only when others could see them too. Now we ask what this tells us about how people come to know anything together.

**Deep truth.** In the lens test, one person saw the dots first, and then others saw them by following written steps. A discovery is not finished when one person sees something. It is finished when the steps are clear enough for someone else to see it too. So knowing is something people build together, out of steps that anyone can follow.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a discovery as a lonely flash in one clever person's head. That picture leaves out the steps, the doubters and the people who repeated the test, and these are where the knowing actually happens.  
*Evidence:* In your water-drop test, the second person counted dots using only your written steps. Where the steps were clear, the two of you agreed. Where they were not, the numbers drifted apart until you fixed the step.

- *who am i:* You are someone who can see something first, and someone who can be shown by others. Both are you, and both are needed.
- *what is life:* Leeuwenhoek found that a drop of pond water holds many living beings, swimming, feeding and turning. Life was there all along, in places nobody had thought to look.
- *what is this world:* The world is much bigger than what a plain eye can see. Each new tool, from a drop of water to a satellite, shows another layer, and people have to learn to read it together.

A discovery travels from one pair of eyes to many. On the way it meets doubt, and it needs steps, tools and many hands. What comes out the other end is not one person's idea any more. It is something a whole community can point to and agree on, and can change when new evidence arrives.

- **Rigveda 1.164.46: Ekam sat, viprah bahudha vadanti:** Truth is one, and the wise speak of it in many ways. (Three people looked through three drops and drew three slightly different pictures. All were looking at the same dots. The differences are the many ways of speaking, and the dots are the one.)
- **Ibn al-Haytham, a scientist who worked in Cairo about a thousand years ago, in his book Doubts Concerning Ptolemy (paraphrased):** A seeker after truth should not simply trust what is written, even by the greatest authorities. The seeker should question it and test it for themselves. (The Royal Society's motto, take nobody's word for it, says the same thing. Doubting Leeuwenhoek, and then checking, was the way to the truth.)
- Science (settled): Light bends when it passes between air and water, and a curved surface of water can magnify, as a lens does.
- Science (settled): Results that others can repeat from written steps are trusted more than results that only one person can get.
- Science (debated): How much of a skill can be passed on in written steps, and how much only by showing, is still discussed by scholars.
- Other view, Michael Polanyi, a Hungarian-British scientist and philosopher (1891 to 1976): We know more than we can tell. Much of knowing is a skill in the hands and eyes, learned from someone who has it. Written steps help, but they cannot carry all of a skill. So knowing is not only steps that anyone can follow. It is also people who have learned how.
- Practice, See it first, then show it (3 min): Look at a leaf, a coin or a flower for one minute, as if you had never seen it. Breathe in slowly three times. Now say out loud what you see, so clearly that someone in another room could find it. Notice what you needed to add.
- Ask: Think of something you can see that nobody else has noticed. How could you share it so others can see it too?
- Ask: If a result cannot be repeated by anyone else, is it knowledge or only a story?
- Ask: Does a thing exist before the first person sees it?
- Ask: What do you know only because you were shown, and not because you checked?

## Story: The Dots in the Newspaper
*A made-up story. The science in it is real: pictures in newspapers are printed as tiny dots in rows, and a water drop in a small hole makes a simple lens. The dots look like yellow, pink, blue and black in colour pictures.*

Every morning in Thrissur, Ammamma sat on the verandah with a glass of filter coffee and the newspaper. She read every page, even the tiny advertisements, with her glasses low on her nose. Anjana was ten and staying for the holidays. She sat beside her on the cool floor.

On Tuesday there was a big photograph on the front page. It showed the Pooram, with elephants standing under bright, shining umbrellas. "A real photograph," said Ammamma, tapping it. "Seeing is believing. A picture does not lie."

Anjana put her hand in her pocket. A week ago, at her school science club, she had made a lens from a strip of kitchen foil with a tiny hole in it and a drop of water in the hole. She had kept it ever since. "Ammamma, can I look at the picture with this?"

She rested the foil on two small stacks of coins above the paper and put her eye close. She moved her head up and down, a hair at a time, until the colours swam into focus. Her mouth fell open.

The elephants' umbrellas were not smooth. They were made of dots. Tiny dots in rows, yellow and pink and blue and black, far too small to see without the drop.

"Ammamma! The picture is made of dots!"

Ammamma smiled kindly, the way she smiled at children who gave a wrong answer. "Anjana. I taught school for forty years. A photograph is a photograph. You are seeing a trick of the water."

"But I saw it!"

"I am sure you saw something," said Ammamma. "That is not the same as the picture being made of dots."

Anjana held out the foil. Ammamma took off her glasses and held it the way she thought it should go. "I see a wet blur," she said. "And my own eyelash." Anjana's cousin Vishnu was called from the kitchen. He saw a bright smear, and then he dropped the foil in the coffee.

Anjana sat on the step with hot ears. She was sure about the dots. Ammamma was sure about the picture. If only she could see what Anjana saw, there would be nothing to argue about. But she could not, and Anjana did not know why.

Her aunt Remya came out with a towel. "Anju. Ammamma is not saying you are wrong. She is saying she cannot see it. You know what your teacher says. A discovery is not finished until someone else can do it."

"Miss Sheela says to write the steps so a stranger can follow them," said Anjana slowly. "Without me in the room."

She fetched her notebook and wrote, Step 1: Make a hole. She stopped. How big? With what? Which way up? She looked at the foil in the coffee. Ammamma had held it too high, like looking through a window. It had to be almost touching the paper. Anjana had never said so. She had only done it with her hands, without thinking.

She wrote seven steps. Press the tip of a sharp pencil once into the foil on a folded towel, and twist. Touch a toothpick tip, wet with one drop of water, to the hole. Rest the foil on two stacks of two coins on the picture. Put your eye as close to the foil as one finger-width. Move your head up and down very slowly until the colours sharpen. She read them twice, crossed out "close" and wrote "one finger-width" instead.

She gave Vishnu the notebook, a fresh foil strip, a toothpick and a cup of water. "I am not allowed to help," she said. "Only the words." She sat on her hands.

Vishnu read the steps with his tongue sticking out. The first drop fell through. The second stayed. For five minutes he moved his head up and down. Then he went very still. "Anju. Dots."

"What colours?" she whispered.

"Yellow. Pink. In rows. Like a kolam."

Mrs. Prameela from next door, a retired science teacher, came over for her usual chat. Remya handed her the notebook. She followed the steps one by one. "I see dots," she said, "but mine look bigger than Vishnu's, and my rows are slanted."

"Your drop is bigger," said Anjana, looking at the foil. "Mine is smaller."

"So three of us have seen three slightly different pictures of the same thing," said Mrs. Prameela, pleased. "And all of them are dots in rows. That is good evidence."

Ammamma had been watching with her arms folded. At last she said, "Give me the words, not the foil." She read the steps aloud, slowly, and did them one at a time with her glasses off. Then she was quiet for a long while.

"Forty years I have read this paper," she said, "and it was dots all along." She held the page up to the light. "I did not believe you, because I could not see it. And I could not see it because nobody had told me how."

"I only saw it first," said Anjana. "The steps showed it to everyone else."

Ammamma laughed and folded the newspaper. "Tomorrow we look at the Onam sale advertisement," she said. "Let us see what else this paper has been hiding."

**For the parent:** Anjana saw the dots first, but Ammamma could not believe it until she could see them too. What changed Ammamma's mind was not Anjana's word. It was a set of steps clear enough for others to follow. When three people followed the steps and each saw dots, the claim became something they all knew. Their pictures were not exactly alike, and that did not weaken the finding. They all agreed on dots in rows.

- Ask: Why could Ammamma not see the dots the first time?
- Ask: What did Anjana have to add to her steps so that a stranger could follow them?
- Ask: Why was it good evidence that three people saw slightly different pictures that were all dots in rows?
- Ask: Why did Anjana stay out of the way and sit on her hands?
- Ask: What changed Ammamma's mind in the end?

   Options: A direct measurement of the movement that Wegener could not make / Nothing new / A reason to doubt it / A map of the Moon  
   **Answer:** A direct measurement of the movement that Wegener could not make. *Why:* Wegener had fossils, rocks and coasts. A direct measurement of movement is the kind of evidence that was missing.
14. (truefalse) Omar says: if two people see slightly different details through the same lens, the finding must be false. Is he right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Hands, drops and eyes differ. What matters is whether they agree on the main finding.
15. (write) Should a new idea that is probably wrong still be tested? Who should pay for the test? Give your view and a reason.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer weighs the cost of a test against the chance that the idea is right, and notes that some surprising ideas, like drifting continents, were right. It gives a reasoned view on who pays.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Marks go for weighing cost against the chance of a surprise.
16. (fill) A finding that only one person has been able to get is not yet ____ knowledge.  
   **Answer:** ['shared']. *Why:* Knowledge becomes shared when other people can follow the steps and get the same result.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Why did the Royal Society doubt Leeuwenhoek's report of tiny living beings in water?  
   Options: They did not like cloth sellers / Nobody else had a lens strong enough to check it / They thought water was empty / They could not read Dutch  
   **Answer:** Nobody else had a lens strong enough to check it. *Why:* A claim that no one else can check must wait. Doubting was not rude. It asked for a way to check.
2. (mcq) When is a discovery really finished?  
   Options: When one person sees something / When the person writes it down / When others can repeat it by following the steps / When it is printed  
   **Answer:** When others can repeat it by following the steps. *Why:* A result that only one person can get could be a mistake. When others repeat it, it becomes shared knowledge.
3. (mcq) Which is the clearest step for a stranger to follow?  
   Options: Make a small hole in the foil / Put the foil on a folded towel, press the tip of a sharp pencil once into it, and twist / Make a hole with something sharp / Poke the foil  
   **Answer:** Put the foil on a folded towel, press the tip of a sharp pencil once into it, and twist. *Why:* The clear step names the tool, what to rest the foil on, the number of presses and the twist.
4. (mcq) A helper follows your written steps and gets a very different result. What should you do first?  
   Options: Tell the helper to try harder / Find the step that was unclear and rewrite it / Ignore the result / Give up  
   **Answer:** Find the step that was unclear and rewrite it. *Why:* If a careful person cannot follow the steps, the steps are the problem. Watching the helper shows which step to fix.
5. (mcq) Why does a drop of water in a small hole make print look bigger?  
   Options: The water is wet / The curved surface bends light as it passes between air and water / The water is cold / The foil is shiny  
   **Answer:** The curved surface bends light as it passes between air and water. *Why:* Light bends when it passes from air into water. A round drop bends it so that small things look bigger.
6. (mcq) Which drop usually magnifies more, a smaller, rounder one or a bigger, flatter one?  
   Options: The smaller, rounder one / The bigger, flatter one / Both the same / Neither magnifies  
   **Answer:** The smaller, rounder one. *Why:* A more strongly curved surface bends light more, so it magnifies more.
7. (mcq) Why did most geologists doubt the idea of moving continents in 1912?  
   Options: There was no evidence at all / No force that could move continents was known, and it came from outside geology / The Earth was too small / Continents are too light  
   **Answer:** No force that could move continents was known, and it came from outside geology. *Why:* The evidence was strong, but the missing 'how' made the idea easy to doubt. New sea-floor evidence in the 1960s supplied it.
8. (mcq) In a test with meat or fruit in three jars, maggots appeared in the open jar but not in the sealed one. What does this show?  
   Options: The meat made the maggots / The maggots came from flies that could reach the meat / The lid made the meat fresh / Maggots live in air  
   **Answer:** The maggots came from flies that could reach the meat. *Why:* The meat was the same in both jars. The only difference was whether flies could reach it.
9. (mcq) In the cloth-covered jar, flies landed and laid eggs on the cloth, but no maggots got into the banana. Why is this important?  
   Options: The smell got through the cloth, so smell alone does not make maggots / The cloth made flies / The banana was too ripe / Cloth is magic  
   **Answer:** The smell got through the cloth, so smell alone does not make maggots. *Why:* Smell passed through the cloth but flies could not. Without flies reaching the banana, there were no maggots. So the flies, not the smell, mattered.
10. (mcq) Why can a good idea be rejected for years?  
   Options: Good ideas are always wrong at first / It may have no explanation yet for how it works, and it goes against what experts believe / Because it is easy / Because nobody reads  
   **Answer:** It may have no explanation yet for how it works, and it goes against what experts believe. *Why:* An idea can be right but lack a mechanism, or need new tools to check it. Evidence can take years to arrive.
11. (mcq) Which is the best reason to keep believing an old idea?  
   Options: It is old / Experts say so / Many fair tests, repeated by different people, still support it / It makes a good story  
   **Answer:** Many fair tests, repeated by different people, still support it. *Why:* Age, fame and a good story do not make an idea true. Repeated fair tests do.
12. (mcq) Many discoveries rest on people who collect measurements and draw them, not only on the person whose name is on the idea. Why should those people be remembered too?  
   Options: Because without their data the pattern could not have been seen / Because they were rich / Because the idea was theirs alone / Because it is polite  
   **Answer:** Because without their data the pattern could not have been seen. *Why:* A pattern in a pile of numbers needs someone to gather them and draw them together. Leaving these people out gives a false picture of how discoveries happen.

## Applications
- Doctors use microscopes to find the tiny beings that cause malaria and other illnesses in a drop of blood.
- Spectacles, cameras and binoculars all use lenses that bend light, as a water drop does.
- Medicines are tested again by different teams before they are approved, so a result must be repeatable.
- Weather stations in India use standard instruments so that readings from different places can be compared.
- Recipe cards, building plans and instruction sheets are methods that a stranger can follow.
- Scientific journals ask authors to describe their methods so that others can repeat the work.
- Citizen scientists count birds, insects and rain in many places with the same steps, so results can be added together.
- Maps of the sea floor help us understand earthquakes, volcanoes and where to lay undersea cables.
- Courts and engineers repeat tests under the same conditions before trusting a result.
- Anyone can ask of a claim: can I try it myself, and what exactly did you do?

## Open questions
- **Did the tiny beings in water exist before Leeuwenhoek saw them?** Almost everyone says yes, but philosophers still discuss what it means for something to exist before it is known. Nobody can check by going back.
- **How much of a skill can be written down?** Some skills pass well in words and some only by watching and doing. Scholars still argue about how much of what we know can ever be put into steps.
- **How many repeats are enough before we trust a result?** More repeats make a result steadier, but there is no single number that works for every kind of test. Scientists judge each case.
- **What are we missing today because nobody has a tool to see it?** Every new tool has shown something that was hidden before. By that history, there are probably things around us that we cannot see yet. Nobody knows what.
- **How can we make sure that the people who did the work get named?** Historians keep finding people whose work was left out of the story. There is no easy rule, and each case needs careful records.
- **Why do some true ideas wait decades to be believed?** Missing explanations, missing tools and the habits of groups all play a part. Historians disagree about how much each one matters in each case.

## Connected maths
- In a water-drop lens test, hole A showed 2 dots across the circle and hole C showed 6 dots across. How many times as wide a patch of the picture is seen through C as through A? What does this say about which lens magnifies more, and by how many times? **3 times as wide. Lens A magnifies about 3 times more than lens C.**. 6 divided by 2 is 3. Through C you see 3 times as wide a patch. If a lens shows a narrower patch, each dot looks bigger. So A magnifies about 3 times as much as C. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In the Water-Drop Lens test, your counts for holes A, B and C were 2, 3 and 6. A second person, using only your steps, got 2, 4 and 6. How far apart are the two sets of counts for each hole, and in total? Do the two sets agree about the order of the holes? **The differences are 0, 1 and 0, which is 1 in total. Yes, both put A first, B second and C third.**. Hole A: 2 and 2 differ by 0. Hole B: 3 and 4 differ by 1. Hole C: 6 and 6 differ by 0. The total is 1. Both people find A has the fewest dots and C the most, so they agree on the order. Small differences are normal when two people hold a drop. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- Leeuwenhoek's claim waited about 3 years, from 1674 to 1677, before others repeated it. Wegener's idea waited about 50 years. About how many times as long did Wegener's wait last? **About 17 times as long.**. 50 divided by 3 is a little less than 17. So Wegener's wait was about 17 times as long. A claim that anyone with a good lens could check was believed fast. An idea that needed ocean maps took far longer. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- Redi tested flies in 1668. The question about microbes was settled by Pasteur in 1862. At one centimetre of string for each year, how long is that string in centimetres, and in metres and centimetres? About how many 50-centimetre Wegener strings would fit along it? **194 centimetres, which is 1 metre and 94 centimetres. About 4 Wegener strings.**. 1862 minus 1668 is 194 years, so 194 centimetres. 100 centimetres make 1 metre, so that is 1 metre and 94 centimetres. 194 divided by 50 is a bit less than 4, so about 4 strings of 50 centimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
- A sheet of paper is about 0.1 millimetre thick. Each fold doubles the layers. After 7 folds there are 128 layers. How thick is the stack in millimetres? After 10 folds there are 1024 layers. About how thick is that in centimetres? **After 7 folds, 12.8 millimetres. After 10 folds, about 10 centimetres.**. 128 layers of 0.1 millimetre is 12.8 millimetres. 1024 layers of 0.1 millimetre is 102.4 millimetres. 10 millimetres make a centimetre, so that is a little over 10 centimetres. The stack becomes thick very fast, which is why you cannot keep folding an ordinary sheet. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- In a Redi's Jars test, the flies counted near the open jar on five days were 0, 2, 5, 6 and 4. Near the cloth jar they were 0, 1, 2, 2 and 1. The lid jar had none. What are the totals? How many more flies were near the open jar than the cloth jar? About how many times as many? **Open: 17. Cloth: 6. Lid: 0. Eleven more near the open jar, about 3 times as many.**. 0 + 2 + 5 + 6 + 4 is 17. 0 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 is 6. 17 minus 6 is 11. 17 divided by 6 is a bit under 3. So about 3 times as many flies came to the open jar than to the cloth jar, and none to the lid jar. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- India moves north about 5 centimetres each year. How far is that in 1,000 years, in metres? How far in 1,000,000 years, in kilometres? **50 metres in 1,000 years. 50 kilometres in a million years.**. 5 centimetres times 1,000 is 5,000 centimetres. 100 centimetres make a metre, so that is 50 metres. 5 centimetres times 1,000,000 is 5,000,000 centimetres. 100,000 centimetres make a kilometre, so that is 50 kilometres. A movement too slow to see adds up to very far over millions of years. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)
- In a made-up sprout notebook, the longest sprouts in the light bowl were 3 millimetres on day 2 and 27 millimetres on day 6. In the dark bowl they were 4 and 34. How much did each bowl grow between day 2 and day 6? Which grew more, and by how much? **Light: 24 millimetres. Dark: 30 millimetres. The dark bowl grew 6 millimetres more.**. 27 minus 3 is 24. 34 minus 4 is 30. 30 minus 24 is 6. The dark sprouts grew a bit more. A dated notebook makes it possible to see this, and to check it later. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
