# Simple machines: how a small push moves a big load
`sci.forces.simple-machines.L3`

**Summary.** A light child can lift a heavy grown-up on a see-saw, just by sitting further from the middle. A see-saw is a lever, one of the simple machines. The six simple machines are the lever, the inclined plane (a ramp), the wheel and axle, the pulley, the wedge and the screw. Each one makes a push or a pull easier, for example by letting a small force move a heavy load. But no machine gives something for nothing: a smaller push always has to travel a longer way. The same lever rule works in your elbow, inside your muscles, and between the Earth and the Moon.

**Intro.** *Can you lift a grown-up off the ground, using only a plank? Most children think they are far too light. On a see-saw, a light child can do it, if she sits in the right place. Where is that place, and why does it work?* About 2,200 years ago, a Greek thinker called Archimedes worked out the rule of the lever. A lever is a long, stiff bar that turns on one point. A later writer, Pappus, says Archimedes boasted: 'Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth.' Was he showing off? Or was he right? By the end of this unit, you can decide.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a simple machine is, and name the six kinds: lever, inclined plane, wheel and axle, pulley, wedge and screw.
1. I can find the fulcrum, the load and the effort on a lever, and spot levers at home and in my own body.
2. I can balance a see-saw or a ruler, and explain that weight further from the fulcrum turns more. A light thing far out can balance a heavy thing close in.
3. I can explain how an inclined plane, or ramp, lets me raise a load with a smaller push, by going a longer way. I can explain that a wedge and a screw are inclined planes too.
4. I can explain how wheels help: rolling instead of dragging, and a big wheel on a thin axle that turns a small push into a strong twist.
5. I can run a fair test with a ruler lever or a ramp, record my results in a table and a bar chart, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
6. I can explain that machines never give something for nothing: a smaller push means a longer distance. I can also give big examples of the lever rule: my arm, tiny levers in my muscles, and the Earth and the Moon balancing round one point.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces make things start, stop, speed up, slow down, turn or change shape. *e.g.* You pull a door open. You push a swing.
- **Machine**: Anything that makes a push or a pull easier to do. A machine can let a small force move a heavy load, change the direction of a force, or make something move further and faster. It does not need a motor. *e.g.* A spoon used to open a tin lid is a machine. So is a bicycle.
- **Simple machine**: A machine with very few parts. There are six kinds: the lever, the inclined plane, the wheel and axle, the pulley, the wedge and the screw. *e.g.* A see-saw, a slide and a doorknob are all simple machines.
- **Load**: The thing a machine moves or lifts. *e.g.* On a bottle opener, the load is the bottle cap.
- **Effort**: The force you put into a machine: your push or your pull. *e.g.* Your hand pressing down on the handle of a bottle opener.
- **Lever**: A stiff bar that turns on one point, called the fulcrum. You push on one part of the bar, and another part moves the load. *e.g.* A see-saw, a spoon opening a tin, and a hand pump are levers.
- **Fulcrum**: The point a lever turns on. It is also called the pivot. *e.g.* The middle post of a see-saw is its fulcrum. The screw in a pair of scissors is a fulcrum.
- **See-saw**: A long plank with a fulcrum in the middle. One side goes up when the other goes down. It is a lever. *e.g.* The see-saw in the park.
- **Balance**: When the turning effect on one side of a fulcrum is just as strong as on the other side, so nothing tips. *e.g.* A vegetable seller's weighing scale balances when the vegetables weigh the same as the weights.
- **Turning effect**: How strongly a force makes something turn round a fulcrum. It depends on how big the force is and how far it is from the fulcrum. Older children call it a moment, or torque. *e.g.* Pushing a door near its handle turns it easily. Pushing near the hinge hardly turns it at all.
- **Inclined plane (ramp)**: A flat, sloping surface that joins a low place to a high place. Its everyday name is a ramp. Pushing a load up a long ramp takes less force than lifting it straight up. *e.g.* The slope at a railway station for trolleys and wheelchairs.
- **Wheel**: A round disc that turns on its middle. *e.g.* Bicycle wheels, the wheels of a bullock cart, and a potter's wheel.
- **Axle**: The rod through the middle of a wheel. The wheel turns round it, or turns with it. *e.g.* A toy car's wheels are joined by thin metal axles.
- **Wheel and axle**: A big wheel or handle fixed to a thin rod, the axle, so that they turn together. A small push on the big wheel makes a strong twist on the thin axle. *e.g.* A doorknob, a tap handle, a screwdriver handle and the winding handle of a well.
- **Roller**: A round thing put under a heavy load so the load rolls instead of sliding. *e.g.* Round logs under a boat being pulled up a beach.
- **Wedge**: A piece of hard material that narrows to a thin edge, like two inclined planes back to back. When the thin edge is pushed in, its sloping sides push things apart sideways. *e.g.* A knife, an axe and your front teeth are wedges.
- **Screw**: An inclined plane wrapped round a rod. The spiral ridge is called the thread. Each turn moves the screw forward only a little, so a small twist gives a strong push. *e.g.* A wood screw goes into a plank as you turn it. A jar lid has a thread inside, and the jar has one outside.
- **Pulley**: A wheel with a groove, and a rope running over it. Pulling down on one end of the rope lifts the load on the other end. A single pulley changes the direction of your pull, not its size. You will meet pulleys properly in Class 4. *e.g.* The wheel above a village well, used to pull up the bucket. The rope that raises a flag on a flagpole.
- **Work (in science)**: In science, work means a force moving something through a distance. You find it by multiplying the push by the distance moved. A machine can make the push smaller, but then the distance gets longer. *e.g.* Carrying a bag up a long ramp takes a small push for a long way. Lifting it straight up takes a big push for a short way.
- **Energy**: What it takes to make something move, warm up or change. Energy never appears from nowhere and never disappears. It only changes from one kind into another. *e.g.* Food gives your muscles energy. Your muscles give it to the see-saw.
- **Balance point (centre of mass)**: The one point where a thing balances, as if all its weight were there. *e.g.* A ruler balances on your finger at its middle. A hammer balances near its heavy head.
- **Trade-off**: When you gain one thing by giving up another. *e.g.* A ramp makes the push smaller, but you have to go a longer way.

## Explanations
### What a simple machine is · *defined*
**Definition:** A simple machine is a tool with few or no moving parts. It makes a push or a pull easier, by changing how big the force is, which way it points, or how far it moves.

We often think a machine must have a motor. It doesn't. A spoon prying open a tin lid is a machine. So is a plank used as a ramp. A machine can do three jobs. It can turn a small force into a bigger one, like a bottle opener. It can change the direction of a force, like the pulley wheel over a well, where you pull down to lift the bucket up. Or it can make something move further and faster than your hand, like a broom. Some machines do two of these at once. Big machines, like bicycles and cranes, are many simple machines joined together.
- e.g. A bottle opener turns a small push on its handle into a big pull on the cap.
- e.g. A pulley over a well lets you pull down to lift the bucket up.
- e.g. A bicycle has levers (the brakes), wheels and axles (the wheels and pedals) and a chain.
### The lever: fulcrum, load and effort · *defined*
**Definition:** A lever is a stiff bar that turns on a point called the fulcrum. You give a push, the effort, at one place. The lever moves the load at another place.

Think of a spoon opening a tin of ghee. The rim of the tin is the fulcrum. The lid is the load. Your hand on the far end of the spoon is the effort. Your hand moves a long way down. The tip of the spoon moves only a tiny way up. But it pushes up much harder than you pushed down. That is the lever's trick. The further your hand is from the fulcrum, compared with the lid, the harder the spoon pushes on the lid.
- e.g. A spoon prying a lid off a tin.
- e.g. A hand pump on a village borewell. You push the long handle down, and the short end lifts the water rod.
- e.g. A crowbar lifting a heavy stone.
### See-saw balance: weight and distance · *defined*
**Definition:** A see-saw or lever balances when the weight on one side, times its distance from the fulcrum, equals the weight on the other side, times its distance.

On a see-saw, the heavier person does not always go down. Distance matters too. A weight far from the fulcrum turns the plank more than the same weight close in. So a child can balance a grown-up who is twice as heavy, by sitting twice as far from the middle. Look at the chart. Two coins sit 5 cm from the pencil, and 2 times 5 is 10. One coin sits 10 cm away, and 1 times 10 is 10. Both sides make 10, so the ruler balances. Archimedes wrote this rule down about 2,200 years ago.
- e.g. A heavy grown-up sits close to the middle of a see-saw. A small child sits at the far end. They balance.
- e.g. A vegetable seller's tarazu has two pans the same distance from the middle. So it balances when both pans weigh the same.
- e.g. One coin 10 cm from a pencil balances two coins 5 cm from it.
### No free gifts: a smaller push goes a longer way · *defined*
**Definition:** A machine can make a push smaller, but then the push must travel a longer distance. Push times distance stays the same, or a little more because of friction.

Here is the deepest idea in this unit. Machines never give you something for nothing. When a spoon lifts a tin lid, your hand moves far, and the lid moves only a little. When you walk up a long ramp, you push less, but you walk further. Scientists call push times distance 'work'. A machine cannot make the work smaller. It only spreads it out into a smaller push over a longer way. This is because energy is never made from nothing. Friction, the grip between touching things, always takes a little extra. So a real machine always needs a little more work than the ideal one.
- e.g. A long ramp: small push, long walk. Lifting straight up: big push, short distance.
- e.g. A see-saw: the child at the far end moves down a long way. The grown-up near the middle rises only a little.
- e.g. A hand pump: your hand moves a long way down, and the rod inside moves a short way up, with a bigger force.
### Three families of levers · *defined*
**Definition:** Levers come in three families, depending on what is in the middle: the fulcrum, the load or the effort.

In the first family, the fulcrum is in the middle, like a see-saw or a pair of scissors. These can make your push stronger. In the second family, the load is in the middle, like a nutcracker or a wheelbarrow. These also make your push stronger. In the third family, your effort is in the middle, like kitchen tongs or a broom. These make your push weaker at the far end, not stronger. So why use them? Because the far end moves further and faster than your hand. A broom's bristles sweep a wide curve while your lower hand moves only a little. Older children call these first-class, second-class and third-class levers.
- e.g. Fulcrum in the middle: see-saw, scissors, the handle of a hand pump.
- e.g. Load in the middle: nutcracker, wheelbarrow, bottle opener, the supari cutter called a sarota.
- e.g. Effort in the middle: chimta tongs, a broom, your own forearm.
### The inclined plane: a gentle slope instead of a big lift · *defined*
**Definition:** An inclined plane, or ramp, is a sloping surface that joins a low place to a high place. The longer and gentler the ramp, the smaller the push needed to move a load up it.

Lifting a heavy box straight up onto a step takes a big push. Pushing it up a ramp takes a smaller push. But you push it a longer way. Look at the chart. A newton is the unit scientists use to measure a push or a pull. To raise a bag 1 metre, you can lift it straight up with a push of 12 newtons. Up a ramp 2 metres long, you need only about 6 newtons. Up a ramp 4 metres long, about 3. Every time, push times distance is 12. Real ramps also have friction, so the push is a bit bigger. Mountain roads that zigzag up the ghats are ramps too. Because they are long and gentle, a truck needs a smaller push from its engine to climb them.
- e.g. Ramps at railway stations, for wheelchairs, trolleys and suitcases.
- e.g. The zigzag roads that climb the ghats to hill stations.
- e.g. A plank used to roll a heavy drum up into a truck.
### Wheels, rollers and the wheel and axle · *defined*
**Definition:** Wheels help in two ways. Rolling is much easier than dragging. And a big wheel fixed to a thin axle turns a small push on the wheel into a strong twist on the axle.

The first trick is rolling. A dragged box rubs along the ground the whole way. A wheel touches the ground at only one small spot, and that spot lifts off as the wheel turns. So there is far less rubbing, which scientists call friction. The second trick is the wheel and axle. Turning a tap's thin rod with bare fingers is hard. Its wide handle makes it easy. Your hand goes round a big circle. The rod turns round a small circle, but strongly. A wheel and axle is a lever that goes round and round. Doorknobs, screwdriver handles, steering wheels and the handle of a well winch all work this way.
- e.g. A screwdriver with a fat handle turns a tight screw more easily than one with a thin handle.
- e.g. The wide handle on a well winch lifts a heavy bucket.
- e.g. A bicycle's pedal cranks turn the front gear wheel.
### Wedges and screws: inclined planes in disguise · *defined*
**Definition:** A wedge is two inclined planes back to back. A screw is an inclined plane wrapped round a rod.

When an axe hits a log, its thin edge goes in a little. Its sloping sides push the two halves of the wood apart sideways, with a much bigger force than the blow. A screw is a long ramp wound round and round a rod. Turning it many times moves it in only a little, but very strongly. That is the trade-off again: a small push over a long way. You can see it for yourself. Cut a triangle from paper, with one square corner. Lay its shortest side along a pencil, and roll the paper round the pencil. The sloping edge winds round in a spiral, just like a screw thread.
- e.g. A knife, an axe and a nail's point are wedges.
- e.g. Your front teeth are wedges, for biting. Your back teeth are flatter, for grinding.
- e.g. A wood screw, a jar lid and a bottle top have screw threads.
### Your body is full of levers · *defined*
**Definition:** Your bones are levers. Your joints are their fulcrums. Your muscles give the effort.

Bend your arm with a water bottle in your hand. Your elbow is the fulcrum. Your biceps, the muscle at the front of your upper arm, pulls on your forearm very close to the elbow. In a grown-up, that is about 5 cm from the elbow. Your hand holds the bottle about 35 cm away. 35 divided by 5 is 7. So the muscle must pull about 7 times as hard as the bottle's weight. Why would a body be built so badly? It isn't bad. It is a trade-off. Because the muscle is close to the fulcrum, your hand moves 7 times as far and as fast as the muscle shortens. That lets you throw, wave and catch. Standing on tiptoe uses your foot as a lever with the load in the middle. Your jaw is a lever too. Its fulcrum is the joint just in front of your ear.
- e.g. Your forearm, with the elbow as fulcrum: effort in the middle, for speed.
- e.g. Standing on tiptoe: the ball of your foot is the fulcrum, and your calf muscle lifts your whole weight.
- e.g. Nodding your head: the joint at the top of your spine is the fulcrum. Your head rocks on it like a see-saw.
### Tiny levers inside your muscles · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside every muscle are billions of tiny molecules called myosin. Each one has a small arm that swings like a lever, pulling on a thread and making the muscle shorter.

In 1954, two teams found out how muscles shorten. Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson were one team. Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke were the other. Muscles are made of two kinds of thin threads that slide past each other. Later, scientists found what does the pulling. Myosin is a tiny molecule with a head and a stiff neck. The head grabs the other kind of thread. A small change near the head is made bigger by the stiff neck, which swings like a lever. In 1993, Ivan Rayment and his team first saw the shape of a myosin head and its lever arm. Each swing moves the thread only a few millionths of a millimetre. But billions of swings together lift your arm. So the lever rule works far below what any eye can see. Scientists still study exactly how each step is timed.
- e.g. Every time you blink, millions of myosin levers swing.
- e.g. Your heart beats because myosin levers in its muscle pull, again and again, all your life.
### The Earth and the Moon on a see-saw · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth and the Moon both swing round one shared balance point. Like a see-saw, the balance point sits much closer to the heavier one, the Earth.

We say the Moon goes round the Earth. That is nearly true, but not quite. Imagine the Earth and Moon on the ends of a giant, invisible see-saw. The Earth is about 81 times heavier than the Moon. So the balance point must be about 81 times closer to the Earth's centre than to the Moon's. That puts it about 4,700 kilometres from the Earth's centre. The Earth is about 6,400 kilometres from its centre to its surface. So the balance point is inside the Earth, about 1,700 kilometres under our feet. Every month the Earth wobbles round it. The Sun and Jupiter have a balance point too. It sits just outside the Sun's surface. In 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz saw a far-away star wobbling like this. The wobble showed a planet was going round it. It was the first planet found going round a star like our Sun. They shared a Nobel Prize in 2019. Thousands of planets have been found since, many of them by their star's wobble.
- e.g. Hold hands with a grown-up and spin round together. You both turn round a point nearer the grown-up.
- e.g. Astronomers find planets round other stars by watching the stars wobble.
### Who first used levers, ramps and wheels? · *debated*
Nobody knows who first used a lever. It was long before anyone wrote things down. Different people get the credit in different stories. Here are the main ones.
- e.g. The shaduf is a pole on a post with a bucket at one end and a heavy stone at the other. People in Mesopotamia and Egypt used it to lift water, about 4,000 years ago.
- e.g. Clay toy carts with wheels have been found at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, cities of the Indus Valley, about 4,500 years old.
- e.g. One of the oldest wooden wheels ever found, from a marsh near Ljubljana in Slovenia, is about 5,100 years old.
- **Archimedes, a Greek thinker in Syracuse, who lived about 2,200 years ago:** He did not invent the lever. But he was the first we know of to prove its rule with geometry: weight times distance must match on both sides.
- **Thinkers in China called the Mohists, writing at about the same time:** Their writings also describe how a balance arm tips when a weight is moved further out. Their work is much less famous in Western books.
- **Al-Jazari, an engineer in what is now Turkey, who finished his Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices in 1206:** He drew and explained dozens of machines: water-lifting machines with wheels, cranks and levers, and clocks with moving figures. One of his water pumps turned round-and-round movement into back-and-forth movement, an idea still used in many machines today. His name is missing from many textbooks.
- **Farmers, builders and women working at home, whose names were never written down:** They used levers, ramps and rollers for thousands of years before anyone wrote rules. In India, the dhenki rice pounder and the well lever called the picottah or etram are two examples. The dhenki was worked mostly by women.
### How were giant stones raised long ago? · *debated*
People built pyramids, temples and forts with stones far too heavy to lift by hand. They had no engines. Most experts agree they used ramps, levers, sledges and rollers, and very many workers. But exactly how they did it is still argued about.
- e.g. The Great Pyramid in Egypt has more than two million stone blocks.
- e.g. The tower of the Brihadeeswarar Temple in Thanjavur, about 1,000 years old, is about 66 metres tall. It is topped by a huge capstone.
- **Many archaeologists:** Long ramps of earth and mud brick were built up beside the pyramid, and the stones were dragged up on sledges. In 2018, a team from France and Britain found a steep ramp with stairs and posts at an old quarry in Egypt, called Hatnub. Ropes may have run round the posts, like a simple pulley.
- **Jean-Pierre Houdin, a French architect, and others:** A ramp may have spiralled up inside the pyramid itself. Many archaeologists are not convinced.
- **A story told in Thanjavur:** The capstone was dragged up a very long earth ramp, starting from a village several kilometres away. Historians are not sure the story is true, or whether the capstone is one stone or several.

## Mostly agreed
- A lever balances when weight times distance from the fulcrum is the same on both sides. *How we know:* Archimedes proved it about 2,200 years ago. Anyone can test it with a ruler, a pencil and some coins, and it works every time.
- Machines can make a push smaller or change its direction, but they never give out more work than you put in. *How we know:* Every careful measurement of a lever, ramp or pulley shows it. A smaller push always goes a longer way. Nobody has ever built a machine that keeps going by itself for ever.
- A longer, gentler ramp needs a smaller push to raise a load to the same height. *How we know:* Pull a loaded toy truck up ramps of different lengths with a rubber band or a forcemeter. The longer ramp needs less pull.
- Rolling a load on wheels or rollers takes much less push than dragging it. *How we know:* Builders have used rollers and wheels for thousands of years. A forcemeter, a spring tool that measures pulls, shows the difference straight away.
- Your bones and muscles work as levers, with your joints as fulcrums. *How we know:* Doctors and scientists have measured where muscles join the bones, using X-rays and careful study of bodies. The lever rule predicts how hard each muscle must pull.

## How we found out
**Simon Stevin, a Flemish engineer, after centuries of failed self-turning wheels, About 1150 to 1775, and Stevin's book in 1586.** For hundreds of years, many inventors believed a clever machine could give out more than you put in. In about 1150, the Indian mathematician Bhaskara II described a wheel that carried mercury, a heavy liquid metal, sealed inside tubes. He thought it would turn for ever by itself. Inventors in Europe tried wheels like it for centuries. None of them worked. In 1586, a Flemish engineer called Simon Stevin used this failure to work out how ramps work. He imagined a loop of beads, like a necklace, hung over a triangle with one long, gentle slope and one short, steep slope. More beads lie on the long slope. Will they pull the loop round? If they did, the loop would keep turning for ever, and that never happens. So the loop must stay still. That means a few beads on a steep slope pull as hard as many beads on a gentle one. So a load needs a smaller pull up a longer, gentler slope. Stevin was so pleased that he put the picture of the beads on the title page of his book. In 1775, the French Academy of Sciences decided to stop checking machines that claimed to run for ever.  
*What people believed before:* Many inventors believed a clever machine could give out more than you put in, and keep turning for ever. Stevin used the fact that this never happens to work out how ramps work.

## Challenging perspectives
- **On a see-saw, the heavier person always goes down.** (Most of us, before we try it; *rejected*)  
  For: If two people sit at the very ends, the heavier one does go down.  
  Against: Distance from the fulcrum matters as much as weight. A heavy grown-up who slides close to the middle can be lifted by a small child at the far end.
- **Simple machines mean less work.** (A common way of talking about machines; *rejected*)  
  For: A machine really does make a job feel easier. You need a smaller push.  
  Against: In science, work means push times distance. A machine makes the push smaller but the distance longer, so the work stays the same. Friction even adds a little. Machines make jobs easier, not smaller.
- **A clever enough wheel could keep turning for ever by itself, giving free energy.** (Bhaskara II, a great Indian mathematician, described such a wheel in about 1150. Many inventors in Europe and elsewhere tried for centuries after him.; *rejected*)  
  For: His wheel carried mercury, a heavy liquid metal, sealed inside tubes. The idea was that the mercury would always make one side heavier, so the wheel would keep turning.  
  Against: No such wheel has ever worked. The weights shift, but the turning effects on both sides always end up balanced, and friction slowly stops the wheel. Energy cannot come from nothing. Bhaskara II was a brilliant mathematician, and this one idea did not work. Even great thinkers can be wrong.
- **Archimedes invented the lever.** (Many popular books and quiz shows; *rejected*)  
  For: Archimedes wrote down the law of the lever, and proved it. He is famous for it.  
  Against: Farmers were lifting water with levers like the shaduf almost 2,000 years before he was born. Archimedes explained the lever. People whose names nobody wrote down invented it and used it.
- **The peoples of ancient Mexico never knew about the wheel.** (Some older books; *rejected*)  
  For: The Aztecs, the Maya and their neighbours did not use carts or wagons.  
  Against: Archaeologists have found little clay animal toys on wheels in Mexico, many centuries old. So they knew the wheel. Why they did not use it for carts is still debated. They had no horses or oxen to pull carts. Their land had many jungles and steep mountains.
- **With a long enough lever, Archimedes really could have moved the Earth.** (A boast that the writer Pappus says Archimedes made; *rejected*)  
  For: The lever rule has no limit. In theory, a long enough lever turns any small push into any big push.  
  Against: The lever would have to be unimaginably long, and much stiffer than any material. He would need a place to stand outside the Earth. And to lift the Earth by even 1 centimetre, his hand would have to travel so far that it would take longer than the age of the universe. The rule is right, but the job is impossible.

## Roots
We have been learning how levers, inclined planes and wheels let a small push move a heavy load. Now we ask what that tells us about strength, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** On the ruler see-saw, one coin 12 cm from the pencil balanced two coins 6 cm from it. As the ruler tips, the single coin moves twice as far as the pair. So a small push can lift a big load, as long as the push moves further than the load.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think lifting something heavy needs a strong push. A lever lets a light push lift a heavy load, by making the push travel further.  
*Evidence:* In the ruler see-saw, one coin 12 cm from the pencil balanced two coins 6 cm from it. In the story, Jonaki lifted the dhenki's heavy head only by standing at the very end of the beam.

- *who am i:* Your arm is a lever, with your elbow as its fulcrum. Your muscle pulls close to the elbow, so your hand can move far and fast.
- *what is life:* Every movement you make comes from billions of tiny myosin molecules swinging like levers inside your muscles.
- *what is this world:* No machine anywhere has ever given out more work than was put in. A smaller push always has to travel further.

A lever, an inclined plane, a wheel and axle, a pulley, a wedge and a screw look like six different tools. Underneath, they all make the same trade: a smaller force over a longer distance. The push times the distance stays the same, so nothing is made and nothing is lost. It is only shared out differently. Physics keeps finding this: things that look separate follow one rule underneath. Six machines sharing one rule is a small, clear example, and a child can test it with a ruler and some coins.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 3, the story of Cook Ding:** A cook carves an ox so skilfully that his knife stays sharp for nineteen years. He never hacks at bone. He guides the blade through the natural gaps between the joints. (A knife is a wedge. Cook Ding puts it exactly where the joints already come apart, so a small push does the cutting. Like Jonaki at the end of the dhenki beam, where the force goes matters as much as how big it is.)
- **Milindapanha, book 2, the chariot:** The monk Nagasena asks King Milinda: is the chariot the wheels? The axle? The pole? No single part is the chariot. 'Chariot' is the name for all the parts put together. (A bicycle is levers, wheels and axles and a chain, working as one. 'Bicycle' is the name for all of them together. Your body is the same, with its bones, muscles and joints.)
- Science (settled): A machine can make a push smaller, but it never makes energy. A smaller push always has to travel further. Friction turns a little of the energy into heat.
- Science (settled): Inside your muscles, tiny molecules called myosin swing like levers and pull thin threads past each other. That is how every muscle shortens.
- Other view, René Descartes, a French philosopher of the 1600s: Descartes argued that animal bodies are machines: parts pushing parts, like a clock, with nothing more to them. Many scientists today also explain living bodies as very complex machines.
- Practice, Find your lever (2 min): Let's hold one arm out in front, palm up. Slowly bend it at the elbow and bring your hand to your shoulder. Then lower it slowly. Do it three times, breathing out as you lift. Put your other hand on your upper arm and feel the muscle work. The muscle moves only a little, but your hand travels a long way. Your elbow is the fulcrum.
- Ask: Jonaki was no stronger at the end of the beam. So where did her extra lifting come from?
- Ask: Are you a machine? What would make you more than one?
- Ask: Is there anything in the world you get for nothing?
- Ask: If no single part is the chariot, where is the chariot?

## Deep and big questions

## Story: Where Jonaki Stood
*The dhenki is a real foot-worked rice pounder, used in Assam, Bengal, Odisha and beyond, and mostly worked by women. Magh Bihu, the feast night of Uruka and pitha rice cakes are real. Jonaki, Bubu, Pehi and what happened to them are made up.*

In a village in Assam, at the edge of the rice fields, the dhenki lived in a shed of its own. It was a long beam of jackfruit wood, as long as three children lying head to toe. It rested on a short wooden post, nearer one end than the other, so it could rock like a see-saw. From the long end hung a heavy wooden head with an iron ring on its nose. Under the head was a hollow in the floor, where the rice went. When someone stepped down hard on the short end, the head rose into the air. When they stepped off, the head fell. Thud. And the rice in the hollow turned, little by little, into flour.

Every winter, before Magh Bihu, the harvest festival, the dhenki woke up. The women of Jonaki's house soaked the new rice overnight. Then they pounded it for pitha, the sweet rice cakes of the festival, stuffed with sesame and jaggery. Jonaki's mother stepped on the beam, steady as a clock. Her aunt Pehi crouched by the hollow with a long bamboo stick. Between thuds, she pushed the rice back under the head. Thud, push. Thud, push. The whole shed smelled of wet rice and woodsmoke.

Jonaki was eight. Every year she asked to step on the dhenki, and every year Pehi laughed. She lifted Jonaki by the armpits, as if she were weighing a fish. 'Too light,' she said. 'The dhenki listens only to heavy feet.'

On the morning of Uruka, the feast night before Magh Bihu, a neighbour came running. Her cow was giving birth, and the calf was stuck. Jonaki's mother knew cows better than anyone in the village. She tied up her mekhela, the long wrapped skirt, and ran. Pehi ran after her with a bucket of warm water.

'Keep the rice covered,' her mother called over her shoulder. 'We'll be back soon.'

They were not back soon. The sun climbed over the bamboo, and then began to slide down the other side. Jonaki lifted the cloth from the basket. The soaked rice was drying at the edges. Rice that dries before it is pounded makes flour that cracks. Pitha made from it falls apart in your fingers. Tonight the whole village would eat together, and her family would bring nothing whole.

Jonaki went into the shed. Her little brother Bubu, who was five, followed her like a shadow. She tipped a handful of rice into the hollow. She climbed onto the short end of the beam and stood in the middle of it, where it felt steady and comfortable. She pressed down with all her weight.

Nothing happened. The head sat in the hollow, as heavy as a sleeping dog.

She jumped. The beam shivered, and the head lifted no more than a finger's width. Bubu laughed, and then he climbed up beside her and jumped too. The head twitched and settled. They jumped until their legs ached and Bubu started to cry.

'Too light,' Jonaki said, in Pehi's voice, and hated it.

She sat down on the cold floor with her back against the post and looked at the beam. It was dark brown all over, rubbed shiny with oil and years. Except in one place. At the very tip of the short end, the wood was worn pale, as smooth as a river stone. There was a shallow dip in it, the shape of a heel.

Jonaki looked at that dip for a long time. That was where her mother's heel went. And Pehi's. And the heels of all the women who had pounded rice in this shed, winter after winter, before Jonaki was born. Not in the steady, comfortable middle. Right at the very end, as far from the post as a foot could go.

She thought of the see-saw at school. When big Dipen sat at the far end, she shot up into the air and stayed there. But once he slid in close to the middle, to tease her, and suddenly she could push him up. She had not been any heavier that day. She had only been further away from the middle than he was.

Jonaki stood up. She stepped onto the beam and walked along it to the very end. She put her heel into the pale dip, where it fitted as if it had been waiting. She pressed down.

The head rose. Not a finger's width this time, but a whole hand's height. Then it dropped, with a small, honest thud. Some grains of rice jumped.

'Bubu,' she said. 'Come here. Not there. Here, right at the end, with me.'

Bubu climbed up and held on to her waist. His small feet squeezed into the dip beside hers. 'Now,' said Jonaki. They pressed down together.

The head swung up, up, as high as Jonaki's knee. 'Off!' They hopped back, and it fell. THUD. The whole shed shook, and the rice in the hollow leapt like popcorn.

They found the rhythm slowly. Press together, lift, hop off, thud. Jonaki jumped down after every few thuds and pushed the rice back with Pehi's long bamboo stick, never her fingers. Then she climbed back to the end. Bubu made up a song to keep time. It had only four words, and he sang them so loudly that the hens ran away.

The light went orange, then grey. When Jonaki's mother came home, with mud on her mekhela and a new calf safe behind her in the neighbour's shed, she heard the thudding from the road. She thought Pehi must have run back early. She looked into the shed.

There were two children at the far end of the dhenki, holding on to each other. Beside the hollow sat a basket, nearly full of soft white flour.

Her mother did not say anything at first. She looked at their feet, pressed into the pale dip at the very tip of the beam. Then she laughed, very quietly.

'So,' she said. 'The dhenki listens to light feet too.'

'Only if they stand in the right place,' said Jonaki.

That night, under the stars, the whole village ate together round the fires of Uruka. Jonaki's family's pitha did not crack. And in the dark shed at the edge of the fields, the pale dip at the end of the beam was very slightly deeper than it had been that morning.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Jonaki does not get heavier. She moves to the very end of the beam, as far from the fulcrum as she can. On a lever, a force far from the fulcrum turns it much more than the same force close in. So her small weight at the tip lifts the heavy head that her weight near the middle could not. When Bubu adds his weight at the same far point, the turning grows again. The pale, worn dip at the end of the beam is the record of generations of women who knew this rule long before anyone wrote it down. The story also quietly asks whose knowledge gets written down, and whose is kept in wood and habit.

- Ask: When Jonaki stood in the middle of the beam, nothing happened. What did she change to make the head rise?
- Ask: Why do you think the wood at the very tip of the beam was worn pale?
- Ask: How did remembering the see-saw at school help Jonaki?
- Ask: Pehi said the dhenki listens only to heavy feet. Was she right, wrong, or half right?
- Ask: Jonaki learned the rule from the women who used the dhenki before her. Who in your family knows something useful that was never written in a book?

- **fair test:** Keep the load and the pencil the same. Change only the size of the handle you turn. Count how many turns it takes to lift the bottle 20 cm, and say how hard each one felt.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes in the box with the point of a pair of scissors.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw your winch and label the wheel, or handle, and the axle.
- **results table:** What you turned | How hard it felt: easy, medium or hard | Turns to lift the bottle 20 cm | How far your hand travelled: short, medium or long
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up makes a hole in each long side of the box, near the top. Put both holes about 3 cm from one short end. 2. Push the pencil through both holes, so it can turn. Leave about 3 cm sticking out on one side. 3. Tape one end of the string to the middle of the pencil. 4. Put the box on a table, with that short end right at the table's edge. Put a heavy book in or on the far end of the box, so it cannot tip. 5. Run the string over the short end and down past the table's edge. 6. Tie the bottle to it.  
  *Parent says:* This is a model of a well winch. The pencil is the axle. Winding it will pull the bottle up, the way a real winch pulls up a bucket.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes two holes in the box. Push a pencil through. Tape string to the pencil. Hang the bottle over the table's edge.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn the bare pencil with your fingertips to wind up the string. 2. Count the turns to lift the bottle 20 cm. 3. Notice how hard it is.  
  *Parent says:* Wind it up by twisting the bare pencil. How does it feel?  
  *Child mode:* Twist the bare pencil with your fingers. Lift the bottle. Is it easy or hard? Count the turns.
  - Note: Turning the bare pencil takes a firm grip, and fingers may slip. That is the point. If it feels easy, add more water to the bottle.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the small card handle firmly to the end of the pencil that sticks out, so it points sideways like a crank. 2. Wind the bottle up by turning the handle's tip. 3. Then do the same with the big handle. 4. Count turns and note how hard each felt.  
  *Parent says:* Now a small handle, then a big one. Turn it from the very tip. What changes?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the small handle on the pencil. Turn it by its tip. Now try the big handle. Which is easiest?
  - Ask: Why is the big handle easiest? (good: My hand is further from the pencil.; My hand goes round a bigger circle with a small push.)
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare how far your hand travelled with each handle, for the same lift.  
  *Parent says:* The bottle went up the same 20 cm each time. Did your hand travel the same distance?  
  *Child mode:* The bottle went up 20 cm each time. With the big handle, did your hand travel further?
  - Check: With the big handle, the winding was easier. What did your hand do? → **It travelled round a bigger circle**
  - Think: A tap, a doorknob and a screwdriver handle all work like your winch. Can you find one at home?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child about real well winches. Tell them about the rahat, or Persian wheel. It lifted water in India for centuries, with a chain of pots.  
  *Parent says:* Wells in many villages had winches like this, with a long handle. Big wheels turned by bullocks lifted pots of water for the fields.  
  *Child mode:* Real wells had winches like yours. Some had giant wheels turned by bullocks!
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me what you found out, in three parts. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a bigger handle makes winding easier. What I saw: turning the bare pencil was hard, the small handle was medium and the big handle was easy. My hand went round a much bigger circle with the big handle. Why: my push was further from the axle, so a small push made a strong twist.

## Worksheets
### Machine Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Many people think a machine must have a motor. Which of these is a machine with no motor?  
   Options: A spoon used to prise the lid off a tin / A pebble lying on the ground / A puddle of water / A cloud  
   **Answer:** A spoon used to prise the lid off a tin. *Why:* A simple machine is a tool with few parts that makes a push or a pull easier. The spoon is a lever, so it is a machine. Machines with motors are only one kind. A see-saw, a ramp and a spoon have no motor at all.
2. (fill) A see-saw turns on the post in its middle. The point a lever turns on is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['fulcrum', 'pivot']. *Why:* The fulcrum, also called the pivot, is where a lever turns. On scissors it is the screw in the middle, and on a door it is the hinges.
3. (match) A spoon is used to lift the lid off a tin of ghee. Match each part to its name.  
   **Answer:** the rim of the tin, where the spoon rests and turns → fulcrum; the tight lid → load; your hand pressing down on the handle → effort. *Why:* The fulcrum is where the lever turns. The load is what you want to move. The effort is your push. Mixing up the load and the effort is the most common slip.
4. (mcq) Bhavana is light. Her uncle is heavy. They get on a see-saw. Where should her uncle sit, so that they balance?  
   Options: At the very end / Closer to the middle / On Bhavana's side / Nowhere. They can never balance  
   **Answer:** Closer to the middle. *Why:* Weight close to the fulcrum turns the plank less. So the heavy uncle moves in towards the middle, and Bhavana stays far out. Picking 'never balance' means only weight was counted. Distance matters too.
5. (truefalse) On a see-saw, the heavier person always goes down, wherever they sit.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Distance from the fulcrum matters as much as weight. A heavy person close to the middle can be lifted by a light person far out. Jonaki, in this unit's story, lifted a heavy rice pounder this way.
6. (write) In the ruler see-saw test, one coin balanced two coins. Where did the one coin have to sit? Why there?  
   **Answer:** The one coin had to sit twice as far from the pencil as the two coins. For example, two coins at 6 cm were balanced by one coin at 12 cm. Coins times distance was 12 on both sides.. *Why:* A strong answer uses real numbers from the test and says that weight and distance together decide the turning.
7. (sort) Sort these into the simple machine each one really is.  
   **Answer:** scissors → Lever; a bottle opener → Lever; a slide in the park → Ramp; the slope at a railway station → Ramp; a doorknob → Wheel and axle; a tap → Wheel and axle; an axe blade → Wedge; a jar lid → Screw. *Why:* Scissors and openers turn on a fulcrum. ### Balance Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Predict: 2 coins sit on left peg 3, and 3 coins sit on right peg 2. What will the see-saw do?  
   Options: Tips left / Tips right / Balances  
   **Answer:** Balances. *Why:* 2 times 3 is 6. 3 times 2 is 6. Both sides make 6, so it balances. Picking 'tips right' means counting coins and forgetting distance.
2. (fill) A key on peg 4 balances 2 coins on peg 2. The key weighs the same as ____ coin.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* 2 coins times 2 is 4. The key times 4 must also be 4. So the key weighs 1 coin. This is how you weigh an unknown thing with a lever.
3. (mcq) Why can't a wheel with shifting weights keep turning for ever by itself?  
   Options: It could, nobody has tried hard enough / The turning on both sides always balances out, and friction slowly stops it / Wheels are too heavy / It needs to be bigger  
   **Answer:** The turning on both sides always balances out, and friction slowly stops it. *Why:* Every design ever tried has failed. Energy cannot come from nothing. Picking 'nobody has tried hard enough' is a hopeful guess. In fact people have tried for over 800 years, since Bhaskara II described one in about 1150.
4. (mcq) The Earth is about 81 times heavier than the Moon. Where is their balance point?  
   Options: Exactly halfway between them / Much closer to the Moon / Much closer to the centre of the Earth, inside the Earth / There isn't one  
   **Answer:** Much closer to the centre of the Earth, inside the Earth. *Why:* Like a heavy grown-up near the middle of a see-saw, the heavy Earth is close to the balance point. It is about 4,700 km from the Earth's centre, inside the Earth. Picking 'halfway' means forgetting that the Earth is far heavier.
5. (truefalse) Astronomers can find planets round other stars by watching the stars wobble.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A planet and its star swing round a shared balance point, so the star wobbles a little. In 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found a planet this way.
6. (mcq) Arvind, a boy in Class 5, says, 'A single pulley over a well makes the bucket lighter.' What is wrong with his idea?

   **Answer:** The bucket weighs just the same. The pulley only lets you pull down instead of up. *Why:* A single fixed pulley changes the direction of your pull, not its size. Pulling down is easier, because you can use your own weight. To need a smaller pull, you must use more pulleys together, and then you pull a longer length of rope.

## Applications
- Hand pumps on village borewells use a long lever handle to lift water.
- Scissors, nutcrackers, the supari cutter called a sarota, tongs and bottle openers are all levers.
- A vegetable seller's tarazu, a two-pan balance, weighs vegetables using the see-saw rule.
- Ramps at railway stations, hospitals, malls and temples let wheelchairs, trolleys and prams in.
- Zigzag roads up the ghats and the Himalayas are long ramps that let vehicles climb.
- Loading planks let workers roll heavy drums and gas cylinders into trucks.
- Taps, doorknobs, screwdrivers and steering wheels are wheels and axles.
- Bicycles combine levers (brake handles), wheels and axles (wheels and pedals) and a chain.
- Wheelbarrows and two-wheeled hand carts combine a lever and a wheel.
- Cranes on building sites use levers, pulleys and counterweights.
- Knives, axes, ploughs and chisels are wedges.
- Jar lids, bottle caps, screws and spiral staircases are screws, ramps wrapped round.
- Doctors and physiotherapists use the lever rule to understand how muscles move bones.
- Astronomers find planets round other stars by watching the stars wobble round their balance point.
- Chemists now build machines out of single molecules. Three of them won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2016 for it.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how were the pyramids and other giant ancient buildings raised?** Most experts agree ramps, levers and sledges were used. Which kinds of ramp, and how they were arranged, is still argued about, and new finds keep changing the picture.
- **How did the builders of India's temples and stepwells, like those in Gujarat and Rajasthan, lift and place stones weighing many tonnes?** Historians have good guesses: ramps, levers, rollers, ropes, pulleys and huge teams of workers. For most buildings, nobody wrote down exactly how it was done, or the names of the builders.
- **Who first used a lever, a ramp or a wheel?** It happened long before writing, and wooden tools rot away. The oldest wheels and toy carts we have found may not be the first.
- **Why did the peoples of ancient Mexico use wheels on toys but not on carts?** There are several ideas: no animals to pull carts, rough land, or other ways that worked better. Historians still debate it.
- **Exactly how does each tiny myosin lever in a muscle time its swing?** Scientists can now see myosin's shape and watch single molecules move. The fine details of each step are still being studied.
- **How small can a machine be?** Chemists have built tiny motors, switches and even a 'nanocar' from single molecules. How useful such machines can become is still unknown.
- **How many planets like the Earth are there round other stars?** Stars' wobbles, and dips in their light, have revealed thousands of planets. Small, Earth-like planets make tiny wobbles that are very hard to see.
- **Was the Thanjavur temple's capstone really hauled up a ramp several kilometres long?** It is a well-known story, but there is no written record from the time it was built. Historians are still unsure.

## Connected maths
- On a ruler see-saw, 3 coins sit 4 cm from the pencil. How far away must 2 coins sit on the other side to balance them? **6 cm.**. On the left, 3 coins times 4 cm is 12. On the right, 2 coins times something must also be 12. 2 times 6 is 12. So the 2 coins sit 6 cm from the pencil. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)
- In the ramp test, suppose lifting the toy truck straight up 10 cm took 6 newtons on a forcemeter. Your long ramp was 60 cm long, reaching the same 10 cm high pile. With no friction, about how big would the pull up the long ramp be? **About 1 newton.**. The ramp is 60 cm long and the pile is 10 cm high. 60 divided by 10 is 6, so the truck travels 6 times as far. Push times distance stays the same, so the pull is about 6 times smaller. 6 newtons divided by 6 is 1 newton. In real life friction adds a little, so you would measure a bit more. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- A doorstep is 15 cm high. Ramp guides say a ramp should be 12 times as long as it is high. How long should the ramp be? Give your answer in metres and centimetres. **180 cm, which is 1 metre and 80 centimetres.**. 15 times 12: 15 times 10 is 150, and 15 times 2 is 30. 150 plus 30 is 180 cm. 100 cm make 1 metre, so 180 cm is 1 metre and 80 centimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- Your biceps pulls 5 cm from your elbow. Your hand holds a water bottle 35 cm from your elbow. How many times as far from the elbow is your hand? **7 times.**. 35 divided by 5 is 7. Check: 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 is 35. So the hand is 7 times as far from the fulcrum. That means the muscle must pull about 7 times as hard as the bottle's weight, and the hand moves 7 times as far. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Think of the park see-saw. Suppose you weigh 20 kg and sit 2 metres from the middle. Your grown-up weighs 40 kg. How far from the middle must the grown-up sit to balance? **1 metre.**. Child: 20 times 2 is 40. Grown-up: 40 times something must be 40. 40 times 1 is 40. So the grown-up sits 1 metre from the middle. The grown-up is twice as heavy, so sits half as far. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)
- In the ramp test, the rubber band stretched 9 cm when the truck was lifted straight up, 4 cm on the short ramp and 2 cm on the long ramp. Draw a bar chart. How much less did it stretch on the long ramp than straight up? **7 cm less. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each way, as tall as its stretch. Then 9 take away 2 is 7. So the band stretched 7 cm less on the long ramp. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)

## Warm-up
- The point a lever turns on is called the ____. → Fulcrum. It is also called the pivot.
- On a see-saw, where should a heavy grown-up sit to balance a light child? → Closer to the middle.
- Lever, ramp or wheel: what is a slide? → A ramp, which scientists call an inclined plane.
- Lever, ramp or wheel: what is a pair of scissors? → Two levers joined at one fulcrum.
- Lever, ramp or wheel: what is a doorknob? → A wheel and axle.
- A longer ramp needs a smaller push. What is the catch? → You push a longer way.
- When you bend your arm, where is the fulcrum? → Your elbow.
- 2 coins at 5 cm balance 1 coin at how many centimetres? → 10 cm, because 2 times 5 and 1 times 10 both make 10.
- Who wrote down the law of the lever about 2,200 years ago? → Archimedes. But people used levers long before him.
- Where is the balance point of the Earth and the Moon? → Inside the Earth, about 1,700 km under the surface.
- Can a machine give you more than you put in? → No. There is no free push.
