# Simple machines: how a small push moves a big load
`sci.forces.simple-machines.L3`

**Summary.** This unit is about simple machines. A simple machine is a tool with few moving parts that makes a push or a pull easier. Levers, ramps and wheels are the three you will meet most. A see-saw is a lever. It balances when the weight on each side, times its distance from the middle, comes out the same. So a light child far out can balance a heavy grown-up sitting close in. Machines never give you something for nothing. A smaller push always has to travel a longer way. The same lever rules work in your elbow, in tiny parts of your muscles, and between the Earth and the Moon.

**Intro.** *Can you lift a grown-up off the ground? You are allowed to use only a plank and the right place to sit. Many children think they can't. Then they try a see-saw.* About 2,200 years ago, a Greek thinker called Archimedes worked out the rule of the lever. A lever is a long, stiff bar that turns on one point. A later writer, Pappus, says Archimedes boasted: 'Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth.' Was he showing off? Or was he right? By the end of this unit, you can decide.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a simple machine is: a tool that makes a push or a pull easier, by changing how big it is or which way it goes.
1. I can find the pivot, the load and the effort on a lever, and spot levers at home and in my own body.
2. I can balance a see-saw or a ruler, and explain that weight further from the pivot turns more. A light thing far out can balance a heavy thing close in.
3. I can explain how a ramp lets me raise a load with a smaller push, by going a longer way.
4. I can explain how wheels help: rolling instead of dragging, and a big wheel on a thin axle that turns a small push into a strong twist.
5. I can run a fair test with a ruler lever or a ramp, record my results in a table and a bar chart, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
6. I can explain that machines never give something for nothing: a smaller push means a longer distance. I can also give big examples of the lever rule: my arm, tiny levers in my muscles, and the Earth and the Moon balancing round one point.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces make things start, stop, speed up, slow down, turn or change shape. *e.g.* You pull a door open. You push a swing.
- **Machine**: Anything that helps us push or pull, by making the force smaller, bigger, or point another way. A machine does not need a motor. *e.g.* A spoon used to open a tin lid is a machine. So is a bicycle.
- **Simple machine**: A machine with very few parts. The main kinds are the lever, the ramp, the wheel and axle, the wedge, the screw and the pulley. *e.g.* A see-saw, a slide and a doorknob are all simple machines.
- **Load**: The thing you want to move or lift. *e.g.* On a bottle opener, the load is the bottle cap.
- **Effort**: The push or pull you give to make a machine work. *e.g.* Your hand pressing down on the handle of a bottle opener.
- **Lever**: A stiff bar that turns on one point. You push one part of it, and another part moves the load. *e.g.* A see-saw, a spoon opening a tin, and a hand pump are levers.
- **Pivot (fulcrum)**: The point a lever turns on. Scientists also call it the fulcrum. *e.g.* The middle post of a see-saw is its pivot. The screw in a pair of scissors is a pivot.
- **See-saw**: A long plank with a pivot in the middle. One side goes up when the other goes down. It is a lever. *e.g.* The see-saw in the park.
- **Balance**: When the turning on one side of a pivot is just as strong as the turning on the other side, so nothing tips. *e.g.* A sabzi seller's weighing scale balances when the vegetables weigh the same as the weights.
- **Turning effect**: How strongly a force makes something turn round a pivot. It depends on how big the force is and how far it is from the pivot. Older children call it a moment, or torque. *e.g.* Pushing a door near its handle turns it easily. Pushing near the hinge hardly turns it at all.
- **Ramp (inclined plane)**: A sloping surface that joins a low place to a high place. Scientists call it an inclined plane. *e.g.* The slope at a railway station for trolleys and wheelchairs.
- **Wheel**: A round disc that turns on its middle. *e.g.* Bicycle wheels, the wheels of a bullock cart, and a potter's wheel.
- **Axle**: The rod through the middle of a wheel. The wheel turns round it, or turns with it. *e.g.* A toy car's wheels are joined by thin metal axles.
- **Wheel and axle**: A big wheel or handle fixed to a thin rod, so they turn together. A small push on the big wheel makes a strong twist on the thin rod. *e.g.* A doorknob, a tap handle, a screwdriver handle and the winding handle of a well.
- **Roller**: A round thing put under a heavy load so the load rolls instead of sliding. *e.g.* Round logs under a boat being pulled up a beach.
- **Wedge**: Two ramps back to back, with a thin edge. It splits or cuts things. *e.g.* A knife, an axe and your front teeth are wedges.
- **Screw**: A ramp wrapped round a rod, like a spiral staircase. *e.g.* A jar lid and a bottle top screw on. A spiral staircase is a giant screw you can walk up.
- **Pulley**: A wheel with a rope running over it. Pulling down on the rope lifts a load up. You will meet pulleys properly in Class 4. *e.g.* The wheel over a village well, used to pull up the bucket.
- **Work (in science)**: In science, work means a force moving something along a distance. Push times distance. Machines can make the push smaller, but then the distance gets longer. *e.g.* Carrying a bag up a long ramp takes a small push for a long way. Lifting it straight up takes a big push for a short way.
- **Energy**: What it takes to make something move, warm up or change. Energy never appears from nowhere and never disappears. It only changes from one kind into another. *e.g.* Food gives your muscles energy. Your muscles give it to the see-saw.
- **Balance point (centre of mass)**: The one point where a thing balances, as if all its weight were there. *e.g.* A ruler balances on your finger at its middle. A hammer balances near its heavy head.
- **Trade-off**: When you gain one thing by giving up another. *e.g.* A ramp makes the push smaller, but you have to go a longer way.

## Explanations
### What a simple machine is · *defined*
**Definition:** A simple machine is a tool with few or no moving parts. It makes a push or a pull easier, by changing how big the force is or which way it points.

We often think a machine must have a motor. It doesn't. A spoon prying open a tin lid is a machine. So is a plank used as a ramp. Every machine does one of two jobs. It can make a small force into a bigger one, like a bottle opener. Or it can change the direction of a force, like a well wheel, where you pull down to lift the bucket up. Some machines do both. Big machines, like bicycles and cranes, are many simple machines joined together.
- e.g. A bottle opener turns a small push on its handle into a big pull on the cap.
- e.g. A pulley over a well lets you pull down to lift the bucket up.
- e.g. A bicycle has levers (the brakes), wheels and axles (the wheels and pedals) and a chain.
### The lever: pivot, load and effort · *defined*
**Definition:** A lever is a stiff bar that turns on a point called the pivot. You give a push, the effort, at one place. The lever moves the load at another place.

Think of a spoon opening a tin of ghee. The rim of the tin is the pivot. The lid is the load. Your hand on the far end of the spoon is the effort. Your hand moves a long way down. The tip of the spoon moves only a tiny way up. But it pushes up much harder than you pushed down. That is the lever's trick. The further your hand is from the pivot, compared with the load, the stronger the push on the load.
- e.g. A spoon prying a lid off a tin.
- e.g. A hand pump at a village well. You push the long handle down, and the short end lifts the water.
- e.g. A crowbar lifting a heavy stone.
### See-saw balance: weight and distance · *defined*
**Definition:** A see-saw or lever balances when the weight on one side, times its distance from the pivot, equals the weight on the other side, times its distance.

On a see-saw, the heavier person does not always go down. Distance matters too. A weight far from the pivot turns the plank more than the same weight close in. So a child can balance a grown-up twice as heavy. The child sits twice as far from the middle. Look at the chart. Two coins sit 5 cm from the pencil, and 2 times 5 is 10. One coin sits 10 cm away, and 1 times 10 is 10. Both sides make 10, so the ruler balances. Archimedes wrote this rule down about 2,200 years ago.
- e.g. A heavy grown-up sits close to the middle of a see-saw. A small child sits at the far end. They balance.
- e.g. A sabzi seller's tarazu has two pans the same distance from the middle. So it balances when both pans weigh the same.
- e.g. One coin 10 cm from a pencil balances two coins 5 cm from it.
### No free gifts: a smaller push goes a longer way · *defined*
**Definition:** A machine can make a push smaller, but then the push must travel a longer distance. Push times distance stays the same, or a little more because of friction.

Here is the deepest idea in this unit. Machines never give you something for nothing. When a spoon lifts a tin lid, your hand moves far, and the lid moves only a little. When you walk up a long ramp, you push less, but you walk further. Scientists call push times distance 'work'. A machine cannot make the work smaller. It only spreads it out into a smaller push over a longer way. This is because energy is never made from nothing. Friction, the grip between touching things, always takes a little extra. So a real machine always needs a little more work than the ideal one.
- e.g. A long ramp: small push, long walk. Lifting straight up: big push, short distance.
- e.g. A see-saw: the child at the far end moves down a long way. The grown-up near the middle rises only a little.
- e.g. A hand pump: your hand moves a long way, and the water rises a short way, but heavily.
### Three families of levers · *defined*
**Definition:** Levers come in three families, depending on what is in the middle: the pivot, the load or the effort.

In the first family, the pivot is in the middle, like a see-saw or a pair of scissors. In the second family, the load is in the middle, like a nutcracker or a wheelbarrow. These make your push stronger. In the third family, your effort is in the middle, like kitchen tongs or a broom. These make your push weaker, not stronger. So why use them? Because the far end moves further and faster than your hand. A broom's bristles sweep a wide arc while your lower hand barely moves. Older children call these first-class, second-class and third-class levers.
- e.g. Pivot in the middle: see-saw, scissors, a hand pump handle.
- e.g. Load in the middle: nutcracker, wheelbarrow, bottle opener, the supari cutter called a sarota.
- e.g. Effort in the middle: chimta tongs, a broom, a cricket bat swung from the shoulder, your own forearm.
### Ramps: a gentle slope instead of a big lift · *defined*
**Definition:** A ramp is a slope that joins a low place to a high place. The longer and gentler the ramp, the smaller the push needed to move a load up it.

Lifting a heavy box straight up onto a step takes a big push. Pushing it up a ramp takes a smaller push. But you push it a longer way. Look at the chart. To raise a bag 1 metre, you can lift it straight up with 12 newtons. A newton is the unit scientists use to measure a push or pull. Up a ramp 2 metres long, you need only about 6 newtons. Up a ramp 4 metres long, about 3. Every time, push times distance is 12. Real ramps also have friction, so the push is a bit bigger. Mountain roads that zigzag up the ghats are ramps too. They are long, so trucks can climb without needing a huge push.
- e.g. Ramps at railway stations, for wheelchairs, trolleys and suitcases.
- e.g. The zigzag roads that climb the ghats to hill stations.
- e.g. A plank used to roll a heavy drum up into a truck.
### Wheels, rollers and the wheel and axle · *defined*
**Definition:** Wheels help in two ways. Rolling is much easier than dragging. And a big wheel fixed to a thin axle turns a small push on the wheel into a strong twist on the axle.

The first trick is rolling. A dragged box rubs along the ground the whole way. A wheel touches the ground at only one small spot, which lifts off as it turns, so there is far less rubbing. You met this in the Friction unit. The second trick is the wheel and axle. Turning a tap's thin spindle with bare fingers is hard. Its wide handle makes it easy. Your hand goes round a big circle. The spindle turns a small circle, but strongly. This is a lever that goes round and round. Doorknobs, screwdriver handles, steering wheels and the cranks of a well all work this way.
- e.g. Try opening a door by twisting the thin metal spindle with the knob taken off. Then use the knob.
- e.g. The wide handle on a well winch lifts a heavy bucket.
- e.g. A bicycle's pedal cranks turn the front gear wheel.
### Wedges and screws: ramps in disguise · *defined*
**Definition:** A wedge is two ramps back to back. A screw is a ramp wrapped round a rod.

When an axe hits a log, its thin edge goes in a little. Its sloping sides push the wood apart, much harder and sideways. A screw is a long ramp wound round and round. Turning it many times moves it in only a little, but very strongly. That is the trade-off again: a small push over a long way. You can see it for yourself. Cut a paper triangle and wrap it round a pencil, starting from one of the short sides. Its sloping edge makes a spiral, just like a screw thread.
- e.g. A knife, an axe and a nail's point are wedges.
- e.g. Your front teeth are wedges. Your back teeth crush like a nutcracker.
- e.g. Jar lids, bottle tops and spiral staircases are screws.
### Your body is full of levers · *defined*
**Definition:** Your bones are levers. Your joints are their pivots. Your muscles give the effort.

Bend your arm with a water bottle in your hand. Your elbow is the pivot. Your biceps, the muscle at the front of your upper arm, pulls on your forearm very close to the elbow. In a grown-up, that is about 5 cm from the elbow. Your hand holds the bottle about 35 cm away. 35 divided by 5 is 7. So the muscle must pull about 7 times as hard as the bottle's weight. Why would a body be built so badly? It isn't bad. It is a trade-off. Because the muscle is close to the pivot, your hand moves 7 times as far and as fast as the muscle shortens. That lets you throw, wave and catch. Standing on tiptoe uses your foot as a lever with the load in the middle. Your jaw cracks nuts like a nutcracker.
- e.g. Your forearm, with the elbow as pivot: effort in the middle, for speed.
- e.g. Standing on tiptoe: the ball of your foot is the pivot, and your calf muscle lifts your whole weight.
- e.g. Nodding your head: your neck is the pivot, a see-saw on top of your spine.
### Tiny levers inside your muscles · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside every muscle are millions of tiny molecules called myosin. Each one has a small arm that swings like a lever, pulling on a thread and making the muscle shorter.

In 1954, two teams found out how muscles shorten. Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson were one team. Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke were the other. Muscles are made of two kinds of thin threads that slide past each other. Later, scientists found what does the pulling. Myosin is a tiny molecule with a head and a stiff neck. The head grabs a thread. A small change near the head is made bigger by the stiff neck, which swings like a lever. In 1993, Ivan Rayment and his team first saw the shape of a myosin head and its lever arm. Each swing moves the thread only a few millionths of a millimetre. But billions of swings together lift your arm. So the lever rule works far below what any eye can see. Scientists still study exactly how each step is timed.
- e.g. Every time you blink, millions of myosin levers swing.
- e.g. Your heart beats because myosin levers in its muscle pull, again and again, all your life.
### The Earth and the Moon on a see-saw · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth and the Moon both swing round one shared balance point. Like a see-saw, the balance point sits much closer to the heavier one, the Earth.

We say the Moon goes round the Earth. That is nearly true, but not quite. Imagine the Earth and Moon on the ends of a giant, invisible see-saw. The Earth is about 81 times heavier than the Moon. So the balance point must be about 81 times closer to the Earth's centre than to the Moon's. That puts it about 4,700 kilometres from the Earth's centre. The Earth is about 6,400 kilometres from its centre to its surface. So the balance point is inside the Earth, about 1,700 kilometres under our feet. Every month the Earth wobbles round it. The Sun and Jupiter have a balance point too. It sits just outside the Sun's surface. In 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz saw a far-away star wobbling like this. The wobble showed a planet was going round it. It was one of the first planets found round a star like our Sun. They shared a Nobel Prize in 2019. Thousands of planets have been found since, many of them by their star's wobble.
- e.g. Hold hands with a grown-up and spin round together. You both turn round a point nearer the grown-up.
- e.g. Astronomers find planets round other stars by watching the stars wobble.
### Who first used levers, ramps and wheels? · *debated*
Nobody knows who first used a lever. It was long before anyone wrote things down. Different people get the credit in different stories. Here are the main ones.
- e.g. The shaduf is a pole on a post with a bucket at one end and a heavy stone at the other. People in Mesopotamia and Egypt used it to lift water, about 4,000 years ago.
- e.g. Clay toy carts with wheels have been found at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, cities of the Indus Valley, about 4,500 years old.
- e.g. One of the oldest wooden wheels ever found, from a marsh near Ljubljana in Slovenia, is about 5,100 years old.
- **Archimedes, a Greek thinker in Syracuse, who lived from about 287 to 212 years before the Common Era:** He did not invent the lever. But he was the first we know of to prove its rule with geometry: weight times distance must match on both sides.
- **Thinkers in China called the Mohists, writing at about the same time:** Their writings also describe how a balance arm tips when a weight is moved further out. Their work is much less famous in Western books.
- **Al-Jazari, an engineer in what is now Turkey, who finished his Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices in 1206:** He drew and explained dozens of machines: water-lifting machines with wheels, cranks and levers, and clocks with moving figures. His name is missing from many textbooks, though the crank he described is in engines today.
- **Farmers, builders and women working at home, whose names were never written down:** They used levers, ramps and rollers for thousands of years before anyone wrote rules. In India, the dhenki rice pounder and the well lever called the picottah or etram are two examples. The dhenki was worked mostly by women.
### How were giant stones raised long ago? · *debated*
People built pyramids, temples and forts with stones far too heavy to lift by hand. They had no engines. Most experts agree they used ramps, levers, sledges and rollers, and very many workers. But exactly how they did it is still argued about.
- e.g. The Great Pyramid in Egypt has more than two million stone blocks.
- e.g. The tower of the Brihadeeswarar Temple in Thanjavur, about 1,000 years old, is about 66 metres tall. It is topped by a huge capstone.
- **Many archaeologists:** Long ramps of earth and mud brick were built up beside the pyramid, and the stones were dragged up on sledges. In 2018, a team from France and England found a steep ramp with stairs and posts at an old quarry in Egypt, called Hatnub. Ropes may have run round the posts, like a simple pulley.
- **Jean-Pierre Houdin, a French architect, and others:** A ramp may have spiralled up inside the pyramid itself. Many archaeologists are not convinced.
- **A story told in Thanjavur:** The capstone was dragged up a very long earth ramp, starting from a village several kilometres away. Historians are not sure the story is true, or whether the capstone is one stone or several.

## Mostly agreed
- A lever balances when weight times distance from the pivot is the same on both sides. *How we know:* Archimedes proved it about 2,200 years ago. Anyone can test it with a ruler, a pencil and some coins, and it works every time.
- Machines can make a push smaller or change its direction, but they never give out more work than you put in. *How we know:* Every careful measurement of a lever, ramp or pulley shows it. A smaller push always goes a longer way. Nobody has ever built a machine that keeps going by itself for ever.
- A longer, gentler ramp needs a smaller push to raise a load to the same height. *How we know:* Pull a loaded toy truck up ramps of different lengths with a rubber band or a forcemeter. The longer ramp needs less pull.
- Rolling a load on wheels or rollers takes much less push than dragging it. *How we know:* Builders have used rollers and wheels for thousands of years. A forcemeter, a spring tool that measures pulls, shows the difference straight away.
- Your bones and muscles work as levers, with your joints as pivots. *How we know:* Doctors and scientists have measured where muscles join the bones, using X-rays and careful study of bodies. The lever rule predicts how hard each muscle must pull.

## Challenging perspectives
- **On a see-saw, the heavier person always goes down.** (Most of us, before we try it; *rejected*)  
  For: If two people sit at the very ends, the heavier one does go down.  
  Against: Distance from the pivot matters as much as weight. A heavy grown-up who slides close to the middle can be lifted by a small child at the far end.
- **Simple machines mean less work.** (A common way of talking about machines; *rejected*)  
  For: A machine really does make a job feel easier. You need a smaller push.  
  Against: In science, work means push times distance. A machine makes the push smaller but the distance longer, so the work stays the same. Friction even adds a little. Machines make jobs easier, not smaller.
- **A clever enough wheel could keep turning for ever by itself, giving free energy.** (Bhaskara II, a great Indian mathematician, described such a wheel in about 1150. Many inventors in Europe and elsewhere tried for centuries after him.; *rejected*)  
  For: His wheel had curved spokes partly filled with mercury, a heavy liquid metal. The idea was that one side would always be heavier, so it would always keep turning.  
  Against: No such wheel has ever worked. The weights shift, but the turning effects on both sides always end up balanced, and friction slowly stops the wheel. Energy cannot come from nothing. Bhaskara II was a brilliant mathematician, and this one idea did not work. Even great thinkers can be wrong.
- **Archimedes invented the lever.** (Many popular books and quiz shows; *rejected*)  
  For: Archimedes wrote down the law of the lever, and proved it. He is famous for it.  
  Against: Farmers were lifting water with levers like the shaduf almost 2,000 years before he was born. Archimedes explained the lever. Unnamed workers, many of them women, invented and used it.
- **The peoples of ancient Mexico never knew about the wheel.** (Some older books; *rejected*)  
  For: The Aztecs, the Maya and their neighbours did not use carts or wagons.  
  Against: Archaeologists have found little clay animal toys on wheels in Mexico, many centuries old. So they knew the wheel. Why they did not use it for carts is still debated. They had no horses or oxen to pull carts. Their land had many jungles and steep mountains.
- **With a long enough lever, Archimedes really could have moved the Earth.** (A boast that the writer Pappus says Archimedes made; *debated*)  
  For: The lever rule has no limit. In theory, a long enough lever turns any small push into any big push.  
  Against: The lever would have to be unimaginably long, and much stiffer than any material. He would need a place to stand outside the Earth. And to move the Earth even a tiny bit, he would have to push for longer than the age of the universe. The rule is right, but the job is impossible.

- *deep:* Could the same rule that balances a see-saw in the park also work for planets and stars? (hint: Think of the Earth and the Moon sitting on the two ends of a see-saw.)
- *big:* On a see-saw, a light child can balance a heavy grown-up by sitting further out. Is fair always the same as equal? (hint: Do both people need the same seat to make the see-saw level? What does each one need?)
- *big:* Ramps help people in wheelchairs, parents with prams and grandparents with sore knees. Who decides whether a building has a ramp, and who is left out when it doesn't? (hint: Look at the steps of a shop, a temple or a school near you. Could everyone get in?)
- *big:* Archimedes gets the credit for the lever, but farmers used levers long before him. Who should get the credit: the person who uses an idea, or the person who writes it down? (hint: Think of a recipe your family makes that nobody ever wrote down.)
- *big:* A machine trades a small push for a long distance. Where do you make trade-offs in your own life? (hint: Saving pocket money takes a long time, but buys something bigger. Is that a kind of lever?)
- *big:* If you had a lever long enough, what would you want to move, and where would you stand? (hint: Remember Archimedes' boast about moving the Earth.)
- *big:* Your muscles are moved by billions of tiny molecules that swing like levers. Are you a machine? (hint: What can you do that a see-saw can't? What can a machine do that you can't?)
- *big:* The peoples of ancient Mexico made toys with wheels but never built carts. Is a better tool always the best choice? (hint: What would a cart need to be useful: roads, animals, flat land?)
- *deep:* Why does a little push far from a door's hinge open it more easily than a big push close to the hinge? (hint: Try it. Where does the door turn from?)

## Story: Where Jonaki Stood
*The dhenki is a real foot-worked rice pounder, used in Assam, Bengal, Odisha and beyond, and mostly worked by women. Magh Bihu, the feast night of Uruka and pitha rice cakes are real. Jonaki, Bubu, Pehi and what happened to them are made up.*

In a village in Assam, at the edge of the rice fields, the dhenki lived in a shed of its own. It was a long beam of jackfruit wood, as long as three children lying head to toe. It rested on a short wooden post, nearer one end than the other, so it could rock like a see-saw. From the long end hung a heavy wooden head with an iron ring on its nose. Under the head was a hollow in the floor, where the rice went. When someone stepped down hard on the short end, the head rose into the air. When they stepped off, the head fell. Thud. And the rice in the hollow turned, little by little, into flour.

Every winter, before Magh Bihu, the harvest festival, the dhenki woke up. The women of Jonaki's house soaked the new rice overnight. Then they pounded it for pitha, the sweet rice cakes of the festival, stuffed with sesame and jaggery. Jonaki's mother stepped on the beam, steady as a clock. Her aunt Pehi crouched by the hollow with a long bamboo stick. Between thuds, she pushed the rice back under the head. Thud, push. Thud, push. The whole shed smelled of wet rice and woodsmoke.

Jonaki was eight. Every year she asked to step on the dhenki, and every year Pehi laughed. She lifted Jonaki by the armpits, as if she were weighing a fish. 'Too light,' she said. 'The dhenki listens only to heavy feet.'

On the morning of Uruka, the feast night before Magh Bihu, a neighbour came running. Her cow was giving birth, and the calf was stuck. Jonaki's mother knew cows better than anyone in the village. She tied up her mekhela, the long wrapped skirt, and ran. Pehi ran after her with a bucket of warm water.

'Keep the rice covered,' her mother called over her shoulder. 'We'll be back soon.'

They were not back soon. The sun climbed over the bamboo, and then began to slide down the other side. Jonaki lifted the cloth from the basket. The soaked rice was drying at the edges. Rice that dries before it is pounded makes flour that cracks. Pitha made from it falls apart in your fingers. Tonight the whole village would eat together, and her family would bring nothing whole.

Jonaki went into the shed. Her little brother Bubu, who was five, followed her like a shadow. She tipped a handful of rice into the hollow. She climbed onto the short end of the beam and stood in the middle of it, where it felt steady and comfortable. She pressed down with all her weight.

Nothing happened. The head sat in the hollow, as heavy as a sleeping dog.

She jumped. The beam shivered, and the head lifted no more than a finger's width. Bubu laughed, and then he climbed up beside her and jumped too. The head twitched and settled. They jumped until their legs ached and Bubu started to cry.

'Too light,' Jonaki said, in Pehi's voice, and hated it.

She sat down on the cold floor with her back against the post and looked at the beam. It was dark brown all over, rubbed shiny with oil and years. Except in one place. At the very tip of the short end, the wood was worn pale, as smooth as a river stone. There was a shallow dip in it, the shape of a heel.

Jonaki looked at that dip for a long time. That was where her mother's heel went. And Pehi's. And the heels of all the women who had pounded rice in this shed, winter after winter, before Jonaki was born. Not in the steady, comfortable middle. Right at the very end, as far from the post as a foot could go.

She thought of the see-saw at school. When big Dipen sat at the far end, she shot up into the air and stayed there. But once he slid in close to the middle, to tease her, and suddenly she could push him up. She had not been any heavier that day. She had only been further away from the middle than he was.

Jonaki stood up. She stepped onto the beam and walked along it to the very end. She put her heel into the pale dip, where it fitted as if it had been waiting. She pressed down.

The head rose. Not a finger's width this time, but a whole hand's height. Then it dropped, with a small, honest thud. Some grains of rice jumped.

'Bubu,' she said. 'Come here. Not there. Here, right at the end, with me.'

Bubu climbed up and held on to her waist. His small feet squeezed into the dip beside hers. 'Now,' said Jonaki. They pressed down together.

The head swung up, up, as high as Jonaki's knee. 'Off!' They hopped back, and it fell. THUD. The whole shed shook, and the rice in the hollow leapt like popcorn.

They found the rhythm slowly. Press together, lift, hop off, thud. Jonaki jumped down after every few thuds and pushed the rice back with Pehi's long bamboo stick, never her fingers. Then she climbed back to the end. Bubu made up a song to keep time. It had only four words, and he sang them so loudly that the hens ran away.

The light went orange, then grey. When Jonaki's mother came home, with mud on her mekhela and a new calf safe behind her in the neighbour's shed, she heard the thudding from the road. She thought Pehi must have run back early. She looked into the shed.

There were two children at the far end of the dhenki, holding on to each other. Beside the hollow sat a basket, nearly full of soft white flour.

Her mother did not say anything at first. She looked at their feet, pressed into the pale dip at the very tip of the beam. Then she laughed, very quietly.

'So,' she said. 'The dhenki listens to light feet too.'

'Only if they stand in the right place,' said Jonaki.

That night, under the stars, the whole village ate together round the fires of Uruka. Jonaki's family's pitha did not crack. And in the dark shed at the edge of the fields, the pale dip at the end of the beam was very slightly deeper than it had been that morning.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Jonaki does not get heavier. She moves to the very end of the beam, as far from the pivot as she can. On a lever, a force far from the pivot turns it much more than the same force close in. So her small weight at the tip lifts the heavy head that her weight near the middle could not. Adding Bubu at the same far point doubles the turning. The pale, worn dip at the end of the beam is the record of generations of women who knew this rule long before anyone wrote it down. The story also quietly asks whose knowledge gets written down, and whose is kept in wood and habit.

- Ask: When Jonaki stood in the middle of the beam, nothing happened. What did she change to make the head rise?
- Ask: Why do you think the wood at the very tip of the beam was worn pale?
- Ask: How did remembering the see-saw at school help Jonaki?
- Ask: Pehi said the dhenki listens only to heavy feet. Was she right, wrong, or half right?
- Ask: Jonaki learned the rule from the women who used the dhenki before her. Who in your family knows something useful that was never written in a book?

### e1 · The Ruler See-saw: can one coin beat two? (35 min)
- **question:** Can one coin balance two coins? If so, where must it sit?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put 2 coins on one side of the ruler. Where will 1 coin on the other side make it balance? Point to the place before you test.
- **fair test:** Use coins that are all the same, such as 1-rupee coins. Keep the pencil in the same place, under the middle of the ruler. Change only one thing at a time: the number of coins, or where they sit. Try each balance twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** No special safety needed. Keep coins away from very small children, who might swallow them.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then, for each row, multiply coins by distance on each side. What do you notice?
- **results table:** Coins on the left | Left distance from the pencil (cm) | Coins on the right | Right distance where it balanced (cm) | Coins times distance on each side
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the ruler across the round pencil on a flat table. 2. Slide it until it balances empty. The pencil should be at the 15 cm mark.  
  *Parent says:* This ruler is a see-saw. The pencil is the pivot, the point it turns on. Let's make it balance with nothing on it.  
  *Child mode:* Put the ruler across a round pencil. Slide it until it balances. The pencil is the pivot.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 1 coin 6 cm to the left of the pencil. 2. Ask your child to balance it with 1 coin on the right.  
  *Parent says:* One coin on each side. Where does the second coin go?

  - Ask: Why does it balance when both coins are 6 cm away? (good: Same weight, same distance.; Both sides are the same.)

  *Child mode:* 4 coins at 3 cm. Where do 2 coins go? Work it out first. Then test it.
  - Think: Archimedes found this rule about 2,200 years ago. Could you have found it without the ruler and coins?
- **s5b (5 min)**

- **s6 (3 min)**

### Machine Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a simple machine?  
   Options: Anything with a motor / A tool with few parts that makes a push or pull easier / Only very big things, like cranes / Something that makes energy  
   **Answer:** A tool with few parts that makes a push or pull easier. *Why:* A simple machine has few parts and makes a push or pull easier. Picking 'a motor' is very common, because many machines we see have one. But a spoon opening a tin is a machine with no motor. Picking 'makes energy' means machines are thought to give extra. They never do.
2. (fill) The point a lever turns on is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pivot', 'fulcrum']. *Why:* The pivot, also called the fulcrum, is where a lever turns. On a see-saw it is the post in the middle.
3. (mcq) On a bottle opener, what is the load?  
   Options: Your hand / The bottle cap / The bottle opener's handle / The table  
   **Answer:** The bottle cap. *Why:* The load is what you want to move: the cap. Picking 'your hand' mixes up the effort, your push, with the load.
4. (match) Match each part of a see-saw to its name.  
   **Answer:** The post in the middle → pivot; The child you lift → load; You pushing down → effort. *Why:* The pivot is where it turns. The load is what is lifted. The effort is your push.
5. (mcq) A heavy grown-up and a light child are on a see-saw. Where should the grown-up sit to balance?  
   Options: At the very end / Closer to the middle / It can never balance / On the child's side  
   **Answer:** Closer to the middle. *Why:* Weight close to the pivot turns the plank less. So the heavy grown-up moves in, and the light child stays far out. Picking 'never balance' means the heavier person is thought to always go down. Distance matters too.
6. (truefalse) On a see-saw, the heavier person always goes down.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Distance from the pivot matters as much as weight. A heavy person close to the middle can be lifted by a light person far out.
7. (sort) Sort these into the right simple machine.  
   **Answer:** see-saw → Lever; slide → Ramp; bicycle wheel → Wheel; scissors → Lever; railway station slope → Ramp; doorknob → Wheel. *Why:* See-saws and scissors turn on a pivot, so they are levers. Slides and slopes join low places to high places, so they are ramps. Bicycle wheels and doorknobs turn round an axle, so they are wheels.
8. (mcq) Why is a long, gentle ramp easier than a short, steep one, when both reach the same step?  
   Options: The long ramp needs a smaller push, but you push for longer / The long ramp makes the load lighter / The short ramp has no friction / There is no difference  
   **Answer:** The long ramp needs a smaller push, but you push for longer. *Why:* A longer ramp spreads the job out: a smaller push over a longer way. Picking 'makes the load lighter' is a common idea. The load weighs just the same. Only the push you need changes.
9. (mcq) Which is easiest: dragging a heavy box, or rolling it on round pencils?  
   Options: Dragging it / Rolling it on pencils / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Rolling it on pencils. *Why:* Rollers touch the floor at only a few small spots, so there is much less rubbing, or friction. Picking 'the same' means the box's weight is thought to be all that matters. How it touches the floor matters too.
10. (fill) A big handle fixed to a thin rod, like a tap or a doorknob, is called a wheel and ____.  
   **Answer:** ['axle']. *Why:* A wheel and axle turns a small push on the big wheel into a strong twist on the thin rod.
11. (mcq) You push a door to open it. Where is it easiest to push?  
   Options: Right next to the hinges / Far from the hinges, near the handle / In the middle of the door, top to bottom / It is the same everywhere  
   **Answer:** Far from the hinges, near the handle. *Why:* The hinges are the door's pivot. A push far from the pivot turns it much more. Picking 'the same everywhere' is common, because the door weighs the same. Try pushing near the hinges. It is very hard!
12. (truefalse) A machine must have a motor.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A see-saw, a ramp and a spoon are all machines with no motor.
13. (mcq) Your arm is a lever. What is its pivot?  
   Options: Your hand / Your elbow / Your fingers / Your shoulder blade  
   **Answer:** Your elbow. *Why:* Your forearm turns at the elbow, so the elbow is the pivot. Picking 'your hand' mixes up the place the load is held with the place it turns.
14. (write) Name three levers you can find in your home. For each one, say where its pivot is.  
   **Answer:** For example: scissors, pivot at the screw in the middle. A spoon opening a tin, pivot at the rim. A door, pivot at the hinges.. *Why:* Any three real levers with the right pivot are correct.
15. (write) Pehi says, 'The dhenki listens only to heavy feet.' Is she right? Use the word pivot in your answer.  
   **Answer:** She is only half right. A light person can lift the heavy head too, if they stand at the very end of the beam, far from the pivot. Weight far from the pivot turns the beam more.. *Why:* A good answer says that where you stand, not only how heavy you are, matters.
16. (draw) Draw a see-saw with a grown-up and a child balancing it. Mark the pivot.  
   **Answer:** The grown-up sits close to the middle. The child sits near the end. The pivot is the post in the middle.. *Why:* Heavy near, light far makes a balance.
### Levers, Ramps and Wheels at Work (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Two coins sit 5 cm from the pencil on a ruler see-saw. Where must one coin go on the other side to balance them?  
   Options: 5 cm from the pencil / 10 cm from the pencil / 2 cm from the pencil / It can't balance  
   **Answer:** 10 cm from the pencil. *Why:* 2 coins times 5 cm is 10. 1 coin times 10 cm is also 10. So the one coin goes 10 cm away. Picking 5 cm means only the place is matched, not the weight. Picking 'can't balance' means fewer coins are thought to always lose.
2. (fill) 3 coins sit on peg 2. To balance them with coins on peg 3, you need ____ coins.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* 3 times 2 is 6. 2 times 3 is also 6. So 2 coins on peg 3 balance 3 coins on peg 2.
3. (mcq) Kitchen tongs (chimta) have your hand in the middle. They make your squeeze weaker at the tips. Why use them?  
   Options: They are cheaper / The tips move further than your fingers, and keep your hand away from the heat / They make your push stronger / They are not a lever  
   **Answer:** The tips move further than your fingers, and keep your hand away from the heat. *Why:* In tongs, the effort is in the middle, so they trade strength for distance. The tips open wide, and your fingers stay away from the hot pan. Picking 'stronger' is very common, because we expect every lever to make pushes bigger. Some levers are for distance and speed instead.
4. (sort) Sort these levers by what is in the middle.  
   **Answer:** see-saw → Pivot in the middle; scissors → Pivot in the middle; nutcracker → Load in the middle; wheelbarrow → Load in the middle; chimta tongs → Effort in the middle; broom → Effort in the middle. *Why:* Scissors and see-saws turn in the middle. In a nutcracker and a wheelbarrow, the load sits between the pivot and your hands. In tongs and a broom, your push is in the middle.
5. (mcq) A worker rolls a heavy drum up a plank into a truck. Then she uses a longer plank to the same truck. What changes?  
   Options: She needs a smaller push, but rolls it further / She needs a bigger push / The drum gets lighter / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** She needs a smaller push, but rolls it further. *Why:* A longer ramp to the same height is gentler, so the push is smaller. But she rolls the drum a longer way. Picking 'the drum gets lighter' is a common mix-up between weight and push.
6. (mcq) Look at the chart. Lifting a bag straight up 1 metre takes 12 newtons. Up a 3-metre ramp, about how much push does it take, with no friction?  
   Options: 36 newtons / 12 newtons / 4 newtons / 3 newtons  
   **Answer:** 4 newtons. *Why:* Push times distance stays the same: 12 times 1 is 12. So for 3 metres, the push is 12 divided by 3, which is 4 newtons. Picking 36 means multiplying instead of sharing. A longer ramp makes the push smaller, not bigger.
7. (truefalse) A well winch with a long handle lifts a heavy bucket more easily than turning the thin rod with your fingers.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The long handle is the big wheel of a wheel and axle. Your hand goes round a big circle with a small push, and the rod twists strongly.
8. (mcq) A bicycle has many simple machines. Which part is a lever?  
   Options: The tyre / The brake handle / The seat / The bell's sound  
   **Answer:** The brake handle. *Why:* The brake handle turns on a pivot. A small squeeze of your fingers becomes a strong pull on the brake cable. Picking the tyre means wheels and levers got mixed up. The tyre is part of a wheel.
9. (match) Match each thing to the simple machine it really is.  
   **Answer:** A knife → wedge; A jar lid → screw; A doorknob → wheel and axle; A slide → ramp. *Why:* A knife is two ramps back to back, a wedge. A jar lid is a ramp wrapped round, a screw. A doorknob is a wheel and axle.
10. (mcq) Your biceps pulls on your forearm about 5 cm from your elbow. Your hand is about 35 cm from your elbow. How many times as hard must the muscle pull as the weight in your hand?  
   Options: About 7 times / About the same / About half / About 35 times  
   **Answer:** About 7 times. *Why:* 35 divided by 5 is 7. The muscle is 7 times closer to the pivot, so it must pull about 7 times as hard. Picking 'about the same' means the arm is thought of as a straight pull. It is a lever, built for speed.
11. (mcq) Why is your arm built so that the muscle must pull so hard?  
   Options: It's a mistake / Your hand moves far and fast while the muscle shortens only a little / Muscles like hard work / It makes the arm lighter  
   **Answer:** Your hand moves far and fast while the muscle shortens only a little. *Why:* It's a trade-off. A small shortening of the muscle swings your hand a long way, fast. That lets you throw and catch. Picking 'a mistake' is a fair first guess. But nature trades strength for speed here.
12. (mcq) Mountain roads up the ghats zigzag instead of going straight up. Why?  
   Options: To make the view better / A longer, gentler road needs a smaller push from the engine / Because straight roads are not allowed / To make the trip shorter  
   **Answer:** A longer, gentler road needs a smaller push from the engine. *Why:* A zigzag road is a long ramp. It is longer, but gentler, so trucks and buses can climb with less push. Picking 'shorter' is the opposite. Zigzags make the trip longer.
13. (write) A small child wants to lift a big brother on a see-saw. Give two different ways to make it work. Explain each one.  
   **Answer:** One way: the brother slides in closer to the middle, so his weight turns the plank less. Another way: two small children sit together at the far end, so there is more weight far out.. *Why:* Moving weight further out, or adding more weight far out, both increase the turning.
14. (write) A machine helped you push less. What did you have to give up? Give an example.  
   **Answer:** I had to push for a longer distance. For example, on a long ramp I pushed gently but for a long way.. *Why:* Every machine trades a smaller push for a longer distance.
15. (forces) A ruler lever lifts a book. You press down on the far end. The pencil is the pivot. Show the forces on the ruler with arrows.  
   Options: your push / book pushes down / pencil pushes up / friction  
   **Answer:** your push down, book pushes down down, pencil pushes up up. *Why:* You push down on one end. The book presses down on the other end. The pencil pivot pushes up and holds the ruler up. There is no sliding, so friction is not the main force here.
16. (draw) Design a ramp for a toy car to reach a 10 cm high box. Draw a short steep ramp and a long gentle ramp. Mark which needs the smaller push.  
   **Answer:** Two ramps from the floor to the top of the box. The long gentle ramp is marked as the smaller push.. *Why:* The longer ramp to the same height needs a smaller push.
### Balance Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Predict: 2 coins on left peg 3. 3 coins on right peg 2. What happens?  
   Options: Tips left / Tips right / Balances  
   **Answer:** Balances. *Why:* 2 times 3 is 6. 3 times 2 is 6. Both sides make 6, so it balances. Picking 'tips right' means counting coins and forgetting distance.
2. (fill) A key on peg 4 balances 2 coins on peg 2. The key weighs the same as ____ coin.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* 2 coins times 2 is 4. The key times 4 must also be 4. So the key weighs 1 coin. This is how you weigh an unknown thing with a lever.
3. (mcq) Why can't a wheel with shifting weights keep turning for ever by itself?  
   Options: It could, nobody has tried hard enough / The turning on both sides always balances out, and friction slowly stops it / Wheels are too heavy / It needs to be bigger  
   **Answer:** The turning on both sides always balances out, and friction slowly stops it. *Why:* Every design ever tried has failed. Energy cannot come from nothing. Picking 'nobody has tried hard enough' is a hopeful guess. In fact people have tried for over 800 years, since Bhaskara II drew one in about 1150.
4. (mcq) The Earth is about 81 times heavier than the Moon. Where is their balance point?  
   Options: Exactly halfway between them / Much closer to the Moon / Much closer to the centre of the Earth, inside the Earth / There isn't one  
   **Answer:** Much closer to the centre of the Earth, inside the Earth. *Why:* Like a heavy grown-up near the middle of a see-saw, the heavy Earth is close to the balance point. It is about 4,700 km from the Earth's centre, inside the Earth. Picking 'halfway' means forgetting that the Earth is far heavier.
5. (truefalse) Astronomers can find planets round other stars by watching the stars wobble.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A planet and its star swing round a shared balance point, so the star wobbles a little. In 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found a planet this way.
6. (mcq) Inside your muscles, tiny molecules called myosin pull on threads. How do they make a small movement bigger?  
   Options: They glue the threads / Each one has a stiff arm that swings like a lever / They push with air / They melt and freeze  
   **Answer:** Each one has a stiff arm that swings like a lever. *Why:* A myosin head grabs a thread, and its stiff neck swings like a lever, making a tiny change into a bigger step. Picking 'glue' is a sensible guess, because they do grab on. But they also let go and swing again.
7. (mcq) Who should get credit for the lever?  
   Options: Only Archimedes / Only the person who first used it / The many unnamed people who used it, and Archimedes who explained its rule / Nobody  
   **Answer:** The many unnamed people who used it, and Archimedes who explained its rule. *Why:* Farmers used levers like the shaduf long before Archimedes. He was the first we know of to prove the rule. Picking 'only Archimedes' is what many books say, because his work was written down.
8. (order) Put these in order from SMALLEST push needed to BIGGEST, to raise the same load 1 metre: lifting straight up, a 2 m ramp, a 4 m ramp, a 3 m ramp.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* The longer the ramp to the same height, the smaller the push. Lifting straight up needs the biggest push.
9. (mcq) A screw is a ramp wrapped round a rod. Why does a screw grip wood so strongly?  
   Options: Many turns of a small push move it in only a little, with a big force / It is sharp / It is made of iron / It is glued  
   **Answer:** Many turns of a small push move it in only a little, with a big force. *Why:* A screw is a long ramp. Each turn moves it forward only a little, so a small twist gives a big push forward. Picking 'sharp' is part of it, since the tip is a wedge. But the thread is a ramp.
10. (mcq) A see-saw has a child of 20 kg sitting 2 metres from the middle. Where must a 40 kg grown-up sit to balance?  
   Options: 4 metres from the middle / 2 metres from the middle / 1 metre from the middle / Half a metre from the middle  
   **Answer:** 1 metre from the middle. *Why:* 20 times 2 is 40. The grown-up is 40 kg, and 40 times 1 is 40. So the grown-up sits 1 metre from the middle. Picking 4 metres means thinking the heavier person must go further out. It is the opposite.
11. (write) The peoples of ancient Mexico made toys with wheels but did not build carts. Give two possible reasons.  
   **Answer:** They had no horses or oxen to pull carts. Their land had thick jungles and steep mountains, where carts would be hard to use. Perhaps people carrying loads, or canoes on lakes, worked better.. *Why:* There is no single answer. Historians still debate it. A good answer gives reasons based on the land and animals.
12. (write) Ramp guides in many countries, including India, suggest a ramp should be about 12 times as long as it is high, for wheelchairs. Why do you think it must be so long?  
   **Answer:** So the slope is gentle. Then a person in a wheelchair can push themselves up with a small push, and won't roll back down too fast.. *Why:* A long, gentle ramp needs a smaller push. That matters for people who push with their arms.
13. (write) Archimedes said, 'Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth.' Was he right? Give one reason for and one reason against.  
   **Answer:** For: the lever rule has no limit, so a long enough lever could make a tiny push into a huge one. Against: the lever would be impossibly long and stiff, there is nowhere to stand, and the push would take longer than the age of the universe.. *Why:* There is no one right answer. A good answer uses the lever rule and also thinks about the real world.
14. (mcq) Which machine changes the DIRECTION of a force, more than its size?  
   Options: A pulley over a well / A nutcracker / A long ramp / A wedge  
   **Answer:** A pulley over a well. *Why:* With a single pulley over a well, you pull down and the bucket goes up. The pull is about the same size. Picking the nutcracker means mixing up direction and size. A nutcracker makes your squeeze stronger.
15. (write) A wheelbarrow carries a heavy load of bricks. Where are its pivot, load and effort? Why is it easier than carrying the bricks?  
   **Answer:** The pivot is the wheel. The load is the bricks in the middle. The effort is my hands lifting the handles. My hands are further from the wheel than the bricks, so I lift with a smaller force. The wheel also rolls instead of dragging.. *Why:* A wheelbarrow is a lever with the load in the middle, and it has a wheel, so it uses two simple machines.
16. (write) Is a see-saw fair if the two people are different weights? How can it be made fair?  
   **Answer:** It can be made fair by moving the heavier person closer to the middle. Then both turn the plank equally. Fair does not have to mean the same seat for both.. *Why:* This links the science to fairness. A thoughtful answer explains that the balance comes from weight and distance together.
17. (draw) Draw your own design for a machine that lifts a bucket of water from a well, using at least two simple machines. Label each one.  
   **Answer:** Any design using two or more simple machines, labelled. For example: a lever like a shaduf with a stone counterweight, and a pulley wheel.. *Why:* Real machines often combine simple machines.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does a simple machine do?  
   Options: Makes energy from nothing / Makes a push or pull easier, by changing its size or direction / Only works with a motor  
   **Answer:** Makes a push or pull easier, by changing its size or direction. *Why:* A simple machine makes a push or pull easier. Picking 'makes energy' means machines are thought to give extra. They never do. Picking 'a motor' is common, but a see-saw has no motor.
2. (mcq) Which of these is a machine?  
   Options: A spoon used to pry open a tin / A pebble lying on the ground / A puddle  
   **Answer:** A spoon used to pry open a tin. *Why:* The spoon is a lever, so it is a machine. Picking a pebble or a puddle means not yet linking machines to helping a push or pull.
3. (mcq) On a see-saw, what is the pivot?  
   Options: The seat / The post in the middle that it turns on / The child  
   **Answer:** The post in the middle that it turns on. *Why:* The pivot is where a lever turns. Picking 'the child' mixes up the load or effort with the pivot.
4. (mcq) Which part of your body is a pivot when you bend your arm?  
   Options: Your elbow / Your fingers / Your muscle  
   **Answer:** Your elbow. *Why:* Your arm turns at the elbow. Picking 'your muscle' mixes up the effort, the muscle's pull, with the pivot.
5. (mcq) A heavy grown-up and a light child want to balance a see-saw. What should they do?  
   Options: The grown-up sits closer to the middle / The child sits closer to the middle / It can't be done  
   **Answer:** The grown-up sits closer to the middle. *Why:* Weight close to the pivot turns less. So the heavy grown-up moves in. Picking 'can't be done' means only weight is counted, not distance.
6. (mcq) 2 coins sit 5 cm from the pivot of a ruler see-saw. Where does 1 coin balance them?  
   Options: 5 cm away / 10 cm away / 1 cm away  
   **Answer:** 10 cm away. *Why:* 2 times 5 is 10, and 1 times 10 is 10. Picking 5 cm means matching the place but forgetting the weight.
7. (mcq) You need to get a heavy box onto a high step. Which ramp needs the smallest push?  
   Options: A short, steep ramp / A long, gentle ramp / They need the same push  
   **Answer:** A long, gentle ramp. *Why:* A longer, gentler ramp needs a smaller push, but over a longer way. Picking 'the same' means the box's weight is thought to be all that matters.
8. (mcq) A longer ramp needs a smaller push. What is the catch?  
   Options: The box gets heavier / You must push it a longer way / There is no catch  
   **Answer:** You must push it a longer way. *Why:* Machines trade a smaller push for a longer distance. Picking 'no catch' means machines are thought to give something for nothing.
9. (mcq) Why is a doorknob easier to turn than the thin metal rod inside it?  
   Options: The knob is softer / The knob is a big wheel, so a small push makes a strong twist on the rod / The rod is broken  
   **Answer:** The knob is a big wheel, so a small push makes a strong twist on the rod. *Why:* A doorknob is a wheel and axle. Your hand goes round a bigger circle, with a smaller push. Picking 'softer' means comfort is mixed up with force.
10. (mcq) Why is it easier to move a heavy box on rollers than to drag it?  
   Options: Rolling has much less rubbing than dragging / Rollers make the box lighter / It isn't easier  
   **Answer:** Rolling has much less rubbing than dragging. *Why:* Rollers touch the floor at only a few spots that lift off as they turn, so there is much less friction. Picking 'lighter' mixes up weight with push.
11. (mcq) In the ramp test, to make it fair, what should you change?  
   Options: Only the ramp's length / The ramp and the truck's load / Everything  
   **Answer:** Only the ramp's length. *Why:* In a fair test, you change one thing only. Then you know that thing caused the difference. Picking two things means you can't tell which one mattered.
12. (mcq) Look at the chart from the ramp test. Which way needed the smallest pull?  
   Options: Lifted straight up / Short, steep ramp / Long, gentle ramp  
   **Answer:** Long, gentle ramp. *Why:* The shortest bar is the long, gentle ramp, so it needed the smallest pull. Picking 'straight up' may mean the tallest bar was read as the best. Here a taller bar means more pull.
13. (mcq) Can any machine give out more work than you put in?  
   Options: Yes, a clever enough one / No, a smaller push always means a longer way / Only machines with wheels  
   **Answer:** No, a smaller push always means a longer way. *Why:* Energy can't come from nothing. People have tried to build never-stopping wheels since at least 1150, and all failed. Picking 'a clever enough one' is a hopeful idea that no one has made work.
14. (mcq) Where is the balance point of the Earth and the Moon?  
   Options: Halfway between them / Inside the Earth / Inside the Moon  
   **Answer:** Inside the Earth. *Why:* The Earth is about 81 times heavier, so the balance point is close to it, about 1,700 km under the surface. Picking 'halfway' forgets that the heavier one sits closer to the balance point, just like on a see-saw.
15. (mcq) Your biceps joins your arm close to the elbow. What does that give you?  
   Options: A hand that moves far and fast / A stronger grip / Nothing, it's a mistake  
   **Answer:** A hand that moves far and fast. *Why:* With the muscle close to the pivot, a small shortening swings your hand far and fast. Picking 'a mistake' is a fair first guess, but it is a trade-off of strength for speed.

## Applications
- Hand pumps at village wells use a long lever handle to lift water.
- Scissors, nutcrackers, the supari cutter called a sarota, tongs and bottle openers are all levers.
- A sabzi seller's tarazu, a two-pan balance, weighs vegetables using the see-saw rule.
- Ramps at railway stations, hospitals, malls and temples let wheelchairs, trolleys and prams in.
- Zigzag roads up the ghats and the Himalayas are long ramps that let vehicles climb.
- Loading planks let workers roll heavy drums and gas cylinders into trucks.
- Taps, doorknobs, screwdrivers and steering wheels are wheels and axles.
- Bicycles combine levers (brake handles), wheels and axles (wheels and pedals) and a chain.
- Wheelbarrows and the hand carts of vegetable sellers combine a lever and a wheel.
- Cranes on building sites use levers, pulleys and counterweights.
- Knives, axes, ploughs and chisels are wedges.
- Jar lids, bottle caps, screws and spiral staircases are screws, ramps wrapped round.
- Doctors and physiotherapists use the lever rule to understand how muscles move bones.
- Astronomers find planets round other stars by watching the stars wobble round their balance point.
- Chemists now build machines out of single molecules. Three of them won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2016 for it.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how were the pyramids and other giant ancient buildings raised?** Most experts agree ramps, levers and sledges were used. Which kinds of ramp, and how they were arranged, is still argued about, and new finds keep changing the picture.
- **How did the builders of India's temples and stepwells, like those in Gujarat and Rajasthan, lift and place stones weighing many tonnes?** Historians have good guesses: ramps, levers, rollers, ropes, pulleys and huge teams of workers. For most buildings, nobody wrote down exactly how it was done, or the names of the builders.
- **Who first used a lever, a ramp or a wheel?** It happened long before writing, and wooden tools rot away. The oldest wheels and toy carts we have found may not be the first.
- **Why did the peoples of ancient Mexico use wheels on toys but not on carts?** There are several ideas: no animals to pull carts, rough land, or other ways that worked better. Historians still debate it.
- **Exactly how does each tiny myosin lever in a muscle time its swing?** Scientists can now see myosin's shape and watch single molecules move. The fine details of each step are still being studied.
- **How small can a machine be?** Chemists have built tiny motors, switches and even a 'nanocar' from single molecules. How useful such machines can become is still unknown.
- **How many planets like the Earth are there round other stars?** Stars' wobbles, and dips in their light, have revealed thousands of planets. Small, Earth-like planets make tiny wobbles that are very hard to see.
- **Was the Thanjavur temple's capstone really hauled up a ramp several kilometres long?** It is a well-known story, but there is no written record from the time it was built. Historians are still unsure.

## Connected maths
- On a ruler see-saw, 3 coins sit 4 cm from the pencil. How far away must 2 coins sit on the other side to balance them? **6 cm.**. On the left, 3 coins times 4 cm is 12. On the right, 2 coins times something must also be 12. 2 times 6 is 12. So the 2 coins sit 6 cm from the pencil. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)
- In the ramp test, suppose lifting the toy truck straight up 10 cm took 6 newtons on a forcemeter. Your long ramp was 60 cm long, reaching the same 10 cm high pile. With no friction, about how big would the pull up the long ramp be? **About 1 newton.**. The ramp is 60 cm long and the pile is 10 cm high. 60 divided by 10 is 6, so the truck travels 6 times as far. Push times distance stays the same, so the pull is about 6 times smaller. 6 newtons divided by 6 is 1 newton. In real life friction adds a little, so you would measure a bit more. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- A doorstep is 15 cm high. Ramp guides say a ramp should be 12 times as long as it is high. How long should the ramp be? Give your answer in metres and centimetres. **180 cm, which is 1 metre and 80 centimetres.**. 15 times 12: 15 times 10 is 150, and 15 times 2 is 30. 150 plus 30 is 180 cm. 100 cm make 1 metre, so 180 cm is 1 metre and 80 centimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- Your biceps pulls 5 cm from your elbow. Your hand holds a water bottle 35 cm from your elbow. How many times as far from the elbow is your hand? **7 times.**. 35 divided by 5 is 7. Check: 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5 is 35. So the hand is 7 times as far from the pivot. That means the muscle must pull about 7 times as hard as the bottle's weight, and the hand moves 7 times as far. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Think of the park see-saw. Suppose you weigh 20 kg and sit 2 metres from the middle. Your grown-up weighs 40 kg. How far from the middle must the grown-up sit to balance? **1 metre.**. Child: 20 times 2 is 40. Grown-up: 40 times something must be 40. 40 times 1 is 40. So the grown-up sits 1 metre from the middle. The grown-up is twice as heavy, so sits half as far. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)
- In the ramp test, the rubber band stretched 12 cm when the truck was lifted straight up, 8 cm on the short ramp and 5 cm on the long ramp. Draw a bar chart. How much less did it stretch on the long ramp than straight up? **7 cm less. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each way, as tall as its stretch. Then 12 take away 5 is 7. So the band stretched 7 cm less on the long ramp. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)

## Warm-up
- The point a lever turns on is called the ____. → Pivot. It is also called the fulcrum.
- On a see-saw, where should a heavy grown-up sit to balance a light child? → Closer to the middle.
- Lever, ramp or wheel: what is a slide? → A ramp.
- Lever, ramp or wheel: what is a pair of scissors? → Two levers joined at one pivot.
- Lever, ramp or wheel: what is a doorknob? → A wheel and axle.
- A longer ramp needs a smaller push. What is the catch? → You push a longer way.
- When you bend your arm, where is the pivot? → Your elbow.
- 2 coins at 5 cm balance 1 coin at how many centimetres? → 10 cm, because 2 times 5 and 1 times 10 both make 10.
- Who wrote down the law of the lever about 2,200 years ago? → Archimedes. But people used levers long before him.
- Where is the balance point of the Earth and the Moon? → Inside the Earth, about 1,700 km under the surface.
- Can a machine give you more than you put in? → No. There is no free push.
