# How we see: light goes straight, a lens makes a sharp picture, and two eyes build depth
`sci.light.how-we-see.L4`

**Summary.** Hold a finger up at arm's length and close one eye, then the other. The finger jumps sideways against the wall, though it never moved. Your child tests whether light can ever go round a corner, makes a lens form an upside-down picture of a window, and measures how far a pencil seems to jump when it is near and when it is far. The jump is how the brain works out distance. Nobody yet knows how two flat pictures from two eyes become one solid world.

**Intro.** *Hold one finger up at arm's length, in front of a plain wall. Close your left eye and look at the finger. Now open the left eye and close the right. What happened to your finger? Did the finger move, or did something else?* The finger did not move. But each of your eyes looks from a slightly different place, so each gets a slightly different picture. Your brain joins the two into one, and it uses the difference to tell how far away things are. In this unit you find out how light travels from the world to your eyes in straight lines. You build a lens that makes a picture of a window. Then you measure the jump, and find out how much of what you see is worked out and not simply seen.

## Learning outcomes

3. I can explain how the lens changes shape to focus on near and far things.
4. I can explain why two eyes give us depth, using what I measured in the finger jump test.
5. I can say where the eyes of hunting and hunted animals sit, and what each gains.
6. I can fill in a results table, compare it with my guess, and finish with claim, evidence and reasoning.
7. I can say what scientists agree about seeing, and what nobody knows yet.

## Terms
- **Ray**: A straight line with an arrow that shows the path of a thin beam of light. *e.g.* A torch beam through a hole in a card follows one straight ray.
- **Ray diagram**: A drawing that uses straight arrows to show how light travels from a source, to a thing, to an eye. *e.g.* A drawing of a lamp, a mango and an eye with arrows from the lamp to the mango and from the mango to the eye.
- **Lens**: A clear, curved piece of glass, plastic or jelly that bends light so that it comes together. It is thicker in the middle than at the edges. *e.g.* A magnifying glass is a lens. The clear lens inside your eye is a lens too.
- **Focus**: To bring the rays from one point of a thing together at one point, so that the picture is sharp, not blurry. *e.g.* A camera focuses on a face, so the face is sharp and the wall behind is blurry.
- **Retina**: The thin layer at the back of the eye that catches the light. A sharp picture forms on it. *e.g.* A picture of a window forms upside down on your retina when you look at a window.
- **Fovea**: A tiny pit in the middle of the retina packed with cone cells. It is the part of your eye that sees finest detail. *e.g.* When you read a word, you point your fovea at each letter in turn.
- **Binocular vision**: Using two eyes together. The two eyes see the same scene from two slightly different places. *e.g.* Two eyes are what let you catch a ball thrown to you.
- **Parallax**: The way a near thing seems to move sideways more than a far thing when you change where you look from. *e.g.* When you look out of a bus window, a nearby tree zips past while a far hill hardly moves.
- **Depth**: How far away different things are from you. A picture on a flat page has no real depth, but it can show clues to it. *e.g.* In a room, the table is nearer than the window, and the window is nearer than the tree outside.
- **Field of view**: How much of the world you can see at once without moving your eyes or head. *e.g.* A horse can see almost all the way round without turning their head.
- **Peripheral vision**: What you see at the edges of your field of view. It is good at noticing movement, but poor at fine detail and colour. *e.g.* You notice a bus coming from the side before you can say its colour.
- **Short sight**: A condition where near things are sharp but far things are blurry, because the eye focuses in front of the retina. Glasses with a curved-in lens fix it. *e.g.* A short-sighted child can read a book easily but cannot read the blackboard.
- **Long sight**: A condition where far things are easier than near things, because the eye focuses behind the retina. Glasses with a curved-out lens fix it. *e.g.* Many older people hold a newspaper at arm's length, to make the letters sharp.
- **Near point**: The closest distance at which your eye can still make a sharp picture of a thing. *e.g.* Bring a pencil tip slowly towards your nose. The place where it turns blurry is your near point.
- **Visual angle**: How wide a thing looks to your eye. It depends on both the size of the thing and its distance from you. *e.g.* A person standing 8 metres away looks half as tall as the same person 4 metres away.
- **Optical illusion**: A picture or scene that your brain reads wrongly, even when you know the facts. *e.g.* Two lines of the same length can look different if they have different ends.
- **Stereoscope**: An instrument that shows one flat picture to each eye so that the brain sees a solid scene. Charles Wheatstone built the first one in 1838. *e.g.* Old 3D picture viewers use a stereoscope.
- **Thaumatrope**: A spinning card with one picture on the front and another on the back. When it spins fast, the two pictures seem to join into one. *e.g.* A bird on one side and a cage on the other seem to put the bird inside the cage.

## Explanations
### Light travels in straight lines · *defined*
**Definition:** Light travels in straight lines unless something bends it or bounces it. That is why we cannot see round corners, and why shadows have sharp edges.

When you shine a torch through three cards with holes, the light only gets through if the holes line up in a straight row. Move one card a little and the light stops. A sunbeam through a window is a straight stripe. A shadow has a sharp edge because light cannot curve round the thing that blocks it. Light can be bent by water or glass, or bounced by a mirror, but in clear air or an empty space it goes straight. Light is also fast. In one second it could go round the Earth more than seven times. So its straight path looks instant.
- e.g. A torch beam in a dusty room is a straight line.
- e.g. You cannot see your friend if she stands behind a wall.
- e.g. The sharp edge of a shadow on a wall shows that light did not curve round the object.
### Rays and ray diagrams · *defined*
**Definition:** A ray is a straight arrow that shows where a thin beam of light is going. A ray diagram uses rays to show the whole journey of light.

Scientists draw light as straight arrows, called rays. The arrow shows the direction the light is going. To draw how you see a mango, you draw an arrow from the lamp to the mango, and an arrow from the mango to your eye. You use a ruler, because light goes straight. A real beam is wider than a line, and a real lamp sends out light in every direction. A ray is a way of drawing one thin path out of all of them. Ray diagrams help us predict things we have not measured yet, such as where a picture will form.
- e.g. A drawing of a candle, a mirror and an eye, with straight arrows from the candle to the mirror and the mirror to the eye.
- e.g. A drawing of the Sun, a tree and a shadow, with arrows from the Sun going past the tree.
- e.g. A drawing in a physics book of light entering a magnifying glass.
### Every point of a thing sends rays in all directions · *defined*
**Definition:** Every point on a lit thing sends light out in every direction. Only a few of those rays go into any one eye.

Think of the tip of a mango. Sunlight lands on it, and it bounces light in every direction, like a tiny lamp. The same is true of every other point on the mango. A very small share of these rays goes into your eye. But because there are so many points and so much light, your eye gets plenty. This is why everyone in a room can see the mango, from every seat. It is also why a plain white wall does not show a picture of the room. Rays from every point of the room land on every point of the wall and mix. To make a picture, the rays must be sorted.
- e.g. Everyone in a class can see the same blackboard from different seats.
- e.g. A white wall lit by a window shows no picture of the window, only brightness.
- e.g. A single candle flame lights the whole room, because it sends light in every direction.
### A hole or a lens sorts the rays into a picture · *defined*
**Definition:** A tiny hole lets through only one ray from each point of a thing, so a picture forms. A lens does the same job, but lets in much more light, by bending the rays from each point back together.

Put a card with a tiny hole between a lit window and a screen. Each point of the window sends one thin ray through the hole, to one place on the screen. The rays from the top land at the bottom, and the rays from the bottom land at the top. So the picture is upside down. A pinhole makes a sharp but dim picture, because it lets little light through. A lens is a wide window that bends the rays. All the rays from one point of the window, which spread out, are bent so that they meet again at one point on the screen. That gives a bright and sharp picture. Your eye does exactly this, with the lens inside it and the retina as the screen. In 1604, Johannes Kepler worked out that the picture on the retina is upside down.
- e.g. A magnifying glass held between a window and a card makes an upside-down picture of the window on the card.
- e.g. A phone camera has a tiny lens that makes a sharp picture on its sensor.
- e.g. Your retina holds a small upside-down picture of everything you look at.
### Focusing: the lens changes shape · *defined*
**Definition:** The lens inside the eye can change its shape. It gets thicker to focus on near things and thinner to focus on far things.

Your retina is always the same distance from your lens. But a near thing and a far thing need different bending to make a sharp picture on it. So the eye changes the lens. Tiny muscles around the lens squeeze it fatter to look at something close, such as a book. When you look at a far hill, they relax and the lens goes flatter. This takes a moment, which is why things look blurry for a second when you glance from a book to the blackboard. The lens gets stiffer with age. From around the age of 40, many people cannot make it thick enough to focus on near print. They need reading glasses. You will measure this when you find the near point of people of different ages.
- e.g. You look up from a book to a clock on the wall, and it is blurry for a moment.
- e.g. A grandparent holds the newspaper at arm's length.
- e.g. A camera has to move its lens in and out to focus, but the eye changes the lens shape.
### Short sight and long sight · *defined*
**Definition:** If the eye's focus lands in front of the retina, far things look blurry. This is short sight. If it lands behind the retina, near things look blurry. This is long sight. Glasses fix both.

In a short-sighted eye, the eyeball is a little too long, or the lens is a little too strong. The rays from far things meet before they reach the retina, and are spreading again by the time they land. Glasses with a lens that spreads light a little, called a concave lens, move the focus back. In a long-sighted eye, the focus lands behind the retina, and a lens that squeezes the light, called a convex lens, moves it forward. Short sight is becoming more common in children in many countries. Studies in several countries suggest that children who spend more time outdoors in daylight are less likely to become short-sighted. Scientists are still working out why. Eye tests at school find who needs glasses.
- e.g. A child who cannot read the board from the back bench but can read a book easily may be short-sighted.
- e.g. Reading glasses for older people are convex lenses.
- e.g. Eye camps and hospitals test for glasses all over India.
### Why far things look small · *defined*
**Definition:** The size a thing looks depends on the angle between the rays from its top and its bottom. A far thing makes a smaller angle, so it looks smaller.

A person stands 4 metres away. Two straight rays go from her head and her feet into your eye. The angle between them is the visual angle. Now she steps back to 8 metres. The rays are still straight, but they come from further away, so the angle is smaller. It is about half as big. So she looks about half as tall. You can check this with a ruler held at arm's length. Because the eye makes a picture on the retina, a smaller angle means a smaller picture. This is also why signs for far readers must be bigger. A sign twice as far away must be twice as tall to look the same size.
- e.g. A person at 8 metres looks about half the height of the same person at 4 metres.
- e.g. A road sign for fast drivers has much bigger letters than a notice in a classroom.
- e.g. The Moon is huge, but looks small, because it is so far.
### Two eyes and depth · *defined*
**Definition:** Your two eyes are a few centimetres apart, so each sees a slightly different picture. The brain uses the difference to work out how far away things are.

Hold a finger up at arm's length and swap eyes. The finger jumps against the wall behind it. Now bring the finger close to your nose and swap again. It jumps much further. The jump is called parallax. Near things jump a lot, and far things jump a little, so the size of the jump tells the brain the distance. Your brain does this all day, below your notice, for every part of the scene. It works best for things within a few metres, such as a ball coming towards you. For far things, the difference is too small. Scientists call seeing with two eyes binocular vision. Reports suggest that somewhere between 1 person in 20 and 1 in 10 cannot use their two eyes together to judge depth.
- e.g. Threading a needle is much harder with one eye closed.
- e.g. A bus window shows parallax: near trees zip by, far hills drift.
- e.g. Astronomers measure the distance to some stars by looking at them from two places half a year apart.
### Depth with one eye · *defined*
**Definition:** Even one eye can judge distance, using clues in the picture and the movement of the head.

A person with sight in only one eye can still judge distance well, using many clues. A near thing looks bigger than the same thing far away. A thing in front of another thing hides part of it, so it must be nearer. Far hills look hazy and bluish. Shadows show shape. And if you move your head a little, near things slide across the view more than far things, which gives the same jump as two eyes, one view after the other. Painters use all these clues, except movement, to make a flat painting look deep. A person with one eye usually learns to use these clues very well. Close one eye and the world does not go flat, but touching the tips of two pencils together gets harder, because the quick difference between two views is missing.
- e.g. A flat painting of a road that narrows into the distance looks deep.
- e.g. A person with sight in one eye sways her head slightly to judge a ball's distance.
- e.g. A far mountain looks blue-grey and hazy, so we know it is far.
### Hunters and hunted: where the eyes sit · *defined*
**Definition:** Hunting animals, such as owls, cats and tigers, have both eyes at the front, which gives depth for a pounce. Hunted animals, such as deer, hens and horses, have eyes at the sides, which gives a wide view.

Eyes at the front see the same scene from two nearby places, so the pictures overlap a lot. That gives strong depth for judging a leap. Eyes at the sides cannot overlap much, but each one sees a different part of the world, so together they see almost all the way round. A horse or a rabbit can watch for danger from nearly every side without turning their head. A hen bobs her head as she walks, which keeps the picture steady for a moment between steps. A chameleon moves each eye separately. Humans have front-facing eyes. One idea is that our early primate ancestors gained them from jumping between branches. Scientists still discuss this. Neither design is better. Each fits the way the animal lives.
- e.g. An owl turns the whole head to look, because her eyes face the front and cannot move much.
- e.g. A deer at a waterhole can see a tiger sneaking from the side.
- e.g. A hen bobs her head as she walks, to keep the picture steady between steps.
### The sharp spot and the edges · *defined*
**Definition:** Only a tiny spot in the middle of the retina, the fovea, sees fine detail. The rest sees blurry shapes, movement and dim light.

Hold your thumb out at arm's length. The sharp part of your vision is about the size of your thumbnail. Everything else is blurry, though the world does not seem that way. Your eyes jump from place to place about three times every second, pointing the sharp spot at one thing after another. Your brain builds a picture that feels sharp everywhere. The edges of your vision have few cone cells and many rod cells, so they notice movement and dim light, but not much colour. That is why you notice a bus coming from the side before you can say its colour. It is also why a faint star seems to vanish when you look straight at it and return when you look a little to the side.
- e.g. You read by moving your sharp spot along a line of words.
- e.g. A faint star appears when you look beside it, and disappears when you stare at it.
- e.g. You see a movement at the edge of your vision before you know what moved.
### You see a moment ago · *idea*
The signals from your eyes take a short time to travel and to be put together by the brain. Scientists think it is about a tenth of a second or more. So what you see is slightly old. A ball moving at 10 metres each second moves a whole metre in that time. Yet fielders catch balls. The brain does not simply report the old picture. It predicts where the ball will be, using what it has learned from many catches. This is why a skilled fielder can catch a ball that she could not possibly have seen in time. There is also an older delay in the light itself. Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach us, so you see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago. Scientists still argue about how the brain does its predicting.
- e.g. A fielder runs to where the ball will land, not to where it is.
- e.g. You see the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago.
- e.g. The stars you see tonight are shown as they were years ago, and for some, hundreds of years ago.
### The Moon looks bigger near the horizon · *debated*
Hold a small lentil or pea at arm's length, in front of the Moon when it is high in the sky. Do the same when the Moon is just above the horizon. The Moon is covered by the same lentil in both. A photograph shows the same size too. Yet to the eye, the Moon near the horizon looks much bigger. This is an illusion, and it has been argued about for more than 2,000 years. Scientists agree that the Moon is not really larger. They do not agree on the reason.
- e.g. The full Moon rising over the sea at Marina Beach in Chennai looks huge.
- e.g. The same Moon high overhead looks small.
- e.g. A photograph shows both at the same size.
- **Many vision scientists, following ideas from Ibn al-Haytham about 1,000 years ago:** The brain thinks the horizon Moon is further away than the high Moon, because there is a lot of ground in between. So it guesses that the horizon Moon must be bigger.
- **Some other scientists:** The sky looks like a flattened dome, so the horizon looks further than the top. The brain scales the Moon's size to fit.
- **Other scientists today:** No single explanation fits all the tests. Several effects probably work together. The mystery is still open.
### Why we have a blind spot and an octopus does not · *defined*
**Definition:** In the eyes of humans and other backboned animals, the nerve fibres lie in front of the light-catching cells, so the nerve must pass through the retina, which leaves a blind spot. An octopus's retina is wired the other way round, so she has no blind spot.

In your eye, the light has to pass through a layer of nerve fibres before it reaches the light-catching cells. The fibres all gather together and leave the eye in one place, the optic nerve. At that place there are no light-catching cells, which makes the blind spot. In an octopus, the light-catching cells face the light directly, and the nerve fibres lie behind them. So she has no blind spot. Eyes of octopuses and eyes of humans look alike. Scientists think the two designs evolved separately, from far-off ancestors that could only sense light. No design is better in every way. A human brain fills in the blind spot, and we have a fovea. An octopus has no blind spot, and can sense polarised light, which we cannot.
- e.g. Your eye has a blind spot, and you do not notice it.
- e.g. An octopus has no blind spot, though her eye looks much like yours.
- e.g. Two eyes cover for each other's blind spots.
### Two pictures that join in the eye · *debated*
A thaumatrope has a bird on one side of a card and a cage on the other. When you twirl it quickly, the bird seems to be inside the cage. A flip book, a cartoon and a film use the same trick. Pictures shown one after another, quickly enough, seem to join or to move. The old explanation, called persistence of vision, said the eye holds on to each picture for a moment. Many scientists now think that this is only part of the story. The brain also builds movement from a series of still pictures. They still argue about how it does so.
- e.g. A film shows 24 still pictures every second, and your brain sees smooth movement.
- e.g. A flip book of a bouncing ball seems to move when you flick the pages.
- e.g. A ceiling fan seems to slow down or turn backwards in some videos.
- **An older explanation, persistence of vision:** The eye holds on to each picture for a short moment, so one picture is still there when the next arrives, and the two seem to be seen together.
- **Many brain scientists today:** The brain does not just add pictures. It actively joins nearby pictures, and builds movement between them. The effect called the phi phenomenon was described by Max Wertheimer in 1912.
- **Other scientists:** Both ideas explain part of it. The exact steps in the brain are not yet clear.

## Mostly agreed
- Light travels in straight lines through clear air, and shadows and sunbeams show it. *How we know:* Three cards with holes only let a torch beam through when the holes are in a line, and a shadow has sharp edges.
- The eye's lens and cornea make a sharp, upside-down picture on the retina. *How we know:* Kepler worked it out in 1604. Scheiner saw the picture on the back of an eye in the early 1600s. A lens held in front of a window makes the same sort of picture on a card.
- The lens changes shape to focus on near and far things, and gets stiffer with age. *How we know:* The near point of the eye moves further away with age, as everyone can measure with a ruler.
- Two eyes give two slightly different pictures, and the brain uses the difference to judge depth. *How we know:* In 1838, Wheatstone made flat drawings look solid by showing one to each eye. Touching pencil tips together is harder with one eye covered.
- Only a small spot in the middle of the retina sees fine detail. *How we know:* The fovea is packed with cone cells, and this has been seen and counted in the retinas of people.
- Animals that hunt tend to have eyes in front, and animals that are hunted tend to have eyes at the sides. *How we know:* Scientists have compared the eye positions and fields of view of hundreds of kinds of animals.

## How we found out
**Charles Wheatstone, 1838.** For a long time, people thought we judge how far things are only from clues in a single picture, such as how big a thing looks and what hides what. About 500 years ago, the artist Leonardo da Vinci wrote that a painting can never look as solid as a real scene. But nobody had tested what the two eyes do. In 1838, Charles Wheatstone, a scientist in London, built an instrument called a stereoscope, with two mirrors. He drew two flat pictures of the same shape, each from a slightly different place, and showed one to each eye. The brain joined the two, and the flat drawings suddenly looked solid. Depth did not need a solid thing. The small difference between two views was enough. Clues in a single picture still matter, but the difference between two views is a clue of its own.  
*What people believed before:* Depth comes only from clues in a single picture, such as size and overlap. Wheatstone's two flat drawings, one for each eye, looked solid, which showed that the difference between the views of two eyes is a clue of its own.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Light can bend round corners, the way sound does.** (Many children, and sometimes adults.; *rejected*)  
  For: We can hear someone talking round a corner, and light is a kind of wave, like sound.  
  Against: Light waves are extremely tiny, so they bend round edges by only a very small amount, much too small to notice. A shadow has a sharp edge, and we cannot see round a corner. Sound waves are much longer, so they bend round corners easily.
- **The picture on the retina is upside down, so the brain must flip it the right way up.** (Many books and many teachers.; *debated*)  
  For: The picture on the retina really is upside down.  
  Against: There is no screen inside the head where a flipped picture is shown. In the 1890s, George Stratton wore glasses that turned his view upside down. After several days his brain learned to connect what he saw with what he touched, and the world seemed normal again. 'Up' is something the brain learns, not a direction in the retina.
- **We have two eyes only so that we have a spare if one is hurt.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: People with one eye can manage well, so a spare seems useful.  
  Against: Two eyes also give us the depth that comes from two views, and a wider field of view. The spare is a bonus, not the main job.
- **Reading in dim light damages your eyes.** (Many parents and grandparents.; *rejected*)  
  For: Reading in dim light makes eyes feel tired and sore.  
  Against: Eye doctors say that reading in dim light does not harm the eyes for good. It can make them tired, and it is easier to read in good light. The tiredness goes away after rest.
- **The Moon really is bigger when it is near the horizon.** (Many people who watch it rise.; *rejected*)  
  For: It looks much bigger, and it looks bigger every time.  
  Against: A photograph and a lentil held at arm's length show the same size in both places. It is an illusion in the brain.
- **Everyone with two healthy eyes sees depth from the difference between them.** (Many people.; *accepted*)  
  For: Most people with two healthy eyes do.  
  Against: A small share of people, reported at between 1 in 20 and 1 in 10, cannot use the two views together, even with healthy eyes. They use the other clues for depth, and manage well. Susan Barry, a neuroscientist in the USA, gained this kind of depth sight as an adult, and wrote a book about it.

## Roots
We have followed light in straight lines, made pictures with a hole and a lens, and used two views to find depth. Now we ask what this tells us about seeing and about the world it shows us.

**Deep truth.** Hold up a finger and swap eyes, and it jumps against the wall. The jump is bigger for near things and smaller for far things. Your brain uses that difference to say how far away things are. So distance is worked out, not seen.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say we see how far away things are, as if distance were printed on them like a label. But nothing in a single view says how far away something is. The brain has to work it out from two views, or from clues such as size and overlap.  
*Evidence:* In the finger jump test, the pencil seemed to jump further when it was held near than when it was held far. When one eye was covered, touching two pencil tips together got harder. If your results are not clear, write them down honestly and try again with a longer strip on the wall.

- *who am i:* You never look out from just one place. Your two eyes are about 6 centimetres apart, and your picture of the world is built from both of them.
- *what is life:* Owls and tigers have both eyes at the front, to judge distance for a pounce. Deer and hens have eyes at the sides, to see almost all the way round. Each body is built for the life it leads.
- *what is this world:* A single view cannot show how far away things are. It takes two viewpoints, or one that moves. Astronomers measure the distance to some nearby stars by looking at them from two places half a year apart.

A single view is flat. Depth appears when two views are put side by side and the small differences between them are measured. Your two eyes do this all day, without telling you. Astronomers do it with two views half a year apart, to find how far away a star is. In both cases, distance is not something anyone looks at. It is something that is worked out from a difference.

- **Ibn al-Haytham, Book of Optics, Book 2:** Ibn al-Haytham, a scientist in Cairo about 1,000 years ago, wrote that we do not see distance and size directly. We judge them from clues, using memory and reasoning. (The brain works out depth from the difference between two views, and from clues such as size and overlap.)
- **Kena Upanishad 1.2:** The Kena Upanishad asks what stands behind the senses. It speaks of the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind, and the eye of the eye. (Eyes only catch light. The picture we see is built by something behind the eyes, and that something is the part of seeing nobody fully understands.)
- **Plato, Republic, Book 7 (the cave):** Plato, a Greek thinker about 2,400 years ago, told of people chained in a cave who see only shadows on a wall and think they are the whole world. (A single flat view can look like the whole world. A second view, or a view that moves, shows what is missing.)
- Science (settled): The brain works out depth from the small difference between the pictures of the two eyes, and from other clues such as size, overlap and movement of the head. This has been tested in many ways since Wheatstone in 1838.
- Science (open): How the brain turns these differences into the experience of a solid world around you is not known.
- Other view, Many engineers who build robots: A robot car with two cameras measures distance by the same sums. There is nothing mysterious about it. Distance is really there in the world, and two cameras can measure it. To say that it is 'worked out' makes it sound less real than it is.
- Practice, Swap eyes and breathe (3 min): Sit facing a wall. Hold up one finger at arm's length. Breathe in slowly. Close your left eye and look at the finger. Breathe out. Open your left eye and close the right. Notice the jump. Do this three times, slowly. Then open both eyes, and notice that you see one finger and one wall. Let yourself wonder who joined the two pictures.
- Ask: You have two views of the world, and you see only one. Where do the two become one?
- Ask: If distance has to be worked out, could the brain ever get it wrong? When?
- Ask: What would the world look like if your eyes were one metre apart?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your two eyes never see the same picture, yet you notice only one. Where do the two pictures become one? (hint: Think about the finger jump. Which picture did you see when both eyes were open?)

## Story: Thamarai and the High Catch
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. A person who sees with one eye can judge distance well, using many clues, including how near things shift against far things when the head moves. Two eyes make this easier for things that are close. The beach, the match, Thamarai, Selvam and everyone else in the story are made up.*

Thamarai was the best fielder on the beach, and half the village still did not believe it.

She was ten. When she was a baby, a bad fever had taken the sight from her left eye. The doctor, a kind woman from Nagercoil, said that her right eye was strong and healthy, and that was all she said. Thamarai did not think about it much. She thought about cricket.

Every evening, when the fishing boats came in, the children of the lane played on the hard sand near the water. They used a taped tennis ball and a flat bat cut from a palmyra branch. The captain was Selvam Anna, who was nineteen and coached the little ones when he came home from college. He was fair, and he was proud of his team.

Thamarai could catch anything. She caught low balls and fast balls and balls that skipped off the sand. But Selvam Anna had read something in a book. "You need two eyes to judge a high catch," he told her kindly. "So I will put you in the corner." And for every big match, he did, where the ball hardly ever came.

On the evening of the Pongal Cup final, the whole beach was full. Their lane was playing the village across the bay. By the last over, the other side needed eight runs to win. The sun hung low over the sea, and the whole sky glowed orange.

The batter was a big girl called Karthika. She swung hard. The ball shot up, higher than the palm trees, and turned into a tiny dark dot against the glare.

Everybody ran. Mythili, the tallest fielder, ran forward, then back, then forward again. She put up her hands, and then she took them down. She had lost the ball in the sun.

Thamarai was standing at the very edge of the beach, where she always stood. She did not run. Instead she did something that made the small children giggle. She swayed her head slowly from side to side, like a hen looking for a grain.

When she was very small, Thamarai had found out a secret. If she held her head still, the world looked flat, like a picture painted on a wall. But when she moved her head a little, near things slid across the view, and far things hardly moved at all. The tall palm on the far dune stayed still. The ball slid. A ball that slid a lot was close. A ball that slid a little was far. No one had taught her this. She had worked it out as a toddler, the way a fisherman learns the sea.

The dark dot slid across the palm tree, quickly. "Close," thought Thamarai, "and coming down fast." She took three steps back. Then one step to the left. She held up both hands.

The ball dropped out of the glare and straight into her hands, as softly as a ripe mango falls from a tree. The other side was out. The Pongal Cup was theirs.

The beach went quiet. Then it exploded. The small ones threw sand in the air.

Later, when the boats were being pulled up the beach, Selvam Anna came and stood beside her. "I read that you need two eyes to judge a high catch," he said slowly. "How did you do it?"

Thamarai shrugged. "Two eyes are better, Anna," she said. "Try this." She held up one finger against the palm tree. "Close your left eye. Now open it, and close the right one."

He did it. His finger leapt sideways against the tree. He did it again. "It jumps," he said.

"It jumps more when you bring it close," said Thamarai. "Two eyes give the brain two views, and the jump tells it how far away something is. I have one eye, so I make my two views myself. One after the other, by moving my head."

Selvam Anna was quiet for a while. Then he laughed, a laugh of surprise. "Then the book was only half right," he said.

From that evening, Thamarai fielded wherever the ball was most likely to go high. And on the first evening of every holiday, Selvam Anna taught the small ones how to find the jump.

**For the parent:** For the parent: to judge how far away something is, the brain uses the small difference between two views. Near things shift more than far things when the view changes. Two eyes give two views at once. A person with one eye can get two views, one after the other, by moving the head. This is the same jump that you see when you swap eyes with a finger held up.

- Ask: Why does a finger seem to jump sideways when you close one eye and then the other?
- Ask: Why does a near thing seem to jump more than a far thing when you move your head?
- Ask: Thamarai moved her head, not her feet. How did that help her to tell where the ball was?
- Ask: A flat painting of a road can look deep, even though it is flat. What clues could the painter use?

  - Ask: Which way up do you think the picture will be? (good: The right way up, like the window.; Upside down.; I do not know, so we will see.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Remind your child about the safety rule: never point the lens at the Sun.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Close the curtains of the other windows to dim the room. Leave one window that does not show the Sun, or use a lamp across the room. Stand the card upright against a book. Hold the lens between the window and the card.  
  *Parent says:* Dim the room. Stand the card up. Hold the lens between the bright window and the card, near the card. Do not point the lens at the Sun.  
  *Child mode:* Dim the room. Stand the card up. Hold the lens between the window and the card.
  - Note: The window must be bright, but must not show the Sun. A window facing away from the Sun is best. Or use a bright lamp across the room. Never point the lens at the Sun, because it can burn.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the lens and move it slowly towards and away from the card. Stop when a small, sharp picture of the window appears. Measure the distance from the lens to the card. Write it in the table. Write which way up the picture is.  
  *Parent says:* Move the lens slowly, closer to the card and then away. Stop when a sharp picture of the window appears. How far is the lens from the card? Which way up is the picture?  
  *Child mode:* Move the lens slowly. Find a sharp picture of the window. Measure how far the card is.
  - Note: You should see a small, coloured, upside-down picture of the window, with the frame and maybe a tree. It is small and a little dim. Dim the room more if it is faint. The sharp place is a narrow range, so move slowly. For a small magnifying glass, the card is typically about 10 to 20 centimetres from the lens. The lens of reading glasses is weaker, so the card may be 30 to 60 centimetres away, and the picture is bigger. If you see only a bright blur, the card is not at the sharp place yet. If nothing shows, make the room darker, and check that the lens is the kind that is thicker in the middle. Roles for 2 to 4 children: one holds the lens, one holds the card, one measures, one writes. Swap, so everyone finds the sharp picture once. One child alone with a parent: the child holds the lens, and the parent holds the card and measures.
  - Check: The picture of the window on the card was upside down. Why? → **The lens bends the rays from the top of the window downwards and from the bottom upwards, so the rays cross**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the phone, with a big black letter F on a bright white screen, about 50 centimetres from the lens. Move the card until a sharp picture of the F appears. Measure again from the lens to the card. Write whether the card is nearer or further than before.  
  *Parent says:* Now a bright thing that is nearer. The phone screen with a big F, half a metre away. Find the sharp picture on the card. Is the card nearer or further than for the window? Is the F upside down?  
  *Child mode:* Use the phone with a big F, half a metre away. Find the sharp picture. Is the card nearer or further now?
  - Note: For a small magnifying glass, the card typically has to move about 2 to 6 centimetres further from the lens than for the window. Measure carefully and write what you really see. If the card does not seem to need moving, try the phone nearer, at about 30 centimetres. The F should be small and upside down, and the picture is dimmer than the screen. Turn the screen brightness to the top and keep the room dim.
  - Check: The bright phone screen was nearer to the lens. The card had to be a little further from the lens for a sharp picture. What does this show? → **A lens needs the card in a different place for near things and far things**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child: your retina cannot move back and forth like the card. What must change in your eye when you look from a far window to a near book?  
  *Parent says:* The card had to move for near and far things. Your retina cannot move. So what must change in your eye when you look from a far window to a near book?  
  *Child mode:* The card moved. Your retina cannot. What changes in your eye?
  - Note: A good answer: the lens in the eye changes shape. It gets thicker for near things, which bends the rays more, and thinner for far things. This is called focusing.
  - Think: The lens in your eye can change shape. Which shape do you think it takes for a near book?
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now about lenses? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a lens makes an upside-down picture of a bright thing. What I saw: a small upside-down window on the card, and the card had to move for a nearer thing. Why: the lens bends the rays from each point of the thing so that they meet again on the card.

### a1 · Hole in Your Hand: two eyes, two pictures (12 min)

- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Roll the paper into a tube about 3 centimetres wide and tape it. Ask your child to guess what they will see. They look through the tube with one eye, and at their hand with the other.

  *Child mode:* Look through the tube with one eye. Keep the other eye on your hand. What will you see?
  - Ask: What do you think you will see on your hand? (good: Nothing special.; A round spot.; A hole.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess is fine. It is a surprise for most children.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the tube to the right eye with the right hand. Hold the left hand open, palm towards you. Put it flat against the side of the tube, about 10 centimetres from the face. Keep both eyes open. Look at a far wall. Slide the hand until you see a hole in your palm.  
  *Parent says:* Tube to your right eye. Left hand beside the tube. Both eyes open. Look at the far wall. Do you see a hole in your hand?  
  *Child mode:* Tube to one eye. Hand beside the tube. Both eyes open. Do you see a hole?
  - Note: It works for most people in a few tries. If it does not, move the hand a little closer to the tube, and try the other eye. Some people need to adjust the tube a few times. The hole is a real result of two pictures being joined. Typically it works within a few tries. If it does not, make the tube a little wider or narrower, and keep the hand flat against the tube. Every child makes their own tube. Siblings can work in pairs: one tries, the other watches and adjusts the hand. Ask each child to draw what they see.
  - Check: You see a round hole in your hand, but your hand is solid. Why does it seem to have a hole? → **The brain joins the picture from each eye, so the round view through the tube seems to sit in the hand**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find your leading eye. Make a triangle with both hands at arm's length. Look through it at a far light switch with both eyes. Close the left eye, then the right. The eye that keeps the switch in the triangle is the leading eye.  
  *Parent says:* Make a small triangle with your hands, at arm's length. Look at a far light switch through it. Close one eye, then the other. Which eye keeps the switch in the hole?  
  *Child mode:* Make a triangle with your hands. Look at a far switch. Close one eye, then the other. Which keeps it?
  - Note: Most people favour the right eye, but many favour the left. The leading eye is the one the brain uses when the two pictures disagree. It does not mean that the other eye is weaker.
  - Think: Most people favour one eye, as they favour one hand. What do you notice about who in your family favours the left eye?

  *Child mode:* Stare at the sticker. A pen comes in from behind. Where will you notice it? Where will you know it is red?
  - Ask: Will you notice the pen moving before you can say its colour, or after? (good: Before, because movement is easy to see.; After, because I need to see it to know it moves.; At the same time.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the 12 papers in a circle around the chair, 1 metre from it, like a clock. Put 12 straight ahead and 6 behind. Put a sticker on the wall at 12 o'clock at eye height. Your child sits on the chair.  
  *Parent says:* Lay out the clock on the floor. Twelve is straight ahead and six is behind. Sit in the middle and look at the sticker on the wall. Do not move your eyes.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the papers in a circle. Sit in the middle. Stare at the sticker. Do not move your eyes.
  - Note: Each paper is 30 degrees from the next, because a full circle is 360 degrees and there are 12 papers. Eleven o'clock is on the left and one o'clock is on the right.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start behind the chair at the 6 paper on the right side. Move the red pen slowly along the circle towards 12, at arm's length from the circle.   *Parent says:* Keep staring at the sticker. I will move the pen in from behind. Say 'now' when you notice it moving. Say 'red' when you are sure of its colour.  
  *Child mode:* Stare at the sticker. Say 'now' when you notice movement. Say 'red' when you know its colour.
  - Note: Make sure the eyes stay on the sticker. If you see them move, start again. Typically movement is noticed at about the 3 paper or a little behind it, and the red colour is named at about the 1 or 2 paper. People differ, so write what you really see. Do not tell your child the numbers until the end. Wiggle the pen a little as you walk, because a still pen at the edge may not be noticed. Walk quietly, and do not make a sound, so that sound does not give it away. Roles for 2 to 4 children: one sits, one walks the pen, one writes, one watches the sitter's eyes. Swap after each side. One child alone with a parent: the parent walks the pen and watches the eyes, and the child writes the result afterwards.
  - Check: You noticed the pen moving much earlier than you could say it was red. Why? → **The edges of the retina notice movement well, but have few cone cells for colour**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat on the left side, from 6 towards 12. Write the two hour papers. Compare the left side with the right side, and then with another person if you can.  
  *Parent says:* Now the left side. Same rules. Say 'now' for movement, and 'red' for colour. Then we compare.  
  *Child mode:* Now the left side. Say 'now' for movement. Say 'red' for colour.
  - Note: Left and right usually give similar results. A person with glasses should wear them for the test. Draw the two wedges on a top-down picture of a head, one for movement and one for colour.
  - Think: You can see a lot without turning your head, but you only see colour well in a smaller space. What does that say about how much of the world you really see in colour at one time?
### a3 · Near Point: how close can you focus? (15 min)
- **record:** Fill in the table with each person's age and near point. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each person.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the card. Ask them to guess who in the family can read the card closest to their eyes. Ask who will need it furthest away. Write the guesses.  
  *Parent says:* We will bring a card with small writing closer to the eye until the words turn blurry. Who in our family do you think will manage the closest? Who the furthest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Bring the writing close until it blurs. Who in our family can go closest? Why?
  - Ask: Who do you think will have the closest near point, and why? (good: The child, because her eyes are young.; The grandparent, because she has seen so much.; Everyone is the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is good. Remember to ask the person to take off reading glasses for the test. Hold the card steady and keep its sharp corners away from the eye. If someone cannot read even at arm's length, write 'more than arm's length'. For 2 to 4 children, work in pairs: one tests, one measures and writes, then swap. For one child, a parent measures.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to cover the left eye with a hand and hold the card at arm's length. Read the small writing. Bring the card slowly closer, until the writing first turns blurry. Stop. Hold still. The helper measures from the card to the corner of the eye. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Cover one eye. Hold the card at arm's length. Bring it slowly closer. Stop the moment the words turn blurry. I will measure.  
  *Child mode:* Cover one eye. Bring the card slowly closer. Stop when the words blur. Someone measures.
  - Note: The near point is the closest distance with sharp writing. Move the card slowly. Do not press the hand on the covered eye. Take the middle of the three numbers. Children's near points are often 7 to 12 centimetres.
  - Check: The writing turned blurry when the card came too near. What does this tell you about the lens in the eye? → **The lens cannot get thick enough to focus on things that close**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat the test with two other family members of different ages. Write their ages and near points in the table. Remind them to take off reading glasses.  
  *Parent says:* Now test someone older. And someone else. How close can they go? Is it the same as you?  
  *Child mode:* Test two other people. Write their ages and near points.
  - Note: Typical near points: a child, 7 to 12 centimetres. An adult in the forties, 15 to 30 centimetres. A person over 60, 40 to 100 centimetres or more. People vary a lot. If someone cannot read even at arm's length, they may need reading glasses and an eye test. That is normal with age.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table or bar chart. Ask your child what pattern they see. Ask why the lens might be less able to get thick as people get older.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the numbers. Who had the closest near point? Who the furthest? What pattern do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the numbers. What pattern do you see?
  - Note: A good answer: older people have a further near point, because the lens gets stiffer and cannot thicken as much. Reading glasses add a lens in front of the eye to help.
  - Think: The lens gets stiffer with age. How does that change the near point?
### a4 · How Big Does It Look? A ruler at arm's length (15 min)

- **s1 (3 min)**

  *Parent says:* We will measure how tall a pole looks on a ruler at arm's length. At 2 metres, then 4 metres, then 8. What happens to its height on the ruler when it moves back? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Measure a pole on a ruler. It moves back. What happens to its height on the ruler? Why?
  - Ask: What will happen to its height on the ruler when it is twice as far away? (good: It will be half as big.; It will stay the same, because she is the same size.; It will get a bit smaller.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The friend stands at the 2 metre mark, holding the pole upright with its bottom on the floor. Your child holds the ruler upright at arm's length, with the elbow locked. They close one eye. Line up the 0 mark with the bottom of the pole. Read the number, in centimetres and millimetres, at the top of the pole. Write it down. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the ruler out at arm's length with a straight arm. Close one eye. Line up the 0 with the bottom of the pole. What number is at the top of the pole?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the ruler at arm's length. Close one eye. Read how tall the pole looks.
  - Note: Keep the arm straight and the ruler the same distance from the eye each time. Use the same eye each time. The friend must hold the pole upright and still, with its bottom on the mark. Ask the friend to stand next to the pole and not behind it, so that the reader can see the pole clearly. Do the test in a safe place, away from traffic and stairs. For 2 to 4 children: one reads the ruler, one holds the pole, one writes. Swap, and compare the readings of different children, who have different arm lengths. For one child with a parent: the parent holds the pole and writes.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The friend moves to the 4 metre mark, and then to the 8 metre mark, with the pole. Measure how tall the pole looks on the ruler at each, using the same arm, the same eye and the same ruler.  
  *Parent says:* Now the pole moves back to four metres. How tall does it look on the ruler now? And at eight?  
  *Child mode:* The pole moves back. Measure again at 4 metres and at 8 metres.
  - Note: Typically the apparent height halves, roughly, each time the distance doubles. If the pole is 60 centimetres tall and your arm's length is about 50 centimetres, it should look about 15 centimetres tall at 2 metres, about 7 to 8 at 4 metres and about 4 at 8 metres. Your numbers will differ. Measurements are rough, because it is hard to read a ruler at arm's length. At 8 metres, read to the nearest millimetre, and do it twice.
  - Check: The pole looked half as tall at 4 metres as at 2 metres. What does this show about distance and size? → **Twice as far away makes a thing look half as tall**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar chart of your three measurements. Ask what happens to the height each time the distance doubles.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your three numbers. What happens each time she steps twice as far away?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your three numbers. What pattern do you see?
  - Think: If you wanted a sign to look the same size from twice as far away, how big would you have to make it?

### p1 · A Bird in a Cage: pictures that join in your eye (45 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts out the card discs with scissors.
- **record:** Keep the thaumatrope and the flip book. Draw the bird and the cage joined together, as you saw them. Write how fast you had to twirl or flick for the pictures to join.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the card, the strings and the notepad. Ask what they think will happen if a bird on one side and a cage on the other are twirled very quickly.

  *Child mode:* A bird on one side. A cage on the other. We twirl the card. What will you see? Why?
  - Ask: What will you see when the card spins fast? (good: Just a blur.; The bird inside the cage.; A bird, then a cage, then a bird.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts two discs from the card by drawing round a mug. Draw a bird on one disc. Flip the disc over like a page, top to bottom, and note which way up the back is. On the second disc, draw a cage, the right way up when it is flipped in the same way. Glue the two discs back to back with a string between them on each side.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a bird on one disc. Draw a cage on the other. Make the cage the right way up when you flip the card like a page. Glue the two discs together with a string on each side.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bird on one disc and a cage on the other. Glue them back to back with strings.
  - Note: The two pictures must be the right way up for each other when the card is flipped by twirling it between the strings. A good way: draw the bird, then flip the card over like a page of a calendar, and draw the cage on the back so that it looks right way up in that position. Make both pictures simple and bold, in the middle of the disc. If the bird and cage do not line up, redraw.
  - Check: Why do the two pictures have to be drawn in the middle of each disc? → **So that they sit on top of each other when the disc flips quickly**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the two strings between your thumbs and first fingers. Twirl the strings so that the disc spins fast. Look at the disc as it spins. Say what you see. Try spinning it slowly and then quickly.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the strings and twirl the disc. Look at it as it spins. What do you see now? What happens when it spins slowly?  
  *Child mode:* Twirl the disc. What do you see? Spin it slowly, too. What changes?
  - Note: At a fast spin, the bird should seem to sit inside the cage. At a slow spin, you see the bird, then the cage, flicking. If the picture does not join, spin faster, or redraw the pictures bolder and in the middle. To spin fast: twist the strings by rolling them between the fingers about 20 times, then pull the strings gently apart and let the disc untwist. Typically the join is clear at about 10 spins a second, so the strings must be tight. Each child makes their own disc, with their own pictures (for example a fish and a bowl, or a flower and a vase). Swap discs and guess what the other child drew on the hidden side. One child alone with a parent: make two discs and compare.

  *Parent says:* Draw a little ball at the corner of each page, a bit higher each time. Then flick the pages with your thumb. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a ball on each page. Move it a bit each time. Flick the pages. What do you see?
  - Note: Draw from the back of the pad to the front, so the flick runs from the first page to the last. Keep the ball the same size and move it a small amount each time. About 15 pages is enough to make a short bounce, but 25 or more pages last longer. Press hard with a pen so that the ball shows, and draw it near the corner where the thumb flicks the pages. If the pages are thick, the ball may be hard to see through the page, so use a dark pen. The smaller the moves, the smoother the motion. Scientists have long said that the eye holds on to each picture for a moment, called persistence of vision. Many now think that the brain also builds movement from still pictures. They are still working out how.
  - Check: In your flip book, each page is a still picture. Why does the ball seem to move? → **The brain joins the pictures that come quickly one after another, and sees movement**

Write down the speed at which the ball moves smoothly. Write the speed at which it looks like separate pictures. It typically looks smooth at about 10 pages a second or more. A short pad takes only about 1 second, so flick it several times.  
  *Parent says:* Flick the flip book slowly. What do you see? Now medium. Now fast. When does the ball seem to move smoothly?  
  *Child mode:* Flick slowly. Flick medium. Flick fast. When does the ball seem to move?
  - Think: A film shows 24 still pictures every second. Why do you think it seems smooth?
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now about how we see pictures that come quickly? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: pictures that come quickly join in my eye and brain. What I saw: the bird was inside the cage when the disc spun fast, and the ball seemed to move in the flip book. Why: the brain joins pictures that come close together in time.

## Challenges
### c1 · Readable From the Far End: design the best sign (35 min)
- **problem:** A visitor is looking for your door from the far end of the lane or corridor. Make a sign with one word on it that a friend can read from as far away as possible. Find out which size of letter and which pair of colours can be read from the furthest distance.
- **your turn:** Now design the best sign for your own front door. Draw it, make it and test it from the lane. How far away could your friend read it? How could you make it readable from further?
- **constraints:** Use one sheet of paper or chart paper for each sign, and one plain word in capital letters, such as EXIT or HOME.; Test every sign in the same light, with the same reader, and let her wear her glasses if she has them.; Measure the distance at which the reader can first read every letter, with a tape measure or with big steps that you have measured once.; Change only one thing between two signs, so that you know what made the difference.
- **general:** Make the letters bigger. A thing twice as tall can be read from about twice as far.; Use thick strokes, so each letter makes a bold shape.; Use two colours that are very different in brightness, such as black on white or black on yellow.
- **creative:** Leave a wide gap between letters, so that they do not run together from far away.; Add a thick black border round the word, so the eye finds it first.; Try colours that are alike in brightness, such as red on green, and see why signs do not use them. Remember that some people see red and green alike.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the challenge together. Ask your child what they think makes a sign easy to read from far away.  
  *Parent says:* Here is your job. Make a sign that a friend can read from as far away as possible. What do you think makes a sign easy to read from far away?  
  *Child mode:* Make a sign that is easy to read from far. What helps? Big letters? Colours?
  - Ask: What do you think will make a sign easier to read from far away? (good: Bigger letters.; Thicker letters.; A bright colour against a dark one.)
  - Note: Let your child make a plan. A good plan changes one thing at a time. Do not give the answer.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the first sign: the word EXIT in black letters 1 centimetre tall on white paper. The reader stands at the far end. The sign-holder walks towards her. When the reader can read every letter, she says 'now'. Measure the distance and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Make a sign with one word in black, with small letters one centimetre tall. Hold it up and walk towards your reader. She says 'now' when she can read every letter. How far is that?  
  *Child mode:* Make a sign with small black letters. Walk towards the reader. She says 'now' when she can read it.
  - Note: A letter 1 centimetre tall can typically be read from about 4 to 7 metres. Make sure the reader does not know the word in advance, or she will guess. Change the word each time. The reader's eyes and the light matter, so keep them the same.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make more signs, changing one thing each time. Try letters 2 centimetres tall in black on white. Then go back to letters 1 centimetre tall. Try black on yellow, yellow on white, and red on green, all 1 centimetre tall. Test each one the same way. Write each distance.  
  *Parent says:* Now change one thing at a time. First make the letters twice as tall. Then try different colours. Which sign can be read from furthest? Which from the closest?  
  *Child mode:* Change one thing at a time. Bigger letters. Different colours. Test each sign. Write each distance.
  - Note: Letters twice as tall are usually readable from about twice as far. Black on white or black on yellow usually beat yellow on white and red on green, because they differ more in brightness. Compare colours with the 1 centimetre letters, so that every sign can be read within your space. If the reader can read a sign from the whole length of your space, make the letters smaller, 5 millimetres, and test again. For 2 to 4 children: take turns as reader, sign-holder and recorder, and have each child design one sign. Test in a safe place, away from traffic. Write what you really see. Some people see red and green as nearly the same, so signs rarely use that pair.
  - Check: A word with letters 2 centimetres tall could be read from twice as far as the same word with letters 1 centimetre tall. Why? → **Twice as tall at twice the distance makes the same angle at the eye, so it looks the same size**
- **s4 (5 min)**

## Applications
- Eye doctors test how well each eye focuses, and prescribe glasses with convex or concave lenses.
- Eye camps and eye hospitals across India test eyes and give out glasses.
- Cameras and phone cameras use a lens to focus a sharp picture on a sensor, as the eye focuses on the retina.
- 3D films and virtual reality headsets show a slightly different picture to each eye, so that the brain builds depth.
- Robot cars and drones use two cameras to measure distance by the same method as two eyes.
- Astronomers measure the distance to some nearby stars by looking from two places half a year apart.
- Road and railway signs use big letters and high-contrast colours, so they can be read in time from far away.
- Binoculars give each eye its own tube and its own view.
- Cricketers and other ball players train to predict where a ball will land.
- Films and animation show still pictures one after another, 24 or more each second, and the brain sees movement.
- Reflective strips and bright jackets help drivers to notice walkers at dusk, because the edges of the eye are good at spotting movement and brightness.
- Painters use size, overlap and haze to give a flat picture the look of depth.
- Pinhole cameras and the camera obscura were used by painters and scientists to make pictures of scenes.

## Open questions
- **How does the brain turn two flat pictures into one solid world?** Scientists know many steps and many parts of the brain that work on depth. But nobody knows how those steps become the experience of seeing a solid world.
- **Why does the Moon look bigger near the horizon?** People have argued about this for more than a thousand years. Several effects probably work together, but no single idea explains all the tests.
- **Why is short sight getting more common in children, and does time outdoors really help?** Studies in several countries suggest that more time outdoors in daylight lowers the chance of short sight. Scientists are still working out why, and what else matters.
- **Can a person who never had two-eyed depth vision learn it later?** Susan Barry, a neuroscientist in the USA, gained depth vision as an adult and wrote about it. Scientists used to think this was impossible. They are now studying how often it can happen.
- **How does the brain decide which parts of the left eye's picture go with which parts of the right eye's picture?** The two pictures are full of similar-looking bits. The brain matches them very quickly, but exactly how it avoids mistakes is still being studied.
- **How does the brain predict where a fast ball will be?** Scientists can measure how fielders move and what the brain does. They are still working out what rules the brain uses to make its prediction.
- **What is it like to see with the eyes of an octopus or a hawk?** We can measure what their eyes respond to and what they can tell apart. We cannot get inside their experience, and we cannot ask them.

## Connected maths
- A child measured the jump of a pencil at three distances: 15 centimetres at 50 centimetres, 35 centimetres at 25 centimetres, and 80 centimetres at 15 centimetres. When the pencil moved from 50 to 25 centimetres, the distance halved. Did the jump less than double, exactly double or more than double? **More than double.**. The jump at 50 centimetres was 15 centimetres. Double that is 30. The jump at 25 centimetres was 35, which is more than 30. So the jump grew by more than double when the distance halved. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A pole looked 16 centimetres tall on a ruler at arm's length when it stood 2 metres away. How tall would it look at 4 metres? At 8 metres? At what distance would it look 2 centimetres tall? **8 centimetres at 4 metres, 4 centimetres at 8 metres, and 2 centimetres at 16 metres.**. Twice as far makes it look half as tall. Half of 16 is 8. Half of 8 is 4. Half of 4 is 2. That was three halvings, from 2 metres: 4, 8, then 16 metres. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- The chart shows the near points of three people. How many times further is the grandparent's near point than the child's? How many centimetres further is it than the parent's? **6 times further than the child's, and 24 centimetres further than the parent's.**. The grandparent's near point is 48 centimetres and the child's is 8. 8 times 6 is 48, so it is 6 times further. The parent's is 24. 48 take away 24 is 24 centimetres. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In the Three Cards test, the middle card was slid sideways in steps. The spot was bright at 0 and 2 millimetres, dim at 4 millimetres and gone at 6, 8 and 10 millimetres. The holes were 5 millimetres wide. At which shift did the spot first dim? Is that close to the width of the hole? What does that suggest? **It first dimmed at 4 millimetres, which is close to the 5 millimetre width of the hole. It suggests that the light stops when the card moves about as far as the width of the hole.**. The spot was bright up to 2 millimetres and dim at 4. The hole is 5 millimetres wide. 4 is a little less than 5. If the card moves about one hole-width, the holes no longer overlap, so the light cannot go straight through all three. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L4`)
- A clock face is a full circle of 360 degrees, and it has 12 hour marks. How many degrees are there between one hour mark and the next? In the clock-face test, a child noticed movement at the 3 mark and named the colour at the 1 mark, counted from the 12 straight ahead. How many degrees further out was the movement mark? **30 degrees between hour marks. The movement mark was 60 degrees further out.**. 360 divided by 12 is 30. So each hour mark is 30 degrees from the next. The 3 mark is 3 steps from the 12, so it is 90 degrees. The 1 mark is 1 step, so 30 degrees. 90 take away 30 is 60 degrees. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- A tennis ball flies at 10 metres every second. The signals from the eye take about a tenth of a second to be used. How far does the ball move in a tenth of a second? What does that tell a fielder? **1 metre. A fielder's brain is working with a picture in which the ball is already 1 metre behind its true place, so she must predict where the ball will be.**. A tenth of a second is one tenth of one second. One tenth of 10 metres is 1 metre. So the ball has moved 1 metre by the time the picture is used. The fielder has to guess ahead. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- Letters 1 centimetre tall could be read from 5 metres in the Readable From the Far End challenge. How far could letters 3 centimetres tall be read? How tall must letters be to be read from 40 metres? **15 metres for 3 centimetre letters, and 8 centimetre letters for 40 metres.**. Letters three times as tall can be read from three times as far, so 3 times 5 is 15 metres. For 40 metres, divide by 5. 40 divided by 5 is 8, so the letters must be 8 times as tall, which is 8 centimetres. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Five children each tried five times to touch pencil tips together. With both eyes, they touched 4, 5, 4, 5 and 3 times. With one eye, they touched 2, 1, 3, 1 and 2 times. How many more touches were there with both eyes? What fraction of all the one-eye tries touched? **12 more touches with both eyes. 9 out of 25 one-eye tries touched.**. Both eyes: 4 plus 5 plus 4 plus 5 plus 3 is 21. One eye: 2 plus 1 plus 3 plus 1 plus 2 is 9. 21 take away 9 is 12. There were 5 times 5, which is 25 tries, so 9 out of 25 touched. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
