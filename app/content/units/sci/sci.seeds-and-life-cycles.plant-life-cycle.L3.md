# A plant's life cycle: seed, seedling, plant, flower, fruit, and seed again
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.plant-life-cycle.L3`

**Summary.** This unit is about the life cycle of a flowering plant. A life cycle is all the stages of a life, from start to new young ones. A plant goes from seed to seedling to grown plant. Then it makes flowers. Each flower can grow into a fruit, and the fruit holds new seeds. Those seeds can grow into new plants, so the circle starts again. Your child tags a flower and watches it turn into a fruit, opens kitchen fruits to find the seeds, and can grow moong all the way from seed to seed. Then they follow the idea out: plants built from air, plants that measure the night, bamboo that waits 48 years to flower, and what nobody knows yet.

**Intro.** *Look at a tomato, or a brinjal. See the little green star, or the green cap, at the top? Where do you think it came from? Here is a clue. Not long ago, this whole tomato was a small yellow flower.* Every fruit in your kitchen was once a flower. Every seed inside it can grow into a plant that makes flowers again. Put a moong seed in a pot today. In about two months, if it gets sun and water, it will give you back a whole handful of moong. Where does the plant come from? Why does it make flowers? And why does it end up exactly where it started, as a seed? This unit is about finding out.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the stages of a flowering plant's life cycle in order: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, fruit and new seeds.
1. I can explain that a fruit grows from a flower and holds the seeds. I can find the flower's leftover parts on a fruit.
2. I can explain why it is called a cycle: the new seeds grow into new plants, so the stages start again.
3. I can watch a real plant change over days and weeks. I can measure it and record it in a table, a bar chart or a line graph.
4. I can give examples of plants with short and long life cycles, and of plants that grow new plants without seeds.
5. I can explain that a plant builds its body mostly from air and water, using sunlight, and gives it back when it dies.
6. I can say what scientists agree on about plant life cycles, and what they still do not know, such as how bamboo counts the years.

## Terms
- **Life cycle**: All the stages a living thing goes through, from the start of its life until it makes young ones of its own. Then the stages start again. *e.g.* Moong: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, pod, new seeds.
- **Stage**: One step or part of a life cycle. *e.g.* Being a seedling is one stage. Flowering is another stage.
- **Seed**: A baby plant packed in a coat, with its own store of food. *e.g.* A moong seed, a mango stone and a grain of rice are all seeds.
- **Seedling**: A very young plant that has just come out of its seed. It has a tiny root, a short stem and its first leaves. *e.g.* The small green shoots in a tray of sprouted methi are seedlings.
- **Seed leaves**: The first leaves, which were already folded up inside the seed. They are often thick and round, and hold food. *e.g.* The two round leaves at the top of a new bean plant.
- **True leaves**: The leaves that grow after the seed leaves. They have the plant's own leaf shape, and they make food from sunlight. *e.g.* A tomato seedling's true leaves are jagged and smell of tomato.
- **Grown plant**: A plant that is big enough and old enough to make flowers. Scientists call it a mature plant. *e.g.* A moong plant starts to flower when it is about one month old.
- **Bud**: A flower, or a leaf, before it opens. It is folded up tight. *e.g.* A rose bud, or the tight green tip at the end of a stem.
- **Flower**: The part of a plant that makes seeds. Many flowers are bright and sweet-smelling. That brings insects and birds to visit them. *e.g.* A hibiscus, a marigold, and the small yellow flowers on a moong plant.
- **Petals**: The soft, often coloured parts around the middle of a flower. *e.g.* A marigold has many orange petals. A hibiscus has five.
- **Sepals**: The small green leaves that wrap a flower bud before it opens. On many fruits they stay on, like a green star or a cap. *e.g.* The green cap on a brinjal and the green star on a tomato are sepals.
- **Pollen**: A fine dust made by flowers, usually yellow. It must reach the middle of a flower before the flower can make seeds. *e.g.* The yellow dust left on your nose after smelling a lily.
- **Pollination**: Pollen being carried to the sticky tip in the middle of a flower. Wind, insects, birds, bats or people can carry it. Only then can most flowers grow a fruit with seeds. *e.g.* A bee covered in yellow dust flying from one pumpkin flower to another.
- **Fruit**: The part of a plant that grows from a flower and holds the seeds. To a scientist, a tomato, a bhindi and a chilli are all fruits. *e.g.* A mango, a pea pod, a cucumber and a bhindi are all fruits.
- **Pod**: A long fruit that holds a row of seeds. It often splits open when it is ripe. *e.g.* Pea pods, moong pods and drumsticks.
- **Ripe**: Fully grown and ready. A ripe fruit often changes colour and gets soft or sweet. Its seeds are ready to grow. *e.g.* A green mango turns yellow and sweet when it is ripe.
- **Annual**: A plant that goes through its whole life cycle in one year or less, then dies. *e.g.* Moong, rice, wheat and marigold are annuals.
- **Perennial**: A plant that lives for many years and can flower again and again. *e.g.* Mango, neem, coconut, rose and tulsi are perennials.
- **Cutting**: A piece of a plant, like a stem or a leaf, that can grow roots and become a new plant without a seed. *e.g.* A money plant stem in a bottle of water grows roots.
- **Clone**: A living thing that is an exact copy of its parent. Plants grown from cuttings are clones. *e.g.* The bananas of one kind on a farm are copies of one plant.
- **Photosynthesis**: How green leaves make food. They take in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the roots. They use the energy of sunlight to turn these into sugar, and they give out oxygen. *e.g.* A tulsi plant in the sun is making sugar right now.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas in the air, which you breathe out. Plants take it in and build their bodies from it. *e.g.* Every breath you breathe out has carbon dioxide in it.
- **Decomposer**: A living thing that breaks down dead plants and animals, and puts their goodness back into the soil. *e.g.* Mushrooms, earthworms and tiny bacteria in a compost pit.

## Explanations
### What a life cycle is · *defined*
**Definition:** A life cycle is all the stages a living thing goes through, from the start of its life until it makes young ones. For a flowering plant the stages are: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, fruit, and new seeds.

It is called a cycle because it goes round in a circle. The last stage, new seeds, is also the first stage of the next plant. So the circle never really ends. A moong seed grows into a moong plant. The plant makes flowers. The flowers turn into pods. The pods hold new moong seeds, and each one can start again. You can start drawing the circle at any stage. A seed is not more of a 'beginning' than a flower is.
- e.g. Moong: seed, seedling, grown plant, yellow flower, green pod, black pod full of new seeds.
- e.g. Mango: mango stone, seedling, young tree, mango flowers, mangoes, new mango stones.
- e.g. Marigold: seed, seedling, plant, orange flower, dry flower head full of thin black seeds.
### From seed to seedling · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed wakes up when it gets water, warmth and air. Its root comes out first and grows down. Then its shoot grows up into the light, and the plant becomes a seedling.

The unit 'Seeds and how they sprout' tells this part of the story in detail. Here is the short version. At first, the seedling lives on the food packed inside the seed. Many seedlings first show two round seed leaves, which were folded up inside the seed. Soon the true leaves grow above them. True leaves have the plant's own leaf shape. Once they open, the seedling can make its own food from sunlight. The seed leaves are no longer needed. They shrink, go yellow and fall off.
- e.g. A bean seedling has two fat seed leaves first, then pointed true leaves.
- e.g. A rice or maize seedling comes up as one thin green spike.
### Growing up: leaves make food from sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** A growing plant makes its own food in its green leaves. This is called photosynthesis. Leaves take in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the roots. Using the energy of sunlight, they turn them into sugar. They give out oxygen.

The plant uses the sugar in two ways. It burns some for energy, the way you burn your food. It also uses sugar to build new stems, leaves and roots. So a plant grows mainly by making more of itself out of air and water. A plant grows slowly at first, then fast, then slowly again once it is grown. The chart shows a made-up example for a moong plant in a pot. It grows fastest in weeks 3 to 5. After about week 6 it puts its effort into flowers and pods instead of getting taller.
- e.g. A plant kept in a dark cupboard grows pale, thin and floppy, then dies. It cannot make food.
- e.g. Leaves turn to face the window. They are reaching for light.
### When a plant is ready to flower · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant flowers when it is big enough, and when the season is right. Many plants find out the season by measuring how long the nights are.

A young plant first grows leaves and roots. When it has grown enough, the tips of some stems stop making leaves and make flower buds instead. Many plants wait for the right season too. In 1920, two American scientists, Wightman Garner and Harry Allard, found that some tobacco and soybean plants flower only when the days get short. In 1938, Karl Hamner and James Bonner showed that what the plant really measures is the length of the night. Flash a light in the middle of a long night, and those plants do not flower. The leaves do the measuring. Then they send a message up the stem to the buds. A Russian scientist, Mikhail Chailakhyan, guessed in 1936 that such a message must exist. It took until 2007 for scientists to find it. It is a protein that travels from the leaves to the bud.
- e.g. Rice in many parts of India flowers when the nights get longer after the monsoon.
- e.g. Some plants, like wheat in cold countries, wait until they have felt a cold winter.
- e.g. Moong flowers about a month after sowing, whatever the day length, once it is big enough.
### A flower is where the fruit will grow · *defined*
**Definition:** A flower is the part of a plant that makes seeds. In its middle is a swollen base with tiny seed-starts inside. When pollen reaches the sticky tip above that base, the base can grow into a fruit, and the seed-starts can grow into seeds.

Look inside a flower from the outside in. First come the green sepals, which wrapped the bud. Then come the petals, often bright and scented, which bring in visitors. Next come thin stalks with yellow dust on their tips. The dust is pollen. In the very middle is a part shaped a little like a bottle. Its base is the ovary. Scientists call the tiny seed-starts inside it ovules. Pollen must be carried to the sticky tip of the bottle. This is called pollination. Insects, birds, bats, wind or people can carry it. Some flowers, like moong, peas and tomato, can do it themselves. After that, the petals fall off and the base starts to swell. That swelling base is the baby fruit. You will learn much more about pollination in the Class 4 unit on flowers.
- e.g. In a pumpkin flower, you can see the baby pumpkin as a little green ball behind the petals.
- e.g. A bhindi flower opens for just one morning. Five days later, a finger-long pod is in its place.
### A fruit is a flower's base grown big, holding seeds · *defined*
**Definition:** To a scientist, a fruit is the part that grows from a flower's base and holds the seeds. So tomatoes, bhindi, brinjal, cucumber, chillies and pea pods are all fruits.

In the kitchen, we call sweet ones fruits and the others vegetables. Botanists, the scientists who study plants, use a different rule. If it grew from a flower and holds seeds, it is a fruit. You can often see what is left of the flower on a fruit. The green star on top of a tomato and the green cap on a brinjal are the old sepals. The little crown at the end of a pomegranate or a guava is the flower's leftovers too. Each seed inside came from one tiny seed-start in the flower. Some fruits have one seed, like a mango. Others have hundreds, like a papaya.
- e.g. A pea pod is a fruit. The peas are its seeds.
- e.g. A pomegranate's crown is the remains of its red flower.
- e.g. A banana's dark tip is where its flower was.
### Why plants make fruits: sending seeds away · *defined*
**Definition:** A fruit protects the seeds and helps them travel away from the parent plant. Many fruits are sweet so that animals eat them and drop the seeds somewhere else.

A seed that falls right under its parent has to fight it for light and water. A seed carried far away has a better chance. So plants have many tricks. Sweet fruits are eaten by birds, bats, monkeys and people. The seeds pass through, or get spat out, far away. Pods can burst and fling their seeds. Some fruits have wings or fluff and fly on the wind. Coconuts float across the sea. Chillies have a clever trick too. In 2001, two American scientists, Joshua Tewksbury and Gary Nabhan, found that the hot taste of chillies keeps away mice and other mammals. Mammals chew seeds up. Birds swallow seeds whole and drop them far away. And birds do not feel the burning at all. You will learn more about how seeds travel in Class 4.
- e.g. A koel eats a ripe banyan fig and drops the seeds on a far-off wall.
- e.g. Moong and pea pods dry, twist and pop, flinging their seeds out.
- e.g. Grass in a shoe brings seeds home after a walk.
### Short and long life cycles · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants finish their whole life cycle in a few weeks or months, then die. They are called annuals. Others live for many years and flower again and again. They are called perennials.

Moong goes from seed to seed in about two months. Rice takes about four months. A mango tree grown from a stone usually takes five to eight years before its first mangoes, and then it fruits every year for a long time. Some plants wait for years, flower just once, and then die. The neelakurinji shrub, which grows in the hills of Kerala and Tamil Nadu, flowers all together only once every 12 years. Its last big bloom near Munnar was in 2018. One kind of bamboo in Mizoram waits about 48 years. Then all the plants of that kind flower together, make their seeds, and die. The talipot palm can wait more than 30 years. Then it makes one of the biggest bunches of flowers of any plant, and dies.
- e.g. Short: moong, methi, marigold and mustard, from seed to seed in a few months.
- e.g. Long: mango, neem and coconut, which flower for years and years.
- e.g. Once in a long life: neelakurinji every 12 years, and bamboo after about 48 years.
### New plants without seeds · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants can also grow new plants from a piece of themselves, such as a stem, a leaf, a root or a bulb. The new plant is an exact copy of its parent, called a clone.

A potato is a swollen underground stem. Each 'eye' on it can sprout a new potato plant. An onion is a bulb, made of thick leaves wrapped round a short stem. Put its bottom in water and it grows new roots and green leaves. A money plant stem grows roots in a bottle of water. Sugarcane is planted as pieces of stem. The patharchatta plant, also called Bryophyllum, grows tiny baby plants all along the edges of its leaves. The bananas we eat have no proper seeds. Farmers grow new banana plants from shoots at the base of the old one. Copies are quick and easy. But they are risky. In the 1950s, a disease wiped out most of the world's favourite banana, the Gros Michel, because every plant was a copy with the same weakness. A new form of that disease now threatens today's banana, the Cavendish.
- e.g. An old potato in a dark basket grows white shoots from its eyes.
- e.g. A rose cutting pushed into damp soil can grow into a new rose bush.
- e.g. Grass spreads by stems that creep along the ground.
### Deep down: a plant is built from air · *defined*
**Definition:** Most of a plant's body is made from carbon dioxide taken from the air, and water. Only a very small part comes from the soil.

This surprises almost everyone, including grown-ups. In the 1600s, a Flemish scientist, Jan Baptist van Helmont, tried a test. He put a small willow tree in a big pot of dried, weighed soil. For five years he gave it only rainwater. The tree grew from about 2 kilograms to about 77 kilograms. The soil lost only about 60 grams. He thought the tree was made of water. He was partly right. We now know most of its dry wood is made of carbon atoms taken from carbon dioxide in the air. In the 1950s, Melvin Calvin and his team in California followed carbon atoms from the air into the sugar a plant made. They used a special kind of carbon they could track. A big neem tree is mostly air and water, put together with the energy of sunlight. The soil gives the plant small amounts of important things, called minerals, like iron and nitrogen.
- e.g. When wood burns, it turns back into carbon dioxide and water vapour, and only a little ash is left. The ash is the part that came from the soil.
- e.g. A pot plant can grow for years without the pot getting much emptier of soil.
### Death feeds the next life · *defined*
**Definition:** When a plant dies, decomposers break it down. Its atoms go back into the soil and the air, and new plants are built from them.

Fungi like mushrooms, tiny bacteria, earthworms, termites and woodlice all feed on dead leaves and stems. As they eat, they break the plant into smaller and smaller pieces. Some of its carbon goes back into the air as carbon dioxide. Its minerals go back into the soil. The next seeds use that air and soil to grow. So the same atoms go round and round through plants, animals, soil and air. The life cycle of a plant sits inside a much bigger cycle of atoms. The carbon in your body was once in plants too, because all your food comes from plants, or from animals that ate plants.
- e.g. A pile of fallen leaves under a tree is gone by the next year. Decomposers ate it.
- e.g. A compost pit turns kitchen peels into dark, crumbly soil in a few months.
### Every seed carries a copy of the plant's instructions · *defined*
**Definition:** Every cell of a plant holds a long set of instructions for building that plant. The instructions are written in a chemical called DNA. Each seed gets a copy from its parents.

That is why a moong seed always grows into moong, never into mango. The instructions are copied again and again, from plant to seed to plant, all the way round the life cycle. When pollen from one plant reaches the flower of another, the seed gets some instructions from each parent. So seeds are a little different from each other, just as brothers and sisters are. Between 1856 and 1863, a monk called Gregor Mendel grew thousands of pea plants in his monastery garden in Brno, now in the Czech Republic. He chose peas because they go round their life cycle in one season. He found patterns in how things like seed colour were passed on. When he published his work in 1866, almost nobody noticed. It was rediscovered in 1900, after he had died. Today he is called the father of genetics, the science of how living things pass on their features.
- e.g. Mango seeds from one sweet tree can grow into trees with quite different mangoes. So farmers grow their best mangoes from cuttings, joined onto young trees.
- e.g. Every rice plant in a field grew from instructions copied from earlier rice plants, going back thousands of years.
### Why did flowering plants take over the world? · *debated*
The first land plants, rather like mosses, appeared over 450 million years ago. Ferns and conifers came long before flowers. The first flowering plants appear in rocks from about 130 million years ago, in the time of the dinosaurs. Then they spread very fast. Today, almost 9 out of every 10 kinds of land plant have flowers. In 1879, Charles Darwin wrote to his friend Joseph Hooker that this sudden spread was 'an abominable mystery'. Scientists still argue about it.
- e.g. Ferns and mosses have no flowers. They spread by spores, a fine dust, instead of seeds.
- e.g. Pine trees make seeds in cones, not in flowers or fruits.
- **Many scientists who study insects and flowers:** Flowers teamed up with insects, birds and bats. Pollen carried from one plant to another made new kinds of plant appear faster.
- **Scientists who study how plants grow:** Flowering plants grow fast and finish their life cycles quickly, so they could take over open land before slower plants.
- **Other scientists:** Fruits let animals carry seeds far and wide. Some also point to changes in the climate and the land at the time. Most think several of these worked together.
### How does bamboo count the years? · *idea*
Melocanna bamboo in Mizoram flowers about every 48 years. All the clumps across hundreds of kilometres flower together, even clumps planted far apart. Then they make huge numbers of fruits and die. In 1958 and again from 2006 to 2008, the flowering fed so many rats that they multiplied and then ate the farmers' crops. People there call this mautam, and it caused famine. How the bamboo keeps time for so long, and stays in step, is not known. Nobody has found a 48-year clock inside a plant.
- e.g. Neelakurinji in the Nilgiri and Munnar hills flowers together every 12 years.
- e.g. Bamboo of one kind planted in another country can still flower at about the same time as its family back home.
- **Daniel Janzen, an American scientist, in 1976:** Flowering all together makes more seeds than the rats and birds can eat, so some survive. Plants that flowered out of step had their few seeds eaten, so the habit spread.
- **Other scientists:** The plants may count something inside them, like rounds of growth, rather than years. Or the weather may nudge them. Nobody has proved how it works.
### A life cycle in space · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants can go through their whole life cycle in space. In 1982, small thale cress plants grown on the Soviet space station Salyut 7 flowered and made seeds. They were the first plants known to go from seed to seed in space.

In space, there is no 'down' to guide the roots, so astronauts and scientists must help plants grow. Since then, wheat, peas, lettuce, zinnia flowers and others have been grown on space stations. Space agencies study this because people living on the Moon or Mars one day will need plants that can keep going round their life cycle there. They will need them for food, and to turn the carbon dioxide that astronauts breathe out back into oxygen.
- e.g. In 2016, an astronaut on the International Space Station grew orange zinnia flowers.
- e.g. Astronauts on the International Space Station have eaten lettuce they grew there.

## Mostly agreed
- Flowering plants go through a life cycle: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, fruit and new seeds. *How we know:* You can watch it in a pot of moong in about two months. Farmers have watched it in every field for thousands of years.
- A fruit grows from the base of a flower, and holds the seeds. *How we know:* Tag a flower with a thread and watch it every day. The petals fall and the base swells into the fruit. The flower's leftovers often stay on the fruit.
- Most flowers make fruit only after pollen reaches the middle of the flower. *How we know:* Cover a pumpkin flower with a paper bag so no insect can reach it, and it shrivels. Add pollen by hand, and it grows. Gardeners and vanilla farmers do this every day.
- A plant builds its body mostly from carbon dioxide and water, using sunlight. *How we know:* Van Helmont's willow grew about 75 kilograms while the soil lost about 60 grams. In the 1950s, Melvin Calvin's team followed carbon from the air into a plant's sugar.
- Many plants decide when to flower by measuring the length of the night. *How we know:* Experiments since 1920 show that flashing a light in the middle of the night can stop some plants from flowering, or make others flower.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A plant gets its food and its body from the soil.** (A very common idea. Many people still think it today.; *rejected*)  
  For: Roots grow in the soil, and plants die if the soil dries out. Plants in good soil grow better.  
  Against: Van Helmont's willow grew about 75 kilograms in five years, but the soil lost only about 60 grams. The soil gives water and small amounts of minerals. The rest of the plant is made from carbon dioxide from the air, using sunlight.
- **Tomatoes, bhindi and cucumbers are vegetables, not fruits.** (Cooks, shopkeepers, and in 1893 the highest court in the United States; *debated*)  
  For: In the kitchen, we use them in sabzi and salads, not in sweets. In 1893, the US Supreme Court decided that a tomato counts as a vegetable for paying taxes.  
  Against: To a botanist, anything that grows from a flower and holds seeds is a fruit. Both are useful ways of sorting. They just answer different questions.
- **Every plant grows from a seed.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Most plants we grow in gardens and fields do come from seeds.  
  Against: Potatoes, sugarcane, bananas, money plants and many roses are grown from pieces of the plant instead. Ferns and mosses have no seeds at all. They spread by spores.
- **When a plant dies, it is wasted.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: A dead plant gives no more flowers or fruit, and it looks like rubbish.  
  Against: Decomposers break it down, and its atoms go back to the soil and the air. New plants are built from them. Forests depend on this. A fallen tree feeds thousands of living things for years.
- **Flowers exist to be beautiful for us.** (A common idea, and many poems; *rejected*)  
  For: People love flowers. We have bred roses and marigolds to be even brighter and bigger.  
  Against: Flowers existed for about 130 million years before there were people. Their colours, scents and sweet nectar bring insects, birds and bats to carry pollen. Some flowers are dull green and are pollinated by the wind. Our love of them is a happy extra.
- **Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old boy, found the way to make vanilla flowers turn into fruit, in 1841.** (Historians of vanilla, and the people of Réunion island; *accepted*)  
  For: His owner, Ferréol Bellier-Beaumont, wrote letters saying Edmond had found it. Farmers still use his method of a thin stick and a thumb.  
  Against: A French botanist, Jean Michel Claude Richard, later claimed that he had taught Edmond. And a Belgian botanist, Charles Morren, had made vanilla flowers fruit in a greenhouse a few years earlier, in the 1830s. But Morren's way was slow and was not used on farms.

## Story: Edmond's Thumb
*Edmond, Ferréol Bellier-Beaumont, the vanilla vine and the year 1841 are real. So are Edmond's method, the fight over the credit, his freedom in 1848 and the name Albius. Bellier-Beaumont is said to have shown Edmond how to pollinate watermelon flowers by hand. We changed it to pumpkins. The conversations and the small details are imagined.*

In 1841, on an island in the Indian Ocean called Bourbon, there grew a vine that would not make fruit. It climbed the trees in the garden of a man named Ferréol Bellier-Beaumont. Its leaves were thick and shiny. Every year it covered itself in pale green flowers that smelled faintly of honey. And every year, by the next evening, each flower had dropped to the ground, and nothing was left behind.

The vine was vanilla. It had come across the sea from Mexico. There, its long pods were dried to make one of the most wonderful smells in the world. In Mexico, the vines hung heavy with pods. But here, and in the hothouses of France, and in the gardens of Java, the vines grew and grew and flowered and flowered, and gave nothing at all. Some people said the plant was homesick.

Edmond was twelve. He was enslaved, which means that Bellier-Beaumont owned him, the way a man might own a cart. The law allowed it then. It was deeply wrong. Edmond's mother had died when he was born, and the old man had brought him up among the plants. Edmond carried water, pulled weeds and followed Bellier-Beaumont along the garden paths. He was always listening.

One morning the old man stopped by the pumpkin patch. 'Look here,' he said. He pointed at two yellow flowers that looked exactly alike. Then he lifted one and turned it over. Behind its petals, where the flower met its stalk, sat a tiny green ball, no bigger than a marble.

'A baby pumpkin,' said Bellier-Beaumont. 'This is a mother flower. That one is a father flower. It has no baby behind it. It only has dust.' He picked the father flower, peeled back its petals, and showed Edmond the yellow powder on the little stalk in its middle. Then he dabbed the powder into the centre of the mother flower, like pressing a stamp onto a letter. 'Now we wait,' he said.

Edmond waited. Two days later, he ran to the pumpkin patch before breakfast. The mother flower's petals had shrivelled. But the green ball behind them had grown to the size of a lime. Another mother flower nearby, which nobody had touched, had gone yellow and soft. When Edmond poked it, it fell off in his hand.

He crouched there for a long time, looking from one to the other. So that was it. A flower was not the end of anything. A flower was a fruit that had not happened yet. Something had to happen first, deep in its middle. The dust had to get in.

That afternoon he went to look at the vanilla vine. Its flowers had already closed. They opened only in the early morning, for a few hours, and never again. So the next day he came back at dawn, when the grass was still wet.

He looked into one flower. He could see no dust anywhere. He looked into a second, and a third. In the middle of each was a little pale column, smooth as candle wax. He touched it gently with a fingertip. At its tip, half-hidden under a tiny cap, was a smear of sticky yellow. The dust! Just below it was a shiny, sticky place, the place where the dust needed to go. But between the two lay a thin flap, like a small tongue, keeping them apart.

Much later, people would learn that in Mexico, certain small bees know how to push past that flap. On Bourbon, there were no such bees. Every year, the flowers had opened for a visitor who never came.

Edmond snapped a thin splinter off a bamboo stick. He slid it under the little flap and lifted it, gently, the way you lift the hand of a sleeping baby. Then he pressed the dusty cap down onto the sticky place with his thumb. He held it for a moment, and let go. The flower looked exactly the same as before. He did another, and another, until the sun was high and the flowers began to close.

For two days, nothing happened. The flowers he had touched wilted, like all the others. But they did not fall. On the third day, Edmond saw it. Where each of his flowers had been, a thin green finger was beginning to grow.

Bellier-Beaumont found them a week later. He stood very still in front of the vine. 'Who did this?' he asked. Edmond's heart was thumping. He had touched the old man's most precious plant without asking. 'I did,' he said. Bellier-Beaumont looked at him for a long moment. 'Show me,' he said.

So Edmond showed him. The splinter. The flap. The thumb.

After that, Edmond was taken from farm to farm across the island, to teach the enslaved workers how to do it. The farmers called it 'marrying' the vanilla. The green fingers took nine months to ripen into long pods. Then the pods were dried until they turned dark and sweet-smelling. Within a few years, the island was sending vanilla across the world.

Later, a well-known botanist said that he had taught Edmond the trick himself. Bellier-Beaumont wrote letters to say that this was not true, and that the boy had found it alone. In 1848, slavery ended on the island, and Edmond became free. He was given a new surname, Albius, from an old word for white, like the vanilla flower. But he was never paid for his idea, and he died poor, in 1880.

The vines did not forget him, though. Today, on farms in Madagascar, in Réunion, as Bourbon is now called, and in Kerala and Karnataka in India, people still walk along the vanilla vines in the early morning. They carry a thin stick. They lift the flap. They press with their thumb.

And when the pods are ripe, each one is packed with thousands of seeds, too small to see one at a time. If you have ever seen tiny black specks in a scoop of vanilla ice cream, you have seen them. Every speck is a seed. Every one began in a flower that opened for a single morning, and that someone, with a splinter and a thumb, helped to become a fruit.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a flower is not the end of a plant's work. It is where the fruit will grow, but only after pollen reaches the sticky place in its middle. The pumpkin flower that was dusted grew into a pumpkin. The one left alone shrivelled. The vanilla flowers had always been able to make fruit, but the insect that carries their pollen did not live on the island. Once Edmond moved the pollen himself, each flower turned into a pod full of seeds. That is the life cycle's turn from flower to fruit to seed. The story also asks who gets the credit for discoveries, and who does not.

- Ask: Why did the vanilla flowers drop off every year without making any pods?
- Ask: What did Edmond learn from the two pumpkin flowers, the one that was dusted and the one left alone?
- Ask: Edmond thought that a flower is 'a fruit that has not happened yet'. What did he mean?
- Ask: A grown-up botanist said he had taught Edmond. Why do you think Bellier-Beaumont wrote letters to say it was not true?
- Ask: The black specks in vanilla ice cream are seeds. What stage of the life cycle are they, and what could they become?

### e1 · Flower to Fruit: tag a flower and watch it change (50 min, 10 days)

### e3 · Be Edmond: help a pumpkin flower become a fruit (35 min, 5 days)
- **question:** Does a mother flower need pollen to grow into a fruit?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will have three mother flowers. One is left open for insects. One is covered with a bag and gets no pollen. One is covered with a bag and gets pollen from you. Which will grow into fruits?

  *Parent does:* Compare all three flowers on day 5.  
  *Parent says:* Edmond found out that vanilla flowers needed someone to carry their pollen. Who carried pollen to our red flower?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your three flowers. Who carried pollen to the open red flower? Edmond did this job for vanilla flowers!
  - Think: Bees, butterflies, moths and even bats carry pollen for free. What would happen to our food if they all disappeared?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like real scientists. Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a mother pumpkin flower needs pollen to become a pumpkin. What I saw: the covered flower with no pollen went yellow and fell off on day 3. The covered flower I brushed with pollen was twice as big by day 4. Why: the pollen has to reach the middle of the flower. Only then can its base grow into a fruit with seeds.
### e4 · Floating Leaves: does a leaf make gas in the light? (40 min)
- **question:** Do leaves make gas when light shines on them?
- **predict:** Guess first. The leaf circles will sink to the bottom of two glasses. One glass goes in bright light and one in a dark cupboard. In which glass will the circles float up?
- **fair test:** Make the two glasses the same. Use the same water, the same pinch of baking soda and drop of soap, and 10 circles cut from the same leaf in each. Change only the light. Count the floating circles in both glasses at the same times.
- **safety:** The syringe has no needle. Do not drink the water. Wash the glasses well afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table every 5 minutes. Then draw a bar for each glass, showing how many circles were floating after 20 minutes.
- **results table:** Minutes | Glass in bright light: circles floating | Glass in the dark: circles floating
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Half fill two clear glasses with tap water. 2. Add a small pinch of baking soda to each, about an eighth of a teaspoon. 3. Add one tiny drop of washing-up liquid to each. 4. Stir gently, so it does not foam.  
  *Parent says:* Baking soda puts extra carbon dioxide in the water. That is the gas leaves use to make food. The drop of soap helps the water soak into the leaf.  
  *Child mode:* Half fill two glasses with water. Add a small pinch of baking soda to each. Add one tiny drop of soap. Stir gently.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay a palak leaf on a soft eraser or your fingertip. 2. Press the straw straight down and twist, to cut out a circle. 3. Cut 20 circles. Try to miss the thick veins.  
  *Parent says:* We need 20 little leaf circles, 10 for each glass.  
  *Child mode:* Press the straw into the leaf and twist. Out pops a circle! Cut 20. Miss the thick lines in the leaf.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pull the plunger out of the syringe. Drop 10 circles in. 2. Push the plunger back in gently, without squashing them. 3. Suck up water from one glass until the syringe is half full. 4. Point it up and push out the air. 5. Cover the tip firmly with a finger. Pull the plunger back hard and hold for 10 seconds, then let go. 6. Do this 3 to 5 times, until the circles sink.  
  *Parent says:* Pulling the plunger sucks the air out of the leaf circles. Water goes in instead, so they sink. Leaves have air spaces inside, like a sponge.  
  *Child mode:* Put 10 circles in the syringe. Suck up some water. Push out the air. Block the tip with a finger. Pull the plunger back hard. Do it again until they sink.
  - Note: Your child can do most of this, but small hands may need help holding the tip shut while pulling. Will the circles not sink after 5 tries? Add one more tiny drop of soap to the water and try again.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Squirt the sunk circles into the first glass. 2. Do the same with 10 more circles for the second glass. 3. If any circle floats, poke it down or sink it again.  
  *Parent says:* All 20 circles are at the bottom now. Let's give them different places to wait.  
  *Child mode:* Squirt the circles into the first glass. Do 10 more for the second glass. Are they all at the bottom?
- **s5 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one glass in bright sunlight, or close under a bright lamp. 2. Put the other in a dark cupboard. 3. Every 5 minutes, count the floating circles in each glass and write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Every five minutes, let's count. Look closely at the circles in the light. Can you see tiny bubbles?  
  *Child mode:* One glass in bright light. One in the dark cupboard. Every 5 minutes, count the floaters. Write the numbers. Look for tiny bubbles.
  - Note: In strong sunlight the first circles usually rise after about 5 to 15 minutes. Most float within 20 to 25 minutes. Under a weak lamp or on a cloudy day it is slower, so move the lamp closer or wait longer. The circles in the dark usually stay down. While you wait, your child can draw the two glasses.
  - Check: Why did the leaf circles in the light float up? → **The leaves made oxygen gas while making food, and the tiny bubbles lifted them**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap the glasses: put the dark glass in the light, and the light glass in the dark. Look again in 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think will happen now?  
  *Child mode:* Swap the two glasses. What will happen now? Look again later.
  - Note: The dark circles now usually float too. The floating ones put in the dark slowly sink again over 20 to 30 minutes. The leaf uses up its oxygen to breathe.
  - Think: A whole tree is doing this in the sun, all day. Where does the oxygen go?
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like real scientists. Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: leaves make gas when light shines on them. What I saw: after 20 minutes, 9 circles were floating in the light, and none in the dark. Why: in light, the leaf makes food from carbon dioxide and water, and gives out oxygen. The bubbles of oxygen lifted the circles.

### p1 · Kitchen to Kitchen: grow moong from seed to seed (130 min, 75 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up makes holes in the bottom of a bucket. Wash your hands after touching soil. Do not use pesticide sprays.
- **record:** A diary page every week: the date, a drawing, the height of the tallest plant, and the number of flowers or pods. At the end, a line graph of height, and the total number of seeds.
- **schedule:** day 1: Soak 8 moong seeds overnight. Fill the pot. Name your plant. (15 min); day 2: Sow the seeds 2 cm deep. Water them. Put the pot in the sun. (10 min); day 10: Look for seed leaves and true leaves. Keep the 3 or 4 strongest plants. Measure. (10 min); day 17: Measure the tallest plant. Draw it. (5 min); day 24: Measure. Draw. Look for buds at the tips. (5 min); day 35: Flowers! Count them. Write the date of the first flower. (10 min); day 42: Pods! Tie a thread on the first pod. Measure it. (10 min); day 55: Measure. Are any pods turning brown or black? (5 min); day 65: Pick the black, dry pods. Dry them in the sun for 2 days. (20 min); day 70: Open the pods. Count the seeds. Make your graph. (20 min); day 75: Sow some new seeds to start again. Sprout and eat some. Read your diary aloud. (20 min)
- **s1 (25 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Soak 8 moong seeds in water overnight. 2. Fill the pot with soil to 2 cm below the top. 3. Next day, push the seeds 2 cm deep, about 5 cm apart. 4. Water gently until water comes out of the bottom. 5. Put the pot in your sunniest spot. 6. Name the plant and draw page 1 of its diary.  
  *Parent says:* These seeds came from our kitchen. If we look after them, they will give us moong back. What shall we call our plant?  
  *Child mode:* Soak 8 moong seeds overnight. Push them 2 cm into the soil. Water them. Put the pot in the sun. Give your plant a name!
  - Note: Moong likes warm weather, about 25 to 35 degrees. In most of north India, sow it between February and August. In much of south India it grows most of the year. In north India's cold months, from November to January, grow matar instead. Matar flowers in about 6 to 8 weeks and makes dry seeds in about 3 months. The pot must get at least 5 hours of direct sun. In shade, moong grows tall and thin and may not flower at all. If you have no sunny spot, ask to use a relative's terrace or a school garden.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look for the seedlings. 2. First come two fat seed leaves, then two plain leaves, and after that leaves in groups of three. 3. Around day 10, keep the 3 or 4 strongest plants. Snip the others at the soil with scissors, instead of pulling them.  
  *Parent says:* Look, its first leaves are fat and round. Those came from inside the seed. The new leaves have their own shape.  
  *Child mode:* Find the seed leaves. They are fat and round. Then find the new leaves. Keep the 3 or 4 strongest plants.

  - Wait: About a week
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Once a week: 1. Measure the tallest plant from the soil to its top. 2. Draw it. 3. Write the number in the diary.  
  *Parent says:* How much did it grow this week? Is it growing faster or slower than last week?  
  *Child mode:* Once a week, measure the tallest plant. Draw it. Write the number down. Did it grow fast or slow?
  - Note: Tiny black or green insects on the tips are aphids. Wash them off with a gentle spray of water. Leaves with yellow patches may have a virus that is common in moong. Snip off that plant and keep the others. Heavy rain can rot the roots, so move the pot under a ledge in a storm.
  - Wait: Every week
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Around day 30 to 40, look for small pale yellow flowers at the ends of short stalks. 2. Write the date of the first flower. 3. Count the flowers each week.  
  *Parent says:* Our plant is a grown plant now. It has stopped just making leaves. It is making flowers.  
  *Child mode:* Look for small yellow flowers. Write the date of the first one. Count the flowers.
  - Note: Moong flowers can pollinate themselves, so you do not need bees. The flowers are small and close by midday, so look in the morning.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A few days after flowering, look for thin green pods where the flowers were. 2. Tie a thread on the first pod. 3. Measure it each week.  
  *Parent says:* Where the flower was, there is now a pod. What do you think is growing inside it?  
  *Child mode:* Find a thin green pod where a flower was. Tie a thread on it. Measure it. What is inside?
  - Check: A thin green pod is growing where your moong flower was. What is the pod? → **The fruit, which grew from the flower and holds the new seeds**
- **s6 (40 min)**

  *Parent says:* One seed went into the soil. How many have we got back? Let's count.  
  *Child mode:* Pick the black pods before they pop open. Dry them in the sun. Open them. Count every seed!

Each pod usually has about 10 to 15 seeds. The plant itself turns yellow and dries once its pods are ripe. That is the end of its life cycle, not a mistake.
  - Check: Your moong plant turns yellow and dries up after its pods ripen. What has happened? → **It has finished its life cycle, as all annual plants do**
- **s7 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sow some of your new seeds in the pot again, to start the next circle. 2. Soak and sprout some to eat. 3. Put the old plants in a compost pit or bin. 4. Read the diary aloud to the family.

  - Note: If the plants die early, from too little sun, too much water or pests, that is sad. It is all right to feel sad. Write a short note to the plant, as the founder suggests, and talk about what it needed. Then try again in the right season.
  - Think: The old moong plant goes into the compost. Its atoms go back to the soil and air. Could some of them end up in the next moong plant?

## Challenges
### c1 · Be the Bee: design a pollen mover (25 min)
- **problem:** On a high balcony, few bees and butterflies visit. Your job is to design the best tool for picking up pollen from one flower and leaving it on another, without hurting the flowers.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own pollen mover. Draw it, make it, and test it the same way. Does it beat the others?
- **constraints:** It must not tear or bruise the petals.; It must be made from things you have at home.; Test every tool the same way: touch the pollen 3 times, then tap the tool 3 times on a sheet of dark paper.
- **general:** A soft paintbrush or a cotton bud, which gardeners use.; A fingertip, like Edmond's thumb.; Gently shaking a plant like tomato or chilli in the morning, so pollen falls inside each flower.
- **creative:** A fuzzy pipe cleaner bent into a bee shape. Bees carry pollen on the fluffy hairs all over their bodies.; An electric toothbrush, switched on and held against a tomato flower's stalk. The shaking makes pollen fall, the way big bees buzz tomato flowers. Some tomato growers use vibrating tools like this.; In 2020, scientists in Japan blew soap bubbles with pollen in them onto pear flowers. Some of the flowers grew fruit.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the flower with your child. 2. Find the pollen. 3. Touch it gently with a fingertip and look at the yellow dust that comes off.  
  *Parent says:* This yellow dust is pollen. A bee carries it from flower to flower without even trying. Let's see how hard it is for us.  
  *Child mode:* Find the yellow dust in the flower. That is pollen. Touch it gently. Look at your fingertip.
  - Note: Hibiscus pollen grains are big enough to see as separate specks with a magnifying glass. Lily pollen stains clothes orange, so take care.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Before testing, help your child draw or write three ideas for a pollen mover.  
  *Parent says:* Think like an engineer. What would carry pollen best? Something fluffy, something smooth, or something sticky?  
  *Child mode:* Think like an engineer! Draw three ideas for a pollen mover. Fluffy, smooth or sticky?
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Test each tool the same way: touch the pollen 3 times, then tap it 3 times on the dark paper. 2. Write the tool's name beside its spot. 3. Compare the spots: lots, some or few yellow specks.  
  *Parent says:* Same test for every tool. Which one picks up and drops off the most pollen?  
  *Child mode:* Test each tool. Touch the pollen 3 times. Tap the paper 3 times. Which leaves the most yellow specks?
  - Check: Why are bees so good at carrying pollen? → **Their bodies are covered in fluffy hairs that pollen sticks to**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the usual ideas and the creative ideas together. Then invent a new one and test it.  
  *Parent says:* Here is what gardeners usually do, and some surprising ideas. Can you invent something better?  
  *Child mode:* Read the usual ideas and the creative ones. Now invent your own pollen mover. Can it beat them?
  - Think: Bees, butterflies, birds and bats do this job for free, all over the world. What would it cost if people had to do it all by hand?
### c2 · The Never-Empty Balcony: plan a garden that is always flowering (25 min)
- **problem:** Plan 6 pots on a balcony or windowsill so that, from June to March, something is always flowering or fruiting. That is good for bees and butterflies, and it gives your family food.
- **your turn:** Now draw your own balcony plan. Mark the sowing date and the flowering months for each pot. Is there any month with no flowers? Fix it.
- **constraints:** Only 6 pots.; At least 2 pots must grow food.; Use the table of how long each plant takes, and show your sowing dates on a calendar.

### Round and Round: the plant life cycle (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (order) Put these stages of a moong plant's life in order, starting with the seed: flower, seedling, seed, pod, grown plant.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* A seed sprouts into a seedling. The seedling grows into a grown plant. The plant makes flowers, and each flower can grow into a pod full of new seeds.
2. (mcq) Why is it called a life CYCLE?  
   Options: Because plants grow fast, like a bicycle / Because the new seeds start the same stages again, so it goes round in a circle / Because it happens once and then stops / Because plants move in circles to follow the Sun  
   **Answer:** Because the new seeds start the same stages again, so it goes round in a circle. *Why:* The last stage, new seeds, is also the first stage of the next plant. So it goes round. Picking 'happens once and then stops' usually means thinking of one plant only. One plant does die, but its seeds carry on the cycle.
3. (fill) A fruit grows from a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['flower']. *Why:* After pollen reaches the middle of the flower, its petals fall and its base swells into the fruit.
4. (mcq) Which part of a flower swells up to become the fruit?  
   Options: The petals / The base in the very middle of the flower / The green stalk / The leaves around it  
   **Answer:** The base in the very middle of the flower. *Why:* The swollen base in the middle of the flower holds tiny seed-starts. It grows into the fruit, and the seed-starts become seeds. Picking 'the petals' is a common idea, because petals are the biggest, brightest part. But the petals fall off. The fruit grows from the middle.
5. (sort) To a scientist, which of these are fruits? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** tomato → Fruit (grew from a flower, holds seeds); bhindi → Fruit (grew from a flower, holds seeds); potato → Not a fruit; green chilli → Fruit (grew from a flower, holds seeds); carrot → Not a fruit; pea pod → Fruit (grew from a flower, holds seeds); palak leaf → Not a fruit. *Why:* A fruit grows from a flower and holds seeds. Tomatoes, bhindi, chillies and pea pods all have seeds inside. A potato is a swollen stem, a carrot is a root and palak is a leaf.
6. (truefalse) The green star on top of a tomato was part of the tomato's flower.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The green star is made of the sepals, the small green leaves that wrapped the flower bud. They stay on while the fruit grows.
7. (mcq) What is a seedling?  
   Options: A very young plant that has just come out of its seed / A seed that has not been planted / A plant that is about to flower / A dead plant  
   **Answer:** A very young plant that has just come out of its seed. *Why:* A seedling has just sprouted, with a tiny root, a short stem and its first leaves. Picking 'a seed that has not been planted' mixes up the seed and the seedling. The seedling comes after the seed wakes up.
8. (match) Match each stage to what happens in it.  
   **Answer:** Seed → waits, then sprouts when it gets water, warmth and air; Seedling → pushes up its first leaves; Grown plant → makes food in its leaves and gets big; Flower → gets pollen in its middle; Fruit → swells and holds new seeds. *Why:* Each stage has its own job. Together they make the whole circle.
9. (mcq) Where does a growing plant get most of its food?  
   Options: It eats soil through its roots / Its green leaves make food from air and water, using sunlight / From the seed, for its whole life / From rain only  
   **Answer:** Its green leaves make food from air and water, using sunlight. *Why:* Green leaves make sugar from carbon dioxide and water, using sunlight. This is called photosynthesis. Picking 'eats soil' is a very common idea. But the soil gives only water and small amounts of minerals. Picking 'from the seed' is true only for the first few days.
10. (fill) A plant that goes through its whole life cycle in one year or less, then dies, is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['annual']. *Why:* Moong, rice and marigold are annuals. Plants that live for many years are called perennials.
11. (truefalse) Every new plant must grow from a seed.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Potatoes grow from their eyes, onions from bulbs, money plants from cuttings and bananas from shoots at the base of the old plant.
12. (mcq) You tag a bhindi flower with a thread. Six days later, what will you most likely find on that spot?  
   Options: The same flower, still open / A young bhindi pod / A new leaf / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** A young bhindi pod. *Why:* A bhindi flower opens for one morning. Then its petals fall and its base grows into a pod, about a finger long after five or six days. Picking 'the same flower' means thinking flowers last a long time. Many last only a day. Some tagged flowers do fall off with no pod, which is normal.
13. (draw) Draw the life cycle of a moong plant as a circle with arrows. Label each stage.  
   **Answer:** A circle with five or six stages joined by arrows: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, pod, and back to seed.. *Why:* A good drawing joins the last stage back to the first one. That is what makes it a cycle.
14. (mcq) Why do many plants make sweet, juicy fruits?  
   Options: So that the plant has something to eat / To make the plant heavier / So that people can sell them / So that animals eat them and carry the seeds far away  
   **Answer:** So that animals eat them and carry the seeds far away. *Why:* Animals eat the fruit and drop or spit out the seeds somewhere else, where the new plant has more room. Picking 'so people can sell them' puts us at the centre. Plants made fruits for millions of years before there were people.
15. (write) Name one fruit you ate this week. Where were its seeds? What part of its flower could you see on it?  
   **Answer:** Any honest answer. For example: I ate a tomato. Its seeds were in jelly in the middle. The green star on top was left from its flower.. *Why:* Linking a real fruit to its seeds and its flower shows the child sees the life cycle in everyday food.
16. (draw) Draw a pumpkin flower from the back. Show the tiny pumpkin behind the petals.  
   **Answer:** A yellow flower with a small green ball where it joins its stalk.. *Why:* Mother pumpkin flowers have a tiny baby pumpkin behind them, even before pollen arrives.
### Watching Plants Change (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the chart of a tagged bhindi pod. On which day was it 6 centimetres long?  
   Options: Day 2 / Day 4 / Day 6 / Day 0  
   **Answer:** Day 4. *Why:* Find 6 on the side of the chart, then follow it across to the line. The line reaches 6 above Day 4. Picking Day 6 usually means reading the last day instead of the matching point.
2. (fill) The bhindi pod was 2 cm long on day 2 and 10 cm long on day 6. It grew ____ cm in those 4 days.  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* 10 take away 2 is 8. So it grew 8 cm in 4 days. That is about 2 cm a day.
3. (mcq) You tagged 5 flowers on a chilli plant. After a week, only 3 had become baby chillies. The other 2 fell off. What does this mean?  
   Options: The experiment went wrong / Not every flower becomes a fruit. Some fall, often when pollen does not reach them, or the plant is too hot, dry or tired. / The plant is dying / Chillies only make fruit at night  
   **Answer:** Not every flower becomes a fruit. Some fall, often when pollen does not reach them, or the plant is too hot, dry or tired.. *Why:* Plants make more flowers than they can turn into fruit. Some flowers do not get pollen, and some drop in very hot or dry weather. Picking 'the experiment went wrong' is a common worry. But a flower falling off is a real result, and worth writing down.
4. (mcq) Look at the chart of five pea pods. How many peas did Pod 4 have?  
   Options: 6 / 7 / 8 / 5  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Pod 4's bar reaches up to 8. Picking 6 usually means reading the bar next to it.
5. (fill) Altogether, the five pea pods had 6 + 7 + 5 + 8 + 6 = ____ peas.  
   **Answer:** ['32', 'thirty-two']. *Why:* 6 + 7 = 13. 13 + 5 = 18. 18 + 8 = 26. 26 + 6 = 32.
6. (mcq) A moong plant makes 20 pods. Each pod has about 10 seeds. About how many seeds does one plant give you?  
   Options: 30 / 200 / 20 / 10  
   **Answer:** 200. *Why:* 20 lots of 10 is 200. So one seed can give back about 200. Picking 30 means adding 20 and 10 instead of multiplying. There are 20 pods, and each one has 10 seeds.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the shortest life cycle to the longest: mango, moong, neelakurinji, rice.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Moong takes about 2 months, rice about 4 months, a mango tree 5 to 8 years before its first fruit, and neelakurinji 12 years to flower.
8. (mcq) In the 1600s, van Helmont grew a willow tree in a pot for five years. The tree gained about 75 kilograms. The soil lost only about 60 grams. Where did most of the tree come from?  
   Options: From the soil / From water and carbon dioxide in the air / From the pot / From fertiliser  
   **Answer:** From water and carbon dioxide in the air. *Why:* The soil hardly changed, so the tree did not come from soil. We now know it was built from water and carbon dioxide from the air, using sunlight. Picking 'from the soil' is what nearly everyone expects, and it is exactly what this test disproved.
9. (mcq) You put leaf circles in a glass of water with a pinch of baking soda. One glass is in bright light, one in a dark cupboard. Look at the chart. Why did the circles in the light float?  
   Options: The light made them lighter / The leaves made oxygen gas while making food, and the bubbles lifted them / The water got hot and boiled / The dark glass had more water  
   **Answer:** The leaves made oxygen gas while making food, and the bubbles lifted them. *Why:* In light, leaves make sugar and give out oxygen. Tiny bubbles of oxygen gather inside the leaf circles, and lift them up. In the dark, no food is made, so they stay down. Picking 'the light made them lighter' is a fair guess. But light has no weight to take away. It gives the leaf the energy to make gas.
10. (match) Match each plant to how a new plant can grow without a seed.  
   **Answer:** Potato → from the eyes on the potato; Onion → from the bulb's bottom in water or soil; Money plant → from a stem cutting in water; Banana → from a shoot at the base of the old plant; Patharchatta (Bryophyllum) → from baby plants on the edges of its leaves. *Why:* Each of these grows a copy of itself from one of its own parts. A new plant grown this way is a clone of its parent.
11. (truefalse) A moong plant dies after its pods ripen.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Moong is an annual. It finishes its life cycle in about two months. Its seeds carry the cycle on.
12. (mcq) Why do farmers in Réunion and in Kerala move vanilla pollen by hand?  
   Options: It makes the pods sweeter / Hand-moving is a religious custom / The insect that carries vanilla pollen in Mexico does not live there / Vanilla flowers have no pollen  
   **Answer:** The insect that carries vanilla pollen in Mexico does not live there. *Why:* In Mexico, certain bees can push past the little flap inside the vanilla flower. Those bees do not live in other countries. So people do the bees' job with a thin stick and a thumb. Picking 'no pollen' misses the point of the story: the pollen was always there, hidden behind the flap.
13. (write) A green chilli plant in a pot makes lots of flowers, but no chillies. Give two reasons why that might happen, and one thing to try.  
   **Answer:** For example: no insects visit the balcony, so pollen does not reach the flowers. Or it is too hot, or the pot dried out. I could gently shake the flowers in the morning, or move pollen with a soft brush, and water it regularly.. *Why:* A flower needs pollen to become a fruit, and a plant drops flowers when it is stressed by heat or thirst.
14. (mcq) Which of these plants flower only once in their whole life, and then die?  
   Options: Mango / Neem / The bamboo in Mizoram that flowers after about 48 years / Rose  
   **Answer:** The bamboo in Mizoram that flowers after about 48 years. *Why:* That bamboo grows for about 48 years, flowers once, makes its seeds and dies. Mango, neem and rose are perennials that flower again and again. Picking mango may come from knowing mango trees take years to fruit. But once they start, they fruit every year.
15. (write) Write the life cycle of a mango tree as a short story, in 5 sentences. Start with a ripe mango falling to the ground.  
   **Answer:** For example: A ripe mango fell to the ground. A monkey ate it and dropped the stone far away. In the rains the stone sprouted into a seedling with red leaves. After six years it was a big tree and made tiny flowers. The flowers turned into mangoes, with new stones inside.. *Why:* Starting with the fruit shows the child understands that a cycle can start at any stage.
16. (draw) Draw a tomato and label the green star. Then draw the flower it came from.  
   **Answer:** A tomato with its green star of sepals on top, and a small yellow star-shaped flower with green sepals behind its petals.. *Why:* The sepals that wrapped the flower bud stay on as the tomato grows.
### Life Cycle Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) In 1938, scientists flashed a light in the middle of the night on plants that flower when nights are long. The plants did not flower. What does this tell us?  
   Options: The plants were scared / The plants were measuring the length of the dark night, and the light broke it into two short nights / The light hurt the plants / Plants cannot see light  
   **Answer:** The plants were measuring the length of the dark night, and the light broke it into two short nights. *Why:* The plants count how long the dark lasts. One flash cut the long night into two short ones, so the plants 'thought' the nights were short. Picking 'the light hurt the plants' is a careful guess. But the plants kept growing healthy leaves. They just did not flower.
2. (mcq) A mango tree with very sweet mangoes is growing in your garden. You plant one of its mango stones. Will the new tree have exactly the same sweet mangoes?  
   Options: Yes, always / Not always, because the seed got some of its instructions from the pollen of another tree / No, mango stones never grow / Only if you water it with sugar  
   **Answer:** Not always, because the seed got some of its instructions from the pollen of another tree. *Why:* Each seed gets instructions from both parents, so it can be a little different. That is why farmers grow their best mangoes by joining a twig of the sweet tree onto a young tree. This makes a copy. Picking 'yes, always' is a natural guess, since a mango seed always grows a mango tree. But the mangoes on it can be different.
3. (truefalse) Scientists know exactly how bamboo counts 48 years before flowering.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is an open question. Nobody has found the bamboo's clock.
4. (mcq) When a big tree burns, only a small heap of ash is left. Where did the rest of the tree go?  
   Options: Back into the air, as carbon dioxide and water vapour / It vanished completely / Down into the soil / It all turned into the ash  
   **Answer:** Back into the air, as carbon dioxide and water vapour. *Why:* Burning turns the tree's carbon and water back into gases. The tree was built mostly from air and water, and fire sends it back there. The ash is the small part that came from the soil. Picking 'vanished completely' usually means thinking matter can disappear. It cannot. It changes form.
5. (mcq) Chillies are hot to mice but not to birds. How does this help the chilli plant?  
   Options: Birds like spicy food / Mice spread the seeds better / It does not help at all / Mice chew up the seeds, but birds swallow them whole and drop them far away  
   **Answer:** Mice chew up the seeds, but birds swallow them whole and drop them far away. *Why:* In 2001, scientists found that the heat keeps away mammals, which chew the seeds. Birds cannot feel it, eat the chillies and carry the seeds far away. Picking 'birds like spicy food' misses the point. Birds do not taste the burn at all.
6. (write) Moong is an annual: it lives one season, makes seeds and dies. A mango tree lives for a hundred years. Which is the better way to live? Give one good reason for each.  
   **Answer:** A balanced answer. For example: moong is quick, so it can make lots of seeds before the dry season comes. A mango tree takes years to start, but then it gives fruit every year and grows big and strong.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Both ways work, in different places and seasons.
7. (fill) Neelakurinji bloomed near Munnar in 2018. It blooms every 12 years. Its next two blooms should be in ____ and ____.  
   **Answer:** ['2030 and 2042', '2030, 2042']. *Why:* 2018 + 12 = 2030. 2030 + 12 = 2042.
8. (mcq) In the 1950s, a disease wiped out most of the world's favourite banana, the Gros Michel. Why did it spread through so many farms?  
   Options: There was too much rain / Bananas are always weak / All the plants were copies of each other, so they all had the same weakness / The bananas were too ripe  
   **Answer:** All the plants were copies of each other, so they all had the same weakness. *Why:* Bananas are grown from shoots, so the plants are clones. A disease that can harm one can harm them all. Picking 'too much rain' is a sensible guess about plant diseases. But the real problem was that there was no difference between the plants.
9. (mcq) Which of these plants has no flowers and no seeds at all?  
   Options: A cactus / A fern / A mango tree / Rice  
   **Answer:** A fern. *Why:* Ferns spread by spores, a fine dust made under their leaves. Ferns are older than flowering plants. Picking cactus is a common idea, because cactus flowers are rare to see. But cactuses do have flowers and fruits.
10. (write) Your food comes from plants. Plants are built mostly from air. Explain, step by step, how a bit of the air could end up in your body.  
   **Answer:** A wheat leaf takes in carbon dioxide from the air. It uses sunlight to make sugar, and stores some as starch in the wheat grain. The grain is ground into atta and made into a roti. I eat the roti, and my body uses the carbon in it to build itself.. *Why:* This follows a carbon atom through photosynthesis and food. It is a real path that carbon atoms take every day.
11. (mcq) In the story, Edmond saw a pumpkin flower that had been dusted with pollen grow into a pumpkin, and one left alone shrivel. What was the one thing that was different between them?  
   Options: Pollen reached the middle of one flower and not the other / One got more sunlight / One was older / One was on a different plant  
   **Answer:** Pollen reached the middle of one flower and not the other. *Why:* Both were mother flowers on the same patch. Only one had pollen pressed into its middle. That was the one thing changed, like a fair test. Picking 'more sunlight' means looking for a difference the story does not show.
12. (mcq) In the Be Edmond experiment, you cover one pumpkin flower with a bag and add no pollen. It shrivels. You cover another with a bag and add pollen by hand. It grows. Why do you cover BOTH with bags?  
   Options: To keep them warm / To hide them from people / Because bags make fruits grow / So that the bag is the same for both, and only the pollen is different  
   **Answer:** So that the bag is the same for both, and only the pollen is different. *Why:* In a fair test, only one thing changes. If only one flower had a bag, you could not tell whether the bag or the pollen made the difference. Picking 'bags make fruits grow' is the opposite of what the bagged, unpollinated flower shows.
13. (write) Is a seed a new plant, or the old plant carrying on? Give one reason for each answer.  
   **Answer:** A balanced answer. For example: it is new, because it is a separate living thing and may be a bit different from its parent. It is the old plant carrying on, because it carries copied instructions and was fed with its parent's food.. *Why:* Scientists and thinkers answer this in different ways. What counts is a reason for each side.
14. (mcq) In 1982, small thale cress plants on a space station flowered and made seeds. Why was that important?  
   Options: It showed plants grow better in space / It showed seeds can fly / It showed a plant can go round its whole life cycle in space / It showed plants do not need water  
   **Answer:** It showed a plant can go round its whole life cycle in space. *Why:* People living far from Earth will need plants that can make new seeds there, again and again. Picking 'plants grow better in space' is not what was found. Growing plants in space is hard.
15. (write) Charles Darwin called the fast spread of flowering plants 'an abominable mystery'. Give two ideas scientists have for why flowering plants spread so fast.  
   **Answer:** Any two of these. Flowers teamed up with insects and birds to move pollen. Flowering plants finish their life cycles quickly. Fruits let animals carry seeds far away. Changes in the climate helped them.. *Why:* Scientists still argue about this. A good answer gives two different ideas.
16. (draw) Draw the life cycle of a moong plant inside a bigger circle that shows where its atoms come from and go to: air, water, soil, and decomposers.  
   **Answer:** An inner circle of seed, seedling, plant, flower, pod and seed. Around it, arrows from the air and the soil into the plant, and arrows from the dead plant through decomposers back to the air and soil.. *Why:* A plant's life cycle sits inside a bigger cycle of atoms going round through air, soil and living things.

13. (mcq) In the Floating Leaves experiment, leaf circles floated up in bright light but stayed down in the dark. What made them float?  
   Options: Bubbles of oxygen the leaf made using light / Hot water / The soap / Wind  
   **Answer:** Bubbles of oxygen the leaf made using light. *Why:* In light, leaves make sugar and give out oxygen, and the bubbles lift the circles. Both glasses had the same soap, so the soap cannot explain the difference. Only the light was different.
14. (mcq) What happens to a plant's body after it dies?  
   Options: It stays there for ever / It vanishes / It turns into a seed / Decomposers break it down, and its atoms go back to the soil and air for new life  
   **Answer:** Decomposers break it down, and its atoms go back to the soil and air for new life. *Why:* Fungi, bacteria and small animals eat the dead plant. Its carbon goes back into the air and its minerals into the soil, where new plants use them. Picking 'it vanishes' usually means thinking matter can disappear. It cannot. It changes form.
15. (mcq) Do scientists know how bamboo counts about 48 years before it flowers?  
   Options: Yes, it counts the monsoons / Bamboo never flowers / No, it is still a mystery / Yes, it has a clock in its roots  
   **Answer:** No, it is still a mystery. *Why:* Nobody knows how bamboo keeps time for so long. There are ideas, but none has been proved. Picking 'it counts the monsoons' is a clever guess that some people have made. But nobody has shown it is true.
16. (mcq) Who found the way to make vanilla flowers grow pods outside Mexico, using a thin stick and his thumb?  
   Options: Isaac Newton / Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old boy, in 1841 / Charles Darwin / Gregor Mendel  
   **Answer:** Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old boy, in 1841. *Why:* Edmond Albius found the method in 1841, on the island now called Réunion. It is still used today. Picking Darwin is a common guess, because he studied plants and flowers. But he did not find this.

## Applications
- Farmers plan sowing and harvest by each crop's life cycle: rice in the monsoon, wheat and mustard in winter.
- Farmers save seeds from one harvest to sow the next year, so their crops go round the cycle again.
- Seed companies print 'days to harvest' on seed packets, so gardeners know how long each life cycle takes.
- Vanilla farmers in Madagascar, Réunion, Kerala and Karnataka pollinate every flower by hand, using Edmond Albius's method.
- Pumpkin, cucumber and gourd growers on terraces move pollen by hand when bees are few.
- Fruit farmers grow mango, guava and lemon trees from grafts and cuttings, so the fruit is exactly like the parent's.
- Potatoes, sugarcane, bananas and roses are grown from pieces of the plant instead of seeds.
- Flower growers use lights at night to stop or start flowering, because many plants measure the length of the night.
- Plant breeders cross plants by moving pollen by hand, to make new kinds of rice, wheat and vegetables.
- Compost pits turn dead plants and kitchen peels back into rich soil for the next crop.
- Seed banks keep seeds of thousands of kinds of plant, so their life cycles can be started again if a crop is lost.
- Citizen-science projects like SeasonWatch ask children to record when trees flower and fruit, to track how the climate is changing.
- Space agencies grow plants from seed to seed on space stations, to plan farms on the Moon and Mars.

## Open questions
- **How does bamboo count about 48 years before it flowers, and how do clumps far apart stay in step?** Nobody has found a clock that could count so long inside a plant. There are ideas, but none has been proved.
- **Why did flowering plants spread so fast and take over the land?** Darwin called it 'an abominable mystery' in 1879. Scientists still argue about how much was due to insects, fast growth, fruits or the climate.
- **Exactly how does a plant decide that it is big enough to flower?** Scientists know many of the signals: day length, cold, age and sugar. How the plant puts them all together is still being worked out.
- **How long can a single plant live?** Some trees are thousands of years old. Some plants, like grasses and aspen trees, spread as copies of themselves for so long that nobody knows where one plant ends.
- **What is the best way to grow plants through their whole life cycle on the Moon or Mars?** Plants have gone from seed to seed in space stations. But Moon and Mars soil is very different, and nobody has grown a crop there yet.
- **How will a warmer world change when plants flower?** Many plants now flower earlier than they used to. If they flower before their insects wake up, the flowers may make less fruit. How much this will happen, and where, is still being studied.
- **Is a seed the same plant as its parent, or a new one?** Biologists count it as a new plant, because it has a new mix of instructions. Some thinkers say it is the same life carrying on. It depends what you mean by 'the same'.

## Connected maths
- In the Kitchen to Kitchen project, a moong plant made 20 pods. Each pod had about 10 seeds. About how many seeds did one plant give back? **About 200 seeds.**. There are 20 pods, and each holds about 10 seeds. That is 20 groups of 10. 20 × 10 = 200. So one seed planted gave back about 200 seeds. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- You sowed moong on 1 July. The first flower opened on 5 August. How many days did the plant take to flower? **35 days.**. July has 31 days. From 1 July to 31 July is 30 days. Then 1 August to 5 August is 5 more days. 30 + 5 = 35 days. That is 5 weeks. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- Look at the chart of peas counted in five pea pods. How many peas were there altogether? Which pod had the most? **32 peas altogether. Pod 4 had the most, with 8.**. Add the bars: 6 + 7 + 5 + 8 + 6. 6 + 7 = 13. 13 + 5 = 18. 18 + 8 = 26. 26 + 6 = 32. The tallest bar is Pod 4, at 8. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Look at the line graph of a bhindi pod. It was 2 cm long on day 2 and 10 cm long on day 6. How much did it grow in those 4 days? About how much is that each day? **8 cm in 4 days. That is about 2 cm each day.**. 10 take away 2 is 8. So it grew 8 cm. It took 4 days, from day 2 to day 6. 8 shared between 4 days is 2. So it grew about 2 cm each day. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- Neelakurinji flowered near Munnar in 2018. It flowers every 12 years. Write the next three years it should flower. **2030, 2042 and 2054.**. Add 12 each time. 2018 + 12 = 2030. 2030 + 12 = 2042. 2042 + 12 = 2054. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Look at the chart from the Floating Leaves experiment. In the light, 9 out of 10 leaf circles were floating after 20 minutes. What fraction is that? How many were still at the bottom? **9 out of 10, which is nine tenths. 1 circle was still at the bottom.**. There were 10 circles in the glass. 9 were floating. So the fraction floating is 9 out of 10, written 9/10, or nine tenths. 10 take away 9 is 1, so 1 was still at the bottom. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
