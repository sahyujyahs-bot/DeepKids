# A plant's life cycle: how a seed grows into a plant that makes new seeds
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.plant-life-cycle.L3`

**Summary.** Every fruit in your kitchen was once a flower, and every seed inside it can grow into a new plant. A life cycle is all the stages of a living thing's life, until they make young of their own. A flowering plant goes from seed to seedling to grown plant. Then they flower, each flower can grow into a fruit, and the fruit holds new seeds. Your child opens kitchen fruits to find the seeds and the flower's leftovers, and can grow moong from seed to seed in two to three months. Then the unit follows the idea further: plants built mostly from air and water, plants that measure the length of the night, and bamboo that flowers only once, after about 48 years.

**Intro.** *Look at a tomato, or a brinjal. See the little green star, or the green cap, at the top? Where do you think it came from? Here is a clue. Not long ago, this whole tomato was a small yellow flower.* Every fruit in your kitchen was once a flower. Every seed inside it can grow into a plant that makes flowers again. Put a moong seed in a pot today. In two to three months, if they get sun and water, they will give you back a whole handful of moong. Which part of a flower turns into the fruit? And why does the plant end up exactly where they started, as seeds?

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Life cycle**: All the stages a living thing goes through, from the start of their life until they make young ones of their own. Then the stages start again. *e.g.* Moong: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, pod, new seeds.
- **Stage**: One step or part of a life cycle. *e.g.* Being a seedling is one stage. Flowering is another stage.
- **Seed**: A baby plant packed in a coat, with their own store of food. *e.g.* A moong seed, a rajma bean and the big seed inside a mango stone are all seeds.
- **Seedling**: A very young plant that has just come out of their seed. They have a tiny root, a short stem and their first leaves. *e.g.* The small green shoots in a tray of sprouted methi are seedlings.
- **Seed leaves**: The first leaves, which were already folded up inside the seed. They are often thick and round, and hold food. *e.g.* The two round leaves at the top of a new bean plant.
- **True leaves**: The leaves that grow after the seed leaves. They have the plant's own leaf shape, and they make food from sunlight. *e.g.* A tomato seedling's true leaves are jagged and smell of tomato.
- **Grown plant**: A plant that is big enough and old enough to make flowers. Scientists call this a mature plant. *e.g.* A moong plant starts to flower when they are about one month old.
- **Bud**: A flower, or a leaf, before it opens. It is folded up tight. *e.g.* A rose bud, or the tight green tip at the end of a stem.
- **Flower**: The part of a plant that makes seeds. Many flowers are bright and sweet-smelling. That brings insects and birds to visit them. *e.g.* A hibiscus, a marigold, and the small yellow flowers on a moong plant.
- **Petals**: The soft, often coloured parts around the middle of a flower. *e.g.* A marigold has many orange petals. A hibiscus has five.
- **Sepals**: The small green leaf-like parts that wrap a flower bud before it opens. On many fruits they stay on, like a green star or a cap. *e.g.* The green cap on a brinjal and the green star on a tomato are sepals.
- **Pollen**: A fine dust made by flowers, usually yellow. It must reach the middle of a flower before the flower can make seeds. *e.g.* The yellow dust left on your finger after touching the middle of a hibiscus.
- **Pollination**: Pollen being carried to the sticky tip in the middle of a flower. Wind, insects, birds, bats or people can carry it. Only then can most flowers grow a fruit with seeds. *e.g.* A bee covered in yellow dust flying from one pumpkin flower to another.
- **Fruit**: The part of a plant that grows from a flower and holds the seeds. To a scientist, a tomato, a bhindi and a chilli are all fruits. *e.g.* A mango, a pea pod, a cucumber and a bhindi are all fruits.
- **Pod**: A long fruit that holds a row of seeds. It often splits open when it is ripe. *e.g.* Pea pods, moong pods and drumsticks.
- **Ripe**: Fully grown and ready. A ripe fruit often changes colour and gets soft or sweet. Its seeds are ready to grow. *e.g.* A green mango turns yellow and sweet when it is ripe.
- **Annual**: A plant that goes through their whole life cycle in one year or less, then dies. *e.g.* Moong, rice, wheat and marigold are annuals.
- **Perennial**: A plant that lives for many years and can flower again and again. *e.g.* Mango, neem, coconut, rose and tulsi are perennials.
- **Cutting**: A piece of a plant, like a stem or a leaf, that can grow roots and become a new plant without a seed. *e.g.* A money plant stem in a bottle of water grows roots.
- **Clone**: A living thing that is an exact copy of their parent. Plants grown from cuttings are clones. *e.g.* All the banana plants of one kind on a farm are copies, grown from shoots of the same parent plants.
- **Photosynthesis**: How green leaves make food. They take in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the roots. They use the energy of sunlight to turn these into sugar, and they give out oxygen. *e.g.* A tulsi plant in the sun is making sugar right now.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas in the air, which you breathe out. Plants take it in and build their bodies from it. *e.g.* Every breath you breathe out has carbon dioxide in it.
- **Decomposer**: A living thing that breaks down dead plants and animals. It puts their minerals back into the soil and their carbon back into the air. *e.g.* Mushrooms, earthworms and tiny bacteria in a compost pit.

## Explanations
### What a life cycle is · *defined*
**Definition:** A life cycle is all the stages a living thing goes through, from the start of their life until they make young ones. For a flowering plant the stages are: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, fruit, and new seeds.

It is called a cycle because it goes round in a circle. The last stage, new seeds, is also the first stage of the next plant. So the circle never really ends. A moong seed grows into a moong plant. The plant makes flowers. The flowers turn into pods. The pods hold new moong seeds, and each one can start again. You can start drawing the circle at any stage. A seed is not more of a 'beginning' than a flower is.
- e.g. Moong: seed, seedling, grown plant, yellow flower, green pod, black pod full of new seeds.
- e.g. Mango: mango stone, seedling, young tree, mango flowers, mangoes, new mango stones.
- e.g. Marigold: seed, seedling, plant, orange flower, dry flower head full of thin black seeds.
### From seed to seedling · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed wakes up when they get water, warmth and air. This is called germination. Their root comes out first and grows down. Then their shoot grows up into the light, and the plant becomes a seedling.

The unit 'Seeds and how they sprout' tells this part of the story in detail. Here is the short version. At first, the seedling lives on the food packed inside the seed. Many seedlings first show two round seed leaves, which were folded up inside the seed. Soon the true leaves grow above them. True leaves have the plant's own leaf shape. Once they open, the seedling can make their own food from sunlight. The seed leaves are no longer needed. They shrink, go yellow and fall off.
- e.g. A bean seedling has two fat seed leaves first, then pointed true leaves.
- e.g. A rice or maize seedling comes up as one thin green spike.
### Growing up: leaves make food from sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** A growing plant makes their own food in their green leaves. This is called photosynthesis. Leaves take in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the roots. Using the energy of sunlight, they turn them into sugar. They give out oxygen.

The plant uses the sugar in two ways. They use some for energy, the way your body uses food. They also use sugar to build new stems, leaves and roots. So a plant grows mainly by making more of themselves out of air and water. A plant grows slowly at first, then fast, then slowly again once they are grown. The chart shows a made-up example for a moong plant in a pot. They grow fastest in weeks 3 to 5. After about week 6 they put their effort into flowers and pods instead of getting taller.
- e.g. A plant kept in a dark cupboard grows pale, thin and floppy, then dies. They cannot make food.
- e.g. Leaves turn to face the window. They are reaching for light.
### When a plant is ready to flower · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant flowers when they are big enough, and when the season is right. Many plants find out the season by measuring how long the nights are.

A young plant first grows leaves and roots. When they have grown enough, the tips of some stems stop making leaves and make flower buds instead. Many plants wait for the right season too. In 1920, two American scientists, Wightman Garner and Harry Allard, found that some tobacco and soybean plants flower only when the days get short. In 1938, Karl Hamner and James Bonner showed that what the plant really measures is the length of the night. Flash a light in the middle of a long night, and those plants do not flower. The leaves do the measuring. Then they send a message up the stem to the buds. A Russian scientist, Mikhail Chailakhyan, guessed in 1936 that such a message must exist. It took until 2007 for scientists to find it. It is a protein that travels from the leaves to the bud.
- e.g. Rice in many parts of India flowers when the nights get longer after the monsoon.
- e.g. Some plants, like wheat in cold countries, wait until they have felt a cold winter.
- e.g. Many kinds of moong flower about 30 to 40 days after sowing in warm weather, whatever the day length.
### A flower is where the fruit will grow · *defined*
**Definition:** A flower is the part of a plant that makes seeds. In its middle is a swollen base with tiny seed-starts inside. When pollen reaches the sticky tip above that base, the base can grow into a fruit, and the seed-starts can grow into seeds.

Look at a flower from the outside in. First come the green sepals, which wrapped the bud. Then come the petals, often bright and scented, which bring in visitors. Next come thin stalks with yellow dust on their tips. The stalks are called stamens, and the dust is pollen. In the very middle is a part shaped a little like a bottle. Its base is the ovary. Scientists call the tiny seed-starts inside it ovules. Pollen must be carried to the sticky tip of the bottle, which is called the stigma. This is called pollination. Insects, birds, bats, wind or people can carry it. Some flowers, like moong, peas and tomato, can pollinate themselves. After that, the petals fall off and the base starts to swell. That swelling base is the baby fruit. You will learn much more about pollination in the Class 4 unit on flowers.
- e.g. In a pumpkin flower, you can see the baby pumpkin as a little green ball behind the petals.
- e.g. A bhindi flower opens for just one morning. Five days later, a finger-long pod is in its place.
### A fruit is a flower's base grown big, holding seeds · *defined*
**Definition:** To a scientist, a fruit is the part that grows from a flower's base and holds the seeds. So tomatoes, bhindi, brinjal, cucumber, chillies and pea pods are all fruits.

In the kitchen, we call sweet ones fruits and the others vegetables. Botanists, the scientists who study plants, use a different rule. If it grew from a flower and holds seeds, it is a fruit. You can often see what is left of the flower on a fruit. The green star on top of a tomato and the green cap on a brinjal are the old sepals. The little crown at the end of a pomegranate or a guava is the flower's leftovers too. Each seed inside came from one tiny seed-start in the flower. Some fruits have one seed, like a mango. Others have hundreds, like a papaya.
- e.g. A pea pod is a fruit. The peas are its seeds.
- e.g. A pomegranate's crown is the remains of its red flower.
- e.g. A banana's dark tip is where its flower was.
### Why plants make fruits: sending seeds away · *defined*
**Definition:** A fruit protects the seeds and helps them travel away from the parent plant. Many fruits are sweet so that animals eat them and drop the seeds somewhere else.

A seed that falls right under their parent has to compete with them for light and water. A seed carried far away has a better chance. So plants have many ways of sending their seeds away. Sweet fruits are eaten by birds, bats, monkeys and people. The seeds pass through, or get spat out, far away. Pods can burst and fling their seeds. Some fruits have wings or fluff and fly on the wind. Coconuts float across the sea. Chillies use their hot taste to choose which animals carry their seeds. In 2001, two American scientists, Joshua Tewksbury and Gary Nabhan, found that the hot taste of chillies keeps away mice and other mammals. Mammals chew seeds up. Birds swallow seeds whole and drop them far away. The hot taste does not burn birds at all. You will learn more about how seeds travel in Class 4.
- e.g. A koel eats a ripe banyan fig and drops the seeds on a far-off wall.
- e.g. Moong and pea pods dry, twist and pop, flinging their seeds out.
- e.g. Grass seeds stick to socks and shoes, and get carried home after a walk.
### Short and long life cycles · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants finish their whole life cycle in a few weeks or months, then die. They are called annuals. Others live for many years and flower again and again. They are called perennials.

Moong goes from seed to seed in two to three months. Rice takes about four months. A mango tree grown from a stone usually takes five to eight years before their first mangoes, and then they fruit every year for a long time. Some plants wait for years, flower just once, and then die. The neelakurinji shrub, which grows in the hills of Kerala and Tamil Nadu, flowers all together only once every 12 years. Their last big bloom near Munnar was in 2018. One kind of bamboo in Mizoram waits about 48 years. Then all the plants of that kind flower together, make their seeds, and die. The talipot palm can wait more than 30 years. Then they make one of the biggest bunches of flowers of any plant, and die.
- e.g. Short: moong, methi, marigold and mustard, from seed to seed in a few months.
- e.g. Long: mango, neem and coconut, which flower for years and years.
- e.g. Once in a long life: neelakurinji every 12 years, and bamboo after about 48 years.
### New plants without seeds · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants can also grow new plants from a piece of themselves, such as a stem, a leaf, a root or a bulb. The new plant is an exact copy of their parent, called a clone.

A potato is a swollen underground stem. Each 'eye' on it can sprout a new potato plant. An onion is a bulb, made of thick leaves wrapped round a short stem. Put its bottom in water and it grows new roots and green leaves. A money plant stem grows roots in a bottle of water. Sugarcane is planted as pieces of stem. A leaf of the patharchatta plant, also called Bryophyllum, laid on damp soil grows tiny new plants from the notches along its edges. The bananas we eat have no proper seeds. Farmers grow new banana plants from shoots at the base of the old one. Copies are quick and easy. But they are risky. In the 1950s, a disease wiped out most of the world's favourite banana, the Gros Michel, because every plant was a copy with the same weakness. A new form of that disease now threatens today's banana, the Cavendish.
- e.g. An old potato in a dark basket grows white shoots from its eyes.
- e.g. A rose cutting pushed into damp soil can grow into a new rose bush.
- e.g. Grass spreads by stems that creep along the ground.
### Deep down: a plant is built from air · *defined*
**Definition:** Most of a plant's body is made from carbon dioxide taken from the air, and water. Only a very small part comes from the soil.

This surprises almost everyone, including grown-ups. In the 1600s, a Flemish scientist, Jan Baptist van Helmont, tried a test. He put a small willow tree in a big pot of dried, weighed soil. For five years he gave the tree only rainwater. The tree grew from about 2 kilograms to about 77 kilograms. The soil lost only about 60 grams. He thought the tree was made of water. He was partly right. We now know most of their dry wood is made of carbon atoms taken from carbon dioxide in the air. In the 1950s, Melvin Calvin and his team in California followed carbon atoms from the air into the sugar a plant made. They used a special kind of carbon they could track. A big neem tree is mostly air and water, put together with the energy of sunlight. The soil gives the plant small amounts of important things, called minerals, like iron and nitrogen.
- e.g. When wood burns, it turns back into carbon dioxide and water vapour, and only a little ash is left. The ash is the part that came from the soil.
- e.g. A pot plant can grow for years without the pot getting much emptier of soil.
### Death feeds the next life · *defined*
**Definition:** When a plant dies, decomposers break them down. Their atoms go back into the soil and the air, and new plants are built from them.

Fungi like mushrooms, tiny bacteria, earthworms, termites and woodlice all feed on dead leaves and stems. As they eat, they break the plant into smaller and smaller pieces. Some of their carbon goes back into the air as carbon dioxide. Their minerals go back into the soil. The next seeds use that air and soil to grow. So the same atoms go round and round through plants, animals, soil and air. The life cycle of a plant sits inside a much bigger cycle of atoms. The carbon in your body was once in plants too, because all your food comes from plants, or from animals that ate plants.
- e.g. A pile of fallen leaves under a tree is gone by the next year. Decomposers ate it.
- e.g. A compost pit turns kitchen peels into dark, crumbly soil in a few months.
### Every seed carries a copy of the plant's instructions · *defined*
**Definition:** Every cell of a plant holds a long set of instructions for building that plant. The instructions are written in a chemical called DNA. Each seed gets a copy from their parents.

That is why a moong seed always grows into moong, never into mango. The instructions are copied again and again, from plant to seed to plant, all the way round the life cycle. When pollen from one plant reaches the flower of another, the seed gets some instructions from each parent. So seeds are a little different from each other, just as brothers and sisters are. Between 1856 and 1863, a monk called Gregor Mendel grew thousands of pea plants in his monastery garden in Brno, now in the Czech Republic. He chose peas because they go round their life cycle in one season. He found patterns in how things like seed colour were passed on. When he published his work in 1866, almost nobody noticed. It was rediscovered in 1900, after he had died. Today he is called the father of genetics, the science of how living things pass on their features.
- e.g. Mango seeds from one sweet tree can grow into trees with quite different mangoes. So farmers join a twig of their best tree onto a young tree. This is called grafting, and the twig grows mangoes just like its parent's.
- e.g. Every rice plant in a field grew from instructions copied from earlier rice plants, going back thousands of years.
### Why did flowering plants take over the world? · *debated*
The first land plants, rather like mosses, appeared over 450 million years ago. Ferns and conifers came long before flowers. The first flowering plants appear in rocks from about 130 million years ago, in the time of the dinosaurs. Then they spread very fast. Today, almost 9 out of every 10 kinds of land plant have flowers. In 1879, Charles Darwin wrote to his friend Joseph Hooker that this sudden spread was 'an abominable mystery'. Scientists still argue about it.
- e.g. Ferns and mosses have no flowers. They spread by spores, a fine dust, instead of seeds.
- e.g. Pine trees make seeds in cones, not in flowers or fruits.
- **Many scientists who study insects and flowers:** Flowers changed together with the insects, birds and bats that visit them. Pollen carried from one plant to another made new kinds of plant appear faster.
- **Scientists who study how plants grow:** Flowering plants grow fast and finish their life cycles quickly, so they could take over open land before slower plants.
- **Other scientists:** Fruits let animals carry seeds far and wide. Some also point to changes in the climate and the land at the time. Most think several of these worked together.
### How does bamboo count the years? · *idea*
Melocanna bamboo in Mizoram flowers about every 48 years. All the clumps across hundreds of kilometres flower together, even clumps planted far apart. Then they make huge numbers of fruits and die. In 1958 and again from 2006 to 2008, the flowering fed so many rats that they multiplied and then ate the farmers' crops. People there call this mautam, and it caused famine. How the bamboo keeps time for so long, and stays in step, is not known. Nobody has found a 48-year clock inside a plant.
- e.g. Neelakurinji in the Nilgiri and Munnar hills flowers together every 12 years.
- e.g. Bamboo of one kind planted in another country can still flower at about the same time as their family back home.
- **Daniel Janzen, an American scientist, in 1976:** Flowering all together makes more seeds than the rats and birds can eat, so some survive. Plants that flowered out of step had their few seeds eaten, so the habit spread.
- **Other scientists:** The plants may count something inside them, like rounds of growth, rather than years. Or the weather may nudge them. Nobody has proved how it works.
### A life cycle in space · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants can go through their whole life cycle in space. In 1982, small thale cress plants grown on the Soviet space station Salyut 7 flowered and made seeds. They were the first plants known to go from seed to seed in space.

In space, there is no 'down' to guide the roots, so astronauts and scientists must help plants grow. Since then, wheat, peas, lettuce, zinnia flowers and others have been grown on space stations. Space agencies study this because people living on the Moon or Mars one day will need plants that can keep going round their life cycle there. They will need them for food, and to turn the carbon dioxide that astronauts breathe out back into oxygen.
- e.g. In 2016, an astronaut on the International Space Station grew orange zinnia flowers.
- e.g. Astronauts on the International Space Station have eaten lettuce they grew there.

## Mostly agreed
- Flowering plants go through a life cycle: seed, seedling, grown plant, flower, fruit and new seeds. *How we know:* You can watch it in a pot of moong in two to three months. Farmers have watched it in every field for thousands of years.
- A fruit grows from the base of a flower, and holds the seeds. *How we know:* Tag a flower with a thread and watch it every day. The petals fall and the base swells into the fruit. The flower's leftovers often stay on the fruit.
- Most flowers make fruit only after pollen reaches the middle of the flower. *How we know:* Cover a pumpkin flower with a paper bag so no insect can reach it, and it shrivels. Add pollen by hand, and it grows. Gardeners and vanilla farmers do this every day.
- A plant builds their body mostly from carbon dioxide and water, using sunlight. *How we know:* Van Helmont's willow grew about 75 kilograms while the soil lost about 60 grams. In the 1950s, Melvin Calvin's team followed carbon from the air into a plant's sugar.
- Many plants decide when to flower by measuring the length of the night. *How we know:* Experiments since 1920 show that flashing a light in the middle of the night can stop some plants from flowering, or make others flower.

## How we found out
**Rudolf Jakob Camerarius, 1694 (his letter on the sex of plants, published in Tübingen).** For a long time, most scholars in Europe did not believe that a flower needs pollen to make seeds. Farmers in ancient Iraq already knew that a date palm gives good dates only if pollen from a male tree reaches their flowers. But scholars did not think this was true of all plants. In Tübingen, in Germany, a doctor and botanist named Rudolf Jakob Camerarius tested it. He grew plants such as mulberry, maize and a small weed called annual mercury. He kept pollen away from the flowers that make seeds. A female mulberry tree with no male tree nearby made fruit, but their seeds were empty. Plants that got no pollen made no good seeds. Plants that got pollen did. In 1694 he wrote that pollen must reach a flower before it can make seeds. Other botanists argued against him for more than 30 years. Later tests, like a palm pollinated by hand in Berlin in 1749, showed he was right.  
*What people believed before:* That plants make seeds without pollen, and have nothing like male and female parts. Camerarius kept pollen away from mulberry, maize and annual mercury, and they made no good seeds.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A plant gets their food and their body from the soil.** (A very common idea. Many people still think it today.; *rejected*)  
  For: Roots grow in the soil, and plants die if the soil dries out. Plants in good soil grow better.  
  Against: Van Helmont's willow grew about 75 kilograms in five years, but the soil lost only about 60 grams. The soil gives water and small amounts of minerals. The rest of the plant is made from carbon dioxide from the air, using sunlight.
- **Tomatoes, bhindi and cucumbers are vegetables, not fruits.** (Cooks, shopkeepers, and in 1893 the highest court in the United States; *debated*)  
  For: In the kitchen, we use them in curries and salads, not in sweets. In 1893, the US Supreme Court decided that a tomato counts as a vegetable for paying taxes.  
  Against: To a botanist, anything that grows from a flower and holds seeds is a fruit. Both are useful ways of sorting. They just answer different questions.
- **Every plant grows from a seed.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Most plants we grow in gardens and fields do come from seeds.  
  Against: Potatoes, sugarcane, bananas, money plants and many roses are grown from pieces of the plant instead. Ferns and mosses have no seeds at all. They spread by spores.
- **When a plant dies, they are wasted.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: A dead plant gives no more flowers or fruit, and they look like rubbish.  
  Against: Decomposers break them down, and their atoms go back to the soil and the air. New plants are built from them. Forests depend on this. A fallen tree feeds thousands of living things for years.
- **Flowers exist to be beautiful for us.** (A common idea, and many poems; *rejected*)  
  For: People love flowers. We have bred roses and marigolds to be even brighter and bigger.  
  Against: Flowers existed for about 130 million years before there were people. Their colours, scents and sweet nectar bring insects, birds and bats to carry pollen. Some flowers are dull green and are pollinated by the wind. Our love of them is a happy extra.
- **Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old boy, found the way to make vanilla flowers turn into fruit, in 1841.** (Historians of vanilla, and the people of Réunion island; *accepted*)  
  For: His owner, Ferréol Bellier-Beaumont, wrote letters saying Edmond had found it. Farmers still use his method of a thin stick and a thumb.  
  Against: A French botanist, Jean Michel Claude Richard, later claimed that he had taught Edmond. And a Belgian botanist, Charles Morren, had made vanilla flowers fruit in a greenhouse a few years earlier, in the 1830s. But Morren's way was slow and was not used on farms.

## Roots
We have been learning how a plant goes round from seed to flower to fruit and back to seed. Now we ask what that circle tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your tomato held perhaps 100 seeds, and one tomato plant grows dozens of tomatoes. Very few of those seeds will ever become plants. We eat most of them with the tomato, and birds, ants and mice eat seeds everywhere. So a plant makes far more seeds than they need, and the extra feeds other living things.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a seed as the start of a new plant, so a seed that never grows seems wasted. But most seeds become food instead, for birds, ants, mice and people. Rice, wheat and dal are seeds too.  
*Evidence:* In Inside the Fruit, one tomato held about 100 seeds and one bhindi about 60. One moong seed gives back about 200 seeds, yet a pot has room for only 3 or 4 plants. The Eat the Life Cycle sort shows how many of our foods are seeds.

- *who am i:* Much of your body was built from seeds you ate: rice, wheat, dal and nuts. Each one is food a plant packed for their young.
- *what is life:* A plant makes far more seeds than they need to carry on. The extra seeds feed many other lives.
- *what is this world:* The air you breathe out can become part of a leaf. Plants, people and soil are built from the same atoms, passed round and round.

A moong plant grows from one seed and gives back about 200. Only a few need to grow to keep moong going. The rest feed ants, birds and people, and the dead plant feeds the decomposers in the soil. Each plant's life cycle is joined to the lives of many others, by the food that passes between them. So one moong plant's life cycle feeds many other lives.

- **Katha Upanishad 1.1.6:** The boy Nachiketa says to his father: look at those who came before us, and those who are here now. Like grain, a person ripens and falls. Like grain, a person is born again. (Long ago, teachers in India looked at grain in the field and saw a circle: ripening, falling and sprouting again. You can watch the same circle in a pot of moong.)
- **Gospel of Matthew 13:3-8, the parable of the sower:** A farmer scatters seed. Some falls on the path, and birds eat it. Some falls on rocks or among thorns, and dies. Some falls on good soil, and gives back a hundred times as much. (The farmer knew what you found by counting: a plant makes many seeds, and only some grow. The seeds on the path never grew, but they fed the birds.)
- **Ecclesiastes 3:1-2, in the Hebrew Bible:** For everything there is a season. There is a time to be born and a time to die, a time to plant and a time to pull up what is planted. (Plants keep to their seasons. Many flower only when the nights are the right length, and annuals die once their seeds are ready.)
- Science (settled): A plant builds their body mostly from carbon dioxide in the air and water, using the energy of sunlight. Very little of it comes from the soil.
- Science (settled): When living things die, decomposers break them down, and their atoms go back into the air and soil to be used by new life. The same atoms go round for billions of years.
- Other view, Many biologists: A plant does not make extra seeds to feed others. Most seeds die or are eaten, so plants that made more seeds left more young. Over a very long time, that is why plants make so many. On this view, the seeds a bird eats are simply lost to the plant.
- Practice, A handful of seeds (3 min): Let's each hold ten moong seeds in one hand. Close your eyes and feel how light they are. Each one holds a tiny plant and their food. Now think of who eats seeds like these: you, at dinner, and the pigeons and ants outside. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out. Open your eyes and put two seeds aside to sow. Who will the other eight feed?
- Ask: A moong plant gives back about 200 seeds. If only a few grow into plants, what happens to the rest?
- Ask: When you eat dal, you are eating seeds that could have grown into plants. What did the plant pack into them?
- Ask: When a moong plant dies after making seeds, has their life ended, or carried on?

## Story: Edmond's Thumb
*Edmond, Ferréol Bellier-Beaumont, the vanilla vine and the year 1841 are real. So are Edmond's method, the fight over the credit, his freedom in 1848 and the name Albius. Bellier-Beaumont is said to have shown Edmond how to pollinate watermelon flowers by hand. We changed it to pumpkins. The conversations and the small details are imagined.*

In 1841, on an island in the Indian Ocean called Bourbon, there grew a vine that would not make fruit. The vine climbed the trees in the garden of a man named Ferréol Bellier-Beaumont. Their leaves were thick and shiny. Every year they covered themselves in pale green flowers that smelled faintly of honey. And every year, by the next evening, each flower had dropped to the ground, and nothing was left behind.

The vine was vanilla. They had come across the sea from Mexico. There, their long pods were dried to make one of the most wonderful smells in the world. In Mexico, the vines hung heavy with pods. But here, and in the hothouses of France, and in the gardens of Java, the vines grew and grew and flowered and flowered, and gave nothing at all. Some people said the plant was homesick.

Edmond was twelve. He was enslaved, which means that Bellier-Beaumont owned him, the way a man might own a cart. The law allowed it then. It was deeply wrong. Edmond's mother had died when he was born, and the old man had brought him up among the plants. Edmond carried water, pulled weeds and followed Bellier-Beaumont along the garden paths. He was always listening.

One morning the old man stopped by the pumpkin patch. 'Look here,' he said. He pointed at two yellow flowers that looked exactly alike. Then he lifted one and turned it over. Behind its petals, where the flower met its stalk, sat a tiny green ball, no bigger than a marble.

'A baby pumpkin,' said Bellier-Beaumont. 'This is a mother flower. That one is a father flower. It has no baby behind it. It only has dust.' He picked the father flower, peeled back its petals, and showed Edmond the yellow powder on the little stalk in its middle. Then he dabbed the powder into the centre of the mother flower, like pressing a stamp onto a letter. 'Now we wait,' he said.

Edmond waited. Two days later, he ran to the pumpkin patch before breakfast. The mother flower's petals had shrivelled. But the green ball behind them had grown to the size of a lime. Another mother flower nearby, which nobody had touched, had gone yellow and soft. When Edmond poked it, it fell off in his hand.

He crouched there for a long time, looking from one to the other. So that was it. A flower was not the end of anything. A flower was a fruit that had not happened yet. Something had to happen first, deep in its middle. The dust had to get in.

That afternoon he went to look at the vanilla vine. Their flowers had already closed. They opened only in the early morning, for a few hours, and never again. So the next day he came back at dawn, when the grass was still wet.

He looked into one flower. He could see no dust anywhere. He looked into a second, and a third. In the middle of each was a little pale column, smooth as candle wax. He touched it gently with a fingertip. At its tip, half-hidden under a tiny cap, was a smear of sticky yellow. The dust! Just below it was a shiny, sticky place, the place where the dust needed to go. But between the two lay a thin flap, like a small tongue, keeping them apart.

Today, people think that in Mexico, certain bees can push past that flap. On Bourbon, there were no such bees. Every year, the flowers had opened for a visitor who never came.

Edmond snapped a thin splinter off a bamboo stick. He slid it under the little flap and lifted it, gently, the way you lift the hand of a sleeping baby. Then he pressed the dusty cap down onto the sticky place with his thumb. He held it for a moment, and let go. The flower looked exactly the same as before. He did another, and another, until the sun was high and the flowers began to close.

For two days, nothing happened. The flowers he had touched wilted, like all the others. But they did not fall. On the third day, Edmond saw it. Where each of his flowers had been, a thin green finger was beginning to grow.

Bellier-Beaumont found them a week later. He stood very still in front of the vine. 'Who did this?' he asked. Edmond's heart was thumping. He had touched the old man's most precious plant without asking. 'I did,' he said. Bellier-Beaumont looked at him for a long moment. 'Show me,' he said.

So Edmond showed him. The splinter. The flap. The thumb.

After that, Edmond was taken from farm to farm across the island, to teach the enslaved workers how to do it. The farmers called it 'marrying' the vanilla. The green fingers took nine months to ripen into long pods. Then the pods were dried until they turned dark and sweet-smelling. Within a few years, the island was sending vanilla across the world.

Later, a well-known botanist said that he had taught Edmond the trick himself. Bellier-Beaumont wrote letters to say that this was not true, and that the boy had found it alone. In 1848, slavery ended on the island, and Edmond became free. He was given a new surname, Albius, from an old word for white, like the vanilla flower. But he was never paid for his idea, and he died poor, in 1880.

The vines did not forget him, though. Today, on farms in Madagascar, in Réunion, as Bourbon is now called, and in Kerala and Karnataka in India, people still walk along the vanilla vines in the early morning. They carry a thin stick. They lift the flap. They press with their thumb.

And when the pods are ripe, each one is packed with thousands of seeds, too small to see one at a time. If you have ever seen tiny black specks in a scoop of vanilla ice cream, you have seen them. Every speck is a seed. Every one began in a flower that opened for a single morning, and that someone, with a splinter and a thumb, helped to become a fruit.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a flower is not the end of a plant's work. It is where the fruit will grow, but only after pollen reaches the sticky place in its middle. The pumpkin flower that was dusted grew into a pumpkin. The one left alone shrivelled. The vanilla flowers had always been able to make fruit, but the insect that carries their pollen did not live on the island. Once Edmond moved the pollen himself, each flower turned into a pod full of seeds. That is the life cycle's turn from flower to fruit to seed. The story also asks who gets the credit for discoveries, and who does not.

- Ask: Why did the vanilla flowers drop off every year without making any pods?
- Ask: What did Edmond learn from the two pumpkin flowers, the one that was dusted and the one left alone?
- Ask: Edmond thought that a flower is 'a fruit that has not happened yet'. What did he mean?
- Ask: A grown-up botanist said he had taught Edmond. Why do you think Bellier-Beaumont wrote letters to say it was not true?
- Ask: The black specks in vanilla ice cream are seeds. What stage of the life cycle are they, and what could they become?

  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: fruits hold seeds, and they grow from flowers. What I saw: the pea pod had 6 seeds and the bhindi about 60. The tomato still had a green star from its flower. Why: the base of each flower swelled into the fruit, and its tiny seed-starts grew into seeds.
### e3 · Be Edmond: help a pumpkin flower become a fruit (35 min, 5 days)
- **question:** Does a female flower need pollen to grow into a fruit?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will have three female flowers. One is left open for insects. One is covered with a bag and gets no pollen. One is covered with a bag and gets pollen from you. Which will grow into fruits? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use female flowers on the same vine that open on the same morning, or as close together as you can. Cover two of them with a bag the evening before they open, so no insect can get in first. Give pollen to only one of the covered flowers. The bag is the same for both covered flowers, so only the pollen is different.
- **safety:** Do this with a grown-up near the vine. Watch for bees, and move away slowly if one comes. Wash your hands afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the three baby fruits on day 4, side by side.
- **results table:** Female flower | Bag? | Pollen added by hand? | Day 2: green or yellow? | Day 4: growing or shrivelled?
- **schedule:** day 1: In the evening, find three female-flower buds about to open. Cover two with bags. Tag all three. (10 min); day 2: Early in the morning, move pollen from a male flower to one covered flower. Cover it again. (12 min); day 3: Take the bags off. Check all three baby fruits. (3 min); day 4: Check: which baby fruits are growing, and which are going yellow? (3 min); day 5: Compare all three. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (7 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look along the vine together. 2. Find a female flower: it has a tiny baby fruit behind its petals. 3. Find a male flower: it is on a thin stalk, with no baby fruit behind it.  
  *Parent says:* These flowers look alike from the front. Turn them over. The female flower has a tiny fruit behind it. The male flower has only pollen. In the story, they were called mother and father flowers.  
  *Child mode:* Look behind each flower. A tiny fruit behind it? That is a female flower. No fruit, on a thin stalk? That is a male flower.
  - Note: A young vine often makes only male flowers for the first week or two. Female flowers come later, and there are fewer of them. Pumpkin, cucumber and karela flowers open early in the morning, about 6 to 9 o'clock, and close by midday. Lauki flowers are white and tori flowers are yellow. Both open in the evening, so do this experiment at dusk with them.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the evening, find three female-flower buds that will open tomorrow. They are big, and their tips are turning yellow or white. 2. Tie a thread on each stalk: red, blue and green. 3. Cover the blue one and the green one with a paper bag or net, tied loosely round the stalk. 4. Leave the red one uncovered.  
  *Parent says:* The bag stops any insect getting in first. The red flower stays open, so insects can visit it.  
  *Child mode:* Find three female buds that will open tomorrow. Tie a thread on each. Cover two with bags. Leave one open.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Next morning, pick a fresh, open male flower. 2. Peel back its petals and touch the middle: yellow dust should come off on your finger. 3. Pick up the dust with the paintbrush or cotton bud. Or use the male flower itself, like a brush. 4. Open the green flower's bag. Brush the pollen gently all over the sticky middle of the female flower. 5. Close the bag again. 6. Do not touch the blue flower.  
  *Parent says:* Now we do what Edmond did. We carry the pollen ourselves. Gently, like painting with the softest brush.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a male flower. Peel back its petals. See the yellow dust? Pick it up with the brush. Open the green flower's bag. Brush the dust gently into its middle. Close the bag.
  - Note: Do this early, before about 10 o'clock for morning flowers, while the flowers are fresh. If no dust comes off on your finger, try a different male flower that opened this morning.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 3, take the bags off, because the flowers have closed. 2. Each day, look at the three baby fruits behind the old flowers. Are they green and growing, or yellow and soft?  
  *Parent says:* Which baby fruits are growing? Which are going yellow?  
  *Child mode:* Take the bags off. Check the three baby fruits each day. Green and growing? Or yellow and soft?
  - Note: A female flower that got pollen usually stays green and starts to swell within 2 or 3 days. One that got none goes yellow and soft, and drops off within 2 to 5 days. The open flower grows only if an insect visited it. In very hot weather, even pollinated flowers sometimes drop. If that happens, try again: a vine in season makes new flowers almost every day.
  - Check: The covered flower with no pollen went yellow and fell off. The covered flower with pollen grew. What does that show? → **A female flower needs pollen to grow into a fruit**
  - Wait: Check once a day for 3 days
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare all three flowers on day 5.  
  *Parent says:* Edmond found out that vanilla flowers needed someone to carry their pollen. Who carried pollen to our red flower?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your three flowers. Who carried pollen to the open red flower? Edmond did this job for vanilla flowers!
  - Think: Bees, butterflies, moths and even bats carry pollen for free. What would happen to our food if they all disappeared?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a female pumpkin flower needs pollen to become a pumpkin. What I saw: the covered flower with no pollen went yellow and fell off on day 3. The covered flower I brushed with pollen was twice as big by day 4. Why: the pollen has to reach the middle of the flower. Only then can its base grow into a fruit with seeds.
### e4 · Floating Leaves: does a leaf make gas in the light? (40 min)
- **question:** Do leaves make gas when light shines on them?
- **predict:** Guess first. The leaf circles will sink to the bottom of two glasses. One glass goes in bright light and one in a dark cupboard. In which glass will the circles float up? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Make the two glasses the same. Use the same water, the same pinch of baking soda and drop of soap, and 10 circles cut from the same leaf in each. Change only the light. Count the floating circles in both glasses at the same times.
- **safety:** The syringe has no needle. Do not drink the water. Wash the glasses well afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table every 5 minutes. Then draw a bar for each glass, showing how many circles were floating after 20 minutes.
- **results table:** Minutes | Glass in bright light: circles floating | Glass in the dark: circles floating
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Half fill two clear glasses with tap water. 2. Add a small pinch of baking soda to each, about an eighth of a teaspoon. 3. Add one tiny drop of washing-up liquid to each. 4. Stir gently, so it does not foam.  
  *Parent says:* Baking soda puts extra carbon dioxide in the water. That is the gas leaves use to make food. The drop of soap helps the water soak into the leaf.  
  *Child mode:* Half fill two glasses with water. Add a small pinch of baking soda to each. Add one tiny drop of soap. Stir gently.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay a palak leaf on a soft eraser or your fingertip. 2. Press the straw straight down and twist, to cut out a circle. 3. Cut 20 circles. Try to miss the thick veins.  
  *Parent says:* We need 20 little leaf circles, 10 for each glass.  
  *Child mode:* Press the straw into the leaf and twist. Out pops a circle! Cut 20. Miss the thick lines in the leaf.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pull the plunger out of the syringe. Drop 10 circles in. 2. Push the plunger back in gently, without squashing them. 3. Suck up water from one glass until the syringe is half full. 4. Point it up and push out the air. 5. Cover the tip firmly with a finger. Pull the plunger back hard and hold for 10 seconds, then let go. 6. Do this 3 to 5 times, until the circles sink.  
  *Parent says:* Pulling the plunger sucks the air out of the leaf circles. Water goes in instead, so they sink. Leaves have air spaces inside, like a sponge.  
  *Child mode:* Put 10 circles in the syringe. Suck up some water. Push out the air. Block the tip with a finger. Pull the plunger back hard. Do it again until they sink.
  - Note: Your child can do most of this, but small hands may need help holding the tip shut while pulling. Will the circles not sink after 5 tries? Add one more tiny drop of soap to the water and try again.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Squirt the sunk circles into the first glass. 2. Do the same with 10 more circles for the second glass. 3. If any circle floats, poke it down or sink it again.  
  *Parent says:* All 20 circles are at the bottom now. Let's give them different places to wait.  
  *Child mode:* Squirt the circles into the first glass. Do 10 more for the second glass. Are they all at the bottom?
- **s5 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one glass in bright sunlight, or close under a bright lamp. 2. Put the other in a dark cupboard. 3. Every 5 minutes, count the floating circles in each glass and write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Every five minutes, let's count. Look closely at the circles in the light. Can you see tiny bubbles?  
  *Child mode:* One glass in bright light. One in the dark cupboard. Every 5 minutes, count the floaters. Write the numbers. Look for tiny bubbles.
  - Note: In strong sunlight the first circles usually rise after about 5 to 15 minutes. Most float within 20 to 25 minutes. Under a weak lamp or on a cloudy day it is slower, so move the lamp closer or wait longer. The circles in the dark usually stay down. While you wait, your child can draw the two glasses.
  - Check: Why did the leaf circles in the light float up? → **The leaves made oxygen gas while making food, and the tiny bubbles lifted them**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap the glasses: put the dark glass in the light, and the light glass in the dark. Look again in 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think will happen now?  
  *Child mode:* Swap the two glasses. What will happen now? Look again later.
  - Note: The dark circles now usually float too. The floating ones put in the dark slowly sink again over 20 to 30 minutes. The leaf uses up its oxygen to breathe.
  - Think: A whole tree is doing this in the sun, all day. Where does the oxygen go?
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: leaves make gas when light shines on them. What I saw: after 20 minutes, 9 circles were floating in the light, and none in the dark. Why: in light, the leaf makes food from carbon dioxide and water, and gives out oxygen. The bubbles of oxygen lifted the circles.

  *Child mode:* Turn the wheel. Start at the flower. Tell the story until you get back to the flower.

### a3 · New Plants Without Seeds: onion, money plant and potato (30 min, 10 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the money plant stem. Do not eat the onion or potato after the test.
- **record:** Draw all three on days 1, 5 and 10. Write when you first saw roots, and when you first saw green shoots.
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up the onion, the money plant stem and the potato. (10 min); day 3: Change the onion's water. Look for roots. (3 min); day 5: Change the water. Look for roots and shoots on all three. Draw. (4 min); day 7: Change the water. Look again. Draw. (3 min); day 10: Compare all three. Draw them. Plant the best one in soil. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Onion: fill a small glass to the top with water. Sit the onion on it, flat roots end down, so only the bottom touches the water. If the glass is wide, push 3 toothpicks into the onion to hold it up. 2. Money plant: take off the leaves near the bottom of the stem. Stand it in a bottle of water so one bump on the stem is under water. 3. Potato: put it on a windowsill or in a warm basket.  
  *Parent says:* None of these is a seed. Let's see if each one can still grow a new plant.  
  *Child mode:* Sit the onion on a glass of water. Only its bottom touches. Put the money plant stem in water. Put the potato somewhere warm.
  - Note: The bumps on a money plant stem are where roots grow from. Some shop onions are treated so they do not sprout. Choose one that already has a green tip, or try two onions. A garlic clove, pointed end up, also works.

  *Parent does:* 1. Pour away the onion's water and give it fresh water, so it does not smell. 2. Look for white roots and green shoots on all three. 3. Draw them on day 5.

  *Child mode:* Compare all three. They grew from pieces, not seeds. Will the new plant look like their parent?
  - Check: A new money plant grows from a stem cutting. How will the new plant compare with the plant the stem came from? → **They will be an exact copy, a clone**
  - Think: If every plant in a field is a copy of the same plant, what happens if a disease comes that they cannot fight?

### p1 · Kitchen to Kitchen: grow moong from seed to seed (130 min, 75 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up makes holes in the bottom of a bucket. Wash your hands after touching soil. Do not use pesticide sprays.
- **record:** A diary page every week: the date, a drawing, the height of the tallest plant, and the number of flowers or pods. At the end, a line graph of height, and the total number of seeds.
- **schedule:** day 1: Soak 8 moong seeds overnight. Fill the pot. Name your plant. (15 min); day 2: Sow the seeds 2 cm deep. Water them. Put the pot in the sun. (10 min); day 10: Look for seed leaves and true leaves. Keep the 3 or 4 strongest plants. Measure. (10 min); day 17: Measure the tallest plant. Draw it. (5 min); day 24: Measure. Draw. Look for buds at the tips. (5 min); day 35: Flowers! Count them. Write the date of the first flower. (10 min); day 42: Pods! Tie a thread on the first pod. Measure it. (10 min); day 55: Measure. Are any pods turning brown or black? (5 min); day 65: Pick the black, dry pods. Dry them in the sun for 2 days. (20 min); day 70: Open the pods. Count the seeds. Make your graph. (20 min); day 75: Sow some new seeds to start again. Sprout and eat some. Read your diary aloud. (20 min)
- **s1 (25 min) · grown-up needed**

  - Note: Moong likes warm weather, about 25 to 35 degrees. In most of north India, sow them between February and August. In much of south India they grow most of the year. In north India's cold months, from November to January, grow matar instead. Matar flowers in about 6 to 8 weeks and makes dry seeds in about 3 months. The pot must get at least 5 hours of direct sun. In shade, moong grows tall and thin and may not flower at all. If you have no sunny spot, ask to use a relative's terrace or a school garden.

  *Child mode:* Find the seed leaves. They are fat and round. Then find the new leaves. Keep the 3 or 4 strongest plants.

  - Wait: About a week
- **s3 (15 min)**

  *Parent says:* How much did they grow this week? Are they growing faster or slower than last week?  
  *Child mode:* Once a week, measure the tallest plant. Draw them. Write the number down. Did they grow fast or slow?
  - Note: Tiny black or green insects on the tips are aphids. Wash them off with a gentle spray of water. Leaves with yellow patches may have a virus that is common in moong. Snip off that plant and keep the others. Heavy rain can rot the roots, so move the pot under a ledge in a storm.
  - Wait: Every week
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Around day 30 to 40, look for small pale yellow flowers at the ends of short stalks. 2. Write the date of the first flower. 3. Count the flowers each week.  
  *Parent says:* Our plant is a grown plant now. They have stopped just making leaves. They are making flowers.  
  *Child mode:* Look for small yellow flowers. Write the date of the first one. Count the flowers.
  - Note: Moong flowers can pollinate themselves, so you do not need bees. The flowers are small and close by midday, so look in the morning.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A few days after flowering, look for thin green pods where the flowers were. 2. Tie a thread on the first pod. 3. Measure it each week.  
  *Parent says:* Where the flower was, there is now a pod. What do you think is growing inside it?  
  *Child mode:* Find a thin green pod where a flower was. Tie a thread on it. Measure it. What is inside?

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A moong seedling must grow many leaves before they can flower. Why?  
   Options: Flowers grow out of old leaves / Leaves keep the flowers warm / The leaves make the food the plant needs to build flowers, pods and seeds / Bees visit only plants with many leaves  
   **Answer:** The leaves make the food the plant needs to build flowers, pods and seeds. *Why:* Flowers, pods and seeds cost a lot of food. The leaves make that food from air and water, using sunlight. A tiny seedling has too few leaves to pay for them. Picking 'flowers grow out of old leaves' comes from seeing flowers among the leaves. But flowers grow from buds on the stem.
2. (mcq) What is a seedling?  
   Options: A seed in a packet / A flower bud / A fruit / A very young plant just out of their seed  
   **Answer:** A very young plant just out of their seed. *Why:* A seedling has just sprouted, with a tiny root, a short stem and their first leaves. Picking 'a seed in a packet' mixes up the seed and the seedling. The seedling is the next stage.
3. (mcq) Kalyani pinches off every brinjal flower on her plant as soon as they open. What will happen?  
   Options: The plant makes bigger brinjals / The plant makes no brinjals / The flowers grow back as brinjals / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** The plant makes no brinjals. *Why:* Each brinjal grows from a flower. Take away every flower, and there is nothing left to swell into a fruit. Picking 'bigger brinjals' is a guess that the plant saves food. But with no flowers, there are no fruits to put that food into.
4. (mcq) The green cap on a brinjal is…  
   Options: A seed / Left over from the brinjal's flower / A leaf that grew later / Put there by the farmer  
   **Answer:** Left over from the brinjal's flower. *Why:* It is made of the sepals, the small green leaves that wrapped the flower bud. They stay on as the fruit grows. Picking 'a leaf that grew later' is a fair guess, because it is green. But it was there first, around the flower.
5. (mcq) Peanuts grow under the ground. Did they grow from a flower?  
   Options: No, they grow from the roots, like a carrot / No, they are swollen stems, like a potato / Yes. After a flower gets pollen, a stalk grows down and pushes the young pod into the soil / No, peanuts grow from leaves  
   **Answer:** Yes. After a flower gets pollen, a stalk grows down and pushes the young pod into the soil. *Why:* A peanut shell is a pod with seeds inside, so it is a fruit. The yellow flowers open above the ground. After pollination, a stalk grows down and pushes the young pod into the soil, where they ripen. Picking 'from the roots' is a natural guess, because we dig peanuts up. But roots do not hold seeds.
6. (mcq) Why is a plant's life called a CYCLE?  
   Options: Because plants spin / Because it only happens once / Because plants grow in circles / Because the new seeds start the same stages again, going round  
   **Answer:** Because the new seeds start the same stages again, going round. *Why:* The new seeds are the start of new plants, so the stages go round in a circle. Picking 'it only happens once' is true for one plant. But their seeds carry the cycle on.
7. (mcq) A moong plant's pods turn black, and the plant turns yellow and dries up. What happens next in the life cycle?  
   Options: The plant grows new flowers / The pods turn back into flowers / The seeds in the pods can grow into new moong plants / Nothing, it is over  
   **Answer:** The seeds in the pods can grow into new moong plants. *Why:* The old plant has finished, but their seeds start the next circle. Picking 'nothing, it is over' looks at the old plant only, and forgets their seeds.
8. (mcq) You tag 5 flowers on a chilli plant. After a week, 3 have become baby chillies and 2 have fallen off. What should you write in your results?  
   Options: 5 out of 5 became fruits / 3 out of 5 flowers became fruits, and 2 fell off / The experiment failed / Only the 3 that worked  
   **Answer:** 3 out of 5 flowers became fruits, and 2 fell off. *Why:* Every result counts, including the flowers that fell. Not every flower becomes a fruit, and that is worth knowing. Picking 'only the 3 that worked' would hide part of what really happened. Scientists write down everything they see.
9. (mcq) Look at the line graph of a bhindi pod. How long was it on day 6?  
   Options: 10 cm / 6 cm / 8 cm / 2 cm  
   **Answer:** 10 cm. *Why:* Find day 6 along the bottom, go up to the line, then across to the side. It reads 10 cm. Picking 6 cm means reading the day number as the length.
10. (mcq) Which plant finishes their whole life cycle, from seed to new seeds, in 2 to 3 months?  
   Options: Mango / Neem / Coconut / Moong  
   **Answer:** Moong. *Why:* Moong is an annual and goes from seed to seed in two to three months. Mango, neem and coconut are perennials that take years to first flower. Picking mango may come from thinking fruit trees are quick, because mangoes come every summer. But a mango tree takes years to start.
11. (mcq) Which of these can grow a new plant WITHOUT a seed?  
   Options: A grain of rice / A sunflower seed / A potato, from its eyes / A moong seed  
   **Answer:** A potato, from its eyes. *Why:* A potato is a swollen stem, and each eye can sprout a new plant, a copy of their parent. The others are all seeds. Picking a rice grain is a natural mistake, because we think of rice as food, not as seeds. But each rice grain is a seed, and they can sprout into a rice plant.
12. (mcq) Where does most of a big tree's body come from?  
   Options: Rain only / Carbon dioxide from the air, and water, put together using sunlight / The soil / Fertiliser  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide from the air, and water, put together using sunlight. *Why:* Leaves build sugar from carbon dioxide and water, using sunlight, and the tree builds their wood from that sugar. Picking 'the soil' is what almost everyone guesses. But a famous test showed a willow gained about 74 kilograms while their soil lost only about 57 grams.
13. (mcq) In the Floating Leaves experiment, leaf circles floated up in bright light but stayed down in the dark. What made them float?  
   Options: Bubbles of oxygen the leaf made using light / Hot water / The soap / Wind  
   **Answer:** Bubbles of oxygen the leaf made using light. *Why:* In light, leaves make sugar and give out oxygen, and the bubbles lift the circles. Both glasses had the same soap, so the soap cannot explain the difference. Only the light was different.
14. (mcq) What happens to a plant's body after they die?  
   Options: It stays there for ever / It vanishes / It turns into a seed / Decomposers break them down, and their atoms go back to the soil and air for new life  
   **Answer:** Decomposers break them down, and their atoms go back to the soil and air for new life. *Why:* Fungi, bacteria and small animals eat the dead plant. Their carbon goes back into the air and its minerals into the soil, where new plants use them. Picking 'it vanishes' usually means thinking matter can disappear. It cannot. It changes form.
15. (mcq) Do scientists know how bamboo counts about 48 years before they flower?  
   Options: Yes, they count the monsoons / Bamboo never flowers / No, it is still a mystery / Yes, they have a clock in their roots  
   **Answer:** No, it is still a mystery. *Why:* Nobody knows how bamboo keeps time for so long. There are ideas, but none has been proved. Picking 'they count the monsoons' is a clever guess that some people have made. But nobody has shown it is true.
16. (mcq) Who found the way to make vanilla flowers grow pods outside Mexico, using a thin stick and his thumb?  
   Options: Isaac Newton / Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old boy, in 1841 / Charles Darwin / Gregor Mendel  
   **Answer:** Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old boy, in 1841. *Why:* Edmond Albius found the method in 1841, on the island now called Réunion. It is still used today. Picking Darwin is a common guess, because he studied plants and flowers. But he did not find this.

## Applications
- Farmers plan sowing and harvest by each crop's life cycle: rice in the monsoon, wheat and mustard in winter.
- Farmers save seeds from one harvest to sow the next year, so their crops go round the cycle again.
- Seed companies print 'days to harvest' on seed packets, so gardeners know how long each life cycle takes.
- Vanilla farmers in Madagascar, Réunion, Kerala and Karnataka pollinate every flower by hand, using Edmond Albius's method.
- Pumpkin, cucumber and gourd growers on terraces move pollen by hand when bees are few.
- Fruit farmers grow mango, guava and lemon trees from grafts and cuttings, so the fruit is exactly like the parent's.
- Potatoes, sugarcane, bananas and roses are grown from pieces of the plant instead of seeds.
- Flower growers use lights at night to stop or start flowering, because many plants measure the length of the night.
- Plant breeders cross plants by moving pollen by hand, to make new kinds of rice, wheat and vegetables.
- Compost pits turn dead plants and kitchen peels back into rich soil for the next crop.
- Seed banks keep seeds of thousands of kinds of plant, so their life cycles can be started again if a crop is lost.
- Citizen-science projects like SeasonWatch ask children to record when trees flower and fruit, to track how the climate is changing.
- Space agencies grow plants from seed to seed on space stations, to plan farms on the Moon and Mars.

## Open questions
- **How does bamboo count about 48 years before they flower, and how do clumps far apart stay in step?** Nobody has found a clock that could count so long inside a plant. There are ideas, but none has been proved.
- **Why did flowering plants spread so fast and take over the land?** Darwin called it 'an abominable mystery' in 1879. Scientists still argue about how much was due to insects, fast growth, fruits or the climate.
- **Exactly how does a plant decide that they are big enough to flower?** Scientists know many of the signals: day length, cold, age and sugar. How the plant puts them all together is still being worked out.
- **How long can a single plant live?** Some trees are thousands of years old. Some plants, like grasses and aspen trees, spread as copies of themselves for so long that nobody knows where one plant ends.
- **What is the best way to grow plants through their whole life cycle on the Moon or Mars?** Plants have gone from seed to seed in space stations. But Moon and Mars soil is very different, and nobody has grown a crop there yet.
- **How will a warmer world change when plants flower?** Many plants now flower earlier than they used to. If they flower before their insects wake up, the flowers may make less fruit. How much this will happen, and where, is still being studied.
- **Is a seed the same plant as their parent, or a new one?** Biologists count them as a new plant, because they have a new mix of instructions. Some thinkers say the seed is the same life carrying on. It depends what you mean by 'the same'.

## Connected maths
- In the Kitchen to Kitchen project, a moong plant made 20 pods. Each pod had about 10 seeds. About how many seeds did one plant give back? **About 200 seeds.**. There are 20 pods, and each holds about 10 seeds. That is 20 groups of 10. 20 × 10 = 200. So one seed planted gave back about 200 seeds. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- You sowed moong on 1 July. The first flower opened on 5 August. How many days did the plant take to flower? **35 days.**. July has 31 days. From 1 July to 31 July is 30 days. Then 1 August to 5 August is 5 more days. 30 + 5 = 35 days. That is 5 weeks. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- Look at the chart of peas counted in five pea pods. How many peas were there altogether? Which pod had the most? **32 peas altogether. Pod 4 had the most, with 8.**. Add the bars: 6 + 7 + 5 + 8 + 6. 6 + 7 = 13. 13 + 5 = 18. 18 + 8 = 26. 26 + 6 = 32. The tallest bar is Pod 4, at 8. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Look at the line graph of a bhindi pod. It was 2 cm long on day 2 and 10 cm long on day 6. How much did it grow in those 4 days? About how much is that each day? **8 cm in 4 days. That is about 2 cm each day.**. 10 take away 2 is 8. So it grew 8 cm. It took 4 days, from day 2 to day 6. 8 shared between 4 days is 2. So it grew about 2 cm each day. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- Neelakurinji flowered near Munnar in 2018. They flower every 12 years. Write the next three years they should flower. **2030, 2042 and 2054.**. Add 12 each time. 2018 + 12 = 2030. 2030 + 12 = 2042. 2042 + 12 = 2054. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Look at the chart from the Floating Leaves experiment. In the light, 9 out of 10 leaf circles were floating after 20 minutes. What fraction is that? How many were still at the bottom? **9 out of 10, which is nine tenths. 1 circle was still at the bottom.**. There were 10 circles in the glass. 9 were floating. So the fraction floating is 9 out of 10, written 9/10, or nine tenths. 10 take away 9 is 1, so 1 was still at the bottom. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
