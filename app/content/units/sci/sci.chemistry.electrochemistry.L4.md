# Batteries, electrolysis and rust: what happens when a metal gives away electrons
`sci.chemistry.electrochemistry.L4`

**Summary.** A lemon with two bits of metal in it can make electricity. How? In this lesson you will build a lemon battery and try different pairs of metals, read the volts on batteries at home, and rust nails with a cell to see why iron rusts.

**Intro.** *A lemon has no battery inside it. So how can a lemon make a pair of earphones crackle?* A lemon alone does nothing to a pair of earphones. A lemon with two copper wires does nothing either. But a lemon with one copper wire and one zinc-coated nail makes the earphones crackle. In this lesson you find out why the two metals must be different. You also make a steel nail rust in ten minutes, and split water with four AA cells. All three happen for the same reason. The cells you use are small ones, and you never use electricity from a wall socket.

## Terms
- **Electron**: A very tiny particle with a minus charge. Every atom has electrons. An electric current in a wire is a flow of electrons. *e.g.* When earphones crackle on a lemon cell, electrons are passing through their wires.
- **Ion**: An atom that has lost or gained electrons, so it carries a charge. Ions can move about in water and in juice. *e.g.* A zinc atom that gives away two electrons becomes a zinc ion and floats off into the lemon juice.
- **Cell and battery**: A cell is two different substances and a liquid or paste between them, which together push electrons round a wire. A battery is two or more cells joined together. *e.g.* One AA cell is a cell. Four lemon cells joined in a row make a battery.
- **Electrode**: A piece of metal or carbon that carries electrons into or out of a liquid. *e.g.* In a lemon cell the zinc nail and the copper wire are the two electrodes.
- **Electrolyte**: A liquid or paste with ions in it. The ions carry charge through it. *e.g.* Lemon juice and salt water are electrolytes. Cooking oil is not.
- **Volt**: The unit that tells how hard a cell pushes electrons. *e.g.* A lemon cell pushes with about 0.9 volts. An AA cell pushes with 1.5 volts.
- **Terminal**: One of the two ends of a cell where a wire is joined. Electrons leave from the minus terminal and return to the plus terminal. *e.g.* On an AA cell the flat end is the minus terminal. The end with the bump is the plus terminal.
- **Metal ladder**: A list of metals in order. At the top are the metals that give away electrons most easily. At the bottom are those that hold them most strongly. Scientists call it the activity series. *e.g.* Zinc is above copper on the ladder. So in a lemon cell the zinc gives the electrons.
- **Electrolysis**: Using an electric current to force a chemical change that would not happen alone. *e.g.* A current from four AA cells splits water into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas.
- **Corrosion**: The slow change of a metal into another substance as it gives away electrons, usually to oxygen. *e.g.* A green crust on an old copper pot and rust on a gate are both corrosion.
- **Rust**: The flaky orange-brown substance that forms when iron corrodes. It is made of iron, oxygen and water joined together. *e.g.* A cycle chain left out in the monsoon is covered in rust within a week.
- **Galvanised**: Coated with a thin layer of zinc. The zinc corrodes in place of the iron under it. *e.g.* A galvanised bucket stays grey for years where a plain iron bucket would rust.
- **Fuel cell**: A cell that joins hydrogen and oxygen into water and gives an electric current as it does so. It is electrolysis of water run backwards. *e.g.* Some buses run on fuel cells. Only water comes out of their exhaust pipes.

## Explanations
### Metals hold some of their electrons loosely · *defined*
**Definition:** An electron is a tiny particle with a minus charge, and every atom has electrons. An ion is an atom that has lost or gained electrons.

Everything is made of atoms, and every atom has electrons. In a metal, each atom holds its outermost electrons loosely. These loose electrons can move from atom to atom through the whole piece. That is why metals carry electricity. A metal atom can also give its loose electrons away completely. Then it is no longer an atom of metal. It has become an ion, and an ion behaves very differently. A zinc atom sits firmly in a solid nail. A zinc ion can leave the nail and float away in juice or water. So when a metal gives away electrons, the metal is slowly used up. Some metals give electrons away easily, and others hold on to them strongly. Batteries, electrolysis and rust all follow from that difference.
- e.g. A copper wire carries a current because its loose electrons can move along it.
- e.g. A zinc nail in lemon juice slowly loses zinc, because zinc atoms become ions and float away.
- e.g. A gold ring stays bright for centuries, because gold atoms hold their electrons strongly.
### A lemon cell: two different metals and a liquid with ions · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell is two different metals, or other substances, with an electrolyte between them. A chemical change in the cell pushes electrons round a wire.

Zinc gives away electrons more easily than copper does. In a lemon cell, zinc atoms leave their electrons behind in the nail. The atoms go into the juice as zinc ions. The electrons left behind crowd the nail, so the nail is the minus terminal. A wire gives them a path to the copper, and they flow along it. At the copper, the electrons are taken by hydrogen ions from the acid in the juice. These become tiny bubbles of hydrogen gas. The copper itself does not change. Inside the lemon, ions drift through the juice and complete the loop. The energy of the current comes from the zinc as it changes. The lemon only supplies the juice, which carries the ions and holds the acid. When the zinc is used up, the cell is dead.
- e.g. A zinc nail and a copper wire in a lemon give about 0.9 volts.
- e.g. Two copper wires in a lemon give nothing, because neither one gives electrons more easily than the other.
- e.g. The same two metals work in a potato, in vinegar or in salt water.
### The metal ladder: why the two metals must be different · *defined*
**Definition:** The metal ladder, or activity series, is a list of metals in order. It runs from those that give away electrons most easily to those that hold them most strongly.

Chemists have tested metals against each other and put them in order. From the top, eight common ones are magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, tin, copper, silver and gold. When two metals share an electrolyte, the one higher on the ladder gives electrons to the lower one. The further apart they are on the ladder, the harder the push, and the more volts the cell gives. Two pieces of the same metal sit on the same rung, so there is no push at all. The ladder also explains where metals are found. Gold is at the bottom and holds its electrons strongly, so it lies in the ground as shiny metal. Iron is higher, so in the ground it is always joined to oxygen as ore. Aluminium is near the top, but a thin, hard skin of aluminium oxide covers it. That skin is why foil often acts as if aluminium were lower on the ladder.
- e.g. Zinc and copper are three rungs apart, and they make a good cell.
- e.g. Copper and copper are on the same rung, and they make no cell.
- e.g. Gold is found as nuggets. Iron is found only as ore.
### Volts say how hard a cell pushes, not how much it can give · *defined*
**Definition:** A volt is the unit for how hard a cell pushes electrons. The number of electrons that pass each second is called the current.

A lemon cell pushes with about 0.9 volts, and an AA cell with 1.5 volts. Cells joined in a row, plus to minus, add their pushes. Four lemon cells give about 3.6 volts, which is more than two AA cells. Yet four lemons light only a small red LED, and only faintly. They can never light a torch bulb. The reason is that a lemon cell lets very few electrons through each second. Its ions drift slowly through the juice, and the metals touch only a little juice. A torch bulb needs hundreds of times more electrons each second than a lemon can pass. An AA cell is built to pass that many. So a battery has two different numbers: how hard it pushes, and how much it can deliver. A wall socket pushes with 230 volts and can deliver enough to kill. That is why this lesson uses only small cells.
- e.g. One lemon cell gives about 0.9 volts. Four lemon cells joined in a row give about 3.6 volts.
- e.g. A red LED needs about 1.8 volts and very few electrons each second, so lemons can light it.
- e.g. A torch bulb needs many electrons each second, so lemons cannot light it.
### Inside the cells you buy · *defined*
**Definition:** A shop cell works like a lemon cell. One substance gives electrons, another takes them, and a paste with ions lies between them.

In a common AA cell, the giver of electrons is zinc, just as in the lemon. In the cheapest kind, called zinc-carbon, the outer can is the zinc. Inside is a black paste that holds manganese dioxide, which takes the electrons. A carbon rod down the middle collects them. As the cell works, the zinc can is eaten away. That is why very old cells leak, and why cells should be taken out of toys that are put away. An alkaline cell uses zinc powder and lasts longer. A phone battery is a different kind, called lithium-ion. In it, lithium ions move from one layered material to another, and charging pushes them back. Button cells need special care. If a small child swallows one, the current makes a strong base in the wet food pipe within two hours. This burns badly. Keep button cells out of reach, and go to hospital at once if one is swallowed.
- e.g. A zinc-carbon AA cell: the zinc can gives electrons, and manganese dioxide takes them.
- e.g. An old cell left in a toy leaks, because its zinc can has been eaten through.
- e.g. A phone battery can be charged, because the change inside it can be pushed backwards.
### Electrolysis: a current forces a change, and splits water · *defined*
**Definition:** Electrolysis is using an electric current to force a chemical change. In the electrolysis of water, the current splits water into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas.

A water molecule is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. The atoms hold on to each other strongly, and boiling does not separate them. A current can. Two electrodes joined to a pack of cells dip into water with a little baking soda in it. The soda gives the water ions, so that it can carry a current. The cells push electrons into the minus electrode. Water molecules there take the electrons and break up, and hydrogen gas bubbles off. What is left behind there turns the water into a base, which is the opposite of an acid. The cells pull electrons out of the plus electrode. Water molecules there give up electrons, and oxygen gas bubbles off. Twice as much hydrogen comes off as oxygen, because water has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom. A battery gives energy as its change runs. Electrolysis uses energy to push a change the other way. Charging a phone is electrolysis inside its battery.
- e.g. Two pencil leads joined to four AA cells bubble in baking soda water.
- e.g. A space station splits water by electrolysis to make oxygen for the crew to breathe.
- e.g. A phone charger pushes the change inside the phone's battery backwards.
### Why iron rusts · *defined*
**Definition:** Rust is the flaky orange-brown substance that forms when iron gives electrons to oxygen while water is present.

A drop of water on a steel plate sets up a tiny cell. Steel is iron with a little carbon in it. Under the middle of the drop, iron atoms give away electrons and go into the water as iron ions. The electrons travel through the steel to the edge of the drop. More oxygen reaches the water there from the air. There the oxygen and water take the electrons. The iron ions meet what the oxygen has made, and together they form rust. So rusting needs three things at once: iron, water and oxygen. Dry iron hardly rusts, and iron deep under still water with no air hardly rusts. Salt speeds rusting, because salty water carries ions well. That is why cycles, grills and cars rust fastest near the sea. Rust is flaky and lets water through, so the iron below keeps rusting. Aluminium corrodes too, but its oxide forms a tight skin that seals the metal.
- e.g. A steel kadai left wet overnight has orange spots by morning.
- e.g. A window grill in Chennai or Kochi rusts faster than one in Jaipur, because sea air is damp and salty.
- e.g. An iron nail kept in a dry tin stays grey for years.
### Four ways to stop rust · *defined*
**Definition:** Rust is stopped by keeping water and oxygen off the iron. It is also stopped by giving the iron electrons from elsewhere, or by changing the iron.

The first way is a coat. Paint, oil, grease and plastic keep water and air off the iron. A scratch in the coat lets rust start. The second way is a bodyguard metal from higher on the metal ladder. A galvanised bucket has a coat of zinc. Zinc gives away electrons more easily than iron, so the zinc corrodes and the iron is spared. This works even at a scratch, as long as the zinc touches the iron. Steel ships and fishing boats carry blocks of zinc bolted to the hull for the same reason. The third way is to push electrons into the iron from a power supply. Long buried pipelines for gas and oil are protected like this. The fourth way is to change the metal. Stainless steel is iron with chromium added. The chromium forms a thin, tight skin of oxide that you cannot see, and it mends itself when scratched.
- e.g. Railings are painted again every few years to keep water off the iron.
- e.g. Tin roofing sheets are galvanised steel, and last for decades in the rain.
- e.g. A stainless steel tumbler holds water every day and does not rust.
### Living bodies move ions and pass electrons too · *defined*
**Definition:** Living cells work by moving ions across their walls and by passing electrons from food to oxygen.

Every nerve and muscle cell in humans and other animals pumps ions across the cell wall. This makes a difference of less than a tenth of a volt between the inside and the outside. A nerve signal is that difference changing, step by step, along the nerve. An electric eel has thousands of flat cells stacked in rows, like lemon cells joined in a row. Their small pushes add up. In 2019 scientists measured 860 volts from one kind of electric eel, and named that kind after Alessandro Volta. Electrons matter to every living being in another way. Your cells take electrons from the food you eat and pass them, in small steps, to the oxygen you breathe. The energy you live on comes from that passing. Some bacteria who live in mud with no air pass their electrons to rust in place of oxygen. They live by using rust the way we use air.
- e.g. A nerve cell holds a difference of about 0.07 volts across its wall.
- e.g. An electric eel stacks thousands of cells in a row to stun a fish.
- e.g. Bacteria called Geobacter pass electrons to rust in mud where there is no air.
### Rust, water and electrons on the Earth and in space · *defined*
**Definition:** The same passing of electrons shapes rocks, planets and spacecraft.

Iron ore is mostly iron joined to oxygen, which is the same kind of substance as rust. A steel plant uses great heat and carbon to pull the oxygen away and leave iron. Rusting puts the oxygen back. So a rusting gate is slowly going back to what it was in the ground. Mars looks red because its dust is full of iron oxides. On the International Space Station, a machine splits water by electrolysis to give the crew oxygen. The Apollo spacecraft that flew to the Moon carried fuel cells. These joined hydrogen and oxygen, gave electric current, and made water that the crew drank. On Earth, engineers now build large electrolysers that run on sunlight and wind. The hydrogen they make can be stored and used later as a fuel.
- e.g. The red hills of iron ore at Ballari in Karnataka and in Odisha are iron joined to oxygen.
- e.g. Mars is called the red planet because of iron oxide dust.
- e.g. The space station makes oxygen for breathing by splitting water.
### What will store the world's electricity? · *debated*
**Definition:** Sunlight and wind do not give power at every hour, so the electricity must be stored. Engineers disagree about the best way.

Solar panels make most of their electricity at midday, and people need most of it in the evening. Something must hold the energy in between. Each way of storing it has gains and costs, and working engineers back different ones.
- e.g. A phone stores energy in a lithium-ion battery.
- e.g. A hydrogen plant stores energy by splitting water.
- **Engineers who back lithium-ion batteries:** They give back about nine tenths of the energy put in, and they get cheaper every year. But lithium and cobalt must be mined, and that harms land and people.
- **Engineers who back sodium-ion batteries:** Sodium comes from common salt, so it is cheap and found everywhere. These batteries are heavier for the energy they hold, and they are still new.
- **Engineers who back hydrogen:** Hydrogen made by splitting water can be stored for months and can run ships and steel plants. But more than half the energy is lost on the way there and back.
### Did life begin at a natural battery? · *idea*
**Definition:** One idea is that life began at vents on the deep sea floor. There, rocks and sea water made a natural electrical difference.

On the deep sea floor there are vents where warm water full of minerals seeps out of the rock. The vent water is a base, and the early sea was slightly acid. Thin walls of minerals with iron and sulfur in them stood between the two. Some scientists think this made a natural difference, like the two sides of a cell. On this idea, the first living things used that difference for energy. Every cell still uses a difference across its wall today. Nobody has shown that life began this way. It is one idea among several.
- e.g. Warm, mineral-rich vents of this kind were found on the Atlantic sea floor in the year 2000.
- **Scientists who favour deep-sea vents, such as Michael Russell and Nick Lane:** Every living cell runs on a difference of ions across its wall. A vent offers that difference ready-made, so life may have started there.
- **Scientists who favour warm ponds on land:** The building blocks of life join up best when they dry out and get wet again. That happens in ponds on land, and it does not happen on the sea floor.

## Mostly agreed
- A battery gives a current because a chemical change inside it moves electrons from one substance to another. *How we know:* In the 1830s Michael Faraday measured how much zinc a cell lost. The amount of electricity always matched the amount of zinc used up.
- Two electrodes of the same metal in the same liquid give no current. *How we know:* Anyone can check this. Two copper wires in a lemon give no reading on a meter and no crackle in earphones.
- An electric current splits water into two parts of hydrogen gas and one part of oxygen gas. *How we know:* William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle first did this in London in May 1800. Every careful measurement since has given two to one.
- Iron rusts only where water and oxygen are both present. *How we know:* A nail in dry air stays grey. A nail in boiled water under a layer of oil stays grey. A nail in open water rusts.
- Rusting is a passing of electrons, like the change in a cell. The iron is eaten at one place and the oxygen takes electrons at another. *How we know:* In the 1920s Ulick Evans in Cambridge put drops of salt water on steel. The steel was eaten under the middle of each drop, and the oxygen took electrons at the edge.
- A metal higher on the metal ladder protects a lower one that it touches. *How we know:* Galvanised steel stays free of rust at a scratch. Zinc blocks on ships are eaten away while the steel hull beside them is not.

## How we found out
**Alessandro Volta, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle, Humphry Davy and Michael Faraday. Jane Marcet wrote Conversations on Chemistry, the book that first taught Faraday the subject., Volta described his pile in 1800, and Nicholson and Carlisle split water in May 1800. Faraday published his measurements in 1834..** In 1800 Alessandro Volta in Italy built the first battery. It was a pile of zinc and copper discs with wet card between them. Volta believed that the electricity came simply from two different metals touching. He thought the wet card only carried it, and that nothing was used up. News of the pile reached London within weeks. In May 1800, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle dipped its two wires into water. Bubbles rose from both wires. One gas was hydrogen and the other was oxygen. So electricity could cause a chemical change. Humphry Davy then argued the reverse: a chemical change inside the pile was making the electricity. He pointed out that the zinc discs were always eaten away. In the 1830s Michael Faraday settled the question by measuring. The amount of electricity a cell gave always matched the amount of zinc it lost. If touching alone made electricity, a battery would run for ever, and no battery does. Faraday had learned his first chemistry from a book by Jane Marcet.  
*What people believed before:* That two different metals make electricity just by touching, with nothing used up. Davy pointed out that the zinc is always eaten away. Faraday showed that the electricity a cell gives matches the zinc it loses.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A lemon battery gets its electricity from the lemon.** (Many books and videos call the lemon the source of power.; *rejected*)  
  For: Without the lemon nothing happens, and a dry, old lemon works badly.  
  Against: Two wires of the same metal in a lemon give nothing. The metals work just as well in salt water. The zinc is used up, and the lemon is only the path.
- **People made batteries in Iraq about 2,000 years ago.** (Wilhelm König, who worked at the national museum in Baghdad, suggested this in 1938.; *minority*)  
  For: A clay jar found near Baghdad held a copper tube with an iron rod inside. Copies filled with vinegar give a small voltage.  
  Against: No wires were found with the jar, and no object that the current could have been used on. Similar jars held rolled-up writings. Most archaeologists think this one was for storage.
- **Stainless steel never rusts.** (The name says so, and most people believe it.; *rejected*)  
  For: A stainless steel tumbler holds water for years and stays bright.  
  Against: The thin skin of chromium oxide can break down in salty water or sea air. Brown spots and small pits then form. Stainless steel resists rust well, but it can still rust.
- **The Iron Pillar of Delhi has hardly rusted in 1,600 years because of what its iron is made of.** (The metal scientist R. Balasubramaniam of IIT Kanpur, in studies published around the year 2000.; *accepted*)  
  For: The iron holds much more phosphorus than modern steel. He showed that this helps a thin, tight layer to form on the surface. The layer keeps water off the iron below.  
  Against: An older view is that Delhi's dry air is the main reason. Both probably help. The part of the pillar below the ground has rusted more.
- **Hydrogen made by splitting water will replace petrol and diesel.** (Many governments and companies. India began a National Green Hydrogen Mission in 2023.; *debated*)  
  For: Hydrogen can be made from water with sunlight, and using it gives back only water.  
  Against: Splitting water takes more energy than the hydrogen gives back. Hydrogen is hard to store. For cars, batteries waste much less energy. Hydrogen may suit ships, steel plants and fertiliser factories better.

## Roots
In this lesson you made a lemon cell crackle, rusted a nail in ten minutes and split water. Here we ask what it means that a useful battery and a rusting gate are the same change.

**Deep truth.** In the Lemon Battery test, the zinc nail gave away electrons and turned dull. In Rust in Ten Minutes, the nail that gave away electrons was eaten into rust. A rusting gate gives its electrons to oxygen in the same way. So a battery is a kind of corrosion in which the electrons are first sent through a wire.  
*Habit it breaks:* We put batteries with useful things that people make, and rust with damage and decay. They are one change. The only difference is whether the electrons pass through a wire, where they can do some work on the way.  
*Evidence:* In the Lemon Battery test, the earphones crackled only when a zinc nail and a copper wire shared the lemon. By the next day the zinc was dull and rough, and the copper was unchanged. In Rust in Ten Minutes, only the nail joined to the plus end of the cells turned to rust. That is the nail that the cells pulled electrons out of.

- *who am i:* Your cells take electrons from your food and pass them to the oxygen you breathe. You stay alive because electrons keep moving through you.
- *what is life:* Every living being, from a bacterium to a banyan tree, lives by passing electrons from one substance to another. A flame passes electrons from fuel to oxygen all at once. A living being does the same thing in many small steps.
- *what is this world:* A steel bridge is iron that people have pulled away from oxygen. The air and the rain slowly join the two again.

A battery, a rusting gate, a breathing child and a bacterium in mud look like four different things. In each of them, electrons leave one substance and go to another. The electron that lights an LED is the same kind of particle as the electron that rusts a nail. Many sages taught that one reality appears in many forms. The science here says something smaller and exact: several changes that we name separately are one kind of change.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.6:** “यथा सोम्यैकेन नखनिकृन्तनेन सर्वं कार्ष्णायसं विज्ञातं स्यात् / वाचारम्भणं विकारो नामधेयं कृष्णायसमित्येव सत्यम्” Uddalaka tells his son that by knowing one small tool of iron, all that is made of iron is known. The different forms are only names, and the truth is that all of them are iron. (The verse is about the one reality behind all things, and it uses iron as its picture. This lesson found a small case of it. 'Battery', 'electrolysis' and 'rust' are three names. The passing of electrons is what all three are.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 40:** “反者道之動。” The chapter begins by saying that returning is how the Way moves. (The text is about nature as a whole, and it is not about chemistry. Yet every change in this lesson could be turned back. Charging pushes a battery's change backwards. In Give It Back, the gases from split water joined again and made the earphones crackle. Rusting returns iron to what it was in the ground.)
- **Dhammapada, verse 277:** “Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā,” The Buddha says that all things that are put together are impermanent. (The verse is about how to live, and it is not about metals. Yet iron shows it plainly. Every iron thing is slowly going back to the oxide it was made from. Paint and zinc slow the return, and they do not end it.)
- Science (settled): Iron ore is iron joined to oxygen. Making steel separates the two, and rusting joins them again.
- Science (settled): Living cells get their energy by passing electrons from food to oxygen in small steps. Peter Mitchell proposed in 1961 how cells use this passing. Jennifer Moyle's experiments helped prove it. It is found in almost all living things.
- Science (idea): Life may have begun at deep-sea vents. Rocks and sea water there made a natural electrical difference, like the two sides of a cell.
- Other view, Aristotle, in his Physics, Book 2: To understand a thing, you must know what it is for, as well as what it is made of. A battery is made for a purpose, and rust is made for none. On this view, calling a battery and rust the same change leaves out what matters most about each.
- Practice, One breath, followed inwards (3 min): Sit still and hold a steel spoon in your closed hand. Breathe in slowly. The oxygen you just took in is travelling to every cell of your body. In each cell it takes electrons that came from your food. Breathe out slowly. Now feel the spoon. It has grown warm from your hand. Some of that warmth comes from electrons passing to oxygen inside you. The spoon is steel, and it would take years of rain to pass its electrons to oxygen. Which of you two is changing faster?
- Ask: In Rust in Ten Minutes, a nail turned to rust and lit nothing. Where did its energy go?
- Ask: A battery is used up as it works. A body rebuilds itself as it works. What makes that difference?
- Ask: If a battery and rust are one kind of change, why do people have two words for them?

## Story: The Grey Lumps Under the Boat
*Jenifer, Stella Periyamma, Xavier Mama and the two boats are made up, and so are the weights and the price. These facts are real. Fishing boats on India's east coast stay out of the sea for about two months from mid-April. Steel boats with bronze propellers carry blocks of zinc bolted to the hull. The zinc is eaten away and is replaced when the boat is hauled out. A zinc block must touch bare metal and must never be painted.*

Every April, when the fishing ban began, the trawler Kadal Rani came out of the sea. A winch pulled the boat up the slipway at Thoothukudi harbour, dripping, and for two months no net went into the water.

Jenifer was nine, and this year her aunt had given her a scraper. Stella Periyamma repaired boats. She could weld a split steel plate in the time it took the tea boy to cross the yard. Her rule for helpers was short. "Scrape first. Ask afterwards."

So Jenifer scraped. Barnacle shells came off the steel hull in grey crusts, and under them the red paint was whole. She worked her way along to the stern, where the bronze propeller hung, as yellow as a temple lamp.

Beside the propeller, two grey lumps were bolted to the hull. They were ugly. They were pitted like pumice stone and crusted with white, and one corner crumbled when she pushed it with the scraper.

"Periyamma! These two are rotten."

"They are," said her aunt, without looking up from her welding.

"The paint is fine. The propeller is fine. Only these are eaten."

"Take them off, then." A spanner slid across the concrete.

It took Jenifer a long time to turn the bolts. When the lumps came away, she carried them to the hanging scale by the store room, because her aunt weighed everything. Each lump weighed one kilogram. In the store room was a box of new ones. They were smooth and silver-grey, as neat as bars of soap. She weighed one of those too. Three kilograms.

She did the sum standing there. In one year, something had eaten two whole kilograms of metal off each lump.

"It is bad metal," she told her aunt. "The sea eats it. We should bolt on something strong. Steel. Or bronze, like the propeller. The sea has not touched the propeller."

"Hm," said Stella Periyamma. She pushed up her mask. "Take this tea to Xavier Mama on the next slipway. Look at his rudder while you are there. Then come and tell me again what to bolt on."

The next boat was called Annai. Xavier Mama stood under the stern with his hands on his hips. He took the tea and did not drink it.

The rudder of Annai was a sorry sight. The steel was full of pits, some as deep as Jenifer's fingertip. Along one edge there were holes right through it, and she could see daylight in them, as in a dosa. The heads of the bolts were worn thin and sharp.

"Last year I saved eight hundred rupees," said Xavier Mama. "I told your aunt: why should I pay every year for lumps that only rot? Leave them off." He tapped the rudder, and brown flakes fell on his foot. "Now I am buying a rudder."

Jenifer looked along the hull. Near the propeller there were four bolt holes with nothing bolted to them. The bronze propeller shone, without a mark on it.

She walked back slowly. Two boats had sat in the same sea for the same year. On one boat the lumps were eaten and the steel was whole. On the other there were no lumps, and the steel was eaten.

"The sea was going to eat something," she said to her aunt. "On our boat it ate the lumps. On his boat there were no lumps, so it ate the rudder."

"Go on."

"So the lumps are not bad metal. They are there to be eaten."

Stella Periyamma put down her torch. "They are zinc. Steel, bronze and zinc all sit in the same salt water, and all are bolted together. When metals touch like that, they do not corrode equally. One of them gives up its electrons first, and that one is eaten. The others are left alone for as long as it lasts. Zinc gives up electrons more easily than steel. Steel gives them up more easily than bronze."

Jenifer thought of the propeller on Annai, bright and whole beside the ruined rudder. "So on his boat the steel was the one."

"On his boat the steel did the zinc's job."

Jenifer fetched two new blocks from the box. Then she had an idea, and fetched the tin of red paint as well. "If I paint them," she said, "they will last two years."

Her aunt took the brush out of her hand, gently. "Paint the zinc, and the sea cannot reach it. Then it protects nothing, and the rudder goes." She scrubbed the hull around the bolt holes with a wire brush until the steel showed bright. "No paint under it either. Metal must touch metal, or the electrons have no road."

They bolted the blocks on to the bare steel, and Jenifer was allowed the last turn of the spanner on each. The new zinc looked too clean for a boat.

"Next April they will be ugly again," she said.

"I hope so," said Stella Periyamma. "The year they come up clean is the year I worry."

In June the ban ended. The winch let Kadal Rani down the slipway, and the water closed over the propeller and the two grey blocks. Jenifer watched until she could not see them. Nobody would look at them again for ten months, and they had already begun to be eaten.

**For the parent:** Two boats sat in the same sea. The boat with zinc blocks lost zinc and kept its steel. The boat without zinc lost its steel. When different metals touch in salt water, the one that gives up electrons most easily is eaten. The others are spared.

- Ask: The bronze propeller was unmarked on both boats. Why did the sea leave it alone?
- Ask: Why would a coat of paint on the zinc block stop it protecting the steel?
- Ask: Where in your home or street is zinc doing this job for iron?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Lemon Battery: which pairs of metals make a lemon crackle? (40 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Earphones crackle when electrons flow through them. Which makes them crackle: two copper wires in a lemon, two zinc nails in a lemon, or one of each? What happens with two lemon cells in a row?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which pair will make the earphones crackle: copper and copper, zinc and zinc, or copper and zinc? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same lemon half for the three pairs. Push every piece in 2 centimetres deep, and keep the two pieces 2 centimetres apart. Use the same earphones and the same listener, in a quiet room. Wipe the plug dry between tests. Only the pair of metals changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the lemons and strips the wire. The nails are sharp. Do not eat the lemons afterwards, because zinc and copper have gone into the juice. Wash your hands at the end.
- **record:** Make a table with six rows, one for each test. The first three rows are copper and copper, zinc and zinc, and copper and zinc. The last three are two cells in a row, two cells facing each other, and four cells in a row. Beside each, write 'none', 'faint' or 'loud'.
- **results table:** What is in the lemon | Crackle: none, faint or loud?
- **schedule:** day 1: Test the three pairs of metals, then two cells and four cells in a row. (35 min); day 2: Pull the zinc nail and the copper wire out of the lemon and compare them. (5 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay out the lemons, the zinc nails, the copper wires and the earphones. Touch the plug to a whole lemon so your child hears that nothing happens. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Earphones crackle when electrons flow through them. A lemon alone gives no crackle. We will push two pieces of metal into the lemon. Which pair will make a crackle: two copper wires, two zinc nails, or one of each? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the copper wires and zinc nails. Pick the pair you think will crackle.
  - Ask: Why do you think that pair will work? (good: Two different metals might behave differently.; Copper is used in wires, so two coppers should be best.; Any metal will do, because the lemon has the power.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children, and many adults, expect the lemon to be the source of the electricity.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Roll each lemon hard on the table to loosen the juice. Cut both in half. Put each half, cut side up, on its own lid. Strip four pieces of copper wire, 10 centimetres each, and twist the thin strands of each together.  
  *Parent says:* Each lemon half sits on its own lid, so that no juice joins one half to another. These nails are steel with a coat of zinc. We will call them zinc nails.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts the lemons and strips the wire. Put each half on a lid.
  - Note: Use only wire that is not joined to anything. If you use foil strips in place of zinc nails, the crackles are a little fainter.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Push two copper wires 2 centimetres deep into one lemon half, 2 centimetres apart. Put the earbuds in your child's ears. Press the end of one wire on the metal band next to the plug's tip. Scrape the tip on the other wire. Record the result. Then do the same with two zinc nails.  
  *Parent says:* Two copper wires are in the lemon. Hold one wire on the band next to the tip of the plug. Now scrape the tip on the other wire. What do you hear? Now we try two zinc nails.  
  *Child mode:* Put two copper wires in the lemon. Scrape the plug on one. Listen. Then try two zinc nails.
  - Note: Expect silence both times. A very faint tick can happen if one piece is dirtier than the other. Wipe both with the dry cloth and try again.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave one copper wire in the lemon half. Push one zinc nail in, 2 centimetres from it. Press the copper wire on the band next to the tip. Scrape the tip on the head of the nail. Record the result.  
  *Parent says:* Now the lemon holds one copper wire and one zinc nail. Scrape the tip of the plug on the nail. What do you hear this time?  
  *Child mode:* Now use one copper wire and one zinc nail. Scrape the plug on the nail. Listen.
  - Note: The crackle comes each time the tip touches the nail or leaves it. This is one lemon cell. If you hear nothing, push both pieces deeper into juicy flesh and check that they do not touch each other.
  - Check: Two copper wires gave no crackle. One copper wire and one zinc nail gave a crackle. What does that show? → **The two metals must be different**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the same cell in a second lemon half. Twist the copper wire of the first half round the nail of the second. Test from the first nail to the second copper wire. Then untwist it, twist the two copper wires together, and test from nail to nail.  
  *Parent says:* Now two cells are joined in a row, copper to zinc. Will the crackle be louder, quieter or the same? Listen. Now I join copper to copper, so that the two cells face each other. What do you expect?  
  *Child mode:* Join two lemon cells in a row. Is the crackle louder? Then join copper to copper. Listen again.
  - Ask: Why did the crackle vanish when the two cells faced each other? (good: Each cell pushed the electrons the opposite way.; The two pushes cancelled out.)
  - Note: In a row, the two pushes add and the crackle is clearly louder. Facing each other, the pushes cancel and there is silence or a very faint tick.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make all four halves into cells and join them in a row, copper to zinc each time. Test from the free nail to the free copper wire. If you have a red LED, darken the room. Touch the LED's long leg to the free copper wire and its short leg to the free nail.  
  *Parent says:* Four cells in a row. Listen first. Now the room is dark. The long leg of the LED goes to the copper end and the short leg to the zinc end. Look closely at the LED.  
  *Child mode:* Join four lemon cells in a row. Listen. In the dark, try the red LED.
  - Note: With zinc nails, four cells give about 3.6 volts and a red LED glows faintly in a dark room. With foil strips the glow may need six cells, or may not show. The LED lights only one way round, so turn it if it stays dark.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they heard, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below. Then twist the copper wire of one cell round its own nail and leave that lemon half until tomorrow.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you heard, and why you think it happened. Now we join this cell's copper to its own zinc, and leave it until tomorrow. Which piece of metal do you think will look different?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you heard. Say why.
  - Ask: The lemon was the same in every test. So what makes the electrons flow? (good: The two metals being different.; One metal gives electrons and the other does not.)
  - Note: The reason: zinc gives away electrons more easily than copper. Zinc atoms leave their electrons in the nail and go into the juice. The electrons flow through the wire and the earphones to the copper. Two pieces of the same metal push equally, so nothing flows. The energy comes from the zinc, and the lemon is the path.
  - Think: If the zinc is giving something away all night, what should the nail look like tomorrow?
- **s8 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The next day, pull the zinc nail and the copper wire out of the lemon half that was left joined. Wipe both. Compare the part of each that was in the juice with the part that stayed dry. Then throw all the lemon halves away.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the part of the nail that sat in the juice, and the part that stayed dry. Now look at the copper wire in the same way. Which metal changed?  
  *Child mode:* Pull out the nail and the wire. Which one looks different now?
  - Note: The part of the nail that sat in the juice is dull, dark and rough, and the copper is bright. A foil strip is thin and pitted, and may have holes. Some zinc also dissolves straight into the acid without sending electrons round the wire.
  - Check: After a night in a joined lemon cell, the zinc nail is dull and rough and the copper is unchanged. Why? → **Zinc atoms gave away electrons and left the nail**
  - Wait: Leave the joined lemon cell until the next day
### e2 · Rust in Ten Minutes: which nail do the cells rust? (25 min)
- **question:** Two steel nails stand in salt water, joined to the two ends of a pack of four AA cells. A third nail stands in salt water alone. Which nail changes most in a few minutes, and what does it turn into?
- **predict:** Before you join the wires, guess. Which nail will change most: the plus nail, the minus nail, or the nail standing alone? What will you see, and why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three nails of the same kind and size. Put the same amount of water and salt in both glasses. Put each nail 3 centimetres deep. Keep both glasses side by side for the same time. The only thing that changes is what each nail is joined to.
- **safety:** A grown-up builds the cell pack and joins the wires. Never let the two bare wire ends touch each other, because the cells get hot. Work near an open window and away from the stove. Do not taste the water. Never try this with a wall socket, a charger or an adapter.
- **record:** Make a table with rows for 1, 3 and 5 minutes. In each row, write what you see at the plus nail, the minus nail and the nail alone.
- **results table:** Minutes | Nail on the plus wire | Nail on the minus wire | Nail alone
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three nails, the two glasses and the cell pack. Explain which nail will go where. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* These three nails are the same. Two will stand in salt water, joined to the plus and minus ends of four cells. One will stand in salt water alone. Which nail will change most in five minutes? What will you see, and why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three nails go into salt water. Two are joined to cells. Point to the nail you think will change most.
  - Ask: Why did you choose that nail? (good: The plus end pulls electrons out, so that nail might be eaten.; The minus end has more electrons, so something will happen there.; The nail alone is in salt water, and salt makes rust.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Stir half a teaspoon of salt into each glass. Push two nails through the holes in the card and rest the card on one glass. The nails should reach 3 centimetres into the water and not touch. Lean the third nail in the other glass. Put the cells in the holder. Twist one wire round the head of each hanging nail, above the water.  
  *Parent says:* I am setting up the nails. The wires must stay above the water, so that only the nails are in it. I will not join the wires to the cells until you are ready to watch.  
  *Child mode:* Stir in the salt. A grown-up hangs the nails and fixes the wires.
  - Note: Write a plus sign and a minus sign on the card beside the two nails. Stand the white paper behind the glasses so the colours show.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Join the two wires to the plus and minus ends of the cell pack. Start the timer. Watch both glasses for five minutes. At 1, 3 and 5 minutes your child says what each nail is doing, and you write it down.  
  *Parent says:* The wires are joined. Watch the plus nail, the minus nail and the nail alone. Tell me everything you see at each one.  
  *Child mode:* Watch all three nails for five minutes. Say what each one does.
  - Note: Fine bubbles stream from the minus nail at once. The plus nail makes few bubbles. Within a minute or two a green-grey cloud sinks from it, and the water slowly turns yellow, then brown. The nail alone shows nothing.
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Take the wires off the cell pack. Lift out all three nails and lay them side by side on tissue paper. Let your child wipe each one and compare them.  
  *Parent says:* The cells are off. Wipe each nail with tissue. Which nail has changed, and which two look as they did before?  
  *Child mode:* Wipe the three nails. Which one changed? Which two stayed shiny?
  - Note: The plus nail is dark, rough and smeared with brown. The minus nail is as shiny as before, or shinier.
  - Check: In Rust in Ten Minutes, which nail turned dark and made a brown cloud? → **The nail on the plus wire**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below. Leave the cloudy glass for 15 minutes and look at the colour again. Then pour it down the sink and wash the glass with soap.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. What were the bubbles at the minus nail? Look at the cloudy water again in a quarter of an hour.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Ask: The nail alone sat in the same salt water. Why did that nail not rust in five minutes? (good: Nothing was pulling electrons out of it quickly.; The air does it slowly, over days.)
  - Ask: How could you use this to stop a steel pipe rusting? (good: Join the pipe to the minus end, so electrons are pushed into it.)
  - Note: The reason: the cells pull electrons out of the plus nail. Each iron atom that loses electrons becomes an ion and leaves the nail. - **record:** Make a table with three rows. The rows are before the cells, straight after 3 minutes of current, and after 1 more minute. Write the crackle and how long it lasted.

  - Note: The wire must touch the black lead, not the wood. Scrape a little wood away from the top lead if you need to.

  - Note: Fine bubbles rise from both tips. The tip on the minus wire makes about twice as many. That gas is hydrogen, and the gas at the plus tip is oxygen. With only two cells the bubbles are few and slow.
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Take both cell wires off and move the cell pack right away. Within five seconds, test the two copper wires with the earphone plug as before. Keep scraping, and count the seconds until the crackle fades. Record the result.  
  *Parent says:* The cells are gone. Only the pencils and the water are left. Scrape the plug now, and keep scraping. What do you hear? Tell me when it fades.  
  *Child mode:* The cells are gone. Scrape the plug at once. Count until the crackle fades.
  - Note: Expect a clear crackle that fades within about half a minute. Test quickly, because the gas on the leads is soon used up.
  - Check: In Give It Back, the cells were taken away and the pencils still made a crackle. What pushed the electrons? → **The two gases on the leads, joining again**
- **s5 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* When the crackle has gone, wait one minute and test again. Then join the cells for one more minute, take them away and test once more. Record both results.  
  *Parent says:* The crackle has faded. Does it come back if we simply wait? Now the cells go on for one more minute. Test again.  
  *Child mode:* Wait one minute and test. Then join the cells again and test.
  - Note: Waiting brings nothing back. One more minute of current brings the crackle back. So the crackle depends on the gases that the current makes.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they heard, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you heard, and why you think it happened.   
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you heard. Say why.
  - Ask: Where did the energy for the crackle come from? (good: From the AA cells, which split the water.; The cells put energy into the gases, and the gases gave it back.)
  - Note: The reason: the current split water into hydrogen at the minus lead and oxygen at the plus lead. That took energy from the cells. With the cells gone, hydrogen gives its electrons back through the earphones to the oxygen side, and water forms again. This is a small fuel cell. Some of the crackle also comes from charge held on the wet surface of the leads.
  - Think: A phone battery is charged, then used, then charged again. How is that like this experiment?

## Worksheets
### Cells, split water and rust (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What does a lemon cell need so that electrons flow through a wire?  
   Options: Two different metals in the juice / Two pieces of the same metal / A very sour lemon and nothing else / A lemon that has been warmed  
   **Answer:** Two different metals in the juice. *Why:* One metal must give away electrons more easily than the other. Two pieces of the same metal push equally, so nothing flows.
2. (fill) A liquid with ions in it, such as lemon juice or salt water, is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['electrolyte']. *Why:* The ions in an electrolyte carry charge through the liquid. Without them the loop is not complete.
3. (truefalse) The electricity of a lemon battery comes out of the lemon.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The energy comes from the zinc as it gives away electrons and is used up. The lemon only gives the juice that carries the ions. Salt water works in its place.
4. (match) Match each part of a lemon cell to its job.  
   **Answer:** The zinc nail → Gives away electrons; The copper wire in the lemon → Passes electrons on to the juice; The lemon juice → Carries ions between the metals; The wire outside the lemon → Gives the electrons a path. *Why:* Each part has one job. Take any one away and the loop is broken, so the current stops.
5. (mcq) In a lemon cell with zinc and copper, which metal is slowly used up?  
   Options: The zinc / The copper / Both equally / Neither  
   **Answer:** The zinc. *Why:* Each zinc atom that gives away electrons becomes an ion and leaves the nail. The copper only passes electrons on.
6. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how electrons travel in a lemon cell.  
   **Answer:** ['A zinc atom leaves two electrons in the nail', 'The electrons flow along the wire', 'They pass through the earphones', 'They reach the copper', 'Hydrogen ions in the juice take them']. *Why:* Electrons start at the metal that gives them and end at whatever takes them. The wire and the earphones are on the way.
7. (truefalse) Two lemon cells joined in a row push harder than one.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Cells joined copper to zinc add their pushes. Two cells of 0.9 volts give about 1.8 volts.
8. (fill) The unit that tells how hard a cell pushes electrons is the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['volt']. *Why:* An AA cell pushes with 1.5 volts. A lemon cell pushes with about 0.9 volts.
9. (mcq) Which two gases does an electric current split water into?  
   Options: Hydrogen and oxygen / Steam and air / Carbon dioxide and oxygen / Hydrogen and nitrogen  
   **Answer:** Hydrogen and oxygen. *Why:* A water molecule is two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. A current separates them. Steam is still water.
10. (sort) Sort these. Must each one be present for iron to rust?  
   **Answer:** iron → Needed for rust; water → Needed for rust; oxygen → Needed for rust; salt → Not needed for rust; sunlight → Not needed for rust; copper → Not needed for rust. *Why:* Rust forms when iron gives electrons to oxygen with water present. Salt only makes it faster.
11. (mcq) Why does a cycle rust faster near the sea than far inland?  
   Options: Salty, damp air carries ions well / Sea air is much colder / Sea air has no oxygen / Sand polishes the paint  
   **Answer:** Salty, damp air carries ions well. *Why:* Salt water is a good electrolyte. It lets the tiny cells on the iron's surface work faster.
12. (write) Two copper wires in a lemon give no crackle in earphones. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Both wires are the same metal, so both hold their electrons equally strongly. Neither gives electrons to the other, so no electrons flow.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a cell needs a difference between its two metals.
13. (write) Name two ways to stop an iron gate rusting. Say how each one works.  
   **Answer:** Paint keeps water and air off the iron. A coat of zinc gives away electrons in place of the iron. So the zinc corrodes and the iron does not.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two different ways and says what each does. One blocks water and oxygen, and the other gives electrons.
14. (draw) Draw a lemon cell. Label the zinc, the copper and the way the electrons flow.  
   **Answer:** A lemon with a zinc nail and a copper wire in it, joined outside by a wire. An arrow on the outside wire points from the zinc to the copper.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows two different metals and a complete loop. The arrow must run from zinc to copper through the outside wire.
15. (mcq) What is a galvanised bucket coated with?  
   Options: Zinc / Paint / Copper / Rust  
   **Answer:** Zinc. *Why:* Galvanised means coated with zinc. The zinc corrodes in place of the steel under it.
### Why it happens (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which pair is furthest apart on the metal ladder, and so pushes hardest in a cell?  
   Options: Magnesium and gold / Zinc and iron / Copper and copper / Tin and copper  
   **Answer:** Magnesium and gold. *Why:* Magnesium is at the top of the ladder and gold is at the bottom. Copper and copper are on the same rung and give no push.
2. (mcq) Four lemon cells give 3.6 volts, yet they cannot light a torch bulb. Why?  
   Options: Too few electrons pass each second / The volts are too high / Lemons contain no acid / The bulb is the wrong colour  
   **Answer:** Too few electrons pass each second. *Why:* Volts say how hard the cells push. A torch bulb also needs many electrons each second, and a lemon passes very few.
3. (mcq) When a current splits water, which gas comes off at the minus electrode?  
   Options: Hydrogen / Oxygen / Steam / Carbon dioxide  
   **Answer:** Hydrogen. *Why:* The cells push electrons into the minus electrode. Water molecules take them and split, and hydrogen bubbles off.
4. (truefalse) Charging a phone pushes the change inside its battery backwards.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The charger's current forces the change the other way. That is electrolysis inside the battery.
5. (truefalse) Stainless steel can never rust.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Its thin skin of chromium oxide can break down in salty water or sea air. Then brown spots and pits form.
6. (odd) Three of these stop rust by keeping water and air off the iron. Which one works differently?  
   Options: Paint / Oil / A plastic coat / A zinc block bolted on  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Paint, oil and plastic are barriers. A zinc block lets water reach the iron, and gives away electrons in the iron's place.
7. (count) One lemon cell gives 0.9 volts. How many cells joined in a row give 4.5 volts?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* Cells in a row add their volts. 0.9 added five times is 4.5.
8. (order) Put these metals in order, from the easiest giver of electrons to the strongest holder.  
   **Answer:** ['Zinc', 'Iron', 'Copper', 'Gold']. *Why:* This is their order on the metal ladder. It is why zinc protects iron, and why gold is found as metal.
9. (sort) Sort these. Does a chemical change make the current, or does a current force the change?  
   **Answer:** a lemon cell → A chemical change makes a current; an AA cell in a clock → A chemical change makes a current; a fuel cell in a bus → A chemical change makes a current; charging a phone → A current forces a chemical change; splitting water → A current forces a chemical change; coating a spoon with silver → A current forces a chemical change. *Why:* The first group are batteries, where a chemical change makes the current. The second group are electrolysis, where a current forces the change. Each one is the other run backwards.
10. (write) Two nails in salt water are joined to cells. Only the nail on the plus wire rusts. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The cells pull electrons out of the plus nail. Iron atoms that lose electrons become ions and leave the nail, and they form rust. The cells push electrons into the minus nail, so it loses none.. *Why:* A strong answer says that rusting is iron losing electrons, and that only the plus nail is losing them.
11. (write) A galvanised bucket gets a deep scratch. Explain why the bare steel in the scratch still does not rust.  
   **Answer:** The zinc around the scratch still touches the steel. Zinc gives away electrons more easily than iron, so the zinc corrodes and the iron keeps its electrons.. *Why:* A strong answer says that zinc protects by giving electrons, and not only by covering the steel.
12. (write) When water is split, twice as much hydrogen comes off as oxygen. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Each water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. So splitting water sets free two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom.. *Why:* A strong answer links the two-to-one amounts of gas to what a water molecule is made of.
13. (perspective) A town wants to store its midday solar power for the evening. People suggest different ways.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that splitting water and joining it again loses more than half the energy. Another is that every battery's metals must be mined.. *Why:* Each view is partly right. A strong answer uses what the unit says about batteries and electrolysis, and takes the other costs seriously.
14. (label) Label three parts of this AA cell cut in half: the zinc can, the black paste and the carbon rod.  
   **Answer:** ['The zinc can: the outer metal wall, which gives away electrons', 'The black paste: it holds manganese dioxide, which takes the electrons', 'The carbon rod: the dark rod down the middle, which collects electrons']. *Why:* A shop cell has the same parts as a lemon cell. It has a giver of electrons, a taker, and a paste with ions between them.
15. (mcq) A steel bolt sits in an aluminium window frame near the sea. Which metal corrodes first?  
   Options: The aluminium around the bolt / The steel bolt / Both at the same speed / Neither of them  
   **Answer:** The aluminium around the bolt. *Why:* Aluminium is above iron on the metal ladder. Where the two touch in salty damp air, the aluminium gives the electrons.
### Testing the idea (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Someone says the lemon is the source of a lemon battery's electricity. Which test checks this best?  
   Options: Try the same metals in salt water / Use a bigger lemon / Use a greener lemon / Squeeze the lemon harder  
   **Answer:** Try the same metals in salt water. *Why:* If the metals still give a current with no lemon at all, the lemon cannot be the source. The other tests all keep the lemon.
2. (mcq) A pile of six foil and copper cells crackles less than a pile of three. What is the likeliest fault?  
   Options: Wet paper is joining two cells together / Six cells always cancel out / The foil is too shiny / The earphones are worn out  
   **Answer:** Wet paper is joining two cells together. *Why:* Each cell must be separate. If liquid runs down the side and joins two cells, those cells stop adding their pushes.
3. (mcq) Engineers join a buried steel pipeline to one end of a power supply to stop rust. Which end?  
   Options: The minus end, which pushes electrons in / The plus end, which pulls electrons out / Either end works equally / Both ends at once  
   **Answer:** The minus end, which pushes electrons in. *Why:* Iron rusts by losing electrons. A pipe joined to the minus end is given electrons, so it loses none. A nail on the plus wire rusts in minutes.
4. (mcq) Iron is found in the ground only as ore, but gold is found as metal. Why?  
   Options: Gold holds its electrons far more strongly / Gold is heavier than iron / Iron formed more recently / Gold lies deeper down  
   **Answer:** Gold holds its electrons far more strongly. *Why:* Iron gives electrons to oxygen easily, so over time it all joins with oxygen. Gold is at the bottom of the metal ladder and stays as metal.
5. (truefalse) A cell with more volts always lasts longer.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Volts say how hard a cell pushes. How long it lasts depends on how much of its substances are left to use up.
6. (truefalse) When a steel plate rusts under a drop of water, the steel itself carries the electrons.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The electrons travel through the metal from the middle of the drop to its edge. The plate does the job of a wire.
7. (match) Match each change to the thing that gives away the electrons.  
   **Answer:** A lemon cell with zinc and copper → Zinc; A rusting gate → Iron; A fuel cell in a bus → Hydrogen; A cell in your body → Food. *Why:* In every one of these, electrons leave one substance and go to another. The only thing that differs is which substance gives the electrons.
8. (count) Split water gives 2 litres of hydrogen for each litre of oxygen. How much hydrogen comes with 7 litres of oxygen?  
   **Answer:** 14. *Why:* There is twice as much hydrogen as oxygen. 7 times 2 is 14.
9. (fill) The slow change of a metal as it gives electrons to oxygen is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['corrosion']. *Why:* Rust is the corrosion of iron. Copper, silver, zinc and aluminium corrode too, each in its own way.
10. (write) Two pencils in water crackle in earphones just after a current has split the water. What stored the energy?  
   **Answer:** The hydrogen and oxygen on the pencil leads stored it. The current used energy to split water into the two gases. When the gases joined again, they gave some of that energy back.. *Why:* A strong answer says the energy was stored in the separated gases, and not in the water as 'electricity'.
11. (write) Plan a fair test: does warm salt water rust a nail faster than cold salt water?  
   **Answer:** Use two nails of the same kind and two glasses with the same amount of water and salt. Keep one glass warm and one cold. Look at both after the same time and compare the rust.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the warmth, and keeps the nails, the salt, the water and the time the same.
12. (write) A boat owner paints the zinc blocks on a steel boat to make them last. Why is this a mistake?  
   **Answer:** The zinc protects the steel by corroding in its place. Under paint, the sea water does not reach the zinc, so it gives no electrons. Then the steel corrodes.. *Why:* A strong answer says the zinc works by being eaten, so saving the zinc gives up the steel.
13. (write) Write one way a torch cell and a rusting gate are the same, and one way they differ.  
   **Answer:** Same: in both, a metal gives away electrons to something else and is used up. Different: in the cell the electrons go through a wire and can light a bulb. In the gate they do no useful work.. *Why:* A strong answer names the passing of electrons as what they share, and the wire as the difference.
14. (perspective) A city must choose new buses to replace its diesel ones. People disagree about which kind.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that splitting water uses more energy than the hydrogen gives back. A good measurement is how far each bus goes on the same amount of solar power.. *Why:* Working engineers hold each of these views. A strong answer uses what electrolysis costs in energy, and suggests a fair comparison.
15. (draw) Draw a water drop on steel. Show where iron is eaten, where the electrons go and where rust forms.  
   **Answer:** A drop on a plate, with a pit under the middle of the drop. Arrows run through the metal from the middle to the edge. A ring of rust lies near the edge.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the eaten spot and the rust in different places, joined by electrons moving through the steel.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which pair, pushed into a lemon, makes electrons flow through a wire?  
   Options: A zinc nail and a copper wire / Two copper wires / Two zinc nails / Two plastic straws  
   **Answer:** A zinc nail and a copper wire. *Why:* The two metals must be different. Zinc gives away electrons more easily than copper, so electrons flow from zinc to copper.
2. (mcq) Why do two pieces of the same metal in a lemon give no current?  
   Options: Neither gives electrons more easily than the other / The same metal is too clean / The lemon only works with copper / Their electrons are too heavy  
   **Answer:** Neither gives electrons more easily than the other. *Why:* A current needs a difference. Two pieces of one metal sit on the same rung of the metal ladder, so there is no push.
3. (mcq) Where does the energy of a lemon battery come from?  
   Options: The zinc, as it is used up / The sourness of the lemon / The earphones / The air around the lemon  
   **Answer:** The zinc, as it is used up. *Why:* The zinc changes as it gives away electrons, and that change gives the energy. The lemon is the path, and salt water can replace it.
4. (mcq) What does a volt measure?  
   Options: How hard a cell pushes electrons / How long a cell lasts / How heavy a cell is / How many cells are in a pack  
   **Answer:** How hard a cell pushes electrons. *Why:* Volts tell the push. How long a cell lasts depends on how much of its substances are left.
5. (mcq) Three lemon cells of 0.9 volts each are joined in a row. What do they give together?  
   Options: 2.7 volts / 0.9 volts / 0.3 volts / 9 volts  
   **Answer:** 2.7 volts. *Why:* Cells in a row add their pushes. 0.9 added three times is 2.7.
6. (mcq) What is electrolysis?  
   Options: Using a current to force a chemical change / Rubbing two things to make charge / Melting a metal with heat / Mixing an acid with a base  
   **Answer:** Using a current to force a chemical change. *Why:* In electrolysis the current comes from outside and forces a change, such as splitting water. It is a battery's change run backwards.
7. (mcq) A current splits water. How do the amounts of the two gases compare?  
   Options: Twice as much hydrogen as oxygen / The same amount of each / Twice as much oxygen as hydrogen / Only hydrogen comes off  
   **Answer:** Twice as much hydrogen as oxygen. *Why:* A water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, so twice as much hydrogen is set free.
8. (mcq) Which three things together make iron rust?  
   Options: Iron, water and oxygen / Iron, salt and sunlight / Iron, oil and air / Iron, heat and paint  
   **Answer:** Iron, water and oxygen. *Why:* Iron gives electrons to oxygen, and water must be there for it to happen. Salt only speeds it up.
9. (mcq) Why does salt water rust iron faster than plain water?  
   Options: It carries ions well / It is heavier / It holds no oxygen / It scratches the iron  
   **Answer:** It carries ions well. *Why:* Rusting works like a tiny cell on the iron's surface. Salt water is a good electrolyte, so the cell works faster.
10. (mcq) How does a coat of zinc protect steel?  
   Options: Zinc gives electrons in place of the iron / Zinc is harder than steel / Zinc keeps the steel warm / Zinc soaks up the water  
   **Answer:** Zinc gives electrons in place of the iron. *Why:* Zinc is above iron on the metal ladder. While the two touch, the zinc corrodes and the iron is spared, even at a scratch.
11. (mcq) A test changes the metals, the liquid and the earphones all at once. What is wrong with it?  
   Options: You cannot tell which change mattered / It uses too many things / It is over too quickly / Nothing is wrong with it  
   **Answer:** You cannot tell which change mattered. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same. Then any difference in the result comes from that one thing.
12. (mcq) How is a rusting gate like a battery?  
   Options: In both, a metal gives away electrons / Both store charge to use later / Both need lemon juice / Both are made of zinc  
   **Answer:** In both, a metal gives away electrons. *Why:* Rusting and a battery are the same kind of change. In the battery the electrons pass through a wire on the way.

## Applications
- Steel boats and ships carry blocks of zinc on the hull. The zinc corrodes, and the steel is spared.
- Roofing sheets, buckets, lamp posts and fence wire are galvanised. Their coat of zinc keeps them free of rust for decades.
- Buried pipelines for gas, oil and water are joined to the minus end of a power supply. The current keeps them from losing electrons.
- Aluminium is made from its ore by electrolysis, using huge currents. Recycling a can saves most of that electricity.
- Gold-plated jewellery and shiny chrome taps get their thin coat of metal by electrolysis.
- Phones, electric scooters, e-rickshaws and electric buses all run on cells that are charged by pushing their change backwards.
- Spacecraft have used fuel cells, which join hydrogen and oxygen to give current and drinking water.

## Spotted around us
- Orange-brown streaks running down a wall below an iron window grill, most of all in towns by the sea.
- Dull grey roofing sheets with a pattern like frost on them. That pattern is the zinc coat.
- A white or green crust on the ends of old cells inside a TV remote.
- Green patches on an old brass lamp or a copper pot.
- An old iron pillar or iron gate standing in the open with very little rust, while a newer iron rod nearby is rusty.
- Painters at work on a steel railway bridge that crosses a sea channel.
- E-rickshaws and electric autos plugged in at a charging stand.

## Open questions
- **Can a good battery be made from common things such as sodium, iron and sulfur?** Lithium and cobalt are scarce, and mining them harms land and people. Batteries made from common elements exist, but so far they hold less energy or wear out sooner.
- **Can water be split cheaply without rare metals?** The best electrolysers use platinum and iridium, which are among the rarest metals on Earth. Many teams are testing cheaper materials, and none yet lasts as long.
- **How did the dust of Mars turn to iron oxide?** Some scientists think the iron rusted in water long ago. Others think it happened slowly in dry air. A study in 2025 found a mineral that forms in cold water, but the question is not closed.
- **Did life begin at a natural electrical difference on the sea floor?** Every living cell runs on a difference of ions across its wall. Deep-sea vents offer such a difference ready-made. Nobody has yet made life start in a laboratory copy of a vent.
- **Where exactly does the first pit of rust start on a smooth piece of metal?** Scientists can now watch metal surfaces almost atom by atom. They still cannot say in advance which spot will give way first.

## Connected maths
- A meter is used on lemon cells joined in a row. One cell gives 0.9 volts, two give 1.8 volts and three give 2.7 volts. What is the rule? How many lemon cells match a pack of three AA cells, which gives 4.5 volts? **Each cell adds 0.9 volts. Five lemon cells give 4.5 volts.**. The readings go up by 0.9 each time: 0.9, 1.8, 2.7. Carry on: four cells give 3.6 and five give 4.5. So five lemon cells push as hard as three AA cells. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- Three lemon cells in a row should give 2.7 volts. A meter shows only 0.9 volts. Each cell gives 0.9 volts when tested alone. What is the likeliest reason? **One of the three cells is joined the wrong way round.**. A cell that faces the other way pushes against the rest. So two cells add and one takes away: 0.9 plus 0.9 minus 0.9 is 0.9. If a cell were dead, the reading would be 1.8. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
- A lemon cell with zinc and copper gives 0.9 volts. A cell with kitchen foil and copper gives 0.5 volts. A red LED needs at least 1.8 volts. What is the smallest number of each kind of cell that will do? **Two zinc cells, or four foil cells.**. Two zinc cells give 0.9 plus 0.9, which is 1.8 volts. Three foil cells give only 1.5 volts, which is too little. Four foil cells give 2.0 volts. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- Bubbles are counted for 30 seconds while water is split. The minus pencil gives 24 bubbles and the plus pencil gives 12. How many times as many is that? At the same speed, how many would each give in 2 minutes? **Twice as many. In 2 minutes the minus pencil gives 96 bubbles and the plus pencil gives 48.**. 24 divided by 12 is 2. Two minutes is four lots of 30 seconds. 24 times 4 is 96, and 12 times 4 is 48. The count is still two to one, because water has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- The table shows rust scores for three nails in salt water over four days. Between which two days did the nail with copper rust fastest? On which day did the nail with zinc first show rust? What might have changed for the zinc that day? **The nail with copper rusted fastest between day 1 and day 2. The nail with zinc first showed rust on day 4, probably because its zinc was nearly used up.**. The copper nail's score went from 0 to 2 in the first day. That is the biggest jump in the table. The zinc nail stayed at 0 for three days and reached 1 on day 4. Zinc protects only while there is zinc left to corrode. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- A boat owner can bolt on new zinc blocks every year for 800 rupees. Without zinc, the steel rudder must be replaced every 3 years for 24,000 rupees. Which costs less over 6 years, and by how much? **The zinc costs 4,800 rupees and the rudders cost 48,000 rupees. The zinc is cheaper by 43,200 rupees.**. Six years of zinc is 6 times 800, which is 4,800 rupees. In 6 years the rudder is replaced twice, and 2 times 24,000 is 48,000 rupees. 48,000 minus 4,800 is 43,200. The rudders cost ten times as much. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)
