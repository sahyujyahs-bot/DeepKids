# Cleaning and saving water: why mud sinks, and how to waste less
`sci.water.saving-water.L3`

**Summary.** Muddy water holds tiny bits of clay that can float for a day or more, even though clay is heavier than water. A pinch of phitkari, called alum in English, makes those bits stick together into clumps that sink in minutes. City water plants clear river water the same way, then pass it through sand and add a little chlorine to kill germs. Clear water is not always safe, so drinking water still needs boiling or a good purifier. Clean water takes a lot of work to make, so it is worth saving. A home saves water by turning taps off, fixing leaks and using kitchen water again for plants and mopping.

**Intro.** *Stir a spoon of soil into a glass of water and leave it on the table. The sand sinks in seconds. But the water above it can stay cloudy all day. Clay is heavier than water, so why doesn't it sink?* Barbers and grandmothers across India keep a white crystal called phitkari, or padikaram in Tamil. In this unit you rub a little of it in muddy water and watch what happens to the cloudiness. You find out why the tiniest bits of dirt stay up, and how cities clean river water for millions of homes. Then you look at your own home and find where clean water is lost, and how to use it twice.

## Terms
- **Muddy water**: Water with tiny bits of soil, sand or clay floating in it, which make it cloudy. *e.g.* After heavy rain, the water in a river or a new puddle looks brown and muddy.
- **Settle**: To sink slowly to the bottom of still water. *e.g.* Leave a bucket of muddy water still, and sand settles to the bottom in a few seconds.
- **Clay**: Soil made of the very smallest bits, far too small to see one at a time. Wet clay is sticky. *e.g.* Red soil and multani mitti are full of clay. They make water cloudy for a long time.
- **Phitkari (alum)**: A white crystal that makes tiny bits in water stick together. Its English name is alum. In Tamil it is padikaram. *e.g.* Barbers rub phitkari on the face after a shave. Families have used it for hundreds of years to clear well and river water.
- **Floc**: A soft clump of tiny dirt bits that have stuck together in water. Flocs are big enough to sink. *e.g.* Rub phitkari in muddy water and, after a few minutes, you see little fluffy flocs drifting down.
- **Coagulation**: Making tiny bits in water stick together into clumps. Phitkari does this. *e.g.* A water treatment plant adds alum to river water. This coagulation makes the mud clump so it can settle out.
- **Filter**: Something with small gaps that lets water through but catches bits that are too big for the gaps. *e.g.* A tea strainer is a filter for tea leaves. A cotton cloth is a finer filter.
- **Sand filter**: A deep layer of sand that water trickles down through. Dirt is caught in the tiny gaps between the grains. *e.g.* Many cities pass their water through beds of sand as big as a room before it goes into pipes.
- **Germs**: Living beings far too small to see, such as bacteria and viruses. Some of them make people ill. *e.g.* Cholera is caused by a germ that lives in dirty water.
- **Chlorine**: A chemical added in very small amounts to drinking water to kill germs. *e.g.* City water sometimes smells a little like a swimming pool. That is the chlorine.
- **Purify**: To make water safe to drink by killing or removing the germs and harmful things in it. *e.g.* Boiling water for one minute purifies it of germs.
- **Conserve**: To use something carefully so that it lasts, and not waste it. *e.g.* Turning off the tap while you brush your teeth conserves water.
- **Reuse**: To use something again for another job. *e.g.* Water used to wash rice can water a potted plant.
- **Waste water**: Water that has been used and is now dirty, such as water from a bath, a sink or a toilet. *e.g.* Waste water from your flat runs down the drain pipe to a sewer.
- **Grey water**: Lightly used water from washing hands, bathing or washing vegetables. It has no toilet waste in it. *e.g.* Grey water from washing vegetables can water plants. Toilet water cannot.

## Explanations
### Why big bits sink fast and tiny bits stay up · *defined*
**Definition:** In still water, a bit of dirt that is heavier than water sinks. But the smaller the bit, the more slowly it sinks. The tiniest clay bits sink so slowly that they can stay floating for a day or more.

When something sinks, the water has to move out of its way, and that slows it down. A big sand grain is heavy for its size and pushes through quickly. A clay bit is so tiny that the water slows it almost to a stop. If a sand grain were as small as a clay bit, it would sink just as slowly. So size matters as much as what the bit is made of. That is why muddy water first drops its sand, then its silt, and keeps its clay floating.
- e.g. A shaken bottle of soil and water shows layers within a minute: small stones at the bottom, sand above them, then fine silt. The water on top stays cloudy.
- e.g. Dust in a sunbeam floats in the air for a long time, though dust is heavier than air. It is the same idea: tiny bits fall very slowly.
- e.g. After the monsoon, a lake can stay brown for weeks, because the fine clay is still floating in it.
### Why clay bits don't clump by themselves · *defined*
**Definition:** Each tiny clay bit carries a small electric charge on its surface. All the bits carry the same kind of charge, so they push each other away. They keep apart and never grow big enough to sink.

You can feel this kind of push with two balloons rubbed on your hair. They push each other away, because they carry the same kind of charge. Clay bits in water do the same thing on a tiny scale. Even when two bits drift close, they push apart again. So the water stays cloudy for a long time.
- e.g. Two balloons rubbed on your hair and hung side by side swing away from each other.
- e.g. Multani mitti stirred into water can keep it cloudy for days.
### How phitkari clears muddy water · *defined*
**Definition:** Phitkari, or alum, dissolves in water. It cancels the charges on the clay bits and forms a sticky jelly that catches them. The bits stick together into flocs. Flocs are big enough to sink. This is called coagulation.

Rub a lump of phitkari in muddy water for a few seconds and wait. After a few minutes, you see small fluffy clumps. These are flocs. They grow and drift down. Within about half an hour, much of the mud lies on the bottom and the water above is clearer. Nothing has been taken away. The same clay is still in the glass, joined into fewer, bigger clumps. Too much phitkari makes the water sour and is not good to drink, so people use only a little.
- e.g. In many Indian villages, people rub a lump of phitkari around the inside of a water pot, then wait for the mud to settle.
- e.g. Water treatment plants in Indian cities add alum, or a similar chemical, to river water as the first cleaning step.
- e.g. In a glass of muddy water with phitkari, the flocs look like tiny bits of brown cotton wool.
### How a sand filter cleans water · *defined*
**Definition:** A sand filter is a deep layer of sand that water trickles down through. The gaps between the sand grains are tiny. Bits of dirt get stuck in the gaps or cling to the grains.

Water takes a twisting path through millions of tiny gaps. Many small bits get caught on the way. In a slow sand filter, a thin layer forms on top of the sand. It is full of tiny living beings, such as bacteria and other microbes. They eat many of the germs in the water. Engineers call this layer by its German name, schmutzdecke, which means 'dirt cover'. So the slimy top of the filter does much of the cleaning. Every few weeks, workers scrape off the top layer, and the living layer grows again.
- e.g. James Simpson built a large slow sand filter for a water company in London in 1829.
- e.g. Under a river bank, river water seeps through sand and gravel. Wells dug near rivers often give clearer water than the river itself.
### How a city cleans its water · *defined*
**Definition:** A city water plant cleans river or lake water in steps. First it adds alum or a similar chemical so the mud clumps. Next the water sits still in big tanks so the clumps settle. Then it trickles through sand filters. Last, a little chlorine is added to kill germs that are left.

Each step catches what the step before missed. Clumping and settling take out most of the mud. The sand catches smaller bits. Chlorine kills germs that are too small for the sand to catch. A little chlorine stays in the water, so it keeps killing germs on the long trip through the pipes. Then the water is pumped to tanks and pipes across the city. Bengaluru's water comes from the Kaveri river, about 100 kilometres away, and has to be pumped uphill to reach the city. All that work is why clean tap water is precious.
- e.g. Chennai, Hyderabad, Bengaluru, Kochi and other cities each run large water treatment plants.
- e.g. A faint swimming-pool smell from the tap means a little chlorine is still working in the water.
### Clear is not the same as safe · *defined*
**Definition:** Clumping, settling and filtering make water clear. But germs are far too small to see, and many can pass through cloth or a home sand filter. To make water safe to drink, it must also be purified.

Water that looks clear can still hold germs, and things dissolved in it, like salt. After using phitkari or a filter, families boil drinking water for at least one minute, or use a purifier that kills germs. Clearing first still helps, because boiling and purifiers work better on clear water. In this unit, never drink the water from any test.
- e.g. A glass of clear well water can carry germs that cause diarrhoea.
- e.g. Boiling water for one minute kills the germs that cause cholera and typhoid.
### Old ways of clearing water in India · *defined*
**Definition:** Long before machines, people in India cleared muddy water with phitkari and with seeds. The kataka seed, called thetran kottai in Tamil and nirmali in Hindi, is rubbed inside a wet clay pot. It makes the mud clump and settle, like phitkari does.

Old Ayurvedic books, such as the Sushruta Samhita, name the kataka seed as a way to clear water. In parts of South India some families still use it. In Sudan, in Africa, women cleared Nile river water with crushed seeds of the moringa tree. That is the same drumstick tree whose pods go into sambar. In the 1970s and 1980s, a German scientist, Samia Al Azharia Jahn, studied how the Sudanese women did it. Scientists now test moringa seeds as a low-cost way to clear water.
- e.g. A clay pot with a kataka seed rubbed on its inside wall.
- e.g. Dried drumstick seeds, crushed into a powder and stirred into muddy water.
### Is alum in drinking water safe? · *debated*
Water plants use alum, and a tiny amount of aluminium can stay in tap water. Most health scientists say the small amounts in properly treated water are safe. Some people still worry, and scientists keep checking. Everyone agrees that using too much alum is wrong, and that plants must measure their dose carefully.
- e.g. The World Health Organization gives a guide value for how much aluminium treated water should have, and water plants test for it.
- e.g. At home, people rub only a little phitkari and let the water settle before using it.
- **Most health scientists:** The small amount of aluminium in well-treated water is safe. We take in far more aluminium from food than from water.
- **Some scientists and many worried people:** In the 1980s, a few studies suggested aluminium might be linked to memory illness in old age. Later studies did not show that clearly. They want plants to keep the amount as low as possible.
### Where water is lost at home · *defined*
**Definition:** Most water wasted at home goes down the drain without doing any job. It runs while nobody is using it, leaks from taps and toilets, or overflows from tanks.

A running tap pours out many litres every minute. If it runs while you scrub a plate or brush your teeth, most of that water does nothing. A small leak in a flush or a tap runs all day and all night. Many building tanks overflow when the motor is left on. The water you wait through for the hot water to come is clean too. Much of this water can be saved by turning taps off, fixing leaks and catching water in a bucket.
- e.g. Scrape leftover food into the bin before washing a plate, so you need less water to rinse it.
- e.g. Catch the cold water from the shower or the geyser pipe in a bucket while you wait for hot water. Use it for the toilet or the mop.
- e.g. A plastic bottle filled with water and put in an old large toilet tank makes each flush use less.
### Using water twice: grey water · *defined*
**Definition:** Grey water is water that has been lightly used, such as water from washing vegetables, rinsing rice, washing hands or bathing. It can be used again for jobs that don't need clean water.

Water from washing rice, dal or vegetables is good for plants. Soapy bath or hand-washing water can be used for flushing the toilet or mopping. Water with a lot of oil, food or strong cleaners should go down the drain. Toilet water is never reused at home, because it carries germs. Some buildings in Bengaluru and Chennai now clean their grey water in small plants and use it for gardens and flushing.
- e.g. Rice-washing water poured into the tulsi pot.
- e.g. The last rinse water from the washing machine, caught in a bucket and used to wash the balcony.
### Why home RO purifiers throw water away · *defined*
**Definition:** An RO purifier pushes water through a very fine sheet that lets water through but holds back salts and germs. The salts left behind are washed away with some of the water. This is called reject water.

RO stands for reverse osmosis. Many home RO purifiers send away two or more litres of reject water for each litre of drinking water, though newer ones waste less. Reject water is salty, so it is not good for plants every day. It is fine for mopping, washing vehicles or flushing the toilet. If your tap water is not salty, a purifier that kills germs without RO may be enough. A water test tells you which you need.
- e.g. Many families put a bucket under the RO's reject pipe and use the water for mopping.
- e.g. Some RO machines show how much water they have purified, so you can compare it with the reject bucket.
### Why save water if the Earth has so much? · *defined*
**Definition:** About 97 out of every 100 litres of water on Earth is salty sea water. Most of the fresh water is frozen in ice or deep underground. Only a small part is in rivers, lakes and the ground near the surface, where people can easily use it.

Water is not used up. It goes round and round in the water cycle. But clean fresh water, in the right place at the right time, is limited. Cities spend a lot of energy cleaning and pumping it. In India, most fresh water goes to farming, so the food we eat carries a lot of water too. Saving water at home matters, and so does wasting less food.
- e.g. Chennai ran very short of water in 2019, when its lakes nearly dried up.
- e.g. Growing rice takes a lot of water, so throwing away a plate of rice also throws away water.

## Mostly agreed
- Tiny bits of clay stay floating for a long time because they are so small, and because their same charges keep them apart. *How we know:* Settling speeds have been measured in labs for over a hundred years. Anyone can watch muddy clay water stay cloudy for a day.
- Alum makes tiny bits in water clump and settle. *How we know:* Water engineers test this every day in 'jar tests', where they add different amounts of alum to jars of river water and watch them clear.
- Filtering through sand removes much of the dirt and many germs from water. *How we know:* Cities with slow sand filters, like Altona in 1892, had far less cholera than cities drinking the same river water unfiltered.
- Water must be boiled, chlorinated or purified to be safe to drink, even when it looks clear. *How we know:* Germs are too small to see. Tests on clear water often find germs, and boiling or chlorine kills them.
- Turning off taps, fixing leaks and reusing grey water save a large share of the water a home uses. *How we know:* Families who measure their water before and after these changes see their use fall.

## How we found out
**The people of Hamburg and Altona in Germany, the scientist Robert Koch, and Max von Pettenkofer, who disagreed, 1892, with Altona's sand filters working since 1859.** In the 1800s, many doctors believed cholera came from bad air rising from dirty ground. In Germany, the famous scientist Max von Pettenkofer said the cholera germ could not make people ill without the right kind of soil. So the city of Hamburg had not built filters for its drinking water. It pumped water straight from the river Elbe. Next door was the town of Altona. Altona took its water from the same river, further down, after Hamburg's sewage had flowed into it. But since 1859, Altona had passed its water through slow sand filters. In 1892 cholera struck Hamburg. About 8,600 people died in a few weeks. Altona, breathing the same air and standing on the same ground, had far fewer cases. On some streets along the border, homes on the Hamburg side fell ill, while homes across the road, on Altona's water, mostly did not. Robert Koch, who had found the cholera germ, used this to show that cholera travels in water. Pettenkofer even drank cholera germs to prove he was right. He got only a mild illness, but the evidence was against him. Hamburg built its own sand filters and opened them in 1893.  
*What people believed before:* Many doctors, led in Germany by Max von Pettenkofer, believed cholera needed bad air or the right soil, so clean drinking water did not matter much. Altona, with filtered water from the same river, stayed mostly safe next to Hamburg. That showed cholera spreads through water, and that filtering stops much of it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If water looks clear, it is clean and safe to drink.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Muddy water often does make people ill, and clear water often does not.  
  Against: Germs and dissolved salts or chemicals are invisible. Clear water can carry cholera. Only purifying makes it safe.
- **Heavy things always sink fast, so if dirt is still floating, it must be lighter than water.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Big stones sink fast and leaves float.  
  Against: Clay is heavier than water, yet its tiny bits float for a day or more. Size slows sinking. Clumped, the same clay sinks in minutes.
- **Saving a bucket of water at home makes no difference, because farms use most of the water.** (Some people, when asked to save water; *debated*)  
  For: In India, most fresh water does go to farming.  
  Against: City water is cleaned and pumped at great cost, and it is short in summer. A family saving 50 litres a day saves over 18,000 litres a year. Many families together save a lot.
- **Every home needs an RO purifier.** (Many advertisements; *debated*)  
  For: RO removes salts and germs, and some tap water in India is salty or has harmful salts.  
  Against: Where water is not salty, a purifier that kills germs, or boiling, is enough. RO also throws away a lot of water and some useful minerals.
- **Cholera spreads through bad air from rotting rubbish.** (The 'miasma' idea, held by many doctors until the late 1800s; *rejected*)  
  For: Cholera was worst in smelly, crowded places.  
  Against: Smelly places also had the dirtiest water. In 1892, Altona's filtered water kept cholera low next to Hamburg, with the same air.

## Roots
We have learned why tiny mud bits stay floating, how phitkari makes them sink, and how a home can waste less clean water. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Without phitkari, your muddy water stayed cloudy for hours, though clay is heavier than water. Tiny clay bits sink very slowly, and their electric charges push them apart. Phitkari let them stick into clumps, and the clumps sank within half an hour. So whether dirt sinks depends on the size of its pieces, not only on what it is.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things into 'heavy things that sink' and 'light things that float', as if only the material decides. But the same clay floated as tiny bits and sank as clumps. How big the pieces are matters just as much.  
*Evidence:* In the Phitkari Test, glass A stayed cloudy for hours. Glass B, with the same clay and the same stirring, grew fluffy flocs that sank, and the water cleared in about half an hour. Nothing was taken out of glass B. The mud was all still there, on the bottom.

- *who am i:* Your kidneys clean your blood all day, passing it through millions of tiny filters. You are a water cleaner too.
- *what is life:* Tiny living beings in sand filters and sewage plants eat the germs and dirt in water. Whole cities depend on their work.
- *what is this world:* The Earth has no new water. The same water goes round and round, and rocks, sand, sun and living beings keep cleaning it.

In muddy water, millions of clay bits each keep apart, because each carries the same charge. While they stay apart, none of them can sink, and the water stays cloudy. Phitkari cancels the charges, and the many bits join into a few big clumps. The clay is the same, and the water is the same. Only the way the bits are held together has changed, and the whole glass looks different.

- **Tao Te Ching 15:** Who can make muddy water clear? Let it be still, and it slowly becomes clear. (Stillness lets sand and silt settle, as in the shaken bottle. But your glass A showed that stillness alone is very slow for the tiniest clay bits. Phitkari helped them do in half an hour what stillness takes days to do.)
- **Anguttara Nikaya 1.45–46, in the Pali Canon of the Buddha's teachings:** In a muddy, stirred-up pool, a person on the bank cannot see the shells, the pebbles and the fish. In a clear, still pool, they can see them all. The Buddha said a stirred-up mind is like the muddy pool, and a settled mind like the clear one. (In the word test, you could not read the word through cloudy water, but once the mud settled you could read it clearly. The water was the same. What changed was what was floating in it.)
- **Jain tradition: straining drinking water through a cloth, still done in many Jain homes:** Many Jains strain their water through a clean cloth before using it, so that tiny living beings in it are not swallowed or harmed. The strained-out beings are returned to water like the water they came from. (A sand filter also catches tiny living beings, and some of them live on in the filter's living layer, cleaning the water for us.)
- Science (settled): Small particles sink far more slowly than large ones. Halve the size, and a particle sinks about four times more slowly.
- Science (settled): Clay particles carry electric charges on their surfaces, and alum neutralises these so the particles clump.
- Science (open): Which of the thousands of microbes in a sand filter's living layer do most of the cleaning is still being studied.
- Other view, Many scientists: Clay bits clump because of electric charges, and that is all. It is a useful fact for cleaning water, but it carries no lesson about people coming together. Reading lessons into mud says more about us than about the mud.
- Practice, Watch the water settle (3 min): Let's sit beside the glass from the Phitkari Test. Stir it gently once, then put the spoon down. Now we sit very still and watch, and breathe slowly. Watch the flocs drift down. Notice your breath getting slower too. After about two minutes, look through the water. What can you see now that you couldn't see before?
- Ask: A clay bit in glass A was falling, but too slowly to see. Was it floating, or falling?
- Ask: The mud in glass B was all still there, only gathered at the bottom. Was the water cleaned, or was the mud only moved?
- Ask: Rocks, sand, sun and tiny living beings have cleaned your water many times. Who cleaned it before it reached you?

## Story: The Brown Tap
*Phitkari (padikaram) and the kataka seed (thetran kottai) are really used to clear water in South India, and clear water really still needs boiling. Meenakshi, her family and this flat in Madurai are made up.*

It was the hottest week of May in Madurai. The Vaigai river was almost dry, and the corporation water came only every second morning.

Meenakshi was in Class 3. She lived on the third floor with her Appa, her Amma and her grandmother, Kamala Paati.

On Tuesday the water came at six in the morning, as it always did. But this time it came out of the tap brown.

Appa held up a steel tumbler of it. 'Look at this! It's like filter coffee.'

The watchman called up from below. 'A big pipe burst on the main road. They mended it last night. Mud got in.'

Appa frowned. 'The whole tank on the terrace is full of this now. We'll have to empty it down the drain and call a tanker.'

Amma shook her head. 'A tanker costs a lot in May. And who knows when the next one will come?'

Meenakshi looked at the tumbler. Tiny brown specks were spinning slowly in the water. A few grains of sand had already dropped to the bottom. The rest of the water stayed brown.

Kamala Paati came out of the kitchen with her old tin box. 'Before we pour away a whole tank of water,' she said, 'let me try something.'

She opened the box. Inside was a lump of white crystal, like a piece of frosted glass.

'Padikaram!' said Meenakshi. 'The barber uses that after a shave.'

'Yes,' said Paati. 'And in my village, near the river, we used it every day. River water came brown after the rains. My mother rubbed padikaram inside the pot, or a thetran kottai seed, and the mud went down.'

Appa laughed. 'Amma, that's an old village trick. This is city water.'

Meenakshi had an idea. 'Let's test it, Appa. Two tumblers of the same brown water. Padikaram in one, nothing in the other.'

She filled two steel tumblers right to the top. Paati held the crystal and swirled it in the first tumbler, ten slow times round. Then she lifted it out. Meenakshi stirred the second tumbler ten times with a spoon, so that both had the same stirring.

'Now nobody touches them,' said Paati.

They sat on the floor and watched. For a few minutes, nothing seemed to happen. Then Meenakshi bent close to the first tumbler.

'Paati, there are little fluffy things in it! Like tiny bits of brown cotton.'

The fluffy bits grew bigger and began to drift down, like slow brown snow. Appa put down his phone and came to look.

After half an hour, the first tumbler had a soft brown layer at the bottom. The water above it was clear enough to see the steel shining through. The second tumbler was still brown all the way down.

'But both had the same mud,' said Appa. 'Why did one sink and not the other?'

Meenakshi thought about it. 'The mud bits were too tiny to sink on their own. They just kept floating. The padikaram made them stick together into clumps. Clumps are big, so they sink.'

'So the mud is still there,' said Appa slowly. 'It's just sitting at the bottom now.'

'Yes,' said Paati. 'Nothing has gone. It has only gathered.'

That afternoon, Appa and Meenakshi filled every bucket in the house from the tank. Paati swirled the padikaram in each one. By evening, every bucket had clear water on top and mud at the bottom.

Appa dipped a tumbler into the clear water. 'Now we can drink it.'

'No, Appa!' said Meenakshi. 'Clear is not the same as clean. Germs are too small to see. The pipe was broken, so germs could have got in too.'

Paati nodded. 'Clear water is ready for boiling. It is not ready for drinking.'

So Amma boiled the drinking water, and let it cool in the clay pot. They used the clear bucket water for cooking, bathing and washing.

At the end, there were only a few cups of thick mud left at the bottom of the buckets. Meenakshi did not pour them down the drain. She stirred them into the soil of the jasmine pot on the balcony.

Appa watched her. 'Only one small bowl of mud. We nearly threw away the whole tank because of that.'

Two days later, the water came clear again. But Appa kept a lump of padikaram on the kitchen shelf, just in case.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the mud in the tank was made mostly of tiny clay bits. They were heavier than water, but so small that they sank too slowly to see. Their same electric charges also kept them apart. Padikaram, which is alum, cancels those charges and makes the bits stick together into fluffy clumps called flocs. The flocs are big enough to sink, so the water above clears. No mud was removed: it only gathered at the bottom. Because germs may have got in through the broken pipe, the clear water still had to be boiled before drinking. By clearing the water instead of throwing it away, the family saved a whole tank.

- Ask: What were the fluffy brown things Meenakshi saw?

- Ask: Paati knew this from her village, and Appa thought it was only an old trick. How did the family find out who was right?

### g1 · Every Drop Counts: a board game of saving water (20 min)
- **rules:** Each player starts with 10 drops and a counter on START.; On your turn, roll the dice and move. ## Experiments
### e1 · The Phitkari Test: can a crystal clear muddy water? (35 min)
- **question:** Two glasses hold the same muddy water. If we rub phitkari in one and not the other, will one clear faster?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will rub phitkari in one glass of muddy water and only stir the other. Which glass will be clearer after 30 minutes? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both glasses get the same water, the same amount of soil and the same stirring. Only the phitkari changes. That way, any difference must come from the phitkari.
- **safety:** Nobody drinks any of the test water. Phitkari is safe to touch, but wash your hands after, and keep it away from eyes and mouths.
- **record:** Fill in the table at 0, 5, 15 and 30 minutes, and once more 2 hours later. Draw both glasses at 30 minutes, showing the clear part and the mud.
- **results table:** Time | Glass A, no phitkari: can you read the letters? | Glass A: height of clear water on top (cm) | Glass B, with phitkari: can you read the letters? | Glass B: height of clear water on top (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill a jug with 2 glasses of tap water. 2. Stir in the soil or multani mitti for one whole minute. 3. Pour half into each glass. 4. Label them A and B.  
  *Parent says:* We are making one batch of muddy water, then splitting it. That way both glasses start exactly the same.  
  *Child mode:* Stir the soil into the jug for a whole minute. Pour half into each glass. Call them A and B.
  - Note: Potting mix that is mostly cocopeat floats and makes brown water that phitkari clears less well. Red soil, garden soil or multani mitti gives the best cloudy water, because they are full of clay. Multani mitti is sold cheaply at most grocery and medical shops.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the paper with the big word behind each glass. 2. Can your child read the word through the water? 3. Write 'no' or 'yes' for both glasses at 0 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Can you read the word through the water? This is how we will measure how clear each glass is.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the paper behind each glass. Can you read the word? Write yes or no.
  - Ask: Why do we look at the word through both glasses, and not just say which looks clearer? (good: So we measure the same way each time; It is fairer than guessing)
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a guess and a reason before adding the phitkari. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* I will rub phitkari in glass B. I will only stir glass A. Which will be clearer in 30 minutes? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess! Which glass will be clearer in 30 minutes? Why? Write it down.
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the phitkari lump and swirl it in glass B, round and round, for 10 seconds. Lift it out. 2. Stir glass A for 10 seconds with a clean spoon. 3. Put both glasses side by side and don't touch them again.  
  *Parent says:* Glass A gets the same stirring as glass B. So if B clears faster, it can only be because of the phitkari.  
  *Child mode:* Swirl the phitkari in glass B for 10 seconds. Stir glass A for 10 seconds too. Now don't touch them!
  - Note: If you only have powder, stir a small pinch, held between finger and thumb, into glass B instead. More is not needed.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 5 minutes, look closely at glass B from the side, near a window.  
  *Parent says:* Look very closely at glass B. Can you see anything new in the water?  
  *Child mode:* Look closely at glass B. What can you see in the water now?
  - Note: Small fluffy clumps, called flocs, usually appear within a few minutes and start drifting down. Glass A looks the same as at the start. If nothing happens in B after 10 minutes, rub the phitkari for another 10 seconds.
- **s6 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At 15 and at 30 minutes: 1. Do the word test on both glasses. 2. Measure the height of the clear water on top with a ruler. 3. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's read the word again, and measure how much clear water is on top of each glass.  
  *Child mode:* Read the word through each glass. Measure the clear water on top. Write it in the table.
  - Check: At 30 minutes, glass B has mud on the bottom and clear water on top. Glass A is still cloudy. What made the difference? → **The phitkari made the tiny mud bits stick into clumps that sank**
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw, and why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: phitkari makes muddy water clear. What I saw: in glass B, fluffy clumps formed and sank, and I could read the word after 30 minutes. Glass A was still too cloudy. Why: the phitkari made the tiny mud bits stick together, and big clumps sink. Look at glass A again 2 hours later, or before bed. It is often still cloudy.
  - Think: The mud in glass B is all still there, on the bottom. So what really changed?
### e2 · The Plate Test: which way of washing wastes least water? (30 min)

  *Child mode:* Which way used least? Were all the plates clean? Why?
  - Note: In most homes, way 1 uses several times as much as way 3, but your numbers depend on your tap. Any result is a real result. If two ways come out the same, try them once more, because one try can be a fluke.

### e3 · The Leaf Blanket: does covering the soil save water? (25 min, 3 days)
- **question:** If we cover the soil in a pot with dry leaves, does it stay wet longer than bare soil?
- **predict:** Guess first. Two pots of wet soil sit in the sun. One has a blanket of dry leaves on top. Which will dry out first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two pots or steel tumblers of the same size, filled with the same soil and the same amount of water, in the same sunny spot. Only the leaf cover changes.
- **safety:** Wash your hands after touching soil.
- **record:** Fill in the table each day. On day 3, draw what the soil looked like when you tipped each pot out.
- **results table:** Day | Bare pot: top feels wet or dry? | Bare pot: wet 2 cm down? | Covered pot: top feels wet or dry? | Covered pot: wet 2 cm down?
- **schedule:** day 1: Fill both pots with the same soil, water them the same, and cover one with dry leaves. Put both in the sun. (10 min); day 2: Feel the top of both, then push a finger 2 cm down. Is it wet or dry? (5 min); day 3: Check again, then tip both out on newspaper and compare. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill both pots with soil, pressed down the same. 2. Pour half a mug of water into each, slowly. 3. Cover the soil in one pot with a layer of dry leaves about 2 cm thick. 4. Put both in the same sunny spot.  
  *Parent says:* Both pots get the same soil and the same water. Only one gets a leaf blanket. Which do you think will stay wet longer, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Fill both pots. Water both the same. Cover one with dry leaves. Put both in the sun. Which will stay wet? Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The next day, feel the top of the bare soil. Lift the leaves and feel the top of the covered soil. Then push a finger 2 cm into each.  
  *Parent says:* Feel the top of each. Then push your finger in, about as deep as your fingernail is long, twice. Wet or dry?  
  *Child mode:* Feel the top of each pot. Push a finger in. Wet or dry?
  - Wait: Check the next day.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, check again. Then tip both pots out onto newspaper and look at the soil inside.  
  *Parent says:* Let's tip them out. Which soil is darker? Darker soil is wetter. What did the leaves do?  
  *Child mode:* Tip both pots out. Which soil is darker and wetter? Why?
  - Note: In hot sun, the bare soil usually turns pale and hard on top by day 2 or 3, while the soil under the leaves stays dark. In very humid weather the difference is smaller, so give it an extra day.
  - Check: Farhan, a boy in Hyderabad, covered one pot with dry leaves. After 3 sunny days, that soil was still damp, and the bare soil was dry and pale. Why? → **The leaves shaded the soil and kept the moving air off it, so less water evaporated**
  - Wait: Check on day 3.

3. (mcq) What does phitkari do in muddy water?  
   Options: It dissolves the mud / It makes the tiny bits clump into flocs, which sink / It kills all the germs / It makes the mud float to the top  
   **Answer:** It makes the tiny bits clump into flocs, which sink. *Why:* Phitkari makes the bits stick together into flocs, and flocs sink. It does not kill all germs, so the water still needs boiling.
4. (mcq) In the Phitkari Test, glass A had no phitkari but was stirred too. Why?  
   Options: To make it muddier / So the only difference between the glasses was the phitkari / Because stirring cleans water / To cool it down  
   **Answer:** So the only difference between the glasses was the phitkari. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. If both are stirred the same, any difference comes from the phitkari.
5. (mcq) Which is the right order of steps at a city water plant?  
   Options: Chlorine, sand, alum, settling / Alum, settling, sand filter, chlorine / Sand filter, alum, chlorine, settling / Settling, chlorine, alum, sand  
   **Answer:** Alum, settling, sand filter, chlorine. *Why:* Alum makes clumps, the clumps settle, sand catches finer bits, and chlorine kills the germs left.
6. (mcq) What does the living layer on top of a slow sand filter do?  
   Options: It blocks the water / Its tiny living beings eat many of the germs / It adds colour / It makes the water salty  
   **Answer:** Its tiny living beings eat many of the germs. *Why:* The living layer is full of microbes who feed on germs and dirt. Much of the cleaning happens there.
7. (mcq) A glass of well water is perfectly clear. Is it safe to drink?  
   Options: Yes, because it is clear / Not until it is boiled or purified, because germs are too small to see / Yes, if it is cold / No, clear water is never safe  
   **Answer:** Not until it is boiled or purified, because germs are too small to see. *Why:* Germs are invisible. Clear water still needs boiling, chlorine or a purifier to be safe. Picking 'never safe' goes too far: purified clear water is safe.
8. (mcq) In the Plate Test, why did washing under a running tap use the most water?  
   Options: The plate got dirtier / Water ran down the drain while the plate was being scrubbed / Running water is heavier / The soap needed more water  
   **Answer:** Water ran down the drain while the plate was being scrubbed. *Why:* The tap poured out water that did no job while the plate was scrubbed.
9. (mcq) Kavitha has four kinds of used water. Which one should go to her balcony plants?  
   Options: Oily water from washing a kadai / Water from rinsing rice / Toilet water / Water with floor-cleaning liquid  
   **Answer:** Water from rinsing rice. *Why:* Rice-washing water has only a little starch and dust, which do not harm plants. The others harm the soil or carry germs.
10. (mcq) Kavitha's RO purifier sends away reject water. What is the best job for it?  
   Options: Drinking / Mopping the floor / Watering plants every day / Making tea  
   **Answer:** Mopping the floor. *Why:* Reject water is salty, so it isn't good for drinking or for plants every day. It is fine for mopping or flushing.
11. (mcq) In 1892, Altona had far less cholera than Hamburg, next door. What was different?  
   Options: Altona had cleaner air / Altona filtered its river water through sand / Altona had no river / Altona's people were stronger  
   **Answer:** Altona filtered its river water through sand. *Why:* Both towns had the same air and ground. Altona's water passed through slow sand filters, and Hamburg's did not. This showed cholera travels in water.
12. (mcq) Which of these saves the most water in a building where the terrace tank overflows every morning?  
   Options: Brushing faster / An alarm or float switch so the motor stops when the tank is full / Using a smaller mug / Drinking less water  
   **Answer:** An alarm or float switch so the motor stops when the tank is full. *Why:* An overflowing tank pours away a lot of clean water every day. Stopping the motor in time saves all of it.

## Applications
- City water treatment plants clear river and lake water with alum before filtering it.
- Families in villages clear well and river water with phitkari or kataka seeds before boiling it.
- Slow sand filters clean drinking water for towns in many countries.
- Sewage treatment plants use living microbes to clean used water before it goes back to rivers.
- Swimming pools use filters and chlorine to keep water clear and safe.
- Mulch on farms and balcony gardens saves irrigation water.
- Float switches stop motors when building tanks are full.
- Grey water systems in some apartment buildings send bath water to flushes and gardens.
- Paper mills and factories reuse treated water many times.
- After floods, aid workers give out alum and chlorine tablets so families can clear and disinfect water.

## Open questions
- **What is the best low-cost way to make water safe for every family?** Boiling uses fuel, chlorine tastes, and filters need care. Scientists are still testing which ways families keep using for years.
- **Exactly which living beings in a slow sand filter do the most cleaning?** The living layer holds thousands of kinds of microbes and tiny animals. Scientists are still finding out who does what, and how they work together.
- **Do seeds like moringa and kataka clear water as well as alum, and are they safe to use every day?** Tests show they work, but results vary with the seeds and the water. Scientists are still studying the best doses and long-term safety.
- **How can cities stop losing so much water from leaking pipes?** Many cities lose a large share of their cleaned water through leaks under the ground. Finding leaks without digging up roads is still hard.
- **How much water will Indian cities have in 30 years?** It depends on rain, groundwater, how cities grow and how much water people save. Forecasts differ a lot.

## Connected maths
- In the Plate Test, washing under a running tap used 8 mugs. Scraping first and using a bowl used 2 mugs. How many times as much water did the running tap use? **4 times as much.**. The running tap used 8 mugs and the bowl way used 2. 8 divided by 2 is 4. Check: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8. So the running tap used 4 times as much. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Your family washes 12 plates a day. Washing them the bowl way saves 6 mugs for each plate. One mug holds half a litre. How many litres does that save in a day? **36 litres a day.**. 12 plates times 6 mugs is 72 mugs. Each mug is half a litre, so 72 mugs is 36 litres. That is about two or three buckets every day. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L3`)
- In the Phitkari Test, the clear water on top of glass B was 0 cm at 5 minutes, 4 cm at 15 minutes and 8 cm at 30 minutes. Glass A stayed at 0 cm, then 1 cm at 30 minutes. Draw a bar chart for 30 minutes. How much more clear water did glass B have? **7 cm more.**. At 30 minutes, glass B had 8 cm of clear water and glass A had 1 cm. 8 minus 1 is 7. So glass B had 7 cm more clear water. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the Phitkari Test, glass B had 4 cm of clear water at 15 minutes and 8 cm at 30 minutes. Arjun predicts it will have 16 cm at 60 minutes. His glass is only 10 cm tall. What is wrong with his prediction? **The clear water can't be taller than the glass. Once the flocs have all settled, the clear part stops growing, a little below the top of the mud layer.**. Doubling worked from 15 to 30 minutes, but the pattern can't go on for ever. The glass is only 10 cm tall, and some of it is filled with mud at the bottom. So the clear part must stop at less than 10 cm. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Divya's RO purifier sends away 3 litres for every 1 litre of drinking water. Her family drinks 8 litres a day. How much reject water is made each day, and how many 10-litre buckets of mopping water is that? **24 litres a day, which is 2 full buckets and 4 litres more.**. 8 litres of drinking water times 3 is 24 litres of reject water. A bucket holds 10 litres. 24 is 10 + 10 + 4. So that is 2 full buckets and 4 litres more. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L3`)
