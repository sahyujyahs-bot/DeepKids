# Design and build: make it, test it, make it better
`sci.making-things.design-and-build.L3`

**Summary.** A first design almost never works perfectly, and a test shows where it goes wrong. Engineers design things for a purpose. They plan, choose materials, build, test and then improve. In this unit you build a boat from one square of aluminium foil and load it with coins until water comes in. You watch where the water comes in, change one thing and test again. A boat floats because the water pushes it up, and a wider boat with taller sides can push aside more water. You also test which kitchen paste joins paper best.

**Intro.** *Here is a square of kitchen foil, as wide as your hand is long. Can you fold it into a boat that carries 20 coins without sinking? Most people's first boat sinks with far fewer. Why, and how do you make the next one better?* Everything people make was designed by someone: your steel tumbler, your chappals, the bus and the Chandrayaan rocket. Designing means deciding what a thing is for, then planning how to make it do that job. Nobody gets it all right the first time. Engineers build, test, find the weak spot and improve. In this unit you do exactly that, with a foil boat, a bucket of water and some coins.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a design is for (its purpose) and the limits it must work within (its constraints).
1. I can follow the design cycle: plan, build, test and improve.
2. I can choose a material for a job and give a reason from a test.
3. I can run a fair test by changing only one thing at a time, and write my results in a table.
4. I can explain why a foil boat floats, and why a boat with a wide base and tall sides carries more.
5. I can use a failed test to find the weak spot in a design, and change the design to fix it.
6. I can tell how Stephanie Kwolek tested a liquid others would have thrown away, and found a very strong fibre.

## Terms
- **Design**: A plan for making something that does a job. It says what the thing will look like, what it is made of and how it is put together. *e.g.* Before building her boat, Kavya drew a design: a square base with walls three fingers high.
- **Purpose**: The job a thing is made to do. *e.g.* The purpose of an umbrella is to keep the rain off you.
- **Constraint**: A limit that a design must work within, such as how much material you may use, how big it can be or how much it may cost. *e.g.* In the foil boat test, one constraint is that each boat must be made from one square of foil, no more.
- **Engineer**: A person who designs and builds things that solve problems, such as bridges, machines, rockets and water pumps. *e.g.* Engineers at ISRO in Bengaluru designed the Chandrayaan-3 lander that landed on the Moon in 2023.
- **Prototype**: A first, trial version of a design, built to be tested before the final one is made. *e.g.* Your first foil boat is a prototype. You test it to find out what to change.
- **Test**: Trying a design out to see how well it does its job. *e.g.* We test a foil boat by adding coins one at a time until water comes in.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To test whether taller walls help, Kavya kept the same size of foil and the same coins, and changed only the height of the walls.
- **Weak spot**: The part of a design that fails first when it is tested. *e.g.* Kavya's first boat let water in at one low corner. That corner was the weak spot.
- **Improve**: To change a design so that it does its job better. A good improvement fixes the weak spot that a test showed. *e.g.* Kavya improved her boat by making all four walls the same height.
- **Material**: What a thing is made of, such as steel, wood, cloth, paper, plastic or foil. *e.g.* A kadai is made of iron, which can take the heat of a stove.
- **Float**: To stay at the top of water instead of sinking. *e.g.* A steel bowl floats in a bucket, but a steel spoon sinks.
- **Load**: The weight a design has to carry or hold up. *e.g.* The coins are the load that the foil boat carries.
- **Joint**: The place where two parts of a design are fixed together. *e.g.* Where two paper strips are stuck together with paste, that is a joint.
- **Adhesive**: A material that sticks two things together, such as glue, paste or sticky tape. *e.g.* Rice paste is an adhesive that people in India have used for a long time to stick paper.

## Explanations
### Every design starts with a purpose · *defined*
**Definition:** A design starts with a purpose, the job the thing must do. Then come the constraints, the limits it must work within.

Before drawing anything, an engineer asks: what is this for, and who will use it? Then come the limits. How much material is there? How big can it be? How much can it cost? The same purpose with different limits leads to different designs. A water pot to carry on the head and a water pot to keep on a shelf hold the same water. But the one for carrying must be light and balanced.
- e.g. A steel tumbler's purpose is to hold a drink. Its rim is rolled over, which makes the thin edge stiffer and smooth on your lips.
- e.g. A pressure cooker's handle is plastic, because heat passes through plastic slowly, so you can hold it.
- e.g. In the foil boat test, the purpose is to carry as many coins as possible. The constraint is one square of foil.
### The design cycle: plan, build, test, improve · *defined*
**Definition:** Engineers work in a loop. They plan a design, build it, test it, look at what went wrong and improve it. Then they test again.

The loop is called the design cycle. It goes round more than once. A first version is called a prototype, and it is built to be tested. The test shows the weak spot, the part that fails first. The engineer changes the design to fix that spot, then tests again. Each round, the design gets a little better. Real engineers may go round the loop many times.
- e.g. Your foil boat: draw it, fold it, load it with coins, see where water comes in, fold a better one.
- e.g. After the Chandrayaan-2 lander crashed on the Moon in 2019, ISRO gave the Chandrayaan-3 lander stronger legs, more fuel and a bigger landing area. It landed safely in August 2023.
- e.g. A cook trying a new rasam recipe tastes it, adds a little more tamarind, and tastes it again.
### Why a boat floats · *defined*
**Definition:** Water pushes up on anything put into it. A boat floats when the water's upward push is as big as the weight of the boat and its load.

When a boat sits in water, it pushes some water out of the way. The water pushes back up on the boat. The more water the boat pushes aside, the bigger that upward push. As you add coins, the boat sinks a little lower and pushes aside more water. A boat with a wide base and tall walls can sink deep and push aside a lot of water before water spills over the top. When water comes over the side, the boat fills and sinks. This idea is called Archimedes' principle, after a Greek thinker who wrote about it over 2,200 years ago.
- e.g. A big steel ship floats because its wide, hollow body pushes aside a huge amount of water.
- e.g. A steel spoon sinks, but a steel bowl of the same steel floats. The bowl's shape pushes aside more water.
- e.g. Push an empty plastic bottle down into a bucket. You can feel the water pushing it back up.
### Change one thing at a time · *defined*
**Definition:** In a fair test you change only one thing between two tries and keep everything else the same. Then you know what made the difference.

Say your second boat has taller walls, a wider base and squarer corners, and it holds more coins. Which change helped? You cannot tell, because you changed three things. If you change only the walls, and the boat holds more coins, the walls made the difference. That is why engineers change one thing at a time. Everything else stays the same: the size of the foil, the kind of coins, the bucket and how you place the coins.
- e.g. Use the same kind of coin for every boat, because coins of different sizes weigh different amounts.
- e.g. Always add coins from the middle outwards, so each boat is loaded the same way.
- e.g. Test each boat more than once if you can, because one try can be a fluke.
### Choosing materials · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is chosen for the job. Its properties must fit the purpose: strong, light, waterproof, bendy, cheap or easy to shape.

No material is best for everything. Foil is good for a test boat because it is thin, light, waterproof and holds a folded shape. Paper would soak up water. Steel would be too hard to fold by hand. Joining materials matters too. A joint is where two parts are fixed together, and a joint is often a weak spot. Engineers test joints as well as materials.
- e.g. Kettuvallam houseboats in Kerala were traditionally made of wooden planks tied together with coir rope, with no nails.
- e.g. Kites are made of thin paper on light bamboo sticks, so the wind can lift them.
- e.g. In the paste test, rice paste, atta paste and sticky tape all join paper, but each joint holds differently.
### A failed test is useful · *defined*
**Definition:** A test that fails shows exactly where a design is weak. That is the information you need to improve it.

When a foil boat sinks, watch closely. Did the water come in at one corner? Did the boat tip to one side? Did a fold leak? Each kind of failure points to a different fix. A low corner needs taller walls. Tipping needs a wider base or coins spread evenly. A leak needs a tighter fold. Engineers keep notes on every failure, so they do not repeat it. ISRO's chairman, S. Somanath, called the Chandrayaan-3 lander a 'failure-based design', because it was planned around everything that could go wrong.
- e.g. A boat that tips to one side tells you the load was not spread evenly, or the base was too narrow.
- e.g. A paper bag whose handle tears at the corner tells you the corner needs to be stronger.
- e.g. A kite that spins round and round tells you it needs a tail or a better balance.
### Do inventions come from one clever idea or from many tries? · *debated*
People often picture an inventor suddenly having a brilliant idea. Real inventions usually come from many rounds of testing and changing. But a good starting idea still matters. People argue about how much each counts.
- e.g. Stephanie Kwolek's very strong fibre came from testing a liquid others would have thrown away.
- e.g. Engineers today can test thousands of designs on a computer before building one.
- e.g. A child's first foil boat already shows ideas that came from their head, such as walls.
- **Many engineers:** Most of a design's quality comes from testing. Ideas are cheap. Building, testing and improving is the real work.
- **Many designers and mathematicians:** A good plan and careful calculation can get a design close to right the first time. Bridges are calculated before they are built, because you cannot test a full-size bridge until it fails.
- **Some historians of invention:** New ideas usually come from many people building on each other's work over years, not from one person alone.

## Mostly agreed
- Testing finds problems that a plan on paper misses. *How we know:* Engineers test small models before big builds. After the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the USA twisted and fell in a strong wind in 1940, testing bridge models in wind tunnels became usual.
- A floating object is pushed up by the water with a force as big as the weight of the water it pushes aside. *How we know:* This is Archimedes' principle. It can be checked by weighing the water that spills out of a full container when something floats in it.
- In a fair test, you change one thing at a time. *How we know:* If two things change at once, you cannot tell which one caused the result. Scientists and engineers everywhere test this way.
- Joints are often the weak spot of a design. *How we know:* When things break, they very often break where two parts meet. You can see it in torn bag handles, loose chair legs and peeling stickers.

## How we found out
**Stephanie Kwolek, a chemist at the DuPont company, 1965.** In 1965, Stephanie Kwolek was a chemist at the DuPont company in the USA. Her team wanted a light, very strong fibre, a thin thread that could be woven into strong cloth. One day she made a liquid that looked wrong. Good liquids for making fibre were usually clear and thick, like honey. Hers was cloudy and runny. People at the time expected a liquid like that to be useless, and such liquids were usually thrown away. The worker who ran the spinning machine did not want to try it at first. He was afraid the cloudy liquid would block the machine's tiny holes. Kwolek did not trust the guess. She wanted a test. She persuaded him to spin it. The fibre that came out was stiffer and stronger than anything they had made before. She tested it again and again to be sure. DuPont later called the fibre Kevlar. It is used today in helmets, cut-proof gloves, ropes and vests that protect police officers. A test, not a guess, showed what the liquid could do.  
*What people believed before:* People expected that a cloudy, runny liquid could not make a good fibre, so such liquids were usually thrown away. Kwolek insisted on testing it, and it made one of the strongest fibres ever made.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A good designer gets it right the first time.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Some experienced builders and tailors make things that work first time, because they have learned from many earlier tries.  
  Against: Their skill came from many earlier failures. New designs, even by experts, almost always need testing and changes. Chandrayaan-3 was built on what went wrong with Chandrayaan-2.
- **The strongest material always makes the best design.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Strong materials break less often.  
  Against: The best material depends on the purpose. A steel kite would never fly. A boat from thick card would soak and sink. Weight, cost, bendiness and whether a material lets water in all matter too.
- **A heavier boat always sinks sooner.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Adding coins does make a foil boat sink lower.  
  Against: Shape matters as much as weight. A huge steel ship weighs far more than a foil boat but floats, because its wide hollow body pushes aside a huge amount of water.
- **Computers will soon design things better than people, with no testing in the real world.** (Some engineers and computer scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Computers can try millions of shapes quickly, and some computer-designed parts are already lighter and stronger than people's designs.  
  Against: Computer models leave things out. Real materials have flaws, and real weather and real users surprise designers. Real-world testing is still needed.

## Roots
We have built foil boats, found their weak spots and made them better. Now we ask what designing tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your first foil boat let water in at a spot you did not expect. Only the water showed you that weak spot. Each better boat came from what the last test showed. So your best boat's shape came from the water and the coins as much as from your first idea.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture an inventor having one clever idea in their head, and the world just carrying it out. But in a real design, much of the final shape comes from what tests show. The maker and the world shape the design together.  
*Evidence:* In the foil boat test, nearly every change came from where the water came in: a low corner, a tipping side or a leaky fold. Kwolek's fibre, Chandrayaan-3's stronger legs and Kavya's bicycle bags in the story all came from what a test showed.

- *who am i:* You are a designer. Every time you try, see what went wrong and try again, you are doing what engineers do.
- *what is life:* Living beings design too. A weaver bird weaves a nest, ants build tunnels, and a spider spins a web, each fitted to their own needs.
- *what is this world:* Everything people make, from a tumbler to a Moon lander, was shaped by many tests against the real world.

A foil square is flat and holds nothing. Folded, it becomes a boat that the water lifts. The boat's best shape was not in your head at the start. It came out of a meeting between your idea, the foil, the coins and the water's push. Every design in the world is made this way, by many meetings between makers and the things they work with.

- **Tirukkural 467, chapter 47 (acting after careful thought), by Thiruvalluvar:** Think first, then begin the work. To begin first and say 'we will think about it later' is a mistake. (Planning and drawing your boat before folding it saved foil and time. But the test still showed weak spots your plan missed. Thinking first and testing after work together.)
- **A doha (couplet) attributed to Kabir:** The clay says to the potter: why do you press and knead me? One day will come when I will knead you. (The potter shapes the clay, but the clay also shapes the potter's work, by how it bends, cracks and holds. In your boat test, the foil and the water shaped your design too.)
- Science (settled): Archimedes' principle: a floating object is pushed up by the water with a force equal to the weight of the water it pushes aside.
- Science (settled): Engineers everywhere test models and prototypes, because tests find problems that plans and calculations miss.
- Science (debated): Computers can now try millions of designs and test them in a model world. How far this can replace real-world tests is still argued.
- Other view, Many engineers and mathematicians: A design comes from the designer's knowledge and calculation. The water does not design anything. It only follows the laws of nature. Tests check a design, but the ideas and the choices are the designer's own.
- Practice, Hands that listen (3 min): Take a fresh square of foil. Close your eyes. Slowly fold up one edge. Feel how the foil bends and where it resists. Fold another edge. Don't plan it. Let your fingers feel what the foil does. Open your eyes. Look at what you made. How much of this shape came from you, and how much from the foil?
- Ask: Your best boat came after two that failed. Did you design it, or did you and the water design it together?
- Ask: If a design is never finished, when do we say something is made?
- Ask: Is a failed test a mistake, or a message?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your best foil boat came after two that sank. Who designed it: you, or you and the water together? (hint: Think about where each change came from. Did you think of it first, or did the water show you?)
- *deep:* Why does a boat with taller walls carry more coins, when it is made of the same amount of foil? (hint: Think about how much water the boat can push aside before water spills over the top.)
- *big:* A spider builds a web, a weaver bird a nest and a termite colony a mound. Are they designers too? (hint: A male baya weaver bird weaves a hanging nest of grass. A female inspects it before she chooses to nest there. What might she be checking?)
- *big:* Is anything ever finished being designed? (hint: Think of phones, bicycles or tumblers. Have their designs stopped changing?)
- *deep:* If you change two things at once and your boat gets better, what do you know, and what don't you know? (hint: Could one change have helped and the other made it worse?)
- *big:* Should a design be best for most people, or work for everyone, including people who use a wheelchair, cannot see well, or are very old? (hint: Think about steps and ramps at a railway station.)

## Story: Two Kudams and a Bicycle
*Made up. Kavya, Arjun, Thatha and the village are invented. Carrying water in kudams (round water pots) from a street tap is still common in many Tamil Nadu villages.*

Every summer holiday, Kavya stayed with her grandfather in a village near Thanjavur. Every morning, before the sun got hot, Thatha fetched water. He walked to the tap at the end of the street and came back with a kudam on each hip. A kudam is a round water pot with a narrow neck.

This summer, Thatha had hurt his back. The doctor said no lifting for a month.

"I'll carry the water," said Kavya. She was ten. She lifted one full kudam and put it down again at once. It was far too heavy to carry all the way home.

Her cousin Arjun, who was eight, laughed. "You can't even lift one."

Kavya looked at her old bicycle leaning against the wall. It had a flat metal carrier behind the seat. "I don't need to lift them far," she said. "The bicycle can carry them. I'll walk beside it and push."

First she thought about the purpose. The bicycle had to carry two kudams of water from the tap to the house. Then she thought about the limits. She could not ride with water on the back, because it would wobble. She could only use things from the house. And she must not spill much, because water was precious.

Her first design was simple. She tied a rope to each kudam's neck and hung one kudam on each side of the carrier.

"We'll test it with half-full pots," she told Arjun. "If it fails, we lose less water."

They filled both pots halfway at the tap and tied them on. Kavya began to push. After five steps, the kudams started to swing. One swung into the back wheel and banged against the spokes. Water splashed out. The bicycle jerked to one side, and Arjun had to grab it.

Kavya did not get cross. She knelt down and looked. "The rope is too long," she said. "The pots swing, and when they swing, they hit the wheel. The swinging is the weak spot."

For her second design, she tied the ropes shorter, so the pots hung higher. They tested again. The pots did not hit the spokes now. But after ten steps, the bicycle leaned more and more to the left.

"Why does it lean?" asked Arjun.

Kavya looked inside the pots. The left one had more water in it. They had not filled them equally. "It's like a see-saw," she said. "The heavier side goes down. Both sides must weigh the same."

They filled the pots to the same mark, using a steel tumbler to count. Five tumblers in each. This time the bicycle stayed upright. But the ropes still rubbed and slid along the carrier, and the pots twisted round.

Kavya went to Thatha. "Do you have two old cloth bags?" she asked. He gave her two of the thick yellow cloth bags from the ration shop.

Her third design put each kudam inside a bag. She tied each bag's handles tightly to the carrier, and its bottom corner to the frame, so it could not swing. The bags held the round pots snugly, like a hand holding a ball.

They tested with half-full pots. The bicycle rolled straight. Nothing swung. Nothing spilled. Then they tested with full pots. Kavya pushed slowly and carefully, all the way home, while Arjun walked beside the bags.

Thatha was sitting on the front step. He watched the bicycle come up the street with two full kudams and not a drop spilled. "How did you think of all that?" he asked.

"I didn't think of all of it," said Kavya. "I thought of the first idea. The swinging showed me the second. The leaning showed me the third."

Arjun had been listening. The next morning, he took a notebook to the tap. He drew the bicycle, the bags and the knots. Under it, he wrote three things that went wrong and how Kavya had fixed each one.

"What's that for?" asked Kavya.

"For next summer," said Arjun. "Then whoever fetches the water can start from your third design, not your first."

Kavya smiled. That was exactly what engineers do.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavya did not invent the final carrier in one go. Each test failed in a different way, and each failure pointed at what to change. Swinging pots showed the ropes were too long. Leaning showed the loads were not equal. Twisting showed the pots needed holding firmly. She tested with half-full pots first, so a failure would waste less water. This is the design cycle: plan, build, test, improve, and test again.

- Ask: What was the purpose of Kavya's design, and what were her limits?
- Ask: Why did Kavya test with half-full pots first?
- Ask: What went wrong in each test, and how did she fix it?
- Ask: Kavya said she did not think of all of it herself. What did she mean?
- Ask: Arjun wrote down every failure. Why might that help the next designer?

## Games
### g1 · Weak Spot Detective (15 min)
- **rules:** Lay the 10 fix cards face up. Shuffle the problem cards face down.; Turn over a problem card and read it aloud.; Find the weak spot, then pick the fix card that mends it. Say why.; Score 1 point if the fix mends the weak spot. Some problems have more than one good fix: any fix that really works scores.; Playing alone: race the clock. How many can you match in 5 minutes?
- **Printable cards: 10 problem cards:** A foil boat let water in over one low corner.; A foil boat tipped when coins were piled at one end.; A paper bag's handle tore off at the corner.; A kite spun round and round and crashed.; A card phone stand slid flat on the table.; A paper tower leaned and fell when a lemon was put on top.; A cloth bag of rice split along its bottom seam.; Water dripped through a fold in a foil boat.; A bird water bowl tipped when a crow landed on the bowl's edge.; Ants crossed a moat because a spoon was left leaning on the bowl.

- **s2 (14 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent does:* Talk about the best round.

  - Think: Do limits make design harder, or can they make you more creative?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Foil Boat: build it, test it, make it better (40 min)
- **question:** How many coins can a boat made from one square of foil carry, and how much better can you make it in three tries?
- **predict:** Guess first. How many coins will your first boat carry before water comes in? Will your third boat carry more, fewer or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every boat uses one square of foil of the same size, the same kind of coin and the same bucket of water. Coins always go in one at a time, starting from the middle and spreading outwards. Between two boats, change only one thing, so you know what made the difference.
- **safety:** Cut foil edges can be a little sharp, so fold them in. Wipe up spilled water at once so nobody slips. Small children must not put coins in their mouths.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each boat. Draw each boat from the side, and mark with an arrow where the water came in first.
- **results table:** Boat | What I changed | Coins it carried | Where the water came in first
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out one foil square, the coins and the bucket of water. 2. Read out the job and the rules.  
  *Parent says:* Here is your job, like a real engineer. Build a boat that carries as many coins as possible. The rules: one square of foil per boat, no tape, and coins go in one at a time. How many coins do you think your first boat will carry? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Your job: make a foil boat that carries lots of coins. Use one square of foil only. No tape. Guess how many coins it will carry. Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What is the purpose of this boat, and what are the limits? (good: To carry as many coins as possible.; We can only use one square of foil, and no tape.)
  - Note: Write the guess in the notebook. Any number is fine. Most children guess between 5 and 30.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws boat 1 from the side and from above. 2. Label the base and the walls, and write how high the walls will be.  
  *Parent says:* Engineers draw before they build. Draw your boat. Show how big the base is and how high the walls go.  
  *Child mode:* Draw your boat first. Show the bottom and the sides. How tall are the sides?
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the foil square into boat 1, following the drawing. 2. Pinch the corners tight so there are no holes.  
  *Parent says:* Now build it the way you drew it. Pinch the corners tightly. A hole would let water in.  
  *Child mode:* Fold your boat like your drawing. Pinch the corners tight. No holes!
  - Note: Let your child fold it their own way, even if you think it will not work well. A flat tray with low sides is a very common first design. It is a good first try, because the test will show what to change.

### e2 · The Paste Test: which kitchen paste joins paper best? (30 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Which joins two strips of paper best: rice paste, atta paste, sticky tape or plain water?

- **results table:** What joined the strips | Joint 1: held, slid apart or paper tore? | Joint 2: held, slid apart or paper tore? | Where it broke

  *Child mode:* Look at rice paste, atta paste, tape and water. Which will hold paper best? Why?

   **Answer:** ['base: the flat bottom', 'wall: the sides that stand up', 'corner: where two walls meet', 'load: the coins inside', 'waterline: where the water reaches on the outside']. *Why:* Naming the parts makes it easier to say exactly where a weak spot is.
15. (perspective) Two engineers are designing a footbridge over a canal in Chennai.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then sees the other's point. Real engineers do both: they calculate, then test models, as with bridge models tested in wind tunnels.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. Calculations are fast and cheap, and tests catch what calculations miss.
### Think Like an Engineer (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Two foil boats carried 20 and 22 coins. Rohini says the second design is clearly better. What is the best reply?  
   Options: She is right / The difference is small. Test each boat again to check it was not a fluke. / Both are bad  
   **Answer:** The difference is small. Test each boat again to check it was not a fluke.. *Why:* A difference of 2 coins could come from chance, such as where a coin landed. Testing again shows whether the difference is real.
2. (mcq) A boat made from a 15 centimetre square carried 30 coins. What would you predict for a boat of the same shape from a 30 centimetre square?  
   Options: About the same / Many more coins / Fewer coins  
   **Answer:** Many more coins. *Why:* A bigger boat of the same shape has a much bigger base and taller walls, so it can push aside far more water. It should carry many more coins, more than twice as many.
3. (mcq) Why might a designer make a phone stand from card instead of steel?  
   Options: Card is stronger / Card is cheap, light, easy to cut and strong enough for the job / Steel would bend  
   **Answer:** Card is cheap, light, easy to cut and strong enough for the job. *Why:* A material only needs to be strong enough. Card is cheap and easy to shape, and a phone is light.
4. (truefalse) If a design passes its test, it can never be improved.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A design that works may still be made cheaper, lighter, safer or easier to use. Phones and bicycles keep being redesigned.
5. (fill) Stephanie Kwolek's very strong fibre was later named ____.  
   **Answer:** ['Kevlar']. *Why:* It is used in helmets, cut-proof gloves, ropes and protective vests.
6. (order) Put Stephanie Kwolek's discovery in order.  
   **Answer:** ['She made a cloudy, runny liquid', 'Others expected it to be useless', 'She persuaded the worker to spin it', 'The fibre was far stronger than expected']. *Why:* The guess said 'useless'. The test showed the opposite.
7. (write) The Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the USA was strong enough to carry cars, but in 1940 it twisted in a strong wind and fell. What does this tell us about testing a design?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the design was tested for one load, the cars, but not for another, the wind. A design must be tested for everything it will meet. After this, engineers began testing bridge models in wind tunnels.. *Why:* This tests whether the child sees that a test must match the real conditions.
8. (write) You want to know whether taller walls or a wider base helps a foil boat more. Plan a fair test. Say what you will change and what you will keep the same.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer makes one boat with taller walls and one with a wider base from the same size of foil, with the same coins, bucket and loading. It compares each with a plain starting boat.. *Why:* A good plan changes one thing at a time and keeps everything else the same.
9. (write) A weaver bird builds a nest. A child builds a foil boat. In what ways are they both designers? In what ways are they different?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says both make something for a purpose, choose materials and shape them. Differences: the bird uses grass and her beak, and builds a home for her young; the child uses foil and tests with coins.. *Why:* This asks the child to compare design across living beings.
10. (draw) Design a container to carry 2 idlis to school without squashing them, using only paper and string. Draw it and label how it protects the idlis.  
   **Answer:** A strong design has stiff sides, for example folded or rolled paper, a lid, and a handle tied at strong points.. *Why:* This applies purpose, constraints and joints to a new problem.
11. (label) Kavya's third bicycle design from the story is drawn here. Label what each part does: cloth bag, tied handles, tied bottom corner, equal water.  
   **Answer:** ['cloth bag: holds the round pot snugly', 'tied handles: fix the bag to the carrier', 'tied bottom corner: stops the bag swinging into the wheel', 'equal water: keeps both sides the same weight so the bicycle stays upright']. *Why:* Each part fixes a weak spot found in an earlier test.
12. (perspective) After Chandrayaan-2's lander crashed in 2019, people in India argued about what to do next.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and gives a reason, then gives the best case for another view. It may use the fact that Chandrayaan-3 landed in 2023 after the design was improved.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement about purpose: what should a country design and build first?
13. (sort) Sort these changes to a foil boat: will each most likely help it carry more coins, or fewer?  
   **Answer:** make the walls taller → More; cut a small slit in the base → Fewer; make the base wider → More; make one wall much lower than the others → Fewer; pinch the corners tighter → More. *Why:* Changes that let the boat sink deeper without water getting in help. Holes and low walls let water in sooner.
14. (match) Match each Indian design to the problem it solves.  
   **Answer:** Kattumaram, logs tied together → a boat that cannot fill with water and sink; Kettuvallam planks stitched with coir → joining wooden planks without iron nails; Food safe legs in bowls of water → keeping ants out of food; Clay matka → keeping water cool. *Why:* Each design solves a real problem with local materials. A kattumaram is a raft of logs, so water flows through it instead of filling it.
15. (truefalse) Changing three things at once is a quick way to find out which change helped.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. If three things change, you cannot tell which one made the difference. Change one at a time.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is the purpose of a design?  
   Options: What it is made of / The job it is made to do / How much it costs  
   **Answer:** The job it is made to do. *Why:* The purpose is the job the thing must do. What it is made of is its material, and cost is one of its limits. Picking 'material' mixes up what a thing is for with what it is made of.
2. (mcq) In the foil boat test, each boat must be made from one square of foil. What is this rule called?  
   Options: A constraint / A prototype / A float  
   **Answer:** A constraint. *Why:* A constraint is a limit a design must work within. A prototype is a trial version, and float is what a boat does.
3. (mcq) What comes right after testing a design in the design cycle?  
   Options: Throw it away / Improve it, then test again / Paint it  
   **Answer:** Improve it, then test again. *Why:* After a test, you look at what went wrong and improve the design, then test again. That loop is the design cycle. Picking 'throw it away' misses that a failed test shows you what to fix.
4. (mcq) Lakshmi's foil boat sank when water came in over one low corner. What should she change for her next boat?  
   Options: Use more coins / Make that corner as high as the other walls / Use a smaller bucket  
   **Answer:** Make that corner as high as the other walls. *Why:* The low corner was the weak spot, so raising it fixes the problem. More coins would make it sink sooner, and the bucket size does not change where water comes in.
5. (mcq) Farhan changed the walls, the base and the corners of his boat all at once, and it carried more coins. What can he NOT tell?  
   Options: That his new boat is better / Which change made it better / How many coins it carried  
   **Answer:** Which change made it better. *Why:* Because he changed three things at once, he cannot tell which one helped. To know, change one thing at a time. He can still see that the new boat is better and count its coins.
6. (mcq) Why does a foil boat float?  
   Options: Foil is lighter than air / The water pushes up on the boat / The coins hold it up  
   **Answer:** The water pushes up on the boat. *Why:* Water pushes up on anything put into it. The more water a boat pushes aside, the bigger that push. Foil is much heavier than air, and the coins push the boat down, not up.
7. (mcq) Why does a boat with taller walls usually carry more coins?  
   Options: It is made of thicker foil / It can sink deeper and push aside more water before water spills in / Tall walls keep the wind off  
   **Answer:** It can sink deeper and push aside more water before water spills in. *Why:* Taller walls let the boat sink lower without water coming over the top. A lower boat pushes aside more water, so the water pushes up harder. It uses the same foil, not thicker foil.
8. (mcq) Which is the best material for a test boat that you fold by hand?  
   Options: Paper towel / Aluminium foil / A steel plate  
   **Answer:** Aluminium foil. *Why:* Foil is thin, light, waterproof and holds a folded shape. Paper towel soaks up water and sinks. Steel is too stiff to fold by hand.
9. (mcq) In the paste test, the plain water joint fell apart at once. Why did we test plain water at all?  
   Options: To see what happens with no paste, so we know the paste does the work / Because water is the best glue / To make the paper soft  
   **Answer:** To see what happens with no paste, so we know the paste does the work. *Why:* Plain water is the 'no paste' joint. Comparing with it shows what each paste adds. It is not a glue.
10. (mcq) Why did ISRO give Chandrayaan-3's lander stronger legs and more fuel?  
   Options: To make it look better / Because the Chandrayaan-2 lander crashed, and they learned from it / Because the Moon had changed  
   **Answer:** Because the Chandrayaan-2 lander crashed, and they learned from it. *Why:* The Chandrayaan-2 lander crashed in 2019. ISRO studied what went wrong and improved the design. Chandrayaan-3 landed safely in 2023.
11. (mcq) Stephanie Kwolek made a cloudy, runny liquid. What did she do that others might not have?  
   Options: Threw it away / Tested it by spinning it into a fibre / Mixed it with water  
   **Answer:** Tested it by spinning it into a fibre. *Why:* Liquids like hers were usually thrown away, because people expected them to be useless. She insisted on testing it, and it made a very strong fibre, later called Kevlar.
12. (mcq) A crow lands on the edge of Divya's bird water bowl, and the bowl tips over. What is the best improvement?  
   Options: Fill it to the top / Use a wider, heavier bowl, or put a stone in the middle / Move it into the sun  
   **Answer:** Use a wider, heavier bowl, or put a stone in the middle. *Why:* A wider, heavier bowl, or a weight in the middle, keeps it steady when a bird lands on the edge. More water might not stop the tipping, and sun would make the water hot.

## Applications
- Engineers test small models of ships in long water tanks before building the real ship.
- Bridge models are tested in wind tunnels before the real bridge is built.
- ISRO tests rockets, landers and satellites on the ground, again and again, before launch.
- Car makers crash-test cars with dummies inside, to make seats and belts safer.
- Tailors make a trial fit and adjust it before the final stitching.
- Cooks test a new recipe on the family, then change the spices.
- Phone apps are tested by users, and then improved in updates.
- Ramps at railway stations and schools are designed so everyone can use them, including people in wheelchairs.

## Spotted around us
- Kattumarams, boats made of logs tied together, on the beaches of Chennai and the Coromandel coast.
- Kettuvallam houseboats on the backwaters of Kerala, traditionally made of planks stitched with coir rope.
- The rolled rim of a steel tumbler, and the plastic handle of a pressure cooker.
- Food safes or shelves with their legs standing in small bowls of water, to keep ants out.
- Ramps beside the steps at metro stations, for wheelchairs, prams and luggage.
- Thick yellow cloth bags at the ration shop, strong enough to carry rice.

## Open questions
- **Can a computer design things better than people, without testing them in the real world?** Computers already design some parts that are lighter and stronger than people's. But real materials and real users still surprise designers, and nobody knows how far computer design can go.
- **How do weaver birds and other animals learn to build better?** Young male weaver birds seem to get better at weaving with practice, but scientists are still finding out how much is learned and how much is born in them.
- **What is the best way to design for everyone, including people with very different bodies and needs?** Designers still argue about whether one design can suit everyone, or whether different people need different designs.

## Connected maths
- Kavya's three foil boats carried 9, 22 and 31 coins. How many more coins did boat 3 carry than boat 1? **22 more coins.**. Boat 3 carried 31 coins and boat 1 carried 9. 31 minus 9 is 22. So boat 3 carried 22 more coins. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Your foil square is 15 centimetres on each side. You fold up walls 3 centimetres high on all four sides. How long is each side of the flat base? **9 centimetres.**. Each side loses 3 centimetres for the wall at one end and 3 centimetres at the other end. 3 + 3 = 6. 15 minus 6 is 9. So the base is 9 centimetres on each side. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- Which of these flat shapes can fold into an open box with no lid? A: a plus shape of 5 squares, one in the middle and one on each side. B: a straight row of 5 squares. **Shape A.**. In shape A, the middle square is the base. The four squares around it fold up to make four walls. In shape B, the squares are in a line, so they roll up into a tube with an open end and no proper base. A flat shape that folds into a solid is called a net. (queues `math.shapes-3d.nets.L4`)
- In the paste test, you test 4 ways of joining (rice paste, atta paste, tape and water), and make 2 joints of each. Each joint needs 2 strips. How many strips do you need? **16 strips.**. 4 ways times 2 joints is 8 joints. Each joint needs 2 strips. 8 times 2 is 16. So you need 16 strips. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Divya's foil boat carried 18 coins on her first try and 6 coins on her second try, with no change to the boat. Her friend's boat carried 14 and 15. Whose boat gave more reliable results? Why might Divya's be so different? **Her friend's boat.**. Her friend's two results, 14 and 15, are only 1 apart. Divya's, 18 and 6, are 12 apart. Something else changed between Divya's tries. Perhaps the boat was squashed after the first test, or the coins were piled on one side the second time. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
