# Design and build: make it, test it, make it better
`sci.making-things.design-and-build.L3`

**Summary.** A tiny foil boat can float and carry a load, but only if it is shaped well. In this lesson you will build a foil boat, test it and make it better, find which kitchen paste joins paper best, and hunt for the weak spot in a design.

**Intro.** *Here is a square of kitchen foil, as wide as your hand is long. Can you fold it into a boat that carries 20 coins without sinking? Most people's first boat sinks with far fewer. Why, and how do you make the next one better?* Everything people make was designed by someone: your steel tumbler, your chappals, the bus and the Chandrayaan rocket. Designing means deciding what a thing is for, then planning how to make it do that job. Nobody gets it all right the first time. Engineers build, test, find the weak spot and improve. In this lesson you do exactly that, with a foil boat, a bucket of water and some coins.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a design is for (its purpose) and the limits it must work within (its constraints).
1. I can follow the design cycle: plan, build, test and improve.
2. I can choose a material for a job and give a reason from a test.
3. I can run a fair test by changing only one thing at a time, and write my results in a table.
4. I can explain why a foil boat floats, and why a boat with a wide base and tall sides carries more.
5. I can use a failed test to find the weak spot in a design, and change the design to fix it.
6. I can tell how Stephanie Kwolek tested a liquid others would have thrown away, and found a very strong fibre.

## Terms
- **Design**: A plan for making something that does a job. It says what the thing will look like, what it is made of and how it is put together. *e.g.* Before folding a boat, you draw a design: a square base with walls three fingers high.
- **Purpose**: The job a thing is made to do. *e.g.* The purpose of an umbrella is to keep the rain off you.
- **Constraint**: A limit that a design must work within, such as how much material you may use, how big it can be or how much it may cost. *e.g.* In the foil boat test, one constraint is that each boat must be made from one square of foil, no more.
- **Engineer**: A person who designs and builds things that solve problems, such as bridges, machines, rockets and water pumps. *e.g.* Engineers at ISRO in Bengaluru designed the Chandrayaan-3 lander that landed on the Moon in 2023.
- **Prototype**: A first, trial version of a design, built to be tested before the final one is made. *e.g.* Your first foil boat is a prototype. You test it to find out what to change.
- **Test**: Trying a design out to see how well it does its job. *e.g.* We test a foil boat by adding coins one at a time until water comes in.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To test whether taller walls help, keep the same size of foil and the same coins, and change only the height of the walls.
- **Weak spot**: The part of a design that fails first when it is tested. *e.g.* A first foil boat that lets water in at one low corner has a weak spot: that corner.
- **Improve**: To change a design so that it does its job better. A good improvement fixes the weak spot that a test showed. *e.g.* You improve a boat that leaked at a low corner by making all four walls the same height.
- **Material**: What a thing is made of, such as steel, wood, cloth, paper, plastic or foil. *e.g.* A kadai is made of iron, which can take the heat of a stove.
- **Float**: To stay at the top of water instead of sinking. *e.g.* A steel bowl floats in a bucket, but a steel spoon sinks.
- **Load**: The weight a design has to carry or hold up. *e.g.* The coins are the load that the foil boat carries.
- **Joint**: The place where two parts of a design are fixed together. *e.g.* Where two paper strips are stuck together with paste, that is a joint.
- **Adhesive**: A material that sticks two things together, such as glue, paste or sticky tape. *e.g.* Rice paste is an adhesive that people in India have used for a long time to stick paper.
- **Raw material**: A raw material is a material taken straight from nature to make something. Clay is the raw material for a pot, and limestone is the raw material for cement. *e.g.* A potter in a village digs wet clay from the riverbank. The clay is the raw material for her pots.
- **Mould (shape)**: A mould is a hollow shape that soft material is pressed into, so it comes out in that shape. An idli stand is a mould, and so is a brick frame. *e.g.* The cups of an idli stand are moulds. The soft batter fills them and comes out in the shape of the cup.

## Explanations
### Every design starts with a purpose · *defined*
**Definition:** A design starts with a purpose, the job the thing must do. Then come the constraints, the limits it must work within.

Before drawing anything, an engineer asks: what is this for, and who will use it? Then come the limits. How much material is there? How big can it be? How much can it cost? The same purpose with different limits leads to different designs. A water pot to carry on the head and a water pot to keep on a shelf hold the same water. But the one for carrying must be light and balanced.
- e.g. A steel tumbler's purpose is to hold a drink. Its rim is rolled over, which makes the thin edge stiffer and smooth on your lips.
- e.g. A pressure cooker's handle is plastic, because heat passes through plastic slowly, so you can hold it.
- e.g. In the foil boat test, the purpose is to carry as many coins as possible. The constraint is one square of foil.
### The design cycle: plan, build, test, improve · *defined*
**Definition:** Engineers work in a loop. They plan a design, build it, test it, look at what went wrong and improve it. Then they test again.

The loop is called the design cycle. It goes round more than once. A first version is called a prototype, and it is built to be tested. The test shows the weak spot, the part that fails first. The engineer changes the design to fix that spot, then tests again. Each round, the design gets a little better. Real engineers may go round the loop many times.
- e.g. With your foil boat, you draw it, fold it, load it with coins, see where the water comes in, and then fold a better one.
- e.g. After the Chandrayaan-2 lander crashed on the Moon in 2019, ISRO gave the Chandrayaan-3 lander stronger legs, more fuel and a bigger landing area. It landed safely in August 2023. Women engineers helped lead both missions. Ritu Karidhal and Muthayya Vanitha led work on Chandrayaan-2, and Kalpana Kalahasti on Chandrayaan-3.
- e.g. A cook trying a new rasam recipe tastes it, adds a little more tamarind, and tastes it again.
### Why a boat floats · *defined*
**Definition:** Water pushes up on anything put into it. A boat floats when the water's upward push is as big as the weight of the boat and its load.

When a boat sits in water, it pushes some water out of the way. The water pushes back up on the boat. The more water the boat pushes aside, the bigger that upward push. As you add coins, the boat sinks a little lower and pushes aside more water. A boat with a wide base and tall walls can sink deep and push aside a lot of water before water spills over the top. When water comes over the side, the boat fills and sinks. This idea is called Archimedes' principle, after a Greek thinker who wrote about it over 2,200 years ago.
- e.g. A big steel ship floats because its wide, hollow body pushes aside a huge amount of water.
- e.g. A steel spoon sinks, but a steel bowl of the same steel floats. The bowl's shape pushes aside more water.
- e.g. Push an empty plastic bottle down into a bucket. You can feel the water pushing it back up.
### Change one thing at a time · *defined*
**Definition:** In a fair test you change only one thing between two tries and keep everything else the same. Then you know what made the difference.

Say your second boat has taller walls, a wider base and squarer corners, and it holds more coins. Which change helped? You cannot tell, because you changed three things. If you change only the walls, and the boat holds more coins, the walls made the difference. That is why engineers change one thing at a time. Everything else stays the same: the size of the foil, the kind of coins, the bucket and how you place the coins.
- e.g. Use the same kind of coin for every boat, because coins of different sizes weigh different amounts.
- e.g. Always add coins from the middle outwards, so each boat is loaded the same way.
- e.g. Test each boat more than once if you can, because one try can be a fluke.
### Choosing materials · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is chosen for the job. Its properties must fit the purpose: strong, light, waterproof, bendy, cheap or easy to shape.

No material is best for everything. Foil is good for a test boat because it is thin, light, waterproof and holds a folded shape. Paper would soak up water. Steel would be too hard to fold by hand. How you join materials matters too. A joint is where two parts are fixed together, and a joint is often a weak spot. Engineers test joints as well as materials.
- e.g. Kettuvallam houseboats in Kerala were traditionally made of wooden planks tied together with coir rope, with no nails.
- e.g. Kites are made of thin paper on light bamboo sticks, so the wind can lift them.
- e.g. In the paste test, rice paste, atta paste and sticky tape all join paper, but each joint holds differently.
### A failed test is useful · *defined*
**Definition:** A test that fails shows exactly where a design is weak. That is the information you need to improve it.

When a foil boat sinks, watch closely. Did the water come in at one corner? Did the boat tip to one side? Did a fold leak? Each kind of failure points to a different fix. A low corner needs taller walls. Tipping needs a wider base or coins spread evenly. A leak needs a tighter fold. Engineers keep notes on every failure, so they do not repeat it. ISRO's chairman, S. Somanath, called the Chandrayaan-3 lander a 'failure-based design', because it was planned around everything that could go wrong.
- e.g. A boat that tips to one side tells you the load was not spread evenly, or the base was too narrow.
- e.g. A paper bag whose handle tears at the corner tells you the corner needs to be stronger.
- e.g. A kite that spins round and round tells you it needs a tail or a better balance.
### Do inventions come from one clever idea or from many tries? · *debated*
People often picture an inventor suddenly having a brilliant idea. Real inventions usually come from many rounds of testing and changing. But a good starting idea still matters. People argue about how much each counts.
- e.g. Stephanie Kwolek's very strong fibre came from testing a liquid others would have thrown away.
- e.g. Engineers today can test thousands of designs on a computer before building one.
- e.g. Even a child's first foil boat has ideas that came from the child's own head, such as giving it walls.
- **Many engineers:** Most of a design's quality comes from testing. Ideas are cheap. Building, testing and improving is the real work.
- **Many designers and mathematicians:** A good plan and careful calculation can get a design close to right the first time. Bridges are calculated before they are built, because you cannot test a full-size bridge until it fails.
- **Some historians of invention:** New ideas usually come from many people building on each other's work over years, not from one person alone.

## Mostly agreed
- Testing finds problems that a plan on paper misses. *How we know:* Engineers test small models before big builds. After the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the USA twisted and fell in a strong wind in 1940, testing bridge models in wind tunnels became usual.
- A floating object is pushed up by the water with a force as big as the weight of the water it pushes aside. *How we know:* This is Archimedes' principle. It can be checked by weighing the water that spills out of a full container when something floats in it.
- In a fair test, you change one thing at a time. *How we know:* If two things change at once, you cannot tell which one caused the result. Scientists and engineers everywhere test this way.
- Joints are often the weak spot of a design. *How we know:* When things break, they very often break where two parts meet. You can see it in torn bag handles, loose chair legs and peeling stickers.

## How we found out
**Stephanie Kwolek, a chemist at the DuPont company, 1965.** In 1965, Stephanie Kwolek was a chemist at the DuPont company in the USA. Her team wanted a light, very strong fibre, a thin thread that could be woven into strong cloth. One day she made a liquid that looked wrong. Good liquids for making fibre were usually clear and thick, like honey. Hers was cloudy and runny. People at the time expected a liquid like that to be useless, and such liquids were usually thrown away. The worker who ran the spinning machine did not want to try it at first. He was afraid the cloudy liquid would block the machine's tiny holes. Kwolek did not trust the guess. She wanted a test. She persuaded him to spin it. The fibre that came out was stiffer and stronger than anything they had made before. She tested it again and again to be sure. DuPont later called the fibre Kevlar. It is used today in helmets, cut-proof gloves, ropes and vests that protect police officers. A test, not a guess, showed what the liquid could do.  
*What people believed before:* People expected that a cloudy, runny liquid could not make a good fibre, so such liquids were usually thrown away. Kwolek insisted on testing it, and it made one of the strongest fibres ever made.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A good designer gets it right the first time.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Some experienced builders and tailors make things that work first time, because they have learned from many earlier tries.  
  Against: Their skill came from many earlier failures. New designs, even by experts, almost always need testing and changes. Chandrayaan-3 was built on what went wrong with Chandrayaan-2.
- **The strongest material always makes the best design.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Strong materials break less often.  
  Against: The best material depends on the purpose. A steel kite would never fly. A boat made of thick card would soak up water and sink. Weight, cost, bendiness and whether a material lets water in all matter too.
- **A heavier boat always sinks sooner.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Adding coins does make a foil boat sink lower.  
  Against: Shape matters as much as weight. A huge steel ship weighs far more than a foil boat but floats, because its wide hollow body pushes aside a huge amount of water.
- **Computers will soon design things better than people, with no testing in the real world.** (Some engineers and computer scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Computers can try millions of shapes quickly, and some computer-designed parts are already lighter and stronger than people's designs.  
  Against: Computer models leave things out. Real materials have flaws, and real weather and real users surprise designers. Real-world testing is still needed.

## Roots
We have built foil boats, found their weak spots and made them better. Now we ask what designing tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the foil boat test, your first boat let water in at a spot you did not expect. Only the test showed you that weak spot. Each better boat came from what the last test showed. So the shape of your best boat came from the tests with water and coins as much as from your first idea.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture an inventor having one clever idea, and the finished thing coming out exactly as planned. But in a real design, much of the final shape comes from what tests show. The maker's idea and the tests shape the design together.  
*Evidence:* In the foil boat test, nearly every change came from where the water came in: a low corner, a tipping side or a leaky fold. Kwolek's fibre, Chandrayaan-3's stronger legs and Kavya's bicycle bags in the story all came from what a test showed.

- *who am i:* You are a designer. Every time you try, see what went wrong and try again, you are doing what engineers do.
- *what is life:* Living beings design too. A weaver bird weaves a nest, ants build tunnels, and a spider spins a web, each fitted to their own needs.
- *what is this world:* Everything people make, from a tumbler to a Moon lander, was shaped by many tests against the real world.

A flat square of foil holds nothing. Folded into a boat, it floats because the water pushes it up. You did not know the boat's best shape at the start. You found it by testing your idea with the foil, the coins and the water. Every design is made this way: a maker tries an idea, the materials show what works, and the maker changes the design.

- **Tirukkural 467, chapter 47 (acting after careful thought), by Thiruvalluvar:** “எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம் துணிந்தபின் / எண்ணுவம் என்பது இழுக்கு.” Think first, then begin the work. To begin first and say 'we will think about it later' is a mistake. (In the foil boat test, planning and drawing your boat before folding it saved foil and time. But the test still showed weak spots your plan missed. Thinking first and testing after work together.)
- **A doha (couplet) attributed to Kabir:** “माटी कहे कुम्हार से, तु क्या रौंदे मोय / एक दिन ऐसा आएगा, मैं रौंदूंगी तोय” The clay says to the potter: why do you press and knead me? One day will come when I will knead you. (The potter shapes the clay, but the clay also shapes the potter's work, by how it bends, cracks and holds. In the foil boat test, the foil and the water shaped your design too.)
- Science (settled): Archimedes' principle: a floating object is pushed up by the water with a force equal to the weight of the water it pushes aside.
- Science (settled): Engineers everywhere test models and prototypes, because tests find problems that plans and calculations miss.
- Science (debated): Computers can now try millions of designs and test them in a computer model. How far this can replace real-world tests is still argued.
- Other view, Many engineers and mathematicians: A design comes from the designer's knowledge and calculation. The water does not design anything. It only follows the laws of nature. Tests check a design, but the ideas and the choices are the designer's own.
- Practice, Fold foil with your eyes closed (3 min): Take a fresh square of foil. Close your eyes. Slowly fold up one edge. Feel how the foil bends and where it resists. Fold another edge. Don't plan it. Let your fingers feel what the foil does. Open your eyes. Look at what you made. How much of this shape came from you, and how much from the foil?
- Ask: Your best boat came after two that failed. Did you design it, or did you and the water design it together?
- Ask: If a design is never finished, when do we say something is made?
- Ask: Is a failed test a mistake, or does it tell you something useful?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your best foil boat came after two that sank. Who designed it: you, or you and the water together? (hint: Think about where each change came from. Did you think of it first, or did the water show you?)
- *big:* A spider builds a web, a weaver bird a nest and a termite colony a mound. Are they designers too? (hint: A male baya weaver bird weaves a hanging nest of grass. A female inspects it before she chooses to nest there. What might she be checking?)
- *big:* Is anything ever finished being designed? (hint: Think of phones, bicycles or tumblers. Have their designs stopped changing?)
- *big:* Should a design be best for most people, or work for everyone, including people who use a wheelchair, cannot see well, or are very old? (hint: Think about steps and ramps at a railway station.)

## Story: Two Kudams and a Bicycle
*Made up. Kavya, Arjun, Thatha and the village are invented. Carrying water in kudams (round water pots) from a street tap is still common in many Tamil Nadu villages.*

Every summer holiday, Kavya stayed with her grandfather in a village near Thanjavur. Every morning, before the sun got hot, Thatha fetched water. He walked to the tap at the end of the street and came back with a kudam on each hip. A kudam is a round water pot with a narrow neck.

This summer, Thatha had hurt his back. The doctor said no lifting for a month.

"I'll carry the water," said Kavya. She was ten. She lifted one full kudam and put it down again at once. It was far too heavy to carry all the way home.

Her cousin Arjun, who was eight, laughed. "You can't even lift one."

Kavya looked at her old bicycle leaning against the wall. It had a flat metal carrier behind the seat. "I don't need to lift them far," she said. "The bicycle can carry them. I'll walk beside it and push."

First she thought about the purpose. The bicycle had to carry two kudams of water from the tap to the house. Then she thought about the limits. She could not ride with water on the back, because it would wobble. She could only use things from the house. And she must not spill much, because water was precious.

Her first design was simple. She tied a rope to each kudam's neck and hung one kudam on each side of the carrier.

"We'll test it with half-full pots," she told Arjun. "If it fails, we lose less water."

They filled both pots halfway at the tap and tied them on. Kavya began to push. After five steps, the kudams started to swing. One swung into the back wheel and banged against the spokes. Water splashed out. The bicycle jerked to one side, and Arjun had to grab it.

Kavya did not get cross. She knelt down and looked. "The rope is too long," she said. "The pots swing, and when they swing, they hit the wheel. The swinging is the weak spot."

For her second design, she tied the ropes shorter, so the pots hung higher. They tested again. The pots did not hit the spokes now. But after ten steps, the bicycle leaned more and more to the left.

"Why does it lean?" asked Arjun.

Kavya looked inside the pots. The left one had more water in it. They had not filled them equally. "It's like a see-saw," she said. "The heavier side goes down. Both sides must weigh the same."

They filled the pots to the same mark, using a steel tumbler to count. Five tumblers in each. This time the bicycle stayed upright. But the ropes still rubbed and slid along the carrier, and the pots twisted round.

Kavya went to Thatha. "Do you have two old cloth bags?" she asked. He gave her two of the thick yellow cloth bags from the ration shop.

Her third design put each kudam inside a bag. She tied each bag's handles tightly to the carrier, and its bottom corner to the frame, so it could not swing. The bags held the round pots snugly, like a hand holding a ball.

They tested with half-full pots. The bicycle rolled straight. Nothing swung. Nothing spilled. Then they tested with full pots. Kavya pushed slowly and carefully, all the way home, while Arjun walked beside the bags.

Thatha was sitting on the front step. He watched the bicycle come up the street with two full kudams and not a drop spilled. "How did you think of all that?" he asked.

"I didn't think of all of it," said Kavya. "I thought of the first idea. The swinging showed me the second. The leaning showed me the third."

Arjun had been listening. The next morning, he took a notebook to the tap. He drew the bicycle, the bags and the knots. Under it, he wrote three things that went wrong and how Kavya had fixed each one.

"What's that for?" asked Kavya.

"For next summer," said Arjun. "Then whoever fetches the water can start from your third design, not your first."

Kavya smiled. That was exactly what engineers do.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavya did not invent the final carrier in one go. Each test failed in a different way, and each failure pointed at what to change. Swinging pots showed the ropes were too long. Leaning showed the loads were not equal. Twisting showed the pots needed holding firmly. She tested with half-full pots first, so a failure would waste less water. This is the design cycle: plan, build, test, improve, and test again.

- Ask: What was the purpose of Kavya's design, and what were her limits?
- Ask: Why did Kavya test with half-full pots first?
- Ask: What went wrong in each test, and how did she fix it?
- Ask: Kavya said she did not think of all of it herself. What did she mean?
- Ask: Arjun wrote down every failure. Why might that help the next designer?

### g1 · Weak Spot Detective (15 min)
- **rules:** Lay the 10 fix cards face up. Shuffle the problem cards face down.; Take turns. On your turn, turn over a problem card and read it aloud.; Find the weak spot, then pick the fix card that mends it. Say why. Fix cards stay on the table, so any fix can be used again.; Score 1 point if the fix mends the weak spot. Some problems have more than one good fix: any fix that really works scores.; The game ends when all 10 problem cards are used. With two or more players, the most points wins. If you play together, try to score 9 or 10 out of 10.; Playing alone: race the clock. How many can you match in 5 minutes?

  *Parent does:* Take turns: turn over a problem card, name the weak spot and choose a fix.

  *Parent does:* Play 3 rounds: build for 3 minutes, test, improve for 1 minute, test again.

  *Child mode:* Build for 3 minutes. Test it. Fix it for 1 minute. Test again.

  *Parent does:* Talk about the best round.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Foil Boat: build it, test it, make it better (40 min)
- **question:** How many coins can a boat made from one square of foil carry, and how much better can you make it in three tries?
- **predict:** Guess first. How many coins will your first boat carry before water comes in? Will your third boat carry more, fewer or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every boat uses one square of foil of the same size, the same kind of coin and the same bucket of water. Coins always go in one at a time, starting from the middle and spreading outwards. Between two boats, change only one thing, so you know what made the difference.
- **safety:** Fold in the cut edges of the foil, because they can be sharp. Keep coins away from children under 5, who may put them in their mouths.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each boat. Draw each boat from the side, or take a photo of it, and mark with an arrow where the water came in first.
- **results table:** Boat | What I changed | Coins it carried | Where the water came in first
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out one foil square, the coins and the bucket of water. 2. Read out the job and the rules.  
  *Parent says:* Here is your job, the kind a real engineer gets. Build a boat that carries as many coins as possible. The rules: one square of foil per boat, no tape, and coins go in one at a time. How many coins do you think your first boat will carry? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make a foil boat that carries lots of coins. Use one square of foil only. No tape.
  - Ask: What must the boat do? What are the rules? (good: To carry as many coins as possible.; We can only use one square of foil, and no tape.)
  - Note: Write the guess in the notebook. Any number is fine. Most children guess between 5 and 30.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws boat 1 from the side, or describes it out loud. 2. Say how big the base is and how high the walls will be.  
  *Parent says:* Engineers plan before they build. Draw your boat, or tell me the plan. Show how big the base is and how high the walls go.  
  *Child mode:* Draw your boat first, or tell your grown-up your plan. Show the bottom and the sides. How tall are the sides?
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the foil square into boat 1, following the drawing. 2. Pinch the corners tight so there are no holes.  
  *Parent says:* Now build it the way you drew it. Pinch the corners tightly. A hole would let water in.  
  *Child mode:* Fold your boat like your drawing. Pinch the corners tight. No holes!
  - Note: Let your child fold it their own way, even if you think it will not work well. A flat tray with low sides is a very common first design. It is a good first try, because the test will show what to change.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Float boat 1 gently in the middle of the water. 2. Add coins one at a time, starting in the middle, then spreading out. 3. Watch closely. Stop when water comes in. 4. Count the coins and write the number. 5. Write where the water came in first.  
  *Parent says:* Let's test it. One coin at a time, starting in the middle. Watch the edges carefully. Where does the water come in first?  
  *Child mode:* Float your boat. Add one coin at a time. Start in the middle. Watch the sides. Where does water come in? Count the coins.
  - Ask: Where did the water come in first? Why there? (good: Over the lowest corner.; On the side where the coins were piled.; Through a hole in a fold.)
  - Note: Count the coins up to the last one that went in before water poured in. Dry the coins on the plate. Water often comes in where a wall is lowest, or the boat tips to one side because the coins were not spread evenly.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the drawing of boat 1 and the note about where water came in. 2. Choose one change to fix that weak spot. 3. Tell your grown-up the one change you will make for boat 2.  
  *Parent says:* That spot where the water came in is the weak spot. What one change would fix it? Only change one thing, so we know if it helped.  
  *Child mode:* Where did the water come in? That is the weak spot. Pick one change to fix it. Say your change out loud.
  - Ask: Why should we change only one thing? (good: So we know which change made the difference.; If we change two things, we cannot tell which one helped.)
  - Note: Good single changes: make all the walls the same height, make the walls taller, or fold the corners more tightly. With one square of foil, taller walls mean a smaller base, so try it and see which works better. If your child wants to change several things, say they can, but then you won't know which change helped. Let them choose.

  *Parent does:* 1. Fold boat 2 from a new foil square. 2. Test it exactly as you tested boat 1. 3. Write the number of coins and where the water came in.  
  *Parent says:* Same foil, same coins, same bucket. Only one thing has changed. Let's see if it helps.  
  *Child mode:* Fold boat 2 from new foil. Test it the same way. Write the number. Did your change help?
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at boat 2's weak spot. 2. Make one more change for boat 3. 3. Fold it from a new foil square and test it the same way.  
  *Parent says:* One more round. What did boat 2 show us? Change one thing again, and test.  
  *Child mode:* Look where boat 2 let water in. Change one more thing. Build boat 3. Test it.
- **s8 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the three numbers. 2. Your child says or writes three things: what they think helps a boat carry more, what they saw, and why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Which boat carried the most? What helped the most? Why do you think it helped?  
  *Child mode:* Which boat carried most coins? What change helped? Why?
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: taller, even walls help a boat carry more. What I saw: boat 1 carried 9 coins and sank at a low corner, and boat 3 carried 31. Why: a boat with taller walls can sink deeper and push aside more water before the water spills in.
  - Check: Two foil boats are made from the same size of foil. Boat A has walls 1 centimetre high. Boat B has walls 3 centimetres high. Which will most likely carry more coins? → **Boat B, because it can sink deeper before water comes in**
### e2 · The Paste Test: which kitchen paste joins paper best? (30 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Which joins two strips of paper best: rice paste, atta paste, sticky tape or plain water?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which joint will hold best when you pull it? Which will come apart first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same paper, strips of the same size and the same overlap for every joint. Only the paste changes. Make 2 joints with each paste, because one try can be a fluke. Plain water is the 'no paste' joint, so you can see what the pastes add.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Stick one broken strip from each paste into your notebook.
- **results table:** What joined the strips | Joint 1: held, slid apart or paper tore? | Joint 2: held, slid apart or paper tore? | Where it broke
- **schedule:** day 1: Make the pastes and join 8 pairs of strips. Label them. Leave them flat to dry overnight. (15 min); day 2: Pull each joint apart slowly. Write what happened and where it broke. (15 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the four ways of joining. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Engineers choose materials for joints too. A joint is where two pieces are joined, and it is often the weak spot. Which of these will hold paper best? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at rice paste, atta paste, tape and water. Which will hold paper best? Why?
  - Ask: Why test plain water too? (good: To see what happens with no paste.; So we know the paste is doing the work.)
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On each strip, draw a pencil line 2 centimetres from one end. 2. Spread a thin layer of paste up to the line. 3. Press a second strip on top, overlapping up to the line. 4. Make 2 joints with each: rice paste, atta paste, tape and water. 5. On each pair, write in pencil what joined it.  
  *Parent says:* Every joint must overlap by the same 2 centimetres. Only the paste changes. That keeps it fair.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line 2 centimetres from the end. Spread paste up to the line. Press the other strip on top. Make 2 of each. On each pair, write what joined it.
  - Note: Use only a thin layer of paste, about as much as covers the overlap. For tape, use a piece 3 centimetres long across the overlap. Tape is a different kind of joint from the pastes, because it covers the outside of both strips. So be careful when you compare tape with the pastes.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay all the strips flat on a plate, away from fans, to dry overnight.  
  *Parent says:* Pastes need time to dry. We'll test them tomorrow.  
  *Child mode:* Lay them flat. Leave them till tomorrow.
  - Note: In a humid flat the pastes may still be damp after one night. Leave them another night.
  - Wait: Leave the strips to dry overnight
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold one end of a joined strip in each hand. 2. Pull apart slowly and steadily, counting to five. 3. Look closely: did the joint slide apart, or did the paper tear? 4. Write it in the table. 5. Do all 8.  
  *Parent says:* Pull slowly, the same way each time. Where does it break? At the joint, or in the paper beside it?  
  *Child mode:* Pull each strip slowly. Count to five. Did the joint slide apart? Or did the paper tear? Write it down.
  - Note: Usually the plain water joint falls apart at once. Dried rice and atta paste often hold so well that the paper tears beside the joint, not at it. Tape usually holds, but may peel slowly. Your results may differ. That is what the test is for.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child says which joint they think is strongest, what they saw in the test, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which joint was strongest? If the paper tore and the joint held, what does that tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Which joint held best? Tell your grown-up what you saw.
  - Check: In the paste test, a rice paste joint held, but the paper tore just beside it. Where was the weak spot? → **The paper**

## Worksheets
### Plan, Build, Test (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a purpose, not a material?  
   Options: Keeping rain off your head / Steel / Cotton  
   **Answer:** Keeping rain off your head. *Why:* Keeping rain off is a job, so it is a purpose. Steel and cotton are materials.
2. (mcq) What is a prototype?  
   Options: The final thing sold in a shop / A first trial version built to be tested / A drawing of a boat  
   **Answer:** A first trial version built to be tested. *Why:* A prototype is built to be tested, so you can find what to improve. A drawing is a plan, not a prototype.
3. (mcq) Your foil boat tipped and sank with all the coins on one side. What should you try next?  
   Options: Use more coins / Use a bigger bucket / Spread the coins evenly  
   **Answer:** Spread the coins evenly. *Why:* The load was all on one side, so that side went under. Spreading the coins keeps the boat level.
4. (mcq) Why do we pinch the corners of a foil boat tightly?  
   Options: To make it shiny / To make it lighter / So water cannot leak through gaps in the folds  
   **Answer:** So water cannot leak through gaps in the folds. *Why:* A gap in a fold lets water in, so the boat fills and sinks. Pinching closes the gaps.
5. (truefalse) A first design usually works perfectly.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. First designs almost always have a weak spot. Testing finds it, so you can improve the design.
6. (truefalse) In a fair test, you change only one thing at a time.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Then you know which change made the difference.
7. (fill) The place where two parts of a design are fixed together is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['joint']. *Why:* Joints are often the weak spot, so engineers test them carefully.
8. (fill) The part of a design that fails first in a test is called the ____ spot.  
   **Answer:** ['weak']. *Why:* The weak spot shows you what to fix next.
9. (order) Put the steps of the design cycle in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Plan it', 'Build it', 'Test it', 'Improve it']. *Why:* First plan, then build, then test, then improve. Then you test again, so the loop goes round.
10. (match) Match each object to the reason for one part of its design.  
   **Answer:** Steel tumbler → rolled rim, stiff and smooth on the lips; Pressure cooker → plastic handle, so heat passes slowly to your hand; Ladle → long handle, to keep your hand away from hot food; Kudam → narrow neck, so less water spills. *Why:* Each part was designed to solve a problem in using that object.
11. (sort) Sort these into purposes and constraints for a school bag.  
   **Answer:** carry books to school → Purpose; must not weigh more than 1 kilogram empty → Constraint; keep a tiffin box upright → Purpose; must cost less than 500 rupees → Constraint; must fit on a child's back → Constraint; keep a water bottle handy → Purpose. *Why:* Purposes are jobs the bag must do. Constraints are limits it must work within, such as weight, cost and size.
12. (write) Your foil boat sank after 12 coins. Write what you would look at before folding the next one, and why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says to look at where the water came in first, because that is the weak spot, and the next boat should fix that spot.. *Why:* This tests the idea that a failed test shows what to change.
13. (write) Why is foil a better material than paper for a test boat? Give two reasons.  
   **Answer:** Foil does not soak up water, and it holds a folded shape. Paper soaks up water, goes soft and sinks.. *Why:* A good answer names two properties that suit the job.
14. (draw) Draw a foil boat that you think will carry many coins. Label the base and the walls, and write how tall the walls are.  
   **Answer:** A wide, flat base with walls all the same height, about 2 to 4 centimetres tall, and tight corners.. *Why:* Wide bases and even, tall walls let a boat push aside a lot of water before water comes in.
15. (perspective) Kavya and her cousin Arjun are planning a bird water bowl for their balcony.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. Shallow is safer for small birds, who can drown in deep water and need to stand. Deep holds water longer in the heat. A strong answer may suggest a design that does both, such as a deep bowl with stones inside to stand on.. *Why:* Both have a point. Designers often face a trade-off, where making one thing better makes another worse.
### Find the Weak Spot (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A foil boat carried 10 coins. With taller walls, it carried 18. Then the corners were pinched tighter too, and it carried 25. Which statement is true?  
   Options: Only the tighter corners helped / Neither change helped / Each change helped  
   **Answer:** Each change helped. *Why:* The coins went up after each single change: 10 to 18, then 18 to 25. Because only one thing changed each time, you can tell each change helped.
2. (mcq) A big steel ship weighs thousands of tonnes but floats. Why?  
   Options: Steel is lighter than water / Ships have engines that hold them up / Its wide, hollow body pushes aside a huge amount of water  
   **Answer:** Its wide, hollow body pushes aside a huge amount of water. *Why:* A ship's hollow shape pushes aside enough water that the upward push equals its weight. Solid steel is heavier than the same amount of water, so a steel bar sinks.
3. (mcq) You are testing a new way to carry water pots on a bicycle. Why test with half-full pots first?  
   Options: So a failure would spill less water / Half-full pots look nicer / Because full pots don't fit  
   **Answer:** So a failure would spill less water. *Why:* Testing with a smaller load first means a failure costs less. Engineers often test small or with less before the full test.
4. (mcq) In the paste test, a tape joint peeled slowly while the rice paste joint held and the paper tore. Which joint was stronger?  
   Options: Rice paste / Tape / You can't tell  
   **Answer:** Rice paste. *Why:* If the paper tore before the rice paste joint gave way, the joint was stronger than the paper. The tape joint gave way first.
5. (truefalse) A stronger material always makes a better design.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The best material depends on the job. A steel kite would be very strong but would never fly.
6. (truefalse) Every object in your home was designed by someone for a purpose.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Someone decided what each thing was for and how to make it, even a simple steel tumbler.
7. (mcq) In a click pen, what does the spring do?  
   Options: It pushes the refill back in after you click / It holds the ink / It makes the pen write  
   **Answer:** It pushes the refill back in after you click. *Why:* The spring pushes the refill back into the pen when you click again. The ink is held in the refill, and the tiny ball at the tip makes the pen write.
8. (fill) Water pushes ____ on a boat floating in it.  
   **Answer:** ['up', 'upwards']. *Why:* The upward push of water is what holds a boat up.
9. (order) Put these steps of making a design better in order, first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['Build a first version', 'Test it and see what goes wrong', 'Change the design to fix what went wrong', 'Test the new version']. *Why:* Each new design comes from what went wrong in the one before. Then it must be tested again.
10. (match) Match each failure to the fix that mends its weak spot.  
   **Answer:** A boat lets water in at one low corner → make all the walls the same height; A boat leaks through a fold → pinch the folds tight; A phone stand slides flat → fold up a lip at the bottom; A water bowl tips when a crow lands → use a wider, heavier bowl. *Why:* A good fix mends the exact spot that failed.
11. (sort) Is each one a fair way to compare two foil boats?  
   **Answer:** same size of foil for both → Fair; one-rupee coins for one boat, five-rupee coins for the other → Not fair; coins added from the middle out for both → Fair; one boat in a bucket, one in a sink full of soapy water → Not fair; same bucket of water for both → Fair. *Why:* In a fair test only the boat's design changes. Different coins or different water would change the result too.
12. (write) A friend says, 'My boat sank, so my design was a failure and I should give up.' What would you tell your friend?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the sinking shows where the weak spot is, so your friend now knows what to change. Every engineer's first design fails somewhere.. *Why:* This tests the idea that a failed test is useful information.
13. (draw) Draw the design cycle as a loop with four arrows. Write one step on each arrow.  
   **Answer:** A circle of four arrows: plan, build, test, improve, with improve leading back to plan or build.. *Why:* The cycle is a loop because you test again after improving.
14. (label) Label the parts of this foil boat: base, wall, corner, load, waterline.  
   **Answer:** ['base: the flat bottom', 'wall: the sides that stand up', 'corner: where two walls meet', 'load: the coins inside', 'waterline: where the water reaches on the outside']. *Why:* Naming the parts makes it easier to say exactly where a weak spot is.
15. (perspective) Two engineers are designing a footbridge over a canal in Chennai.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then sees the other's point. Real engineers do both: they calculate, then test models, as with bridge models tested in wind tunnels.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. Calculations are fast and cheap, and tests catch what calculations miss.
### Think Like an Engineer (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Two foil boats carried 20 and 22 coins. Someone says the second design is clearly better. What is the best reply?  
   Options: That is right / The difference is small. Test each boat again to check it was not a fluke. / Both are bad  
   **Answer:** The difference is small. Test each boat again to check it was not a fluke.. *Why:* A difference of 2 coins could come from chance, such as where a coin landed. Testing again shows whether the difference is real.
2. (mcq) A boat made from a 15 centimetre square carried 30 coins. What would you predict for a boat of the same shape from a 30 centimetre square?  
   Options: Many more coins / About the same / Fewer coins  
   **Answer:** Many more coins. *Why:* A bigger boat of the same shape has a much bigger base and taller walls, so it can push aside far more water. It should carry many more coins, more than twice as many.
3. (mcq) Why might a designer make a phone stand from card instead of steel?  
   Options: Card is stronger / Steel would bend / Card is cheap, light, easy to cut and strong enough for the job  
   **Answer:** Card is cheap, light, easy to cut and strong enough for the job. *Why:* A material only needs to be strong enough. Card is cheap and easy to shape, and a phone is light.
4. (truefalse) If a design passes its test, it can never be improved.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A design that works may still be made cheaper, lighter, safer or easier to use. Phones and bicycles keep being redesigned.
5. (fill) When a test shows a weak spot, a designer changes the design and then ____ it again.  
   **Answer:** ['tests', 'test', 'checks']. *Why:* A change might fix one problem and cause another, so each new version must be tested too.
6. (order) Put these steps of a surprising discovery in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Someone makes something new', 'People guess it will be useless', 'The maker tests it anyway', 'The test shows it is far better than expected']. *Why:* The guess said 'useless'. The test showed the opposite. A test, not a guess, decides.
7. (write) The Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the USA was strong enough to carry cars, but in 1940 it twisted in a strong wind and fell. What does this tell us about testing a design?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the design was tested for one load, the cars, but not for another, the wind. A design must be tested for everything it will meet. After this, engineers began testing bridge models in wind tunnels.. *Why:* This tests whether the child sees that a test must match the real conditions.
8. (write) You want to know whether taller walls or a wider base helps a foil boat more. Plan a fair test. Say what you will change and what you will keep the same.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer makes one boat with taller walls and one with a wider base from the same size of foil, with the same coins, bucket and loading, and compares each with a plain starting boat. With one square of foil, a wider base means lower walls, so the test shows which choice carries more coins.. *Why:* A good plan changes one thing at a time and keeps everything else the same.
9. (write) A weaver bird builds a nest. A child builds a foil boat. In what ways are they both designers? In what ways are they different?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says both make something for a purpose, choose materials and shape them. Differences: the bird uses grass and his beak to weave a nest; the child uses foil and tests with coins.. *Why:* This asks the child to compare design across living beings.
10. (draw) Design a container to carry 2 idlis to school without squashing them, using only paper and string. Draw it and label how it protects the idlis.  
   **Answer:** A strong design has stiff sides, for example folded or rolled paper, a lid, and a handle tied at strong points.. *Why:* This applies purpose, constraints and joints to a new problem.
11. (label) This bicycle carries two round water pots in cloth bags. Label what each part does: cloth bag, tied handles, tied bottom corner, equal water.  
   **Answer:** ['cloth bag: holds the round pot snugly', 'tied handles: fix the bag to the carrier', 'tied bottom corner: stops the bag swinging into the wheel', 'equal water: keeps both sides the same weight so the bicycle stays upright']. *Why:* Each part fixes a weak spot: slipping, swinging or tipping.
12. (perspective) After Chandrayaan-2's lander crashed in 2019, people in India argued about what to do next.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and gives a reason, then gives the best case for another view. It may use the fact that Chandrayaan-3 landed in 2023 after the design was improved.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement about purpose: what should a country design and build first?
13. (sort) Sort these changes to a foil boat: will each most likely help it carry more coins, or fewer?  
   **Answer:** make the walls taller → More; cut a small slit in the base → Fewer; spread the coins evenly → More; make one wall much lower than the others → Fewer; pinch the corners tighter → More. *Why:* Changes that let the boat sink deeper without water getting in help. Holes and low walls let water in sooner.
14. (match) Match each Indian design to the problem it solves.  
   **Answer:** Kattumaram, logs tied together → a boat that cannot fill with water and sink; Kettuvallam planks stitched with coir → joining wooden planks without iron nails; Food safe legs in bowls of water → keeping ants out of food; Clay matka → keeping water cool. *Why:* Each design solves a real problem with local materials. A kattumaram is a raft of logs, so water flows through it instead of filling it.
15. (truefalse) One way of carrying water is the best for every job.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A narrow-necked kudam, a bucket and a bottle each suit a different job. The best design depends on the job.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is the purpose of a design?  
   Options: What it is made of / How much it costs / The job it is made to do  
   **Answer:** The job it is made to do. *Why:* The purpose is the job the thing must do. What it is made of is its material, and cost is one of its limits. Picking 'material' mixes up what a thing is for with what it is made of.
2. (mcq) In the foil boat test, each boat must be made from one square of foil. What is this rule called?  
   Options: A prototype / A float / A constraint  
   **Answer:** A constraint. *Why:* A constraint is a limit a design must work within. A prototype is a trial version, and float is what a boat does.
3. (mcq) In the design cycle, what comes right after testing a design?  
   Options: Throw it away / Improve it, then test again / Paint it  
   **Answer:** Improve it, then test again. *Why:* After a test, you look at what went wrong and improve the design, then test again. That loop is the design cycle. Picking 'throw it away' misses that a failed test shows you what to fix.
4. (mcq) A foil boat sank when water came in over one low corner. What should you change for the next boat?  
   Options: Use more coins / Use a smaller bucket / Make that corner as high as the other walls  
   **Answer:** Make that corner as high as the other walls. *Why:* The low corner was the weak spot, so raising it fixes the problem. More coins would make it sink sooner, and the bucket size does not change where water comes in.
5. (mcq) You changed the walls, the base and the corners of a foil boat all at once, and it carried more coins. What can you NOT tell?  
   Options: Which change made it better / That the new boat is better / How many coins it carried  
   **Answer:** Which change made it better. *Why:* Three things changed at once, so you cannot tell which one helped. To know, change one thing at a time. You can still see that the new boat is better and count its coins.
6. (mcq) Why does a foil boat float?  
   Options: Foil is lighter than air / The coins hold it up / The water pushes up on the boat  
   **Answer:** The water pushes up on the boat. *Why:* Water pushes up on anything put into it. The more water a boat pushes aside, the bigger that push. Foil is much heavier than air, and the coins push the boat down, not up.
7. (mcq) Why does a boat with taller walls usually carry more coins?  
   Options: It can sink deeper and push aside more water before water spills in / It is made of thicker foil / Tall walls keep the wind off  
   **Answer:** It can sink deeper and push aside more water before water spills in. *Why:* Taller walls let the boat sink lower without water coming over the top. A lower boat pushes aside more water, so the water pushes up harder. It uses the same foil, not thicker foil.
8. (mcq) Which is the best material for a test boat that you fold by hand?  
   Options: Paper towel / A steel plate / Aluminium foil  
   **Answer:** Aluminium foil. *Why:* Foil is thin, light, waterproof and holds a folded shape. Paper towel soaks up water and sinks. Steel is too stiff to fold by hand.
9. (mcq) In the paste test, the plain water joint fell apart at once. Why did we test plain water at all?  
   Options: Because water is the best glue / To see what happens with no paste, so we know the paste does the work / To make the paper soft  
   **Answer:** To see what happens with no paste, so we know the paste does the work. *Why:* Plain water is the 'no paste' joint. Comparing with it shows what each paste adds. It is not a glue.
10. (mcq) A team's first Moon lander crashed. What is the best thing to do for the next one?  
   Options: Make it look better / Hope the Moon has changed / Study what went wrong, and make those parts stronger  
   **Answer:** Study what went wrong, and make those parts stronger. *Why:* A failure shows where a design is weak. India's Chandrayaan-2 lander crashed in 2019. ISRO gave Chandrayaan-3 stronger legs and more fuel, and it landed safely in 2023.
11. (mcq) A scientist makes a cloudy, runny liquid. Everyone expects liquids like it to be useless. What should she do?  
   Options: Throw it away / Mix it with water / Test it anyway, for example by spinning it into a fibre  
   **Answer:** Test it anyway, for example by spinning it into a fibre. *Why:* A guess is not a test. Stephanie Kwolek insisted on testing a liquid like this, and found it made a very strong fibre, later called Kevlar.
12. (mcq) You put out a bird water bowl. A crow lands on the edge and the bowl tips over. What is the best improvement?  
   Options: Use a wider, heavier bowl, or put a stone in the middle / Fill it to the top / Move it into the sun  
   **Answer:** Use a wider, heavier bowl, or put a stone in the middle. *Why:* A wider, heavier bowl, or a weight in the middle, keeps it steady when a bird lands on the edge. More water might not stop the tipping, and sun would make the water hot.

## Applications
- Engineers test small models of ships in long water tanks before building the real ship.
- Bridge models are tested in wind tunnels before the real bridge is built.
- ISRO tests rockets, landers and satellites on the ground, again and again, before launch.
- Car makers crash-test cars with dummies inside, to make seats and belts safer.
- Tailors make a trial fit and adjust it before the final stitching.
- Cooks test a new recipe on the family, then change the spices.
- Phone apps are tested by users, and then improved in updates.
- Ramps at railway stations and schools are designed so everyone can use them, including people in wheelchairs.

## Open questions
- **Can a computer design things better than people, without testing them in the real world?** Computers already design some parts that are lighter and stronger than people's. But real materials and real users still surprise designers, and nobody knows how far computer design can go.
- **How do weaver birds and other animals learn to build better?** Young male weaver birds seem to get better at weaving with practice, but scientists are still finding out how much they learn and how much they are born knowing.
- **What is the best way to design for everyone, including people with very different bodies and needs?** Designers still argue about whether one design can suit everyone, or whether different people need different designs.

## Connected maths
- Three foil boats carried 9, 22 and 31 coins. How many more coins did boat 3 carry than boat 1? **22 more coins.**. Boat 3 carried 31 coins and boat 1 carried 9. 31 minus 9 is 22. So boat 3 carried 22 more coins. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Your foil square is 15 centimetres on each side. You fold up walls 3 centimetres high on all four sides. How long is each side of the flat base? **9 centimetres.**. Each side loses 3 centimetres for the wall at one end and 3 centimetres at the other end. 3 + 3 = 6. 15 minus 6 is 9. So the base is 9 centimetres on each side. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- Which of these flat shapes can fold into an open box with no lid? A: a plus shape of 5 squares, one in the middle and one on each side. B: a straight row of 5 squares. **Shape A.**. In shape A, the middle square is the base. The four squares around it fold up to make four walls. In shape B, the squares are in a line, so they roll up into a tube with an open end and no proper base. A flat shape that folds into a solid is called a net. (queues `math.shapes-3d.nets.L4`)
- In the paste test, you test 4 ways of joining (rice paste, atta paste, tape and water), and make 2 joints of each. Each joint needs 2 strips. How many strips do you need? **16 strips.**. 4 ways times 2 joints is 8 joints. Each joint needs 2 strips. 8 times 2 is 16. So you need 16 strips. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- A foil boat carried 18 coins on the first try and 6 coins on the second try, with no change to the boat. Another boat carried 14 and 15. Which boat gave more reliable results? Why might the first boat's two results be so different? **The second boat, which carried 14 and 15.**. The second boat's two results, 14 and 15, are only 1 apart. The first boat's results, 18 and 6, are 12 apart. Something else changed between the first boat's two tries. Perhaps the boat was squashed after the first test, or the coins were piled on one side the second time. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
