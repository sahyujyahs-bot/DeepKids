# One bowl, six tastes: how traditional and festival meals balance a diet
`sci.food.balanced-diet.L4`

**Summary.** At Ugadi, many families in Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana start the year with a pachadi that holds six tastes in one bowl: sweet jaggery, sour tamarind, salt, bitter neem flowers, hot pepper and raw mango. Why would a festival dish contain a bitter flower? Your child tests the school-book 'tongue map' with a blind taste test, and finds that every taste can be felt all over the tongue. They sprout moong and time how long each batch takes to cook, to see what soaking and sprouting change in a food. They plan a balanced festival feast from many parts of India, keep a seven-day food diary, and find out why a sweet taste does not always mean a food is good for the body.

**Intro.** *Look at a spoon of Ugadi pachadi. It holds sweet jaggery, sour tamarind, salt, bitter neem flowers, hot pepper and raw mango. All six are in one spoon. Why would anyone put a bitter flower in a festival dish?* Many traditional meals look as if someone planned them. A Kerala sadya on a banana leaf can have twenty dishes, and each dish has its own place on the leaf. A Gujarati thali has roti, dal, rice, a vegetable, buttermilk and something sweet. Nobody wrote these meals from a nutrition book. Yet they hold grains, pulses, vegetables and milk, and a mix of tastes. So how does a meal become balanced? And is a tasty meal always a balanced one? In this unit you test your own tongue, wake up some moong seeds, and plan a festival feast.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Staple food**: The main food a region eats every day, which gives most of its energy. It is usually a grain or a root. *e.g.* Rice in Kerala, wheat in Punjab, ragi in parts of Karnataka and bajra in Rajasthan are staple foods.
- **Food variety**: Eating many different foods across a day and a week, so that the body gets all the nutrients it needs. *e.g.* Dal, rice, a green vegetable, curd and a fruit on a plate is more variety than rice with a pickle.
- **Traditional food**: A food or meal that families in a place have cooked and passed on for many generations. *e.g.* Idli with sambar in Tamil Nadu and makki roti with sarson saag in Punjab are traditional meals.
- **Seasonal food**: A food that grows or is eaten in one part of the year. *e.g.* Raw mango is a summer food. Sesame and jaggery sweets are eaten in the cool months.
- **Taste bud**: A tiny group of taste cells. Taste buds sit on your tongue, the roof of your mouth and the back of your throat. *e.g.* You have thousands of taste buds, and each one can sense more than one taste.
- **Umami**: The taste of savoury, 'meaty' foods. It was named in Japan in 1908 and is made by a chemical called glutamate. *e.g.* Ripe tomato, mushroom, cheese and a spoon of well-cooked dal all have umami.
- **Shad rasa**: The six tastes of Ayurveda, a very old Indian system of medicine: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent. *e.g.* Jaggery is sweet, tamarind is sour, salt is salty, chilli is pungent, neem is bitter and raw banana is astringent.
- **Pungent**: The hot, burning feeling of chilli, pepper and ginger. Science counts it as a feeling of heat and pain, not as a taste. *e.g.* Black pepper in venn pongal makes your tongue feel hot.
- **Astringent**: A dry, puckering feeling in the mouth, like a tight skin on the tongue. Science counts it as a feeling of touch. *e.g.* A raw banana, strong tea and an unripe guava all feel astringent.
- **Millet**: A small-seeded grain that grows well in dry land with little water. Ragi, jowar and bajra are millets. *e.g.* Ragi mudde in Karnataka, jowar roti in Maharashtra and bajra rotla in Gujarat are made from millets.
- **Sprouting**: When a soaked seed wakes up and starts to grow a tiny root. A sprouted seed is alive and growing. *e.g.* Moong beans soaked and kept damp for a day send out small white tails.
- **Fermented food**: A food changed by living microbes, such as yeast and bacteria, into something new. *e.g.* Idli and dosa batter, curd and toddy are fermented foods.
- **Ultra-processed food**: A packaged food made in a factory from refined ingredients, with added sugar, salt, fat, flavours or colours. *e.g.* Many chips, instant noodles, fizzy drinks and packet biscuits are ultra-processed foods.

## Explanations
### A balanced diet is a pattern over many days · *defined*
**Definition:** A balanced diet is a mix of many different foods, eaten over days and weeks, that gives the body enough of every nutrient it needs.

In Class 3 you built a balanced plate. A plate is one meal. A body does not count meals, it counts days. You can eat very little greens at lunch and make up for it at dinner or tomorrow. What matters is the pattern. No single food has everything the body needs. The one food that comes close is a mother's milk, for a baby in the first six months. After that, a body needs variety. Eating many kinds of grain, pulse, vegetable, fruit and milk food helps, because each one carries a different mix of nutrients. A balanced diet is also not the same for everyone. A growing child, a grandmother, a runner and a pregnant woman need different amounts. The Indian Council of Medical Research and its National Institute of Nutrition, in Hyderabad, publish the Dietary Guidelines for Indians that families can use.
- e.g. A child who eats dal, a green vegetable and curd at lunch can have a lighter dinner, and still be balanced over the day.
- e.g. A family that eats only rice and potato for a week is not balanced, even if each meal fills the stomach.
- e.g. A pregnant woman needs more iron than other adults, so her plate holds more greens, pulses and iron-rich foods.
### Traditional meals are balanced in their own way · *defined*
**Definition:** A traditional meal is a set of dishes that people in a region have long eaten together. Many of them join a staple food, a pulse, a vegetable and a milk food.

Look at the same job on different plates. In Tamil Nadu a meal is rice, sambar or rasam, a vegetable, curd and a pickle. In Gujarat it is roti, dal, rice, a vegetable, buttermilk and a sweet. In Bengal it is rice, dal, fish or vegetables, and a bitter dish like shukto at the start. In Nagaland and Manipur it is rice, boiled leafy vegetables, and a chutney of chilli and fermented fish or bamboo shoot. In Punjab it is makki roti with sarson saag, butter and lassi. The dishes are different, but each plate has a staple for energy, a pulse or fish or milk for building, and vegetables for protection. These meals grew over many generations from what the land grew, and from families noticing which foods kept them strong. They were not planned from a table of nutrients, and they are not perfect. But they often work.
- e.g. A Kerala sadya joins rice, parippu, sambar, avial, thoran, pulissery, pickles and payasam on one banana leaf.
- e.g. A Gujarati thali has bajra or wheat roti, dal, kadhi, a vegetable, rice and chaas.
- e.g. A Hyderabadi biryani with raita joins rice, meat or vegetables, and curd.
### Six tastes or five: two ways of sorting what the tongue tells us · *debated*
Ayurveda, a very old Indian system of medicine, sorts food by six tastes, called shad rasa. They are sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent. The Charaka Samhita, a very old Ayurvedic text, describes them in its chapter on tastes. It teaches that a healthy meal holds all six. Taste scientists today find five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Each has its own kind of sensor on the taste cells. Pungent and astringent are missing from the science list, because they are not tastes. The heat of chilli is a signal of heat and pain. A dry, puckering mouth after raw banana is a signal of touch. The two lists do not match. But both are careful ways of sorting what a mouth tells us.
- e.g. Jaggery tastes sweet in both lists.
- e.g. Chilli is pungent in shad rasa. In science it is a heat signal from nerves, not a taste.
- e.g. Umami is on the science list but has no place in the six rasas.
- **Ayurveda, in the Charaka Samhita:** The six tastes are the main way a cook can see what a food will do in the body. A meal with all six keeps a person well. The tastes are tools for choosing food.
- **Taste scientists:** Taste is the work of five kinds of sensor on the taste cells. Chilli heat and the dry feel of tea come from nerves of touch and pain. The six rasas are a useful cook's list, but they are not a map of the tongue's sensors.
### The tongue map is wrong: every taste is sensed everywhere · *defined*
**Definition:** Taste cells for every taste are found all over the tongue, and also on the roof of the mouth and in the throat. There is no tongue map with one zone for each taste.

A tongue is covered in tiny bumps. Many of them hold taste buds. A taste bud is a small group of 50 to 100 taste cells, packed like segments of an orange. Each cell senses one kind of taste, and one bud can hold cells for several. A taste cell lives only about ten days to two weeks, and then a new one grows. So your sense of taste is being rebuilt all the time. The old tongue map taught that the tip tastes sweet and the back tastes bitter. In fact, all the tastes are sensed in all the zones. The zones differ a little in how sensitive they are. The middle of the tongue has fewer taste buds, so it is a little less sensitive. But there is no zone that is blind to a taste.
- e.g. A drop of lemon water on the tip of the tongue tastes sour, and so does a drop at the back.
- e.g. Salt on the tip is salty, even though the old map put salt on the sides.
- e.g. When you burn your tongue on hot tea, the taste buds are damaged, and new ones grow back within about two weeks.
### Taste is a signal about what is in the food · *idea*
Why do we have taste at all? One idea is that each taste is a message about what is in the food. Sweet tells you there is sugar, a quick fuel. Salty tells you there are minerals the body needs for its nerves and water balance. Sour is the taste of acids, in unripe fruit, in lemon and amla, and in food that has started to go bad. Bitter warns of food that may be poisonous, because many poisons in plants are bitter. Umami tells you there is protein. This fits what babies do. Newborns make a relaxed, almost smiling face for sweet and a screwed-up face for bitter, before they have eaten anything. But it is only an idea. Taste is not a perfect guide. Some bitter foods, like karela and neem, are good for us in small amounts, and some poisons taste sweet. We also learn tastes. A baby tastes flavours of the mother's food in the womb and in her milk, and later likes those flavours more. Julie Mennella, a scientist in the USA, showed this in the 1990s and 2000s.
- e.g. Children who are given a little karela many times often learn to like it, because bitter can be learned.
- e.g. A very ripe mango tastes sweet and a little sour, which tells you it holds sugar and acid.
- e.g. A rotten idli batter tastes sharply sour, which is why we do not eat it.
### A taste can be copied without the goodness · *defined*
**Definition:** A taste is a signal from the chemicals in a food. A food can give the signal without giving what the signal once meant.

A ripe mango gives sweetness together with fibre, vitamins and water. A bottle of sweet fizzy drink gives the same sweet signal with sugar and almost nothing else. Your tongue cannot tell the difference. It only reports sweet. Long ago, sweet foods were rare, mostly fruit and honey. A body that loved sweet was well fed. Today a factory can add sugar, salt and fat to a packet of chips or biscuits. The signal is strong, so we want more, and the food does not give back the goodness. The World Health Organization advises that sugars added to food and drink should be less than one tenth of the energy we eat in a day. Some foods are made so that eating them is hard to stop. That is why one packet of chips is rarely enough.
- e.g. A sweet fizzy drink gives sugar and no fibre, no protein and almost no vitamins.
- e.g. Instant noodle flavour packets give a strong salty signal and little else.
- e.g. A guava gives sweetness together with fibre and a lot of vitamin C.
### Festival foods: made from the season, rich in energy, made to share · *defined*
**Definition:** A festival food is a dish made for a special day. Many of them use what the season gives, are rich in energy, and keep well or feed many people.

Festival foods follow the year. Pongal in January is cooked from the new rice harvest, with jaggery from sugarcane, moong dal and ghee. Makar Sankranti is in the cool months, and Maharashtra gives til-gul, sesame with jaggery, which is rich in fat and energy. Ugadi is in spring, when raw mango and neem flowers come, so the pachadi is made of them. Onam is at the end of the monsoon, and its sadya is full of vegetables, banana and coconut. Diwali sweets and savouries, like laddu and murukku, have a lot of sugar and oil and very little water. Microbes cannot grow in food with little water, so these keep for weeks and can be shared. Many festival foods are rich in sugar and ghee because the day is special. Eaten on a few days of the year, they fit into a balanced diet. Eaten every day, they would not.
- e.g. Pongal: new rice, moong dal, jaggery and ghee, cooked in a new pot.
- e.g. Til-gul in Maharashtra at Makar Sankranti: sesame and jaggery, for the cool months.
- e.g. Diwali murukku and laddu, which keep for weeks because they have little water.
### Millets: very old grains with new attention · *defined*
**Definition:** Millets are small-seeded grasses grown as grain. Ragi, jowar, bajra, kodo, little millet and foxtail millet are all millets.

People in India have grown millets for thousands of years. They grow in dry land with little water and little care. Ragi is rich in calcium, and has far more than rice does. Bajra and jowar bring fibre and iron. Fibre slows the body's taking in of sugar, and feeds the microbes in the gut. After the Green Revolution of the 1960s, India grew far more rice and wheat, and less millet. Rice and wheat were given at ration shops and became the grains everyone knew. Millets came back into attention. In 2023 the United Nations kept an International Year of Millets, which India had asked for. Advertisements sometimes call millets a superfood. 'Superfood' is a selling word, not a science word. Millets are good food, but no single food gives the body everything.
- e.g. Ragi mudde and ragi dosa in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. Jowar roti in Maharashtra and Karnataka, and bajra rotla in Gujarat.
- e.g. Samai, thinai and varagu in Tamil Nadu are the names for little millet, foxtail millet and kodo millet.
### Old ways of preparing food: soaking, sprouting, fermenting and a squeeze of lemon · *defined*
**Definition:** Soaking, sprouting and fermenting are ways of preparing plant foods that make the minerals inside them easier for the body to take in.

Grains, pulses and seeds hold a store of phosphorus in a chemical called phytic acid. Phytic acid holds on to iron, zinc and calcium, so the gut can take in less of them. When seeds are soaked and begin to sprout, they wake up and make enzymes that break phytic acid down. The same happens, more slowly, when microbes ferment the batter for idli and dosa. Sprouting also makes a little vitamin C, which dry seeds do not have. A squeeze of lemon on greens or dal adds vitamin C that helps the gut take in iron from plant foods. Tea and coffee drunk with a meal do the opposite, and can lower how much iron is taken in. This is why guides for Indian families say to leave an hour between a meal and tea. Families found these ways by trying, long before anyone knew about phytic acid. Hotz and Gibson wrote about this in 2007.
- e.g. Soaking dal for an hour or two before cooking, or soaking rajma overnight.
- e.g. Idli and dosa batter that is left to ferment overnight.
- e.g. A squeeze of lemon on palak or on sprouts.
### Food is not shared equally: too little and too much · *defined*
**Definition:** Some children do not get enough food or enough of the right foods, and others eat too much of foods that are high in sugar, fat and salt. Both are called malnutrition.

India grows more food than it needs, and yet many people do not eat a balanced diet. The National Family Health Survey, done in 2019 to 2021, found that more than a third of children under five were too short for their age, and that more than half of women aged 15 to 49 had anaemia, a shortage of iron in the blood. At the same time, more children than before were overweight. The reasons are not about what food exists. They are about money, work, how far a market is, what is sold in shops, and who in the family eats first and last. Free school meals are one answer. Tamil Nadu began giving meals to children in schools in the 1950s, and a national scheme now serves them across India. Studies have found that school meals bring more children to school, as well as feeding them.
- e.g. A midday meal of rice, dal and a vegetable can be the biggest meal of the day for a child.
- e.g. A family with less money buys more rice and less milk, fruit and pulses, because rice fills the stomach at lower cost.
- e.g. A child in a city who eats packets and fizzy drinks every day can be overweight and short of iron at the same time.
### People taste the same food differently · *idea*
Ask a class to taste a piece of raw karela. Some will say it is very bitter and some will say it is only a little bitter. Scientists think part of this comes from genes. Bitter taste cells use many kinds of sensor, and the genes for these sensors are a little different from person to person. In 1931 Arthur Fox, a chemist in the USA, let a powder called PTC float into the air. A colleague nearby said it tasted bitter, and Fox tasted nothing. People turned out to fall into two groups, those who taste PTC as bitter and those who do not, and it comes from a gene. Experience matters too. A child who grows up eating karela often finds it less harsh. So when two people taste the same food and disagree, are they tasting different things, or tasting differently? Scientists would say a bit of both. The food is the same, but each tongue and each past is different.
- e.g. One person finds coriander leaves fresh, and another finds them soapy. Part of this comes from genes.
- e.g. A person who grew up on fermented bamboo shoot may enjoy a smell that a visitor finds strong.
- e.g. Two cousins eat the same pachadi, and one calls the neem too bitter while the other asks for more.

## Mostly agreed
- A balanced diet is a mix of many foods over days, not one perfect plate and not one magic food. *How we know:* The ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024) are built on this idea. Studies of food variety and health across many countries point the same way.
- Every basic taste can be sensed on every part of the tongue that has taste buds. *How we know:* In 1974 Virginia Collings tested people carefully and found this. Later, scientists found the sensor proteins on taste cells all over the tongue (Chandrashekar and colleagues, Nature, 2006).
- Soaking, sprouting and fermenting grains and pulses can make it easier for the body to take in iron and zinc. *How we know:* These steps lower phytic acid in the food. Hotz and Gibson reviewed the studies in the Journal of Nutrition in 2007.
- Millets are nutritious grains that grow in dry land with little water. *How we know:* The Indian Food Composition Tables (2017) list their fibre and minerals, and the Indian Institute of Millets Research in Hyderabad studies how they grow.
- Eating a lot of sugar, salt and fat in packaged food, day after day, goes with eating more energy than the body needs. *How we know:* In a 2019 study by Kevin Hall and colleagues, adults eating mostly ultra-processed food ate about 500 kilocalories more each day than when they ate unprocessed food.
- Many children and women in India are short of iron and other nutrients, even though India grows enough food. *How we know:* The National Family Health Survey 2019 to 2021 measured anaemia and the height of young children in every state.

## How we found out
**David Hänig (1901), Edwin Boring (1942) and Virginia Collings (1974), 1901 to 1974.** For about a hundred years, children were shown a map of the tongue. Sweet was at the tip, sour and salty were at the sides, and bitter was at the back. The map began in 1901, when a German scientist named David Hänig tested how strongly people sensed tastes on different parts of the tongue. He found only small differences. In 1942 the American psychologist Edwin Boring drew those numbers as a chart, and textbooks turned the chart into a map where each taste had its own zone. Nobody checked it for decades. In 1974 Virginia Collings, a scientist in the USA, tested people carefully. Every taste could be sensed in every part of the tongue that has taste buds, and the differences were small. Later scientists found the sensors for each taste on cells all over the tongue.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that each taste is sensed only in its own zone on the tongue. A chart of small differences in how sensitive each part is had been turned into a map of separate zones. Collings tested people part by part and found that every taste is sensed everywhere.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Each taste has its own zone on the tongue, with sweet at the tip and bitter at the back.** (This 'tongue map' was drawn in a book in 1942 and then copied into school books for decades.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some zones are a little more sensitive to some tastes. The tip is quite good at sweet, and the back is quite good at bitter.  
  Against: In 1974 Virginia Collings, and many scientists after her, found that every taste is sensed in every zone. The differences are small. Your own blind test shows this.
- **Traditional food is always healthier than modern food.** (Many people, in advertisements and in conversations about old and new ways of eating.; *debated*)  
  For: Many traditional meals are built on whole foods, with a staple, a pulse, vegetables and often fermented foods. Old ways of soaking, sprouting and fermenting do help.  
  Against: Some traditional diets lacked certain nutrients. A diet of mostly polished rice has little thiamine. Some festival foods are very rich in sugar and ghee. Tradition is a good start, but it needs checking.
- **If you crave a food, your body needs what is in it.** (A common belief, and sometimes an argument in diet books.; *debated*)  
  For: The body does have a real hunger for salt when it is short of it. Some people crave soil or ice when they are short of iron.  
  Against: Most cravings for sweet or salty snacks come from habit, mood and factory food that is made to be hard to stop. A craving for a packet of biscuits does not mean the body needs biscuits.
- **Millets are a new 'superfood' that fixes diets.** (Some advertisements and food brands.; *rejected*)  
  For: Millets are rich in fibre and minerals, and ragi is rich in calcium.  
  Against: Millets are not new, and 'superfood' is not a science word. No single food gives the body everything. A dish made of millet with a lot of sugar and oil is not a health food.
- **Festival sweets are bad and should be avoided.** (Some health writers, and some families that worry about sugar.; *debated*)  
  For: Sweets are rich in sugar, and a lot of sugar every day is linked to health problems.  
  Against: A balanced diet is about the pattern over weeks. A laddu on Diwali or a payasam on Onam fits into a pattern that is mostly varied whole food. Sharing festival food also has value for a family.

## Roots
We have tested the tongue, woken up seeds and planned festival meals. Now we ask what these tell us about taste, about food, and about where a meal ends and we begin.

**Deep truth.** Your tongue does not measure goodness. It reports sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami, which once pointed to energy, minerals, ripe fruit, danger and protein. A sweet drink can give the sweet report with none of the fruit. So wanting a taste is not the same as needing the food.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'it tastes good, so it is good for me', and we say 'I crave it, so my body needs it'. But taste is only a signal made by the food's chemicals. It was a good guide for whole foods, and a food made in a factory can copy the signal without the goodness.  
*Evidence:* In the blind taste test, a spoonful of sugar in water gave a clear sweet signal on every part of the tongue, though sugar water has no protein, fibre or vitamins. The pachadi sends six different signals from six different foods, and the tongue reads them all. The tongue tells you what is touching it, and says nothing about what the food will do inside you.

- *who am i:* You are the one who tastes, and your taste is partly built from your genes, your mother's food and the meals you grew up on.
- *what is life:* Your taste cells die and are replaced about every ten days, so the part of you that tastes is being rebuilt all the time, like the food it senses.
- *what is this world:* A sweet fruit, a bitter leaf and a salty crystal are different chemicals, and living beings built a sense that reads each one as a message.

A bitter flower, a sweet lump of jaggery and a sour fruit are all made from the same few kinds of atoms, rearranged by plants using sunlight. Your tongue does not read atoms. It reads shapes of molecules, which fit its sensors like keys in locks. Then your brain turns those shapes into sweet, sour and bitter. So the taste is made by the meeting of the food and the one who tastes. The food was built by a plant. The tongue was built by a body that came from other bodies, going back a very long way. When you eat, one built thing meets another, and the meal becomes you.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.2:** The text says that all beings are born from food, live by food, and go back into food when they die. It calls food the first of all things, because it is what a living being is made of. (Your tongue, your blood and your bones are made from the dal, rice and milk of past meals. The meal does not end at your mouth. It goes on being you.)
- **Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana 26:** This old Ayurvedic text teaches six tastes, sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent, and says that a good meal brings all of them in a fitting way. (The Ugadi pachadi is this teaching in a bowl. Science lists five tastes instead of six, but both ways of sorting ask the cook to look for balance, and not to let one taste be the boss.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Miracle of Mindfulness (1975), the tangerine:** The Buddhist teacher Thich Nhat Hanh asks us to eat a tangerine slowly, giving it our whole attention. He says that if our mind is somewhere else, we are not really eating the tangerine. (The tongue test is a way of paying attention to taste. When you slow down, a taste is no longer one word, sweet, but a whole small world.)
- Science (settled): Taste cells for sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami each use their own kind of sensor. Scientists have been finding these sensors for about twenty-five years. A child's tongue has them all over.
- Science (debated): Whether fat is a sixth basic taste, and whether the body senses other things in food that we do not call tastes, is still argued.
- Other view, Many nutrition scientists: Tastes are pleasant things to talk about, but a body needs nutrients, and nutrients are measured, not tasted. They would say that a meal with all six rasas is not proof that it is balanced, and that traditional meals, loved as they are, should still be checked against what children actually need.
- Practice, One slow taste (3 min): Take a very small piece of jaggery or one raisin. Put it on your tongue and do not chew. Close your eyes. Notice the first taste. Then move it to the side of your tongue, then the back. Notice if the taste changes. Breathe slowly through your nose. Then chew very slowly and notice what comes next.
- Ask: Is sweet a thing in the sugar, or a thing that happens when sugar meets a tongue?
- Ask: When you eat, where does the food stop and you begin?
- Ask: If a taste is a message, who sent it, and who is it for?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you taste a bitter karela and your friend says it is not bitter, are you tasting different things, or tasting differently? (hint: Think about the food, the tongue, the genes and the past, and which of them differs between you.)
- *deep:* Where does a meal end and you begin? (hint: Think about the rice you ate last week, the cells in your body, and the days it takes for food to become part of you.)
- *deep:* If a sweet drink gives the same sweet signal as a mango, what else might your senses report that is not the whole story? (hint: Think of a colour that looks different to a bee, or a smell that is strong to a dog and weak to you.)
- *big:* Every region of India built a balanced meal from different foods. What does that tell us about what a balanced diet really is? (hint: Compare a Kerala sadya, a Gujarati thali and a Naga meal. What do they share?)
- *big:* India grows enough food, yet many children go without a balanced meal. What would make meals fairer? (hint: Think about money, markets, school meals and who eats first in a home.)
- *big:* Almost every culture marks special days with special food. Why might sharing food matter so much to people? (hint: Think about who cooks, who is invited, and what is left out on an ordinary day.)
- *big:* Traditional cooks in the past found that soaking and fermenting helped, without knowing about phytic acid. How can people know something works before they know why? (hint: Think about how a family learns a recipe, and what a test adds to a recipe.)
- *deep:* Is a taste in the food, in the tongue, or in the brain? (hint: Think about the same sugar tasting sweet to you, and what a bee or a cat would sense from the same sugar.)

## Story: Bevu-Bella
*A made-up story, set in Mysuru at Ugadi. In Karnataka the neem-and-jaggery mix of Ugadi is called bevu-bella. The science in it is real. Tastes are sensed all over the tongue, smell is sensed by the nose, and a loss of smell after an illness is real and often comes back slowly. Nandini, Ajji and the family are made up.*

In Mysuru, everyone agreed that Ajji made the best Ugadi pachadi. She had made it every spring for fifty years. Nandini was in Class 4, and this year she was allowed to hold the bowl.

But this year Ajji was not herself. In February she had a long fever. The fever went, and she got up, and she walked to the market again. Yet she sat on the kitchen cot with her hands in her lap, looking at the pots.

'Nothing smells,' she told Nandini. 'Not the jasmine, not the coffee, not the rasam. Yesterday I put my nose over the rasam pot, and it was like an empty pot.' She took a spoon of last week's rasam and tasted it. 'Sour? Salt? I cannot tell. A cook with no tongue. Better to buy the pachadi from the shop.'

Nandini looked at the brass plate. On it lay the six things Ajji always used: a lump of jaggery, a ball of tamarind, a pinch of salt, a few tiny neem flowers, some black pepper and a raw mango. 'Six tastes in one bowl,' Ajji always said. 'Life will have all six this year, so we taste them all on the first day.'

Nandini remembered something from her notebook. 'Ajji,' she said slowly. 'I think your tongue is still working. Can I test it?'

'Test an old tongue?' Ajji laughed, for the first time that week. 'Go on, then.'

Nandini set out four steel katoris. One had sugar water, one salt water, one lemon water, and one plain water. 'Plain water is the catch,' she explained. 'If you say sweet to plain water, you are only guessing.' She hid the cups behind a tray and shuffled them. Ajji shut her eyes and stuck out her tongue. Nandini dipped a clean cotton bud and touched the tip.

'Sweet,' said Ajji at once.

Nandini touched the side of the tongue with another bud. 'Salt.' She touched the very back. 'Sour! Oh, my cheeks!' Ajji's eyes flew open. 'But I can taste. I can taste!'

'You can,' said Nandini. 'The tongue tastes sweet, sour, salty and bitter. All of them, all over. What you lost is your smell. The smell is what makes a mango taste of mango.'

Ajji frowned. 'My teacher said the sweet is at the front and the bitter is at the back.'

'Try the sour at the front,' said Nandini. Ajji tried it. She made the same face. 'Hm,' she said. 'Fifty years I have been telling the children the wrong map.'

Then she went quiet again. 'But the pachadi is not about one taste,' she said. 'It is right only when no taste is the boss. Sweet, sour, salt, bitter, hot, raw. My nose always told me when it was right. Now I have no nose.'

Nandini opened her notebook to a clean page. 'Then we make a table,' she said. 'You use your tongue for each taste, one at a time, and I will write it down.'

They did it slowly. Nandini stirred a little jaggery into a spoon of water. Ajji tasted and said, 'Two.' It was Nandini's scale from the notebook, nought for nothing and three for very strong. She added the tamarind and Ajji said, 'Two.' The salt, 'Two.' Then the neem flowers, just three of them, for neem is strong. 'One. Add three more. Now two.' Then a pinch of pepper, 'Two, and it burns, so it is not really a taste, it is a fire.' Then the raw mango, cut small. 'Two, and my mouth goes dry.'

Nandini wrote six twos on the page. She mixed everything in the big bowl. Ajji tasted from the tip of the spoon, and then from the side, and then from the back. Her eyes filled.

'It is all there,' she said. 'Everything is there. But it has no smell. It is not my pachadi.'

Nandini's little brother Harsha, who was six, had sneaked in and was standing beside the cot with a spoon. He took a taste. His face went through everything, sweet, sour, then a scowl, then a grin.

'Ajji,' he said. 'It is like the whole year in one spoon.' He licked the spoon. 'Can I have more?'

Ajji looked at him, and then at the page of six twos in Nandini's handwriting. 'For fifty years my nose did the cooking, and my tongue only agreed,' she said. 'This year my tongue cooked, and my granddaughter was my notebook.' She took the spoon from Harsha and held it up. 'Bring me another bowl. Everyone must taste it.'

They ate the pachadi that evening, a spoonful each, with the festival meal. Nobody said it was not the same. Nobody said it was.

In June, Ajji was drinking her morning coffee when she stopped, with the steel tumbler halfway to her mouth. She did not say anything. She only closed her eyes and breathed in. Nandini wrote the date on the page in her notebook, under the six twos.

**For the parent:** Ajji's tongue was never gone. Every taste can be sensed all over the tongue. What she had lost was her sense of smell.

- Ask: Why did Nandini put a cup of plain water among the other cups?
- Ask: Why was the pachadi right when all six tastes scored the same?
- Ask: What would you do if you had to make a dish for someone who could not smell?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Blind Taste Test: does each taste have its own place on the tongue? (43 min)
- **question:** Is sweet sensed only at the tip, and bitter only at the back? Or can every part of the tongue sense every taste?
- **predict:** Before you start, draw a tongue and mark where you think each taste is strongest: sweet, salty, sour and bitter. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Every touch uses the same amount of liquid on a new cotton bud, for the same 2 seconds, with the tongue dried first. The tester has their eyes shut and does not know which cup is which. Only two things change: the taste and the part of the tongue. Plain water is the control. It checks whether the tester is really tasting or only guessing. The order of the touches is shuffled, so that no taste is always tested first or last.
- **safety:** Everything in the cups is ordinary food in tiny amounts. Do not test anyone who is allergic to lemon, karela or methi. A grown-up does the grating. Use only drops of karela juice, and use a new bud for every touch. Never put a used bud back in a cup, and do not let the child bite the bud. Touch the back of the tongue gently, and stop at once if the child coughs or gags.
- **record:** Write the taste that was said and the strength from 0 to 3 for every touch. Then colour a drawing of the tongue to show where each taste was named correctly.
- **results table:** Cup | Tip: taste said and strength (0 to 3) | Left edge: taste said and strength (0 to 3) | Right edge: taste said and strength (0 to 3) | Back: taste said and strength (0 to 3)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a large tongue on paper. Ask your child to mark where they think sweet, salty, sour and bitter are strongest. Ask why. Write the guess down. Do not correct it.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a tongue. Where do you think sweet is strongest? Where is bitter strongest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a tongue. Mark where each taste is strongest. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: Why do you think that taste is strongest there? (good: My teacher showed a map like that.; I think the tip tastes sweet things first.; I am not sure, I am guessing.)
  - Note: Many children have seen the old tongue map in a book, and will draw it. That is fine. Write down exactly what they say, and do not say yet that the map is wrong. If your child does not know, ask them to guess anyway.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up makes the cups while the child looks away. Cup 1: 1 teaspoon of sugar in 3 tablespoons of water. Cup 2: a quarter teaspoon of salt in 3 tablespoons of water. Cup 3: 1 teaspoon of lemon juice in 3 tablespoons of water. Cup 4: karela juice. Cup 5: plain water.  
  *Parent says:* I will make five cups while you look away. One is sweet, one is salty, one is sour, one is bitter, and one is only water.  
  *Child mode:* Look away while a grown-up makes five cups. One of them is only water.
  - Note: For the bitter cup, grate a piece of karela as big as your thumb, squeeze it through a clean cloth, and mix 1 teaspoon of the juice into 3 tablespoons of water. No karela? Soak half a teaspoon of methi seeds in 3 tablespoons of water for 8 hours, and use that water. Taste a drop of each cup yourself first. A cup that has almost no taste needs more sugar, salt, lemon or karela. A cup that is harsh needs more water. Write the key on a paper and fold it: 1 sweet, 2 salty, 3 sour, 4 bitter, 5 water. Keep it hidden.
  - Check: Why is there a cup of plain water among the tastes? → **To check whether the tester is really tasting, or only guessing**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rinse the child's mouth with water. Ask them to sit with eyes shut and tongue out. Dry the tongue with a tissue. Show how you will touch the tip once with a damp bud, so the child knows what is coming.  
  *Parent says:* Shut your eyes and stick out your tongue. I will dry it first. Then I will touch it with a damp bud for two seconds.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Stick out your tongue. A grown-up will dry it and touch it with a bud.
  - Note: Touch the bud to the rim of the cup after dipping it, so it is damp and not dripping. A dripping bud spreads the liquid over the whole tongue, and spoils the test. Practise once with a bud dipped in plain water.
- **s4 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do 20 touches in the order on your list. Dip a new bud into the cup. Touch the bud to the named part of the tongue for 2 seconds. Ask what the taste is. Write down the taste and its strength from 0 to 3. Rinse and wait 20 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you taste, and how strong it is, from nothing to very strong. Rinse your mouth. Wait twenty seconds. Then we do the next one.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you taste. Say how strong it is, from 0 to 3. Then rinse.
  - Ask: What do you notice when the taste is on the back of your tongue? (good: It is the same taste as at the tip.; It was a bit weaker.; It was stronger than I thought.)
  - Note: The order of the 20 touches, in cup and part of the tongue. 1: sweet, back. 2: sour, tip. 3: water, left edge. 4: salty, right edge. 5: sweet, left edge. 6: water, back. 7: sour, right edge. 8: salty, tip. 9: water, right edge. 10: sweet, tip. 11: salty, left edge. 12: sour, back. 13: water, tip. 14: bitter, left edge. 15: salty, back. 16: sour, left edge. 17: sweet, right edge. 18: bitter, right edge. 19: bitter, back. 20: bitter, tip. Bitter comes mostly at the end on purpose, because it lingers and would spoil the other tastes. Your child may start to guess bitter near the end. Note it if they do. Use a new bud every time. The left edge and right edge are about halfway back along the side. For the back, touch the middle of the tongue as far back as is comfortable. Some answers may be wrong, or the strength may be 0. That is a result. Write what you hear.
  - Check: Your child says 'sweet' for a touch of plain water. What does that mean for the test? → **Sometimes the tester guesses, so the other answers need careful reading**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a tongue with four zones. For each taste, put a coloured dot in every zone where it was named correctly. Count the dots. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why. Then compare with their first guess.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you find? Why do you think it came out that way?  
  *Child mode:* Colour a dot where each taste was named right. Say what you thought, what you found, and why.
  - Note: Most children find that every taste was named correctly in nearly every zone, though some were stronger than others. The tip and the edges are usually stronger than the middle, because they have more taste buds. If the dots do not look like that, say so. Messy results are real results. Wet buds, nerves and guessing can all spoil a result. After your child has said what they found, you can explain: taste buds for every taste are spread across the tongue. The old map came from a mistake in the 1940s. The card on the tongue map tells the whole story.
  - Think: Look at your first drawing. Which part did the test show to be wrong?
### e2 · Wake Up the Moong: what do soaking and sprouting change in a seed? (74 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Does a moong seed that has been soaked, or soaked and sprouted, cook faster than a dry one? And how much heavier do the seeds get?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will cook softest first: dry moong, soaked moong or sprouted moong? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three batches start as 50 grams of the same moong. They cook in the same amount of water, in pans of the same size, on the same low flame. Only the preparation differs: dry, soaked, or soaked and sprouted. The dry batch is the control. Each batch is tested for softness the same way, by pressing one cooled seed between thumb and finger.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cooking, because the pans and the steam are hot, and a grown-up lifts the seeds out with a spoon. Cooled seeds are pressed on a plate or between thumb and finger only after they have cooled for a minute. Do not eat raw sprouts if anyone at home is very young, old or ill. Wash sprouts well and cook them.
- **record:** Write the weights, the tail length and the cooking times in the table. Draw the three kinds of seed: dry, soaked and sprouted. Then write what you thought, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Seeds | Weight at the start (g) | Weight after soaking or sprouting (g) | Length of the tail (mm) | Minutes to turn soft
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Weigh three portions of moong. Put B and C to soak in water in the evening. (12 min); day 2: In the morning, drain and weigh B and C. Put B in fresh water in the fridge. Cover C with a damp cloth and let it sprout. Rinse C twice, morning and evening. (8 min); day 3: Weigh and measure all three. Cook them side by side and test every 5 minutes. Write what you found. (54 min)
- **s1 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Weigh three portions of 50 grams of moong, or measure 4 level tablespoons each. Put them in bowls A, B and C. Pour 150 millilitres of water on B and on C. Leave A dry. Write the weights.  
  *Parent says:* We will wake up two bowls of moong, and leave one asleep. Which will cook fastest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put equal moong in three bowls. Add water to B and C. Leave A dry.
  - Ask: What do you think the moong in water will do overnight? (good: The seeds will get bigger.; They will soak up the water.; They might start to grow.)
  - Note: Do this in the evening. The soaking takes 8 to 10 hours. Write the guesses on the table. If you have no scale, write the volume of the seeds in a measuring cup instead.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* In the morning, drain B and C. Weigh each one and write the weights. Put B back in clean water in the fridge. Put C in a sieve, cover it with a damp cloth, and keep it in a warm, dark place. Rinse C twice a day.  
  *Parent says:* The seeds have soaked all night. Let us weigh them. Bowl B goes to the fridge in water. Bowl C goes in the sieve, to grow.  
  *Child mode:* Drain B and C. Weigh them. B goes in the fridge. C goes in the sieve under a damp cloth.
  - Note: In a warm kitchen, tiny white tails show by the next morning. In cool weather, sprouting can take two days. If C is not sprouting, keep it warm and give it another day. Do not let the seeds sit in water, or they will go sour. If sprouts smell bad or feel slimy, throw them away.
  - Check: The soaked moong weighs more than the dry moong. Where did the extra weight come from? → **Water that the seeds soaked up**
  - Wait: Leave C to sprout for about a day, rinsing the seeds in the morning and evening.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, look at C. Measure the longest tail with a ruler in millimetres. Weigh A, B and C and write the weights. Draw one dry, one soaked and one sprouted seed.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely at the three kinds of seed. What is different? How long is the tail on the sprouted ones?  
  *Child mode:* Look at A, B and C. Measure a tail on C. Draw one seed of each kind.
  - Ask: What is the white tail on the sprouted moong? (good: It is a root.; It is the start of a new plant.; The seed has woken up.)
  - Note: A tail is the first root, and a good length is a few millimetres to a centimetre. If your sprouts are very short, write that down. The seeds are alive, and have started to grow.
- **s4 (40 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up boils 150 millilitres of water in each of three pans. Add A, B and C, one to a pan. Turn the flame low and start a timer. Every 5 minutes, lift one seed from each pan onto a plate. Let the seed cool, and press the seed between thumb and finger. Soft means the seed squashes with no hard middle.  
  *Parent says:* All three pans start together. Every five minutes, I lift one seed from each pan. When it squashes with no hard middle, we write down the time.  
  *Child mode:* Cook A, B and C together. Every 5 minutes, press one seed. Write the time when it is soft.
  - Ask: Which pan will be soft first? How can you tell? (good: The sprouted one, because it is already awake.; The seeds that soaked are softer to start.; I will press one and see.)
  - Note: Stop at 40 minutes. If the dry moong is still hard at 40 minutes, write 40 plus. That is a result. You can read the card on old ways of preparing food while you wait. The time for each batch depends on your moong and your flame, so your numbers may differ from anyone else's. Use the same flame for all three pans.
  - Check: The sprouted moong turned soft in 10 minutes, and the dry moong needed more than 30. What does this suggest? → **The water in the seed and the start of growth made the seed softer to cook**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table. Compare the weights and the cooking times. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why. Taste a little of each cooked batch if your child wants to.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you find? Why do you think it came out that way?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your numbers. Say what you thought, what you found, and why.
  - Note: Most families find that the dry moong takes much the longest, the soaked moong is quicker, and the sprouted moong quickest of all. If your numbers do not follow this, say so. After your child has said what they found, explain: soaking lets water in and starts the seed waking up. Sprouting goes further. The seed makes enzymes that break down some of the stored food, and makes a little vitamin C, which dry seeds do not have. The card on old ways of preparing food says more.
  - Think: People soak dal before cooking, and sprout moong for salads. What might they have noticed?

## Worksheets
### Tastes and a Balanced Plate (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which sentence best describes a balanced diet?  
   Options: Many different foods eaten over days, so the body gets all the nutrients it needs / One perfect plate at every single meal / Only the foods that people call healthy  
   **Answer:** Many different foods eaten over days, so the body gets all the nutrients it needs. *Why:* A body counts days and weeks, not single meals. Many kinds of food together give all the nutrients. Choosing only 'healthy' foods can still leave out some groups.
2. (truefalse) Each taste is sensed only in its own zone of the tongue.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Taste buds for every taste are spread over the tongue. The zones differ only a little in how strongly they sense each taste. The old tongue map was a mistake.
3. (fill) A small group of taste cells on the tongue is called a taste ____.  
   **Answer:** ['bud']. *Why:* A taste bud is a group of 50 to 100 taste cells packed together like segments of an orange. You have thousands of them.
4. (match) Match each food to the job it mostly does in the body.  
   **Answer:** Rice → energy; Dal → building the body; Palak (spinach) → protecting the body; Ghee → energy; Curd → building the body. *Why:* Rice and ghee are rich in starch and fat, so they give energy. Dal and curd are rich in protein, which builds the body. Palak is rich in vitamins and minerals, which protect the body.
5. (sort) Sort each food by the taste it mainly gives.  
   **Answer:** jaggery → Sweet; tamarind → Sour; karela → Bitter; lemon → Sour; neem flowers → Bitter; honey → Sweet. *Why:* Jaggery and honey have sugar, which tastes sweet. Tamarind and lemon have acids, which taste sour. Karela and neem have bitter chemicals.
6. (mcq) A green chilli makes your mouth feel hot. What does science say this heat is?  
   Options: A signal of heat and pain from nerves, not one of the five tastes / The sixth basic taste / A sweet taste in disguise  
   **Answer:** A signal of heat and pain from nerves, not one of the five tastes. *Why:* Chilli heat is felt by nerves that sense heat and pain. Science lists five tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Ayurveda counts pungent as one of its six rasas.
7. (truefalse) A glass of sweet fizzy drink and a ripe mango send the same sweet signal to the tongue.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The tongue senses sugar and reports sweet. It cannot tell whether fibre, vitamins and water come with the sugar. The mango gives them, and the fizzy drink gives almost none.
8. (count) Zara, a girl in Class 4 in Hyderabad, does 16 touches in a blind taste test. She names the taste wrongly 3 times. How many touches did she name correctly?  
   **Answer:** 13. *Why:* 16 take away 3 is 13. She named 13 touches correctly. A wrong answer is still a result, because it shows how often a tester guesses.
9. (write) A friend says that sweet is only tasted at the tip of the tongue. What would you say to your friend? Use what you found in the blind taste test.  
   **Answer:** I would say that is the old tongue map, and it is wrong. In our test, sweet was named correctly on the tip, the edges and the back. Taste buds for every taste are spread across the tongue. Some zones are a little more sensitive, but none are blind to a taste.. *Why:* A strong answer names the evidence from the test, that every taste was sensed in every zone. It may also say that small differences in strength are not the same as separate zones.
10. (mcq) A thali has rice, roti, puri and halwa. Which food jobs are missing?  
   Options: Body-building foods and protective foods / Energy foods / None, because the thali is full  
   **Answer:** Body-building foods and protective foods. *Why:* Rice, roti, puri and halwa are all mostly energy foods. The thali also needs dal, curd or another pulse or milk food for building, and vegetables or fruit for protection.
11. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Ragi, jowar, bajra, rice.  
   **Answer:** Rice, because the other three are millets.. *Why:* Ragi, jowar and bajra are millets, small-seeded grains that grow well in dry land. Rice is a different grain that needs much more water.
12. (fill) Soaked moong seeds weigh more than dry moong because they have taken in ____.  
   **Answer:** ['water']. *Why:* A dry seed has very little water. The seed soaks water in through their skin and swells. The extra weight is the water.
13. (draw) Draw a plate for a festival meal that has all three food jobs. Label each food with its job.  
   **Answer:** A plate with at least one energy food such as rice or roti, one body-building food such as dal or curd, and one protective food such as a vegetable or fruit, each labelled.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows all three jobs on the one plate and labels them correctly. Two dishes may share a job, and one dish may do more than one job.
14. (mcq) Why can many festival sweets, like laddu, be kept for weeks?  
   Options: They have very little water, so microbes cannot grow in them / They are always kept in a fridge / They are cooked with a lot of water  
   **Answer:** They have very little water, so microbes cannot grow in them. *Why:* Microbes need water to grow. A laddu is made of sugar, flour, ghee and nuts, with very little water in it, so spoilage microbes grow very slowly.
15. (write) A laddu at Diwali fits in a balanced diet, but a laddu at every meal would not. Why not?  
   **Answer:** A laddu is rich in sugar and ghee, so it gives a lot of energy and few other nutrients. A balanced diet is a pattern over many days. One laddu on a special day is a small part of the pattern. Laddus at every meal would crowd out dal, vegetables and fruit.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a balanced diet is a pattern, and that one festival sweet is a small part of it. It may also say that eating it every day would leave less room for other foods.
16. (truefalse) A balanced meal must look the same in every part of India.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A Kerala sadya, a Gujarati thali and a Naga meal are all different. Each joins a staple, a pulse or fish or milk food, and vegetables, using what grows nearby.
### How Taste and Food Work (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which taste is on the science list of five basic tastes, but is not one of the six rasas?  
   Options: Umami / Pungent / Astringent  
   **Answer:** Umami. *Why:* Umami is the savoury taste of foods like ripe tomato and mushroom. Ayurveda's six rasas are sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent. Pungent and astringent are feelings, not tastes.
2. (mcq) In 1942 a chart of small differences in how strongly each part of the tongue senses a taste was turned into a map of separate zones. What was the mistake?  
   Options: Small differences in strength were read as separate zones / Taste buds were found only on the tip / People could not taste at all  
   **Answer:** Small differences in strength were read as separate zones. *Why:* Hänig's numbers in 1901 showed only small differences. When they were drawn as a map, each taste seemed to have its own zone. Careful tests in 1974 showed every zone senses every taste.
3. (truefalse) A taste cell lives as long as the person who owns it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A taste cell lives only about ten days to two weeks, and then a new one grows. Your sense of taste is being rebuilt all the time.
4. (fill) Lemon and tamarind taste sour because they hold ____.  
   **Answer:** ['acid', 'acids']. *Why:* Sour is the taste of acids. Lemon, tamarind, curd and unripe mango all have acid in them.
5. (match) Match each taste to what it may be telling you about the food.  
   **Answer:** Sweet → there is sugar, a quick fuel; Salty → there are minerals; Bitter → it may be poisonous; Umami → there is protein; Sour → there is acid, as in unripe fruit. *Why:* These are ideas about why we have taste, and scientists mark them as ideas and not as proof. Taste is not a perfect guide, because some bitter foods are safe, and some poisons taste sweet.
6. (order) Put the steps of the Wake Up the Moong test in the right order.  
   **Answer:** ['Weigh three equal portions of dry moong', 'Soak two of the portions overnight', 'Drain and weigh them, and let one bowl sprout', 'Cook all three together and test one seed from each pan every 5 minutes', 'Compare the weights and the cooking times']. *Why:* The soaking and sprouting come before the cooking, because the cooking test compares the three kinds of seed. All three batches must be cooked in the same way so that the test is fair.
7. (count) Dry moong took 36 minutes to turn soft. Soaked moong took 18 minutes and sprouted moong took 9 minutes. How many times as long did the dry moong take as the sprouted moong?  
   **Answer:** 4 times. *Why:* 36 divided by 9 is 4. The dry moong took 4 times as long as the sprouted moong, which fits the idea that sprouting has already started to soften the seed.
8. (sort) Sort each way of preparing or eating food by its main effect.  
   **Answer:** soaking dal before cooking → Lowers phytic acid; fermenting idli batter → Lowers phytic acid; a squeeze of lemon on palak → Helps the body take in iron; strong tea drunk with a meal → Lowers the iron taken in; sprouting moong → Lowers phytic acid. *Why:* Phytic acid holds on to iron and zinc, and soaking, sprouting and fermenting break it down. Vitamin C in lemon helps the gut take in iron from plant foods. Tea can lower how much iron the body takes in.
9. (perspective) A packet of millet biscuits has the word 'superfood' on the front. The first two ingredients are sugar and palm oil.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view, gives a reason and refers to what is in the biscuits, for example that sugar and oil come first on the list.. *Why:* 'Superfood' is a selling word, not a science word. A label's ingredient list is listed from most to least by weight, so sugar and oil being first means there is more of them than of millet.
10. (write) Why does a sweet fizzy drink give a sweet taste without the goodness of a mango?  
   **Answer:** The tongue senses sugar and reports sweet. It cannot tell what else is in the food. A mango has sugar together with fibre, vitamins and water. A fizzy drink has sugar and almost nothing else. So both taste sweet, but only the mango gives the goodness.. *Why:* A strong answer says that taste is a signal from a chemical, and that the same signal can come with or without the other nutrients.
11. (mcq) Two cousins taste the same piece of karela. One says it is very bitter and the other says it is a little bitter. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Their taste sensors differ a little because of genes and past experience / One of them must be telling a lie / The karela changed between the two bites  
   **Answer:** Their taste sensors differ a little because of genes and past experience. *Why:* People have slightly different genes for bitter sensors, and the foods they grew up on also change how strong a taste feels. So the same food can taste different to two people.
12. (truefalse) Free school meals can bring more children to school as well as feeding them.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Studies of school meals in India have found that more children come to school when a meal is served. Tamil Nadu began giving school meals in the 1950s, and a national scheme now reaches children across India.
13. (mcq) A family eats rice, rice flakes and potato at most meals. Which change would improve their balance most?  
   Options: Add dal or other pulses, vegetables, curd or milk, and fruit / Eat more rice so nobody is hungry / Stop eating rice completely  
   **Answer:** Add dal or other pulses, vegetables, curd or milk, and fruit. *Why:* Rice, rice flakes and potato are all energy foods. The family's diet lacks building foods and protective foods. Adding them is better than removing the rice, which is still a useful staple.
14. (draw) Draw a bowl of Ugadi pachadi with its six ingredients. Label the taste of each one.  
   **Answer:** A bowl with six labelled ingredients: jaggery (sweet), tamarind (sour), salt (salty), neem flowers (bitter), pepper (pungent) and raw mango (astringent).. *Why:* The pachadi holds all six rasas. A strong drawing labels each ingredient with its taste, and may note that pungent and astringent are feelings and not tastes.
15. (write) Old cooks soaked dal and fermented batter long before anyone knew about phytic acid. How can people find out that something works before they know why?  
   **Answer:** People can try a thing many times and notice what happens. If a family soaks dal and everyone does better, they keep doing it. Later, scientists can test it and find the reason. Knowing that it works and knowing why are two different steps.. *Why:* A strong answer says that noticing a pattern comes before explaining it. It may also say that a test can show whether the old way really works, or whether it only seems to.
16. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Ugadi pachadi, shukto, pavakka thoran, payasam.  
   **Answer:** Payasam, because it is sweet, and the other three each have a bitter taste.. *Why:* Ugadi pachadi has neem flowers, shukto has a little karela, and pavakka thoran is made of karela. Payasam is a sweet milk dish.
### Thinking Like a Food Scientist (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Design a fair blind test to find out whether people can tell a spoon of sugar water from a spoon of apple juice with the same sweetness. Say what you keep the same and what you change.  
   **Answer:** I would put the sugar water and the apple juice in two cups the same size, with the same amount in each, and let the tester shut their eyes. A helper would shuffle the cups so the tester does not know which is which. The only thing I change is which liquid it is. I keep the temperature, the amount and the spoon the same. I would test many people and write down how often they are right.. *Why:* A strong answer keeps everything the same except the one thing being tested, hides which cup is which from the tester, and tests more than once. A control of two identical cups would also be a good idea.
2. (mcq) A friend tests her own tongue with her eyes open and with no plain water cup. Which problem matters most?  
   Options: She can see which cup is which, so her answers are not blind / She used cups instead of bowls / She rinsed her mouth between tests  
   **Answer:** She can see which cup is which, so her answers are not blind. *Why:* If the tester can see the cup, she may name the taste she expects and not the taste she feels. Hiding the cups, and adding plain water, makes the test fair. Rinsing is a good idea, not a problem.
3. (perspective) Ayurveda says a good meal holds all six tastes. A nutrition scientist says a body needs nutrients, which are measured, and not tasted.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view and gives a reason, for example that tastes are a good guide for a cook, but nutrients must still be checked.. *Why:* Both views have something right. Tastes are a convenient guide, but they do not measure nutrients. The cook uses both, which is often how real planning works.
4. (count) A festival feast has 15 dishes. One third are protective, and one third are body-building. How many are energy foods?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* One third of 15 is 5. So 5 are protective and 5 are body-building. That leaves 15 take away 10, which is 5 energy foods. The feast has an equal number of each job.
5. (mcq) A sweet like mysore pak keeps for weeks. Which fact explains this best?  
   Options: It is made of sugar, flour and ghee with almost no water / It is cooked at a very high heat / It is wrapped in leaves  
   **Answer:** It is made of sugar, flour and ghee with almost no water. *Why:* Microbes need water to grow. A food with sugar and fat but little water gives them almost nothing to work with, so it keeps. Heat during cooking does not protect food once it has cooled.
6. (write) A neighbour says, 'When I crave biscuits, it means my body needs them.' What would you say, using what you know about taste?  
   **Answer:** A craving is a wish for a taste. Our body does have a real hunger for salt when we are short of it. But a craving for sugar, salt and fat in a packet is often habit, because factory food is made to be hard to stop. The biscuit gives the taste signal but not much else. So a craving does not always mean the body needs the food.. *Why:* A strong answer separates a taste signal from a need. It may name that some cravings, such as for salt, can be real, and that others come from habit and factory food.
7. (truefalse) Because pongal is a traditional food, it can be eaten at every meal without any limit.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sakkarai pongal is rich in sugar and ghee, which is why it is a festival dish. A traditional food is not automatically right for every day. The pattern over days matters.
8. (match) Match each festival to the foods of its season.  
   **Answer:** Pongal in January → new rice, moong dal and jaggery; Ugadi in spring → raw mango and neem flowers; Makar Sankranti in the cool months → sesame and jaggery; Onam after the monsoon → banana, coconut and many vegetables. *Why:* Festival foods follow what the land gives in each part of the year. Rice is harvested around Pongal, raw mango and neem come in spring, and sesame is eaten in the cool months.
9. (fill) Taste scientists count five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and ____.  
   **Answer:** ['umami']. *Why:* Umami is the savoury taste, made by glutamate. It was named by the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda in 1908, from the taste of kombu seaweed.
10. (write) A hungry child gets a midday meal of rice, dal and a vegetable. Why might this meal matter more to her than to a child who eats well at home?  
   **Answer:** If her family has little money, this may be her biggest and best meal of the day. It gives energy from the rice, building food from the dal and protection from the vegetable. A child who eats well at home already gets these foods, so the school meal adds less.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the school meal fills a gap. It may mention that a balanced meal is not equally available to every child, and that this is a question of fairness.
11. (mcq) Which lunch best suits a guest who eats no meat, no onion and no garlic, and is allergic to groundnuts?  
   Options: Rice, dal with cumin and ginger, a coconut vegetable stir-fry, curd and fruit / Dal tadka with garlic and fried onion / Rice with groundnut chutney and curd  
   **Answer:** Rice, dal with cumin and ginger, a coconut vegetable stir-fry, curd and fruit. *Why:* The first meal has energy from rice, building food from dal and curd, and protection from vegetables and fruit, without meat, onion, garlic or groundnut. The other two break at least one rule.
12. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Idli batter, curd, mishti doi, sabudana khichdi.  
   **Answer:** Sabudana khichdi, because it is not fermented. The other three are made by microbes.. *Why:* Idli batter, curd and mishti doi are all changed by living microbes. Sabudana khichdi is made from sago pearls cooked with peanuts and potato, and no fermentation is needed.
13. (draw) Draw three moong seeds in a row: a dry one, a soaked one and a sprouted one. Label what is different about each.  
   **Answer:** A small hard seed labelled dry, a larger swollen seed labelled soaked, and a swollen seed with a small white tail labelled sprouted.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the seed getting bigger as the seed takes in water, and the tail, which is the first root, on the sprouted one.
14. (sort) Sort each statement by whether the blind taste test supports it.  
   **Answer:** Sour can be tasted on the tip of the tongue → Supported; Bitter can be tasted only at the back → Not supported; Salty can be tasted on the edges of the tongue → Supported; Sweet can be tasted only at the tip → Not supported. *Why:* In the test, every taste was named in every zone. So the statements that say a taste is sensed in one place only are not supported.
15. (write) Where does a meal end and you begin? Use what you know about food, cells and taste to give your view.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer may say that the rice in a meal becomes part of the body within days, so the meal does not end at the mouth. It may also say that the taste cells that sense the meal are themselves rebuilt from earlier meals. It may leave the question open, with a reason.. *Why:* This is an open question with no single answer. A strong answer gives a view, uses evidence from the unit, and stays honest about what is not known.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A girl eats only rice and ghee every day and always feels full. What is the best way to describe her diet?  
   Options: Not balanced, because it leaves out many groups of food / Balanced, because it fills her stomach / Balanced, because ghee is healthy  
   **Answer:** Not balanced, because it leaves out many groups of food. *Why:* Feeling full is not the same as getting every nutrient. Rice and ghee are both energy foods. A balanced diet also needs building foods and protective foods over the days.
2. (mcq) Where on the tongue can you taste sour?  
   Options: Anywhere there are taste buds / Only on the sides / Only at the back  
   **Answer:** Anywhere there are taste buds. *Why:* Taste buds for every taste are found all over the tongue. The old map that put sour only on the sides was a mistake.
3. (mcq) In the blind taste test, what is the job of the cup of plain water?  
   Options: It checks whether the tester is really tasting or only guessing / It makes the other cups taste weaker / It gives the tester a drink  
   **Answer:** It checks whether the tester is really tasting or only guessing. *Why:* Plain water is the control. If the tester names a taste for it, the test shows that some answers may be guesses.
4. (mcq) Which of these is not one of the five basic tastes found by science?  
   Options: Pungent / Umami / Bitter  
   **Answer:** Pungent. *Why:* Pungent is the hot feeling of chilli and pepper, sensed by nerves of heat and pain. Umami and bitter are basic tastes sensed by taste cells.
5. (mcq) Why do sprouted moong seeds cook faster than dry moong seeds?  
   Options: They have taken in water and started to grow, so the seeds are softer inside / They are a different kind of seed / They are cooked on a hotter flame  
   **Answer:** They have taken in water and started to grow, so the seeds are softer inside. *Why:* In a fair test, the seed and the flame are the same. Soaking lets water in, and sprouting starts the seed breaking down the stored food, so the seed softens sooner.
6. (mcq) A mango and a sweet fizzy drink both taste sweet. What is the main difference between them?  
   Options: The mango has fibre and vitamins with its sugar, and the drink has almost none / The mango has no sugar in it / The tongue can tell which one is healthier  
   **Answer:** The mango has fibre and vitamins with its sugar, and the drink has almost none. *Why:* The tongue only reports sweet. It cannot tell what else comes with the sugar. A mango gives fibre, water and vitamins, and a fizzy drink gives sugar and very little else.
7. (mcq) A thali has rice, dal, a vegetable and curd. What does the dal mainly do for the body?  
   Options: It helps build the body / It mostly gives energy / It does nothing the rice does not do  
   **Answer:** It helps build the body. *Why:* Dal is rich in protein, which the body uses to grow and mend. Rice is the main energy food in this thali. The two do different jobs.
8. (mcq) Why is Ugadi pachadi made with raw mango and neem flowers?  
   Options: They are in season in spring, when Ugadi falls / They are the only foods that taste bitter and sour / They are the cheapest foods in the market all year  
   **Answer:** They are in season in spring, when Ugadi falls. *Why:* Festival foods often follow the season. Raw mango and neem flowers appear in spring. Their tastes also fit the idea of a bowl with all six tastes.
9. (mcq) Which action helps the body take in iron from dal and palak?  
   Options: A squeeze of lemon on the meal / Drinking strong tea with the meal / Eating the meal very fast  
   **Answer:** A squeeze of lemon on the meal. *Why:* Vitamin C in lemon helps the gut take in iron from plant foods. Tea drunk with a meal can lower how much iron is taken in.
10. (mcq) Why might two people taste the same piece of karela and disagree about how bitter it is?  
   Options: Their taste sensors and past experiences differ a little / One of them is imagining it / Karela tastes different each time it is cut  
   **Answer:** Their taste sensors and past experiences differ a little. *Why:* Genes for bitter sensors vary from person to person, and the foods a child grows up eating change how a taste feels. Both people are tasting honestly.
11. (mcq) Which meal best suits a guest who eats no meat and needs energy for a long run?  
   Options: Rice, dal, a vegetable, curd and a banana / A packet of chips and a fizzy drink / Only a cup of tea  
   **Answer:** Rice, dal, a vegetable, curd and a banana. *Why:* The first meal gives energy from the rice and banana, building food from dal and curd, and protection from the vegetable, with no meat. The other two give little besides sugar, salt and fat.
12. (mcq) In a week's food diary, the row for protective foods is very short. What does this tell you?  
   Options: This week had few vegetables and fruits, so add some next week / The family has done something wrong and must be told off / Protective foods are not needed  
   **Answer:** This week had few vegetables and fruits, so add some next week. *Why:* A short row is information about the week, not a mistake. The next step is to add some of that group in a way the family enjoys.

## Open questions
- **Why do some people love bitter foods like karela while others cannot bear them?** Genes explain part of it, and the foods a child grows up with explain part. Scientists do not yet know how much each part matters, or how a child's liking can change.
- **Is fat a sixth basic taste?** Some scientists found sensors for fatty acids on the tongue and have proposed a taste for fat. Others say the evidence is not yet strong enough to add it to the list.
- **Do the six rasas of Ayurveda match anything that can be measured in the body?** Some of the rasas, like sweet and bitter, match tastes that science has measured. Others, like pungent and astringent, are feelings. How far the old system lines up with the new one is still being studied.
- **Does eating a very wide variety of plants make the gut healthier?** A large study in 2018 found that people who ate more than 30 kinds of plant a week had more kinds of microbe in their gut. Nobody yet knows how much this changes health.
- **How much of what we like to eat is learned before we are born, and how much is born into us?** Julie Mennella found that babies prefer flavours they met in the womb and in their mother's milk. But how long this lasts, and how much a child's liking can change later, is still being studied.
- **Why do people everywhere mark special days with special food?** Historians and scientists have ideas about sharing, memory and belonging, but nobody can say for certain where the habit began.

## Connected maths
- In a blind taste test, Zainab names the taste correctly in 14 of her 16 touches. What fraction of her touches were correct? Is that more than three quarters? **14 out of 16, which is 7 out of 8. Yes, it is more than three quarters.**. Three quarters of 16 is 12, because 16 divided by 4 is 4, and 4 times 3 is 12. Zainab named 14, which is more than 12. 14 out of 16 is the same as 7 out of 8, because both numbers can be halved. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- This is a made-up example. 50 grams of dry moong weighs 115 grams after soaking. How many grams of water did the seeds take in? Is that more than half of the soaked weight? **65 grams of water. Yes, it is more than half.**. 115 take away 50 is 65. Half of 115 is 57 and a half. 65 is more than 57 and a half, so more than half of the soaked weight is water. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L4`)
- In a made-up test, dry moong takes 36 minutes to turn soft, soaked moong takes 18 minutes, and sprouted moong takes 9 minutes. How many times as long does dry moong take as sprouted? How many minutes do you save by sprouting? **4 times as long. You save 27 minutes.**. 36 divided by 9 is 4, so the dry moong takes 4 times as long. 36 take away 9 is 27 minutes saved. The sprouted moong halves the soaked time again, since 18 divided by 2 is 9. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In a food diary, one picture stands for 2 servings. Energy foods have 7 pictures, body-building foods have 4, protective foods have 2 and snacks have 3. How many servings of protective food did the child eat? How many more servings of energy food than protective food? **4 servings of protective food. 10 more servings of energy food.**. 2 pictures of 2 servings each is 4 servings of protective food. Energy has 7 pictures of 2, which is 14 servings. 14 take away 4 is 10 more servings of energy food. (queues `math.data.pictographs.L4`)
- A festival feast has 12 dishes: 5 energy foods, 3 body-building foods and 4 protective foods. What fraction of the dishes are protective? If the planner wants an equal number of each job, how many dishes of each job would that be? **4 out of 12, which is one third. Four dishes of each job.**. 4 out of 12 is the same as 1 out of 3, because both numbers divide by 4. An equal split of 12 dishes between 3 jobs is 12 divided by 3, which is 4 each. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A packet says ragi has about 350 milligrams of calcium in every 100 grams, and polished rice has about 10 milligrams in every 100 grams. How many times as much calcium does ragi have? How many grams of ragi hold 700 milligrams of calcium? **35 times as much. 200 grams of ragi.**. 350 divided by 10 is 35, so ragi has 35 times as much calcium. 700 is twice 350, so you need twice 100 grams, which is 200 grams of ragi. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Suppose a child eats about 1,600 kilocalories a day. Advice from the World Health Organization is that sugar added to food and drink should be less than one tenth of that energy. One gram of sugar gives about 4 kilocalories. What is the most added sugar in grams for one day? A 250 millilitre bottle of a sweet drink has 10 grams of sugar in every 100 millilitres. Is the sugar in the bottle more than half of the day's limit? **160 kilocalories, which is 40 grams of sugar. The bottle has 25 grams, which is more than half of 40.**. One tenth of 1,600 is 160 kilocalories. 160 divided by 4 is 40 grams. 250 millilitres is 2 and a half times 100 millilitres, so the bottle holds 2 and a half times 10 grams, which is 25 grams. Half of 40 is 20, and 25 is more than 20. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
