# The solar system: planets, their satellites, and the true distances between them
`sci.sky.solar-system.L5`

**Summary.** Ganymede, a moon of Jupiter, is wider than the planet Mercury. So what makes a world a planet and not a moon? In this lesson you will balance heavy and light dough balls to find the point they go round, and set a marble Earth and a peppercorn Moon at the true distance.

**Intro.** *Ganymede is a moon of Jupiter, and it is wider than the planet Mercury. Pluto was a planet when your grandparents went to school, and today it is not. If size is not the test, what makes one world a planet and another a satellite?* In this lesson you find the answer, and you test it with your own hands. First you set a marble Earth and a peppercorn Moon the right distance apart. Then you balance two dough balls on a pencil, to find the point that two worlds both go round. You also find out how much more dough a ball needs when it is twice as wide. At the end you can say why Pluto's name changed, and where the solar system ends.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name the eight planets in order, and say what sets the Earth apart.
1. By the end I can tell a star, planet, dwarf planet and satellite apart by what each goes round.
2. By the end I can explain why a satellite can be bigger than a planet, using Ganymede and Mercury.
3. By the end I can use the balance rule to find the point that two worlds both go round.
4. By the end I can build a true model of the Earth, the Moon and the Sun on one scale.
5. By the end I can explain why a ball twice as wide holds eight times as much.
6. By the end I can give the three tests for a planet, and one argument on each side about Pluto.
7. By the end I can give three different answers to where the solar system ends.

## Terms
- **Solar system**: The Sun and everything that goes round it: eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and dust. Gravity holds it all together. *e.g.* The Earth, the Moon, Jupiter, Pluto and Halley's Comet all belong to the solar system.
- **Star**: A huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light and heat. The Sun is the star nearest to us. *e.g.* The Sun is a star. Every other star in the night sky is a far-away sun.
- **Planet**: A large round world that goes round a star and gives off no light of its own. A planet has also cleared its path, which means that no other large bodies share the path it travels round the Sun. *e.g.* The Earth is a planet. It goes round the Sun once in about 365 days.
- **Orbit**: The path one body follows round another body. Gravity keeps the body on that path. *e.g.* The Moon's orbit round the Earth takes about 27 days. The Earth's orbit round the Sun takes one year.
- **Satellite**: A body that goes round a planet or another world that is not a star. A natural satellite is also called a moon. *e.g.* The Moon is the Earth's natural satellite. Ganymede is a satellite of Jupiter.
- **Artificial satellite**: A machine that people have put into orbit. It goes round the Earth or another world, as a moon does. *e.g.* The International Space Station goes round the Earth about 400 kilometres up. India's first satellite was Aryabhata, in 1975.
- **Dwarf planet**: A round world that goes round the Sun but has not cleared other bodies from its path. It is not a satellite of anything. *e.g.* Pluto, Ceres and Eris are dwarf planets. Pluto shares its path with thousands of icy bodies.
- **Asteroid**: A rocky or metal body that goes round the Sun and is too small to pull itself round. Most asteroids are between Mars and Jupiter. *e.g.* Vesta is an asteroid about 525 kilometres wide. Most asteroids are far smaller and shaped like lumpy potatoes.
- **Kuiper belt**: A wide ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune. It lies about 30 to 50 times as far from the Sun as the Earth does. *e.g.* Pluto is one of the largest bodies in the Kuiper belt. You say the name 'KY-per'.
- **Balance point**: The point between two bodies where they would balance, as on a see-saw. Two worlds held by gravity both go round this point. Scientists call it the barycentre. *e.g.* The balance point of the Earth and the Moon is inside the Earth, about 4,700 kilometres from its centre.
- **Mass**: How much matter a thing is made of. Mass is measured in kilograms. A body with more mass has a stronger pull of gravity. *e.g.* The Earth has 81 times the mass of the Moon. The Sun has about 333,000 times the mass of the Earth.
- **Diameter**: The width of a ball or a circle, measured straight through its centre. *e.g.* The Earth's diameter is about 12,742 kilometres. The Moon's is about 3,474 kilometres.
- **Volume**: How much space a thing takes up. A ball twice as wide has eight times the volume. *e.g.* Jupiter is about 11 times as wide as the Earth. Its volume is about 1,300 times the Earth's.
- **Scale**: The rule that turns real sizes into model sizes. One scale must be used for every size and every distance in a true model. *e.g.* On the scale of 1 centimetre to 8,000 kilometres, the Earth is a marble. The Moon is a peppercorn 48 centimetres away.
- **Astronomical unit**: The distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometres. It is used to measure the solar system. The short form is AU. *e.g.* Jupiter is about 5 astronomical units from the Sun. Neptune is about 30.
- **Revolution**: One full trip of a body round another body. One revolution of the Earth round the Sun is one year. *e.g.* Mercury makes one revolution in 88 days. Neptune takes about 165 years.
- **Heliosphere**: The huge bubble of thin gas that the Sun blows out around itself. Its edge is far beyond all the planets. *e.g.* The spacecraft Voyager 1 left the heliosphere in 2012, about 120 times as far from the Sun as the Earth.
- **Axis**: The imaginary line through a spinning world, from one pole to the other. The Earth spins on its axis once in about 24 hours. *e.g.* The Earth's axis leans about 23.5 degrees. Uranus leans so far that it spins almost on its side.

## Explanations
### Star, planet or satellite: what a body goes round decides the name · *defined*
**Definition:** A star makes its own light. A planet is a large round world that goes round a star. A satellite goes round a planet or another world that is not a star.

The Sun is a star, because it makes light and heat deep inside itself. The Earth makes no light, and it goes round the Sun, so it is a planet. The Moon goes round the Earth, so it is a satellite. These names say nothing about size. Ganymede is 5,268 kilometres wide and Mercury is 4,879 kilometres wide. Ganymede is the wider world, yet it is a satellite, because it goes round Jupiter. If Ganymede went round the Sun alone, it would be called a planet.
- e.g. The Sun makes its own light, so it is a star.
- e.g. Mercury goes round the Sun, so it is a planet, even though it is smaller than two moons.
- e.g. Ganymede and Titan are both wider than Mercury. But they go round Jupiter and Saturn, so they are satellites.
### The eight planets, and the Earth among them · *defined*
**Definition:** Eight planets go round the Sun. In order from the Sun they are Mercury, Venus, the Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

The first four are small and made of rock and metal. The next four are giants made mostly of gas and ice. The Earth is the third planet and the largest of the four rocky ones. It is the only planet with seas of liquid water on its surface. It is the only world where we know living beings exist. Its air is about one-fifth oxygen, and living beings made nearly all of that oxygen. The Earth also has one very large Moon. All eight planets go round the Sun the same way, on paths that lie almost flat, like rings on a plate.
- e.g. Widths in kilometres: Mercury 4,879, Venus 12,104, the Earth 12,742, Mars 6,779.
- e.g. Widths in kilometres: Jupiter 139,820, Saturn 116,460, Uranus 50,724, Neptune 49,244.
- e.g. Distance from the Sun in astronomical units: Mercury 0.4, Venus 0.7, the Earth 1, Mars 1.5, Jupiter 5.2, Saturn 9.5, Uranus 19, Neptune 30.
### Satellites: natural ones and the ones people make · *defined*
**Definition:** A natural satellite, or moon, is a body that goes round a planet without anyone putting it there. An artificial satellite is a machine that people have put into orbit.

Mercury and Venus have no moons. The Earth has one and Mars has two. The giant planets have many. By 2025, astronomers had counted about 95 moons for Jupiter and more than 270 for Saturn. They had counted about 28 for Uranus and 16 for Neptune. The counts keep rising. Seven moons are wider than Pluto, and our Moon is one of them. People have also made satellites since 1957. The International Space Station goes round the Earth about 400 kilometres up, once every 90 minutes. Television and weather satellites sit about 36,000 kilometres up. A spacecraft that goes round the Moon or Mars is a satellite of that world.
- e.g. Natural satellites: the Moon, Ganymede, Titan, and Phobos, a moon of Mars only 22 kilometres wide.
- e.g. Artificial satellites: Aryabhata in 1975, the INSAT weather satellites and the International Space Station.
- e.g. The Chandrayaan-2 orbiter goes round the Moon. So it is a satellite of a satellite.
### Two worlds both go round their balance point · *defined*
**Definition:** Two bodies held by gravity both go round one point between them, called the balance point or barycentre. The point lies nearer the heavier body.

In The Two-Ball Balance, you balanced a heavy dough ball and a light dough ball on a pencil. The pencil balanced at a point nearer the heavy ball. A ball twice as heavy sat half as far from the point. Worlds follow the same rule. The Earth has 81 times the mass of the Moon. So the Earth is 81 times nearer to their balance point than the Moon is. That puts the point about 4,700 kilometres from the Earth's centre, which is inside the Earth. The Moon goes round this point in a wide circle. The Earth goes round it too, in a small circle, once every 27 days. The Sun and Jupiter also share a balance point. It lies just outside the Sun's surface.
- e.g. The Earth and the Moon: the balance point is inside the Earth, about 1,700 kilometres below the ground.
- e.g. Pluto and its moon Charon: the balance point is in the empty space between them, so both circle a point outside Pluto.
- e.g. The Sun and Jupiter: the balance point is about 46,000 kilometres above the Sun's surface.
### What counts as a planet: the three tests of 2006 · *debated*
**Definition:** In 2006 the International Astronomical Union agreed three tests. A planet goes round the Sun, is heavy enough to have pulled itself round, and has cleared other bodies from its path.

Pluto passes the first two tests. It goes round the Sun, and it is round. It fails the third, because it shares its path with thousands of icy bodies in the Kuiper belt. So since 24 August 2006, Pluto has been called a dwarf planet. Nothing about Pluto changed that day. The way people sort worlds changed. Many scientists who study planets still disagree with the third test, and the argument goes on.
- e.g. The Earth passes all three tests. It is far heavier than everything else near its path put together.
- e.g. Ceres goes round the Sun and is round, but it shares the asteroid belt with millions of rocks. So it is a dwarf planet.
- e.g. The Moon is round, but it goes round the Earth. So it is a satellite and fails the first test.
- **Mike Brown, an American astronomer whose team found Eris in 2005:** The eight planets are far heavier than everything else near their paths. Pluto is one of thousands of similar icy bodies. Calling eight worlds planets shows a real difference in how the solar system is built.
- **Alan Stern, the American scientist who led the New Horizons mission to Pluto:** A planet should be judged by what it is, not by where it is. Pluto is round and has mountains, glaciers, air and five moons. Any world heavy enough to be round should be called a planet, even if that gives more than a hundred planets.
### Dwarf planets and small bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** A dwarf planet is round and goes round the Sun, but has not cleared its path. Asteroids and comets also go round the Sun, but they are too small to be round.

Astronomers list five dwarf planets so far: Ceres, Pluto, Eris, Haumea and Makemake. Ceres lies in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. The other four lie beyond Neptune. Eris is almost exactly as wide as Pluto and has more mass. Asteroids are lumps of rock and metal, and most are in the asteroid belt. Comets are lumps of ice and dust that come in from far beyond Neptune. In Round or Lumpy, every world under 300 kilometres wide was lumpy, and every world over 900 kilometres wide was round. Why are big worlds round and small ones lumpy? A big world has strong gravity, and it pulls every part towards the centre. A ball is the shape where every part is as near the centre as it can be.
- e.g. Round: Ceres at 940 kilometres wide, and Pluto at 2,377 kilometres wide.
- e.g. Lumpy: Phobos at 22 kilometres wide, and the asteroid Eros at about 17 kilometres wide.
- e.g. In between: Vesta, at 525 kilometres wide, is nearly round but not quite.
### A true model uses one scale for sizes and distances · *defined*
**Definition:** A scale turns every real length into a model length in the same way. A model is true only if sizes and distances use the same scale.

In Marble Earth, Peppercorn Moon, 1 centimetre stood for 8,000 kilometres. The Earth became a marble 1.6 centimetres wide. The Moon became a peppercorn 48 centimetres away. On the same scale the Sun is a ball 1.74 metres wide, as tall as a grown-up. It stands about 190 metres away, at the far end of a long street. Jupiter is a ball about 17 centimetres wide, nearly a kilometre from the Sun. Neptune is a lemon about 5.6 kilometres away. Pictures in books cannot show this. They shrink the distances far more than the sizes, so the planets fit on one page.
- e.g. The Earth and the Moon: a marble and a peppercorn, 48 centimetres apart.
- e.g. The Sun: a ball 1.74 metres wide, about 190 metres from the marble.
- e.g. The nearest star after the Sun is about 50,000 kilometres away on this scale. That is further than the distance once round the real Earth.
### A ball twice as wide holds eight times as much · *defined*
**Definition:** When a ball becomes twice as wide, it also becomes twice as deep and twice as tall. So its volume becomes 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 times as much.

In Twice as Wide, a dough ball 4 centimetres wide took 8 of the small balls that were 2 centimetres wide. A ball 6 centimetres wide took 27, which is 3 x 3 x 3. The same rule works for worlds. Jupiter is about 11 times as wide as the Earth. So about 11 x 11 x 11 = 1,331 Earths would fit inside Jupiter. The Sun is 109 times as wide as the Earth, and about 1.3 million Earths would fit inside it. The rule also works the other way. The Moon is about a quarter as wide as the Earth, but about 49 Moons would fit inside the Earth.
- e.g. Twice as wide: 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 times the volume.
- e.g. Three times as wide: 3 x 3 x 3 = 27 times the volume.
- e.g. Jupiter, about 11 times as wide as the Earth: about 1,300 times the volume.
### Almost all the mass of the solar system is in the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun holds about 99.86 percent of the mass of the whole solar system. Everything else together is less than 2 parts in every 1,000.

In A Thousand Grains of Rice, 1,000 grains stood for the mass of the whole solar system. All but about one and a half grains belonged to the Sun. Jupiter was about one grain. Every other planet, moon, asteroid and comet together made less than half a grain. The Earth was a speck too small to pick up. This is why the Sun's gravity rules the solar system. It is also why the balance point of the Sun and any planet lies in or near the Sun.
- e.g. The Sun has about 333,000 times the mass of the Earth.
- e.g. The Sun has about 1,047 times the mass of Jupiter.
- e.g. Jupiter has about two and a half times the mass of all the other planets put together.
### The Earth and the Moon: a planet with an unusually large satellite · *idea*
**Definition:** The Moon is about a quarter as wide as the Earth. No other planet has a moon so large compared with itself.

The Moon is 3,474 kilometres wide and about 384,400 kilometres away. That distance is about 30 Earths side by side. Lasers bounced off mirrors on the Moon show that it moves away about 3.8 centimetres each year. The Moon's pull raises the tides in the sea. How did the Earth get such a large Moon? The leading idea is that a world about the size of Mars hit the young Earth about 4.5 billion years ago. Rock thrown out by the crash gathered into the Moon. Moon rocks brought back by astronauts fit this idea, but scientists still argue about the details.
- e.g. The Moon is about one-quarter of the Earth's width and has one eighty-first of its mass.
- e.g. Ganymede is wider than the Moon, but it is only about one twenty-seventh of the width of Jupiter.
- e.g. Charon is about half as wide as Pluto. Pluto is a dwarf planet, so it is not counted here.
- **Most planet scientists today:** A world about the size of Mars struck the young Earth, and the Moon formed from the rock thrown out. This explains why Moon rock is so like the Earth's outer rock.
- **Scientists who test the idea:** Moon rock is almost too much like Earth rock for a simple crash. Newer ideas include a much harder crash, or several smaller ones. Nobody saw it happen, so the evidence is still being weighed.
### Where does the solar system end? · *debated*
**Definition:** There is no single agreed edge. The answer depends on what you measure. The choices are the last planet, the last icy bodies, the Sun's bubble of gas, and the reach of the Sun's gravity.

Neptune, the last planet, is 30 astronomical units from the Sun. The Kuiper belt of icy bodies reaches to about 50. The Sun also blows out a thin wind of gas in every direction. This wind makes a bubble called the heliosphere. The spacecraft Voyager 1 crossed the edge of the bubble in August 2012, about 120 astronomical units out. But the Sun's gravity reaches much further. Comets seem to come from a far shell of icy bodies called the Oort cloud. It may reach 100,000 astronomical units out. Nobody has seen the Oort cloud itself. Astronomers worked it out from the paths of comets.
- e.g. By the last planet: 30 astronomical units, at Neptune.
- e.g. By the Sun's bubble: about 120 astronomical units, where Voyager 1 crossed in 2012.
- e.g. By the Sun's gravity: perhaps 100,000 astronomical units, at the edge of the Oort cloud.
- **A scientist who studies the Sun's wind:** The solar system ends where the Sun's wind stops and the thin gas between the stars begins. Voyager 1 crossed that edge in 2012.
- **A scientist who studies orbits:** The solar system is everything the Sun's gravity holds. By that measure Voyager 1 is still deep inside it. It will need about 300 years to reach the Oort cloud.
### A wobbling star gives away hidden planets · *defined*
**Definition:** A star with a planet goes round the balance point it shares with that planet. From far away, the star seems to wobble.

In The Hidden Ball, the visible ball swung in a circle, and the circle was wider when the hidden ball was heavier. A star with a planet does the same. Astronomers cannot easily see a planet beside a bright, far star. But they can measure the star's small wobble. In 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found a planet this way, going round a star called 51 Pegasi. Since then thousands of planets have been found round other stars. Someone watching our Sun from far away could find Jupiter the same way.
- e.g. The Sun moves round its balance point with Jupiter once in about 12 years.
- e.g. The first planet found by a star's wobble: 51 Pegasi b, in 1995.
- e.g. A heavier planet, or one closer to its star, makes a wobble that is easier to measure.
### The Earth's two movements and its tilt · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth spins once on its axis in about 24 hours, which gives day and night. It goes once round the Sun in about 365 and a quarter days, which is one year.

The Earth's axis leans about 23.5 degrees away from the upright line, where upright means at right angles to the flat surface of the Earth's path round the Sun. This tilt gives the seasons. The Earth's distance from the Sun does not. The Earth is nearest the Sun in early January, when north India has winter. It is furthest in early July, in the middle of the monsoon. The gap is only about 3 percent, so it changes the heat very little. Mars leans about 25 degrees, so it has seasons too. Each Martian season lasts about twice as long as one on the Earth, because a Martian year is 687 Earth days. Uranus leans about 98 degrees, so for years one pole faces the Sun.
- e.g. Spin: one turn in about 24 hours, so every place has a day and a night.
- e.g. Journey: one trip round the Sun in about 365 and a quarter days.
- e.g. Tilt: about 23.5 degrees for the Earth, and about 25 degrees for Mars.

## Mostly agreed
- Eight planets go round the Sun in the same direction, on paths that lie almost in one flat sheet. *How we know:* Astronomers have tracked every planet for hundreds of years. Spacecraft have visited all eight, and each arrived where the orbits said it would be.
- The Sun holds about 99.86 percent of the mass of the solar system. *How we know:* The time a planet takes to go round the Sun shows how hard the Sun pulls. The orbits of moons and spacecraft give the mass of each planet in the same way.
- The Moon is about 384,400 kilometres away, and it moves away about 3.8 centimetres each year. *How we know:* Apollo astronauts and Soviet rovers left mirrors on the Moon between 1969 and 1973. Scientists time laser flashes that bounce back from them.
- A satellite can be larger than a planet. Ganymede and Titan are both wider than Mercury. *How we know:* The Voyager spacecraft, and later Galileo and Cassini, flew past these moons and measured them.
- Two bodies held by gravity both go round a shared balance point, and the heavier body moves less. *How we know:* Isaac Newton showed it in 1687. Astronomers have measured the wobble of the Sun, of Pluto and of thousands of stars with planets.

## How we found out
**Giuseppe Piazzi, Clyde Tombaugh, James Christy, and Mike Brown with Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz, Ceres was found in 1801 and Pluto in 1930. Charon was found in 1978 and Eris in 2005. The vote was on 24 August 2006..** On 1 January 1801, Giuseppe Piazzi, an astronomer in Palermo in Italy, found a new world between Mars and Jupiter. He named it Ceres, and books listed it as a planet. Then more small worlds were found in the same region. By the 1850s there were many, and astronomers moved them all to a new list, the asteroids. In 1930 Clyde Tombaugh, at the Lowell Observatory in the United States, found Pluto. Astronomers expected a large planet, heavier than the Earth, because Percival Lowell had predicted one. The sums behind his prediction were done by his team of human computers, led by the mathematician Elizabeth Williams. But that expectation was wrong. In 1978 James Christy found Pluto's moon, Charon. The moon's orbit let astronomers weigh Pluto. It had only about 2 parts in 1,000 of the Earth's mass. From 1992, more icy worlds were found beyond Neptune. In 2005 Mike Brown, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz found Eris, which has more mass than Pluto. Either Eris was a tenth planet, or Pluto was not a planet. On 24 August 2006, in Prague, astronomers voted for three tests, and Pluto became a dwarf planet.  
*What people believed before:* That Pluto was a large planet, heavier than the Earth. In 1978 the orbit of its moon Charon showed that Pluto has only about 2 parts in 1,000 of the Earth's mass.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Pluto is a planet, and any world heavy enough to be round should be called one.** (Alan Stern and many scientists who study planets; *debated*)  
  For: Pluto is round and has mountains of ice, glaciers, thin air and five moons. A name should describe what a world is like. Even the Earth could not clear a path as wide as Pluto's.  
  Against: This rule would give well over a hundred planets, and large moons would count too. The eight planets are far heavier than everything else near their paths. Pluto is not.
- **The Earth and the Moon are a double planet, not a planet and a satellite.** (The writer Isaac Asimov and a few astronomers; *minority*)  
  For: The Moon is very large compared with the Earth. The Sun pulls the Moon more than twice as hard as the Earth does. The Moon's path round the Sun always curves towards the Sun.  
  Against: The balance point of the Earth and the Moon is inside the Earth. By the usual rule, that makes the Moon a satellite. Astronomers have agreed no official meaning for 'double planet'.
- **A satellite is always smaller than a planet.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Every moon is smaller than the planet it goes round. Most moons are tiny.  
  Against: Ganymede and Titan are wider than the planet Mercury. Seven moons are wider than Pluto. A satellite is named for what it goes round, not for its size.
- **The Sun stands still at the centre while the planets go round it.** (Many school books and pictures; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun holds almost all the mass, so it moves very little. For most purposes the picture works well.  
  Against: The Sun goes round the balance point it shares with the planets, mostly with Jupiter. That point is often outside the Sun. The whole solar system also goes round the centre of the Milky Way.
- **A ninth planet, several times heavier than the Earth, lies far beyond Neptune.** (Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown, American astronomers, in 2016; *debated*)  
  For: Several far icy bodies have paths that lean the same way. The pull of an unseen planet would explain that.  
  Against: Nobody has seen such a planet, in spite of long searches. Other astronomers say the pattern may come from where telescopes happened to look.

## Roots
We have learned what planets and satellites are, how far apart they are, and that two worlds both go round a balance point. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In The Two-Ball Balance, the pencil balanced close to the heavy ball. When the pair turned on its thread, both balls went round that point, and the heavy one made the smaller circle. The Earth and the Moon go round such a point, and so do the Sun and Jupiter. So nothing in the solar system stays still while the rest go round it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say the Moon goes round the Earth, and the Earth goes round the Sun. These words make us picture a big body that stands still in the middle. But every pull of gravity works both ways. The big body moves too, only less.  
*Evidence:* In The Two-Ball Balance, the ball with 4 pieces still made a small circle when the pair turned on its thread. The Earth makes a small circle every 27 days, round a point about 4,700 kilometres from its centre. The Sun circles a point that is often outside its own surface, mostly because of Jupiter.

- *who am i:* You live on a world that moves in several ways at once. The Earth carries you round the Sun, and round the balance point it shares with the Moon. You feel still only because everything near you moves with you.
- *what is life:* The Moon and the Sun pull on every living being on the Earth. Each being pulls back on them, a very little. The tides that feed crabs and shore birds twice a day come from that pull.
- *what is this world:* The solar system has no fixed middle that stays still. It is the Sun, the planets and the moons, all pulling on one another with gravity and all moving.

The Sun, the planets and the moons all formed from one cloud of gas and dust. They are separate bodies now, but gravity still joins every one of them to every other. The Moon pulls the Earth, the Earth pulls the Moon, and both pull the Sun. So no body rules from a still centre. The many worlds move together as one system, and each pair of them goes round a balance point that the two share.

- **Nicholas of Cusa, On Learned Ignorance (1440), Book 2, chapters 11 and 12:** The universe has no fixed centre. The Earth is not at the middle of everything, and it is not at rest. (Cusa reasoned this out a hundred years before Copernicus. The Two-Ball Balance shows a small part of it: even the heavy ball moved.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 5:** “तदेजति तन्नैजति तद्दूरे तद्वन्तिके / तदन्तरस्य सर्वस्य तदु सर्वस्यास्य बाह्यतः” It moves, and it moves not. It is far, and it is near. It is inside all this, and it is outside all this. (The verse speaks of the one Self, not of planets. Science finds no body that stands still. The sages say there is a stillness of another kind, which holds all movement.)
- **Ernst Mach, The Science of Mechanics (1883), chapter 2:** We can only say that one thing moves compared with other things. Nobody can point to a place that is truly at rest. (When we say the Earth goes round the Sun, we have chosen to treat the Sun as still. A balance point is a better choice than the Sun, but it also moves, round the centre of the galaxy.)
- Science (settled): Two bodies held by gravity both go round their shared balance point. Isaac Newton showed in 1687 that the Sun itself is always moving for this reason.
- Science (idea): The Moon formed when a world about the size of Mars struck the young Earth. If so, the Moon is made partly of the young Earth's own rock.
- Other view, A practical astronomer: The Sun holds 99.86 percent of the mass, and its wobble is tiny beside the orbits of the planets. Saying that the planets go round the Sun is true enough for almost every purpose. Talking a great deal about the balance point can make a small correction sound like a completely new picture of the solar system.
- Practice, Pulled and pulling (3 min): Stand facing each other and hold each other's wrists. Lean back slowly, both together, until your arms are straight. Stay there for three slow breaths. Feel that each of you is holding the other up. Now turn slowly, a quarter of a circle, and notice whose feet move further. Then stand up straight and let go gently.
- Ask: In The Two-Ball Balance, even the heavy ball moved. Is there anything in your life that pulls on you without being pulled by you?
- Ask: If nothing in the solar system stays still, what do we mean when we say 'sit still'?
- Ask: Pluto's name changed and Pluto did not. Which of your own names and labels could change without changing you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Pluto did not change in 2006, but its name did. Does a name tell you about the thing, or about the people who chose the name? (hint: Think of the three tests for a planet. Who wrote them, and could they have written other tests?)
- *deep:* The Moon goes round the Earth, and the Earth also moves in a small circle round the point it shares with the Moon. Can one thing pull on another without being pulled back? (hint: Think of The Two-Ball Balance. The heavy ball also moved in a circle when the pair turned on its thread.)
- *big:* On the marble scale, the Earth is a marble 190 metres from the Sun. Why does the Earth feel so big to us? (hint: Think of how long a journey round the Earth takes, and how far you have ever travelled.)

## Story: Thirty Steps to Neptune
*Venetia Burney was a real girl. On 14 March 1930, aged eleven, she suggested the name Pluto at breakfast in Oxford. Her grandfather Falconer Madan passed the idea to Professor Herbert Hall Turner, who sent a telegram to the Lowell Observatory. The name was announced on 1 May 1930, and her grandfather gave her five pounds. The clay walk in the University Parks, the words spoken at breakfast and what Venetia thought are imagined. So is her answer to the telephone calls in 2006. She said later that she was not sure why the name came to her.*

On the morning of 14 March 1930, Venetia Burney was eating her breakfast and thinking about nothing in particular. She was eleven years old. She lived in Oxford, in England, with her mother and her grandfather.

Her grandfather, Falconer Madan, had looked after a great library for most of his life. He was retired now. He read the newspaper aloud at breakfast, whether anyone had asked him to or not.

"Here is something," he said, and he shook the page flat. "A new planet has been found, beyond Neptune. An observatory in Arizona, in America, has been hunting for it for years. A young man there has found it on his photographs." He looked over the top of the paper. "It has no name yet."

Venetia put down her spoon.

She was thinking of a morning the year before. Her teacher had taken the class to the University Parks with a bag of clay, and had set a big lump of it on the grass. "This is the Sun," she had said. "Now, one step for the Earth."

A girl took one step and pressed a tiny lump of clay into the grass. Mars went half a step further. After that the steps grew long. There were five steps to Jupiter and nearly ten to Saturn. Uranus took nineteen.

Venetia had been given Neptune. She counted thirty steps, right across the grass and in under the trees. When she turned round, the Sun was a brown dot far behind her. She could not see the Earth at all.

She had stood beside Neptune's little lump, in the shade, feeling slightly cold. So this is how far it is, she had thought. From here the Sun would hardly warm you. It would look like one bright star among the others.

And now the newspaper said there was a planet further out than that. It was further than thirty steps. By her reckoning it was out past the trees, in the part of the Parks where nobody walked.

She thought about what such a place would be like. It would be dark and very cold. Even at noon, the sky would look like night.

Venetia knew the old Greek and Roman stories well. All the other planets had names from them. Jupiter was the king of the gods. Mars was the god of war, and Neptune was the god of the sea. There was one more god, she remembered, who ruled a dark kingdom under the ground, where sunlight never came. He owned a helmet that made its wearer invisible. No planet had his name yet.

"Why not call it Pluto?" she said.

Her grandfather lowered the newspaper. He did not laugh. He had spent his life among books, and he knew a good name when he heard one. "Why not, indeed," he said.

That same morning he wrote a note to his friend Herbert Hall Turner, who was a professor of astronomy. Professor Turner was away in London, at a meeting where the astronomers could talk of nothing but the new planet. When he came home and read the note, he sent a telegram to the observatory in Arizona. It said that a small girl, Venetia Burney, suggested the name Pluto for a dark and gloomy planet.

Then, for weeks, nothing happened. Venetia went to school. Names arrived at the observatory from all over the world. Some people wanted Minerva, and some wanted Cronus.

At last the astronomers at the observatory voted, and every one of them chose Pluto. It helped that the first two letters, P and L, were the initials of Percival Lowell. He had built the observatory and begun the search, and he had died before the planet was found.

On 1 May 1930 the name was announced to the world. Venetia's grandfather gave her a five-pound note. That was a great deal of money for a girl of eleven.

Venetia grew up and became a teacher. For seventy-six years, children in every country learned nine planets. The last of the nine had the name she had given it at breakfast.

But the astronomers went on measuring. Pluto turned out to be small. It was smaller than our own Moon. Then, far out beyond Neptune, they began to find other icy worlds, one after another, on paths like Pluto's. One of them had even more mass than Pluto. Pluto was not alone out there, past the trees. It was one of a crowd.

In August 2006 the astronomers met and voted again. This time they voted on what the word 'planet' should mean. By the new meaning there were eight planets. Pluto was something else, called a dwarf planet.

Venetia was eighty-eight by then. People telephoned to ask her how she felt about it. She told them she had been largely indifferent to the argument.

Earlier that same year, a spacecraft had set off for Pluto. It carried an instrument built by students and named after her. The journey took more than nine years, and Venetia did not live to see the pictures.

The pictures showed mountains of ice, and a wide pale plain shaped like a heart. Pluto was going round the Sun, as it had done for billions of years, forty steps out and past the trees. The word 'planet' had changed. The world she had named was the same as ever.

**For the parent:** For the parent: there are two turns. The first is at the breakfast table. Venetia has walked the true distances, so she can picture how dark a world beyond Neptune must be, and the name follows. The second is in 2006. The astronomers change the meaning of a word, and Pluto does not change at all. The story shows what a scale model is for. It also shows that 'planet' is a label people chose.

- Ask: Why did walking thirty steps help Venetia picture the new planet better than a drawing in a book could?
- Ask: From as far away as Neptune, why would the Sun look like a bright star?
- Ask: What did astronomers find beyond Neptune that made them think again about Pluto?
- Ask: In 2006, what changed: Pluto, or the meaning of a word? How do you know?
- Ask: If you could name a new world, what would you want to know about it first?

  *Parent does:* 1. Roll one small ball of dough and show that it is 2 cm wide. 2. Ask how many such balls will make a ball 4 cm wide. 3. Ask how many will make a ball 6 cm wide. 4. Write down both guesses and the reasons.

Stand a book on each side of the ball and measure the gap. It should be 2 cm. 4. Cut 26 more pieces of the same length and roll each into a ball.

  - Ask: Two balls did not make the ball twice as wide. Why not? (good: The new dough has to make the ball taller and deeper too, not only wider.; The dough spreads all round the ball.)

- **s4 (7 min)**

  - Note: Expect about 4.6 cm at 12 balls, 5 cm at 16 and 5.4 cm at 20. The ball should reach 6 cm at about 27 balls. If your count is between 24 and 30, the test has worked.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the two counts side by side: about 8 and about 27. 2. Ask your child to look for a pattern with the numbers 2 and 3. 3. Ask how many small balls a ball 10 cm wide would need. 4. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Twice as wide took about eight. Three times as wide took about twenty-seven. What is the pattern? Now tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your counts, about 8 and about 27. Find the pattern with 2 and 3. How many for a ball 10 cm wide? Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: What is the pattern? (good: 8 is 2 x 2 x 2, and 27 is 3 x 3 x 3.; A ball gets wider, taller and deeper together, so you multiply three times.)
  - Ask: A ball 10 cm wide is 5 times as wide as a small ball. How many small balls does it need? (good: 5 x 5 x 5 = 125.)
  - Note: After your child has explained, tell them this. Jupiter is about 11 times as wide as the Earth. So about 11 x 11 x 11 = 1,331 Earths would fit inside it. The Sun is 109 times as wide as the Earth, and holds about 1.3 million Earths.
  - Check: A ball is 3 times as wide as a small ball. How many small balls of dough does it hold? → **27**
### e3 · The Hidden Ball: can a wobble show how heavy a ball you cannot see? (35 min)
- **question:** A heavy ball and a hidden ball turn round their balance point. How does the width of the heavy ball's swing change when the hidden ball is heavier? Can you work out the hidden ball's weight from the swing?
- **predict:** Guess first. When the hidden ball is heavier, will the visible ball swing wider, narrower or the same? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Keep the visible ball at 4 pieces of dough and use the same pencil each time. Only the dough hidden in the cup changes. Watch from the same place, level with the visible ball. Measure each swing twice.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three secret pairs. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Secret pair | Width of the swing (cm) | Distance of the visible ball from the thread (cm) | My guess for the hidden pieces | Real hidden pieces
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the pencil with a ball of 4 pieces on one end. 2. Say that a cup will hide a second ball on the other end. 3. Ask how the visible ball will move, if the hidden ball is heavy and if it is light. 4. Write down the guess and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* A cup will hide a second ball. You will see only this ball. Will it swing wider or narrower if the hidden ball is heavier? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A cup will hide a second ball. Will the visible ball swing wider if the hidden ball is heavier? Say why. Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: A heavy ball pulls the balance point towards itself, so the visible ball is further out.; The visible ball swings in a bigger circle when its partner is heavy.; It swings less, because a heavy partner holds it still. (A fair guess. The test will show.))
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the ruler flat on the wall, at the height of the visible ball. 2. Send your child out of the room. 3. Put 1 piece of dough in a cup, close it, and tape it to the pencil end. 4. Slide the pencil across your finger until it balances. Tie the thread there.  
  *Parent says:* Please wait outside while I hide the dough. I will call you in when the pair is ready. Do not look at the cup until I say.  
  *Child mode:* Wait outside the room while your grown-up hides some dough in a cup. Do not peek.
  - Note: Pair A has 1 piece in the cup. With an 18 cm pencil, the thread goes about 3.6 cm from the visible ball. Use the smallest, lightest cups. A cup weighs a few grams, so pair A may swing up to 8 cm. Hang the thread from a chair back or a door handle, so the pencil hangs level.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Call your child in. They sit about 2 metres away, level with the visible ball. 2. Twist the thread about 5 turns and let the pair turn. 3. Your child marks the far left and the far right place of the visible ball against the ruler. 4. They measure the width between the two marks, twice.  
  *Parent says:* Watch only the visible ball, with the ruler behind it. Where does it reach on the far left, and on the far right? How wide is the swing?  
  *Child mode:* Sit 2 metres away. Watch the visible ball. Mark its far left and far right places. Measure the width. Do it twice.
  - Ask: What is the width of the swing? Half of it is how far the visible ball is from the thread. (good: About 7 cm, so the ball is about 3.5 cm from the thread.; A small swing, because the hidden ball is light.)
  - Note: Stand behind your child and spin the pair gently. If the pair tips, slide the thread a few millimetres towards the low end. Watch from the side. From above, the visible ball would trace a circle, and from the side it shows only the left and right swing. Expect a width of about 7 or 8 cm. If you have a phone, film the swing from 2 metres. Pause the film at the two far ends.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child leaves the room again. 2. Hide 2 pieces in the cup and tie the thread at the new balance point. 3. Your child measures the swing as before. 4. Do the same for 4 pieces. 5. Write both widths in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the second secret pair, and then the third. Measure each swing the same way. Which swung widest? Which swung narrowest?  
  *Child mode:* Wait outside again while 2 pieces, then 4 pieces, are hidden. Come back and measure each swing the same way.
  - Note: Pair B has 2 pieces hidden. With an 18 cm pencil, expect a width of about 12 cm. Pair C has 4 pieces, and the width is about 18 cm. Pair C is the easiest to see, because the thread hangs in the middle of the pencil.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to put the 3 widths in order, from the narrowest to the widest. 2. Ask them to guess the hidden pieces for each pair. 3. Use this rule: half the width is the visible ball's distance. The rest of the pencil is the hidden ball's distance. 4. Open the cups and compare. 5. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Put your three widths in order. Use the rule from The Two-Ball Balance to guess the hidden dough in each cup. Then we open them. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Put your 3 widths in order. Guess the dough in each cup. Open the cups. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: How can the visible ball tell you the weight of a ball you cannot see? (good: The balance point sits nearer the heavier ball, so the visible ball swings wider when the hidden ball is heavy.; The width gives the visible ball's distance, and the rule gives the weights.)
  - Note: Work one together. A swing of 12 cm gives 6 cm for the visible ball and 12 cm for the hidden ball. The visible ball has 4 pieces. The hidden ball has 4 x 6 ÷ 12 = 2 pieces. After your child has explained, tell them this. Astronomers cannot see most planets round other stars. In 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz saw a star swing a little and worked out that a planet was pulling it. They did this with light, not a ruler.
  - Check: A star swings in a small circle. What does a wider circle suggest about a hidden planet? → **The planet is heavier or further away**

## Challenges
### c1 · An Honest Solar System on One Strip of Paper (35 min)
- **problem:** Show the eight planets and the Sun on strips of paper so that nobody is misled. A picture that draws sizes and distances on two different scales must say so. You may use as many strips as you like.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own honest picture of the solar system. Say what it shows well and what it leaves out. Write a caption that tells the truth.
- **constraints:** Join 4 sheets of A4 paper end to end for a strip about 1.2 metres long.; On the distance strip, 1 astronomical unit is 3.5 cm. Mark the Sun at one end and Neptune at about 105 cm.; Show each planet's size to one scale. Put the sizes on a separate strip, or in a table.; Write a caption that says what each scale is, and that the sizes and distances do not use the same scale.
- **general:** Mark each planet's distance as a dot on the long strip, and write its name above.; Draw a second row of circles for the planets' sizes, on a scale of 1 cm for 10,000 km.; Add a table with each planet's distance and width.
- **creative:** Fold the long strip like a concertina, so that it fits in a pocket and opens to show the gaps.; Tape the strip along a corridor or a street and walk it, with one step for each astronomical unit.; Use a string of beads, one bead for each astronomical unit, with the planets as knots.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Remind your child of Fix the Picture. A book's picture of the solar system drew the planets close together. 2. Ask what a reader might wrongly believe. 3. Read the problem and the four constraints together.  
  *Parent says:* In Fix the Picture, the book's picture put the planets near each other. What might a reader get wrong? How could we draw it without misleading anyone?  
  *Child mode:* A book's picture showed the planets close together. How could you draw it truthfully?
- **s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Join four sheets of paper end to end. 2. Mark the Sun at one end. 3. Multiply each planet's distance in astronomical units by 3.5, and mark the dot. 4. Draw the planets' sizes on a second row. 5. Check each dot against the table.  
  *Parent says:* Mercury is 0.4 astronomical units from the Sun. Multiply by 3.5 to find its place on the strip. Then do the rest. Check each dot.  
  *Child mode:* Join 4 sheets of paper. Mark the Sun. Multiply each distance by 3.5. Mark each planet.
  - Note: Distances in astronomical units: Mercury 0.4, Venus 0.7, the Earth 1, Mars 1.5, Jupiter 5.2, Saturn 9.5, Uranus 19, Neptune 30. On the strip: 1.4 cm, 2.5 cm, 3.5 cm, 5.3 cm, 18.2 cm, 33 cm, 66.5 cm and 105 cm. The four inner planets will be crowded into 5 cm.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the caption. It must name both scales. 2. Ask your child what the picture shows well and what it leaves out. 3. Compare it with the book's picture.  
  *Parent says:* What does your picture show better than the book's picture? What does it still leave out?  
  *Child mode:* Write a caption. Name both scales. Say what your picture leaves out.
  - Ask: What does your picture leave out? (good: The sizes are on another scale, so the planets look too small on the strip.; The planets' paths are circles, but the strip shows only a line.; It does not show moons or dwarf planets.)
  - Check: Why must a picture of the solar system name its scales? → **Sizes and distances may not use the same scale**
### c2 · A Double World: build a pair that balances in empty space (30 min)
- **problem:** Build a heavy ball and a light ball that hang level from one thread, with the thread outside the heavy ball. The heavy ball must be at least 6 times as heavy as the light ball.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own double world. Take a photo of the pair, or draw it. Show where the balance point is, and write the two distances. Write the mass ratio and how far outside the heavy ball the point lies.
- **constraints:** The heavy ball must have at least 6 times the mass of the light ball.; The thread must be at least 1 cm outside the surface of the heavy ball.; The pair must hang level for 10 seconds without touching anything.; Use a stick or pencil no longer than 40 cm, thread, tape, dough, coins and nuts or washers.
- **general:** Use a longer stick, so the balance point is far from the heavy ball.; Make the heavy ball small, by packing it with coins or nuts instead of dough.; Keep the heavy ball only 6 or 7 times as heavy as the light one. A much lighter partner moves the point towards the heavy ball.
- **creative:** Hang the two balls on opposite ends of a 40 cm stick, with a dense stack of coins as the heavy ball.; Use a stack of 6 coins against 1 coin, on a 25 cm stick.; Use 7 steel nuts against 1 nut, on a 30 cm chopstick or stick.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem and the four constraints together. 2. Ask your child what makes the balance point lie outside the heavy ball. 3. Let them choose how to build the heavy ball and how long the stick should be.  
  *Parent says:* In The Two-Ball Balance, 8 pieces against 1 piece balanced inside the big ball. What could we change so the point lies outside?  
  *Child mode:* The point must lie outside the heavy ball. Plan how. A longer stick? A smaller heavy ball?
  - Ask: What two things push the balance point outside the heavy ball? (good: A longer gap between the balls.; A small heavy ball, made of something dense.; A heavy ball that is not too much heavier than the light one.)
- **s2 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Build the heavy ball and the light ball. 2. Tape each to an end of the stick, with its middle at the end. 3. Slide the stick across your finger to find the balance point. 4. Tie the thread there. Measure the distance from the thread to the heavy ball's edge.  
  *Parent says:* Build your pair. Find the balance point. Is the thread inside the heavy ball, or outside? How far outside?  
  *Child mode:* Build the pair. Find the balance point. Tie the thread. Is it outside the heavy ball?
  - Note: A stack of 6 one-rupee coins is small, about 1 cm tall and 2 cm wide. A single coin is the light ball. With a 25 cm stick, the balance point is 25 ÷ 7 = 3.6 cm from the stack's middle. That is about 2.5 cm outside the stack. The stick's own weight pushes the point a little further out. A dough ball of 6 pieces is too big for this, so use coins or nuts.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang the pair by its thread. 2. Check that it stays level for 10 seconds. 3. Check the four constraints. 4. If it fails, change one thing and try again.  
  *Parent says:* Hang your pair. Does it stay level? Is the thread more than 1 centimetre outside the heavy ball? What would you change?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the pair. Does it stay level for 10 seconds? Check each constraint.
  - Check: A heavy ball is 8 times as heavy as a light ball. When is their balance point outside the heavy ball? → **When the gap is more than 9 times the heavy ball's radius**

## Worksheets
### Planets, moons and what each world goes round (core, 15 questions)
1. (order) Put these planets in order from the Sun, nearest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Venus', 'Mars', 'Jupiter', 'Neptune']. *Why:* The order from the Sun is Mercury, Venus, the Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The four rocky planets come first and the giants after them.
2. (match) Match each body to the world it goes round.  
   **Answer:** The Moon → The Earth; Ganymede → Jupiter; Charon → Pluto; The Earth → The Sun; Phobos → Mars; The Chandrayaan-2 orbiter → The Moon. *Why:* A body is named by what it goes round. The Earth goes round the Sun, so it is a planet. Each of the others goes round a world that is not a star, so each is a satellite.
3. (fill) A body that makes its own light and heat is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['star']. *Why:* The Sun is a star. Planets and moons only reflect the Sun's light.
4. (truefalse) A moon can be wider than a planet.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Ganymede and Titan are both wider than Mercury. A moon is named for what it goes round, not for its size.
5. (mcq) Ganymede is 5,268 km wide and Mercury is 4,879 km wide. What is Ganymede?  
   Options: A planet, because it is wider than Mercury / A moon, because it goes round Jupiter / A dwarf planet, because it is smaller than Jupiter / A star  
   **Answer:** A moon, because it goes round Jupiter. *Why:* Ganymede goes round Jupiter, so it is a moon. Choosing 'a planet' uses width as the test. Width is not one of the tests.
6. (sort) Sort these worlds by shape. Is each one round or lumpy?  
   **Answer:** The Earth → Round; Pluto → Round; Ceres → Round; Phobos → Lumpy; Eros → Lumpy; Deimos → Lumpy. *Why:* A world with enough mass pulls every part towards its centre, and becomes a ball. Phobos, Deimos and Eros are only 12 to 34 km wide, so their pull is too weak.
7. (mcq) Which of these is NOT one of the three tests for a planet?  
   Options: It goes round the Sun / It is heavy enough to be round / It has at least one moon / It has cleared its path  
   **Answer:** It has at least one moon. *Why:* Mercury and Venus are planets and have no moon. The three tests are going round the Sun, being round, and having cleared the path.
8. (truefalse) In 2006, Pluto shrank, so it stopped being a planet.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Pluto did not change at all. Astronomers agreed on three tests for a planet, and Pluto fails the third one.
9. (write) In The Two-Ball Balance, one ball had 4 pieces and the other had 1. Where did the pencil balance? Say why.  
   **Answer:** It balanced about 4 times nearer the heavy ball than the light ball. On an 18 cm pencil that was about 4 cm from the heavy ball and 14 cm from the light ball. A ball that is 4 times as heavy needs only a quarter of the reach to balance.. *Why:* A strong answer says where the pencil balanced and gives numbers or a ratio. It says the heavier ball sits nearer the balance point.
10. (draw) Draw a pencil with a heavy ball on one end and a light ball on the other. Mark the balance point.  
   **Answer:** The heavy ball is drawn bigger. The balance point is marked on the pencil close to the heavy ball. The part of the pencil on the light ball's side is longer.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the balance point nearer the heavy ball. It shows two parts of the pencil, and the longer part is on the light ball's side.
11. (count) A ball of dough is 4 times as wide as a small ball. How many small balls make it?  
   **Answer:** 64. *Why:* A ball 4 times as wide is also 4 times as tall and 4 times as deep. So it holds 4 x 4 x 4 = 64 small balls.
12. (mcq) The Moon is about a quarter as wide as the Earth. About how many Moons would fit inside the Earth?  
   Options: About 4 / About 16 / About 50 / About 400  
   **Answer:** About 50. *Why:* The Earth is nearly 4 times as wide, so the Moon fits in about 4 x 4 x 4 = 64 times. The true figure is about 49, because the Moon is a little more than a quarter as wide.
13. (perspective) Should Pluto be called a planet?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a fact. One reason for the first: Pluto shares its path with thousands of icy bodies. One for the second: Pluto is round, has air and has five moons. One for the third: Eris, found in 2005, has more mass than Pluto, so the list of planets would keep growing.. *Why:* A strong answer uses a real fact, such as the Kuiper belt, Pluto's moons or Eris. It treats the other views fairly. Scientists still argue about this.
14. (label) Label each part of this drawing of the solar system.  
   **Answer:** ['The asteroid belt: the ring of rocks between Mars and Jupiter', 'The Kuiper belt: the ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune', 'A rocky planet: one of the first four from the Sun', 'A giant planet: one of the last four from the Sun']. *Why:* Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars are rocky. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune are giants of gas and ice. The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter, and the Kuiper belt lies beyond Neptune.
15. (odd) Three of these go round the Earth. Which one does not?  
   Options: The Moon / The International Space Station / Aryabhata, the satellite of 1975 / Ceres  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Ceres goes round the Sun, in the asteroid belt. The Moon is a natural satellite, and the other two are artificial satellites.
### Balance points, scales and cubes (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) One world has 3 times the mass of its partner. How much nearer the balance point is the heavy world?  
   Options: 3 times nearer / The same distance / Half as near / 9 times nearer  
   **Answer:** 3 times nearer. *Why:* The heavy world sits as many times nearer the balance point as it is times heavier. With 3 times the mass, it is 3 times nearer.
2. (mcq) Balls of 4 pieces and 1 piece sit at the ends of a 20 cm pencil. Where is the balance point?  
   Options: 4 cm from the heavy end / 5 cm from the heavy end / 10 cm from the heavy end / 16 cm from the heavy end  
   **Answer:** 4 cm from the heavy end. *Why:* The pencil is cut into 4 + 1 = 5 equal parts, and the balance point is 1 part from the heavy end. 20 ÷ 5 = 4 cm. The middle, 10 cm, is only right for equal balls.
3. (truefalse) The Earth stays still while the Moon goes round it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both go round their balance point. The Earth's circle is small, about 4,700 km from its centre, because the Earth is 81 times as heavy.
4. (write) In The Towel Spin, the coin lay nearer the grown-up than the child. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The coin marked the middle of the turning. The grown-up was heavier, so the middle was nearer to the grown-up. The child, who was lighter, went round in the wider circle.. *Why:* A strong answer says both people went round one point. It says the heavier person sits nearer that point, and links this to the Earth and the Moon.
5. (mcq) In the marble model, the Earth is 1.6 cm wide. About how wide is the Moon?  
   Options: 1.6 cm / About 4 mm / About 1 mm / About 5 cm  
   **Answer:** About 4 mm. *Why:* The Moon is about a quarter as wide as the Earth. A quarter of 16 mm is about 4 mm, the size of a peppercorn.
6. (count) On a scale of 1 cm for 100 km, how many centimetres stand for 384,000 km, the Moon's distance?  
   **Answer:** 3840. *Why:* 384,000 ÷ 100 = 3,840 cm, which is 38.4 metres. On this scale the Earth is 1.27 metres wide.
7. (truefalse) A picture that draws the planets big and close together uses one scale for sizes and distances.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Real distances are far larger than real sizes. A picture that shows both close together has shrunk the distances far more than the sizes.
8. (mcq) A world is 10 times as wide as another and made of the same stuff. How much more material does it hold?  
   Options: 10 times / 30 times / 100 times / 1,000 times  
   **Answer:** 1,000 times. *Why:* It is 10 times as wide, as tall and as deep. 10 x 10 x 10 = 1,000.
9. (match) Match each world to about how many Earths would fit inside it.  
   **Answer:** The Sun → About 1.3 million; Jupiter → About 1,300; Saturn → About 760; Neptune → About 58. *Why:* The number of Earths is the cube of how many times as wide the world is. The Sun is 109 times as wide, and 109 x 109 x 109 is about 1.3 million.
10. (sort) Does each world go round the Sun, or go round a planet?  
   **Answer:** Ceres → Goes round the Sun; Ganymede → Goes round a planet; Mercury → Goes round the Sun; Phobos → Goes round a planet; Eris → Goes round the Sun; Titan → Goes round a planet. *Why:* What a body goes round decides its group. Ceres, Mercury and Eris go round the Sun. Ganymede, Phobos and Titan go round a planet.
11. (order) Put these in order, from the least mass to the most mass.  
   **Answer:** ['The Moon', 'The Earth', 'Jupiter', 'The Sun']. *Why:* The Earth has 81 times the Moon's mass. Jupiter has about 318 times the Earth's mass. The Sun has about 1,047 times Jupiter's mass.
12. (write) Ganymede is wider than Mercury. Yet Ganymede is a moon and Mercury is a planet. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** A body is named by what it goes round. Ganymede goes round Jupiter, so it is a moon. Mercury goes round the Sun, so it is a planet. Width is not a test.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the name depends on what the body goes round, and says that size is not the test.
13. (perspective) Where does the solar system end?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For the first: the wind is something that Voyager 1 could measure. For the second: the Sun's gravity still holds comets far beyond Voyager 1. For the third: planets are what most people mean by the solar system.. *Why:* There is no single agreed edge, because the answer depends on what you measure. A strong answer says what is being measured and treats the other views fairly.
14. (draw) Draw the Earth and the Moon on a scale of 1 cm for 40,000 km. Show their sizes and the gap.  
   **Answer:** The Earth is about 3 mm wide. The Moon is about 1 mm wide. They are about 9.6 cm apart.. *Why:* 12,742 ÷ 40,000 = 0.32 cm. 3,474 ÷ 40,000 = 0.09 cm. 384,400 ÷ 40,000 = 9.6 cm. A strong drawing shows a wide gap and two very small bodies.
15. (fill) The balance point of the Earth and the Moon lies inside the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['Earth']. *Why:* The point is about 4,700 km from the Earth's centre, and the Earth's radius is 6,371 km.
### Testing ideas about the solar system (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A new round icy world is found beyond Neptune, among thousands of icy bodies. What is it, by the 2006 tests?  
   Options: A planet / A dwarf planet / A moon / A star  
   **Answer:** A dwarf planet. *Why:* It goes round the Sun and is round, but it has not cleared its path. That is a dwarf planet.
2. (truefalse) Seven moons in the solar system are wider than Pluto.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. They are Ganymede, Titan, Callisto, Io, our Moon, Europa and Triton. Pluto is 2,377 km wide.
3. (truefalse) Two worlds with exactly the same mass have their balance point halfway between them.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each world is as far from the point as the other, so the point is halfway. The balance point only moves towards the heavier one.
4. (mcq) The Sun and Jupiter share a balance point just above the Sun's surface. What does this show?  
   Options: Jupiter is wider than the Sun / The Sun moves a little as Jupiter goes round / Jupiter does not go round the Sun / The Sun has no gravity  
   **Answer:** The Sun moves a little as Jupiter goes round. *Why:* The Sun goes round the balance point too, in a small circle. It does so once in about 12 years, as Jupiter goes round.
5. (count) In A Thousand Grains of Rice, the Sun has 998.6 grains and Jupiter 1. How many grains are left for everything else?  
   **Answer:** 0.4. *Why:* 1,000 − 998.6 − 1 = 0.4 grains. All the other planets, moons, asteroids and comets together are less than half a grain.
6. (count) A drawn Earth is 4 mm wide. The Sun is 11,700 Earth-widths away. On that scale, how many metres away is it?  
   **Answer:** 46.8. *Why:* 4 x 11,700 = 46,800 mm. 46,800 mm is 46.8 metres. A page could never show that.
7. (count) Uranus is about 4 times as wide as the Earth. Roughly how many Earths would fit inside it?  
   **Answer:** 64. *Why:* 4 x 4 x 4 = 64. The true figure is about 63, because Uranus is a little under 4 times as wide.
8. (write) In The Hidden Ball, the visible ball swung wider when the hidden ball was heavier. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The balance point sits nearer the heavier ball. When the hidden ball was heavy, the point moved towards it. That left the visible ball further from the point, so it went round in a wider circle.. *Why:* A strong answer says the point moves towards the heavier ball. It links the distance from the point to the size of the circle.
9. (perspective) Is the Moon a satellite of the Earth, or are they a double planet?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For the first: the balance point is about 4,700 km from the Earth's centre. For the second: the Moon's size and the Sun's pull on it. For the third: the numbers are agreed, and only the name is argued over.. *Why:* A strong answer uses a fact such as the balance point or the Sun's pull. Astronomers have never agreed an official meaning for 'double planet'.
10. (match) Match each world to why it is not a planet.  
   **Answer:** Pluto → It has not cleared its path; The Moon → It goes round a planet; Vesta → It is too small to be round; The Sun → It is a star. *Why:* Pluto fails the third test. The Moon fails the first, since it goes round the Earth. Vesta is too small to have pulled itself round. The Sun makes its own light.
11. (order) Put these in order, from nearest to furthest from the Sun.  
   **Answer:** ['Neptune', 'The end of the Kuiper belt', "The edge of the Sun's bubble", 'The Oort cloud']. *Why:* Neptune is 30 astronomical units out. The Kuiper belt reaches about 50. The Sun's bubble ends about 120 out. The Oort cloud may reach about 100,000.
12. (mcq) Nobody has seen the Oort cloud. How do astronomers know it is probably there?  
   Options: A telescope photographed it / Comets seem to come from far away in every direction / Voyager 1 flew through it / Moon rock holds pieces of it  
   **Answer:** Comets seem to come from far away in every direction. *Why:* Astronomers worked out where comets come from by studying their paths. Voyager 1 will need about 300 years to reach the Oort cloud.
13. (truefalse) A planet with a heavier moon wobbles more than one with a lighter moon, at the same distance.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A heavier moon pulls the balance point further from the planet's middle, so the planet goes round in a bigger circle. This is how astronomers find planets round other stars.
14. (write) Many people think the Sun stands still while the planets go round it. What would you tell them? Use a fact.  
   **Answer:** The Sun moves too. The Sun and Jupiter go round a balance point that lies just above the Sun's surface, once in about 12 years. Every planet pulls the Sun a little, so the Sun is never still.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the pull works both ways, and uses a fact such as the Sun and Jupiter's balance point. It is fair about the idea: the Sun moves very little.
15. (fill) A model is true only if it uses one ____ for sizes and distances.  
   **Answer:** ['scale']. *Why:* A scale turns every real length into a model length in the same way. A model with two scales can mislead.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which is the largest of the four rocky planets?  
   Options: Mercury / Venus / The Earth / Mars  
   **Answer:** The Earth. *Why:* The Earth is 12,742 km wide, a little wider than Venus at 12,104 km. Mars and Mercury are much smaller.
2. (mcq) Which of these is true of the Earth and no other planet?  
   Options: It has seas of liquid water on its surface / It has a moon / It has air / It goes round the Sun  
   **Answer:** It has seas of liquid water on its surface. *Why:* Other planets have moons, air and orbits. Only the Earth has liquid water on its surface, and it is the only world where we know living beings exist.
3. (mcq) Which of these bodies goes round a planet?  
   Options: Ceres / The Moon / Eris / Vesta  
   **Answer:** The Moon. *Why:* The Moon goes round the Earth. Ceres, Eris and Vesta all go round the Sun.
4. (mcq) Which of these is most likely to be lumpy?  
   Options: The Earth / The Moon / Phobos, 22 km wide / Ceres, 940 km wide  
   **Answer:** Phobos, 22 km wide. *Why:* A small body has weak gravity, so it cannot pull its bumps down. Phobos is shaped like a potato. The other three are round.
5. (mcq) Titan, a moon of Saturn, is 5,150 km wide. Mercury is 4,879 km wide. What can you say?  
   Options: Titan is a planet / A moon can be wider than a planet / Mercury must be a moon / Titan goes round the Sun  
   **Answer:** A moon can be wider than a planet. *Why:* Titan is wider than Mercury, but it goes round Saturn, so it is a moon. Choosing 'Titan is a planet' uses width as the test.
6. (mcq) Where is the balance point of the Earth and the Moon?  
   Options: Halfway between them / Inside the Earth, about 4,700 km from its centre / At the Moon's centre / Outside the Earth, near the Moon  
   **Answer:** Inside the Earth, about 4,700 km from its centre. *Why:* The Earth has 81 times the Moon's mass. 384,400 ÷ 82 is about 4,700 km. The Earth's radius is 6,371 km, so the point is inside it.
7. (mcq) A heavy ball and a light ball turn on a thread tied at their balance point. Which ball makes the smaller circle?  
   Options: The heavy ball / The light ball / Neither, because the heavy ball stays still  
   **Answer:** The heavy ball. *Why:* The heavy ball is nearer the balance point, so its circle is smaller. It still goes round, as it did in The Two-Ball Balance.
8. (mcq) With the Earth as a marble, the Sun is a ball as tall as a grown-up. About how far apart are they?  
   Options: About 2 metres / About 20 metres / About 190 metres / About 19 kilometres  
   **Answer:** About 190 metres. *Why:* The Sun is about 11,700 Earth-widths away. A marble 1.6 cm wide gives about 187 metres.
9. (mcq) A ball is twice as wide as another, made of the same dough. How much more dough does it hold?  
   Options: 2 times / 4 times / 8 times / 16 times  
   **Answer:** 8 times. *Why:* It is twice as wide, as tall and as deep. 2 x 2 x 2 = 8.
10. (mcq) How does the Sun's mass compare with the mass of all the planets together?  
   Options: About equal / About 10 times as much / More than 700 times as much / A little less  
   **Answer:** More than 700 times as much. *Why:* The Sun has about 333,000 Earth masses. All the planets together have about 450. So the Sun has more than 700 times as much.
11. (mcq) Pluto goes round the Sun and is round. Which test does it fail?  
   Options: It does not go round the Sun / It is not round / It has not cleared its path / It has no moon  
   **Answer:** It has not cleared its path. *Why:* Pluto shares its path with thousands of icy bodies. That is why it is a dwarf planet.
12. (mcq) Voyager 1 left the Sun's bubble of gas in 2012. Has it left the solar system?  
   Options: Yes, completely / It depends on how the edge is measured / No, it is still between Mars and Jupiter / Nobody can tell where it is  
   **Answer:** It depends on how the edge is measured. *Why:* By the Sun's wind, it has left. By the Sun's gravity, it is still deep inside. There is no single agreed edge.

## Applications
- Astronomers find planets round other stars by the small wobble that each planet causes in her star. Thousands of planets have been found this way and by other methods.
- Television, phone and weather satellites sit about 36,000 km above the Earth. At that height they go round once a day. So a rooftop dish can point at one spot in the sky for ever.
- India's Aditya-L1 spacecraft stays about 1.5 million km from the Earth, in a spot between the Earth and the Sun. The pulls of the Earth and the Sun are balanced there.
- Map makers, architects and model engineers all use one scale for every length in a model. A model with two scales would mislead people.
- A cook who needs a pot that holds 8 times as much rice buys a pot of the same shape that is only twice as wide. The same cube rule tells engineers how much a tank holds.
- Astronomers use the three tests of 2006 to sort each new round, icy world found beyond Neptune. This tells them how it fits into the solar system.
- The Moon's pull on the Earth lifts the tides. The Earth and the Moon pull on each other, and that is why both go round the balance point they share.

## Spotted around us
- A very bright, steady light low in the west after sunset, or low in the east before sunrise. It is usually Venus, which does not twinkle like a star.
- A TV dish on a roof. It always points at the same spot in the sky, where a satellite sits 36,000 km up.
- A bright dot that moves smoothly across the sky in a few minutes, with no blinking light. It may be the International Space Station.
- A cloud picture on the weather forecast on television. It was taken by a weather satellite going round the Earth.
- A planet next to the crescent Moon, on an evening when the two come close. A phone sky app tells you which planet it is.
- The Aryabhata satellite on the back of an old 2-rupee note, in a family collection or a museum.
- A planetarium show, with the planets and moons projected on a dome.
- Look at a ball of any size in a shop and ask how many smaller balls would fit inside it. If the small ball is half as wide, the answer is eight.

## Open questions
- **Is there a ninth planet far beyond Neptune?** Several icy bodies have paths that lean the same way, which an unseen planet could explain. Nobody has found one, and some astronomers think the pattern is chance.
- **How exactly did the Moon form?** The leading idea is a crash with a world the size of Mars. But Moon rock is very like Earth rock, which a simple crash does not explain, so the details are still argued over.
- **How did Triton, Neptune's big moon, come to go round Neptune backwards, against the way Neptune spins?** Some scientists think Neptune caught Triton from the Kuiper belt. Nobody has shown how a world that big could be caught and settle into a round path.
- **What is in the Oort cloud, and how far does it reach?** Nobody has seen it. Its size and its number of icy bodies are worked out from the paths of comets, so they could be wrong.
- **Why do other planetary systems often have planets between the sizes of the Earth and Neptune, when ours has none?** Many systems found so far have such planets close to their star. Astronomers do not yet know whether ours is unusual, or whether telescopes find those planets more easily.

## Questions to ask kids
- Which is bigger, the planet Mercury or Ganymede, the moon of Jupiter? Which is called a planet?
- If the Earth is a marble, how far away would the Sun be? How long would it take you to walk there?
- Who in our family would be nearer the balance point on a see-saw, the heavier person or the lighter one?
- How many small boxes would fit in a box twice as wide as the small box? Look at the cupboard and test it.
- Which things around us were made by people and go round the Earth? Which things go round it without help?
- Pluto was a planet when your grandparents were at school. What other things have changed their names or their groups since then?
- Why do you think the pictures of the planets in a book put them so close together?
- If we sent a message to a spacecraft past Neptune, would it be answered before dinner? Why?

## Connected maths
- In Marble Earth, Peppercorn Moon, 1 cm stands for 8,000 km. Mars is 6,779 km wide. How wide is a model Mars? **About 0.85 cm, or 8.5 millimetres.**. 6,779 divided by 8,000 is about 0.85. So a model Mars is a little over half as wide as the 1.6 cm marble Earth. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- In the made-up results of The Two-Ball Balance, 4 pieces against 1 gave parts of 14.1 cm and 3.9 cm. How many times as long is the long part? Is that close to 4? **About 3.6 times, a little under 4.**. 14.1 divided by 3.9 is about 3.6. The rule says 4. The pencil itself has weight too. Its weight is spread along its whole length, so it moves the balance point a little towards the middle of the pencil. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Twice as Wide, a dough ball is 12 cm wide. A small ball is 2 cm wide. How many small balls would fill it? **216 small balls.**. 12 divided by 2 is 6, so the big ball is 6 times as wide. 6 x 6 x 6 = 216. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In A Thousand Grains of Rice, Jupiter has 1 grain of the 1,000. What percentage of the solar system's mass is that? **0.1 percent.**. 1 divided by 1,000 is 0.001. As a percentage, that is 0.1 percent. So 99.9 percent of the mass is not Jupiter. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A book's picture shows the Earth 3 mm wide and the Sun 30 mm from it. The true distance is 11,700 Earth-widths. How many times too small is the drawn distance? **1,170 times too small.**. The true distance on the same scale is 3 x 11,700 = 35,100 mm. 35,100 divided by 30 is 1,170. So the picture has squeezed the gap 1,170 times. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- On the strip in An Honest Solar System, 1 astronomical unit is 3.5 cm. Saturn is 9.5 astronomical units from the Sun. How far along the strip is Saturn? **33.25 cm, about 33 cm.**. 9.5 x 3.5 = 33.25. So Saturn's dot goes about 33 cm from the Sun. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L5`)
- In The Hidden Ball, the visible ball has 3 pieces. The pencil is 20 cm long. The visible ball swings 10 cm wide. How many pieces are hidden? **1 piece.**. The visible ball swings in a circle 10 cm wide, so it is 5 cm from the thread. The pencil is 20 cm long, so the hidden ball is 20 - 5 = 15 cm from the thread. 15 is 3 times 5, so the visible ball is 3 times as heavy as the hidden ball. The visible ball has 3 pieces, so the hidden ball has 3 divided by 3, which is 1 piece. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What does a satellite go round? → A planet, or another world that is not a star.
- Can a moon be wider than a planet? → Yes. Ganymede and Titan are wider than Mercury.
- How many planets are there, and which is the furthest from the Sun? → Eight. Neptune is the furthest.
- What are the three tests for a planet? → It goes round the Sun, it is round, and it has cleared its path.
- Where is the balance point of the Earth and the Moon? → Inside the Earth, about 4,700 km from its centre.
- A ball is twice as wide as another. How many times as much does it hold? → Eight times.
- About how much of the solar system's mass is in the Sun? → About 99.86 percent.
- What is an astronomical unit? → The distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometres.
