# Rights and tax money: what India's laws promise every family
`sci.india.government-and-rights.L3`

**Summary.** Almost every bill from a shop or restaurant has a line called GST. That is tax, money paid to the government with every purchase. So the books, roads and street lights we call 'free' are paid for by all of us together. A right is something the law promises every person. India's laws promise every child aged 6 to 14 a free school place. They promise every citizen the right to ask how public money is spent. They promise rural families paid work when they need it. In this unit you hunt for the tax on your own family's bills, check a pretend work register for cheating, and find out where the things in your home were made.

**Intro.** *Pick up a school textbook or a printed bill. A government school gives that book free. A shop bill has a small line called GST. Is the free book really free? Who paid for it?* The book was paid for with public money. Public money is the money the government collects from people, mostly as tax. Some of it came from the GST on bills like yours. In this unit you find out how much tax your family pays when it shops. Then you learn three laws that come from this idea. Every child has a right to go to school. Every citizen has a right to know how public money is spent. Every rural family has a right to ask for paid work.

## Terms
- **Government**: The government is the group of people chosen to run the country, a state or a town for everyone. In India, people vote to choose them. *e.g.* The Tamil Nadu government runs the state's government schools and buses.
- **Law**: A law is a rule for the whole country or state. Everyone must follow it, including the government. A law made by Parliament is called an Act. *e.g.* The law says a car must stop at a red light.
- **Constitution**: The Constitution is India's most important law. It says how the country is run and what rights every person has. It came into force on 26 January 1950. *e.g.* We celebrate Republic Day on 26 January because the Constitution began that day.
- **Right**: A right is something every person is allowed to have or do, and the law protects it. Nobody has to earn it or pay for it. *e.g.* Every child aged 6 to 14 in India has the right to a free school place.
- **Tax**: Tax is money people must pay to the government. The government uses it to pay for things everyone shares, such as roads, schools and hospitals. *e.g.* When you buy a packet of biscuits, a little of the price is tax.
- **GST**: GST stands for Goods and Services Tax. It is the tax added to the price of most things we buy in India. A bill often shows it as CGST and SGST. Half goes to the central government and half to the state. *e.g.* A restaurant bill for a plate of dosa shows a few rupees of CGST and the same of SGST.
- **Public money**: Public money is money the government holds for everyone. Most of it comes from tax. It is spent on things that belong to all of us. *e.g.* The street light outside your building was paid for with public money.
- **Right to Education Act**: This law, passed in 2009, says every child aged 6 to 14 must get a free school place, up to Class 8. Schools may not test small children before giving them a seat. *e.g.* A 6-year-old in Madurai joins Class 1 at a government school without paying any fee.
- **Right to Information Act**: This law, passed in 2005, lets any citizen ask a government office for information. The office must answer within 30 days. People call it RTI. *e.g.* A family in Kochi asks the town office how much the repair of their road cost.
- **Public Information Officer**: The Public Information Officer is the person in each government office whose job is to answer RTI questions. *e.g.* A letter about the road repair goes to the Public Information Officer of the town office.
- **Rural job guarantee**: This is a law that promises paid work to village families who ask for it, on jobs like digging ponds. From 2006 it was called MGNREGA and promised 100 days a year. From 1 July 2026, a new law promises 125 days. *e.g.* In a village near Anantapur, women dig a farm pond and are paid by the day.
- **Job card**: A job card is a small book given free to a village family that wants work under the job guarantee. It records the days each person worked and the wages paid. *e.g.* Ratnamma's job card shows she worked 12 days on the pond in March.
- **Muster roll**: A muster roll is the attendance list at a work site. It shows who worked on which days, so they can be paid. *e.g.* The muster roll for the pond has 6 names and a tick for each day each person came.
- **Social audit**: A social audit is a meeting where the work records are read aloud in the village. The people who did the work say whether the records are true. *e.g.* At the social audit, a worker stands up and says, 'I worked 10 days, not 6.'
- **Transparency**: Transparency means being open, so anyone can see how decisions are made and money is spent. Glass is transparent because you can see through it. *e.g.* A board at a building site shows the work's cost and the date it will finish.
- **Globalisation**: Globalisation means the countries of the world becoming more connected through trade, travel, jobs and the internet. *e.g.* A phone with parts from many countries is put together in Chennai and sold in Kenya.
- **Import and export**: An import is something brought into India from another country to sell. An export is something made in India and sold to another country. *e.g.* India exports rice and medicines. India imports oil and many electronic parts.

## Explanations
### What a right is · *defined*
**Definition:** A right is something every person may have or do, which the law protects. A person does not have to earn it or pay for it.

India's Constitution lists Fundamental Rights, such as being equal before the law and speaking freely. Parliament can also make laws that give new rights. A right is different from a gift. A gift can be taken back when the giver changes their mind. A right stays, and if an office refuses it, a person can complain or go to court. Rights also come with work for someone. A child's right to school means the government must build schools and pay teachers.
- e.g. Every child aged 6 to 14 has the right to a free school place.
- e.g. Every citizen has the right to ask a government office for information.
- e.g. Every person has the right to be treated equally by the law, whatever their religion, caste or whether they are a girl or a boy.
### Tax and public money · *defined*
**Definition:** Tax is money people must pay to the government. Public money is what the government collects, mostly from tax, to spend on things everyone shares.

Most people never hand money straight to the government. They pay tax inside the price of what they buy. GST began in India on 1 July 2017. Since 22 September 2025, most goods carry GST of 5% or 18%. That means 5 or 18 rupees for every 100 rupees of price. Some things, such as fresh vegetables, carry none. People who earn more also pay income tax. So a family that only buys rice, soap and a bus ticket is still paying tax. This is why the government's money is really the people's money.
- e.g. A bill shows CGST 2.5% and SGST 2.5%. That adds up to 5 rupees of tax on every 100 rupees of price.
- e.g. The road, the drain, the bus stop and the government hospital are paid for with public money.
- e.g. A small kirana shop's handwritten slip often shows no GST. Very small shops do not have to collect it.
### The right to go to school · *defined*
**Definition:** Under the Right to Education Act, 2009, every child aged 6 to 14 has the right to free school up to Class 8, in a school near home.

For a long time, millions of children in India did not go to school. Many had to work, or had no school nearby. In 2002, the Constitution was changed to make free school a Fundamental Right for children aged 6 to 14. The Right to Education Act came into force on 1 April 2010. It says government schools must not charge fees. A school may not hold a test before giving a small child a seat, or ask for a donation. A child must not be turned away for having no birth certificate. A teacher must not hit or frighten a child. Private schools must keep a quarter of the seats in their first class for children from poor families, and the government pays for those seats.
- e.g. A school in Hyderabad asks a 5-year-old to sit an entry test. The law does not allow this.
- e.g. A private school in Mysuru keeps 25 of its 100 first-class seats for children from poor families.
- e.g. A girl whose family moved from Odisha to Chennai is given a seat though she has no birth certificate.
### Lunch at school · *defined*
**Definition:** Government schools in India give children a free cooked lunch on school days. The scheme is now called PM POSHAN.

A hungry child finds it hard to learn, and a free lunch brings many children to school. Madras, now Chennai, began giving lunch to poor school children about a hundred years ago. In the 1950s, the government of Madras State, led by K. Kamaraj, spread free lunches across the state. In 2001, India's Supreme Court ordered every government primary school in the country to serve a cooked lunch. Since 2022, Tamil Nadu also gives a free breakfast in its government primary schools.
- e.g. A Class 3 child in a government school in Madurai eats rice, sambar and an egg at lunch.
- e.g. Studies have found that more girls come to school when lunch is served.
### The right to know · *defined*
**Definition:** Under the Right to Information Act, 2005, any citizen may ask a government office for information, and the office must answer within 30 days.

The money a government office spends came from the people, so the people may ask how it was spent. The person asking does not need to give a reason. They write a short letter to the office's Public Information Officer, in English, Hindi or the state's language. The fee is 10 rupees for central government offices, and families below the poverty line pay nothing. States set their own fees. If the question is about someone's life or freedom, the answer must come within 48 hours. If an officer delays without good reason, they can be fined. Some things may be kept secret, such as information that would harm the country's safety or a person's private life. Tamil Nadu and Goa made state laws like this in 1997. The law for the whole country came into force on 12 October 2005.
- e.g. A mother in Chennai asks the city corporation when the broken street light outside her building will be fixed.
- e.g. Villagers ask how much money was spent on their school's new toilet, and who built it.
- e.g. A student asks the university for a copy of their own marked answer sheet.
### Paid work for village families · *defined*
**Definition:** A rural job guarantee is a law that promises paid manual work to any village family whose adults ask for it.

In many villages, there is little paid work in the months between harvests. Many people then leave for cities to look for jobs. India's National Rural Employment Guarantee Act was passed in 2005. Its first work began on 2 February 2006, in Bandlapalli village in Anantapur district, Andhra Pradesh. In 2009 it was renamed after Mahatma Gandhi and became MGNREGA. It promised every village household up to 100 days of paid work a year. A family asks the Gram Panchayat, the elected village council. They get a free job card. If no work is given within 15 days, the family must be paid an allowance. Women and men get the same wage, and wages go into a bank or post office account. A work site must have drinking water, shade and first aid. On 1 July 2026, a new law, the VB-G RAM G Act, replaced MGNREGA. It promises up to 125 days of work a year.
- e.g. Women in a Telangana village dig a farm pond, so rainwater stays for the dry months.
- e.g. A family in Kerala asks the Gram Panchayat for work and gets a dated receipt for their request.
- e.g. Workers build a small check dam across a stream, so the water soaks into the ground.
### Why open records stop cheating · *idea*
When records are kept shut in an office, a dishonest person can write a false name or false days, and nobody notices. When the records are read aloud to the people they are about, the false parts stand out. A worker knows how many days she worked. A villager knows whether a road was really built. That is why the job guarantee law requires a social audit, where the village checks the records. Andhra Pradesh was one of the first states to hold regular social audits of the job scheme, from 2006. Many studies have found that open records reduce cheating. They do not stop it completely, and researchers still argue about how much they help.
- e.g. A muster roll shows a man working 12 days, but he was in Hyderabad all month. The villagers know, and say so at the meeting.
- e.g. A board at a building site lists the cost of the work, so anyone passing can ask questions.
### Trade with the whole world · *debated*
A phone, a toy or a cooking oil may come from far away. Ships, aeroplanes and the internet have made trade between countries fast and cheap. This is a big part of globalisation. In 1991, India made new rules that opened it up much more to world trade. India now exports rice, medicines, cars, clothes and computer services. It imports oil, gold and many electronic parts. Many things are made in steps across many countries. A 'Made in' label names only where the thing was finished. People agree that trade has made many things cheaper. They argue about who gains and who loses.
- e.g. Tomatoes, potatoes and chillies first came to India from the Americas, about 500 years ago. Now they feel completely Indian.
- e.g. Many phones sold in India are now put together in factories near Chennai and in Noida, from parts made in many countries.
- e.g. A handloom weaver in Kanchipuram sells silk saris to buyers in other countries.
- **Many economists:** Since India opened up to trade in 1991, it has grown faster, and far fewer people live in deep poverty. Trade brings cheaper goods and new jobs in factories and offices.
- **Followers of Gandhi's swadeshi idea:** Gandhi asked Indians to use things made close to home, such as khadi cloth, because machine-made cloth from Britain had ruined many Indian weavers. People in this tradition say cheap imports still put local makers out of work, and shipping goods across the world burns a lot of fuel.
### Does the job guarantee work? · *debated*
The rural job guarantee was one of the biggest work schemes in the world. People who study it agree on some things. It gave many poor families money in the hardest months, and more than half its workdays went to women. They also agree that wages were often paid late, and some money was stolen with false names. They disagree about how much good it did overall, and about the new law of 2026.
- e.g. In a drought year, a family's job card wages pay for food until the rains come.
- e.g. A study in Andhra Pradesh found that paying wages with smart cards, checked by fingerprint, reduced stealing.
- **The economist Jean Drèze, who helped write the 2005 law:** The law gave poor families a right, not a favour. They could demand work and be paid. It stopped many people from going hungry in bad years.
- **Critics of the scheme:** A lot of money was wasted or stolen, and some of the work built little of lasting use. The money might help more if it were spent in other ways, such as on roads or training.
- **Critics of the new 2026 law:** The new law gives more days, but states must now pay a bigger share of the cost, and there is a fixed budget. They worry that poorer states will offer less work.

## Mostly agreed
- Almost everyone in India pays tax, because GST is added to the price of most things people buy. *How we know:* GST appears on the printed bills of shops, restaurants and online orders. You can check your own family's bills.
- Since 2010, every child in India aged 6 to 14 has had a legal right to free school up to Class 8. *How we know:* The Right to Education Act, 2009, and Article 21A of the Constitution, added in 2002.
- Any Indian citizen may ask a government office for information, and does not have to give a reason. *How we know:* The Right to Information Act, 2005. Lakhs of people use it every year.
- Reading work records aloud to the workers helps catch false entries. *How we know:* The public hearings of the MKSS in Rajasthan in the 1990s, and the regular social audits held in states such as Andhra Pradesh.

## How we found out
**The MKSS, started by Aruna Roy, Nikhil Dey, Shankar Singh and others, and the workers of central Rajasthan, 1990 (MKSS begins), 1994 and 1995 (the first public hearings), 2000 (Rajasthan's law), 2005 (the Right to Information Act).** In the early 1990s, workers in the villages of central Rajasthan were often paid less than the minimum wage on government work sites. Officials said the records showed everyone had been paid in full. Those records were kept in offices, and villagers were not allowed to see them. Most people believed that official records, signed and stamped, must be true. A group called the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan, or MKSS, set out to check. It had been started in 1990 by Aruna Roy, Nikhil Dey, Shankar Singh and others. In 1994 and 1995, they got copies of bills and muster rolls and read them aloud at village public hearings, called jan sunwai. Workers stood up and said, 'I never got this money.' Some names belonged to people who had died or moved away. Some works on paper had never been built. The records were not the truth. Their slogan was 'Hamara paisa, hamara hisaab': our money, our accounts. Their campaign helped bring Rajasthan's right to information law in 2000, and the Right to Information Act for all of India in 2005.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that signed and stamped government records must be true, and that ordinary people had no need to see them. When the records were read aloud to the workers, many entries turned out to be false.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Things the government gives, such as school books and lunches, are free.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: The family does not pay anything at the school gate.  
  Against: They are paid for with public money, and most of that comes from tax that almost every family pays when it shops. Nobody pays at the gate, but everyone has paid already.
- **Only rich people pay tax.** (A common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Rich people pay income tax, and poor people mostly do not.  
  Against: GST is in the price of most things anyone buys, so a poor family that buys soap or oil pays tax too.
- **The government's records must be true, because they are official.** (Many people, before the right to information campaigns; *rejected*)  
  For: Records are written by trained officers and signed and stamped.  
  Against: Public hearings in Rajasthan in the 1990s found records of payments to workers who were never paid, and of works that were never built.
- **Trade with other countries makes everyone better off.** (Some economists and leaders; *debated*)  
  For: Trade has made phones, clothes and many other things much cheaper, and India's exports have created many jobs.  
  Against: Some people lose their work when cheaper goods come from far away, such as weavers and toy makers. The gains are not shared equally.
- **Too many RTI questions slow the government down.** (Some officers and politicians; *debated*)  
  For: Answering lakhs of questions takes officers' time, and some questions are asked only to trouble an officer.  
  Against: Many offices now put information online before anyone asks, which saves time. Supporters say the questions catch waste worth far more than the time spent.

## Roots
We have found the tax on our own bills and learned the rights it pays for. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Nearly every bill you checked had GST on it, even small ones. So almost every family that shops pays tax, yours too. That tax pays for 'free' school books, roads and street lights. So the government spends money that belongs to all of us together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'government money' and call school books and lunches 'free', as if the money came from nowhere. But it was collected a few rupees at a time from almost every family's shopping. 'Free' only means everyone has already paid.  
*Evidence:* The Bill Detective: the GST lines on the family's own supermarket, restaurant and medical shop bills. Only loose fresh food or a tiny shop's handwritten slip had none.

- *who am i:* You are a taxpayer already. Every packet of biscuits you buy puts a few rupees into the shared purse.
- *what is life:* Life in a country is shared. Your road, your school and your lunch were paid for by families you will never meet, and you help pay for theirs.
- *what is this world:* A world where strangers pool their money to build things for everyone, and then have the right to ask how it was spent.

One family's GST on one bill is a few rupees. Crores of families shopping every day pour those rupees into one shared purse. That one purse then becomes many things: a pond in Anantapur, a lunch in Madurai, a street light in Kochi. Many small payments become one, and the one becomes many shared things again. That is why every one of those families has a right to see the accounts.

- **Thirukkural 520:** Let the ruler look into the work of his officers every day. When they do not go wrong, the world does not go wrong. (This couplet was written in Tamil about 2,000 years ago, and it asks for daily checking of the people who handle public work. A social audit and an RTI letter are ways for ordinary people to do that checking today.)
- **Ashoka, Rock Edict VI (about 256 BCE):** Ashoka ordered that reports on the people's affairs be brought to him at any hour, whether he was eating, resting or travelling, so that he could deal with them at once. (A ruler who wants to hear the people's problems at any time is close to the idea behind the Right to Information Act: the people's business should not be hidden or delayed.)
- **Mahatma Gandhi, 'Gandhi's talisman', a note from his last years:** When in doubt, recall the face of the poorest and weakest person you have seen, and ask whether the step you are about to take will be of any use to them. (When the shared pot can pay for only some things, this is one test for choosing. The rural job guarantee and free school lunches were built for the poorest families first.)
- Science (settled): GST has been added to the price of most goods and services in India since 1 July 2017, so almost every family that shops pays some tax.
- Science (debated): Studies, including one of smart-card wage payments in Andhra Pradesh, have found that open records and checks reduce cheating in public schemes. How much they help still differs from place to place.
- Other view, Some philosophers and economists, such as Robert Nozick: Tax is taken by law, not given freely, so it is not really 'shared' money at all. Some people go further, and say the government should take and spend much less, leaving families to choose for themselves what to pay for.
- Practice, The faces behind the road (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Think of the road outside our building. Now picture the people who paid for it. A fruit seller. A nurse. A grandmother buying soap. Picture the people who built it. Women carrying stones. A driver on a roller. Take three slow breaths. Now open your eyes. Next time you walk on that road, remember all those people are under your feet.
- Ask: If the road was paid for by everyone, whose road is it?
- Ask: You pay a little tax when you buy biscuits. Does that give you a say in how it is spent? How much of a say?
- Ask: Is a thing still free if everyone has already paid for it?

## Story: The Name on the List
*Social audits of the rural job guarantee are real: the Gram Sabha meets, a social audit team reads out the muster rolls, and workers say whether they are true. Andhra Pradesh held them regularly from 2006. Lakshmi, her family, Sujatha and this village are made up.*

Lakshmi was eight, and she lived in a village of red earth and groundnut fields near Anantapur, in Andhra Pradesh. She was in Class 3 at the government school. She liked school lunch, and she liked reading more than anything.

Her grandmother, Ammamma Ratnamma, could not read. When Ammamma was small, there was no school near the village, and girls were kept at home to fetch water. Now, every summer, Ammamma dug earth for the new farm pond with the other women of the village. The work was paid under the job guarantee. Ammamma kept her job card wrapped in a plastic cover, inside the rice tin, so the rats could not eat it.

Lakshmi's uncle, Ravi Mama, did not dig the pond. He had gone to Hyderabad in March to work as a mason on a tall building. Every Sunday evening he called on the phone. He told Lakshmi how many floors the building had grown that week.

One morning in May, a jeep stopped near the panchayat office. A woman got out with a bag full of papers. Her name was Sujatha, and she led the social audit team.

'What is a social audit?' Lakshmi asked her teacher.

'It is a checking meeting,' said her teacher, Mrs Fathima. 'The team has copied the records of the pond work. Tomorrow they will read them aloud to the whole village. The workers will say if they are true.'

'Why read them aloud?' asked Lakshmi. 'Can't the officers just check them in the office?'

'The officers can check that the sums add up,' said Mrs Fathima. 'But only the workers know if they really worked on those days.'

The next afternoon, the whole village sat under the big neem tree. Men sat on one side and women on the other, and children sat in front, in the dust. Sujatha stood with a long register, the muster roll. It listed every worker's name, with a tick for each day they came.

She read slowly, in Telugu. 'Ratnamma. Twelve days in April. Paid for twelve days.'

Ammamma frowned. She leaned over and whispered to Lakshmi. Lakshmi took the job card out of its plastic cover and counted the stamped days. 'Fifteen,' she whispered back. 'Ammamma worked fifteen days.'

Ammamma stood up. Her voice shook a little, because she had never spoken in a meeting before. 'I worked fifteen days, not twelve. My job card says so.'

Sujatha checked the card and wrote a note. 'Three days are missing,' she said. 'Ratnamma garu must be paid for three more days.' A few women clapped.

Sujatha read on, name after name. Then she read, 'Ravi. Twelve days in April. Paid for twelve days.'

Lakshmi sat up very straight. Ravi Mama had been in Hyderabad all of April. He had told her about the building every Sunday. He could not have dug the pond.

Her heart was thumping. Everyone would look at her. But it was true, and she knew it. She raised her hand.

'Ravi Mama was not here in April,' she said. 'He was working in Hyderabad. He called us every Sunday from there.'

The crowd went quiet. Then an old man said, 'That's right. Ravi left before Ugadi.' A woman said, 'I never saw him at the pond.'

Sujatha looked at the worksite helper, the mate, who kept the muster roll. He looked at his feet. At last he said that he had added some names of people who were away. The wages for those days had gone into an account he could use.

Sujatha wrote it all down. 'This will go to the officers,' she said. 'The money must be paid back.' Then she turned to Lakshmi. 'Thank you. The register looked perfect in the office. It took someone who knew the truth to find the mistake.'

That night, Ravi Mama called from Hyderabad. Lakshmi told him everything. He laughed, and then he went quiet.

'So someone used my name for money,' he said. 'And my niece caught them.'

Ammamma was rolling chapatis by the stove. 'Tomorrow,' she said, 'you will teach me to read the job card myself.'

Lakshmi got out her slate. She wrote a large R and a T, the first letters of Ratnamma in English. Ammamma copied them carefully, her tongue between her teeth.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the muster roll looked correct in the office, because its sums added up. The mistakes showed only when the record was read to the people it was about. Ammamma knew her own days, and Lakshmi knew where her uncle had been. This is why the job guarantee law requires social audits, and why the Right to Information Act matters: a record can be checked only by people who are allowed to see it. Lakshmi could help because she could read, which is what the Right to Education Act is for. And the wages, the school and the audit were all paid for with public money, from the tax that families like Lakshmi's pay.

- Ask: Why did the officers in the office not notice that Ravi's name was wrong?
- Ask: How did Lakshmi know that Ravi Mama was not at the pond?
- Ask: Ammamma's voice shook when she stood up. Why do you think it was hard for her to speak?
- Ask: How did Lakshmi being able to read help her family and her village?
- Ask: Who do you think should check the records of work done near your home?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bill Detective: how much of what we pay is tax? (30 min)
- **question:** Does every bill have tax on it? For every 100 rupees of price, how many rupees go to the government?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will every bill have tax on it, or only some? For every 100 rupees of price, how many rupees do you think are tax: 1, 5, 20 or 50? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Look at every bill in the same way: find the total, find each tax line, and find the rate printed beside it. Use at least 5 bills from different kinds of shop, because one bill could be unusual.
- **record:** Fill in the table for every bill. Circle the bill with the most tax for every 100 rupees, and the bill with none.
- **results table:** Bill | What was bought | Total paid (rupees) | Tax (rupees) | Tax rate printed | Rupees of tax in every 100 of price
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the bills on a table. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write the guess at the top of the page.  
  *Parent says:* These are bills from our shopping. Before we look closely, guess. Will every bill have tax on it? Out of every 100 rupees, how much is tax? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the bills. Guess: does every bill have tax? How much in every 100 rupees? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: Because the price already looks right, so tax must be small.; Because I saw GST on a bill before.; Any reason is good. We will test it.)
  - Note: Children often guess there is no tax at all, or that tax is half the price. Both are fine. Do not tell them the answer yet. Before you start, cover any card or phone numbers with a strip of paper.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take one bill. 2. Find the total and write it in the table. 3. Find the lines called CGST and SGST, or IGST, or GST. 4. Highlight them and add them up. 5. Do the same for every bill.  
  *Parent says:* Tax on a bill usually hides near the bottom. Look for the letters GST. If there are two lines, CGST and SGST, add them together.  
  *Child mode:* Find the total on each bill. Then hunt for GST near the bottom. Add CGST and SGST together. Write the tax.
  - Note: CGST goes to the central government and SGST to the state. They are usually equal. Some supermarket bills show a small tax table at the bottom, sorted by rate. Add up the tax column. A handwritten slip usually has no GST at all. That is a real result, so write 0.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look beside each tax line for a percent sign, such as 2.5% or 9%. 2. Add the two rates together. 3. Write it as rupees of tax in every 100 rupees of price.  
  *Parent says:* The rate tells you how many rupees of tax are added to every 100 rupees of price. 2.5% and 2.5% make 5 rupees in every 100.  
  *Child mode:* Find the numbers with a percent sign. Add the two together. That is tax in every 100 rupees.
  - Note: Percent means 'out of every hundred'. Your child does not need to calculate it, only to add the two printed rates. 9% and 9% make 18 rupees in every 100. If a bill has items at two rates, write both. Since September 2025, most things carry 5% or 18% in all.
  - Check: A bill shows CGST 9% and SGST 9%. How many rupees of tax are added to every 100 rupees of price? → **18 rupees**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the bills in a line from the most tax in every 100 to the least. 2. Find any bill with no tax. 3. Talk about why.  
  *Parent says:* Line up the bills from the most tax to the least. Which bill had none? Why might that be?  
  *Child mode:* Line up the bills. Most tax first. Which bill had no tax? Why?
  - Ask: Why might the vegetable seller's slip have no tax? (good: Fresh vegetables carry no GST.; Very small shops don't have to collect it.)
  - Ask: Which bills had the most tax? (good: Things like plastic goods, electronics or paint often carry 18%.; Food and medicines mostly carry 5%.)
  - Note: Most food and medicine bills show 5% in all. Many other goods show 18%. Loose fresh vegetables, fruit and loose grain carry none. Very small shops do not have to collect GST.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to say the claim, the evidence and the reason. 2. Then ask where the tax goes.  
  *Parent says:* So what did you find? Say what you think, what you saw on the bills, and why. Then tell me: where does all this tax money go?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say which bills showed it. Where does the tax money go?
  - Ask: Who paid tax this week in our family? (good: We all did, when we bought things.; Anyone who went shopping.)
  - Ask: Where does the tax money go? (good: To the government.; It pays for roads, schools, hospitals and street lights.)
  - Think: If your family's money helped pay for the road, should you be allowed to ask how it was spent?

## Open questions
- **How much do social audits and open records really reduce cheating?** Many studies find they help, but they are hard to measure, because hidden cheating is hidden. Results differ from state to state.
- **Will the new 2026 rural job law give poor families more work, or less?** It promises 125 days instead of 100, but states must now pay a bigger share and there is a fixed budget. It is too new to know.
- **Who gains most from trade with the whole world, and who loses?** Economists agree trade made many goods cheaper. They still argue about its effects on small makers, jobs and the environment.
- **What is the fairest way to collect tax?** GST charges everyone the same rate, so it takes a bigger share of a poor family's money. People disagree about how to balance this with income tax.
- **How can a right be made real where there are too few teachers, doctors or officers?** A law can promise a right, but it needs people and money to keep it. Nobody has found a perfect way.

- In the Bill Detective, a packet of biscuits and some oil cost 400 rupees before tax. The tax was 5 rupees for every 100 rupees. How much tax was added, and what was the total? **20 rupees of tax. The total was 420 rupees.**. 400 is four hundreds. Each hundred has 5 rupees of tax, so 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20 rupees. The total is 400 + 20 = 420 rupees. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L3`)
- Under the new rural job law, a village family can get up to 125 days of work a year. Suppose the wage is 300 rupees a day. How much could the family earn in a year? **37,500 rupees.**. Split 125 into 100 and 25. 100 days at 300 rupees is 30,000 rupees. 25 days at 300 rupees is 7,500 rupees. 30,000 + 7,500 = 37,500 rupees. Real wages are set for each state every year, so 300 is a practice number. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Made Where survey, Kavya found 12 things made in India, 5 in China, 1 in Vietnam and 2 with no label. How many more things were made in India than in all other countries together? **6 more.**. Other countries: 5 + 1 = 6. India: 12. 12 - 6 = 6. So 6 more were made in India. The 2 with no label are not counted, because we don't know where they were made. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Selvi posts an RTI letter on 5 March. The office must reply within 30 days. By what date must the answer come? **4 April.**. March has 31 days. From 5 March to 31 March is 26 days. 4 more days reach 4 April. 26 + 4 = 30. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- In A Week of Bills, one family paid about 300 rupees of GST in a week. Their building has 40 families. If each paid about the same, how much tax would the building pay in a week? **About 12,000 rupees.**. 40 families times 300 rupees. 4 times 3 is 12, so 40 times 300 is 12,000. That is about 12,000 rupees in one week, from one building. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
