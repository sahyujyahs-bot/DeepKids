# Hearing: how two ears find where a sound is, and why noise can hide a voice
`sci.sound.hearing.L4`

**Summary.** Sit with your eyes shut while someone clinks a spoon on a katori somewhere around you. You can point straight at it. Now cover one ear and point again, and your finger goes wrong. The sound has not changed. Each ear gets only a loudness and a moment, and your brain builds the place from the difference between the two. Your child tests this, then finds out why a running tap can hide a whisper that is still reaching her ears, how a decibel number grows, and how the inner ear sorts high notes from low ones along a coiled tube no longer than a thumb. The unit goes on to hearing aids, cochlear implants and sign language, and to ears on a cricket's knees. A Hungarian scientist, Georg von Békésy, showed in the 1940s that the ear sorts pitches with a travelling wave, and not with strings as the great Helmholtz had guessed.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes. A friend claps somewhere in the room. Without peeking, point to where the clap came from. Now press one ear shut with a warm cap and try again. Which time do you point better, and why?* With two ears open, you point well. With one ear covered, you miss by a lot. A sound is just a wave in the air, and it carries no label that says where it came from. Your brain works out the place by comparing what the two ears hear. In this unit you test that yourself. You also find out why a loud sound can hide a quiet one, how the inner ear sorts high and low notes, how hearing aids and signing help people who hear less, and how other animals hear with their knees, their antennae and their jaws.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain how two ears tell where a sound is, using the tiny differences in time and loudness between them.
1. I can test how well I find where a sound is with two ears and with one ear covered, and explain what I find.
2. I can read a decibel number, say how the scale grows, and use it to judge how long a sound is safe to listen to.
3. I can explain why a loud sound can hide a quiet one, and what a speaker can do to be heard in noise.
4. I can describe how the cochlea sorts high and low pitches along its length, and how hair cells send signals to the brain.
5. I can say how hearing aids and cochlear implants help, and how people who cannot hear communicate and enjoy music.
6. I can tell how Georg von Békésy found a travelling wave in the ear, and give examples of ears in unusual places.

## Terms
- **Sound direction**: Where a sound comes from. Scientists call finding it sound localisation. *e.g.* When a cuckoo calls in a tree, you turn your head to the right tree without thinking. That is your sense of sound direction.
- **Time difference**: The tiny gap between the moment a sound reaches the nearer ear and the moment it reaches the farther ear. *e.g.* A clap on your right reaches your right ear about six ten-thousandths of a second before your left ear. That is much too short to notice, yet your brain uses it.
- **Head shadow**: The way your head blocks some sound from reaching the far ear, so the far ear hears it a little softer. *e.g.* A whistle on your left sounds a little softer in your right ear, because your head is in the way.
- **Pinna**: The part of the ear you can see on the side of your head, the ear flap. Its folds change sounds a little depending on the direction they come from. *e.g.* Folded forwards, your pinna lets in more sound from the front. Cupped behind with a hand, it catches more sound from the back.
- **Decibel**: The unit for how strong a sound is. The short way to write it is dB. Zero decibels is about the faintest sound a young person can hear. *e.g.* A whisper is about 30 decibels. A normal conversation is about 60. A motorbike passing close is about 95.
- **Masking**: When a loud sound hides a quieter one, so that you cannot hear the quiet one even though it is still reaching your ear. *e.g.* You cannot hear a friend whisper while the water tap is running hard.
- **Cocktail party effect**: Your ability to pick out one voice in a noisy crowd by listening only to it. It was named after a party, but it happens in any crowd. *e.g.* At a wedding hall full of talk, you can still follow your cousin's voice, and you notice if someone across the hall says your name.
- **Basilar membrane**: A thin strip inside the cochlea that moves when sound arrives. It is stiff and narrow at the start of the cochlea and soft and wide at the far end. *e.g.* Unrolled, the basilar membrane is about as long as the width of your thumb.
- **Travelling wave**: A wave that moves along the basilar membrane when sound arrives. It grows to a peak at one place and then dies away. The place of the peak depends on the pitch. *e.g.* A high note makes the wave peak near the start of the cochlea. A low note makes it peak near the far end.
- **Outer hair cells**: About 12,000 tiny cells in each cochlea that make the wave on the basilar membrane bigger and sharper, so that we can hear very quiet sounds. *e.g.* When outer hair cells are damaged by loud noise, quiet sounds and sounds close in pitch become hard to tell apart.
- **Hearing threshold**: The quietest sound a person can just hear. It is different for each pitch. *e.g.* You find your threshold at a pitch by turning a sound down until you can only just hear it.
- **Audiogram**: A chart of a person's hearing threshold at different pitches. Each ear has its own line. *e.g.* An audiogram of a child with normal hearing is a line near the top of the chart. A line that drops at the right end means high sounds are hard to hear.
- **Hearing aid**: A small device worn in or behind the ear that makes sounds louder, using a microphone, an amplifier and a tiny speaker. *e.g.* A grandfather who hears speech faintly can wear a hearing aid that makes the voices louder.
- **Cochlear implant**: A device that is placed in the ear by an operation. It sends signals from a microphone straight to the hearing nerve through about twenty tiny wires, instead of using the hair cells. *e.g.* A child who was born with no working hair cells can hear speech with a cochlear implant.
- **Indian Sign Language**: A full language of hand shapes, movements and faces, used by Deaf people across India. It has its own words and its own grammar, different from spoken Hindi or English. *e.g.* Two Deaf friends can chat for an hour in Indian Sign Language without any sound.
- **Tinnitus**: A ringing, buzzing or hissing that a person hears when there is no sound outside. *e.g.* After a very loud concert, many people hear a ringing in their ears for a few hours.
- **Balance canals**: Three small fluid-filled loops in the inner ear, one for each way the head can turn. They tell the brain when your head moves. *e.g.* When you spin around and stop, the fluid keeps moving for a moment, and the world seems to spin.
- **Ear stones**: Tiny stones made of chalk that sit on hair cells in the ears of fish and in the balance part of our ears. They help sense sound, gravity and movement. *e.g.* A rohu fish has ear stones inside her head and she can tell which way is down.

## Explanations
### Two ears tell where a sound is · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound from one side reaches the nearer ear a tiny moment earlier and a little louder. The brain compares the two ears and works out the direction.

Your ears are about 20 centimetres apart. Sound in air travels about 340 metres each second. So a clap right beside your right ear needs about six ten-thousandths of a second longer to reach your left ear. You do not notice that moment. But the brain can notice a difference as small as about one hundred-thousandth of a second, which tells it the direction to within a few degrees. Your head also blocks some sound, so the far ear hears a high sound a little softer. The brain combines the two clues. For low sounds the timing clue works best. For high sounds the loudness clue works best. A sound straight in front or straight behind reaches both ears together and equally loudly, so these two places are easy to mix up. With one ear covered, both clues vanish, and the brain has to guess.
- e.g. A clap on your right reaches your right ear first and is a little louder there.
- e.g. A sound straight ahead reaches both ears at the same time and equally loudly.
- e.g. With one ear blocked, you tend to point towards the open ear, because the sound seems louder there.
### The ear flap, head turns and the front-back puzzle · *defined*
**Definition:** The folds of the pinna change a sound a little depending on its direction, and turning the head changes what each ear hears. Both help the brain tell front from back and up from down.

A sound straight in front and a sound straight behind reach both ears at the same time. So time and loudness do not tell them apart. The folds of the pinna help, because sound from the front is shaped in a slightly different way from sound from behind. The brain learns these shapes over many years. In experiments, people who wore small moulds that changed the folds of their pinnae were at first confused about up and down, and then learned again over some weeks. The easiest fix is to turn your head. Turn your head left. A sound in front moves to your right ear. A sound behind moves to your left ear. Animals do this all the time. A cat swivels her ears to listen. Dogs and horses turn their pinnae separately. A barn owl has ears at different heights, so she can tell whether a mouse is above or below her.
- e.g. A sound straight ahead moves to your right ear when you turn your head to the left.
- e.g. A cat turns her ears towards a noise to hear the noise better.
- e.g. A barn owl has one ear opening higher than the other, so she can tell up from down.
### The decibel scale · *defined*
**Definition:** The decibel scale measures how strong a sound is. Every 10 decibels more means 10 times as much sound energy, and a sound that seems about twice as loud.

A whisper is about 30 decibels. A conversation is about 60. That is 30 decibels more, which is three steps of 10. Each step multiplies the sound energy by 10, so a conversation has 10 times 10 times 10, or 1,000 times, the energy of a whisper. Yet it only sounds about 8 times as loud to us, because our ears squash a very big range into a smaller one. That is why the scale is useful. Our ears can handle sounds from the faintest to a jet plane close by, which differ in energy by a factor of about a trillion. The decibel numbers for these are only 0 to about 130. Common sounds: leaves rustling about 20, a quiet room 35, a refrigerator 40, a busy street 80, a mixer-grinder 85 to 90, a motorbike passing close 95, a car horn close by 110, a firecracker close by 140.
- e.g. A conversation at 60 decibels has 1,000 times the sound energy of a whisper at 30 decibels.
- e.g. A busy street at 80 decibels sounds about 4 times as loud as a conversation at 60.
- e.g. Sounds above 120 decibels can hurt at once, because they are far above the loudest comfortable level.
### How long is a sound safe? · *defined*
**Definition:** The louder a sound, the shorter the time it is safe to listen. Health agencies say that about 85 decibels is safe for about 8 hours, and that every 3 decibels more means half the safe time.

The hair cells in your cochlea can be damaged by loud sound, and in people they do not grow back. Damage depends on both the loudness and the time. At 85 decibels, such as a busy road or a mixer-grinder, the safe time is about 8 hours. At 88 decibels it is about 4 hours. At 91 it is about 2 hours, at 94 about 1 hour, at 97 about 30 minutes and at 100 decibels about 15 minutes. At 110 decibels, such as a loud speaker at a wedding, harm can start in a minute or two. Indian rules for noise say that in homes and residential areas, sound should be no more than 55 decibels by day and 45 by night, and that a firecracker should not be louder than about 125 decibels when measured 4 metres away. Earplugs or moving away reduce the level a lot. Most hearing damage from noise has no pain at the time, which is why we must go by numbers. Never push cotton buds, pins or anything else into the ear. The ear cleans itself, and a push can hurt the eardrum.
- e.g. At 85 decibels, about 8 hours is the safe limit.
- e.g. At 100 decibels, such as a drill or a motorbike near the ear, only about 15 minutes is safe.
- e.g. Each extra 3 decibels halves the safe time, so 97 decibels gives half the time of 94.
### Why a sound gets fainter as you move away · *defined*
**Definition:** Sound spreads out in every direction, so the same energy covers a bigger and bigger area as you move away. In open air, each time you double the distance, the sound level falls by about 6 decibels.

A ring of ripples from a marble gets lower as it spreads, because its energy is shared along a longer ring. Sound in the air spreads over the surface of a growing ball. Doubling the distance makes the ball four times bigger in area, so each bit gets a quarter of the energy. A quarter of the energy is about 6 decibels less. So if a fan reads 60 decibels at 1 metre, it reads about 54 at 2 metres, 48 at 4 metres and 42 at 8 metres in the open. Inside a room, the walls bounce some sound back, so the drop is usually smaller, about 3 to 5 decibels for each doubling. This is why the first step away from a loud speaker helps a lot, and why a whisper only reaches a few metres.
- e.g. A fan that reads 60 decibels at 1 metre reads about 54 decibels at 2 metres in the open.
- e.g. Moving from 1 metre to 8 metres away from a loudspeaker lowers the level by about 18 decibels in the open.
- e.g. A whisper that is clear at 1 metre can be lost at 5 metres, because it has spread out and is smaller than the noise of the room.
### Masking: why noise hides a voice · *defined*
**Definition:** Masking happens when a louder sound makes a quieter sound impossible to hear, even though the quieter sound still reaches the ear. Sounds close in pitch to the noise are masked the most.

In the Hiding a Whisper test, the whisper was still reaching your ears when the tap was running, but you did not hear it. The tap's sound moves the same part of the basilar membrane as the whisper does, and the whisper is smaller than the tap's wave there, so it is lost. A sound that is far from the noise in pitch is masked less. This is why a thin, high whistle is easier to find in a crowd of drums. It also helps to be close, since each halving of the distance adds a lot to the level, and to watch the speaker's mouth, because seeing lips helps the brain fill in what the ear misses. The brain can also choose what to listen to. In a crowded hall you can follow one voice and ignore the rest. This is called the cocktail party effect, and it needs both ears, because the brain uses the direction to separate the voices.
- e.g. You cannot hear a whisper while the tap runs full, though the whisper is still there.
- e.g. A thin whistle is easier to hear over drums than a low voice, since its pitch is far from the drums.
- e.g. In a crowded hall you can follow one friend's voice by listening only to it.
### The cochlea sorts high and low pitches along its length · *defined*
**Definition:** When sound enters the cochlea, it makes a wave travel along the basilar membrane. The wave peaks at a place that depends on the pitch. High pitches peak near the start. Low pitches peak near the far end.

The cochlea is a coiled tube about 3.5 centimetres long when unrolled, filled with fluid. Inside runs the basilar membrane. At the start it is narrow and stiff. At the far end it is about five times wider and much softer. A stiff, narrow part moves best for high sounds. A soft, wide part moves best for low sounds. So a sound of 10,000 hertz makes the wave peak near the start, and a sound of 100 hertz makes it peak near the far end. Along the membrane there are about 15,000 hair cells in each ear. Each hair cell sits at one place, so each one tells the brain about one pitch. About 30,000 nerve fibres carry the messages to the brain. The brain reads which hair cells are signalling, and so it knows the pitch. A sound with many pitches, such as a voice, makes many places move at once.
- e.g. A high whistle peaks near the start of the cochlea.
- e.g. A low drum peaks near the far end of the cochlea.
- e.g. A sound with many pitches, like a voice, makes many places on the membrane move at once.
### Why the middle ear is needed: air to water · *defined*
**Definition:** Sound moving from air into a liquid mostly bounces back. The eardrum and three tiny bones collect the sound over a big area and push it into the cochlea's fluid over a small area, so that much more of the sound gets in.

Call to a friend swimming underwater. Almost all your voice bounces off the surface of the water and she can hardly hear you. Only about one-thousandth of the sound energy goes from air into water. Your cochlea is full of fluid, so it has the same problem. The middle ear is the answer. The eardrum is about twenty times bigger in area than the small window it pushes on, so the push is made much stronger. The three small bones also work as a lever. Together they let most of the sound in. The smallest bone, the stirrup, is about 3 millimetres long, the smallest bone in the body. When the middle ear is blocked by a cold or fluid, sounds seem muffled because less gets through.
- e.g. A voice calling from above hardly reaches someone underwater, because most of the sound bounces off the surface.
- e.g. A cold that blocks the middle ear makes everything seem muffled.
- e.g. The stirrup, a bone about 3 millimetres long, is the smallest bone in the body.
### The ear makes sound too · *defined*
**Definition:** The outer hair cells push on the basilar membrane and make the wave bigger and sharper. This push is so strong that the ear itself gives off faint sounds, which doctors can record.

In 1978 a British scientist, David Kemp, found that a healthy ear, in a quiet room, can send out a tiny sound of its own. These sounds are called otoacoustic emissions. They come from the outer hair cells, which keep pushing the membrane at the pitch of the sound and so boost it. Without the boost, quiet sounds would be much harder to hear. Doctors now use a tiny microphone in the ear to record these emissions and check a newborn baby's hearing in a minute or two. The test does not need the baby to do anything. If the ear is healthy, the sound comes out. If the outer hair cells are damaged, it does not.
- e.g. A healthy ear sends out a faint sound that a tiny microphone can record.
- e.g. Hospitals use this test to check a newborn baby's hearing, without the baby doing anything.
- e.g. A damaged cochlea gives no emission, and so the test shows a problem.
### When hearing is lost, and what can help · *defined*
**Definition:** Hearing loss happens when sound cannot get through the outer or middle ear, or when the hair cells and nerve cannot send the signal on. Hearing aids make sound louder. Cochlear implants send signals straight to the nerve.

The World Health Organization estimates that about 1.5 billion people, about one in five, live with some hearing loss. Some loss comes from a blocked ear or fluid in the middle ear, which can often be treated. Other loss comes from damaged hair cells, from loud noise, illness, some medicines or growing old. A hearing aid has a microphone, a small amplifier and a tiny speaker, and it makes sound louder, so that the hair cells that still work get a bigger signal. If almost all the hair cells have been lost, a cochlear implant can help. A tiny microphone behind the ear sends signals to about twenty wires coiled inside the cochlea. Each wire excites the nerve at its own place, and so sounds the way the cochlea would sort pitches. It sounds different from normal hearing, but many people, including children who were born deaf, learn to understand speech with it. Early tests and early help matter most for small children, who are learning to speak.
- e.g. A hearing aid makes sounds louder for a grandparent whose hair cells have worn out with age.
- e.g. A cochlear implant can help a child who was born with no working hair cells.
- e.g. A blocked ear from a cold can be treated, and hearing returns.
### Living without hearing: sign language and feeling music · *defined*
**Definition:** Sign languages are full languages made of hand shapes, movements and facial expressions. Many Deaf people also feel music through their bodies.

Sign languages are not spoken language shown by hand. Linguists have shown since the 1960s that they have their own grammar and can say anything a spoken language can. India has its own, Indian Sign Language. The Indian Sign Language Research and Training Centre in New Delhi, set up in 2015, works to make it widely used and taught. Deaf people also enjoy music. Evelyn Glennie, a Scottish percussionist, lost most of her hearing by about the age of twelve. She plays barefoot to feel the vibration of the floor and the drums through her feet, legs and hands, and she has played in concert halls all over the world. When you talk with someone who hears less, face them, keep your mouth visible, speak clearly without shouting, reduce background noise, and be patient.
- e.g. Indian Sign Language has its own words and grammar, and is not the same as spoken Hindi or English.
- e.g. Evelyn Glennie plays percussion barefoot so that she can feel the vibrations of the music.
- e.g. To talk with a person who hears less, face them and cut down the background noise.
### Ears in unusual places · *defined*
**Definition:** Other animals have ears of different shapes in different places. All of them turn a vibration into a signal for the animal's brain.

A grasshopper has her ears on the sides of her belly, each covered with a thin eardrum. A cricket or a katydid has her ears on her front legs, just below the knees. A male mosquito hears with his feathery antennae, and uses them to follow the wingbeat of a female, which is a sound of about 400 to 500 beats each second. A fish has no ear flap, but she has ear stones inside her head, and a line of sensors along her sides that feel water waves. A moth can hear a hunting bat and dive to escape. A barn owl has ear openings at different heights on her head, and she finds a mouse in total darkness. In every case a thin skin or a hair is moved by sound, and the movement is changed into signals. Ears were invented many times in the history of life.
- e.g. A cricket hears with ears below the knees on her front legs.
- e.g. A male mosquito follows the wingbeat of a female with his antennae.
- e.g. A moth hears a hunting bat's call and dives to escape.
### The ear also senses balance · *defined*
**Definition:** Next to the cochlea, three fluid-filled loops and two small stone-covered patches sense movement of the head and which way is down. They are the balance part of the ear.

When your head turns, fluid in the loops lags behind a little and bends hair cells, just as sound bends the hair cells in the cochlea. The three loops are at right angles, so they can sense turns in every direction. The two patches have tiny stones on top of hair cells, and the stones are pulled by gravity, so they tell you which way is down and when you speed up or slow down. When you spin round and stop, the fluid keeps moving for a few moments and your brain thinks you are still spinning. That is dizziness. Balance also needs your eyes and the feel of your feet. When you close your eyes, you have to rely more on your ears and feet, which is why standing on one leg gets harder.
- e.g. After spinning around and stopping, the fluid in the loops keeps moving, so you feel dizzy.
- e.g. Standing on one foot is harder with your eyes shut.
- e.g. Tiny stones on hair cells tell you which way is down, even in the dark.
### What is tinnitus, and where does it come from? · *debated*
Many people hear a ringing, hissing or buzzing in a quiet room, with no sound outside. It often starts after loud noise or with hearing loss. Scientists agree that it is very common, but they do not agree on where it comes from. Some think it is made by damaged hair cells sending faulty signals. Others think the brain makes it, when it has less sound to listen to and turns up its own volume, so it begins to hear noise it makes itself.
- e.g. After a very loud concert, many people hear ringing for a few hours.
- e.g. In a very quiet room, many people with healthy hearing notice a faint hiss.
- **Some ear scientists:** The ringing starts in the damaged ear, where faulty hair cells send false signals to the brain.
- **Many brain scientists:** The ringing is made in the brain. When the ear sends less signal, the brain turns up its own volume, and then it hears its own noise.
### Is a sound in the air, or in the ear? · *debated*
A wave of squeezes and spreads moves through the air when a drum is struck. That is certain. But is that wave the sound, or is the sound what a listener hears? The first idea says a sound exists with no one there. The second says it needs a listener, because a wave has no loudness or pitch of its own, only an amount of energy and a rate, and loudness and pitch are what we hear. Each person is right about some part of the idea. The disagreement is about the word.
- e.g. A drum struck in an empty room makes a wave in the air.
- e.g. A loudness or a pitch is something a listener has. A wave has an amplitude and a frequency.
- **Many physicists:** A sound is the pressure wave in the air. It exists whether anyone is there or not. Hearing is what an ear and brain do with it.
- **Many philosophers of perception:** A sound is what a listener hears. A wave has only energy and rate. Loudness, pitch and place are made by the ear and brain.

## Mostly agreed
- Animals with two ears use the tiny differences in time and loudness between the ears to find where a sound is. *How we know:* People with one ear blocked or deaf in one ear point to sounds badly. Experiments with headphones that delay a sound in one ear by a tiny fraction of a second move where the sound seems to be.
- A loud sound can permanently damage the hair cells of the cochlea, and in people these cells do not grow back. *How we know:* Hearing tests of workers in noisy factories show lasting loss. Looking at the cochlea under a microscope after loud noise shows missing hair cells.
- Every 10 decibels more is 10 times the sound energy. *How we know:* It follows from how the decibel scale is defined, and sound meters follow it.
- A sound makes a travelling wave along the basilar membrane, and the place of its peak depends on the pitch. *How we know:* Georg von Békésy saw it under a microscope in the 1940s. Later measurements in living animals confirmed it and showed that the outer hair cells sharpen the peak.
- A loud sound can hide a quieter one that is close to it in pitch. *How we know:* Hearing tests played to thousands of people show that a quiet sound near a loud sound in pitch cannot be heard.
- Sign languages are full languages with their own grammar. *How we know:* Linguists have studied them since the 1960s and shown that children learn them in the same stages as spoken languages, and that they can say anything.

## How we found out
**Georg von Békésy (1899 to 1972), a Hungarian scientist, 1863 (Helmholtz's idea), the 1940s (Békésy's work), 1961 (the Nobel Prize).** In 1863 the great German scientist Hermann von Helmholtz suggested that the inner ear works like a piano. He thought stretched fibres lay across the cochlea, short ones at one end and long ones at the other, and that each shook for one pitch like a string. It was a clever idea, and part of it was right: different places do answer to different pitches. In the 1920s and 1940s, a Hungarian scientist named Georg von Békésy looked at real ears. He studied ears from people who had died, because living ears are too small to look into. Under a microscope, he found no stretched strings. The membrane was soft. When he played a sound, he saw a wave travel along the membrane and rise to a peak at one place. High sounds peaked near the entrance, and low sounds near the far end. So the ear sorts pitches by where a wave peaks, not by strings. Békésy won the Nobel Prize in 1961.  
*What people believed before:* Helmholtz thought the cochlea had stretched fibres like piano strings, each shaking for one pitch. Békésy showed there were no stretched strings. A wave travels along a soft membrane and peaks at a place that depends on the pitch.

## Challenging perspectives
- **We find where a sound is only from how loud it is in each ear.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: A sound on your right is louder in your right ear, and that does help.  
  Against: Experiments with headphones show that a tiny delay between the ears, with no change in loudness, also moves where a sound seems to be, especially for low sounds. The brain uses both clues.
- **Blind people sense walls and doors through the skin of their faces.** (Many people in the 1800s and early 1900s, who called it facial vision; *rejected*)  
  For: Blind people can walk along a corridor without touching walls, and some say they feel the wall on their face.  
  Against: In 1944 Supa, Cotzin and Dallenbach tested blind and sighted people who walked towards a wall. When their ears were plugged, they bumped into it, even though the face was uncovered. When their faces were covered but their ears were free, they still stopped. They were hearing the echo of their own footsteps.
- **Each pitch has its own stretched fibre in the cochlea, like a string on a piano.** (Hermann von Helmholtz, a great German scientist, in 1863; *rejected*)  
  For: Different places along the membrane do respond to different pitches, as the idea says.  
  Against: In the 1940s Georg von Békésy looked at real cochleas and found no stretched strings. The membrane is soft, and sound makes a wave that travels along it and peaks at a place. The place idea was right. The strings were not.
- **Sign language is just gestures for spoken words, and not a real language.** (Many hearing people for a long time; *rejected*)  
  For: Signs can look like pictures of things, and some people think that means the language is simple.  
  Against: Linguists since the 1960s have shown that sign languages have grammar, new words, jokes, poetry and dialects, and children learn them in the same stages as spoken languages. India has its own, Indian Sign Language.
- **Tinnitus is always caused by damage in the ear.** (Many doctors in the past; *debated*)  
  For: It often starts after loud noise or hearing loss, and the damaged hair cells fit the pattern.  
  Against: Some people with a cut hearing nerve still have tinnitus, and some people with healthy hearing hear it in a very quiet room. Many scientists now think the brain plays a large part.

## Roots
We have tested how two ears find a sound, how noise hides a voice, and how the inner ear sorts pitches. Now we ask what it tells us about what a sound is, and about the one who hears it.

**Deep truth.** Each of your ears hears only a loudness and a moment. Your brain builds 'left' or 'behind' from the difference between the two. With one ear covered, the same sound seemed to come from somewhere else. So where a sound is comes partly from the listener.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a sound is over there, as if the place were inside the sound. A sound wave carries no label that says where it came from. The place is worked out by the listener from two ears.  
*Evidence:* In the Sound Clock, pointing was good with two ears open and went wrong when one ear was covered, although the sound was exactly the same. If your results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* You hear the world through a head with two ears and a brain that compares them. Someone with one ear hears the same room, and builds the places of its sounds in a slightly different way.
- *what is life:* A barn owl has ears at different heights, so she can tell above from below. A cricket hears with her knees, and a mosquito with his antennae. Every kind of listener builds a world to suit their own body.
- *what is this world:* A sound is a wave of squeezes in the air, and it carries energy and rhythm, but no map. The map and the loudness are made where the wave is heard.

A sound spreads out from its source in every direction, and each ear catches only a tiny piece of it. From the difference between two pieces, a brain makes direction. From the movement along a coiled membrane, it makes pitch. So the world we hear is made in a meeting between a wave and a listener. The same wave meets a bat, a moth or a person, and each one builds a different place from it.

- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.7:** The Upanishad says that when a drum is beaten, one cannot grasp the sounds that go out from it, but by grasping the drum or the drummer, one grasps the sound. (A sound is not something you can catch alone. You find it through the drum, the air and the listener together.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2 (the piping of earth):** Zhuangzi, an ancient Chinese thinker, described the wind blowing across ten thousand hollows in the earth, each one making its own sound. He asked who makes them sound, when the wind is the same. (The same wave sounds different in each ear and in each hollow, because the one who receives it shapes what is heard.)
- Science (settled): People can notice a difference of about one hundred-thousandth of a second between the ears, and use it to find the direction of a sound.
- Science (open): How the brain joins the clues from two ears, the shape of the pinna, head turns and the eyes into one place in space is not fully understood.
- Other view, Many physicists: A sound has a place and a direction whether anyone hears it or not. The wave comes from the drum, and the drum is over there. The listener only finds the place, and does not make it.
- Practice, The sound sit (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Listen for one minute. Each time you hear a sound, point with one finger to where you think it is. Then turn your head slowly to the left, and notice how the sounds move to the right. Open your eyes and look at where the sounds were. Were you right?
- Ask: If you had one ear, would you hear the same room?
- Ask: When a bat's call is too high for you to hear, is there a sound in the room?
- Ask: Where is the sound of a drum: in the drum, in the air, or in the listener?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Each of your ears hears only a loudness and a moment. The 'where' comes from comparing them. So is the place of a sound out there in the room, or built inside you? (hint: Think about what happened when you covered one ear.)
- *deep:* Someone who is deaf in one ear and someone with two working ears sit in the same room. Do they hear the same room? (hint: Think about which clues each of them has to build the place of a sound.)
- *deep:* A whisper was still reaching your ears while the tap ran, but you could not hear it. Was the whisper there? (hint: Think about the difference between a sound reaching you and a sound you hear.)
- *deep:* A moth hears a bat's call that no person can hear. If a sound is out there but nobody can hear it, is it still a sound? (hint: Think about whether sound is a wave in the air or what a listener hears.)
- *big:* In a noisy party, you can follow one voice and ignore the others. Who chooses what you hear: you, or your ears? (hint: Think about how it feels to choose, and how you notice when someone says your name.)
- *big:* Sign language uses no sound at all. Is it still a kind of listening? (hint: Think about what happens in the person who is watching.)
- *big:* Silence is not the same as hearing nothing. In a quiet room many people hear a faint hiss. What are you hearing when it seems silent? (hint: Think about what the ear and brain do when there is little sound to listen to.)
- *big:* People across the world make music, which does nothing for food or safety. Why do you think hearing gives us such a strong feeling for it? (hint: Think about drums, a lullaby and a song at a festival.)

## Story: The Whistle at the Pooram
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Two ears use tiny differences in loudness and timing to find the direction of a sound. Turning the head helps tell front from back, because a sound in front moves the opposite way in your ears from a sound behind. A steady, thin whistle can be followed through loud drumming. The big festival in Thrissur, called the Pooram, really has rows of decorated elephants and large groups of drummers, but the girl and her family are invented.*

Anjana was nine, and she had never seen so many people in one place. It was the day of the great festival in Thrissur. The whole town was packed around the temple ground, and the air smelled of jasmine and fried banana.

Her grandmother, Ammamma, held her hand tightly. Ammamma had been a teacher for forty years. Round her neck, on a red string, hung a steel whistle. She had used it for years to call children in from the playground.

"If we lose each other," said Ammamma, "stand still, and I will blow the whistle. Then walk towards it. Do not run."

A long row of elephants stood in the sun, dressed in gold. In front of them, a hundred drummers began to play. The chenda drums were so loud that Anjana felt the beat in her chest and in her teeth.

Then the crowd surged. A man pushed past with a big bunch of balloons, and Anjana's hand slipped out of her grandmother's.

She turned in a circle. There were faces, shoulders, flags, and balloons. There was no red string. "Ammamma!" she shouted. But the drums swallowed her voice. She could not even hear herself.

She stood still, as she had been told. Her heart was banging. The drums were so loud that she could not think.

Then, from far away, came a thin, bright sound. Peeeeep. It was the whistle. Anjana spun round. But the sound seemed to come from everywhere at once, from the temple wall, from the elephants and from the sky.

She ran towards where she thought it was. The whistle blew again, and now it seemed to come from behind her. She turned and ran the other way. It seemed to come from behind her again.

Anjana stopped. She was shaking. She tried to remember something. Last month, her teacher, Madam Lakshmi, had played a game in class. A girl sat with her eyes shut, and the class rang a small bell somewhere in the room. The girl had to point to it.

"You have two ears for a reason," Madam Lakshmi had said. "A sound reaches the nearer ear a tiny bit sooner and a tiny bit louder. Your brain compares the two. It works out the place without you even trying."

Anjana shut her eyes and held herself still. The whistle blew. It was a little louder in her right ear. She turned her head towards the right.

Peeeeep. Now it seemed to come equally to both ears. "Straight ahead," she whispered. "Or straight behind." She remembered the next part of the lesson. She turned her head a little to the left. The whistle moved to her right ear. "If it were behind me," she said, "it would have moved to my left. So it is in front."

She opened her eyes and walked a few steps. When the whistle was louder in one ear, she turned her head until it sounded the same in both. Then she walked on. Some steps she was wrong, but each time Ammamma blew, Anjana corrected her way.

The drums thundered. The whistle was thin and high, and so it did not drown. Each time it came, her ears could find it among the booming, like finding a bright thread in a dark cloth.

Ammamma stood on a step by the temple wall, looking in every direction. Then she saw a small girl walking in a straight line towards her, her head tilted a little to one side, like a bird listening for a worm.

Anjana ran the last few steps and put her arms round her grandmother. "I walked towards the whistle," she said. "I used both my ears."

"I did not think you would find me," said Ammamma. Her voice shook. "I thought I would have to stand here and blow all night. How did you do it?"

"The sound was louder on one side," said Anjana, "so I turned until it was the same on both. And when I could not tell front from back, I turned my head and watched which ear it moved to."

Ammamma looked at her for a long moment. "Madam Lakshmi," she said, smiling, "I owe you a box of sweets."

That night, at home, Anjana taught her little brother Kiran the game. He sat with his eyes shut in the middle of the room, and she rang the steel whistle from a different corner each time. Kiran pointed. Sometimes he was right, and sometimes he got front and back wrong. Anjana showed him how to turn his head.

**For the parent:** A sound carries no map. Each ear hears only a loudness and a moment. The brain builds the direction by comparing the two. When two ears hear the same, the sound is straight ahead or straight behind, and turning the head tells which. A thin, steady sound is easier to follow through a loud noise than a low one.

- Ask: Why did the whistle seem to come from everywhere at first?
- Ask: How did Anjana tell that a sound which reached both ears equally was in front of her?
- Ask: Why was the thin whistle easier to follow than Anjana's own voice, when the drums were playing?
- Ask: What could Ammamma have done to help Anjana find her even faster?

The card is not turned over yet.; Everybody guesses which band of the ladder the sound belongs to, and says why.; Turn the card over and read the decibel number. Place the card on the right band of the ladder.; A player whose guess was in the right band gets one point. A player who guessed the band next door gets half a point.; Take turns to read cards. The game ends when all the cards are on the ladder.; At the end, look at the bands above 85 decibels. Put the safe times from the backs in order, from the longest to the shortest.; Playing alone, guess each band before turning the card over, and try to score 20 or more out of 24.

Very faint, close to the quietest sound a young person can hear. Safe all day.); A wall clock ticking in a quiet room (back: About 25 decibels. Safe all day.); A whisper (back: About 30 decibels. Safe all day.); A quiet bedroom at night (back: About 35 decibels. Safe all day.); A refrigerator humming (back: About 40 decibels. Safe all day.); A mosquito buzzing near your ear (back: About 40 decibels. Safe all day, though it is annoying.); Birds singing in a garden (back: About 45 decibels. Safe all day.); People talking quietly (back: About 50 decibels. Safe all day.); A tap running into a steel bucket (back: About 55 decibels. Safe all day.); A normal conversation (back: About 60 decibels. That is 1,000 times the energy of a whisper. Safe all day.); A classroom full of chatting children (back: About 65 decibels. Safe all day, but tiring for the teacher.); A car passing 10 metres away (back: About 70 decibels. Safe all day, but it masks speech.); A vacuum cleaner running in the room (back: About 75 decibels. Safe for many hours.); A busy city street (back: About 80 decibels. Safe for many hours, but it is wise to take quiet breaks.); A mixer-grinder running (back: About 85 decibels. The safe time is about 5 minutes. Turn them down.); A loudspeaker at a wedding, standing near it (back: About 110 decibels. The safe time is about 1 and a half minutes.); Thunder very close overhead (back: About 115 decibels. A short peal, but loud enough to hurt.); An ambulance siren close by (back: About 120 decibels. Harm in seconds. Move away.); A jet plane taking off nearby (back: About 130 decibels. Can hurt at once.); A firecracker going off close by (back: About 140 decibels. Can hurt at once and cause lasting damage.)

- **s2 (4 min)**

  *Parent says:* Here are three cards. A whisper, a normal conversation and a firecracker. Place each one on the ladder where you think it goes. Then we check the backs.  
  *Child mode:* Place the whisper, the conversation and the firecracker on the ladder.
  - Check: A whisper is about 30 decibels and a normal conversation is about 60 decibels. How much more sound energy does the conversation have? → **1,000 times as much**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the rest of the cards. For each, everyone guesses the band first. Then turn it over. Keep a score. After every six cards, ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Read the sound on the front. Guess the band. Then turn the card over and read the decibels. Right band is one point. The band next door is half a point.  
  *Child mode:* Read a card. Guess the band. Turn it over. Place it on the ladder.
  - Ask: Which sound surprised you most, and why? (good: Headphones turned up full, because I did not know they can be that loud.; The mosquito, because it seems so small.; A city street, because it is louder than I thought.)
  - Note: Children often underestimate headphones and a mixer. Do not lecture. Let the numbers speak, and keep the score light.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the finished ladder. Ask your child to point to the sounds that are safe all day and the sounds that need a time limit.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the finished ladder. Show me the sounds that are safe all day. Now show me the sounds that need a time limit.  
  *Child mode:* Point to the sounds safe all day. Point to the ones with a time limit.
  - Note: Sounds below about 80 decibels are safe all day. At 85 decibels and above, the safe time starts to shrink.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the safe times from the backs in order, from longest to shortest. Ask the question below, and let your child work it out before you say the answer.  
  *Parent says:* At 85 decibels the safe time is 8 hours. Each 3 decibels louder halves it. So how long is it safe at 100 decibels?  
  *Child mode:* Each 3 decibels louder halves the safe time. At 100 decibels, how long?
  - Check: The safe time at 85 decibels is 8 hours, and every 3 decibels more halves it. About how long is it safe at 100 decibels? → **About 15 minutes**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Sound Clock: can you find a sound with one ear? (35 min)
- **question:** How well can you find where a sound is with two ears, and how much worse is it with one ear covered?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Out of 16 clicks, how many will you point to exactly right with two ears, and how many with one ear covered? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same chair, the same spoon and katori, the same distance of 1.5 metres and the same eight places every time. Each place is clicked twice in each round, in a mixed-up order. Only the ears change. Keep your eyes shut or covered for every click. Keep your head still in Rounds A and B.
- **safety:** Never push cotton wool, earbuds or anything else into the ear. Clear the floor of anything to trip over before eyes are covered. Make each click gently and keep the katori at least 1 metre from the listener's head.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three rounds. Then finish this sentence: 'With one ear covered, my pointing went ...'
- **results table:** Round | My guess: how many exactly right out of 16? | Exactly right | One place off | More than one place off | Exactly right plus one place off
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the chair, the katori and the eight places. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* I will click a spoon on a katori around you, and you will point to it with your eyes shut. First with two ears, then with one ear covered. How many out of 16 will you get exactly right each time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes and point to the click. Guess how many you will get right. Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think covering one ear will change your pointing? (good: One ear hears less, so I cannot tell where it is.; The sound will seem to come from the open side.; Two ears work like two eyes, so one makes it harder.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write it down, with the reason. Children often guess they will get most right with two ears, and some think one ear will not matter.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put the chair in the middle of a clear space. Lay the eight paper slips on the floor in a circle 1.5 metres from the chair, with Front straight ahead. Make a pile of 16 slips, two of each place, and shuffle them.  
  *Parent says:* The chair is the middle. The slips make a circle around it, 1.5 metres away. I will pick a slip, go to that place quietly, and click once. You will point and say where you think I am.  
  *Child mode:* Sit on the chair. The slips make a circle around you. Eyes shut soon.
  - Note: Wear soft socks so your footsteps do not give you away. Wait 3 seconds after reaching a place before you click. Pick the slips from a bag or a shuffled pile so the order is random. Write the true place and the pointed place for each click. Children may peek, so cover the eyes properly.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover your child's eyes. Draw a slip and move to that place quietly. Click once. Your child points and says front, left, back or so on. Write it down. Do all 16 slips. Keep the head still.  
  *Parent says:* Keep your head still. When you hear the click, point with one finger and say where it is. Even if you are not sure, make a guess.  
  *Child mode:* Eyes covered. Keep your head still. Point to each click.
  - Note: Round A is two ears, head still. Mark each pointing as exactly right, one place off, or more than one place off. Front and back mix-ups count as more than one place off. Expect about 10 or 11 exactly right, and a few front and back mix-ups.
  - Check: A click straight in front of your head and a click straight behind your head reach both ears together. Why are these two places the hardest to tell apart? → **Both ears hear the same loudness and the same time for each, so the main clues are the same**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now cover one ear with a soft cover. Shuffle the slips and do another 16 clicks. Keep the head still. Mark each one as before. Note which side most of the mistakes lean towards.  
  *Parent says:* This time one ear is covered. Same game. Keep your head still. Point when you hear the click. Do you think it will be harder or easier?  
  *Child mode:* One ear is covered. Same game. Keep your head still.
  - Note: Round B is one ear covered. Expect only about 4 to 6 exactly right, and many wrong answers leaning toward the open ear, because the sound seems louder there. If your child finds that sounds on the covered side seem to come from the front, that is normal. Remember: nothing goes inside the ear. Use an earmuff, a cap or a hand.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the ear cover off. Shuffle the slips and do a third round of 16 clicks. This time your child may turn the head from side to side while listening. Eyes stay covered.  
  *Parent says:* Back to two ears. This time you may turn your head from side to side as you listen. Does it help?  
  *Child mode:* Two ears again. This time you may turn your head. Does it help?
  - Note: Round C is two ears with head turns. Expect the front and back mix-ups to almost vanish, and 13 or more exactly right. When the head turns, the sound changes which ear is nearer, and the brain uses the change.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say three things: what I think now, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What numbers show it? And why do you think it happened? What did turning your head do?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think now. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this: 'With two ears I was right a lot. With one ear covered I made many more mistakes. I think the brain finds where a sound is by comparing what the two ears hear. When I could turn my head, I got even better, because the head turn changed what the ears heard.' Now read the card 'Two ears tell where a sound is'.
  - Check: With one ear covered, the same clicks made you point wrongly. What changed between Round A and Round B? → **Only the clues the brain got, because one ear gave no comparison**
### e2 · Hiding a Whisper: how far can a whisper reach in noise? (30 min)
- **question:** How much shorter is the distance a whisper reaches when there is noise, and what can a speaker do to be heard?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How far will a whisper reach in a quiet room? How far with a tap running gently? How far with the tap running hard? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The listener sits in the same place with eyes shut for every test. The whisperer uses the same quiet whisper for every digit. Only the noise changes. Five digits are whispered at each distance, picked in a mixed-up order. A distance counts as heard if the listener gets at least 4 of the 5 right.
- **safety:** Do not make the noise louder than a loud conversation, so that nobody has to shout and nobody's ears are hurt. Wipe up water at once.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then finish this sentence: 'The louder the noise, the ...' and write one thing that helped a whisper be heard.
- **results table:** Noise | My guess: how far will a whisper reach? (metres) | Digits right at 1 metre (out of 5) | Digits right at 2 metres (out of 5) | Digits right at 3 metres (out of 5) | Furthest distance with 4 or 5 right (metres)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the corridor, the tap and the list of digits. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* I will whisper numbers to you from different distances. First in a quiet room. Then with a tap running gently. Then with it running hard. How far will my whisper reach each time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how far a whisper will reach in quiet, and in noise. Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the noise will change how far the whisper reaches? (good: The noise is louder than the whisper, so it covers it.; I will have to listen harder.; The whisper will still arrive, but I won't be able to hear it.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A whisper is about 30 decibels. In a quiet room it often reaches 4 or 5 metres. Write the guess in metres.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 metres along the floor from the listener's chair. Write the digit lists. Put the bucket under the tap, if the room has one. If not, get a fan ready.  
  *Parent says:* We mark the floor at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 metres from the chair. For each distance I will whisper five digits. I have written them down in a mixed-up order.  
  *Child mode:* Mark the floor at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 metres. Sit on the chair.
  - Note: If your tap is not in the same room, use a table fan on its highest setting, or a radio tuned between two stations, as your loud noise. Put the noise source near the listener, about 1.5 metres to one side. Make sure the noise is steady. The listener keeps eyes covered.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the quiet round first. Stand at 1 metre. Whisper five digits one at a time. The listener says each digit. Write down how many are right. Then move to 2, 3, 4 and 5 metres.  
  *Parent says:* Listen carefully. I will whisper a number. Say it back. If you don't hear it, take a guess. We start with a quiet room.  
  *Child mode:* Eyes covered. Listen for each number. Say it back. Guess if unsure.
  - Note: Whisper clearly but quietly, always the same way. Stop moving back when the listener gets fewer than 4 of 5 right. The furthest distance with 4 or 5 right is the whisper reach. Expect 3 to 5 metres in a quiet room.
  - Check: Why do we whisper five digits at each distance, and not just one? → **One lucky guess could fool us, so we need several tries**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn on the tap gently, with a steady trickle into the bucket. Repeat the whispers at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 metres. Then turn the tap on hard and repeat. Write the scores.  
  *Parent says:* Now the tap runs gently. Same whispers, same places. Listen and say the number. Then we will turn the tap up and do it again.  
  *Child mode:* Tap running. Listen for each number. Say it back.
  - Note: Expect a shorter whisper reach with the gentle tap, about 2 or 3 metres, and a much shorter one with the tap hard, about 1 metre or less. If the whisper cannot be heard even at 1 metre, move to half a metre. The whisper is still reaching the ear. It is hidden by the noise.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep the tap running hard. Stand at a distance where the listener got fewer than 4 right. Try two helps in turn. First, let the listener take the scarf off and watch your mouth. Second, move one step closer.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to help the whisper get through the noise. First, you watch my mouth while I whisper. Then I come one step closer. Which helps more?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the whisperer's mouth. Then try one step closer. Which helps more?
  - Note: Seeing the lips usually helps a lot, because the brain uses the movements of the mouth to fill in what the ear misses. Moving closer helps too. Ask your child which one they would pick if they could pick only one in a noisy hall.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say three things: what I think now, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What numbers show it? And why do you think it happened? Was the whisper still arriving when the tap was loud?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think now. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this: 'The louder the noise, the shorter the distance a whisper reached. In a quiet room it reached about 4 metres. With the tap hard it reached less than 1 metre. I think the noise hid the whisper, even though it still reached my ear. Watching the lips and moving closer helped.' Now read the card 'Masking: why noise hides a voice'.
  - Check: With the tap running hard, you could not hear the whisper. What is the best explanation? → **The whisper still reached your ear, but the louder noise hid it**
### e3 · Doubling the Distance: how fast does a sound fade as you move away? (30 min)
- **question:** Each time you double your distance from a steady sound, how many decibels does it drop?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If the fan reads 60 decibels at 1 metre, what will it read at 2 metres, 4 metres and 8 metres? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same fan on the same setting, the same phone and app, and the same height, one metre from the floor, for every reading. Only the distance changes. Take three readings at each distance and use the middle one, because an app jumps around.
- **safety:** Do not put the phone close to a loud sound for long. Switch off the fan when you finish. Keep fingers away from fan blades.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then work out the drop in decibels each time the distance doubled. Finish this sentence: 'Each time I doubled the distance, the sound dropped by about ...'
- **results table:** Distance from the fan (metres) | My guess (decibels) | Reading 1 | Reading 2 | Reading 3 | Middle reading
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the fan and the app. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess in the table for each distance.  
  *Parent says:* We will measure the sound of the fan at four distances. If it reads 60 decibels at 1 metre, what will it read at 2 metres? At 4 metres? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how loud the fan is when you double the distance. Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the sound will get quieter in that way? (good: It gets half as loud each time.; It drops by the same amount each time.; It spreads out as it goes.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children guess halving, so 30 decibels at 2 metres. Write each guess in the table. If no app is available, borrow a phone, or skip this experiment.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put the fan on a table at one end of the room. Switch it on at a steady speed. Mark 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 metres from the fan on the floor. Open the app and check that it reads a number.  
  *Parent says:* We place the fan at one end. We mark 0.5 metres, 1 metre, 2 metres and 4 metres along the floor. Then we open the app and check that it works.  
  *Child mode:* Switch on the fan. Mark the floor at four distances. Open the app.
  - Note: Phone microphones are not accurate. The numbers may be off by 5 decibels or more, and some apps do not read below 30 or above 90. That is fine here. We are looking at how the number changes between distances, not at the exact number. Hold the phone at the height of the fan, pointing at it, and do not move it between readings.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the phone at 0.5 metres, facing the fan. Wait 10 seconds. Read the number and write it down. Do this three times. Then do the same at 1, 2 and 4 metres.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the phone steady, pointing at the fan. Wait ten seconds for the number to settle. Read it and write it down. Three readings at each distance.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the phone steady. Wait ten seconds. Read the number. Write it down.
  - Note: Expect readings like 62, 57, 53 and 49 decibels for 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 metres in a room. The drops will not be the same each time. In a room with walls, sound bounces back, so each doubling drops about 3 to 5 decibels. In open air it is about 6.
  - Check: At 1 metre the fan reads 57 decibels. At 2 metres it reads 53 decibels. What is the drop? → **4 decibels**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three readings in order at each distance, and pick the middle one. Work out the drop from one distance to the next. Look for a pattern.  
  *Parent says:* For each distance, put the three readings in order and pick the middle one. Then take away the next middle reading. What is the drop each time you double the distance?  
  *Child mode:* Pick the middle reading. Find the drop each time the distance doubles.
  - Note: The drops are likely to be similar to each other, around 3 to 6 decibels. In open air, sound spreads over a ball that gets four times bigger in area each time the distance doubles, so each bit gets a quarter of the energy, which is a drop of about 6 decibels.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say three things: what I think now, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What numbers show it? And why do you think it happened? Where does the sound's energy go as you move away?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think now. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this: 'Every time I doubled the distance, the number dropped by about 4. It was not halving. I think the sound spreads out in all directions, so there is less in each place.' Now read the card 'Why a sound gets fainter as you move away'.
  - Check: Why does a sound get quieter as you move away from it? → **The same energy spreads over a bigger and bigger area, so each part gets less**

### a2 · The Paper Snail: build a model of the cochlea (25 min)

  *Child mode:* Draw a wave that peaks at 14 centimetres. Draw another that peaks at 28.
  - Note: A real wave starts at the narrow end, grows bigger as it travels, then rises to a peak and dies away very fast just past the peak. The hair cells at the peak are the ones that signal strongly. Draw the wave as a bump that grows and then drops.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Curl the strip into a snail, with the narrow end on the outside. Tape it so it stays curled. Count the turns. Real cochleas have about two and a half turns.  
  *Parent says:* Real cochleas are coiled up like a snail to fit inside the head. Curl your strip with the narrow end on the outside. Wrap it round two or three times.  
  *Child mode:* Curl the strip into a snail. Narrow end outside. Tape it.
  - Note: The paper snail will not curl perfectly. It does not need to. The point is to see how a 3.5 centimetre tube can fit into a space the size of a pea. The coil shape does not change how the wave travels.

## Worksheets
### Two Ears and Loud Sounds (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A clap happens close to your right ear. Which ear gets the sound first, and which hears it a little louder?  
   Options: The right ear gets it first and hears it louder / The left ear gets it first and hears it louder / Both ears get it at the same time and equally loudly  
   **Answer:** The right ear gets it first and hears it louder. *Why:* The sound reaches the nearer ear first and is louder there, because the head blocks some sound from the far ear. The brain uses both clues.
2. (fill) A sound straight in front of you reaches both ears at the same ____.  
   **Answer:** ['time']. *Why:* Straight ahead is the same distance from both ears, so the sound arrives together and equally loud. That is why front and back are hard to tell apart.
3. (mcq) Why is it harder to say where a sound is with one ear covered?  
   Options: The brain has no second ear to compare with / The sound becomes quieter in the air / The covered ear is the one that finds direction  
   **Answer:** The brain has no second ear to compare with. *Why:* Direction comes from comparing two ears. With one ear covered, the time and loudness differences are lost, so the brain must guess.
4. (truefalse) A whisper is about 30 decibels and a conversation is about 60 decibels, so the conversation has twice the sound energy of the whisper.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 30 decibels more is three steps of 10, and each step is 10 times the energy. So the conversation has 1,000 times the energy.
5. (mcq) How many times more sound energy does a sound have if it is 10 decibels higher?  
   Options: 10 times / 2 times / 10 more units  
   **Answer:** 10 times. *Why:* Every 10 decibels is a multiplication by 10 in energy. That is why the numbers on the scale stay small even for huge differences.
6. (mcq) At 85 decibels the safe listening time is about 8 hours, and each 3 decibels more halves it. About how long is it safe at 94 decibels?  
   Options: About 1 hour / About 4 hours / About 15 minutes  
   **Answer:** About 1 hour. *Why:* 94 is 9 more than 85, which is three steps of 3. Halve 8 hours three times: 4 hours, 2 hours, 1 hour.
7. (odd) Three of these are things the ear does with a sound. Which one is not?  
   Options: The ear flap collects sound and guides it in / Three tiny bones pass the sound on to the cochlea / Hair cells turn the wave into signals for the brain / The ear makes the sound louder at its source  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The ear collects, passes on and detects sound. The source of a sound is outside the ear, and the ear cannot change it.
8. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Pinna → the ear flap on the side of the head; Masking → a loud sound hiding a quiet one; Cochlea → the coiled tube where hair cells turn waves into signals; Decibel → the unit for how strong a sound is; Tinnitus → hearing ringing when there is no sound outside. *Why:* These words are the tools for talking about hearing.
9. (sort) Is each one a clue the brain can use to find the direction of a sound, or not?  
   **Answer:** the sound reaches one ear before the other → A clue for direction; the sound is louder in one ear → A clue for direction; the sound changes when you turn your head → A clue for direction; the temperature of the room → Not a clue for direction; the colour of the walls → Not a clue for direction. *Why:* The brain uses the time, the loudness, the folds of the ear flap and the changes when the head turns. The temperature of the room and the colour of the sound do not help.
10. (order) Put these in order along the cochlea, from where the wave peaks first (the narrow start) to where it peaks last (the far end).  
   **Answer:** ['a high whistle of 3,000 hertz', 'a voice of about 250 hertz', 'a deep drum of 100 hertz']. *Why:* High pitches make the wave peak near the start of the cochlea. Low pitches make it peak near the far end.
11. (truefalse) Hair cells destroyed by very loud noise grow back in a few weeks, so loud noise never causes lasting harm.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In people, hair cells do not grow back. Loud noise can cause lasting loss of hearing, often with no pain at the time.
12. (mcq) You cannot hear a whisper while a tap runs hard. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: The whisper still reaches your ear, but the louder noise hides it / The whisper never leaves the speaker's mouth / Your ears stop working when there is noise  
   **Answer:** The whisper still reaches your ear, but the louder noise hides it. *Why:* This is called masking. The louder sound moves the same part of the ear as the quiet one, so the quiet one is lost.
13. (label) Label the model of the basilar membrane: the narrow stiff end, the wide soft end, where high pitches peak, and where low pitches peak.

## Applications
- Hearing aids make sounds louder for people whose hair cells still work but have become less sensitive, for example in old age.
- Cochlear implants let many children who were born deaf learn to understand speech.
- Hospitals check newborn babies' hearing in a minute or two by recording the faint sounds the ear gives out, so that help can begin early.
- Earplugs and ear defenders protect the hair cells of people who work near drills, machines and aircraft.
- Noise rules and sound meters set safe limits for homes, schools, hospitals and for firecrackers, and help check them.
- Concert halls and classrooms are designed so that sound reaches every seat clearly, and so that echoes and noise do not hide speech.
- Music files such as MP3s are made smaller by dropping sounds that a louder sound next to them would hide anyway, which uses the idea of masking.
- Stereo sound and surround sound use the time and loudness differences between two ears to make a sound seem to come from a place.
- Sign language interpreters and captions allow Deaf people to follow lessons, news and films in India.
- Ear trumpets, megaphones and hearing horns use a wide mouth and a narrow end to gather and focus sound.

## Open questions
- **How does the brain join the clues from two ears, the shape of the ear flap, head turns and the eyes into one place in space?** Scientists know each clue and can measure how well people use them. They do not yet know how the brain combines all of them so quickly and so well.
- **Can people's damaged hair cells ever be made to grow back?** Birds and fish can grow new hair cells after damage, but people cannot. Scientists are testing ways to make human ears do the same, but there is no sure cure yet.
- **What really causes the ringing of tinnitus?** Some scientists think it starts in the damaged ear, and others think the brain makes it. It probably involves both, but nobody can yet explain it fully or cure it.
- **How do people with a cochlear implant experience music?** Many understand speech well, but find music flat. Scientists are studying how to give implants a richer sound, and what music is like for each person.
- **Why does music, which does nothing for food or safety, move us so strongly?** Scientists have many ideas, from group bonding to the way the brain predicts patterns, but nobody knows which are right, and the answer may be a mixture.
- **Is a sound a wave in the air, or what a listener hears?** Physicists and philosophers use the word in different ways. The wave is certain. Whether the sound is the wave or the hearing is a question about the meaning of the word, and people still argue about it.

## Warm-up
- Which ear hears a sound from your right first? → The right ear, and a little louder too.
- Why is it hard to tell a sound straight in front from a sound straight behind? → It reaches both ears at the same time and equally loudly.
- What can you do to tell front from back? → Turn your head and notice which ear the sound moves to.
- What does a decibel number tell you? → How strong a sound is.
- Every 10 decibels more is how many times the energy? → 10 times.
- What is masking? → A loud sound hiding a quieter one.
- Where on the basilar membrane do high pitches peak? → Near the narrow, stiff start.
- Do hair cells grow back in people? → No.
- What does a cochlear implant do? → Sends signals straight to the hearing nerve.
- Is Indian Sign Language a real language? → Yes, with its own grammar.
