# Kinds of chemical reactions: joining, splitting, swapping and burning
`sci.chemistry.kinds-of-reactions.L4`

**Summary.** Squeeze lemon into milk and curds form, and a second squeeze makes no more. Why does it stop? In this unit you will find out which ran out, heat a powder to see if it splits, and build one reaction each of four kinds.

**Intro.** *A cook squeezes lemon juice into hot milk to make paneer. The milk was a liquid, and the lemon juice was a liquid. So where did the solid curds come from?* The curds were in the milk all along, as tiny clusters too small to see. The acid in the lemon juice changed them, so they stuck together and came out as a solid. That is one kind of chemical reaction. In this unit you meet four kinds. In a joining reaction, two substances become one. In a splitting reaction, one substance becomes two or more. In a swapping reaction, substances exchange parts. In burning, a fuel reacts with oxygen and gives out heat and light. You make curds, heat baking soda under a cold lid, mix plaster and shake soap with vinegar.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what reactants and products are, and that a reaction breaks old bonds and makes new ones.
1. I can describe a joining reaction, and give plaster and water as an example.
2. I can describe a splitting reaction, and say what heat does to baking soda.
3. I can describe a swapping reaction, and say what vinegar does to soap.
4. I can explain why lemon juice makes curds in milk, and what a precipitate is.
5. I can describe burning as a fuel reacting with oxygen, giving out heat and light.
6. I can explain why a reaction stops when one reactant is used up.
7. I can sort a reaction into its kind, and say when one reaction fits two kinds.

## Terms
- **Chemical reaction**: A change in which substances turn into new substances. The atoms stay the same, but they end up with new partners. *e.g.* Lemon juice and hot milk react. They make curds and whey, which are new substances.
- **Reactant**: A substance that you start with in a reaction. It is used up as the reaction goes on. *e.g.* In making curds, the reactants are the milk protein and the acid in lemon juice.
- **Product**: A new substance that a reaction makes. *e.g.* When plaster of Paris sets, the product is a hard solid called gypsum.
- **Bond**: The hold between two atoms that are joined. A reaction breaks some bonds and makes new ones. *e.g.* In water, one oxygen atom has a bond to each of two hydrogen atoms.
- **Joining reaction**: A reaction in which two or more substances become one new substance. Chemists also call it a combination reaction. *e.g.* Plaster of Paris and water join and become one hard solid.
- **Splitting reaction**: A reaction in which one substance becomes two or more new substances. Chemists also call it a decomposition reaction. *e.g.* Heat splits baking soda into washing soda, water and carbon dioxide.
- **Swapping reaction**: A reaction in which substances exchange parts. Chemists also call it a displacement reaction. *e.g.* Soap and vinegar swap parts. They make a white fatty solid and a salt that stays dissolved.
- **Burning**: A fast reaction between a fuel and oxygen that gives out heat and light. Chemists also call it combustion. *e.g.* Candle wax burns with oxygen from the air and makes carbon dioxide and water.
- **Fuel**: A substance that can burn. Most fuels contain carbon and hydrogen. *e.g.* Wax, wood, cooking gas, petrol and dry leaves are fuels.
- **Precipitate**: A solid that forms inside a liquid during a reaction. It forms because one product does not dissolve in water. *e.g.* The white bits that appear when vinegar is added to soap water are a precipitate.
- **Curds**: The soft white solid that comes out of milk when an acid is added. Curds are mostly milk protein and fat. *e.g.* Paneer is made by pressing the curds from hot milk and lemon juice.
- **Whey**: The thin, pale yellow-green liquid left after the curds come out of milk. It is mostly water, with milk sugar and minerals. *e.g.* After paneer is strained, the liquid left in the bowl is whey.
- **Casein**: The main protein in milk. It floats in milk as tiny clusters that are too small to see. *e.g.* Lemon juice makes the casein clusters stick together, and they become curds.
- **Used up**: A reactant is used up when none of it is left to react. Then the reaction stops. *e.g.* Once all the casein has become curds, more lemon juice makes no more curds.
- **Plaster of Paris**: A white powder made by heating a rock called gypsum. Mixed with water, it joins with the water and sets hard. *e.g.* Doctors use plaster of Paris for casts, and artists use it for statues.
- **Word equation**: A short way to write a reaction in words. The reactants go before the arrow and the products go after it. *e.g.* Plaster of Paris + water → gypsum.

## Explanations
### A reaction breaks old bonds and makes new ones · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical reaction is a change in which substances turn into new substances. Bonds between atoms break, and new bonds form.

Everything is made of atoms. In most substances the atoms are joined to each other, and each join is called a bond. In a chemical reaction, some bonds break and new bonds form. The atoms themselves do not change, and none is made or lost. They only end up with new partners. The substances you start with are the reactants. The new substances are the products. Breaking a bond takes energy in. Making a bond gives energy out. That is why some reactions feel warm and others need heating. There are millions of known reactions. Chemists sort them into kinds by what the atoms do. This unit uses four kinds: joining, splitting, swapping and burning.
- e.g. Milk protein and lemon acid are reactants. Curds and whey are products.
- e.g. When plaster sets, new bonds form, and the cup feels warm.
- e.g. Baking soda splits only when heat is put in.
### Joining: two substances become one · *defined*
**Definition:** In a joining reaction, two or more substances join and make one new substance. Chemists call it a combination reaction.

The pattern of a joining reaction is A + B → AB. Plaster of Paris is a good example. The powder and the water join, and the product is one solid called gypsum. The water does not dry away. It becomes part of the solid, which is why plaster sets hard while it is still wet. Rusting is a joining reaction too. Iron joins with oxygen and water, and the product is rust. Joining reactions make new bonds, so many of them give out heat. A cup of setting plaster feels warm. Quicklime joins with water so strongly that the water can boil. Lime plaster on a wall slowly joins with carbon dioxide from the air, and turns hard.
- e.g. Plaster of Paris + water → gypsum.
- e.g. Iron + oxygen + water → rust.
- e.g. Quicklime + water → slaked lime, which is the chuna used in whitewash.
### Splitting: one substance becomes two or more · *defined*
**Definition:** In a splitting reaction, one substance breaks into two or more new substances. Chemists call it a decomposition reaction.

The pattern of a splitting reaction is AB → A + B. Bonds must break, so many splitting reactions need energy put in. The energy can be heat, electricity or light. Heat splits baking soda into three substances: washing soda, water and carbon dioxide. The water leaves as steam, and the carbon dioxide leaves as a gas. That gas is what makes a cake or a dhokla rise. Electricity splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. Heat splits seashells and limestone into quicklime and carbon dioxide. Splitting is the opposite of joining. Heat drives the water out of gypsum and makes plaster of Paris. Adding water joins them again.
- e.g. Baking soda, heated → washing soda + water + carbon dioxide.
- e.g. Water, with electricity → hydrogen + oxygen.
- e.g. Limestone, heated strongly → quicklime + carbon dioxide.
### Swapping: substances exchange parts · *defined*
**Definition:** In a swapping reaction, substances exchange parts and make new substances. Chemists call it a displacement reaction.

The pattern of a swapping reaction is AB + CD → AD + CB. Each substance has two parts, and the parts change partners. Soap is made of a long fatty part joined to sodium. Vinegar is an acid, and an acid has hydrogen to give. When they meet, the sodium and the hydrogen change places. The fatty part with hydrogen is a waxy solid that does not dissolve, so the water turns milky. The sodium ends up in a salt that stays dissolved. Sometimes only one part moves. An iron nail in blue copper sulphate water slowly gets a brown coat of copper. The iron takes the place of the copper.
- e.g. Soap + vinegar → a white fatty solid + a dissolved salt.
- e.g. Stomach acid + an antacid → a salt + water.
- e.g. Iron + copper sulphate → copper + iron sulphate.
### Precipitation: why milk and lemon make curds · *defined*
**Definition:** Precipitation is the forming of a solid inside a liquid during a reaction. The solid is called a precipitate.

Milk looks like one smooth liquid, but it is water with many things in it. The main protein, casein, floats as tiny clusters. Each cluster carries a small electric charge of the same kind. Charges of the same kind repel, so the clusters push each other away and stay spread out. An acid gives hydrogen to the clusters, and the hydrogen cancels their charge. Some calcium leaves the clusters and goes into the liquid. Now nothing keeps the clusters apart. They stick together, trap the fat, and come out as curds. The curds are a precipitate. Hot milk curdles with less acid than cold milk. Sour milk curdles in the same way, because bacteria in the milk have made an acid.
- e.g. Hot milk + lemon juice → curds + whey.
- e.g. Soap + hard water → a grey scum on the bucket.
- e.g. Dripping water in a cave leaves solid limestone behind, which grows into a stalactite.
### Burning: a fuel reacts with oxygen · *defined*
**Definition:** Burning is a fast reaction between a fuel and oxygen. It gives out heat and light. Chemists call it combustion.

A fuel is a substance that can burn. Wax, wood, cooking gas and petrol are fuels, and they are made mostly of carbon and hydrogen. When a fuel burns, its bonds break. The carbon joins with oxygen and makes carbon dioxide. The hydrogen joins with oxygen and makes water, which leaves as steam. These new bonds are very strong, so burning gives out a lot of energy. Burning needs three things: a fuel, oxygen and enough heat to start. Water, sand and ash do not burn, because their atoms are already joined with oxygen. Your cells also react sugar with oxygen and make carbon dioxide and water. They do it in many small steps, with no flame.
- e.g. Wax + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, with heat and light.
- e.g. Cooking gas + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
- e.g. Hydrogen + oxygen → water. Some rocket engines burn hydrogen.
### A reaction stops when one reactant is used up · *defined*
**Definition:** A reaction goes on only while every reactant is present. When one reactant is used up, the reaction stops, however much of the others is left.

Reactants are used in fixed amounts. A cup of milk holds a fixed amount of casein. Once all of the casein has become curds, extra lemon juice has nothing to work on. The leftover acid stays in the whey. If fresh milk is added, the leftover acid makes new curds, until the acid is used up too. The reactant that runs out first decides how much product is made. Chemists call it the limiting reactant. A candle under a glass goes out for the same reason: the oxygen runs low while wax is still left. Cooks, medicine makers and factory engineers all measure reactants, so that little is wasted.
- e.g. More lemon in whey makes no new curds, because the casein is used up.
- e.g. A candle under a glass goes out with wax still left.
- e.g. Baking soda that has been fully heated gives no more water when it is heated again.
### One reaction can fit two kinds · *debated*
The four kinds are a way of sorting, and some reactions fit two kinds. A burning candle is an example. The wax comes apart, and its atoms join with oxygen. So this one burning reaction is a splitting and a joining at the same time. When hydrogen burns, two substances become one, so it is a burning and a joining at once. Vinegar and baking soda first swap parts. One product, carbonic acid, then splits into water and carbon dioxide, which makes the fizz. People who study chemistry do not all sort reactions in the same way.
- e.g. Hydrogen + oxygen → water is both burning and joining.
- e.g. Vinegar and baking soda swap parts, and then one product splits.
- e.g. Heating gypsum splits off water. Adding water joins the two again.
- **Many school books:** They sort reactions by their pattern: joining, splitting, swapping and burning. The pattern is easy to see in a word equation, and it helps you guess the products.
- **Many working chemists:** They sort reactions by what moves between the substances. In some reactions electrons move, and in others hydrogen moves. By this sorting, burning and rusting are the same kind.
- **Chemists who study how reactions happen:** They say every reaction is a chain of small steps. In each step a bond breaks or a bond forms. For them, the kinds are names for common chains of steps.
### The four kinds inside living bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** Living cells run thousands of reactions every second. Most of them are joining, splitting or swapping reactions.

Your body uses every kind of reaction in this unit. Digestion is mostly splitting. Long chains of starch and protein are cut into small units, and each cut uses one water molecule. Growing is mostly joining. Your cells join small units into long chains of protein, and so build muscle, skin and hair. Your cells also react sugar with oxygen. The products are the same as in burning: carbon dioxide and water. Plants do the reverse. A green leaf joins carbon dioxide and water into sugar, using the energy of light, and gives out oxygen. Living beings make precipitates too. A clam builds their shell from calcium and carbon dioxide dissolved in sea water. Your bone cells lay down a hard solid from substances dissolved in your blood.
- e.g. Starch + water → sugar, in your mouth and gut.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide + water, with light → sugar + oxygen, in a leaf.
- e.g. A hen builds the shell of each egg from calcium dissolved in her blood.
### The four kinds on the Earth and in space · *defined*
**Definition:** The same kinds of reaction shape rocks, air and the clouds between the stars.

Rain joins with carbon dioxide from the air and becomes a very weak acid. Over thousands of years this acid dissolves limestone and makes caves. Inside the cave, dripping water loses carbon dioxide, and solid limestone precipitates. It grows into stalactites, as in the Borra Caves in Andhra Pradesh. High above the Earth, sunlight splits oxygen molecules into single atoms. Each atom joins another oxygen molecule and makes ozone, which shields life from harsh sunlight. Astronomers have found more than 200 kinds of molecule in the clouds between the stars, including water. The Sun itself does not burn. Inside the Sun, the centres of atoms join. That is a nuclear reaction, and it changes the atoms themselves.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide + water → carbonic acid, in rain.
- e.g. Sunlight splits oxygen high in the air, and the atoms join to make ozone.
- e.g. Water and other molecules form on dust grains in clouds between the stars.

## Mostly agreed
- In every chemical reaction the atoms are kept. None is made and none is lost. *How we know:* Antoine Lavoisier weighed reactions in sealed glass vessels in the 1770s and 1780s. The total weight was the same before and after. Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier recorded and drew the experiments.
- Burning is a reaction with oxygen. A burning thing takes something in from the air. *How we know:* Metals such as tin and mercury become heavier when they are heated in air. Lavoisier showed that the extra weight equals the weight of the air that was used up.
- Acid curdles milk by cancelling the charge on the casein clusters, so that they stick together. *How we know:* Dairy scientists measure the charge on casein clusters as acid is added. The charge falls to zero at about pH 4.6, and the casein then comes out of the milk.
- Substances react in fixed amounts, so a reaction stops when one reactant is used up. *How we know:* The French chemist Joseph Proust analysed many compounds in the 1790s. Each compound always held the same elements in the same proportions by weight, however it was made.
- Breaking bonds takes energy in, and making bonds gives energy out. *How we know:* Chemists measure the heat of reactions in an insulated cup with a thermometer. Setting plaster warms the cup. Baking soda splits only while heat is supplied.
- Many reactions can run in both directions. The amounts and the conditions decide which way they go. *How we know:* Claude-Louis Berthollet saw a well-known swapping reaction running backwards at the salt lakes of Egypt in 1799. In 1864 Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage in Norway turned this into an exact rule.

## How we found out
**Torbern Bergman in Sweden, Claude-Louis Berthollet and Joseph Proust from France, and Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage in Norway, Bergman's tables came out in 1775. Berthollet was in Egypt from 1798 to 1799, and published in 1801 and 1803. Guldberg and Waage published in 1864..** For a long time, chemists thought that each substance has a fixed liking for every other substance. A substance that likes a partner more would pull it away from a substance that likes it less. In 1775 the Swedish chemist Torbern Bergman printed large tables of these likings. By this idea, a swap could go only one way. In 1798 the French chemist Claude-Louis Berthollet went to Egypt. At the Natron Lakes he saw crusts of soda at the edges of the salt water. The ground there was limestone. In his laboratory, soda and a dissolved lime salt always swapped parts, and made limestone and common salt. At the lakes, salt and limestone were slowly making soda. The swap was running backwards. Berthollet reasoned that the huge amount of salt was driving it. Amounts matter, as well as likes. He published the idea in 1801 and 1803. He also claimed that substances join in any proportions. Joseph Proust showed that this part was wrong. In 1864 Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage in Norway turned Berthollet's idea into an exact rule.  
*What people believed before:* That each substance has fixed likes, so a swapping reaction can go only one way. Berthollet saw the same swap running backwards at the Natron Lakes, where one substance was present in a huge amount.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Burning is a splitting. A burning thing gives off a substance called phlogiston and leaves ash behind.** (Georg Stahl, a German chemist, in about 1700, and most chemists for the next 70 years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Wood turns into a small heap of light ash, so it seems to have lost something.  
  Against: Metals get heavier when they burn. Lavoisier showed that burning things join with oxygen from the air. The gases that wood gives off were not being weighed.
- **Each substance has fixed likes and dislikes, so a swapping reaction can go only one way.** (Torbern Bergman, a Swedish chemist, in 1775.; *rejected*)  
  For: In the laboratory, the same swap gave the same products every time.  
  Against: Berthollet saw the same swap running backwards in Egypt, where one reactant was present in a huge amount. Amounts matter as well as likes.
- **Milk that has curdled has always gone bad.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Milk left out for too long turns sour and lumpy, and it can make people ill.  
  Against: Fresh hot milk curdled with lemon juice is paneer, and it is safe. Old milk curdles because bacteria have made acid in the milk. The curdling is the same reaction, but the source of the acid is different.
- **If you keep adding one reactant, you keep getting more product.** (A common first guess.; *rejected*)  
  For: Two lemons give more juice than one, so more lemon should give more curds.  
  Against: The amount of product is set by the reactant that runs out first. Once the casein is used up, extra lemon juice only makes the whey more sour.
- **Every reaction belongs to exactly one of the four kinds.** (A simple reading of school books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Most reactions that children meet fit one pattern well.  
  Against: Burning hydrogen is also a joining. The fizz of vinegar and baking soda is a swap followed by a split. The kinds are a way of sorting that people made.
- **Sorting reactions by pattern is less useful than sorting them by what moves between the substances.** (Many working chemists.; *debated*)  
  For: Sorting by moving electrons or moving hydrogen explains why reactions happen, and covers reactions that fit no simple pattern.  
  Against: The four patterns can be seen without knowing about electrons, and they help a learner guess the products. Both ways of sorting are in use.

## Roots
In this unit you sorted reactions into four kinds: joining, splitting, swapping and burning. Here we ask what is the same under all four.

**Deep truth.** In The Pan and the Lid, heat broke bonds in baking soda, and one powder became three substances. In Powder and Water, plaster and water made new bonds and became one hard solid. In Soap and Sour, old bonds broke and new bonds formed in the same bottle. So every reaction, whatever its kind, is made of the same two steps: bonds break and bonds form.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort reactions into kinds, and then we treat the kinds as separate things. The kinds are patterns that people noticed. Under every pattern, the same two steps are happening.  
*Evidence:* In Four Plates, every reaction was built by pulling some seeds apart and pushing some together. The burning plate needed both steps. In the card game Join, Split, Swap or Burn, three cards fit two kinds at once. That happens because every kind is made of the same two steps.

- *who am i:* The calcium in your bones was once in milk, in grass, in soil and in rock. It has changed partners many times, and for now it is part of you.
- *what is life:* A living being keeps thousands of joining, splitting and swapping reactions going at once. A clam building a shell and a child digesting lunch are both doing chemistry.
- *what is this world:* About ninety kinds of atom are found in nature. Breaking bonds and making bonds is enough to turn them into milk, stone, flame and you.

A seashell, a lump of lime, a plastered wall and a stalactite all hold calcium atoms, with different partners. Reactions move atoms from one substance to another, and nothing is added or lost on the way. Many sages taught that the many things of the world are forms of one source. The science here says something close and plain. All substances are built from the same few kinds of atom. Old bonds break and new bonds form, and that is how one substance becomes another.

- **Empedocles, fragment 17 (Diels-Kranz B17), about 450 BCE:** “δίπλ’ ἐρέω· τοτὲ μὲν γὰρ ἕν ηὐξήθη μόνον εἶναι / ἐκ πλεόνων, τοτὲ δ’ αὖ διέφυ πλέον’ ἐξ ἑνὸς εἶναι / πῦρ καὶ ὕδωρ καὶ γαῖα καὶ ἠέρος ἄπλετον ὕψος,” The Greek thinker Empedocles said that all things are made of a few lasting 'roots'. At one time they grow together into one, and at another time they grow apart into many. (This is joining and splitting, said 2,400 years ago. His four roots were earth, water, air and fire, which are not the elements of chemistry. But his idea that change is lasting parts coming together and apart is what chemists later found.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** “यथा सोम्यैकेन मृत्पिण्डेन सर्वं मृन्मयं विज्ञातं स्यात् / वाचारम्भणं विकारो नामधेयं मृत्तिकेत्येव सत्यम्” Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that by knowing one lump of clay, everything made of clay is known. The different shapes are names. What is real in all of them is the clay. (In a reaction the atoms stay and only their arrangement changes. Chemistry adds one thing that the verse does not say. The new arrangement really behaves in a new way, because curds do not act like milk.)
- **Dhammapada, verse 277:** “Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā,” The Buddha teaches that all things that are put together are impermanent. (Every substance in this unit could be changed by some reaction. Even a hard plaster wall is slowly joining with carbon dioxide from the air.)
- Science (settled): In a chemical reaction no atom is made or lost. Lavoisier showed this in the 1780s by weighing reactions in sealed vessels.
- Science (settled): Chemists can now follow a bond as it breaks, using laser flashes far shorter than a billionth of a second. Ahmed Zewail won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1999 for this work.
- Science (idea): Some scientists think that life began as reactions that kept each other going, before there were any cells.
- Other view, Many chemists who teach and predict reactions: The kinds are more than names. A joining and a swapping behave differently, and knowing the kind tells you what the products will be. It is true that every reaction is bonds breaking and forming. But that is too general to help anyone at the laboratory bench.
- Practice, One breath, one reaction (3 min): Sit still and breathe in slowly. The oxygen in this breath will react with food inside your cells. Hold it for a count of three. Now breathe out slowly on to your palm. Your breath is warm and damp. The warmth, the water and the carbon dioxide were made by reactions in your cells. Do this five times, and notice each breath going in and coming out.
- Ask: The calcium in a shell becomes lime, then plaster on a wall, then stone again. Is it the same thing all the way through?
- Ask: If every reaction is bonds breaking and bonds forming, why do curds, flames and rust look so different?
- Ask: The atoms in your body change partners every second. What stays the same in you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Milk turns into curds and whey. Is the milk gone, or is it still there in another form? (hint: Every atom of the milk is still in the bowl, just joined in a new way. So what is it that has changed?)
- *deep:* Burning fits two kinds at once. So are the four kinds found in nature, or made by people? (hint: Think of sorting clothes by colour or by owner. Is one way the true way?)
- *deep:* Gypsum can be split into plaster and joined into gypsum again and again. Can every reaction be undone? (hint: Could you turn curds and whey back into milk? Could you turn smoke back into wood? A tree actually does something close to that, because it builds wood from the carbon dioxide in the air.)
- *big:* Your body splits food and joins the pieces into you. Are you a thing, or reactions that keep going? (hint: A candle flame keeps the same shape, yet the wax and the air going through it change every second. Are you a little like that flame?)
- *big:* People burned seashells into lime for thousands of years before anyone knew about atoms. Did they understand the reaction? (hint: They knew exactly what to do and what would happen. Is that the same as knowing why?)
- *big:* Burning fuel cooks food and moves buses. It also changes everyone's air. Who should decide how much is burned? (hint: The people who burn the most fuel are not always the people harmed most by the change in the air.)

## Story: The Sack That Did Not Hiss
*Thangam, her grandmother Ponni Aaya, the mason Murugesan and their village on the Tamil Nadu coast are made up. Aaya is a Tamil word for grandmother. These things are real. People on India's coasts have burned seashells in small kilns to make lime for plaster for a very long time. A kiln's heat splits the shell into quicklime and carbon dioxide gas. Quicklime joins with water, hisses, swells and gets very hot. Quicklime kept in damp air slowly joins with water, and then with carbon dioxide from the air. After that it no longer heats up in water. Lime plaster on a wall hardens over months and years. It takes in carbon dioxide and turns back into the substance of shell. Quicklime burns skin and eyes, so children must never handle it.*

Six sacks of lime stood in Aaya's shed, and the mason's cart was already coming down the beach road.

Thangam was nine. Her grandmother, whom she called Aaya, burned seashells into lime, as her own mother had done. Every house in the village had been plastered with Aaya's lime. So had the old school, and the temple wall that faced the sea.

"Murugesan needs all six today," Aaya said. "The big house on the east street must be plastered before the wedding. Go and see that the sacks are tied."

Thangam knew how lime was made. She had helped to carry the clam shells up from the shore in baskets. She had watched Aaya pack the round kiln, one layer of shells and one layer of charcoal, up to the top. The kiln burned for a night and a day. The shells came out with the same shape, but they were white, light and easy to crumble. Aaya called them live lime. She never touched them with wet hands, and she tied a cloth over her nose when she filled the sacks.

Two of the sacks were from yesterday's burning. The other four were from the burning three weeks ago, before the rains came. It had rained every day since then, and the shed roof dripped in one corner. The air in the shed felt as wet as a towel.

One of the old sacks had split at the seam, and a few white lumps had rolled out. Thangam picked up a broken pot and put one lump in it. She liked this part. She stood back, as Aaya had taught her, and poured in half a coconut shell of water from the full length of her arm.

Nothing happened.

There was no hiss and no crack. No steam rose. The lump sat in the water like a stone from the road. Thangam waited, and then she touched the outside of the pot. It was cold.

She thought the water might be at fault, so she fetched more from the well. It made no difference.

Then she took a lump from one of yesterday's sacks, with the iron ladle and not with her fingers. She put it in a second pot and poured water from the same coconut shell.

The lump hissed at once. It cracked and swelled and fell apart, and steam rolled over the rim. When she held her palm near the pot, the heat pushed it away.

The two lumps had come from the same shells, the same kiln and the same water. One was alive, and one was not.

"Aaya!" she called. "The old lime has died."

Aaya came and looked into both pots. She did not seem surprised, and she did not explain. "Why do you think it died?" she asked.

Thangam looked at the dripping corner of the roof. She looked at the old sacks, which were damp and dark at the bottom.

"Live lime wants water," she said slowly. "When it gets water, it hisses and gets hot, and then it is finished. It can do that only once." She pressed her hand flat on an old sack. "These have been sitting in wet air for three weeks. They took the water out of the air, a little every day. They have already hissed, Aaya. They did it so slowly that nobody heard."

Aaya nodded. "And after the water, it takes something more from the air, a part of the air that you cannot see. Then it is on its way back to being shell. Half of that sack is shell dust now. If you plaster a wall with shell dust, the wall comes off on your hand."

The cart stopped outside. Murugesan the mason jumped down. He was a large, cheerful man, and he was in a hurry.

"Six sacks, Ponni Aaya. I have eight men waiting."

"Two sacks," said Thangam.

He laughed. "I can count six from here, little one."

"Four of them are dead," she said. "Watch." She set out two clean pots and put one old lump and one new lump in them. She gave him the coconut shell and made him stand back.

He poured water on the old lump, and nothing happened. He poured water on the new one and jumped away from the steam.

For a moment Murugesan said nothing. Then he said, "If I had put that on the wedding house, it would be dusting off the wall while the guests ate." He turned to her grandmother. "Who taught her to test it?"

"She asked the lime," said Aaya, "and the lime answered."

He took the two live sacks. He would come back for more in three days.

That evening Thangam helped to carry the four old sacks to the kiln. Aaya packed their dull lumps between layers of charcoal, just as she packed fresh shells.

"Will the fire bring them back?" Thangam asked.

"The fire will drive out the water and the unseen air. What is left will be live lime again. It has been round this road before."

Thangam went and stood by the wall of their own house. Aaya's mother had plastered it long before Thangam was born. She put her palm on it, and it was cool and hard.

Once it had been shells in the sea. The fire had made it into lime, and water had made it into paste. Then the air had come to it slowly, for years, and now it was almost shell again.

Behind her, Aaya lit the kiln.

**For the parent:** Thangam saw that live lime can join with water only once. The old sacks had slowly joined with water from the damp air. So nothing was left to react when she poured water on them. Heat could split them again and make them ready to join once more.

- Ask: Both lumps came from the same shells and the same kiln. Why did only one of them get hot in water?
- Ask: The kiln's fire and the water did opposite things to the lime. Which one was a splitting and which was a joining?
- Ask: Thangam tested a lump from each sack before the mason took them. What could you test before trusting an old packet of baking soda?

## Experiments
### e1 · Second Squeeze: which ran out, the milk or the lemon? (30 min)
- **question:** Lemon juice turns hot milk into curds and whey. If more lemon juice is added to the whey, do more curds form? If fresh milk is added to the whey, do curds form?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will more lemon juice make new curds in the whey? Will a spoon of fresh milk make new curds in the whey? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both tumblers get whey from the same bowl, 5 tablespoons each, at the same warmth. Tumbler A gets only lemon juice. Tumbler B gets only hot milk. Stir each one the same way, 5 times round. Wait 1 minute before you decide. Only one thing differs between the tumblers: what was added.
- **safety:** A grown-up heats the milk and pours everything that is hot. The child adds lemon juice and stirs with a long spoon. Keep hands away from the hot bowl.
- **record:** Draw a table with three columns: the tumbler, what you added, and what you saw after 1 minute. Then write how many tablespoons of milk tumbler B took before the milk stayed white.
- **results table:** Tumbler | What I added | What I saw after 1 minute
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Squeeze and strain the lemons. Mark the tumblers A and B. Ask for both guesses and the reasons, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will turn milk into curds and whey with lemon juice. Then we will add more lemon to some whey, and fresh milk to some whey. Which one will make new curds? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Lemon makes curds in milk. Will more lemon make curds in whey? Will more milk? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think more lemon juice will or will not make new curds in the whey? (good: More lemon should make more curds.; The whey has no milk left in it.; The curds have already come out.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect more lemon to make more curds. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat 300 millilitres of milk until it just starts to rise, then turn off the flame. Pour 200 millilitres into the steel bowl. Cover the pan to keep the rest hot. Your child adds lemon juice, 1 teaspoon at a time, and stirs gently for 10 seconds each time.  
  *Parent says:* Add one teaspoon of lemon juice and stir slowly. Tell me what you see. Count each teaspoon aloud. Stop when you see white lumps in a clear, pale liquid.  
  *Child mode:* Add lemon juice, one teaspoon at a time. Stir slowly. Count the teaspoons until curds appear.
  - Note: Most milk needs 2 to 4 teaspoons. The first teaspoon often seems to do nothing. When the whey turns clear and pale yellow-green, add 1 more teaspoon and stop. Write down the total.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Pour the bowl through the strainer into the second bowl. Keep the curds aside. Measure 5 tablespoons of the warm whey into tumbler A and 5 into tumbler B.  
  *Parent says:* The solid in the strainer is called curds. The thin liquid is called whey. Look at the whey closely. Do you see any milk left in it?  
  *Child mode:* Strain out the curds. Put 5 tablespoons of whey in A. Put 5 in B.
  - Note: Do the next step straight away, while the whey is still warm. Cold whey curdles new milk slowly and only a little.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add 2 teaspoons of lemon juice to tumbler A. Add 1 tablespoon of hot milk from the pan to tumbler B. Stir each 5 times. Watch both for 1 minute against the light, and write what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Tumbler A gets more lemon juice. Tumbler B gets fresh milk. Stir both, and watch for one minute. In which tumbler do new curds appear?  
  *Child mode:* Add lemon to A. Add milk to B. Stir. Which one makes new curds?
  - Note: Tumbler A usually stays clear. Tumbler B usually shows white flecks or soft lumps within a minute. If B only turns cloudy, the whey has cooled. Stand the tumbler in a bowl of hot water for 2 minutes.
  - Check: In Second Squeeze, why do both tumblers get whey from the same bowl? → **So only the added thing differs**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep adding hot milk to tumbler B, 1 tablespoon at a time. Stir, wait half a minute, and look for new curds. Count the tablespoons. Stop when the new milk stays white and makes no curds. Then add 1 teaspoon of lemon juice to B.  
  *Parent says:* Add another tablespoon of milk to B and look for new curds. Keep count. Tell me when the milk stays white. Now add one teaspoon of lemon juice. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Add milk to B, one tablespoon at a time. Count. Stop when the milk stays white.
  - Note: The milk usually stays white after 2 to 6 tablespoons in all. If curds still form when the milk in the pan is finished, write that down as your result. The last teaspoon of lemon juice should bring curds back.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. In the first bowl, which ran out, the milk or the lemon? In tumbler B at the end, which ran out?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Why did more lemon juice make no new curds in tumbler A? (good: The part of the milk that makes curds was used up.; All the casein had already come out as curds.; The lemon had nothing left to work on.)
  - Ask: Why did tumbler B stop making curds after a few spoons of milk? (good: The leftover acid in the whey was used up.; Each spoon of milk used some of the acid.; This time the lemon ran out, and not the milk.)
  - Note: The reason: curds are made from casein, the main milk protein, and the acid in lemon juice. In the first bowl, all the casein became curds, and some acid was left in the whey. So more lemon did nothing in A, and fresh milk made curds in B. Each spoon of milk used up some of the leftover acid. When the acid was gone, the milk stayed white. A reaction stops when one reactant is used up. Use the curds as paneer, and use the whey from the first bowl in dal or dough.
  - Think: A cook has curdled a pot of milk, but the whey still looks milky. Should she add more milk or more lemon juice?
### e2 · Powder and Water: which powder joins with the water? (35 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Plaster of Paris and fine sand are each mixed with water. Does either one get warm? Does either one go hard while it is still wet? What happens to each lump in water the next day?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will either cup get warm? Which paste will go hard first, and will it be hard before it is dry? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two paper cups of the same size. Put 6 level tablespoons of powder and 3 tablespoons of water in each. Use water from the same tumbler. Stir each one 20 times. Keep the cups side by side, away from the sun and the fan. Touch and poke both cups at the same times. Only the powder differs.
- **safety:** A grown-up measures and mixes the plaster. Do not breathe in the dust, and keep it away from eyes. Never put a finger or a hand into setting plaster, because it gets hot and grips the skin. Throw leftover plaster in the dustbin and never down a sink.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for 0, 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes. For each cup write warm or not warm, and soft or hard. On day 2, write what each lump did in water.
- **results table:** Minutes after mixing | Cup A, plaster: warm? hard? | Cup B, sand: warm? hard?
- **schedule:** day 1: Mix the two cups, then touch and poke them every 5 minutes for 20 minutes. (25 min); day 2: Tear off the cups, put both lumps in water for 5 minutes, and say what the results show. (10 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Spread newspaper. Mark the cups A and B. Show your child the two dry powders. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will mix each powder with the same amount of water. Will either cup get warm? Which will go hard first? Will it be hard before it is dry? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the two powders. Which will go hard with water? Will a cup get warm? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think a paste goes hard? (good: The water dries away.; The powder sticks together.; The powder and the water make something new.)
  - Note: Most children say that things go hard by drying. Do not correct this. The test will check it.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put 6 level tablespoons of plaster in cup A and 6 of sand in cup B. Add 3 tablespoons of water to each. Stir each one 20 times. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* I will mix the plaster, because the dust must not be breathed in. You stir the sand. Both cups get the same water and the same stirring.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up mixes the plaster. You stir the sand 20 times. Start the timer.
  - Note: The plaster should look like thick curd. If it is too stiff to stir, add 1 more tablespoon of water to both cups.
- **s3 (16 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes, your child holds a palm on the outside of each cup. Then they poke the top of each paste with a toothpick. Write warm or not warm, and soft or hard.  
  *Parent says:* Hold each cup for a slow count of five. Is one warmer? Now poke each one gently. Is the top wet? Is it hard? Tell me, and I will write it down.  
  *Child mode:* Every 5 minutes, hold each cup. Poke each top. Say warm or not, soft or hard.
  - Note: Cup A usually thickens in 5 to 10 minutes and feels warm between 10 and 20 minutes. The warmth is gentle with this amount, so compare the two cups with the same hand. Cup B stays cool and soft.
  - Check: In Powder and Water, the plaster goes hard while its top is still wet. What does that show? → **It did not harden by drying**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave both cups on a shelf until the next day. Ask the question below before you leave them.  
  *Parent says:* By tomorrow both cups will be dry. We will put both lumps in a bowl of water. What do you think each lump will do?  
  *Child mode:* Leave the cups until tomorrow. What will each lump do in water?
  - Wait: Leave both cups until the next day
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. Tear the paper cups away. Put both lumps in a bowl of water for 5 minutes. Then your child presses each lump with a thumb.  
  *Parent says:* Both lumps are dry now. Put them in the water and wait five minutes. Now press each one. Which one is still a solid block?  
  *Child mode:* Tear off the cups. Put both lumps in water. Wait 5 minutes. Press each one.
  - Note: The plaster stays a hard block. The sand lump falls apart, or turns back into wet sand. Chalk powder turns into a soft paste.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Where is the water that we put into the plaster cup?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What three things show that the plaster and the water made a new substance? (good: The cup got warm.; It went hard while it was still wet.; The lump stayed hard in water the next day.)
  - Ask: Where did the water in cup B go? (good: It dried into the air.; The sand is the same sand as before, so the water just dried away.)
  - Note: The reason: plaster of Paris joins with water. The two become one new solid called gypsum, and the water is locked inside it. Making the new bonds gives out heat, so the cup felt warm. This is a joining reaction. Sand does not react with water. The water only dried away, and the same sand was left. Stick chalk is often made of gypsum, so it is plaster that has already joined with water.
  - Think: A doctor wraps a wet plaster bandage round a broken arm. Why does the cast feel warm for a few minutes?
### e3 · Soap and Sour: what does vinegar do to soap? (25 min)
- **question:** Soap water makes foam when it is shaken. If vinegar is added, does the soap water still foam? Does dish wash liquid behave in the same way?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which bottle will have the tallest foam, and which the shortest? Will the liquid in any bottle change how it looks? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three clear bottles of the same size. Put 4 tablespoons of liquid in each. Add 2 teaspoons of water or vinegar to each. Shake every bottle 10 times, in the same way. Measure the foam at once with a ruler. Do the whole test twice. Bottles A and B differ only in what was added. Bottles B and C differ only in the kind of cleaner.
- **safety:** Keep soap water and vinegar away from eyes. Do not taste anything in this test. A grown-up grates the soap.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for each bottle. Write the foam height for try 1 and try 2. Write how the liquid looks: clear, cloudy or milky, with or without white bits.
- **results table:** Bottle | Foam height, try 1 (cm) | Foam height, try 2 (cm) | How the liquid looks
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set out the three bottles, the soap and the vinegar. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Bottle A will hold soap water and plain water. Bottle B will hold soap water and vinegar. Bottle C will hold dish wash liquid and vinegar. Which will foam most when we shake it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the three bottles. Each holds soap or dish wash, with water or vinegar. Which will foam most? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think vinegar will or will not change the foam? (good: Vinegar is sour and soap is soapy, so they may react.; Vinegar is mostly water, so nothing will change.; Lemon takes the soapy feel off my hands.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Grate the soap. Stir 1 level teaspoon into 200 millilitres of warm water until no bits are left. Put 4 tablespoons into bottle A and 4 into bottle B. Put 4 tablespoons of plain water and 3 drops of dish wash liquid into bottle C.  
  *Parent says:* Stir until the soap has dissolved. Now measure four tablespoons into A and four into B. Bottle C gets plain water and three drops of dish wash liquid.  
  *Child mode:* Stir the soap into warm water. Fill A and B. Put water and dish wash in C.
  - Note: Soap water is a little cloudy, and that is fine. If your tap water is hard, use filtered or boiled water, because hard water also reacts with soap.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add 2 teaspoons of plain water to A. Add 2 teaspoons of vinegar to B and 2 to C. Close the lids. Your child shakes each bottle 10 times and measures the foam at once. Write the heights and how each liquid looks.  
  *Parent says:* Add the water to A and the vinegar to B and C. Look at bottle B before you shake it. What do you see? Now shake each bottle ten times and measure the foam.  
  *Child mode:* Add water to A. Add vinegar to B and C. Shake each 10 times. Measure the foam.
  - Note: Bottle B usually turns milky white as soon as the vinegar goes in, and makes little or no foam. Bottle A foams well. Bottle C stays clear and still foams.
  - Check: In Soap and Sour, why does bottle A get 2 teaspoons of plain water? → **So every bottle holds the same amount**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Empty and rinse the bottles, and do the whole test once more. Then leave bottle B to stand for 5 minutes and look at the top of the liquid.  
  *Parent says:* We do the test twice, because one try can be a fluke. Now let bottle B stand. Look at the top of the liquid. What has collected there?  
  *Child mode:* Do the test once more. Then let B stand. What floats on top?
  - Note: White, greasy bits usually float to the top of bottle B. Let your child rub one between two fingers. It feels waxy, and it does not foam.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Is the white solid in bottle B still soap?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What shows that the white bits in bottle B are a new substance and not soap? (good: They do not dissolve in the water.; They do not make foam.; They feel waxy, not slippery.)
  - Ask: Why did the dish wash liquid still foam with vinegar? (good: It is a different substance from soap.; It does not make a solid with vinegar.)
  - Note: The reason: soap has a long fatty part joined to sodium. Vinegar is an acid, and it has hydrogen to give. The sodium and the hydrogen swap places. The fatty part with hydrogen is a waxy solid that does not dissolve, so it shows as white bits. A solid that forms like this is called a precipitate. The sodium stays dissolved as a salt. This is a swapping reaction. Dish wash liquid is made of other substances, which stay dissolved in sour water and in hard water.
  - Think: Soap leaves a grey ring on a bucket in homes with hard water. What might be happening to the soap?

## Activities
### a1 · The Pan and the Lid: can heat split a powder? (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the heating and lifts the hot lids. The heated powder is washing soda, which stings eyes and skin. Do not taste it, and wash hands after the activity.
- **record:** Draw three lids: from the empty pan, from the first heating and from the second heating. Draw the drops on each one.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the dry baking soda and the dry pan. Rub a little powder between your fingers to show it is dry. Ask the question below and write down the answer.  
  *Parent says:* This powder is dry, and the pan is dry. I will heat the powder under a cold lid. What do you think we will find under the lid? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The powder is dry. A grown-up heats it under a lid. What will be under the lid? Why?
  - Ask: Could anything come out of a dry powder when it is heated? (good: No, because it is dry.; Maybe smoke.; Maybe a gas, because baking soda makes cakes rise.)
  - Note: Most children expect nothing, or smoke. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat the empty dry pan on a low flame under the first cold lid for 1 minute. Lift the lid with a cloth and show the underside. Take the pan off the flame.  
  *Parent says:* First we heat the pan with nothing in it. Look under the lid. Is it wet or dry? We need this, so we know what an empty pan does.  
  *Child mode:* First the empty pan is heated. Look under the lid. Is it wet or dry?
  - Note: The lid should be dry. This is the comparison for the next step.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put 2 heaped teaspoons of baking soda in the pan. Cover it with the second cold lid. Heat on a low flame for 1 minute. Lift the lid with a cloth and show the underside.  
  *Parent says:* Now the powder goes in. I will heat it for one minute under a new cold lid. Look under the lid now. What do you see? Where did it come from?  
  *Child mode:* Now the powder is heated. Look under the lid. What do you see?
  - Ask: The powder was dry. So where did the drops come from? (good: Out of the powder.; The heat pushed water out of the baking soda.; The powder had water joined inside it.)
  - Note: A mist of small water drops usually covers the lid. A cold lid works best. If you see no drops, heat for 1 more minute.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat the powder without a lid for 5 more minutes, stirring now and then. Watch the powder as you stir. Then cover the pan with the third cold, dry lid for 1 minute. Lift the lid and look.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the powder while I stir. Does it move like a dry powder? Now I cover it with a new dry lid. Do you think drops will come again? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the powder as it is stirred. Then a new lid goes on. Will drops come again?
  - Note: While it splits, the powder shifts and bubbles gently, almost like a liquid, because gas is leaving it. The third lid should stay dry, or show very few drops.
  - Check: In The Pan and the Lid, heated baking soda makes water drops once and not the second time. Why? → **The baking soda was used up**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let the pan cool fully. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Is the powder in the pan still baking soda?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Note: The reason: heat splits baking soda into three substances. They are washing soda, water and carbon dioxide gas. The water left as steam and turned to drops on the cold lid. The gas left unseen, and made the powder bubble. The powder left in the pan is washing soda. This is a splitting reaction, and it needs heat. The same splitting makes a cake rise in the oven. Keep the washing soda for cleaning greasy vessels, or wash it away with plenty of water.
  - Think: A cake batter with baking soda rises in the oven. Which of the three products lifts the cake?
### a2 · Will It Burn? Seven things in a candle flame (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up lights the candle and holds every item in the flame with tongs. Tie back long hair and keep loose sleeves away. Drop anything that burns into the bowl of water. Do this near an open window.
- **record:** Draw a table with three columns: the thing, your guess, and what happened. Use the words 'burned' and 'did not burn'.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay out the seven things: paper, dry leaf, stone, steel spoon, salt, ash and water. Your child writes a guess for each one. Ask for the reason.  
  *Parent says:* I will hold each of these in a candle flame for ten seconds. Which ones will burn? What do you think the burning ones have in common? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the seven things. Guess which ones will burn. Why those?
  - Ask: What do you think makes a thing able to burn? (good: It is dry.; It came from a plant.; It is thin and light.)
  - Note: Children often say that dry things burn. Salt, ash and stone are dry too, so the test will check that idea.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Light the candle. With tongs, hold each thing in the flame for 10 seconds. Hold the salt, the ash and the water in the steel spoon. Drop anything burning into the bowl of water.  
  *Parent says:* Watch each one closely. Does it catch fire and keep burning on its own? Or does it only get hot? Tell me what you see.  
  *Child mode:* Watch each thing in the flame. Does it burn by itself? Or only get hot?
  - Note: The paper and the leaf burn. The stone, the spoon, the salt and the ash do not. The water boils away. Salt turns the flame yellow, but the salt itself does not burn. The spoon gets a black coat of soot, which comes from the candle.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Blow out the candle. Sort the seven things into two groups. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. What do the paper and the leaf share that the others lack?  
  *Child mode:* Sort the things into two groups. What do the burning ones share?
  - Note: The reason: burning is a reaction between a fuel and oxygen. Paper and leaves were made by plants. They are built from carbon and hydrogen, which join with oxygen and give out heat. Water is hydrogen that has already joined with oxygen. Stone and ash are also joined with oxygen already. Salt is sodium joined with chlorine. A thick steel spoon carries the heat away. Very fine steel wool can burn.
  - Check: Why does ash not burn in a candle flame? → **Its atoms have already joined with oxygen**
  - Think: Water is made of hydrogen and oxygen, and hydrogen burns very well. So why does water put a fire out?
### a3 · Four Plates: build one reaction of each kind (20 min)
- **record:** For each plate, write the word equation. Then write how many atoms of each kind there were before and after.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set out the seeds and say which atom each one stands for. Show that seeds placed touching each other are joined by bonds, and make one molecule.  
  *Parent says:* Each seed is one atom. Seeds that touch are joined by bonds, and together they are one molecule. One chana touching two moong is a water molecule. Build one for me.  
  *Child mode:* Each seed is an atom. Touching seeds are joined. Build one water molecule.
  - Note: Water is one oxygen and two hydrogens. Check that your child puts the chana in the middle.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Plate 1, joining. Build carbon dioxide: 1 rajma touching 2 chana. Build water beside it. Then push all six seeds into one group, with the rajma in the middle. Plate 2, splitting. Build that same group of six, and pull it apart into water and carbon dioxide.  
  *Parent says:* On plate one, carbon dioxide and water join into carbonic acid, which is in soda. How many molecules before? How many after? On plate two, do the reverse.  
  *Child mode:* Plate 1: push two molecules into one. Plate 2: pull one molecule into two.
  - Ask: In the joining, did the number of atoms change? Did the number of molecules change? (good: The atoms stayed at six.; The molecules went from two to one.; Nothing was added or lost.)
  - Note: Carbonic acid is 1 carbon, 3 oxygens and 2 hydrogens. It splits again when soda goes flat.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Plate 3, swapping. Build an acid: 1 moong touching the peppercorn. Build a base: the groundnut touching 1 chana, which touches 1 moong. Now let the moong of the acid and the groundnut change places.  
  *Parent says:* This acid is hydrogen joined to chlorine. A base is the opposite of an acid, and it can cancel an acid. This base is sodium joined to oxygen and hydrogen. Swap the acid's hydrogen with the sodium. What two things have you made?  
  *Child mode:* Plate 3: build the acid and the base. Swap the moong and the groundnut. What did you make?
  - Ask: What are the two products on plate 3? (good: Groundnut with peppercorn, which is sodium with chlorine. That is salt.; A chana with two moong. That is water.)
  - Note: The products are table salt and water. This is the reaction between stomach acid and one kind of antacid.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Plate 4, burning. Build methane, the gas in piped cooking gas: 1 rajma touching 4 moong. Build 2 oxygen molecules: 2 chana touching, twice. Rearrange them into 1 carbon dioxide and 2 waters. Count the atoms before and after.  
  *Parent says:* Methane is the fuel. It burns with two oxygen molecules. Take everything apart and build carbon dioxide and water. Did the methane split? Did anything join with oxygen?  
  *Child mode:* Plate 4: build methane and two oxygens. Rebuild them as carbon dioxide and two waters.
  - Note: Before: 1 carbon, 4 hydrogens and 4 oxygens. After: the same 9 atoms. The methane came apart, and its atoms joined with oxygen. So this one burning reaction is a splitting and a joining at the same time.
  - Check: On the burning plate of Four Plates, 9 seeds are used before the reaction. How many after? → **9**
  - Think: On every plate you broke some bonds and made new ones. Was there any plate where you only did one of these?

### c1 · The Sharpest Coin Print: find the best plaster mix (35 min)
- **problem:** Plaster of Paris joins with water and sets hard. With too much water the print is weak and blurred. With too little, the paste sets before it takes the shape. Find the mix that copies a coin most sharply.
- **your turn:** Now make your own best print. Choose the amount of water from your results, and add one idea of your own. Say why you think it will give a sharper print.
- **constraints:** Use 2 level tablespoons of plaster for every mix.; Change only the amount of water.; Press the same kind of coin, the same way, into every mix.; Lift the coins after 20 minutes, and judge every print in the same way.; A grown-up mixes the plaster. No finger goes into wet plaster.
- **general:** Mix 2 tablespoons of plaster with 1 tablespoon of water. This is the usual mix, like thick curd.; Rub oil on each coin so that it lifts off cleanly.; Press a coin in gently after 1 minute, and leave it still.
- **creative:** Tap the cup on the table ten times before pressing, to bring air bubbles to the top.; Sprinkle the powder on to the water and let it sink before stirring, as plaster workers do.; Press a leaf, a shell or a key in place of the coin, and compare the fine lines.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Mark the cups 1, 2 and 3. Rub a drop of oil on each of the 3 coins. Ask which mix your child expects to win, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Cup one gets two tablespoons of water. Cup two gets one. Cup three gets half. Which will copy the coin best? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three mixes: runny, medium and stiff. Which will copy the coin best? Why?
  - Ask: What might go wrong with too much water, or with too little? (good: Too much water may stay soft.; Too little may set before the coin goes in.; A stiff paste may not fill the small letters.)
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put 2 level tablespoons of plaster in each cup. Add 2 tablespoons of water to cup 1, 1 tablespoon to cup 2 and half a tablespoon to cup 3. Stir each 20 times. After 1 minute, your child presses one oiled coin into each.  
  *Parent says:* I will mix, and you will press the coins. Press each one gently until it is level with the top. Then leave it and do not move it.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up mixes. You press one oiled coin gently into each cup.
  - Note: Press the coin into cup 3 first, because the stiff mix sets soonest. Use three coins of the same kind, such as three 2-rupee coins.
- **s3 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wait 20 minutes. Your child touches the outside of each cup now and then. Then lift the coins, tip out each disc and compare the prints. Try to read the year. Scratch the back of each disc with a fingernail.  
  *Parent says:* Which cup feels warmest? Now look at the three prints. In which one is the writing clearest? Which disc is hardest to scratch?  
  *Child mode:* Wait 20 minutes. Compare the three prints. Which can you read? Which is hardest?
  - Note: The middle mix usually gives the sharpest and hardest print. The runny mix is slow, soft and chalky. The stiff mix is lumpy, with gaps.
  - Check: A plaster mix has too much water. What happens to the extra water? → **It is left over and weakens the cast**
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child chooses the best amount of water and adds one idea of their own. Make one more print and compare it with the best of the three.  
  *Parent says:* Now make your own best print. What will you change? Tell me why before you start.  
  *Child mode:* Make your own best print. Change one thing. Say why first.
  - Note: Praise a clear reason as much as a sharp print. Throw all leftover plaster in the dustbin, never in the sink.

## Worksheets
### The four kinds of reaction (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What happens to the atoms in a chemical reaction?  
   Options: They get new partners / They are destroyed / They turn into other atoms / They stop moving  
   **Answer:** They get new partners. *Why:* A reaction breaks some bonds and makes new ones. Every atom is still there afterwards, joined to new partners.
2. (match) Match each kind of reaction to its pattern.  
   **Answer:** Joining → Two substances become one; Splitting → One substance becomes two or more; Swapping → Substances exchange parts; Burning → A fuel reacts with oxygen. *Why:* Count the substances before and after. Two into one is joining, and one into two is splitting. An exchange is swapping. A fuel with oxygen is burning.
3. (fill) A solid that forms inside a liquid during a reaction is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['precipitate']. *Why:* A precipitate forms when one product of a reaction does not dissolve in water. Curds in milk are a precipitate.
4. (truefalse) Plaster of Paris goes hard only because its water dries away.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Plaster joins with the water and makes a new solid. It is hard while it is still wet, and it stays hard in water.
5. (sort) Sort each reaction. Is it a joining or a splitting?  
   **Answer:** plaster of Paris and water become gypsum → Joining; iron, oxygen and water become rust → Joining; quicklime and water become slaked lime → Joining; heated baking soda becomes three substances → Splitting; electricity turns water into hydrogen and oxygen → Splitting; a heated seashell becomes quicklime and a gas → Splitting. *Why:* In a joining, there are fewer substances after the reaction than before. In a splitting, there are more.
6. (mcq) Heat turns baking soda into washing soda and two other substances. Which two?  
   Options: Water and carbon dioxide / Oxygen and salt / Smoke and ash / Hydrogen and oxygen  
   **Answer:** Water and carbon dioxide. *Why:* The water leaves as steam and can be caught on a cold lid. The carbon dioxide leaves as a gas you cannot see.
7. (fill) The thin, pale liquid left after curds come out of milk is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['whey']. *Why:* Whey is mostly water, with milk sugar and minerals. The curds hold most of the protein and fat.
8. (truefalse) Burning needs oxygen.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Burning is a reaction between a fuel and oxygen. A flame goes out when the oxygen around it runs low.
9. (mcq) Vinegar is added to soap water. What do you see?  
   Options: The water turns milky white / The foam gets taller / A flame appears / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** The water turns milky white. *Why:* Soap and vinegar swap parts. One product is a waxy solid that does not dissolve, so the water turns milky.
10. (order) Lemon juice is stirred into hot milk. Put what happens in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The acid cancels the charge on the casein clusters', 'The clusters stop repelling each other', 'The clusters stick together', 'Curds separate from the whey']. *Why:* The charge keeps the clusters apart. Once the acid cancels it, they can stick together and come out as a solid.
11. (write) More lemon juice is added to whey, and no new curds form. Explain why in two sentences.  
   **Answer:** Curds are made from casein and acid. All the casein in the milk had already become curds, so the extra lemon juice had nothing to react with.. *Why:* A strong answer names the reactant that was used up, the casein. It says that a reaction needs every reactant.
12. (write) A cup of setting plaster feels warm. Where does the heat come from?  
   **Answer:** The plaster and the water join and make new bonds. Making bonds gives out energy, and we feel it as heat.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a reaction is happening, and that making new bonds gives out energy.
13. (draw) Draw a swapping reaction with four shapes. Show the two substances before and the two substances after.  
   **Answer:** Before: two pairs of joined shapes, for example a circle with a square and a triangle with a star. After: the same four shapes in new pairs, such as the circle with the star and the triangle with the square.. *Why:* A strong drawing uses the same four shapes before and after. Only the partners change.
14. (perspective) Is burning a splitting or a joining?  
   **Answer:** Any view can be argued well. The first child is right that the fuel comes apart. The second is right that oxygen is taken in. The chemist joins the two views.. *Why:* A strong answer uses evidence, such as the need for oxygen or the gases given off. It also answers another view fairly.
15. (mcq) Which of these is a fuel?  
   Options: Candle wax / Water / Sand / Ash  
   **Answer:** Candle wax. *Why:* Wax is made of carbon and hydrogen, which can join with oxygen. Water, sand and ash are already joined with oxygen.
### Why each kind happens (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why do many splitting reactions need heat or electricity?  
   Options: Breaking bonds takes energy / Heat adds new atoms / Heat makes substances heavier / Cold substances have no bonds  
   **Answer:** Breaking bonds takes energy. *Why:* To split a substance, bonds must break, and that takes energy in. Heat and electricity supply the energy.
2. (mcq) Why do casein clusters stay spread out in fresh milk?  
   Options: They carry the same charge and repel / They are too heavy to move / The milk is too cold / They are dissolved like sugar  
   **Answer:** They carry the same charge and repel. *Why:* Charges of the same kind repel. Each cluster pushes the others away, so they stay spread through the milk.
3. (mcq) A candle under a glass goes out while wax is still left. Which reactant ran low?  
   Options: Oxygen / Wax / Carbon dioxide / Water  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* Burning needs fuel and oxygen. The glass keeps new air out, so the oxygen runs low and the flame stops.
4. (truefalse) In a swapping reaction, some atoms are destroyed.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The parts only change partners. Every atom that was there before is there afterwards.
5. (truefalse) Water does not burn, because its hydrogen has already joined with oxygen.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Water is what hydrogen becomes when it burns. It has no more joining with oxygen left to do.
6. (odd) Three of these are joining reactions. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Iron rusting / Plaster setting / Quicklime meeting water / Soda going flat  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Rusting, setting plaster and quicklime with water each make one substance from two or more. Soda goes flat when carbonic acid splits.
7. (count) Methane (1 carbon atom, 4 hydrogen atoms) burns with 4 oxygen atoms. How many atoms are in the products?  
   **Answer:** 9. *Why:* 1 + 4 + 4 = 9 atoms before the reaction. A reaction keeps every atom, so there are 9 atoms in the products.
8. (sort) Sort each reaction. Does it give out heat, or does it need energy put in?  
   **Answer:** plaster setting → Gives out heat; a candle burning → Gives out heat; quicklime joining with water → Gives out heat; baking soda splitting → Needs energy put in; seashells splitting in a kiln → Needs energy put in; water splitting into two gases → Needs energy put in. *Why:* Making new bonds gives out energy, so joining and burning give out heat. Breaking bonds takes energy, so these splitting reactions need heat or electricity.
9. (order) Lime for plaster starts as a seashell. Put the steps in order.  
   **Answer:** ["A kiln's heat splits the shell into quicklime and a gas", 'The quicklime joins with water', 'The wet lime is spread on a wall', 'The lime joins with carbon dioxide from the air and hardens']. *Why:* The shell is split once and then joins twice, first with water and then with carbon dioxide. At the end it is the same substance as the shell.
10. (write) Whey stops making curds after a few spoons of fresh milk. Which reactant ran out? How do you know?  
   **Answer:** The acid ran out. The new milk still had casein in it, yet it stayed white. One more teaspoon of lemon juice made curds again.. *Why:* A strong answer names the acid, and gives evidence: the milk stayed white, or more lemon brought the curds back.
11. (write) A dry powder is heated, and water drops appear on a cold lid above it. What does this tell you?  
   **Answer:** The water came out of the powder. Heat split the powder into new substances, and one of them was water. An empty pan gives no drops, so the water did not come from the pan.. *Why:* A strong answer says the powder was split, and uses the empty pan as the comparison.
12. (write) Dish wash liquid still foams when vinegar is added, but soap does not. Explain the difference.  
   **Answer:** Soap swaps parts with the acid in vinegar and becomes a waxy solid that does not dissolve or foam. Dish wash liquid is made of different substances, which stay dissolved in sour water.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the soap became a new substance, and that the dish wash liquid did not.
13. (perspective) Milk left out overnight has turned sour and lumpy. Is that the same change as making paneer?  
   **Answer:** Any view can be argued well. The reaction that makes the lumps is the same in both. What differs is where the acid came from, and what else is in the milk.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the reaction from the question of safety, and uses what acid does to casein.
14. (label) Label three parts of this word equation: plaster of Paris + water → gypsum.  
   **Answer:** ['Reactants: plaster of Paris and water, before the arrow', "Arrow: it means 'become'", 'Product: gypsum, after the arrow']. *Why:* Reactants are what you start with, and they go before the arrow. Products are what the reaction makes, and they go after it.
15. (fill) The reactant that runs out first, and so stops the reaction, is called the ____ reactant.  
   **Answer:** ['limiting']. *Why:* The limiting reactant sets how much product is made. Whatever is left of the other reactants stays unused.
### Testing ideas about reactions (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Hydrogen burns with oxygen and makes only water. Which two kinds does this reaction fit?  
   Options: Burning and joining / Burning and splitting / Swapping and splitting / Joining and swapping  
   **Answer:** Burning and joining. *Why:* A fuel reacts with oxygen, so it is burning. Two substances become one, so it is also a joining.
2. (mcq) Vinegar and baking soda swap parts and then fizz. Which step makes the gas?  
   Options: Carbonic acid splits / Two gases join / The vinegar burns / Water swaps with air  
   **Answer:** Carbonic acid splits. *Why:* The swap makes carbonic acid. Carbonic acid then splits into water and carbon dioxide gas, which is the fizz.
3. (mcq) People once said a burning thing loses a substance. Which finding does not fit that idea?  
   Options: Metals get heavier when they burn / Wood leaves a little light ash / Flames give out heat / Candles get shorter  
   **Answer:** Metals get heavier when they burn. *Why:* A thing that only loses something cannot get heavier. Burning metals gain weight because they join with oxygen.
4. (mcq) A mix holds twice the water that its plaster can join with. What happens to the extra water?  
   Options: It is left over / It becomes plaster / It makes the cast harder / It turns into heat  
   **Answer:** It is left over. *Why:* Plaster joins with a fixed amount of water. When the plaster is used up, the rest of the water is left over and dries away.
5. (truefalse) A swapping reaction can run backwards if one of its products is present in a huge amount.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Berthollet saw this at the salt lakes of Egypt. Amounts help to decide which way a reaction runs.
6. (truefalse) The Sun shines because hydrogen burns with oxygen inside it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Inside the Sun, the centres of hydrogen atoms join. That is a nuclear reaction, and it needs no oxygen.
7. (match) Match each everyday change to its kind of reaction.  
   **Answer:** A cake rises in the oven → Splitting; A grey ring forms on a soapy bucket → Swapping; A brass lamp slowly goes dark → Joining; An incense stick glows → Burning. *Why:* Baking soda splits and gives off gas. Soap swaps sodium for calcium. Brass joins with gases from the air. Incense is a fuel reacting with oxygen.
8. (count) 200 millilitres of milk needs 3 teaspoons of lemon juice to curdle. How many teaspoons does 1 litre need?  
   **Answer:** 15. *Why:* 1 litre is 1000 millilitres, which is 5 times 200. So it needs 5 times 3 teaspoons, which is 15.
9. (fill) Sunlight splits oxygen molecules high in the air. The atoms join other oxygen molecules and make ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ozone']. *Why:* Ozone has three oxygen atoms. A layer of it high in the air shields living beings from harsh sunlight.
10. (write) Many people think a paste hardens only by drying. Describe a test that could show a reaction instead.  
   **Answer:** Mix the paste and check it while it is still wet. If it is hard and wet at once, it did not harden by drying. Also feel for warmth, and put the dry lump in water to see if it stays hard.. *Why:* A strong answer names something to observe that drying cannot explain: hard while wet, warmth, or staying hard in water.
11. (write) A cook adds lemon juice to hot milk, but the whey still looks milky. What should be added, and why?  
   **Answer:** More lemon juice. Milky whey still has casein in it, so the acid ran out first. More acid will turn the rest of the casein into curds.. *Why:* A strong answer works out which reactant is left over, and adds the one that ran out.
12. (write) Fresh quicklime gets hot in water. Quicklime kept in damp air for weeks does not. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Quicklime gets hot because it joins with water. In damp air it joins slowly with water from the air. After some weeks none is left to join, so nothing happens in water.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the joining has already happened, so the reactant is used up.
13. (write) Your cells and a candle both turn fuel and oxygen into carbon dioxide and water. Name one difference.  
   **Answer:** A candle does it fast, with a flame and a lot of heat at once. Cells do it in many small steps, with no flame, and keep much of the energy to use.. *Why:* A strong answer compares speed, flame or steps. It does not say the two are unrelated.
14. (perspective) Are the four kinds of reaction found in nature, or are they only a way of sorting?  
   **Answer:** Any view can be argued well. The patterns are real enough to predict products. Some reactions still fit two kinds, and other ways of sorting also work.. *Why:* A strong answer gives an example reaction as evidence, and says what the sorting is useful for.
15. (draw) Draw milk before and after lemon juice, as a very strong microscope would show it. Show the casein clusters.  
   **Answer:** Before: many small round clusters spread evenly, each with the same charge sign. After: the clusters stuck together in a few big lumps, with clear liquid between them.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the same clusters before and after. They are spread out at first and clumped afterwards.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which pattern shows a joining reaction?  
   Options: A + B → AB / AB → A + B / AB + CD → AD + CB / A → A  
   **Answer:** A + B → AB. *Why:* In a joining, two substances become one. The second pattern is a splitting and the third is a swapping.
2. (mcq) One substance breaks into two or more new substances. What kind of reaction is this?  
   Options: Splitting / Joining / Swapping / Burning  
   **Answer:** Splitting. *Why:* One substance becoming two or more is a splitting. Heated baking soda does this.
3. (mcq) What does burning need, besides a fuel and some heat to start?  
   Options: Oxygen / Water / Carbon dioxide / Salt  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* Burning is a fuel reacting with oxygen. Water and carbon dioxide are what burning makes.
4. (mcq) Why does lemon juice make curds in hot milk?  
   Options: Acid makes casein clusters stick together / Lemon juice freezes the milk / Lemon juice dries the milk / Lemon pulp turns white  
   **Answer:** Acid makes casein clusters stick together. *Why:* The acid cancels the charge that keeps the casein clusters apart. They stick together and come out as curds.
5. (mcq) Plaster of Paris sets hard while it is still wet. What happened to the water?  
   Options: It joined with the plaster / It dried into the air / It sank to the bottom / It turned into a gas  
   **Answer:** It joined with the plaster. *Why:* Plaster and water join and make one new solid. The water is locked inside the solid.
6. (mcq) A reaction stops, although plenty of one reactant is left. What is the most likely reason?  
   Options: The other reactant is used up / The atoms have worn out / The products have escaped / The bowl is too large  
   **Answer:** The other reactant is used up. *Why:* A reaction needs every reactant. When one is used up, the reaction stops, however much of the other is left.
7. (mcq) Soap and vinegar make a white solid and a dissolved salt. What kind of reaction is this?  
   Options: Swapping / Joining / Splitting / Burning  
   **Answer:** Swapping. *Why:* Two substances exchange parts and make two new substances. The sodium and the hydrogen change places.
8. (truefalse) In a chemical reaction, atoms are made and destroyed.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Atoms are only rearranged. The same atoms are there before and after.
9. (mcq) Which of these will not burn in a candle flame?  
   Options: Ash / Paper / A dry leaf / A cotton thread  
   **Answer:** Ash. *Why:* Ash is what is left after burning. Its atoms have already joined with oxygen.
10. (mcq) Dry baking soda is heated, and drops of water collect on a cold lid. Where did the water come from?  
   Options: From the baking soda as it split / From the steel of the lid / From the air in the kitchen / From the gas cylinder  
   **Answer:** From the baking soda as it split. *Why:* Heat splits baking soda into washing soda, water and carbon dioxide. The water leaves as steam and turns to drops on the cold lid.
11. (mcq) Which reaction fits two kinds at once?  
   Options: Hydrogen burning to make water / Sand drying in the sun / Sugar dissolving in tea / Ice melting  
   **Answer:** Hydrogen burning to make water. *Why:* Hydrogen burning is a fuel reacting with oxygen, and it is two substances becoming one. The other three are not chemical reactions.
12. (mcq) What is a precipitate?  
   Options: A solid formed inside a liquid / A gas that escapes from a liquid / A liquid that dissolves a solid / A powder that is stirred into water  
   **Answer:** A solid formed inside a liquid. *Why:* A precipitate is a new solid that appears inside a liquid, because one product does not dissolve.

## Applications
- Paneer and cheese makers add an acid or an enzyme to milk. They measure it, so that all the casein becomes curds and little acid is left over.
- Doctors set broken bones in casts of plaster of Paris. The wet bandage joins with water and turns hard within minutes.
- Bakers use baking soda because heat splits it and gives off carbon dioxide. The gas makes cakes, dhoklas and biscuits rise.
- Water works add alum to muddy water. The alum forms a fluffy precipitate, which traps the fine mud and sinks. The clear water is drawn off the top.
- Shampoos and dish wash liquids were invented because soap swaps parts with hard water and makes scum. These cleaners do not make scum, because they stay dissolved in hard water.
- Engines and stoves are built to mix fuel with enough oxygen. With too little oxygen, burning makes soot and a poisonous gas called carbon monoxide.

## Spotted around us
- A sweet shop in Kolkata, where cooks split hot milk with sour whey to make chhena for rasgulla and sandesh.
- A grey or white ring round the inside of a bathroom bucket. It is soap that has swapped parts with the calcium in hard water.
- White crust inside a kettle or a water heater. Heating breaks down a substance that was dissolved in hard water. That makes limestone, which does not dissolve, so it forms a solid crust.
- A doctor's plaster cast on an arm, or a plaster Ganesha drying before Ganesh Chaturthi in Mumbai or Hyderabad.
- The blue flame of a gas stove in any kitchen. The yellow flames of oil lamps at Karthigai Deepam in Tamil Nadu.
- Stalactites in the Borra Caves in Andhra Pradesh and the Mawsmai Cave in Meghalaya. Each one is a precipitate that grew drop by drop.

## Open questions
- **Which chemical reactions first led to life on the Earth?** No one was there to see it, and the first reactions left no fossils. Scientists test ideas in the laboratory, but no one has yet made life from simple chemicals.
- **How exactly are the casein clusters in milk built?** The clusters are too small and too soft to see clearly. Dairy scientists have several models of them, and they still disagree.
- **Can we split carbon dioxide cheaply, using only sunlight, as a leaf does?** Leaves do it at ordinary temperatures. Laboratories can copy parts of it, but the methods are still slow or costly.
- **Can a computer predict the products of any reaction before it is tried?** Computers do well for small molecules. For large ones, the sums are too big, and the answer often has to be found by experiment.
- **What happens during the instant when a bond is half broken?** Very short laser flashes can now catch molecules in the middle of reacting. Only a few simple reactions have been followed in this way.

## Connected maths
- In Second Squeeze, 200 millilitres of hot milk needed 3 teaspoons of lemon juice to make curds. How many teaspoons would 600 millilitres of the same milk need? **9 teaspoons.**. 600 millilitres is 3 times 200 millilitres. So it holds 3 times as much casein, and needs 3 times 3 teaspoons, which is 9. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In Second Squeeze, a tumbler with 5 tablespoons of whey made curds from 4 tablespoons of milk. The 5th tablespoon stayed white. What would happen with 10 tablespoons of the same whey? **About 8 tablespoons of milk would make curds, and the 9th would stay white.**. 10 tablespoons of whey hold twice as much leftover acid as 5. Each tablespoon of milk uses up the same amount of acid. So twice as much milk can be curdled: 2 times 4 is 8. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
- A chemist heats 84 grams of baking soda until it has all split. 53 grams of washing soda are left in the pan. How many grams left the pan as water and carbon dioxide? **31 grams.**. No atom is lost in a reaction. So the products together weigh 84 grams. 84 − 53 = 31 grams left as steam and gas. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- In The Sharpest Coin Print, the best mix was 2 tablespoons of plaster to 1 tablespoon of water. A sculptor uses 9 tablespoons of water. How much plaster does she need? **18 tablespoons of plaster.**. The plaster is always 2 times the water. 2 times 9 is 18. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In Soap and Sour, the foam in bottle A was 4 cm and then 4 cm. In bottle C it was 3 cm and then 3.5 cm. In bottle B it was 0.5 cm both times. Can you be sure that A foams more than C? Can you be sure that A foams more than B? **Yes, you can be sure that A foams more than B. No, you cannot be sure that A foams more than C from these tries alone.**. A's foam is 8 times as tall as B's, and both tries agree. A and C differ by only 0.5 to 1 cm. C's own two tries differ from each other by 0.5 cm. So the gap between A and C is not much bigger than the gap between two tries of the same bottle. That gap could be chance. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- The table shows the four reactions from Four Plates. Which number is the same before and after on every plate? Which number can change? **The number of atoms never changes. The number of molecules can change.**. Atoms: 6 and 6, 6 and 6, 5 and 5, 9 and 9. Molecules: 2 become 1 in the joining, and 1 becomes 2 in the splitting. In the swapping and the burning the count of molecules happens to stay the same. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
