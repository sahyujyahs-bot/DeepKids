# Homeostasis: how your body keeps its warmth and water steady
`sci.body.keeping-balance.L3`

**Summary.** On a hot May afternoon and on a cold winter morning, the inside of your body stays at about 37 degrees. Keeping the inside steady like this is called homeostasis. When you get hot, you sweat, and the drying sweat carries heat away. When you get cold, you shiver, and your working muscles make heat. When you are low on water, you feel thirsty. A small part of your brain, the hypothalamus, keeps checking and correcting all of this, day and night.

**Intro.** *Put the back of your hand on your cheek. Now put it on a steel plate or a tile floor. Which feels warmer? The inside of your body stays about the same warmth in hot summer and cold winter. How does it do that?* Think about the hottest day you remember, maybe in May. Now think about a cold winter morning, when you wanted to stay under the blanket. The air around you changed a lot between those two days. But the inside of your body stayed almost exactly the same warmth, about 37 degrees. A steel plate left in the sun gets hot. Left outside on a winter night, it gets cold. You are not like the plate. Something inside you keeps checking your warmth and fixing it. That checking and fixing has a name: homeostasis.

## Terms
- **Homeostasis**: Homeostasis means keeping the inside of your body steady and just right. Say it like this: ho-mee-oh-STAY-sis. *e.g.* Your body stays about 37 degrees warm, on a hot day and on a cold day.
- **Body temperature**: Body temperature is how warm the inside of your body is. *e.g.* Most people are about 37 degrees inside. A fever is when it goes higher.
- **Thermometer**: A thermometer is a tool that measures how hot or cold something is. *e.g.* A doctor puts a thermometer under your arm or in your mouth.
- **Degrees Celsius**: Degrees Celsius are the units we use to measure warmth. We write them like this: °C. *e.g.* Water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius. You are about 37 degrees Celsius.
- **Sweat**: Sweat is the salty water your skin lets out when you are hot. *e.g.* After playing cricket in the sun, your forehead is wet with sweat.
- **Sweat gland**: A sweat gland is a tiny tube in your skin that makes sweat. You have millions of them. *e.g.* Your palms and the soles of your feet have lots of sweat glands.
- **Evaporation**: Evaporation is when a liquid slowly turns into a gas and goes into the air. It is how wet things dry. *e.g.* Wet clothes on the line dry because the water evaporates.
- **Shivering**: Shivering is when your muscles shake by themselves to make heat. *e.g.* Coming out of a cold river, your teeth chatter and your body shakes.
- **Goosebumps**: Goosebumps are little bumps on your skin. Tiny muscles pull each hair up straight when you are cold. *e.g.* When a cold wind blows on your arm, the hairs stand up.
- **Blood vessels**: Blood vessels are the tubes that carry blood around your body. *e.g.* When you are hot, the blood vessels near your skin open wide. That is why your cheeks go red.
- **Thirst**: Thirst is the feeling that tells you that your body needs water. *e.g.* After running in the park, you want a big glass of water.
- **Hypothalamus**: The hypothalamus is a small part of your brain, about the size of an almond. It watches your warmth and water and decides what to do. *e.g.* When your blood gets too warm, your hypothalamus tells your skin to sweat.
- **Set point**: A set point is the 'just right' number your body tries to stay at. *e.g.* Your warmth set point is about 37 degrees. An AC also has a set point, like 26 degrees.
- **Feedback loop**: A feedback loop is a circle of steps. Something notices a change, something decides, and something acts to undo the change. Then it checks again. *e.g.* Too hot, so you sweat. You cool down, so the sweating stops.
- **Sensor**: A sensor is something that notices a change, like heat, cold or saltiness. *e.g.* Tiny nerve endings in your skin are sensors for hot and cold.
- **Dehydration**: Dehydration means your body has lost more water than you have drunk. *e.g.* On a long, hot bus trip with no water, you might feel tired, dizzy and have a headache.
- **Humidity**: Humidity is how much water is already in the air. *e.g.* Kochi in the monsoon is very humid. Wet clothes take ages to dry.
- **Heatstroke**: Heatstroke is when the body gets dangerously hot and cannot cool itself down. It needs a doctor quickly. *e.g.* In a strong heatwave, people working in the sun can get heatstroke.
- **ORS**: ORS means oral rehydration solution. It is clean water with the right amount of salt and sugar mixed in. It puts back water and salt the body has lost. *e.g.* When someone has loose motions, a doctor may say to sip ORS.

## Explanations
### What homeostasis is · *defined*
**Definition:** Homeostasis is the way a living thing keeps their inside steady and just right, even when the outside keeps changing.

Your body needs many things to stay just right. It needs the right warmth, the right amount of water, the right amount of salt and the right amount of sugar in the blood. If any of these goes too high or too low for long, the body cannot work well. So the body keeps checking each one, and fixing it when it drifts. Keeping balance takes small corrections all the time. Riding a bicycle is similar: you stay upright by steering a little left, then a little right.
- e.g. On a hot day you sweat, and on a cold day you shiver. Both keep you near 37 degrees.
- e.g. When you have lost water, you feel thirsty, and you drink.
- e.g. When you have not eaten for a while, you feel hungry. Food brings your blood sugar back up.
### The body's thermostat: notice, decide, act · *defined*
**Definition:** A feedback loop is a circle of three steps. A sensor notices a change. A control centre decides what to do. The body acts to undo the change.

Tiny sensors in your skin feel warmth. They send messages along nerves to your hypothalamus. That is a small part of your brain, about the size of an almond. It also feels the warmth of the blood flowing through it. It compares your warmth with the set point, the 'just right' number, which is about 37 degrees. If you are too warm, it tells your skin to sweat. If you are too cold, it tells your muscles to shiver. As you get back to just right, the sweating or shivering stops. Scientists call this negative feedback, because the action works against the change. An AC with a thermostat works the same way.
- e.g. An AC set to 26 degrees switches on when the room is warmer, and off when the room is cool enough.
- e.g. A geyser heats water and switches off when the water is hot enough.
- e.g. In your overhead water tank, a floating ball rises as the tank fills and shuts the tap off.
### Sweating cools you because the sweat dries · *defined*
**Definition:** Sweat cools you when it evaporates, which means when it dries into the air. Drying takes heat away from your skin.

Sweat itself is not cold. It comes out as warm as you are. The cooling happens when it dries. Water is made of tiny particles called molecules, always jiggling. The fastest ones fly off into the air first. They carry heat energy away with them. So the water left behind, and your skin under it, gets cooler. That is why a fan feels so good on a sweaty back. The moving air helps the sweat dry faster. Sweat that drips off onto the floor does not cool you much, because it did not dry on your skin. A grown-up working hard on a hot day can sweat more than a litre in an hour. Drinking water puts it back.
- e.g. A wet hand held in front of a fan feels colder than a dry hand.
- e.g. A clay matka keeps water cool because a little water seeps out and dries on its outside.
- e.g. After a bath, you feel chilly until you dry off.
### Shivering, goosebumps and curling up · *defined*
**Definition:** When you are too cold, your body makes more heat and tries to keep the heat it has. Shivering makes heat. Goosebumps and curling up help to keep it.

Muscles make heat whenever they work. When you shiver, your muscles tighten and loosen very fast, by themselves. You do not move anywhere, but you make a lot of heat. Shivering can make a few times more heat than sitting still. At the same time, tiny muscles in your skin pull each hair upright. These are goosebumps. In a furry animal like a cat or a monkey, standing fur traps a warm layer of air. Our body hair is too thin to trap much, so goosebumps are a leftover from our furry relatives. Curling up into a ball also helps. It means less of your skin touches the cold air, so less heat escapes.
- e.g. Your teeth chatter when you come out of a cold swimming pool.
- e.g. A cat fluffs up their fur on a cold winter morning.
- e.g. Babies have a special brown fat that makes heat without shivering.
### Red cheeks and white fingers: blood carries the heat · *defined*
**Definition:** Your blood carries heat around your body. Your body sends more blood near the skin to lose heat, and less blood near the skin to keep heat.

Blood flows through tubes called blood vessels. The blood is warm, because it passes through your busy insides. When you are hot, the tiny vessels near your skin open wider. More warm blood flows near the skin, and heat escapes into the air. That is why your face goes pink after running. When you are cold, those vessels squeeze narrow. Less blood goes to your skin, fingers and toes. Your middle stays warm, but your fingers turn pale and cold. The body is choosing to keep heat around your heart and brain, where it matters most.
- e.g. Your cheeks go red after a game of kho-kho.
- e.g. Your fingers go white and stiff after holding an ice lolly for a long time.
- e.g. An elephant flaps their huge ears. They are full of blood vessels, and the flapping cools the blood.
### Thirst and the water balance · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body keeps the amount of water inside you just right. Thirst tells you to drink more, and your kidneys decide how much water to let go as pee.

More than half of your body is water. Every day you lose some of it, in sweat, in pee and even in your breath. When your blood has too little water, it becomes a little too salty. Sensors in your hypothalamus notice this saltiness. They make you feel thirsty. They also send a chemical message to your kidneys, which says 'save water'. Then your pee becomes less and darker. When you have had plenty to drink, your pee is pale and you go to the toilet more often. Thirst is your body asking for water before things go wrong.
- e.g. On a hot day you drink more and pee less.
- e.g. Pale yellow pee usually means you have had enough to drink. Dark yellow pee usually means drink more.
- e.g. Breathe on a cold mirror. The mist is water leaving your body.
### Why sticky monsoon days feel hotter · *defined*
**Definition:** Humidity is how much water is already in the air. When the air is very humid, sweat dries slowly, so it cools you less.

Remember, sweat cools you only when it dries. Air can hold only so much water. In Rajasthan in May, the air is very dry, and sweat dries almost as soon as it comes out. In Chennai or Kochi during the monsoon, the air is already full of water. Your sweat sits on your skin and drips off. You feel sticky and hot, even at the same temperature. That is why weather reports sometimes give a 'feels like' temperature. It adds in the humidity.
- e.g. Wet clothes dry fast on a dry, windy day, and slowly on a damp monsoon day.
- e.g. You feel sticky in a crowded, closed room.
- e.g. A desert cooler works well in Hyderabad in May, but poorly in Chennai in July.
### How hot is too hot for a person? · *debated*
If the air is very hot and also very humid, sweat can hardly dry, so it stops cooling you. Then your body cannot get rid of its heat, and it heats up. This is dangerous, and it is called heatstroke. Scientists measure this danger with a 'wet-bulb' temperature, which mixes heat and humidity into one number. They agree there is a limit. They do not agree exactly where it is, and it is different for babies, old people, sick people and people working hard.
- e.g. Heatwaves in India and Pakistan already come close to the danger limit on some days.
- e.g. People working outside, like builders and farmers, are most at risk.
- e.g. Shade, water, rest and wet cloths help the body keep up.
- **Steven Sherwood and Matthew Huber, 2010:** They worked out a limit on paper. A wet-bulb temperature of 35 degrees, even for a healthy person resting in the shade with plenty of water, would be the edge of survival.
- **Daniel Vecellio, Larry Kenney and others, 2022:** They tested young, healthy people in a hot, humid room. Their bodies could not keep steady at a lower number, about 31 degrees wet-bulb. So the real limit may be lower than the paper limit.
- **Colin Raymond and others, 2020:** They found weather stations, including in Pakistan and around the Persian Gulf, where the 35 degree level had already been reached for an hour or two.
### Why about 37 degrees? · *idea*
Nobody knows for sure why mammals like us keep ourselves at about 37 degrees. Being warm costs a lot of food. Lizards do not keep themselves warm, so they can eat far less than a mouse of the same size. So why pay so much? Scientists have ideas, but no final answer.
- e.g. Birds are even warmer than us, about 40 to 42 degrees.
- e.g. A lizard warms up by lying on a hot rock in the sun.
- e.g. A lizard is slow on a cold morning until the sun warms them. A mouse can run fast even on a cold night.
- **Aviv Bergman and Arturo Casadevall, 2010:** Their idea is that 37 degrees is too hot for most fungi, the moulds and yeasts, to grow in us. It is also not so hot that we must eat all day. They worked out that about 36.7 degrees balances the two best.
- **Many other biologists:** They think being warm let early mammals stay quick and active at night and in the cold. The exact number might partly be an accident of history.
### Does the body react, or does it predict? · *debated*
The usual story is that the body waits for a change, then fixes it. But the body often acts before the change arrives. When you drink water, your thirst goes away in about a minute. That is long before the water has reached your blood. Your mouth and throat tell your brain 'water is coming'. In the same way, your body gets ready for food at the smell of dinner.
- e.g. Thirst goes away soon after you drink, before the water is used.
- e.g. Your mouth waters when you smell parathas being cooked.
- e.g. Many people's bodies start warming up just before they wake in the morning.
- **Walter Cannon, 1926, and most textbooks:** The body has set points, and it corrects any drift away from them. This is homeostasis.
- **Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer, 1988:** They said the brain mostly predicts what the body will need, and changes the set points to match. They called this allostasis.
- **Zachary Knight and his team, 2016:** They found thirst cells in the brains of mice that switch off within moments of drinking. The cells were predicting, before the blood changed.
### Staying steady costs energy · *defined*
**Definition:** A living body uses energy, all the time, to stay different from its surroundings. Without that energy, it would slowly drift to the same warmth as the room.

Leave a tumbler of hot filter coffee on the table. It cools down until it is the same warmth as the room. That is what non-living things do. A hot thing always gives heat to a colder thing, until they are equal. You do not drift like the coffee. That is because your cells are always using food to make heat. Even sitting still, a grown-up gives off about as much heat as an old-fashioned 100 watt bulb. The physicist Erwin Schrödinger wrote about this in 1944, in a small book called What Is Life? He said living things stay ordered by taking in energy and giving out heat. So keeping your warmth steady needs food every day.
- e.g. A tumbler of hot filter coffee always cools to the room's warmth.
- e.g. A crowded room gets warm from all the people's body heat.
- e.g. Food is your fuel. A child eats every day partly to keep warm.
### Every living thing keeps balance · *defined*
**Definition:** Homeostasis is not only for humans. All living things, even one tiny cell, keep their inside different from their outside, and just right.

Each of your cells has tiny pumps in their skin. They push one kind of salt out and pull another kind in, all day long. A big share of the energy you use at rest goes into these pumps. Bacteria do it too. Animals have many clever ways to stay just right. Dogs have sweat glands only on their paw pads, so they pant to cool down. Camels let their warmth go up by about 6 degrees during a hot day, to save water from sweating. Storks and some vultures wee on their own legs to cool off. Honeybees keep the middle of their hive at about 35 degrees. They fan with their wings, and spread water drops to cool it. Some sacred lotus flowers even keep themselves warm, at about 30 to 35 degrees, on cool nights.
- e.g. A dog pants with their tongue out on a hot afternoon.
- e.g. A camel crosses the desert with little water.
- e.g. A lotus flower in a pond can be warmer than the air around it.
### Does the Earth keep its own balance? · *debated*
The Earth has stayed warm enough for liquid water, and for life, for more than three billion years. That is surprising, because the Sun was dimmer long ago. Some scientists think the Earth has its own slow thermostat. Others think part of the reason is luck.
- e.g. Rain slowly washes carbon dioxide out of the air into rocks. This happens faster when the Earth is warm, so it cools the Earth down.
- e.g. Tiny sea plants and trees change the air, and so change the weather.
- e.g. Today, people are adding carbon dioxide faster than any slow thermostat can fix.
- **James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, 1970s:** Their Gaia idea says living things and the Earth together act like one self-regulating system that keeps conditions good for life.
- **James Walker, Paul Hays and James Kasting, 1981:** They described a slow rock thermostat that works even without life. Warmth speeds up rain and rock weathering, which pulls carbon dioxide out of the air. It takes hundreds of thousands of years.
- **Many Earth scientists today:** They agree there are balancing loops, but they say the Earth is not a body with a set point. Some loops even push it further away from balance, like melting ice making the Earth darker and warmer.
### Is 'normal' body temperature changing? · *debated*
In 1868, a German doctor called Carl Wunderlich said normal body temperature is 37 degrees. He used about a million readings from about 25,000 people. But today, many studies find healthy people are a little cooler, closer to 36.6 degrees. Each person also has their own normal. You are cooler in the early morning and warmer in the late afternoon.
- e.g. Your temperature under your arm is a little lower than in your mouth.
- e.g. You are slightly warmer after running, and cooler when you have just woken up.
- e.g. Old people are often a little cooler than children.
- **Myroslava Protsiv, Julie Parsonnet and others, 2020:** They studied old army records and modern records in the USA. People's temperatures have dropped by about half a degree in 150 years. They think less illness and comfortable homes may be the reason.
- **Other scientists:** They say the old thermometers and methods were different, so some of the change may be in the measuring, not in the bodies.

## Mostly agreed
- The inside of a healthy human body stays at about 37 degrees, whatever the weather. *How we know:* Doctors have measured millions of people in hot and cold countries. Their inside temperature stays within about one degree of 37, unless they are ill.
- Sweat cools the body when it dries on the skin. *How we know:* A wet thing in moving air cools faster than a dry one. You can measure this with two tumblers and a thermometer. In very humid air, where sweat cannot dry, people get hotter.
- Shivering makes heat. *How we know:* Scientists measure how much oxygen people use. A shivering person can use several times more, because their muscles are working hard and making heat.
- A part of the brain called the hypothalamus controls warmth, thirst and hunger. *How we know:* In animal experiments, warming or cooling a tiny spot in this part of the brain makes the animal sweat, pant or shiver, even when the rest of their body is normal.
- Thirst and the kidneys keep the body's water balanced. *How we know:* When people drink less, their pee becomes darker and smaller in amount. When they drink more, their pee becomes paler and larger in amount. Blood tests show the saltiness of the blood stays nearly the same.
- Losing too much water and salt can be dangerous, and ORS can save lives. *How we know:* In 1971, in refugee camps near the Bangladesh border, Dr Dilip Mahalanabis and his team gave ORS to people with cholera when there was not enough drip fluid. Far fewer people died than usual. Since then ORS has saved many millions of lives.

## How we found out
**Walter Cannon (the dry mouth idea), and the scientists who tested it, including Bengt Andersson, 1918 to 1953.** In 1918, the American scientist Walter Cannon, who later named homeostasis, said that thirst was simply the feeling of a dry mouth. When the body is low on water, he said, it makes less spit, the mouth dries, and so we want to drink. It sounded sensible, but other experiments did not fit. In the 1930s, scientists studied dogs whose swallowed water ran out through a small tube in the neck, so it never reached the stomach. Their mouths were wet, yet they drank, stopped for a short while, and then drank again and again. In 1953, the Swedish scientist Bengt Andersson put a tiny drop of very salty water into the hypothalamus of some goats. The goats' mouths were not dry, and their bodies were not short of water. Within a minute or two they began to drink, and they drank several litres. So thirst starts in the brain, when it senses that the blood has become too salty. A wet mouth stops thirst only for a short time.  
*What people believed before:* That thirst is only the feeling of a dry mouth.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Normal body temperature is exactly 37.0 degrees for everyone.** (Many people, and many old books, following Carl Wunderlich's work in 1868.; *rejected*)  
  For: Wunderlich measured many thousands of people, and the average was close to 37.  
  Against: Each person has their own normal. It changes through the day. Many modern studies find an average a little below 37, closer to 36.6. So 37 is a rough guide, not an exact rule.
- **If you sweat a lot, you must be unfit or dirty.** (A common idea, and many advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sweat can smell when bacteria on the skin feed on it.  
  Against: Sweating is your body working well. Fit people often start to sweat sooner, because their bodies have learned to cool down quickly. Sweat itself has almost no smell.
- **Humans became such great sweaters so they could hunt by running animals down in the heat.** (Dennis Bramble and Daniel Lieberman, 2004.; *debated*)  
  For: Humans have millions of sweat glands and little fur. We can run long distances in the heat better than most animals. A few groups of hunters have done this kind of running hunt.  
  Against: Other scientists say running hunts were rare. Sweating may have helped early humans walk and work in hot open land, without any hunting.
- **You must drink exactly 8 glasses of water every day.** (A popular rule you may hear at school or on posters.; *debated*)  
  For: Many people do need about that much, especially in a hot country like India.  
  Against: In 2002, a scientist called Heinz Valtin looked for proof of this exact rule and did not find it. How much you need depends on your size, the weather, how active you are, and how much water is in your food. Thirst and pee colour are good guides for a healthy child.
- **The Earth is like one living body that keeps itself just right.** (James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, the Gaia idea, 1970s.; *debated*)  
  For: The Earth has stayed good for life for billions of years, even as the Sun got brighter. Living things do change the air and the weather.  
  Against: A body has a set point that it defends. The Earth does not seem to have one. Some Earth changes push it further from balance, not back towards it.
- **Health is a balance of three doshas, vata, pitta and kapha.** (Ayurveda, India's old system of medicine, still used by many families.; *minority*)  
  For: Like homeostasis, it says health is balance, and it pays close attention to heat, cold, food, water and sleep.  
  Against: Scientists have not found doshas as things that can be measured in the body. Science measures temperature, water, salt and sugar instead. Treatments must be tested carefully either way.

## Roots
We have been learning how the body keeps itself just right, by sweating, shivering and feeling thirsty. Now we ask what this steady balance tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** After jumping, your cheeks turned red. You did not decide to do that. A small part of your brain felt the heat and opened up the blood vessels in your skin. So your body checks and corrects its own warmth all day, without asking you.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of 'me' as the one who runs our body, because we choose to walk, eat and talk. But our warmth is kept by a loop that works without our noticing: sensors notice, the hypothalamus compares with about 37 degrees, and skin and muscles act.  
*Evidence:* In Jump and Notice, your cheeks went red and you could not sweat on purpose. In My Steady Inside, your face felt hot after running, but the thermometer under your arm rose less than one degree.

- *who am i:* You are a body that keeps itself near 37 degrees, every minute. Most of that work is done by parts of you that work without you noticing.
- *what is life:* All living things keep their inside just right while the outside changes. A cell, a beehive and a lotus flower all do it.
- *what is this world:* Your heat and water leave you all the time, into the air of the room. Food and drink from the world bring them back.

The same loop keeps balance at every size. One cell pumps salt to keep their inside right. Your hypothalamus keeps your whole body near 37 degrees. Honeybees fan their wings to keep their hive near 35 degrees. Each one notices a change and acts to undo it, so one simple pattern shows up in countless living things.

- **Bhagavad Gita 2.14:** Krishna says that cold and heat, pleasure and pain, come from the senses touching the world. They come and go. They do not last. Learn to bear them steadily. (The Gita is talking about the mind, not body warmth. But it uses the same example: heat and cold come and go, and something in you can stay steady while they do.)
- **Anguttara Nikaya 6.55, the Buddha's teaching to Sona:** Sona, a monk who used to play the vina, was trying far too hard. The Buddha asked him: if the strings are too tight, does the vina play well? If they are too loose? Only strings tuned just right make music. Balance your effort the same way. (Your body does not aim for as hot as possible or as cold as possible. It aims for just right, and keeps tuning, like a vina.)
- **Tao Te Ching 77:** The way of nature is like bending a bow. What is too high is pressed down, and what is too low is lifted up. Where there is too much, it takes away. Where there is too little, it adds. (That is how a feedback loop works. Too hot, and your body cools you. Too cold, and it warms you. Too little water, and you feel thirsty.)
- Science (settled): Warming or cooling a tiny spot in the hypothalamus makes an animal pant or shiver, even when the rest of their body is at its normal warmth. That spot works as the body's thermostat.
- Science (settled): In mice, thirst cells in the brain switch off within moments of drinking, long before the water reaches the blood. So the brain can correct a change before it has fully happened.
- Other view, Many biologists: They say the body keeps its balance by chemistry and nerve signals alone, the way a thermostat keeps a room cool. They see no need for any mind in it. Walter Cannon called his 1932 book The Wisdom of the Body, but they say he meant it only as a way of speaking.
- Practice, Cooling breath (3 min): Let's sit tall and still. Curl your tongue into a little tube, or if you can't, just open your lips a little. Breathe in slowly through your mouth. Feel how cool the air is on your tongue. Now close your mouth and breathe out slowly through your nose. Let's do that five times. Your tongue felt cool because the air was drying it, like sweat on skin. Now just sit and notice. Is your body hot, cold or just right? You didn't have to do anything to stay just right. Who is doing it?
- Ask: Your hypothalamus decides when you sweat, and you do not notice it deciding. Is that still you deciding?
- Ask: Who keeps you just right while you sleep?
- Ask: When your sweat dries into the air, is it still part of you?
- Ask: A lotus flower keeps itself warm on a cool night. What do you think the flower senses while it does that?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Sweat comes out as warm as you are. So how can it cool you down? (hint: Think about wet clothes drying on a line. Where does the water go, and what does it take with it?)
- *deep:* Why do you shiver even when you don't want to? (hint: Who decides to shiver? Is it you, or a part of you that works without asking?)
- *big:* If your body is always changing water, heat and atoms, are you the same 'you' as last year? (hint: Think of a river. The water is always new, but we still call it the same river.)
- *deep:* Why do you feel thirsty as a feeling, instead of your body just quietly fixing itself? (hint: What would happen if your body needed water, but you never felt like drinking?)
- *big:* Is 'just right' the same for everyone? (hint: Is your mother's comfortable room warmth the same as yours? Is your normal temperature the same as a friend's?)
- *big:* In a heatwave, who can cool down easily, and who cannot? (hint: Think about a person with an AC in an office, and a person carrying bricks on a building site.)
- *big:* A beehive keeps its middle at about 35 degrees, but no single bee is in charge. How can a hive keep balance with no boss? (hint: Each bee fans their wings or fetches water when the place where they stand gets too warm.)
- *big:* Does the Earth have a thermostat? (hint: Think of what rain, forests and oceans do to the air.)
- *deep:* When you have a fever, your body moves its 'just right' number up. Why might a body want to be hotter when it is ill? (hint: Many germs grow more slowly when it is warmer.)

## Story: The Pot That Sweats
*Imagined. The village, Ruksana, Imran and their mother are made up. It is true that a clay matka cools water because water seeps through and dries on its outside, and that heatwaves in western Rajasthan can pass 45 degrees.*

I am a pot. I am made of the brown earth of the riverbank, shaped on a wheel and baked in a fire. I sit in the corner of a small house at the edge of the desert, near Barmer, on a little stand of bricks. People call me the matka. I have no eyes, no ears and no heart. But I have a skin full of tiny holes, so small that you cannot see them. Through those holes, I feel everything.

Every morning, Ruksana fills me. She is ten, and she walks to the hand pump before the sun is up. She pours the water in carefully, so that it does not slop. Then she puts a steel plate on my head and a tumbler on the plate. All day long, the family comes to me. They lift the plate, dip the tumbler, and drink.

That May was the hottest May anybody could remember. The air shook above the sand. The goats lay flat in the thin shade of the khejri tree and would not get up. At noon the radio said forty-seven degrees, and then it said nothing at all, because the electricity went off. The fan in the ceiling slowed down, turned once more, and stopped.

Imran, Ruksana's little brother, was four. He did not like the heat. He did not like anything that day. He cried, and kicked his sheet off the charpai, and cried again. His cheeks were red as chillies. His hair stuck to his forehead in wet curls. Their mother had gone to the next village to help her sister, and she would not be back until the evening.

"Drink," said Ruksana. She dipped the tumbler into me and held it to his mouth. He drank a little, made a face, and pushed it away. "It's not cold," he said. "It's never cold. I want cold."

He was wrong, of course. My water is always cooler than the air. Nobody in that house ever asked why. They thought I was simply a cold sort of thing, the way a stone is hard. But I am not cold at all. I am clay, baked in a fire. Left empty in the sun, I would be as hot as a roof.

Here is my secret. A little of the water inside me creeps out through my tiny holes. It spreads over my outside in a thin, damp skin. And then the dry desert air drinks it. Each tiny bit of water that flies away takes a little heat with it. So I am always giving a little away, and that is why the rest stays cool. When the air is damp, in the monsoon, I do not work so well. But in May, with the hot dry wind blowing, I work better than ever.

Ruksana sat on the floor beside her brother and wiped her own face with her dupatta. She was hot too. She leaned back against me. And for the first time, she noticed. My side was cool. Not wet, not dripping, just cool and a little damp, like a forehead in the evening. She turned and put both palms flat on me. Then she put her cheek against me, and closed her eyes.

"Ammi says the matka sweats," she said, half to herself. "That's why it stays cool."

She sat up straight. She looked at her little brother, red and cross and dry-eyed now, too tired even to cry. His skin had been wet an hour ago. Now it looked dry and hot, and that frightened her more than the crying.

Ruksana took the thin cotton cloth from the shelf, the one Ammi used for rotis. She dipped it into me, deep, and wrung it out until it was only damp. She laid it over Imran's arms and his legs and the back of his neck. Then she picked up the hand fan made of palm leaves and began to fan him. Slowly, steadily, the way the dry wind fans me.

"What are you doing?" Imran mumbled.

"I'm making you into a matka," she said.

He almost laughed. She kept fanning. Each time the cloth began to dry, she dipped it again. She found the ORS packet that Ammi kept in the tin by the door. She mixed it into a tumbler of my water, exactly as the picture on the packet showed, and gave it to him in small sips. She did not make him gulp it. A sip, then fanning. A sip, then fanning.

The shadow of the khejri tree crept across the yard. Imran's cheeks went from chilli red to pink. His breathing slowed. Then, at last, small beads of sweat came back onto his forehead, and he fell asleep. Ruksana kept fanning, very gently, until her arm ached.

When their mother came home in the cool of the evening, she found the two of them asleep on the floor beside me. The damp cloth was still over Imran's legs. The palm fan was still in Ruksana's hand. Ammi put the back of her fingers on her son's forehead, then on her daughter's. She went to the neighbour's house to fetch the nurse, just to be safe. Then she sat down beside me, and dipped the tumbler, and drank for a long time.

People think that keeping cool means holding on to cold. It does not. I have never held on to anything. I stay cool because I let a little go, all the time, into the hot air. Ruksana understood that, that day, with her cheek against my side. I think she understood something else too, though I am only a pot, and I may be wrong. Her brother's body had been doing the same thing all along. It had only needed someone to help it.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the matka is cool not because it is a cold thing, but because water seeps out and evaporates from its surface, carrying heat away. Ruksana's turn is realising that sweat works the same way, and that her brother's body had become too hot and dry to cool itself. A damp cloth and fanning do what sweat does, and ORS puts back water and salt. In real life, a hot, dry, floppy child in a heatwave needs a doctor or nurse quickly.

- Ask: Why was the water in the matka cooler than the air, even though the matka is made of baked clay?
- Ask: What did Ruksana notice when she put her cheek against the matka?
- Ask: Why did Ruksana dip the cloth in water and fan her brother? What was she copying?
- Ask: Why do you think dry, hot skin frightened Ruksana more than the crying?
- Ask: The pot says it stays cool 'because it lets a little go'. What do you think it means?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Sweaty Tumbler Test (35 min)
- **question:** Does a wet cloth, like sweat on skin, cool something down faster than a dry cloth?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which tumbler will be cooler after 20 minutes: the one in the wet cloth or the one in the dry cloth? Or will they be the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, keep everything the same except the wet cloth. Use two tumblers that are the same. Pour the same amount of the same warm water in each. Use the same kind of cloth. Put both the same distance from the fan.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the warm water. It should feel comfortable to touch, like bath water, not hot. Keep fingers and the cloth away from the fan blades.
- **record:** Fill in the table every 5 minutes. Then work out how many degrees each tumbler dropped. Draw one bar for each tumbler to show the drop.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Wet cloth tumbler (degrees) | Dry cloth tumbler (degrees)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the two tumblers and the two cloths. 2. Ask your child to guess which will cool faster after 20 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Your skin gets wet with sweat when you are hot. Does being wet really help you cool down? Let's test it with two tumblers. Which one will be cooler after 20 minutes, the wet one or the dry one?  
  *Child mode:* You will test two tumblers of warm water. One wears a wet cloth, like sweat. One wears a dry cloth. Which will cool faster? Guess first.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Wet things feel cold.; When I come out of the bath I feel cold.; The fan dries the wet one.)
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the same amount of warm water into both tumblers, about three-quarters full. 2. Measure the warmth of each with the thermometer. 3. Write both numbers next to 0 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* First we measure where we start. Both should be nearly the same. We write them down next to zero minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to pour the warm water. Measure each tumbler with the thermometer. Write the numbers next to 0 minutes.
  - Note: Aim for about 40 degrees, comfortably warm, like a body or a bath. If they differ by a degree, that is fine. Write it down anyway.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wet one hanky in tap water and squeeze it until it stops dripping. 2. Wrap it around one tumbler. 3. Wrap the dry hanky around the other. 4. Put both in front of the fan, the same distance away. Switch the fan on.  
  *Parent says:* This wet cloth is like sweat on your skin. The dry cloth is like dry skin. The fan is like a breeze.  
  *Child mode:* Wet one cloth and squeeze it. Wrap it round one tumbler. Wrap the dry cloth round the other. Put both by the fan.
- **s4 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every 5 minutes, measure each tumbler and write the numbers in the table. Do this at 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Every 5 minutes, let's measure both. Which one is dropping faster?  
  *Child mode:* Every 5 minutes, measure both tumblers. Write the numbers. Which one is cooling faster?
  - Note: Put the thermometer in the water each time and wait until the number stops changing. Wipe it between tumblers. While you wait, touch both cloths. The wet one feels cold. A fever thermometer will not do, because most cannot read below about 32 degrees. On a humid monsoon day the difference is smaller, but it is still there. If you have no thermometer, a family member can touch each tumbler with their eyes closed and say which is cooler.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out how much each tumbler dropped. Take the 20 minute number away from the 0 minute number.  
  *Parent says:* Let's see how much each tumbler cooled. Start number take away end number.  
  *Child mode:* Take the end number away from the start number. That is how much each tumbler cooled.
  - Check: In the sweaty tumbler test, the wet cloth tumbler cooled more. Why? → **The water drying from the cloth carried heat away**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: What I think: being wet helps you cool down. What I saw: the wet cloth tumbler went from 40 to 31 degrees, and the dry one only went from 40 to 37. Why: the water dried in the fan's breeze and carried heat away. That is how sweat cools me.
  - Think: What do you think would happen on a sticky, humid monsoon day, when the wet cloth dries slowly?
### e2 · My Steady Inside: does running change my temperature? (25 min)
- **question:** Does my inside temperature change much when I run around, or does my body keep it steady?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. After 2 minutes of running on the spot, will your temperature under your arm go up a lot, a little, or not at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, measure the same way each time. Use the same thermometer, under the same arm, for the same length of time, until it beeps.
- **safety:** Run on the spot only for 2 minutes, somewhere you cannot slip. Wipe the thermometer with soap and water before and after.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then circle the biggest and smallest temperatures. How far apart are they?
- **results table:** When | Temperature under my arm (degrees) | How my face and hands feel | Sweaty? (yes or no)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wipe the thermometer. 2. Ask your child to guess how much their temperature will change after running.  
  *Parent says:* When you run, your face gets hot and red. But does the inside of your body get hotter too? Let's guess, then measure.  
  *Child mode:* When you run, you feel hot. Does your inside get hotter too? Guess: a lot, a little, or not at all?
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits still for 2 minutes. 2. Tuck the thermometer under their arm, tip in the armpit, arm pressed down. 3. Wait for the beep. 4. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Sit still. Squeeze your arm gently against your side. We wait for the beep.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Put the thermometer tip in your armpit. Squeeze your arm down. Wait for the beep. Write the number.
  - Note: Under the arm usually reads a little lower than the mouth, around 36 to 37 degrees. Any steady number in that range is normal.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child runs on the spot for 2 minutes. 2. Straight away, take their temperature again the same way. 3. Ask how their face and hands feel.  
  *Parent says:* Run on the spot, as fast as you like, for 2 minutes. Now quickly, thermometer under your arm again. How does your face feel?  
  *Child mode:* Run on the spot for 2 minutes. Then take your temperature again. Touch your face. Is it hot? Are you sweaty?
  - Ask: What is your body doing to cool down? (good: Sweating.; My cheeks are red, so blood is near my skin.; Breathing fast.)
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rests for 10 minutes and sips water. Read the story or a card while you wait. 2. Take the temperature once more.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's rest and drink some water. In 10 minutes we will measure again.  
  *Child mode:* Rest for 10 minutes. Sip some water. Then take your temperature one more time.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the three numbers together. Then ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Your face felt very hot, but your inside only changed a tiny bit. Tell me: what do you think, what did you see, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your three numbers. Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: my body keeps my inside steady. What I saw: my temperature went from 36.5 to 36.8 and back to 36.6, but my face felt very hot. Why: I sweated and my skin got red, so the heat got out.
  - Check: After running, your face felt hot, but your temperature under your arm went up by less than one degree. Why? → **Your body sweated and sent blood to the skin to get rid of the extra heat**
### e3 · The Matka Test: can a pot sweat? (20 min)
- **question:** Does water stay cooler in a clay matka than in a steel pot? And why?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. After 3 hours, will the matka water be cooler, the same, or warmer than the steel pot water? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same water for both, the same amount, and leave both in the same place, in the shade, side by side.
- **safety:** Use clean drinking water if anyone will drink it afterwards. A grown-up helps carry the full matka, because it is heavy.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the matka with little arrows showing water creeping out and drying away.
- **results table:** Pot | Start temperature (degrees) | Temperature after 3 hours (degrees) | How the outside feels
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Soak the matka in water for an hour beforehand. 2. Fill both pots with the same water. 3. Measure the warmth of each and write it down. 4. Cover both with plates.  
  *Parent says:* People say a matka keeps water cool. Is that true, or just a story? Let's test it against a steel pot.  
  *Child mode:* Fill a matka and a steel pot with the same water. Measure each one. Write the numbers. Cover them.
- **s2 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put both pots in the shade, side by side, where a little breeze blows.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait. The matka has tiny holes we cannot see. Watch what its outside looks like later.  
  *Child mode:* Put both pots in the shade. Now wait 3 hours.
  - Wait: Leave for 3 hours
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure the water in each pot again. 2. Feel the outside of each pot. 3. Write everything down.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure again. Now feel the outside of the matka. Feel the steel pot. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each pot again. Then feel the outside of each. Which one feels cool and damp?
  - Ask: Why is the outside of the matka damp? (good: Water creeps through the clay.; The pot is sweating.)
  - Note: On a dry, warm day the matka water is often several degrees cooler than the steel pot water. On a very humid day, the difference is small. That is a great result too, because it shows drying is the key.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then compare the matka with sweaty skin.  
  *Parent says:* How is the matka like your skin on a hot day? Tell me what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* How is the matka like your sweaty skin? Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Check: In the matka test, the matka water stayed cooler. What is the best reason? → **Water seeps out and dries on the outside, carrying heat away**
  - Think: Mohammed Bah Abba, a teacher in Nigeria, put a clay pot inside a bigger clay pot with wet sand between them. Vegetables kept fresh for many days. Why do you think it worked?

  - Ask: What did you notice? (good: My cheeks are red.; My forehead is wet.; I am breathing fast.; I feel hot and thirsty.)
  - Ask: What do you wonder? (good: Why do I go red?; Why do I sweat?; Who told my body to do that?)

### a2 · Cold Hands, Warm Hands (10 min)
- **safety:** Keep a hand in the cold water for one minute at most, and take it out sooner if it hurts. A grown-up checks the warm water is not hot.
- **record:** Write down how many seconds the colour took to come back on the cold hand, the other hand, and the warmed hand.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child presses the pad of a finger on their dry hand for 3 seconds, then lets go. 2. Count the seconds until the white spot is pink again. 3. Now they put the other hand in the cold water for one minute, then dry it.  
  *Parent says:* Press your fingertip hard for three seconds and let go. See the white spot? Let's count until it is pink again. Now put your other hand in the cold water for one minute.  
  *Child mode:* Press a fingertip for 3 seconds. Let go. Count until it is pink. Then put your other hand in cold water.
  - Note: On a warm hand the colour usually comes back in about 1 or 2 seconds. Count out loud: one thousand, two thousand.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press a fingertip of the cold hand for 3 seconds and let go. 2. Count until it is pink again. 3. Ask your child why it is slower.  
  *Parent says:* Now the cold hand. Press, let go, and count. Was it slower? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Press a cold fingertip. Let go. Count. Is it slower this time?
  - Ask: Why do you think the colour came back more slowly? (good: Less blood is going to the cold hand.; The blood tubes got narrow.)
  - Note: The cold fingertip usually takes clearly longer to turn pink. A cold hand may look pinker or blotchy rather than pale, and that is fine. The slow return of colour is what to look at. After your child has guessed, explain: in the cold, the tiny blood vessels in the skin squeeze narrow. Less warm blood flows there, so less heat escapes, and your body keeps its heat for your middle.
  - Check: In Cold Hands, Warm Hands, the cold fingertip took longer to turn pink after you pressed it. Why? → **The blood vessels in the cold skin had squeezed narrow, so less blood flowed there**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child puts the cold hand in the warm water for one minute, dries it, and does the press test again.  
  *Parent says:* Now warm it up for a minute. Press and count again. Is it fast again? The blood vessels have opened wide, so warm blood comes back quickly.  
  *Child mode:* Put the cold hand in warm water. Press a fingertip again. Count. Is it fast now?
  - Think: Why do you think your cheeks go red when you run, and your fingers go cold in winter?
### a3 · Thermostat Hunt (15 min)
- **safety:** Do not touch the iron, geyser or stove. Only look, and ask a grown-up how they work.
- **record:** Draw one machine's feedback loop: what notices, what decides, what acts.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk around the house together. Look for the fridge, AC, geyser, iron, overhead tank float and pressure cooker.

11. (mcq) Why can't goosebumps keep us warm the way fur keeps a cat warm?  
   Options: We have no muscles in our skin / Goosebumps make us hotter than cats / Our body hair is too thin and short to trap a layer of air / Cats don't have hair  
   **Answer:** Our body hair is too thin and short to trap a layer of air. *Why:* Goosebumps pull our hairs up, just like a cat's. But our hairs are too thin to trap a blanket of still air. So goosebumps are a leftover from our furry relatives.
12. (draw) Draw the Earth's slow rock thermostat as a loop: Earth gets warmer, more rain and weathering, less carbon dioxide in the air, Earth cools.  
   **Answer:** A loop of four arrows: warmer Earth, more rain washing rocks, less carbon dioxide, cooler Earth, back to the start.. *Why:* This loop works over hundreds of thousands of years. It cannot fix fast changes, like the carbon dioxide people are adding today.
13. (mcq) A lotus flower can keep itself at about 30 to 35 degrees on a cool night. What does this tell us?  
   Options: Lotus flowers have fur / Some plants can do a kind of warmth homeostasis too / Plants are animals / The pond is boiling  
   **Answer:** Some plants can do a kind of warmth homeostasis too. *Why:* Scientists measured sacred lotus flowers in 1996 and found they keep themselves warm. Balance-keeping is not only for animals.
14. (write) In the story The Pot That Sweats, the clay matka says, 'I stay cool because I let a little go.' How is your body like the matka?  
   **Answer:** My body lets a little water out as sweat. When the sweat dries, it takes heat from my skin. So, like the matka, I cool down by losing a little water.. *Why:* Sweat cools you the same way the matka cools its water: water evaporates from the outside and carries heat away.
15. (write) Challenge the belief. Many people say a hot cup of tea on a hot day cools you down. Others say it can only make you hotter. Use what you know about sweat to explain how both could be partly right. Does the weather, dry or humid, change your answer?  
   **Answer:** The hot tea adds a little heat to my inside. But it also makes me sweat more. On a dry day, the extra sweat dries fast and takes away more heat than the tea added, so I may end up cooler. On a humid day, the sweat cannot dry well, so the tea may just make me hotter.. *Why:* A strong answer weighs both: the heat the tea adds, and the cooling from extra sweat. It sees that the answer depends on whether the sweat can dry. A small study in 2012 found this in cyclists.
16. (truefalse) A healthy body keeps its temperature at exactly the same number all day long.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Homeostasis keeps the temperature close to 37 degrees, not exactly on it. It is a little lower early in the morning and a little higher in the evening or after running. The body corrects big changes, not tiny ones.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Gayathri, a girl in Class 3, plays in the noon sun. Then she sits in a room with the AC on. What happens to the temperature of her inside?  
   Options: It goes up to match the sun / It stays close to 37 degrees the whole time / It goes down to match the AC  
   **Answer:** It stays close to 37 degrees the whole time. *Why:* Her skin feels hot and then cool, but her inside stays near 37 degrees. Her body sweats in the sun and saves heat in the cold room. That is homeostasis.
2. (mcq) Which of these is an example of homeostasis?  
   Options: Your hair growing longer / A tumbler of tea cooling down / Your body keeping its water just right on a hot day  
   **Answer:** Your body keeping its water just right on a hot day. *Why:* Homeostasis means keeping the inside steady. The tea just drifts to the room's warmth, and growing hair is change, not balance.
3. (mcq) After a bath, you stand wet in front of a fan and feel chilly. Why?  
   Options: The water on your skin dries and carries heat away / The fan makes cold air inside itself / Bath water is always colder than the air  
   **Answer:** The water on your skin dries and carries heat away. *Why:* The fan does not make cold air. It helps the water on your skin dry, and drying takes heat from you. Sweat cools you in the same way.
4. (mcq) Lakshmi is shivering after a swim. What is the shivering doing for her?  
   Options: Cooling her down / Making her sweat / Making heat with fast-working muscles  
   **Answer:** Making heat with fast-working muscles. *Why:* Shivering is muscles shaking fast. Working muscles make heat, so shivering warms her up.
5. (mcq) After a long game of cricket in the sun, Farzana feels thirsty. Why?  
   Options: She lost water in sweat, her blood got a little saltier, and her brain noticed / Her tummy is empty / The sun dried her mouth from the outside  
   **Answer:** She lost water in sweat, her blood got a little saltier, and her brain noticed. *Why:* Sweat takes water out of the body. The blood gets a little too salty, and the hypothalamus makes her feel thirsty. Picking 'tummy is empty' mixes up thirst with hunger.
6. (mcq) Dark yellow pee usually means…  
   Options: You drank too much water / You need to drink more water / You are very cold / You ate too much sugar  
   **Answer:** You need to drink more water. *Why:* When you are low on water, your kidneys save it, so your pee is smaller in amount and darker. Picking 'drank too much' is the opposite: lots of water makes pee pale.
7. (mcq) In a feedback loop, what happens after the body acts?  
   Options: It keeps acting for ever / It forgets / It checks again, and stops when things are just right / It starts growing  
   **Answer:** It checks again, and stops when things are just right. *Why:* A feedback loop checks again. If you kept sweating for ever, you would get too cold and lose too much water.
8. (mcq) A geyser switches off when its water reaches the set warmth. Which part of your body does the same job as the geyser's thermostat?  
   Options: Your sweat glands / Your skin / The hypothalamus in your brain  
   **Answer:** The hypothalamus in your brain. *Why:* The thermostat compares the water with the set number and decides. In you, the hypothalamus compares your warmth with 'just right' and decides. Sweat glands only act on the order.
9. (mcq) Remember the sweaty tumbler test, with a wet cloth and a dry cloth on two tumblers of warm water by a fan? Why did we use the same tumblers and the same water?  
   Options: To save washing up / To make it a fair test, so only the wet cloth was different / Because the fan needs it / So the water would boil  
   **Answer:** To make it a fair test, so only the wet cloth was different. *Why:* In a fair test, you change only one thing. Then you know that thing caused the difference. Here, it was the wet cloth.
10. (mcq) Remember the sweaty tumbler test? The wet tumbler dropped 9 degrees and the dry one dropped 3 degrees. Which is true?  
   Options: They cooled the same / The dry one cooled more / The wet one cooled 3 times as much / The wet one got warmer  
   **Answer:** The wet one cooled 3 times as much. *Why:* 9 divided by 3 is 3. So the wet one cooled 3 times as much. Drying water carried heat away.
11. (mcq) Why does an elephant flap their big ears on a hot day?  
   Options: To make heat / Moving air cools the warm blood flowing through the ears / To shade their eyes from the sun  
   **Answer:** Moving air cools the warm blood flowing through the ears. *Why:* An elephant's ears are thin and full of blood vessels. Flapping moves air over them, so the blood loses heat before it flows back into the body.
12. (mcq) Honeybees keep their hive close to 35 degrees. What do they do on a very hot day?  
   Options: Fan their wings and spread drops of water, which dry and cool the hive / Fly towards the sun / Sit still and wait  
   **Answer:** Fan their wings and spread drops of water, which dry and cool the hive. *Why:* Bees bring water and spread it in the hive. Then they fan their wings, and the drying water carries heat away. It is the same trick as sweating.
13. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still NOT know for sure?  
   Options: That sweat cools us by drying / Why our just-right number is about 37 degrees / That shivering makes heat / That thirst makes us drink  
   **Answer:** Why our just-right number is about 37 degrees. *Why:* The other three are well tested. Why 37 is still an open question, with ideas like keeping fungi out.
14. (mcq) Why is a very humid heatwave more dangerous than a dry one at the same temperature?  
   Options: Humid air has no oxygen / Humid air makes you shiver / Sweat cannot dry well, so the body cannot cool itself / It isn't more dangerous  
   **Answer:** Sweat cannot dry well, so the body cannot cool itself. *Why:* Sweat cools only by drying. In very humid air it hardly dries, so the body heats up. Scientists are still working out exactly where the danger limit is.

## Applications
- Doctors use a thermometer to check for fever, when the body's set point has moved up.
- ORS packets put back water and salt for people with loose motions, cholera or heat exhaustion.
- Heat action plans, like the one started in Ahmedabad in 2013, warn people, open cool shelters and give out water during heatwaves.
- Clay matkas and surahis cool drinking water without electricity.
- Desert coolers blow air through wet pads, using the same trick as sweat.
- ACs, fridges, geysers and irons use thermostats, which are feedback loops.
- Cricketers and athletes plan drinks breaks and cooling towels in hot matches.
- Astronauts wear a suit of thin water tubes under their spacesuit, to carry body heat away.
- Newborn babies in hospital are kept in warm cots or held skin to skin, because they lose heat quickly.
- Builders and farmers are told to work early and late in the day in summer, and rest in the shade at noon.
- Engineers design buildings with shade, thick walls and courtyards to stay cool, like old havelis in Rajasthan.

## Open questions
- **Why did mammals end up at about 37 degrees, and birds at about 40?** There are ideas, like keeping fungi out, but no test can rewind evolution to check.
- **Exactly how hot and humid can it get before healthy people cannot survive?** It is hard and unsafe to test on people. Different studies give different limits, and it depends on age, health and work.
- **How does the brain choose its set points, and move them, as in a fever?** Scientists know some of the brain cells involved, but not the full picture of how they work together.
- **Why do we feel thirst and cold as feelings, and not just fix them without noticing?** This is part of the big puzzle of how the brain makes feelings at all. Nobody knows.
- **Is the whole Earth a self-balancing system, and how strong are its balancing loops?** The Earth is hard to experiment on, and its slow loops take thousands of years.
- **Why has normal body temperature dropped over the last 150 years?** Some scientists think it is less illness and warmer homes. Others think it is how temperatures were measured. Old data cannot be re-measured.

## Connected maths
- In the sweaty tumbler test, both tumblers started at 40 degrees. After 20 minutes, the wet cloth tumbler was 31 degrees. The dry cloth tumbler was 37 degrees. How many degrees did each one cool? How many times as much did the wet one cool? **The wet one cooled 9 degrees. The dry one cooled 3 degrees. The wet one cooled 3 times as much.**. 40 take away 31 is 9. 40 take away 37 is 3. 9 divided by 3 is 3. You can check: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Look at this thermometer. It was under a child's arm in the My Steady Inside test. The scale goes up in steps of half a degree. What does it read? **37 degrees.**. The line stops at the big mark labelled 37. The small marks on either side are 36 and a half, and 37 and a half. So it reads 37 degrees, which is normal. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- In My Water Diary, a child drank 5 cups on day 1, 8 cups on day 2 when they played outside, and 6 cups on day 3. Each cup holds 200 millilitres. How many cups did they drink in all? How many millilitres did they drink on day 2? **19 cups in all. 1,600 millilitres on day 2.**. 5 + 8 + 6 = 19 cups. On day 2, 8 cups of 200 millilitres each is 8 times 200, which is 1,600 millilitres. That is a little more than one and a half litres. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In Keep It Just Right, your counter starts on box 5. You draw 'Played cricket at noon: +2', then 'Drank hot coffee: +1'. Which box are you on? How many do you need to take away to get back to 5, and which body cards add up to that? **Box 8. You need to take away 3. Sweat (minus 2) and Red cheeks (minus 1) add up to 3.**. 5 + 2 = 7. 7 + 1 = 8. To get from 8 to 5 you need to take away 3. Sweat takes away 2, which leaves you on 6. Red cheeks take away 1 more, and that brings you to 5. In the game you play one card a turn, so it takes two turns. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)
