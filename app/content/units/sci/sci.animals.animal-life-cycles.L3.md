# Animal life cycles: how young animals grow into adults
`sci.animals.animal-life-cycles.L3`

**Summary.** A tadpole has a tail and gills, yet they grow into a frog with legs and lungs. Every animal's life goes through stages, from egg or baby to adult, and the adults make new young. This circle of stages is called a life cycle. A chick looks like a small hen and simply grows. But a tadpole and a caterpillar rebuild their whole bodies into a new shape. This big change is called metamorphosis, and it is switched on by chemical messages called hormones.

**Intro.** *Here is a puzzle. A small animal lives in water. They breathe with gills, like a fish. They have a long tail and no legs. They scrape algae off stones. Algae are tiny water plants that make a green, slimy coat. A few weeks later, the very same animal breathes air, has four legs and no tail, and eats insects. Nobody swapped them. What happened?* The animal is a tadpole, and they became a frog. In the monsoon, many ponds and puddles in India fill with tadpoles. In this unit you will open a hen's egg and find every part a chick needs. You will test whether air can pass through an eggshell. And you will find out where a tadpole's tail goes. It does not fall off.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Life cycle**: All the stages of an animal's life, in order. The last stage makes eggs or babies, so the cycle starts again. *e.g.* Egg, then chick, then hen. Then the hen lays an egg.
- **Stage**: One part of a life cycle. The animal looks or lives differently in each stage. *e.g.* A tadpole is one stage of a frog's life.
- **Egg**: The first stage for many animals. It holds a tiny beginning and the food it needs to grow. *e.g.* Hens, frogs, butterflies, snakes and fish all start as eggs.
- **Hatch**: To break out of an egg. *e.g.* A chick hatches by pecking a crack in their shell.
- **Young**: The baby stage of an animal, before they are grown up. *e.g.* A chick is the young of a hen. A tadpole is the young of a frog.
- **Adult**: The grown-up stage. Adults can make eggs or babies of their own. *e.g.* A hen is an adult chicken. A butterfly is an adult insect.
- **Metamorphosis**: A big change in body shape as a young animal grows up. The body is rebuilt into a new form. *e.g.* A caterpillar becomes a butterfly. A tadpole becomes a frog.
- **Frogspawn**: A clump of frog eggs, each one inside a ball of clear jelly. It floats in water. *e.g.* In the monsoon, you may see frogspawn in a pond or a paddy field.
- **Tadpole**: The young of a frog or a toad. They live in water, swim with a tail and breathe with gills. *e.g.* Tadpoles wriggle in rain puddles after the first monsoon showers.
- **Gills**: Thin, frilly body parts that take oxygen from water, so an animal can breathe underwater. *e.g.* Fish have gills. Very young tadpoles have gills too.
- **Lungs**: Two soft bags inside the body that take oxygen from air. *e.g.* You breathe with lungs. A frog grows lungs as they grow up.
- **Froglet**: A young frog that has legs but still has a stub of a tail. *e.g.* A froglet climbs out of the water onto a stone.
- **Larva**: The young stage of an insect that looks very different from the adult. Two or more are called larvae. *e.g.* A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly. A wriggler is the larva of a mosquito.
- **Caterpillar**: The larva of a butterfly or a moth. Their main job is to eat and grow. *e.g.* Lime butterfly caterpillars eat curry leaves and lemon leaves.
- **Moult**: To shed an old skin that has grown too tight, after growing a new, bigger one underneath. *e.g.* A caterpillar moults four or five times. A snake also moults.
- **Pupa**: The resting stage of an insect, between larva and adult. Inside, the body is rebuilt. Two or more are called pupae. *e.g.* A butterfly's pupa is called a chrysalis.
- **Chrysalis**: The hard case of a butterfly pupa. It is the pupa's own skin, not something they build. *e.g.* A lime butterfly chrysalis can be green or brown, like the twig they hang on.
- **Cocoon**: A case of silk that a moth caterpillar spins around themself before they become a pupa. *e.g.* A silkworm spins a cocoon. Silk saris are made from cocoon threads.
- **Yolk**: The yellow part of an egg. It is food for the growing chick. *e.g.* When you crack an egg, the round yellow ball is the yolk.
- **Embryo**: A living thing in their very first stage, while they are still growing inside an egg or inside their mother. *e.g.* A chick embryo grows inside the egg for 21 days.
- **Hormone**: A chemical message carried in the blood. It tells parts of the body what to do and when. *e.g.* A hormone tells a tadpole's body when to start becoming a frog.
- **Complete metamorphosis**: Four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult. The larva looks nothing like the adult. *e.g.* Butterflies, moths, mosquitoes, beetles, bees and ants.
- **Incomplete metamorphosis**: Three stages: egg, nymph and adult. The nymph looks like a small adult without wings. *e.g.* Grasshoppers, cockroaches and dragonflies.
- **Nymph**: The young of an insect that does not have a pupa stage. They look a bit like the adult, and grow their wings slowly. *e.g.* A young grasshopper is a nymph.

## Explanations
### What a life cycle is · *defined*
**Definition:** A life cycle is all the stages an animal goes through in their life, in order. The adults make eggs or babies, so the cycle goes round again.

Every animal started small. A hen started as an egg. You started as a single cell, smaller than a full stop. Each animal goes through stages, one after another. The last stage is the adult. Adults make eggs or babies, and those young grow up and make young of their own. That is why we draw a life cycle as a circle, not a line.
- e.g. A hen lays an egg. A chick hatches. The chick grows into a hen. The hen lays eggs.
- e.g. A cow has a calf. The calf grows into a cow. The cow has a calf.
- e.g. A frog lays frogspawn in a pond. Tadpoles hatch, become frogs, and lay frogspawn.
### The hen: an egg, a chick and a hen · *defined*
**Definition:** A hen's life cycle has three stages: egg, chick and adult. The chick looks like a small, fluffy hen. They grow bigger without changing their shape.

A hen lays an egg. If a rooster has mated with her, the egg can grow a chick. The hen sits on her eggs to keep them warm, about as warm as your body. She turns them with her beak many times a day. Inside, the chick grows for 21 days. They use the yolk as food and the egg white for water and more food. On day 21 the chick pecks a crack with a tiny hard tip on their beak, called the egg tooth. They push their way out, wet and tired. In a few hours they are dry and fluffy, and they can walk and peck. At about five or six months old, a young hen lays her first egg.
- e.g. Most shop eggs come from farms with no roosters. These eggs are not fertilised, so they can never hatch.
- e.g. A chick follows the first moving thing they see, usually their mother. They learn to peck by copying her.
- e.g. Wild chickens still live in Indian forests. They are called red junglefowl. Farm chickens come mainly from them.
### Inside an egg · *defined*
**Definition:** An egg is a complete home and food store for a growing chick. Each part has a job.

A chick growing in an egg has three problems. They need food for three weeks, they need air, and they must not be squashed. Each part of the egg solves one of these. The yellow ball is the yolk. It is the chick's food. On top of the yolk is a tiny white spot. In a fertilised egg, this is where the chick begins. The clear egg white holds water and cushions the yolk. Two twisted white cords, called chalazae, hold the yolk in the middle. Under the shell are two thin skins, called membranes. At the round end they come apart and leave a pocket of air. The shell looks solid, but it has thousands of tiny holes, so air can pass in and out.
- e.g. The air pocket is at the fat, round end of the egg. The chick's beak pokes into it just before hatching.
- e.g. Put a raw egg in very hot water, and tiny bubbles come out of the shell. The warmed air inside is escaping through the holes.
- e.g. Brown and white eggs are the same inside. The shell colour depends on the kind of hen.
- e.g. A growing chick takes calcium from the inside of their shell to build their bones. The shell gets thinner, which makes hatching easier.
### The frog: from tadpole to frog · *defined*
**Definition:** A frog's life cycle has four main stages: frogspawn, tadpole, froglet and adult frog. The tadpole changes their whole body to become a frog. This is metamorphosis.

In the monsoon, a female frog lays hundreds or thousands of eggs in water. Each egg sits in a ball of jelly. A male frog fertilises them. After a few days, tiny tadpoles wriggle out. At first they breathe with feathery gills, and they eat green slime and soft plants. Then the change begins. Back legs grow first. Then the front legs pop out. Lungs grow inside, so the young frog can breathe air. Their mouth gets wider and their gut gets shorter, ready to eat insects. Their tail gets shorter every day. Now they are a froglet. Soon the tail is gone, and the frog hops onto land. In many Indian frogs, all this takes one to three months.
- e.g. Indian bullfrogs and common toads breed in rain puddles and ponds after the first monsoon showers.
- e.g. A tadpole has gills like a fish. A frog has lungs, and can also breathe through their wet skin.
- e.g. Some frogs in the Western Ghats skip the tadpole stage. Tiny froglets hatch straight out of their eggs on land.
### The butterfly: egg, caterpillar, chrysalis and butterfly · *defined*
**Definition:** A butterfly has four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult. The larva is called a caterpillar. The pupa is called a chrysalis. This is complete metamorphosis.

A lime butterfly lays a tiny round egg on a curry leaf or a lemon leaf. Her caterpillars can eat only leaves of this plant family, and she will never feed them. So she lays each egg right on the caterpillar's food. After a few days, a caterpillar chews their way out. Often they eat their own eggshell first. Then they eat leaves all day. Their skin cannot stretch much, so they grow a new skin underneath and wriggle out of the old one. This is called moulting. They moult four times. After two or three weeks, they stop eating. They tie themself to a stem with a silk belt and moult one last time. Under that skin is the chrysalis. For about ten days they hang still while their body is rebuilt inside. Then the chrysalis splits, and a butterfly climbs out. They pump blood into their crumpled wings, wait for them to dry, and fly.
- e.g. Birds eat caterpillars. Young lime caterpillars are brown and white, like bird droppings, so birds leave them alone.
- e.g. Older lime caterpillars are green, the colour of the leaves they eat.
- e.g. A grown butterfly does not grow any more. They drink nectar, find a mate and lay eggs.
### Metamorphosis: a body that rebuilds itself · *defined*
**Definition:** In metamorphosis, some parts of the young animal's body are taken apart and new parts are built, often from the same material.

When a tadpole's tail gets shorter, it does not drop off. The cells of the tail break themselves down. Other cells swallow the pieces, and the blood carries the material to the growing legs, lungs and head. During the biggest changes the tadpole stops eating, and lives on their own tail. Inside a chrysalis, most of the caterpillar's body is broken down too. But small groups of cells have been waiting inside the caterpillar since they hatched. One group becomes a wing, another a leg, another an eye. They grow fast, using the broken-down caterpillar as their building material. So the butterfly is built out of the caterpillar.
- e.g. A tadpole's tail is taken back into their body and used again.
- e.g. The wings of a butterfly start as tiny flat pockets of cells inside the caterpillar.
- e.g. Your body rebuilds itself too, slowly. The skin cells on top are rubbed off all the time and replaced from underneath.
### Three ways to grow up · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals just grow bigger. Some change a little, step by step. Some change completely, with a resting pupa stage in the middle.

Scientists sort life cycles into three kinds. In the first kind, the baby looks like a small adult and just grows. Hens, cows, snakes and people grow up like this. In the second kind there are three stages: egg, nymph and adult. A nymph looks like a small adult without wings. Each time they moult, their wings grow a bit more. In the third kind there are four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult. The larva looks nothing like the adult. Frogs have no pupa, but their tadpoles change completely, so frogs go through metamorphosis too.
- e.g. Just grows: hen, cow, snake, crocodile, human.
- e.g. Egg, nymph, adult: grasshopper, cockroach, dragonfly.
- e.g. Egg, larva, pupa, adult: butterfly, silkworm moth, mosquito, ladybird, honeybee.
### Why change shape at all? · *idea*
Why would an animal rebuild their whole body? Scientists have three main ideas. First, each stage can be good at one job. A caterpillar is built for eating. A butterfly is built for flying to find mates and new plants. Second, the young and the adults do not compete for food. Tadpoles eat slime in water, and frogs eat insects on land. Third, it helps the young spread out. Caterpillars cannot travel far, but butterflies can fly to new places. These ideas fit what we see. But nobody can go back in time to watch how metamorphosis began.
- e.g. A caterpillar eats leaves. A butterfly drinks nectar. They never fight over food.
- e.g. Tadpoles live in water and frogs on land, so they use two different homes.
- e.g. Mosquito wrigglers eat tiny specks of food in water, such as microbes. Adult mosquitoes fly and drink plant juices, and females also drink blood to make eggs.
### The switch: hormones and genes · *defined*
**Definition:** A hormone is a chemical message carried in the blood. Hormones tell a young animal's body when to start changing.

How does a tadpole know when to become a frog? A small part of their neck, called the thyroid, makes a hormone. When there is enough of this hormone in the blood, the change starts. Legs grow, the gills go and the tail shrinks. In 1912, a scientist named Friedrich Gudernatsch fed tadpoles a little dried thyroid from a horse. They turned into tiny frogs much too early. Insects use different hormones, but they work the same way. A caterpillar and the butterfly they become have exactly the same genes. Genes are the instructions inside every cell. Hormones switch on different instructions at different times, so one set of genes builds two very different bodies.
- e.g. Your thyroid makes the same hormone as a tadpole's. It helps you grow. It needs iodine, which is why salt in India has iodine added.
- e.g. Tadpoles in warm water usually change sooner than tadpoles in cold water.
- e.g. If you test the genes of a caterpillar and of the butterfly they became, they match exactly.
### Are they still the same animal? · *debated*
A caterpillar is broken down and rebuilt into a butterfly. So is the butterfly the same animal as the caterpillar? People have argued about questions like this for thousands of years. Scientists have found one clue: some memories may survive the change.
- e.g. The butterfly has the same genes as the caterpillar.
- e.g. Much of the caterpillar's body is broken down, but parts of their nerves are kept and rebuilt.
- e.g. Most of your body is made of different material from when you were a baby. Are you still the same person?
- **Biologists:** Yes, they are the same animal. They are one living thing all the way through, with one set of genes. They just have two very different bodies at two times of their life.
- **Douglas Blackiston, Elena Silva Casey and Martha Weiss, scientists in the USA, in 2008:** They taught caterpillars of a moth to stay away from one smell. After metamorphosis, many of the adult moths still stayed away from it. So some memory may pass through the chrysalis. Other scientists want more tests before they are sure.
- **Philosophers:** Some philosophers say an animal stays the same one because their life goes on without a break, even while their material and their shape change. Others say there is no simple answer, and it depends on what you mean by 'the same'.
### Maria Sibylla Merian: watching, not guessing · *defined*
**Definition:** People once believed that caterpillars and flies grew out of mud, dew or rotting food. Careful watching showed that every insect hatches from an egg laid by a parent.

Maria Sibylla Merian was born in Germany in 1647. At 13, she began raising caterpillars at home. She painted each one with their eggs, their chrysalis and the plant they ate. In 1679 she published a book on how caterpillars change into butterflies and moths. At 52, she sailed to Suriname in South America with her daughter to study insects there. For a long time people called her an artist, not a scientist. Today she is honoured as one of the first people to study life cycles properly.
- e.g. Merian painted the plant each caterpillar ate, because she saw that the mother butterfly chooses that plant.
- e.g. A Dutch scientist, Jan Swammerdam, cut open caterpillars in the 1660s. He found folded wings already growing inside.
### Going further out: the material goes round · *idea*
A life cycle is a circle of animals. The material in them goes round in a bigger circle. A caterpillar eats leaves, and the leaves were built from air, water and sunlight. When a butterfly dies, small animals and microbes break their body down. Their atoms go back into the soil and air, and plants take them up again. So the atoms in a butterfly's wing may once have been in a frog, a hen or a person. Almost all the atoms in an egg, apart from hydrogen, were made inside stars long ago.
- e.g. Leaves that fall become soil. The soil feeds new plants.
- e.g. The calcium in an eggshell came from the hen's food, and before that from rocks.
- e.g. Scientists agree that the carbon and oxygen in your body were made in stars.
### What nobody knows yet · *idea*
Scientists are not sure how complete metamorphosis first began, more than 300 million years ago. They do not know exactly how each waiting cell inside a caterpillar knows which part to become. They are not sure how much a butterfly remembers of being a caterpillar. And nobody knows what being a chrysalis feels like from the inside.
- e.g. Fossils show that insects with larvae lived more than 300 million years ago.
- e.g. Scientists are still testing which memories survive in moths and beetles.
- e.g. Some frogs skip the tadpole stage, and scientists study why.

## Mostly agreed
- Every animal comes from a parent or parents, usually starting as an egg. Animals do not grow out of mud or rotting food. *How we know:* Francesco Redi showed in 1668 that maggots grow only where flies can lay eggs. Since then, scientists have watched the life cycles of thousands of kinds of animals.
- Frogs, butterflies and many other animals go through metamorphosis. Their young look very different from the adults. *How we know:* Anyone can watch it. People raise tadpoles and caterpillars at home and in classrooms every year, and see the same stages.
- Metamorphosis is switched on by hormones, chemical messages in the blood. *How we know:* In 1912, tadpoles fed thyroid changed into frogs early. In 1916, tadpoles whose thyroid had been removed stayed tadpoles.
- A tadpole's tail and much of a caterpillar's body are broken down and reused to build the new body. *How we know:* Scientists can watch the tail cells break apart under a microscope. Tadpoles stop eating while their tails shrink.
- A caterpillar and the butterfly they become are one living thing with exactly the same genes. *How we know:* Gene tests on the larva and the adult give the same result. Only which genes are switched on is different.
- A chick takes about 21 days to grow inside their egg, if the egg is kept warm and turned. *How we know:* Poultry farmers have hatched millions of chicks in warm boxes called incubators. The timing is the same almost every time.

## How we found out
**Caspar Friedrich Wolff, Albrecht von Haller and Christian Pander, 1759 to 1817.** In the 1600s and 1700s, many scientists believed that every egg held a tiny, ready-made chick. They thought the chick only had to swell up and hatch. This idea was called preformation. In 1759, a young German doctor, Caspar Friedrich Wolff, tested it. He opened hen's eggs after different numbers of days of warming, and looked at the growing chick under a microscope. He did not find a tiny chick that just got bigger. He saw blood vessels appear where there had been none, and new parts take shape step by step. In later work he showed that the chick's gut forms when a flat sheet of cells folds into a tube. So, Wolff said, the chick is built part by part out of material that has no shape at first. Famous scientists of his time, such as Albrecht von Haller, disagreed with him. But others who watched eggs day by day saw the same thing. In 1817, Christian Pander showed that the early chick is made of three thin layers, and each layer builds different parts. Today scientists agree that Wolff was right.  
*What people believed before:* Every egg holds a tiny, ready-made chick that only has to grow bigger. Wolff looked inside eggs on different days and saw the chick's parts form step by step.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Small animals, like flies and caterpillars, grow out of mud, dew or rotting food.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,350 years ago, and most people for about 2,000 years after him.; *rejected*)  
  For: It looks true. Leave food out, and maggots appear. Nobody sees the tiny eggs being laid.  
  Against: Francesco Redi covered meat jars with thin cloth in 1668. No maggots grew in them. Maria Sibylla Merian and others watched insects lay eggs and raised the young. Every animal comes from an egg or a parent.
- **Inside the chrysalis, the caterpillar turns completely into soup.** (Many books, websites and teachers say this.; *rejected*)  
  For: If you open a chrysalis a few days in, much of the inside is runny. Most of the caterpillar's body really is broken down.  
  Against: Not everything becomes soup. The small groups of waiting cells stay whole and grow into wings, legs and eyes. Parts of the nerves and breathing tubes are kept and rebuilt.
- **A butterfly comes out of a cocoon.** (A very common mistake, even in some famous picture books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Moths do come out of cocoons. Both are cases that hold a pupa, so people mix them up.  
  Against: A cocoon is a silk case that a moth caterpillar spins around themself. Most butterfly caterpillars spin no silk case. Their own hard skin is the chrysalis.
- **Caterpillars began when an ancient insect mated with a worm-like animal, and their two life cycles joined.** (Donald Williamson, a British scientist, from the 1990s to the 2000s.; *rejected*)  
  For: It would explain why a larva and an adult look like two different animals.  
  Against: Gene studies show that a caterpillar's genes come from their insect parents, like all their other genes. No sign of another animal's genes has been found. Most scientists reject the idea.
- **A caterpillar is like a baby that hatched very early, before they had finished growing in the egg.** (James Truman and Lynn Riddiford, two scientists in the USA, in 1999.; *debated*)  
  For: The waiting cells in a caterpillar are like the parts of an embryo that have not grown yet. The same hormones are involved in both.  
  Against: It is hard to test something that began more than 300 million years ago. Other scientists have different ideas about how the pupa stage began.
- **If you touch a tadpole or a caterpillar, they will die.** (Something many children are told.; *rejected*)  
  For: It is true that soft young animals are easily hurt. Tadpoles need wet skin, and squeezing a caterpillar can harm them.  
  Against: A gentle touch with a wet hand does not usually kill them. But it does stress them, so look and don't handle. Soap, sunscreen or salt on your hands can harm a tadpole's skin.

## Roots
We have been learning how a chick grows inside an egg and how a tadpole becomes a frog. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The egg you opened held a yolk, a white and a tiny white spot, but no chick. If the egg is fertilised and kept warm, a chick takes shape in that spot. Their heart beats on day 2, their eyes form next, and their feathers grow from about day 9. A chick is built part by part over 21 days, from an egg that held none of those parts.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a tiny chick curled up in every egg, only waiting to grow bigger and hatch. Many scientists believed this too, in the 1600s and 1700s. In fact the egg holds the chick's food and a tiny spot, and the chick's parts are made one after another.  
*Evidence:* In Open an Egg like a Scientist, you found a yolk, a white, two twisted cords and a tiny white spot, but no chick. Scientists who open eggs on each day of warming see the heart, the eyes and the feathers form one after another. The chick video in this unit shows it too.

- *who am i:* You also began as one tiny cell. Your body was built from the food your mother ate, and since then from your own food.
- *what is life:* A living thing builds their new body out of food: a chick from their yolk, a frog partly from their tail, a butterfly from the caterpillar they were.
- *what is this world:* The yolk came from what the hen ate: grain, greens and insects. So every chick is built from the world around their mother.

A chick, a frog and a butterfly each build a new body out of material that is already there. The chick uses the yolk and white. The tadpole uses their own tail. The butterfly is built from the broken-down caterpillar. All of that material came from food, which came from plants, which were built from air, water and sunlight. So the same material passes from body to body, and takes a new shape each time.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2 (Discussion on Making All Things Equal), the butterfly dream:** Zhuangzi, a Chinese thinker, dreamed he was a butterfly, happily fluttering. He woke up and was Zhuangzi again. Then he wondered: was he a man who had dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming he was a man? He said this is called 'the changing of things'. (A caterpillar becomes a butterfly and is still one living thing. Zhuangzi asks whether 'me' is as fixed as it feels.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to break open a tiny banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing, sir.' Uddalaka says that from this fine, unseen thing the whole great banyan tree grows. 'That is the truth. You are that.' (Shvetaketu could see nothing inside the seed, yet a whole tree grows from them. The egg's tiny spot is the same: no chick is there to see, yet a chick grows from it.)
- **Heraclitus, quoted by Plato in Cratylus 402a:** Heraclitus, a Greek thinker, said that everything flows. You cannot step into the same river twice, because new water is always flowing in. (A body is like a river. Its material keeps changing, but the animal goes on. A tadpole's tail becomes part of the frog, and the frog goes on.)
- Science (settled): A living body is built from what it takes in: food, water and air.
- Science (settled): Your own body rebuilds itself all the time. Your red blood cells last about four months and are then replaced.
- Other view, Many biologists, and the Charvaka thinkers of ancient India: Many biologists say a growing chick or a changing tadpole is chemistry following genes, step by step. They say nothing more is needed to explain it. The Charvaka thinkers said only matter is real. Many careful people hold this view.
- Practice, Breathing in the egg (3 min): Let's curl up small on the floor, like a chick inside an egg. Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly, five times. Be very still. A chick in their egg breathes too, through thousands of tiny holes in their shell. Feel the air going in through your nose. That air came from the room around you. Now, very slowly, uncurl. Stretch your arms out wide, as if you are breaking out of the shell.
- Ask: The yolk became a chick. What will the food you eat today become?
- Ask: If almost every bit of you is new since you were a baby, what makes you still you?
- Ask: Is a butterfly the end of a caterpillar, or the same animal carrying on?
- Ask: Where were the atoms of your hand before they were in you?

## Story: Where the Tails Go
*Everything about the tadpoles is real: back legs first, the tadpole stopping eating, and the tail being broken down and used again. Kavya, Farida, their town near the Western Ghats and their bucket are made up.*

The first monsoon rain came to Kavya's town in the middle of the night. It came so hard that it woke her up. By morning the empty plot behind the water tank had turned into a brown, shining pond. By the next evening it was singing.

"Frogs," said Farida, from the wall. Farida lived in the next building. She had been Kavya's best friend for four years, which was half of their whole lives. "Hundreds of them. Listen."

They listened. The singing sounded like a hundred people trying to start a hundred scooters.

Three days later there were clumps of clear jelly floating at the edge of the pond, each little ball with a black dot inside. Three days after that, the dots had become commas. Then the commas began to wriggle.

"We should keep some," said Kavya. "Just a few. Just to see."

Kavya's mother said yes, on two conditions. They had to use pond water, never tap water. And they had to put every single one back in the pond when it was done. So the girls filled an old blue bucket with pond water and a stone with green slime on it. They scooped up five tadpoles in a steel tumbler. They named them after the five fingers of a hand. Thumb, Pointer, Tall, Ring and Little.

Farida had a notebook. She drew lines in it and made a table, because that is the kind of person Farida was. Every second day they measured each tadpole, as gently as they could, against a ruler held outside the bucket. Body. Tail. Legs, none.

It was on the ninth day, while they were measuring, that Farida said it. "We're moving. In September. Abbu got a job in Hyderabad."

Kavya held the ruler very still.

"It's only September," said Farida. "That's ages."

It was not ages. It was eleven weeks. Kavya counted.

After that, Kavya did not want to look at the table. But Farida kept filling it in, every second day, in her neat small numbers. Body, 9 millimetres. Tail, 16. Then, on day fifteen, beside Tall's name, she drew two tiny lines. Back legs.

By day twenty-two all five had back legs. They looked like tadpoles who had put on shorts. Then one morning, Pointer's front legs popped out, both at once, like a child pulling arms out through a jumper.

And then the tails began to go.

Kavya noticed it first. She was not looking at the notebook. She was looking at the bucket. "Thumb's tail is shorter," she said. "Look. It's broken."

Farida looked at the numbers. Thumb's tail had been 18 millimetres. Now it was 14.

"Something is biting them," said Kavya. "Or the tails are falling off." She tipped the bucket a little towards the light and looked at the bottom. There was the slimy stone. There was some mud. There were no tails.

"Maybe the others ate it," said Farida.

That was a horrible thought. They watched for a long time. Nobody bit anybody. The tadpoles just hung in the water, very still, their new legs dangling. And there was something else. The girls dropped in a pinch of boiled spinach, the way they always did. The tadpoles did not eat it. Not one of them.

"They're sick," said Kavya. Her voice went small. "They're not eating and their tails are breaking and they're going to die."

But Farida had turned back three pages in the notebook. She ran her finger down the numbers, and then down them again.

"Kavya," she said. "Look at Little."

Little had been the smallest. His tail had gone from 15 millimetres, to 12, to 8. But it had not gone in one snap, the way a broken thing would go. It had gone a little at a time, every single day. And all that time his front legs had been getting longer and fatter. His head had been getting wider. His mouth, which had been a tiny round sucker, was turning into a wide frog's grin.

"It's not falling off," said Farida slowly. "It's going in."

Kavya looked at Little. She looked at the empty bottom of the bucket. She thought about the spinach, uneaten.

"They don't need to eat," she said. "They've got lunch."

"Their own tails," said Farida.

"They're eating their own tails from the inside," said Kavya. "And making legs out of them."

It was the most disgusting and the most wonderful thing either of them had ever heard. They laughed until Kavya's mother came to see what was wrong.

Later, Kavya's mother found a page on her phone and read it to them. She said it was true. The tail cells break themselves down, and the blood carries the material away to the growing frog. Nothing is thrown out. The tadpole doesn't lose their tail. They turn it into the frog.

Seven days later, Little was sitting on the slimy stone with his head out of the water. He had a stub of tail no bigger than a grain of rice. He was looking at the sky, which he had never seen before, with eyes that had been built for air.

They carried the five froglets back to the pond in the steel tumbler, all together, on a Sunday at the end of August. The pond was smaller now and full of grass. The froglets climbed out onto the mud, one by one, and sat there blinking. Then Thumb hopped, and then they all did, into the grass, and were gone.

Farida closed the notebook. It was full.

"You keep it," she said.

"It's yours. You made the table."

"You noticed the tails," said Farida. "Anyway, I'll make a new one in Hyderabad. They have frogs there too. Probably."

In September, Farida moved. Kavya did not cry at the gate, though she did cry later, a bit. That night she lay in bed and listened to the frogs behind the water tank. There were not hundreds now. There were a few. Somewhere in the grass, five small frogs were not singing yet. They would be, in a year or two, with lungs and wide mouths built partly out of tails.

She thought that she should write to Farida about it. She thought that when she did, she would not write that something was lost. She would write that something had gone in.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the tadpoles look as if they are breaking, but they are rebuilding. The tail's cells break down, and the body reuses the material to build a frog. That is why the tadpoles stop eating. The girls work this out from their records, not by a lucky guess: the numbers show the tail shrinking a little each day, not snapping.

- Ask: Why did Kavya think the tadpoles were sick? What did Farida's notebook show instead?
- Ask: Why did the tadpoles stop eating spinach when their tails were shrinking?
- Ask: Where did the tadpoles' tails go?
- Ask: Why did Kavya's mother say to use pond water and to put every tadpole back?
- Ask: At the end, Kavya decides to write that something 'had gone in', not that something was lost. What do you think she means?

### e1 · Can an egg breathe? Bubbles from a shell (20 min)

### e2 · The Naked Egg: what is under the shell? (35 min, 3 days)
- **question:** What holds an egg together if the shell is taken away?

  *Parent does:* 1. Wrap the string round the fattest part of the egg. 2. Pinch where it meets. 3. Lay the string along the ruler and write the number in the table.

  *Child mode:* Day 2. Look at your egg. Is the shell going? Pour away the old vinegar. Add new vinegar.
  - Wait: Leave it for 1 day
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the egg out with a spoon and rinse it gently under the tap. 2. Rub off any leftover shell. 3. Measure it with the string again. 4. In a dark room, shine a torch through it. 5. Drop it from 2 cm, about the width of two fingers, onto a tray.  
  *Parent says:* The shell is gone. What is holding the egg together now? Gently feel it. Now let's shine a light through it.  
  *Child mode:* Rinse your egg. Feel it gently. Measure it again. Shine a torch through it. Can you see the yolk?
  - Note: The egg is held together by its two thin skins, the membranes. It is usually a little bigger, because water from the vinegar soaks in through the skins.   - Check: In the Naked Egg experiment, the shell is gone but the egg does not spill. What holds it together? → **Two thin skins under the shell**
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Then throw the egg away and wash hands.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: an egg has a skin under its shell that holds it together. What I saw: after two days in vinegar the shell was gone, but the egg stayed whole and bounced a little. It also grew from 14 to 15 centimetres around (made-up numbers). Why: the vinegar broke down the shell, but not the skins. Water soaked in through the skins, so the egg got bigger.
  - Think: The shell keeps the chick safe, and the skins keep germs out. What else in nature has a hard outside and a soft skin inside?

  *Child mode:* With a grown-up, scoop a few tadpoles into pond water. Add a slimy stone and a stick. Keep it in the shade. You are borrowing them.

- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the ruler outside the tub, level with one tadpole. 2. Measure their body, from nose to the start of the tail. 3. Measure their tail. 4. Write both numbers. 5. Do the same for each tadpole. 6. Drop in a pinch of spinach.  
  *Parent says:* Measure gently, from outside. We don't need to touch them.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the ruler outside the tub. Measure the body. Measure the tail. Write the numbers. Give a pinch of spinach.
  - Wait: Do this every second day
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look with a magnifying glass at the place where the body meets the tail.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely where the tail starts. Can you see two tiny bumps? Those are the first legs.  
  *Child mode:* Look where the tail starts. Can you see two tiny bumps? Those are legs!
  - Check: In Tadpole Watch, which legs does a tadpole grow first? → **The back legs**
  - Wait: Around day 15
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When front legs appear, check every day. Notice whether the tadpoles eat the spinach. Make sure the stick is there so froglets can climb out.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the tail now. Is it snapping off, or getting shorter a little each day? And are they still eating?  
  *Child mode:* Front legs! Now watch the tail. Is it getting shorter? Are they eating?
  - Note: Tadpoles usually stop eating while the tail shrinks. Don't worry. Their mouth and gut are being rebuilt, and they live on the material of their own tail. The froglets must be able to climb out, or they can drown, because they now breathe air.
  - Check: In Tadpole Watch, the tails got shorter a little each day, and the tadpoles stopped eating. Why? → **The tail is broken down and used as food for building the frog**
  - Wait: Around day 25 to 35
- **s5 (15 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* They're ready. Let's take them home. Then we'll make a picture of what happened to the tail.  
  *Child mode:* Take your froglets back to their pond. Let them hop away. Then draw a line chart of tail length.
  - Note: Tadpoles change at their own speed, and some take up to three months. Keep going until each has only a stub of tail. If you cannot find tadpoles, watch the tadpole video in this unit instead.
  - Wait: Around day 40 to 45
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think happens to a tadpole's tail? What did you see? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the tail doesn't fall off, it goes into the body. What I saw: the tail grew to 18 millimetres, then shrank a little every day, to 8 and then 2. We never found a tail in the tub, and the tadpoles stopped eating. Why: the body breaks the tail down and uses it to build the frog.

### a4 · Rebuild the Body: from caterpillar to butterfly (15 min)

### a6 · Wriggler Check: find and stop mosquito babies (15 min)

  *Parent says:* Mosquitoes lay their eggs in still water. Their babies live in the water for about a week before they fly. Let's check every place water stands still.  
  *Child mode:* With a grown-up, shine a torch into still water. Check pots, buckets and coolers. Look for tiny wriggling things.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If you find wrigglers, scoop a spoonful into the glass. Look closely together.

  *Child mode:* Look at the wrigglers in the glass. They hang from the top to breathe. Can you find a curled one? That is a pupa.

### Life Cycle Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A big caterpillar on a curry leaf plant stops eating, hangs very still from a twig and holds on with silk. Predict: what is about to happen?  
   Options: Their skin splits, and a chrysalis forms underneath / They are about to die of hunger / They will lay eggs / They are asleep and will wake up as the same caterpillar  
   **Answer:** Their skin splits, and a chrysalis forms underneath. *Why:* A caterpillar stops eating and hangs still just before the chrysalis stage. Inside, the body gets ready to be rebuilt into a butterfly. Picking 'lay eggs' is a mix-up. Only the adult butterfly lays eggs.
2. (order) Put the stages of a mosquito in order: flying adult, tumbler, egg, wriggler.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* A mosquito lays eggs on still water. Wrigglers hatch and eat. Each wriggler becomes a tumbler, which is the pupa. A flying adult comes out of the tumbler. The first three stages all live in water.
3. (write) Why do we draw a life cycle as a circle, and not as a straight line?  
   **Answer:** Because the adult makes new eggs or babies, and they go through the same stages again. A frog lays frogspawn, the frogspawn becomes tadpoles, and the tadpoles grow into frogs who lay frogspawn.. *Why:* A life cycle has no end point. Each adult starts the next round, so the stages go round and round.
4. (mcq) Which stage of a butterfly does most of the eating?  
   Options: The caterpillar / The chrysalis / The egg / The butterfly  
   **Answer:** The caterpillar. *Why:* The caterpillar eats leaves all day and grows fast. The chrysalis does not eat at all. The butterfly only sips nectar. Picking 'the butterfly' is a common guess, because that is the stage we see most.
5. (fill) The big change in body shape, like a caterpillar becoming a butterfly, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['metamorphosis']. *Why:* Metamorphosis means a big change of shape. The young animal's body is rebuilt into a new form.
6. (fill) A young frog that has four legs and a stub of a tail is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['froglet']. *Why:* A froglet has legs and still has a short tail stub. Soon the tail is gone and they are an adult frog.
7. (sort) Sort these animals. Do their babies look like small adults, or do they change shape completely?  
   **Answer:** hen → Look like small adults; frog → Change shape completely; cow → Look like small adults; butterfly → Change shape completely; mosquito → Change shape completely; dog → Look like small adults. *Why:* Chicks, calves and puppies look like small versions of their parents. Tadpoles, caterpillars and mosquito wrigglers look nothing like their parents, and go through metamorphosis.
8. (truefalse) A butterfly comes out of a cocoon.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A butterfly comes out of a chrysalis, which is the pupa's own hard skin. A cocoon is a silk case that a moth caterpillar spins around themself.
9. (truefalse) A small butterfly is a young one, who will grow bigger.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A butterfly does not grow at all after coming out of the chrysalis. All the growing happens in the caterpillar stage. A small butterfly is a grown-up of a small kind, or one who had less food as a caterpillar.
10. (mcq) A tadpole has a tail and gills. A frog has legs and lungs. Why must a tadpole change before living on land?  
   Options: Gills work only in water, and a tail cannot hop. Lungs and legs suit life on land / The tadpole gets bored of the water / Frogs cannot swim, so they must leave the water  
   **Answer:** Gills work only in water, and a tail cannot hop. Lungs and legs suit life on land. *Why:* Each body suits a place. Gills take oxygen from water but collapse in air, and a tail is for swimming. Lungs breathe air, and legs hop and climb. Picking 'frogs cannot swim' is wrong, because frogs swim very well.
11. (draw) Draw the life cycle of a hen as a circle, with arrows between the stages. Then write one way it is different from a frog's life cycle.  
   **Answer:** Egg, chick, young hen, adult hen, with an arrow back to the egg. A strong answer says the chick already looks like a small hen, while a tadpole looks nothing like a frog.. *Why:* A life cycle is drawn as a circle, because the adult hen lays eggs and the cycle starts again. A hen has no metamorphosis.
12. (write) Your friend Nila says, 'A caterpillar and a butterfly are two different animals.' What would you tell her?  
   **Answer:** They are the same animal at two different stages of their life. The caterpillar makes a chrysalis, and their body is rebuilt into the butterfly. They even have the same genes.. *Why:* Metamorphosis changes the body, but it is one animal all the way through. Scientists check this with genes, which match exactly.
13. (write) In the Wriggler Check, you looked for mosquito babies in still water at home. Why does tipping out the water in a flowerpot tray stop mosquitoes?  
   **Answer:** Mosquito eggs, wrigglers and tumblers all live in water. If the water is tipped out, the young cannot grow into flying adults. Doing it every week breaks the life cycle.. *Why:* Three of the four stages of a mosquito live in water. Take away the water, and the cycle stops before the adult stage.
14. (match) Match each part of an egg to its job.  
   **Answer:** yolk → food for the chick; white → water and a cushion; twisted cords → hold the yolk in the middle; shell → protects, and lets air in through tiny holes. *Why:* Every part of an egg does a job for the growing chick. Together they make a complete home and food store.
15. (perspective) Two sisters argue about which way of growing up works better.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason, such as young and adults using different food, or a chick being ready to move at once. It also names a real cost of its own side, such as a chrysalis that cannot run away, or a hen who must find food for her chicks.. *Why:* Both ways work, which is why both are common. Each has a cost and a benefit. Scientists still argue about why metamorphosis first began.
### Inside the Change (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A grasshopper's babies look like small grasshoppers without wings. Which kind of life cycle is this?  
   Options: Egg, nymph, adult, with no pupa / Egg, larva, pupa, adult / Just grows, like a hen  
   **Answer:** Egg, nymph, adult, with no pupa. *Why:* A young grasshopper is a nymph. They look like a small adult, and their wings grow a bit more each time they moult. They have no pupa. Picking 'just grows' is close. But a grasshopper sheds their whole skin to grow, and their wings appear bit by bit. A chick hatches with their wings already there.
2. (mcq) Which part of a hen's egg gives the chick their first breath of air before hatching?  
   Options: The air pocket at the round end / The yolk / The twisted cords / The white  
   **Answer:** The air pocket at the round end. *Why:* Two skins under the shell come apart at the round end, making an air pocket. Just before hatching, the chick pokes their beak in and starts breathing with their lungs. Picking 'the white' is a natural guess, because the white is clear. But the white is mostly water, not air.
3. (mcq) Look at this chart of one tadpole's tail length. What happened to the tail?  
   Options: It grew, then shrank a little at a time / It fell off in one go on day 30 / It stayed the same / It grew all the time  
   **Answer:** It grew, then shrank a little at a time. *Why:* The line goes up to 18 millimetres by day 20, then comes down step by step: 14, 8, then 2. A tail that fell off would drop to zero in one step. Picking 'fell off in one go' is what most people expect, before they measure.
4. (fill) A chemical message carried in the blood, which tells a tadpole when to change, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['hormone']. *Why:* A hormone is a chemical message. A tadpole's thyroid makes the hormone that starts their metamorphosis.
5. (fill) A young insect that looks like a small adult without wings, and has no pupa stage, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['nymph']. *Why:* Nymphs grow their wings bit by bit each time they moult. Grasshoppers, cockroaches and dragonflies have nymphs.
6. (mcq) In 1912, Friedrich Gudernatsch fed some tadpoles a little dried thyroid from a horse. What happened?  
   Options: They turned into tiny frogs much too early / They stopped growing / Their tails grew longer  
   **Answer:** They turned into tiny frogs much too early. *Why:* The thyroid makes the hormone that starts metamorphosis, so extra hormone started the change early. Picking 'stopped growing' gets it the wrong way round: the tadpoles changed faster, not slower.
7. (sort) Sort these insects by their life cycle.  
   **Answer:** butterfly → Egg, larva, pupa, adult; grasshopper → Egg, nymph, adult; mosquito → Egg, larva, pupa, adult; cockroach → Egg, nymph, adult; honeybee → Egg, larva, pupa, adult; dragonfly → Egg, nymph, adult. *Why:* Butterflies, mosquitoes and bees have a pupa stage. Grasshoppers, cockroaches and dragonflies do not. Their nymphs grow wings slowly.
8. (truefalse) A caterpillar and the butterfly they become have exactly the same genes.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Genes are the instructions in every cell. They are the same all through their life. Hormones switch different instructions on at different times.
9. (truefalse) Inside a chrysalis, absolutely everything turns into soup.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Much of the caterpillar's body is broken down. But the waiting cells stay whole and grow into wings, legs and eyes, and some parts are kept and rebuilt.
10. (write) In the Naked Egg experiment, vinegar took away the eggshell, but the egg did not fall apart. What held the egg together? Why would this help a growing chick?  
   **Answer:** Two thin, strong skins under the shell, called membranes, held the egg together. They keep water in and germs out, and hold the white and yolk in place while the chick grows.. *Why:* The shell is only the outside guard. The membranes inside also protect the growing chick.
11. (write) Why does a lime butterfly lay her eggs on a curry leaf or lemon plant, and not on any plant?  
   **Answer:** Her caterpillars can only eat leaves from that plant family. She never meets her babies, so she lays them right on their food.. *Why:* Choosing the plant is the only care a butterfly mother gives. Maria Sibylla Merian noticed this in the 1600s and painted each caterpillar with their plant.
12. (write) In the dough model, you squashed a caterpillar into a ball and built a butterfly from the same dough. What does the model show? What does it get wrong?  
   **Answer:** It shows that the butterfly is built from the caterpillar's own material, using waiting cells. It gets wrong that real waiting cells grow from tiny to big, and that some parts are kept, not broken down.. *Why:* Every model is useful but not perfect. Saying what it gets wrong is part of thinking like a scientist.
13. (write) A tadpole's thyroid makes the hormone that starts the change into a frog. Predict: what would happen to a tadpole whose body could not make this hormone? Why?  
   **Answer:** The tadpole would never become a frog. With no hormone, there is no message to start the change. Scientists have found that such tadpoles keep growing and stay tadpoles, sometimes becoming very big.. *Why:* A hormone is the signal that starts metamorphosis. Without the signal, the body carries on as it is.
14. (perspective) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3, found a lime butterfly caterpillar on the curry leaf plant on her balcony. Her family has different ideas.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, for example that a cage protects the caterpillar from wasps, or that they are safest on their own plant with no handling. It then answers back fairly from another side, for example that wild caterpillars face wasps and birds, or that a cage needs fresh leaves every day.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. Raising a caterpillar teaches a lot and keeps them safe from predators, but it needs care every day. Watching them on the plant needs no cage but you may miss stages.
15. (draw) Draw the life cycle of a grasshopper and of a butterfly side by side. Circle the stage that the grasshopper does not have.  
   **Answer:** Grasshopper: egg, nymph, adult. Butterfly: egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly. The chrysalis, or pupa, is circled.. *Why:* A grasshopper nymph grows a little more like an adult at each moult, so they need no pupa. A caterpillar looks nothing like a butterfly, so the body is rebuilt inside a chrysalis.

1. (mcq) Francesco Redi covered some jars of meat with thin cloth, and left others open. Maggots grew only in the open jars. What did this show?  
   Options: Maggots come from eggs laid by flies, not from the meat / Cloth stops meat going bad / Maggots don't like cloth  
   **Answer:** Maggots come from eggs laid by flies, not from the meat. *Why:* Flies could not reach the covered meat, so they couldn't lay eggs there. So maggots must come from fly eggs. Picking 'cloth stops meat going bad' misses the point. The covered meat still went bad, it just had no maggots.
2. (perspective) In 2008, Doug Blackiston and his team taught moth caterpillars to stay away from one smell. After metamorphosis, many of the adult moths still stayed away from it. Scientists explain this in different ways.

It answers back fairly, for example that a body rebuilt in a chrysalis might still keep some brain cells.. *Why:* Both ideas fit the first result. Good scientists design a test that would turn out differently if one idea were true and the other false.
3. (mcq) Some bush frogs in the Western Ghats lay their eggs on wet leaves, far from any pond. Predict: what hatches from these eggs?  
   Options: A tiny froglet. The tadpole stage happens inside the egg / A tadpole, who then crawls to a pond / Nothing, because frog eggs must be in water  
   **Answer:** A tiny froglet. The tadpole stage happens inside the egg. *Why:* These bush frogs skip the free-swimming tadpole. The young grows through the tadpole stage inside the egg, and a tiny froglet hatches. Picking 'nothing' is a fair first guess, because most frog eggs need ponds.
4. (truefalse) For most of their life, a cicada is a winged adult singing in the trees.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A cicada spends years under the ground as a young nymph, sucking juice from roots. Some cicadas stay down there for 13 or even 17 years. Then they climb up, moult, and live as singing adults for only a few weeks. The adult is the short last stage, not the whole life.
5. (truefalse) Your thyroid makes a hormone of the same kind as a tadpole's.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Your thyroid makes the same kind of hormone. It helps you grow and use energy. It needs iodine, which is why salt in India has iodine added.
6. (write) Some towns put small fish that eat wrigglers into ponds and water tanks. Explain how this stops mosquitoes. What would happen to the mosquitoes if the fish were taken away?  
   **Answer:** The fish eat the wrigglers, so fewer wrigglers become tumblers and flying adults. This breaks the mosquito life cycle. Without the fish, more wrigglers would survive and there would be more mosquitoes.. *Why:* Taking away one stage of a life cycle stops the next stage from happening.
7. (truefalse) A dragonfly nymph lives in water and breathes underwater before it grows wings.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Dragonfly nymphs live in ponds and have gills. When they are fully grown they climb out, moult, and fly away as adults with wings.
8. (fill) The small groups of cells inside a caterpillar that wait, then grow into wings, legs and eyes, are called ____ discs.  
   **Answer:** ['imaginal']. *Why:* Imaginal discs. 'Imago' is the old scientific word for an adult insect.
9. (order) Put these events inside a fertilised hen's egg in order, over 21 days.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* The heart beats after about two days. Feathers start after about ten. Just before hatching, the chick pokes into the air pocket and takes a first breath of air, then cracks the shell on about day 21.
10. (mcq) In the 1600s, many people thought caterpillars grew out of rotting leaves or mud. Maria Sibylla Merian watched caterpillars for years and painted each one with the plant they ate. What did her careful watching show?  
   Options: Each caterpillar hatches from an egg laid by a butterfly or moth on their own food plant / Butterflies grow out of flowers / Caterpillars never eat  
   **Answer:** Each caterpillar hatches from an egg laid by a butterfly or moth on their own food plant. *Why:* Merian raised caterpillars and recorded every stage, from egg to adult, with their food plant. This helped show that small animals come from eggs, not from mud. Picking 'flowers' mixes up where butterflies feed with where they come from.
11. (write) A frog lays hundreds of eggs, but only a few become frogs. A hen lays fewer eggs and looks after her chicks. Give one good thing about each way.  
   **Answer:** Lots of eggs: even if most are eaten, a few will survive, and the parent doesn't need to stay. Fewer eggs with care: each chick has a much better chance, because the hen keeps them warm and safe.. *Why:* Both ways work, in different lives. Scientists compare these two ways: many young with no care, or a few young with lots of care.
12. (perspective) Silk is made from the cocoons of silk moths. Usually the pupa inside is killed, so the thread stays in one long piece. People see this in different ways.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives a clear view with a reason, for example the moth's life or the weavers' work. It fairly states what the other side has right. There is no single right answer.. *Why:* This is a question about fairness, not a fact. Both sides have real reasons, and a good answer weighs them.
13. (write) The axolotl is a kind of salamander from Mexico. Most axolotls keep their gills and tail and live in water all their life, yet they grow up and lay eggs. Does an axolotl still have a life cycle? Use what you know about hormones to explain why they stay in their water form.  
   **Answer:** Yes. An axolotl still goes from egg to young to adult who lays eggs, and round again. They skip the big change because their body does not start metamorphosis, like a tadpole with too little thyroid hormone. A strong answer links the missing change to the hormone signal.

   **Answer:** Yes. My body breaks down food and uses some of it to build new cells, like skin, blood and muscle. So some of today's rice will become part of me.. *Why:* Like a tadpole using their tail, or a chick using their yolk, your body takes food apart and builds with the pieces.    **Answer:** A tiny flat patch of cells inside the caterpillar, then a big wing inside the chrysalis.. *Why:* The wing was there all along as a tiny flat pocket of cells, waiting. In the chrysalis, these cells grow fast, using the caterpillar's material.

## Applications
- Poultry farms and hatcheries keep eggs in warm boxes called incubators for 21 days, turning them, to hatch chicks.
- Health workers stop dengue and malaria by finding and removing still water where mosquito wrigglers grow.
- Some towns put small fish that eat wrigglers into ponds and tanks, to break the mosquito life cycle.
- India makes silk from the cocoons of silkworm moths: mulberry silk in Karnataka, tasar in Jharkhand, and muga and eri silk in Assam.
- Farmers time their pest control to the right stage of an insect's life cycle, because caterpillars, not moths, eat the crops.
- In many hospitals, doctors check a newborn baby's thyroid hormone, because this hormone helps babies grow.
- Scientists use frogs and tadpoles to check whether ponds and rivers are clean, because their soft skin takes in what is in the water.
- Butterfly parks and gardens grow both nectar plants and caterpillar food plants, so butterflies can finish their whole life cycle.
- Fruit flies, which have a life cycle of about ten days, help scientists study genes and growth in laboratories.
- Doctors study how a tadpole rebuilds their body to learn how wounds heal and how the body regrows parts.

## Open questions
- **How did complete metamorphosis, with a pupa stage, first begin?** It started more than 300 million years ago. Fossils of soft larvae and pupae are rare, so scientists rely on genes and living insects, and they still disagree.
- **How much does a butterfly remember of being a caterpillar?** A 2008 experiment with moths suggests some memory survives. But memory is hard to test in insects, and scientists don't know how it would pass through the rebuilding.
- **How does each waiting cell inside a caterpillar know whether to become a wing, a leg or an eye?** Scientists know many of the genes involved. But how all the signals work together, at the right time and place, is still being studied.
- **Why do some frogs skip the tadpole stage altogether?** Some frogs in the Western Ghats hatch as froglets on land. Scientists have ideas, like avoiding fish in streams, but they can't yet say for certain which reasons mattered most.
- **Why are frogs disappearing from so many places in the world?** A skin disease caused by a fungus, loss of ponds, poisons and changing weather all play a part. How much each one matters is different in each place, and still argued about.
- **What, if anything, does a pupa feel?** We can't ask a pupa. Their nerves are being rebuilt. Nobody knows whether there is anything it is like to be a chrysalis.
- **Could a body be taught to rebuild itself like a tadpole, to heal injuries in people?** Scientists study tadpoles, which can regrow a lost tail at some stages. Using this in people is a hope, not yet a result.

- In Tadpole Watch, a tadpole's tail was 18 millimetres long on day 20. On day 35 it was 8 millimetres long. How much shorter did it get? **10 millimetres shorter.**. The tail started at 18 millimetres and ended at 8 millimetres. To find the difference, take away: 18 minus 8 is 10. So the tail got 10 millimetres shorter. You can check by adding: 8 plus 10 is 18. ## Warm-up
- Name the four stages of a butterfly. → Egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly.
- What is a baby frog called? → A tadpole.
- How many days does a chick grow inside their egg? → About 21 days, which is three weeks.
- Does a tadpole's tail fall off? → No. It is broken down and used to build the frog.
- Butterflies come out of a cocoon. True or false? → False. Butterflies come out of a chrysalis. Moths come out of cocoons.
- Which part of an egg is the chick's food? → The yolk.
- What is the big change from tadpole to frog called? → Metamorphosis.
- Grasshopper or butterfly: which has a pupa? → The butterfly. Grasshoppers have nymphs that slowly grow wings.
- Where do mosquito babies live? → In still water.
- What tells a tadpole's body when to change? → A hormone, a chemical message in their blood.
