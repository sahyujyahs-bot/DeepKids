# Plant life cycles: seven named stages, and why some plants live four months and others for centuries
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.plant-life-cycle.L5`

**Summary.** A mustard plant grows from a seed, flowers, scatters new seeds and dies, all in about four months. A coconut palm does not flower until about the eighth year, and then makes coconuts for seventy years more. Both go through the same seven stages, from germination to seed dispersal. A plant that lives for one season is called an annual. A plant that lives for many years is called a perennial. Your child names the seven stages and compares how long different plants live. Then they run the Bad Year Test with a die. It shows where a short life leaves more seeds, and where a long life does.

**Intro.** *Rice, wheat, ragi, moong and mustard plants all die a few months after they are sown. Mango, coconut and tamarind trees live longer than people do. Why do some plants live for one season, when other plants can live for hundreds of years?* A short life is not a mistake, and a short-lived plant is not a weak plant. A mustard plant that dies in April has already left thousands of seeds. In this unit you first learn the names of the seven stages that every flowering plant goes through. You see how ferns, mosses and pines make new plants without any flowers. You sort the plants in your kitchen by how long they live. Then you roll a die for twelve years of weather, in two different places. You count the seeds that a short-lived plant and a long-lived plant leave in each place.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the seven stages of a flowering plant's life cycle in order, and say what happens in each stage.
1. I can say how flowering plants differ from non-flowering plants, and how ferns, mosses and conifers make new plants.
2. I can sort plants into annuals, biennials and perennials, and give Indian examples of each.
3. I can compare how long different plants live, and what share of their life passes before they first flower.
4. I can explain why annuals leave more seeds in places that are often cleared, and perennials in places that are left alone.
5. I can estimate the age of a plant from a record, from tree rings or from how fast the plant grows, and say how sure I am.

## Terms
- **Life cycle**: All the stages in the life of a living being, from the start of their life until they make young of their own. Then the young go through the same stages. *e.g.* A moong seed grows into a plant, the plant flowers, and the pods hold new moong seeds.
- **Germination**: The first stage of a plant's life cycle. The seed takes in water, and a root and then a shoot grow out. *e.g.* Moong seeds on a wet cloth germinate in about a day.
- **Growth**: The stage in which a plant becomes bigger and makes more roots, stems and leaves. The leaves make the plant's food from light, air and water. *e.g.* A mustard seedling grows from 2 leaves to a plant as tall as your knee in about five weeks.
- **Flowering**: The stage in which a plant makes flowers. Flowers are the parts of the plant that make seeds. *e.g.* A mustard plant opens the first yellow flowers about six weeks after sowing.
- **Pollination**: The carrying of pollen from the male part of a flower to the female part of a flower of the same kind. Insects, birds, bats or the wind carry the pollen. *e.g.* A bee who visits one pumpkin flower and then another carries pollen between them on her hairs.
- **Fertilisation**: The joining of a male cell from a pollen grain with an egg cell inside the flower. A seed starts to grow only after fertilisation. *e.g.* After pollen lands on a bhindi flower, each pollen grain grows a thin tube down to an egg cell.
- **Fruit and seed formation**: The stage after fertilisation. The fertilised egg cells grow into seeds, and the part of the flower around them grows into a fruit. *e.g.* A bhindi flower falls after one day, and the small green pod left behind grows longer every day.
- **Seed dispersal**: The carrying of seeds away from the parent plant by wind, water, animals or a bursting pod. *e.g.* A ripe mustard pod splits when dry and throws the seeds a short way.
- **Flowering plant**: A plant that makes flowers, and makes seeds inside a fruit. *e.g.* Mango, paddy, grass, rose, coconut and mustard are all flowering plants.
- **Non-flowering plant**: A plant that never makes a flower or a fruit. Ferns and mosses make spores. Conifers make seeds on cones. *e.g.* A fern on a wet wall, a cushion of moss and a pine tree are non-flowering plants.
- **Spore**: A single cell, far too small to see alone, that can grow into a new fern or moss. A spore holds no young plant and almost no food. *e.g.* The brown dots under a fern leaf are cases full of spores.
- **Cone**: The part of a conifer that makes pollen or seeds. The seeds lie on the scales of a woody cone, with no fruit around them. *e.g.* A chir pine cone from the Himalaya holds two winged seeds on each scale.
- **Annual**: A plant that goes through the whole life cycle within one year or one growing season, and then dies. *e.g.* Paddy, wheat, ragi, moong, mustard, sunflower and marigold are annuals.
- **Biennial**: A plant that takes two years. In the first year the plant grows leaves and stores food. In the second year the plant flowers, makes seeds and dies. *e.g.* A carrot stores food in the root in year one. If left in cool ground, the plant flowers in year two.
- **Perennial**: A plant that lives for more than two years. Most perennials flower many times. *e.g.* Mango, coconut, neem, tamarind, banyan, tea and curry leaf plants are perennials.
- **Lifespan**: The length of time from the start of a living being's life to their death. *e.g.* A moong plant has a lifespan of about 3 months. A coconut palm has a lifespan of about 80 years.
- **First flowering age**: The age at which a plant flowers for the first time. Before that age, the plant only grows. *e.g.* A mustard plant first flowers at about 6 weeks. A coconut palm first flowers at about 8 years.
- **Generation**: One full round of a life cycle, from a seed to the seeds that the grown plant makes. *e.g.* Mustard can pass through three generations in one year, if there is water. A coconut palm takes about eight years for one.
- **Disturbance**: An event that clears the plants from a piece of ground. Floods, fire, landslides, ploughing and digging are disturbances. *e.g.* A river in flood strips the plants from the bank. A plough clears a field before every crop.
- **Trade-off**: A choice in which having more of one thing means having less of another. *e.g.* A plant has only so much food. Food spent on seeds this year cannot be spent on roots and wood for next year.

## Explanations
### The seven stages of a flowering plant's life cycle · *defined*
**Definition:** A life cycle is all the stages from the start of a life until that living being makes young. A flowering plant goes through seven stages, always in the same order.

The first stage is germination: the seed takes in water, and a root and a shoot grow out. The second is growth: the plant makes roots, stems and leaves, and the leaves make food. The third is flowering. The fourth is pollination: pollen is carried from the male part of a flower to the female part. The fifth is fertilisation: a male cell from the pollen joins an egg cell inside the flower. The sixth is fruit and seed formation: each fertilised egg cell grows into a seed, and a fruit grows around the seeds. The seventh is seed dispersal: the seeds are carried away from the parent plant. Then a seed germinates, and the stages begin again. If any one stage fails, no new seeds are made. Pollination and fertilisation are different stages. Pollination brings the pollen to the flower. Fertilisation happens afterwards, inside the flower, where nobody can see.
- e.g. Bhindi: the seed germinates in about a week. The plant flowers after about six weeks, and each flower lasts one day.
- e.g. Mango: bees and flies pollinate the tiny flowers in spring. The fruit and the big seed form over about four months.
- e.g. Paddy: wind carries the pollen. Each fertilised flower forms one grain, which is a fruit with one seed inside.
### Flowering and non-flowering plants: the same round without flowers · *defined*
**Definition:** Flowering plants make seeds inside fruits. Non-flowering plants make new plants without any flower or fruit. Ferns and mosses use spores, and conifers make seeds on cones.

Most plants around us are flowering plants. Grass, paddy, coconut and banyan all flower, even when the flowers are small and green. Ferns never flower. Under a fern leaf are brown dots, which are cases of spores. A spore is one cell with no young plant inside and almost no food. A spore that lands on a damp spot grows into a flat green plant smaller than a fingernail. Male cells swim across that tiny plant through a film of water to reach an egg cell. From the fertilised egg cell the leafy fern grows. So a fern still has germination, growth, fertilisation and dispersal. The stages a fern does not have are flowering, pollination and fruit formation. Mosses also use spores, which form in small capsules on thin stalks. Conifers, such as pine and deodar, make pollen in small cones. Wind carries the pollen to bigger seed cones. The seeds form on the cone scales, with no fruit around them.
- e.g. Chir pine and deodar in the Himalaya are conifers. A chir pine cone takes about two years to ripen.
- e.g. Ferns grow on wet walls, wells and tree trunks all over Kerala, the Nilgiris and Meghalaya.
- e.g. Moss forms green cushions on shaded, damp bricks during the monsoon.
### Annual, biennial and perennial: three lengths of life · *defined*
**Definition:** An annual lives for one growing season. A biennial lives for two years and flowers in the second. A perennial lives for more than two years.

The names come from the Latin word for year. An annual germinates, grows, flowers, makes seeds and dies within one year, often within three or four months. When the parent dies, only the seeds are alive. A biennial spends the first year growing leaves and storing food in a thick root or a bulb. In the second year the plant uses that food to flower, and then dies. A perennial lives on after making seeds. Some perennials are trees with wood. Others are soft plants that die back to the ground in the dry season and grow again from roots or underground stems. A woody stem is a good sign of a perennial, but it is not a sure test. Banana and ginger have no wood, and both are perennials. The same kind of plant can also live differently in different places. A chilli plant often dies in a cold winter, but lives for several years in a warm place.
- e.g. Annuals: paddy, wheat, ragi, bajra, moong, mustard, groundnut, sunflower, marigold.
- e.g. Biennials: carrot, beetroot, cabbage and onion, when they are left to flower in cool places.
- e.g. Perennials: mango, coconut, jackfruit, tamarind, neem, tea, coffee, pepper vine, banana, ginger, grass in a lawn.
### First flowering age, and flowering once or many times · *defined*
**Definition:** The first flowering age is how old a plant is at the first flowering. Some plants flower once and die. Others flower again and again.

Every plant grows for a while before flowering. A mustard plant lives about 120 days and first flowers at about day 40. So one third of the life passes before the first flower. A coconut palm lives about 80 years and first flowers at about 8 years, which is one tenth of the life. A mango tree grown from a seed first flowers at about 5 years. That tree can live 100 years, so one twentieth of the life passes first. All annuals and biennials flower in one season and then die. Most perennials flower many times. A few perennials grow for many years, flower only once and then die. Neelakurinji, a shrub of the Palani and Munnar hills, grows for 12 years. Then all the shrubs on a hillside flower together, make seeds and die. The talipot palm of Kerala and Sri Lanka grows for many decades, often put at 30 to 80 years, and flowers once. A plant that flowers once can put all the stored food into one very large seed crop.
- e.g. Mustard: first flower at about 40 days, in a life of about 120 days.
- e.g. Coconut palm: first flower at about 8 years, then flowers every month for about 70 years.
- e.g. Neelakurinji flowered across the hills in 1994, 2006 and 2018, each time 12 years apart.
### Why there are both annuals and perennials: a trade-off · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant has only so much food to spend. An annual spends nearly all of it on seeds in one season. A perennial keeps some for roots, wood and buds, so the plant can live to make seeds again.

Food spent on seeds cannot be spent on staying alive. This is a trade-off. An annual moves almost everything from the leaves and stem into the seeds, and the plant dies. A perennial holds food back to build wood and deep roots. So a perennial makes no seeds at all for the first years. The reward comes later. A grown tree has leaves high in the light and roots deep in the ground, so the tree can make a large seed crop every year. Which way leaves more seeds? That depends on how safe it is to be a grown plant. A seed is small, and most seedlings die. A grown tree usually lives through the next year. So where grown plants are safe, living on is the better bet. But a flood, a fire or a plough kills grown plants and takes away all the years that the perennial spent growing. An annual loses only one season, and new plants grow from seeds in the soil. The Bad Year Test in this unit lets you count this with a die.
- e.g. A paddy plant ends as dry straw, with almost all the food moved into the grain.
- e.g. A mango seedling makes no fruit for about five years, and then fruits for a hundred years.
- e.g. In 2018, the cyclone called Gaja blew down lakhs of coconut palms in Tamil Nadu. A new palm needs about eight years to bear.
### Where annuals live and where perennials live · *defined*
**Definition:** Annuals are most common where the ground is often cleared or where the growing season is short. Perennials are most common where plants are left alone for many years.

An event that clears the plants from the ground is called a disturbance. Floods, fires, landslides, ploughing and digging are disturbances. On ground that is disturbed often, such as a riverbank, a roadside or a ploughed field, most of the plants are annuals. They germinate fast, flower young and leave seeds before the next disturbance. Deserts also have many annuals. After a good rain in the Thar, small annuals germinate, flower and seed within six to eight weeks. Then they wait as seeds, sometimes for years. In an old forest the ground is rarely cleared, and almost every plant is a perennial. When a plot of land is cleared and then left alone, the plants change in a regular order. Annual weeds cover the plot in the first year. Perennial grasses and herbs follow, then shrubs, and after many years trees. Annuals are a small share of all flowering plant species, yet they cover a large part of the world's farmland.
- e.g. The weeds in a pavement crack or on a building site are mostly annuals.
- e.g. A sacred grove, such as a kavu in Kerala or a devarakadu in Kodagu, is full of very old perennials.
- e.g. A paddy field is ploughed before every crop, so farmers make a disturbance on purpose.
### How long plants live: from two months to nearly five thousand years · *defined*
**Definition:** Lifespan is the time from the start of a life to death. Plant lifespans range from about two months to several thousand years.

Thale cress, a small weed that scientists grow in laboratories, goes from seed to seed in about two months. Moong lives about three months, mustard about four and paddy about five. A banana stem lives about one year, but new stems keep coming from the base of the clump. A coconut palm lives about 80 years. Many trees outlive every person who planted them. The Great Banyan in the botanic garden at Howrah is said to be at least 250 years old. A peepal tree at Anuradhapura in Sri Lanka was planted more than 2,200 years ago. A giant sequoia in California lived for more than 3,000 years. The oldest living single tree with a counted age is a bristlecone pine in California, at about 4,800 years. One bristlecone lifespan is as long as 14,400 mustard lifespans, one after another. The oldest trees are often small and slow-growing, and live in cold, dry places. Slow growth makes dense wood that insects and rot cannot easily enter.
- e.g. Thimmamma Marrimanu, a banyan in Andhra Pradesh, is said to be more than 550 years old.
- e.g. Some baobab trees in Africa have been dated to more than 2,000 years.
- e.g. A talipot palm lives for many decades and then dies after one flowering.
### How we find out a plant's age · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant's age can be known from a written record, counted from growth rings in the wood, measured by carbon dating, or estimated from how fast the plant grows.

The surest way is a record of the planting. The peepal at Anuradhapura has a planting date in old chronicles. The second way is to count rings. In places with seasons, a tree adds one ring of wood each year. Scientists drill out a core of wood as thin as a pencil, so the tree is not cut down. Tropical trees with no clear seasons often have no clear rings. The third way is carbon dating, which measures how old a piece of wood is. Baobabs were dated this way, because old baobabs are hollow. The fourth way is to estimate from the rate of growth. A coconut palm makes about 12 to 14 leaves a year, and each fallen leaf leaves a ring-shaped scar on the trunk. So the number of scars divided by 13 gives a rough age. An estimate from a growth rate is only as good as the rate. Plants grow faster in a wet year and slower in a dry year. So an honest estimate is given as a range, such as 'between 20 and 30 years'.
- e.g. In 1957, cores from a bristlecone pine showed it was more than 4,600 years old.
- e.g. A coconut trunk with 390 leaf scars is about 30 years old, because 390 divided by 13 is 30.
- e.g. In the Leaf Clock in this unit, you estimate the age of a potted plant from the leaves made in three weeks.
### How a plant passes from growth to flowering · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant flowers when a chemical signal made in the leaves reaches the buds. The leaves make the signal when the plant is big enough and the season is right.

Many plants tell the season by the length of the night. In 1934, James Knott covered parts of spinach plants to find which part senses this. He found that the leaves sense it, and the buds do not. In 1936, the scientist Mikhail Chailakhyan gave only the leaves of a chrysanthemum the right day length. The buds, which never had it, still made flowers. He said that a signal must travel from the leaves to the buds, and he named the signal florigen. Nobody could find florigen for seventy years. In 2007, two teams found it. Florigen is a small protein made in the leaves. The protein travels in the plant's sap to the buds, and there switches on the genes that build a flower. A young plant does not answer the season until they have grown enough leaves. So the first flowering age depends on the size of the plant and on the season together. Plants sense the season and their own size, and then flower. Some scientists describe this as a choice the plant makes, and others describe the response as automatic.
- e.g. Chrysanthemums flower when nights grow long, and so do many paddy varieties.
- e.g. A leaf from a flowering plant, grafted onto a plant that is not flowering, can make that plant flower.
- e.g. Farmers light chrysanthemum fields at night to delay the flowers until a festival.
### Our food comes mostly from annuals · *defined*
**Definition:** Most of the food energy that people eat comes from annual plants, above all rice, wheat and maize. Farmers chose annuals because an annual puts a large share of the plant into seeds.

Look at a ripe paddy plant. About half of the dry weight of the plant above the ground is grain. No wild perennial puts that much into seeds, because a perennial keeps food back for next year. About ten thousand years ago, people began to save and sow the seeds of wild annual grasses and pulses. Each year they kept seed from the plants with the most and biggest grains. An annual also suits a ploughed field, because ploughing is a disturbance and annuals do well after one. Rice, wheat and maize together now give people about half of their food energy. Perennials feed us too. Coconut, banana, mango, jackfruit, tea, coffee, pepper and sugarcane are perennials. A perennial crop is not sown each year, and the roots hold the soil all year. But a farmer must wait some years for the first harvest. Many farmers in Kerala and Karnataka grow both together. Under coconut palms they grow banana, pepper, ginger and vegetables.
- e.g. A thali with rice, sambar, a vegetable and curd: the rice and the dal both come from annuals.
- e.g. Coconut oil, pepper and tamarind in the same thali come from perennials.
- e.g. After the cyclone of 2018, farmers in Tamil Nadu grew groundnut and banana between their new coconut seedlings.
### Should our grain crops be perennial? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that perennial plants hold soil better than annual crops. They disagree about whether perennial grains can feed as many people.

Every year, farmers plough and sow the fields of annual grain again. Bare, ploughed soil can wash away in heavy rain. A perennial grain would be sown once and harvested for years. In China, scientists at Yunnan University crossed rice with a wild perennial rice from Africa. Their perennial rice, called PR23, gave eight harvests in four years from one sowing. The yields were close to those of annual rice. In Kansas in the United States, the Land Institute breeds a perennial grass whose grain is sold as Kernza. Its roots go three metres deep. So far, its grain yield is much lower than the yield of wheat.
- e.g. Perennial rice PR23 has been grown by farmers in Yunnan since 2018.
- e.g. Kernza grain is used in some bread and breakfast foods in the United States.
- e.g. Sugarcane is a perennial grass that Indian farmers already cut and regrow for two or three years.
- **Scientists who breed perennial grains, such as Fengyi Hu in Yunnan and the Land Institute in Kansas:** A perennial grain is sown once. The farmer saves seed, ploughing and many days of work each year. Deep roots hold the soil and find water in a dry spell. The yields can be raised by breeding, as the yields of annuals once were.
- **Many crop scientists who work on annual grains:** A plant that saves food for next year has less to put into grain. This is the trade-off, and breeding cannot remove it fully. Weeds and diseases also build up in a field that is never ploughed. With more people to feed, lower yields would mean clearing more forest for fields.
- **Farmers who grow annuals and perennials together:** The two can share one field. Trees and perennial crops hold the soil and give something every year. Annual grains between them give the staple food. Neither one has to do everything.
### Does a tree grow old? · *debated*
**Definition:** Humans and other animals grow weaker with age, and this is called ageing. Scientists disagree about whether long-lived trees age in this way.

A tree makes new leaves, new buds and a new layer of wood every year. So the working parts of a very old tree are always young. In 2001, scientists compared bristlecone pines from 23 years old to more than 4,700 years old. The old trees made pollen and seeds as healthy as those of the young trees. In 2020, scientists in China studied ginkgo trees up to 667 years old. The growing layer under the bark showed no sign of ageing. Very old trees usually die from outside causes, such as storms, fire, drought, fungi or the axe. But old trees also carry more wounds and rot, and they must lift water higher.
- e.g. A bristlecone pine about 4,700 years old still made seeds that germinated well.
- e.g. Old ginkgo trees kept making new wood and defence chemicals at the same rate as young ones.
- e.g. An old banyan replaces a rotted trunk with new trunks that grow down from the branches.
- **Scientists who say some trees do not age:** The growing points of an old tree work as well as those of a young tree. Nothing inside the tree sets a time limit. A tree dies when a storm, a fire, a drought or a disease kills them, and the chance of that adds up over the years.
- **Scientists who say trees age slowly:** No tree lives for ever, and the oldest trees grow very little. Damage, rot and small errors in the cells build up with time. The tree becomes less able to survive the next storm or drought. That slow weakening is ageing, even if it takes thousands of years.
### One life cycle, one trip round the Sun · *idea*
An annual lives for one growing season because the Earth has seasons. The Earth's axis is tilted, so the days grow longer and shorter as the Earth goes round the Sun. In much of India the year also has one rainy season, which the monsoon brings. An annual fits a whole life into the wet or mild part of one trip round the Sun, and waits out the rest as a seed. Near the equator, where every month is warm and wet, annuals are few. Some scientists think that the first annuals arose from perennials when climates became drier or more seasonal. The same change, from perennial to annual, has happened many separate times in different plant families. Nobody has watched it happen, so this is an idea that fits the evidence. A year on Mars lasts 687 Earth days. Nobody knows how a mustard plant would time a life there.
- e.g. Wheat in Punjab is sown in the cool season and harvested before the hot summer.
- e.g. Ragi in Karnataka is sown with the monsoon and harvested when the rains end.
- e.g. In the rainforests of the Andaman Islands, almost every plant is a perennial.

## Mostly agreed
- Every flowering plant goes through the same stages in the same order: germination, growth, flowering, pollination, fertilisation, fruit and seed formation, and seed dispersal. *How we know:* Gardeners, farmers and botanists have watched thousands of kinds of plants from seed to seed. When pollination or fertilisation is stopped in a test, no seeds form.
- Ferns and mosses make new plants from spores, and conifers make seeds on cones. None of them ever flowers. *How we know:* Spores from a fern leaf have been grown on damp soil under a microscope many times. The tiny flat plant and then the new fern can be watched.
- Annuals put a much larger share of their body into seeds than perennials do. *How we know:* Scientists dry and weigh whole plants and their seeds. About half of the dry weight of a paddy plant above the ground is grain.
- Annuals are most common on ground that is often disturbed and in dry places with short growing seasons. *How we know:* Counts of plants on cleared plots, riverbanks and fields show mostly annuals in the first years. Annuals are most common in hot, dry regions.
- Some trees live for thousands of years. *How we know:* Ring counts on cores of wood from bristlecone pines give ages of more than 4,600 years. Carbon dating of baobab wood gives more than 2,000 years for the oldest.
- A signal made in the leaves, called florigen, tells the buds to make flowers. *How we know:* Giving only the leaves the right day length makes a plant flower. In 2007 the signal was found to be a small protein that travels from leaf to bud.

## How we found out
**Edmund Schulman, an American scientist who studied tree rings, Schulman first cored the bristlecone pines in 1953. He sampled Methuselah in 1957, and his account was published in 1958..** Until the 1950s, most people believed that the biggest trees were the oldest. The giant sequoias of California can be as tall as a 20-storey building or more, and stumps of cut sequoias showed more than 3,000 rings. Edmund Schulman was a scientist at the University of Arizona who studied tree rings. He wanted very old trees, because their rings record the wet and dry years of the past. He noticed that the oldest trees he found were not in rich forests. They grew slowly in dry, rocky places. In 1953 he heard of old pines high in the White Mountains of California. The trees there were short and twisted, with more bare wood than bark. Schulman drilled out thin cores of wood, which does not kill a tree, and counted the rings under a microscope. Many trees were more than 4,000 years old. In 1957 he worked out that one tree was more than 4,600 years old. That tree is now known as Methuselah. He wrote that these trees live so long because of their hard life. Slow growth makes dense wood full of resin, which insects, rot and fire cannot easily destroy.  
*What people believed before:* That the biggest trees, growing in the best places, are the oldest. Ring counts showed that small, slow-growing pines on dry mountains were many centuries older than the giant sequoias.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A plant that lives longer is a more successful plant.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: A long-lived tree makes seeds for hundreds of years, and is still standing when short-lived plants are gone.  
  Against: On ground that is often cleared, annuals leave more seeds than perennials. Annuals cover riverbanks, fields and deserts that most trees cannot hold.
- **An annual plant dies because the plant is weak or worn out.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: The plant turns yellow and dry soon after the seeds ripen, which looks like wearing out.  
  Against: The plant moves the food from the leaves into the seeds, and that empties the leaves. When the flowers of a soybean plant are picked off, the plant stays green much longer.
- **The biggest trees are the oldest trees.** (Many people, and many scientists before the 1950s.; *rejected*)  
  For: A tree gets bigger every year, so a very big tree must have had many years to grow.  
  Against: The oldest trees with counted ages are small bristlecone pines on dry, cold mountains. A tree in good soil can grow huge in a few hundred years.
- **Perennial grains could replace annual rice and wheat.** (Scientists who breed perennial grains.; *debated*)  
  For: Perennial rice in China has given eight harvests from one sowing, with yields close to annual rice. Perennial roots hold the soil.  
  Against: A plant that saves food for next year has less for grain. Perennial wheat-like grains still yield far less than wheat. Weeds and diseases build up in unploughed fields.
- **Neelakurinji shrubs count twelve years before they flower.** (Some botanists who study plants that flower together after many years.; *debated*)  
  For: Flowerings have been recorded every 12 years since 1838. Shrubs on different hills flower in the same year.  
  Against: Nobody has found a part of the plant that counts years. Other scientists think the shrubs flower when they reach a certain size or store of food, and that the weather keeps them in step.

## Roots
In this unit you named the seven stages of a plant's life, and you ran the Bad Year Test with a short-lived and a long-lived plant. Here we ask what short and long lives show about life, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the Bad Year Test, the perennial left more seeds in the old grove, where the ground was rarely cleared. The annual left more seeds on the riverbank, where the ground was cleared often. Neither plant won in both places. So a short life suits ground that is often cleared, and a long life suits ground that is left alone.  
*Habit it breaks:* We rank lives by their length. We think of a tree that lives 500 years as a greater plant than a weed that lives four months. The count of seeds shows that each length of life works well in one kind of place and badly in the other.  
*Evidence:* In the Bad Year Test, the same die rolls decided the years for both plants. Over three runs of 12 years, the perennial usually left more seeds in the old grove, and the annual usually left more on the riverbank. In the world, annuals cover riverbanks, fields and deserts, and old forests are almost all perennials. If your own totals were different, write down what you found.

## Story: Six Times Blue
*Valli, Paatti, the clerk and the tahsildar are made up, and so is the scene in the office. These things are real. Neelakurinji is a shrub of the Palani hills, the Nilgiris and the Munnar hills. The shrubs grow for 12 years, flower together, make seeds and die. Flowerings have been recorded every 12 years since 1838, and the last ones were in 1994, 2006 and 2018. Elders of the Paliyan people of the Palani hills are said to have counted their ages in kurinji flowerings. Bees make honey from the flowers. The rules for an old-age pension are more complicated than in this story.*

In August the hill behind Valli's village turned blue.

It did not happen in a day. First there were a few flowers on the bushes by the path, small and pale, like scraps of sky. Then there were more. By the end of the month the whole slope was blue from the stream to the ridge, and the bees were so loud that Valli could hear them from the doorway.

She was ten years old. For all ten years those bushes had been green and nothing else. She had walked through them every day and never once seen a bud.

Paatti stood beside her, looking up at the hill. "My sixth," she said.

Three days later they took the bus to the taluk office in Kodaikanal. Paatti's knees were bad now, and she could not climb to the honey cliffs any more. A neighbour had said there was a pension for people over sixty.

The clerk was a young man from Madurai, down in the plains. He had a form, a pen and a wall full of files. Behind his chair hung a tourism poster that nobody in the office looked at any more.

"Date of birth?" he asked.

"I do not have one," said Paatti.

"Everybody has one, Amma. I mean the paper. A birth certificate. A school record."

"I was born in the forest. Nobody wrote it down. I did not go to school."

The clerk put down his pen. "Then how do I know that you are sixty?"

"I have seen the kurinji flower six times," said Paatti. "The first time I could not walk yet. My mother carried me through the flowers on her back. She told me so."

The clerk smiled, not unkindly. "Amma, flowers come every year. I have a jasmine in a pot at home. She flowers every week. If I counted my age in jasmine flowers, I would be a thousand years old."

He slid the form back across the desk. "Bring some proof. Anything written."

They sat on the bench outside, waiting for the afternoon bus. Paatti was quiet. Valli was angry, and the anger made it hard to think.

The clerk was not stupid. He was right about his jasmine. A jasmine bush flowers, and rests, and flowers again, year after year, and the bush goes on living. The mango tree by the bus stand did the same. So did the coffee bushes and the orange trees. Every plant he knew flowered again and again.

The kurinji was a different kind of plant. Valli knew the whole of the kurinji's life, because Paatti had told her every part.

After the flowers came the seeds. After the seeds, every bush on the hill dried up and died, all in the same season. The next monsoon, the ground under the dead sticks was covered in seedlings. The seedlings grew for eleven years and made nothing but leaves. In the twelfth year, every one of them flowered together. A kurinji flowered once in a whole life, and that life was twelve years long.

So the bushes that were blue today had all germinated in the same monsoon. Their parents had flowered twelve years before, and the parents of those twelve years before that.

"Paatti," said Valli. "Give me the form."

She turned it over and borrowed a pencil from the tea seller. At the bottom she wrote this year, 2018. She took away twelve and wrote 2006 above it. Then 1994. Then 1982, 1970 and 1958. Six years, one above the other.

"In 1958 you were on your mother's back," she said. "You could not walk yet. So you were born in 1957, or the year before."

"If you say so," said Paatti. "I only know the flowers."

Valli went back in alone. The clerk looked up.

"Sir," she said. "How many years have you worked in this office?"

"Four. Why?"

"In those four years, did you ever see the hills blue, before this month?"

He thought about it. "No."

"Your jasmine flowers every week and stays alive. The kurinji grows for twelve years, flowers once, and dies. All of them together. These are the years." She put the paper on his desk. "My grandmother has seen all six. I have seen one. You have seen one as well, sir. It is outside the window."

The clerk looked at the six numbers. "A girl with a pencil is not proof either," he said. But he said it slowly.

"Then read your own wall," said Valli.

He turned in his chair. The poster behind him showed a blue hillside. He had sat under it for four years. Across the top it said: KURINJI SEASON 2018. THE FLOWER THAT BLOOMS ONCE IN TWELVE YEARS.

He read it twice. Then he picked up the paper and went into the inner room, where the tahsildar sat. Valli heard a woman's voice ask a question, and then laugh.

The tahsildar came out herself. She was a grey-haired woman with spectacles on a chain. "My father was posted here in 1970," she said. "He wrote to us that the hills had gone blue. I have the letter still." She looked at Paatti through the doorway, and at the six numbers. "The planters wrote down the flowerings long before that. It has never been eleven years, and never thirteen."

She took the clerk's pen. On the form, beside Date of birth, she wrote: About 1957. Under it she wrote: Reckoned by six flowerings of kurinji, 1958 to 2018.

On the bus home, the road ran along the blue hillside for a long way. Paatti held the stamped paper in both hands.

"He thought I was counting jasmine," she said, and laughed until she coughed.

Valli pressed her forehead to the window. By next summer these bushes would be dry sticks, and under them the seedlings would be coming up. She counted on her fingers.

"At my second blue, I will be twenty-two," she said. "At my third, thirty-four."

"And I will see my seventh," said Paatti. "I intend to. Somebody has to keep the count."

**For the parent:** For the parent: the clerk knows only plants that flower again and again, so flowers cannot be a calendar for him. Valli sees that kurinji has a different life cycle. Each shrub lives 12 years and flowers once, and all the shrubs on the hills flower together. That makes each flowering a mark 12 years from the last. Six flowerings, from 1958 to 2018, give Paatti's age.

- Ask: The clerk said that flowers come every year. For which plants is he right, and why is kurinji different?
- Ask: Why does it matter that all the kurinji shrubs on the hill flower in the same year?
- Ask: Valli wrote 2018, 2006, 1994, 1982, 1970 and 1958. How did she get from those years to about 1957?
- Ask: Three things helped the clerk believe Valli: her sums, the poster and the tahsildar's letter. Which is the strongest evidence, and why?
- Ask: Could you count a person's age in mango flowerings? What would you need to know first?

- **results table:** Stem tip | New leaf joints by day 8 | New leaf joints by day 15 | New leaf joints by day 22
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess the plant's age. Tie a thread below the youngest open leaf on three stem tips. A grown-up writes the remembered age on a folded paper. (15 min); day 8: Count the new leaf joints above each thread. (5 min); day 15: Count again. (5 min); day 22: Make the final count, work out the rate, count all the leaf joints on the longest stem and estimate the age. Open the folded paper. (15 min)
- **s1 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose a potted plant. 2. Ask your child to guess the plant's age and give a reason. 3. Find three stem tips. On each, tie a thread loosely below the youngest leaf that has opened flat. 4. Write the plant's remembered age on a slip of paper, fold it and put it away.  
  *Parent says:* A leaf joint is the place where a leaf joins the stem. A plant adds new leaf joints only at the tip of a stem. How old do you think this plant is? How many new leaf joints will each tip make in three weeks? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how old the plant is. Say why. Tie a thread below the youngest open leaf. Do this on three tips.
  - Ask: What makes you think the plant is that old? (good: The plant is big, so they must be old.; The stem is long and has many leaves.; I remember when we bought the plant.)
  - Note: If nobody remembers when the plant arrived, write your best guess and mark it as a guess. A plant with one stem works too. Then use one thread, and say that the result is less sure. If you have no potted plant, sow three rajma in a cup today and use them. Their age will be known exactly.
  - Wait: Leave the plant in the usual place for one week
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 8. Count the leaf joints above each thread that carry an open leaf. Write the three numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Count only the leaves above the thread that have opened flat. A rolled-up leaf does not count yet.  
  *Child mode:* Count the new open leaves above each thread. Write the three numbers.
  - Wait: Count again one week later
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 15. Count again and write the three numbers. Ask whether the three tips are growing at the same rate.  
  *Parent says:* Count again. Are all three tips making leaves at the same rate? Why might one be slower?  
  *Child mode:* Count again. Write the numbers. Is one tip slower than the others?
  - Ask: Why might one stem tip make leaves more slowly than another? (good: That stem gets less light.; That stem is older or thinner.; It may just be chance.)
  - Wait: Make the final count one week later
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 22. 1. Make the final count. Add the three numbers and divide by 3. This is the rate for one tip in three weeks. 2. On the longest stem, count every leaf joint from the soil to the thread. Count bare joints too. 3. Divide the joints by the rate, and multiply by 3 to get weeks. 4. Open the folded paper and compare. 5. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they found and why the two ages differ.  
  *Parent says:* First find the rate: how many leaf joints does one tip make in three weeks? Now count all the joints on the longest stem. How many lots of three weeks did the stem need? Now say what you thought the age was, what you found, and why the two ages may differ.  
  *Child mode:* Find how many leaves one tip makes in three weeks. Count all the leaf joints on the longest stem. Work out the weeks. Open the paper. How close were you?
  - Ask: Your estimate and the remembered age are probably different. What could make the estimate too low or too high? (good: The plant grows faster in the rains and slower in the cold months.; Someone may have cut the stem back.; The plant was already some months old when we bought them.; Three weeks is a short time to measure a rate.)
  - Ask: How would you make the estimate better? (good: Measure the rate for longer, in more than one season.; Count on more stems.; Give the answer as a range.)
  - Note: Example: three tips made 6 leaf joints in three weeks, so one tip makes 2. The longest stem has 40 joints. 40 divided by 2 is 20 lots of three weeks, which is 60 weeks, or about 14 months. An estimate within double or half of the remembered age is a good result. Plants grow faster in warm, wet months, so a rate measured in one season does not fit the whole year. If no new leaves opened in three weeks, the plant is resting. Write that down, and measure again in a warmer month. Scientists estimate the ages of palms and of trees without rings in this same way, and they give a range.
  - Think: A coconut palm makes about 13 leaves a year, and each fallen leaf leaves a scar on the trunk. How could you find the age of a palm?

### a3 · Who Senses the Night? Read the plant experiments (20 min)
- **record:** Draw a plant with leaves and a bud. Draw an arrow for the signal and write what you think the signal does.

### p1 · Same Seed, Different Start Day: does methi flower at the same age or on the same date? (100 min, 85 days)

## Applications
- A farmer who wants carrot or cabbage seed leaves a few plants in the ground for a second year, because these are biennials.
- Flower growers light chrysanthemum fields at night, so that the flowers open later and are ready for a festival.
- Tree rings in old timber show which years were wet and which were dry. Scientists use them to study the weather of past centuries.
- Plant breeders cross rice with wild perennial relatives, to try to make rice that is sown once and harvested for years.
- Farmers in Kerala and Karnataka grow banana, pepper and ginger under coconut palms, so that the field gives something every year while the palms grow.
- A seed packet states how many days a plant takes from sowing to the first flower. A farmer uses it to plan when to sow.
- After clearing a plot of land, gardeners expect annual weeds first and perennial plants later, and plan their work around that order.
- Foresters and park staff use growth rates, rings and records to estimate the age of old trees, and decide which trees to protect.

## Spotted around us
- Weeds growing in a pavement crack or on a building site. Most of them are annuals, which flower young and leave seeds fast.
- A banana clump with stems of different heights. The oldest stem has fruited and will be cut. New stems grow from the base.
- A coconut trunk with ring-shaped scars where old leaves fell. Each year makes about 13 scars.
- A carrot or onion that has been left in the kitchen too long and has put up a flower stalk.
- A peepal or banyan tree beside a temple or a road. Ask how old the grown-ups think the tree is, and why they think so.
- Marigold or chrysanthemum flowers sold in a bunch before a festival. Farmers time the flowering for the festival day.
- A thali with rice, dal, a vegetable and curd. The rice and dal come from annuals, and the pepper, tamarind and coconut come from perennials.

## Open questions
- **How do neelakurinji shrubs count twelve years?** Nobody has found a part of the plant that keeps time. Some scientists think the shrubs flower when they reach a certain size or store of food, and that the weather keeps them in step.
- **Do very old trees age, or do they only die from outside causes?** Studies of bristlecone pines and ginkgo trees found no sign of ageing in the growing parts, but other scientists think slow damage builds up. No study has followed a tree for thousands of years.
- **Can a perennial grain ever give as much grain as annual rice and wheat?** Perennial rice in China gave yields close to annual rice in tests, but a perennial wheat-like grain still gives much less. The trade-off may be possible to break, or may not.
- **How do bamboo plants of one kind flower in the same year, over hundreds of kilometres?** Muli bamboo in Mizoram flowers about every 48 years. Scientists do not yet know what keeps separate clumps in step.
- **What was the age of the oldest tree that ever lived?** Trees rot, burn and fall, and their rings are lost. Nobody can count a tree that no longer exists, so there may have been older trees than any we know.

- In a grove with no cleared year in 12 years, the annual leaves 5 seeds a year and the perennial leaves 12 seeds a year after two growing years. Which leaves more, and how many times as many? **The perennial, with 120 seeds against 60. That is twice as many.**. The annual: 12 × 5 = 60 seeds. The perennial grows for 2 years, so it makes seeds in 10 years: 10 × 12 = 120. 120 ÷ 60 = 2. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A field is cleared when a die shows 1 or 2. About how many years in 12 should you expect to be cleared? About how many are good years? **About 4 cleared years and 8 good years.**. A 1 or 2 is 2 faces out of 6, which is one third. One third of 12 is 4. So 12 − 4 = 8 good years. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Name the seven stages of a flowering plant's life cycle in order. → Germination, growth, flowering, pollination, fertilisation, fruit and seed formation, and seed dispersal.
- What is the difference between pollination and fertilisation? → Pollination carries pollen to the flower. Fertilisation joins a male cell and an egg cell inside it.
- How does a fern make new plants? → With spores, which are single cells.
- How long does an annual plant live? → One growing season, then it dies and leaves seeds.
- What is a biennial? → A plant that grows leaves in year one, and flowers in year two.
- Name two perennial plants from a kitchen. → Tamarind and coconut, or pepper and curry leaf.
- 40 days of 120 days is what share? → One third.
- Why do we repeat a test that depends on chance? → One run can be a fluke, and the average shows the pattern.
