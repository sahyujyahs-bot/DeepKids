# Heat on the move: why smoke rises and the Sun warms you across empty space
`sci.energy.heat-moves.L4`

**Summary.** Hold your palm beside a burning diya and it feels warm. Hold it a hand's width above, and it feels far hotter. Yet air is a poor carrier of heat, and the Sun's warmth reaches your face across millions of kilometres where there is no air at all. Your child watches hot and cold drops of coloured water go opposite ways in one glass, shields a palm from a flame with a card, and tests which wrapping lets the Sun warm a tumbler most. They find two new ways heat travels, convection and radiation. They follow both from a boiling pot to sea breezes, the Sun's bubbling surface and a candle flame that turns into a ball in space.

**Intro.** *Hold your hand beside a lit diya, 10 centimetres away. It feels warm. Now hold a card between the flame and your hand. The warmth stops at once. But hold your hand above the flame and the card beside it changes nothing. Why does the card block one kind of warmth but not the other?* Last year you saw that heat creeps through a steel spoon from part to part. That is conduction. But air barely carries heat that way, and the Sun's warmth crosses empty space where there is nothing to creep through. So heat must have other ways to travel. In this unit you find them. You send hot and cold drops through a glass of water, you spin a paper spiral with rising air, and you test which wrapping lets the Sun warm a tumbler the most.

## Terms
- **Conduction**: Heat passing from one part of a solid to the next part that touches it. *e.g.* A steel ladle standing in hot sambar slowly warms all the way up its handle.
- **Convection**: Heat carried from place to place by a moving liquid or gas. *e.g.* Hot rasam rises in the middle of the pot and cooler rasam sinks at the sides.
- **Radiation**: Heat that travels as invisible light-like waves, and needs nothing to travel through. *e.g.* You feel the warmth of a bonfire on your face even when the air around you is cold.
- **Fluid**: Anything that can flow, which means any liquid or any gas. *e.g.* Water, oil and air are all fluids.
- **Expand**: To take up more room. *e.g.* Air in a balloon expands when you warm it.
- **Density**: How much stuff is packed into a space. A dense thing is heavy for its size. *e.g.* A steel katori is denser than the same size of water.
- **Convection current**: A loop of moving fluid, rising where it is warm and sinking where it is cool. *e.g.* In a pot of boiling water, the water goes up in the middle, across the top, down at the sides and round again.
- **Thermal**: A column of warm air rising from hot ground. *e.g.* Black kites circle on a thermal above a hot road without flapping.
- **Sea breeze**: A wind that blows from the cool sea onto the warm land during the day. *e.g.* On the Marina beach in Chennai, the afternoon wind comes in from the sea.
- **Infrared**: Light our eyes cannot see, which carries most of the heat we feel from warm things. *e.g.* A heater glowing dull red also sends out a lot of infrared that you feel as warmth.
- **Absorb**: To take in. A thing that absorbs radiation takes it in and gets warmer. *e.g.* A black school bag absorbs sunlight and gets hot.
- **Reflect**: To bounce back. A thing that reflects radiation sends it away and stays cooler. *e.g.* A white roof reflects most of the sunlight that lands on it.
- **Insulator**: A material that carries heat very slowly. Still air that cannot circulate is one. *e.g.* A woollen shawl keeps you warm by holding still air against your skin.
- **Land breeze**: A wind that blows from the cooler land out to the warmer sea at night. *e.g.* A fishing boat can leave a beach before dawn with the wind behind it.
- **Heat source**: Anything that gives out more heat than it takes in, such as a flame, the Sun or a hot stove. *e.g.* A lit gas burner is the heat source for a pot of dal.

## Explanations
### Three ways heat travels · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat travels from a hotter place to a cooler place in three ways. Conduction passes it from part to touching part. Convection carries it in a moving liquid or gas. Radiation sends it as invisible waves that need nothing to travel through.

In Class 3 you met conduction. A steel spoon in hot tea warms up its handle because each tiny part of the steel passes its jiggling to the next. But conduction needs something to touch, and it is slow. So heat has two more ways. Convection works in liquids and gases, which can flow. The hot part moves, and takes its heat along. Radiation does not need anything at all. A bonfire warms your face by sending out waves that cross the cold air and the air does not carry them. Most real things use more than one way at once. A pot of dal on a stove is warmed by conduction through the base, by convection in the dal, and by radiation to your face if you lean over it.
- e.g. A steel ladle in hot sambar warms its handle by conduction.
- e.g. Rice grains rising and falling in a boiling pot show convection.
- e.g. The warmth on your face from a bonfire is radiation.
### Why warm air and warm water rise · *defined*
**Definition:** When a liquid or gas is warmed, it expands and becomes less dense. The cooler fluid around it is heavier, so it sinks and pushes the lighter warm fluid up.

Heat makes the tiny particles in a fluid move faster, and they push a little further apart. The same amount of stuff now takes up more room, so each spoonful is lighter than before. That is what less dense means. Air that is warmed from 27 to 57 degrees Celsius becomes about a tenth lighter. Water warmed from 20 to 60 degrees becomes about one and a half in a hundred lighter. Surrounded by cooler, heavier fluid, the warm part is lifted, the same way a cork is lifted in water. Cooler fluid does not rise. It sinks, and that sinking is what pushes the warm fluid up. Water has one odd habit. Between 4 and 0 degrees it gets lighter again as it gets colder, which is part of why ice floats and why a lake freezes from the top.
- e.g. Smoke from an agarbatti curls upwards in still air.
- e.g. A hot-air balloon rises because the air inside is less dense than the air outside.
- e.g. Steam from an idli cooker climbs to the ceiling.
### A convection current is a loop · *defined*
**Definition:** A convection current is a loop. Fluid warms and rises in one place, spreads along the top and cools, sinks in another place, and flows back along the bottom to be warmed again.

Warm fluid cannot rise forever, because there is nowhere for it to go. It has to spread out at the top and cool. Once it is cooler it is heavier, so it sinks. At the bottom it is drawn back towards the heat. So the up and the down are not two things. They are one loop that keeps itself going for as long as the heat lasts. You can see a loop in a pot of boiling water, because rice grains ride it up the middle and down the sides. A rooftop solar water heater in India works the same way. Water heated in the black panel rises into the tank, and cool water sinks down to take its place, with no pump at all. A fridge freezer sits at the top for the same reason. The air it cools sinks and chills the food below.
- e.g. Rice grains circling in a boiling pot.
- e.g. A rooftop solar water heater, where hot water collects at the top of the tank.
- e.g. The freezer box at the top of a single-door fridge.
### Convection in the big world · *defined*
**Definition:** The same loop works at every size. On a beach in the day, land warms faster than sea, so air rises over the land and cool sea air flows in as a sea breeze. At night the land cools faster and the wind turns round.

Land warms and cools quickly. The sea warms and cools slowly, because it is deep and the water mixes. On a sunny afternoon the land is hotter, so air over it rises. Cooler air from over the sea slides in underneath to take its place. This is the sea breeze, and it is why the Marina beach in Chennai is pleasant in the evening. At night the land cools below the sea and the loop runs the other way, as a land breeze. Sailors and fishing communities around the world have used both winds, leaving with the land breeze and coming home with the sea breeze. Birds do the same with a thermal. Black kites in Indian cities circle upwards on rising warm air above hot roads and roofs, without flapping. Part of the monsoon works like a sea breeze that lasts for months, as the land heats up through the summer and pulls in moist air from the Indian Ocean. The full story has more in it, such as the Himalaya and ocean currents.
- e.g. A sea breeze on Kozhikode beach in the afternoon.
- e.g. A black kite bird circling over a hot tin roof in Madurai.
- e.g. The summer monsoon winds, partly pulled in by the heating land.
### Convection inside the Earth and the Sun · *debated*
**Definition:** Convection also happens in solid-looking rock deep inside the Earth, and in the glowing gas at the Sun's surface. Scientists agree that both happen. They still argue about how much the Earth's slow rock currents move the plates on the surface.

Under the Earth's thin crust, the mantle is made of hot rock that is so squeezed that it flows very slowly, a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Hotter rock rises and cooler rock sinks, in giant slow loops. On the Sun, the surface is covered in bubbles of hot gas called granules, each about 1,000 kilometres across, rising and sinking in about ten minutes. This is the same loop as in a pot of boiling dal, at a size that is almost too big to picture.
- e.g. The Sun's surface looks like boiling rice when it is photographed up close.
- e.g. The mantle under India flows very slowly beneath the plate that carries us northwards.
- **Geologists who stress slab pull:** The cold, heavy edge of a plate sinks into the mantle and pulls the rest of the plate after it. On this view the sinking part of the loop is what moves the plates.
- **Geologists who stress the mantle's flow:** The slow currents of rock under the plates drag them along like a conveyor belt. Most geologists today think both ideas are partly true, and they are still working out how much each one counts.
### Radiation: heat that needs nothing to travel through · *defined*
**Definition:** Radiation is energy carried by waves of light, including light our eyes cannot see. All warm things send it out, and it can cross empty space.

A hot thing sends out energy as waves. The waves are a kind of light, and the part that carries most of the warmth you feel is called infrared. We cannot see it, but we feel it on our skin. These waves need nothing to carry them. They travel through air, through glass and through the empty space between the Sun and the Earth, which is about 150 million kilometres. Light and warmth from the Sun take about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to arrive. Every warm thing sends out infrared, and that includes you. A person sitting still gives out about as much heat as an old 100 watt bulb. A thermal camera shows this as colour. Some snakes, including pythons and pit vipers, have small pits that sense infrared, so they can find a warm mouse in the dark. In India, the physicist Anna Mani, who was born in Kerala in 1918, led the building of instruments at the India Meteorological Department in Pune to measure sunshine and solar radiation across the country.
- e.g. The Sun warming your face on the terrace.
- e.g. The warmth you feel standing near a hot tandoor with no touching.
- e.g. A thermal camera picture of a crowded classroom.
### Dark and light, shiny and dull · *defined*
**Definition:** When radiation lands on a thing, it can be absorbed, reflected, or pass through. Dark, dull things absorb the most and get warmest. White and shiny things reflect the most and stay coolest.

A black cloth in the Sun takes in nearly all the light that falls on it, and turns it into heat. A white cloth sends much of it back. A sheet of kitchen foil, with its shiny side out, reflects almost all the infrared that lands on it. That is why a solar cooker has a shiny reflector, why rescue blankets are silver, and why a thermos has silver walls. There is a second half to the rule. The things that are best at taking in radiation are also best at giving it out. A black pot cools quickly once it leaves the stove, and a shiny one stays hot for longer. In Ahmedabad, some families in hot, crowded neighbourhoods paint their tin roofs white to reflect the Sun, and the rooms below can be a few degrees cooler.
- e.g. A black school bag gets hotter than a white one in the Sun.
- e.g. A white-painted terrace stays cooler than a bare dark one.
- e.g. A silver thermos holds hot chai warm for hours.
### Which way is the heat really going? · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat always moves from a hotter place to a cooler place. There is no cold stuff that moves. When something feels cold, heat is moving out of you into it.

When you open a fridge, it feels as if the cold comes out. But what is really happening is that warm air from the kitchen goes in, and cool, heavy air from inside spills out and slides along the floor. The heat is going into the fridge, not the cold out of it. A steel plate feels colder than a wooden board in the same room because steel takes heat out of your hand faster. Likewise, 'heat rises' is not quite right. Hot air rises and hot water rises. But heat in a solid spoon travels in every direction, including downwards, and radiation goes out in every direction at once.
- e.g. Opening a fridge door on a hot day.
- e.g. A steel tumbler of cold water, which warms up from the room around it.
- e.g. A hot spoon laid flat that still warms its whole handle.
### A flame in space is a ball · *defined*
**Definition:** Convection needs gravity. Where nothing falls, as in a spacecraft, hot air does not rise, so a candle flame becomes a small round ball and many fires go out.

On Earth a candle flame is shaped like a teardrop, because the hot gas around it is lighter and rises, and fresh air flows in underneath to feed the flame. In the weightlessness of a spacecraft, nothing is heavier or lighter in the way that matters, so nothing is pushed up and fresh air does not flow in. NASA and other space agencies have burned candles in space, and the flame is a small, dim, blue ball. It only burns because oxygen slowly drifts in by itself. It is why the station has fans running all day. Without them, an astronaut's own breath would collect in a bubble round their face while they slept.
- e.g. Candle flame experiments on the space station, run by NASA.
- e.g. The fans on the space station that keep air moving.
- e.g. A candle flame on Earth with its tall teardrop shape.
### Using all three: keeping cool and keeping warm · *defined*
**Definition:** To keep a place cool, block radiation with a white or shiny roof and let rising hot air leave through high openings. To keep a place warm, trap still air, because air that cannot move carries heat very slowly.

Old Chettinad houses in Tamil Nadu have a central open courtyard and tall wooden ventilators high on the walls. Hot air collects under the roof and leaves through the top, and cooler shaded air from the courtyard flows in low down. Convection does the work. A woollen shawl, a quilt and a double-glass window work the other way. They hold a layer of still air that cannot circulate, and still air is a very poor conductor and cannot carry heat by convection either. A thermos uses all three ideas. It has two walls with nothing between them, so no conduction and no convection, and silver coating on the walls to reflect radiation.
- e.g. The high ventilators in an old Chettinad house.
- e.g. A white-painted roof in Ahmedabad.
- e.g. A woollen shawl in a Kashmir winter.

## Mostly agreed
- Heat flows by itself only from a hotter place to a cooler place. *How we know:* No one has seen it flow the other way on its own. A cold tumbler in a warm room always warms, and a hot tumbler always cools.
- When a fluid is warmed, it expands and becomes less dense, so it rises through the cooler fluid around it. *How we know:* The densities of water and air have been measured at many temperatures, and they match what hot and cold drops of dye do in a glass.
- Radiation can carry heat across empty space. *How we know:* The Sun's warmth reaches the Earth through about 150 million kilometres of nearly empty space, and spacecraft are warmed by sunlight where there is no air.
- Dark, dull surfaces absorb more radiation than light, shiny ones. *How we know:* Thermometers in sunlight rise most under black covers and least under shiny foil, in many tests since the 1800s.
- Every object above absolute zero sends out infrared radiation. *How we know:* Thermal cameras read it from people, animals, buildings and stars.
- A sea breeze blows onto the land in the day because the land warms faster than the sea. *How we know:* Weather stations along coasts record the wind turning towards the land in late morning and back at night.

## How we found out
**William Herschel, in 1800, and Eunice Foote, in 1856, 1800 and 1856.** In 1800, the astronomer William Herschel wanted to know whether the colours in sunlight carry different amounts of heat. Most people then thought of heat as a weightless fluid, and not as anything to do with light. Herschel let sunlight pass through a glass prism, which spreads it into a rainbow. He put thermometers with blackened bulbs in the different colours. The temperature rose from violet to red. Then he did something clever. He placed a thermometer just beyond the red edge, where there was no visible light at all. It got the hottest of all. So there were invisible rays that carry heat, and they behave like light. We now call them infrared. In 1856, an American scientist named Eunice Foote showed that sunlight warms a jar of carbon dioxide more than a jar of ordinary air. A man read her paper aloud for her at a science meeting.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought heat was a weightless fluid called caloric, completely separate from light. Herschel's thermometer beyond the red end of sunlight showed that heat can travel as invisible rays, like light, and Foote's jars showed that different gases take in these rays differently.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Heat rises.** (Many people, and many textbooks.; *rejected*)  
  For: Smoke, steam and hot-air balloons all rise.  
  Against: Hot fluids rise, but heat itself goes in every direction. A hot spoon warms downwards as well as upwards, and radiation goes out sideways and down.
- **Cold flows into a room when you open a window in winter.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels as if cold air pours in and cold air does move in.  
  Against: There is no cold stuff. Heat flows out of the warm room to the cold outside, and the cold air that comes in is just air that has less heat.
- **Heat is a weightless fluid called caloric that flows from hot things to cold things.** (Many scientists of the 1700s, including Antoine Lavoisier.; *rejected*)  
  For: It explained why heat flows from hot to cold, and why heat seemed to pass through things.  
  Against: Count Rumford saw in 1798 that boring a cannon made heat without end, which a fixed amount of fluid could not do. Heat is energy of motion, and radiation is light.
- **Heat and light are two completely different things that travel together in sunlight.** (Many scientists before 1800.; *rejected*)  
  For: We feel heat and see light with different senses.  
  Against: In 1800, William Herschel found that heat reached its peak just beyond the red end of sunlight spread out by a prism, where nothing was visible. Infrared turned out to be light our eyes cannot see.
- **The Sun warms the air between the Sun and us, and the warm air warms us.** (Many students.; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels warmer in the Sun than in the shade.  
  Against: Radiation passes through clear air and mostly warms the ground and our skin. If it warmed the air in between, mountain tops, which are closer to the Sun, would be warmer, but they are colder.
- **The slow currents of the Earth's mantle are what move the continents.** (Arthur Holmes proposed it in 1931, and many geologists agree.; *debated*)  
  For: The mantle does convect, and plates do move.  
  Against: Many geologists now think the pull of cold, heavy plate edges sinking into the mantle matters more than the mantle's flow underneath.

## Roots
We have watched hot and cold drops move opposite ways, and felt warmth from a flame through a gap. Now we ask what these tell us about the world and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Heat has no 'up' of its own. In your glass, the hot drop rose and the cold drop sank. Gravity pulls the heavier cold water down, and that pushes the lighter hot water up. In a spacecraft, a candle flame is a ball, because warm air does not rise there.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'heat rises' as if rising were a law of heat itself. But rising is what happens when gravity pulls the cooler, heavier fluid down. Take away gravity, and heat stops rising.  
*Evidence:* In Colour Currents, the same glass sent a hot drop up and an ice-cold drop down. The only difference was which one was heavier than the water around it. On the space station, candle flames are round, and fans must keep the air moving.

- *who am i:* You are a warm thing in a cooler room. You give off about as much heat as a 100 watt bulb, and a gentle column of warm air rises from your head all day.
- *what is life:* A black kite, a vulture or an eagle can rise for an hour without a wingbeat, lifted by warm air that the ground gives them. Living beings find the flows of the world and use them.
- *what is this world:* The sea breeze, the monsoon, slow loops in the Earth's rock and bubbles on the Sun are all the same rise and fall, at very different sizes.

Where everything is the same temperature, nothing flows. Every wind, current, cloud and living body runs because two places differ in temperature. A difference is like a 'two', and the flow is the world trying to join them back into one. The Sun keeps making new differences, so the flowing never stops. Wind, rain, rivers and life are the many that come from one steady difference, kept alive by one star.

- **Heraclitus, fragment 60 (Diels and Kranz numbering):** Heraclitus, a Greek thinker of about 500 BCE, taught that the road up and the road down are one and the same. (In a convection current, the rising and the sinking are one loop. Neither can happen without the other.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.2.3 and 6.2.4:** This text says that Being first sent out heat, and then from heat came water, and from water came food. Heat is named as the first of the many forms. (Heat is where this unit starts, too. It is the thing that moves, mixes and drives the loops in water, air and rock.)
- Science (settled): Heat flows by itself only from hotter to cooler places. This is the second law of thermodynamics, and it has never been seen to fail.
- Science (debated): How much the Earth's slow mantle currents move the plates, compared with the pull of sinking plate edges, is still argued.
- Other view, Aristotle, a Greek thinker of the 300s BCE: Aristotle taught that fire and air rise because rising is their nature. They belong up high, and heavy earth and water belong down low. On this view, rising needs no pushing from anything below.
- Practice, Hands that give heat (3 min): Sit still and breathe slowly. Hold your palms facing each other, about three centimetres apart, without touching. Keep them there for one minute. Notice the warmth between them. Where is it coming from, and where is it going? Now hold one palm above your own head, without touching your hair. Feel the warm air rising from your body.
- Ask: If you could turn off gravity for one minute in your kitchen, what would happen to the steam from a pot of rice?
- Ask: Is 'rising' something that warm air does, or something that happens between warm air and the cool air around it?
- Ask: If heat is always trying to even out, why are there still hot places and cold places in the world?

## Story: The House That Breathes
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Warm air collects near a roof and, if there is a high opening, it leaves, while cooler air comes in through a low opening. Old courtyard houses in the Chettinad region of Tamil Nadu were built with high ventilators and an open courtyard, which helps with this.*

In April, when the heat in Karaikudi made the road shimmer, Meenakshi went to stay with her Paati. Paati's house was very old. It had a long front hall, thick walls, and in the middle an open square to the sky, which the family called the muttram. High up on the walls of the hall were tall wooden ventilators, carved with flowers. They were so far above the floor that Meenakshi had never seen anyone open or close them.

Outside, the afternoon was forty-one degrees. Inside the hall, it was always cooler. Meenakshi had never asked why.

Then Ravi Mama came from Chennai with a tape measure and a plan. He was Meenakshi's mother's brother, and he built flats for a living. He walked through the hall and shook his head.

'This house wastes cool air,' he said. 'I will put glass sheets over those high ventilators, shut the doors, and put one air conditioner in this hall. Then the cool stays in.'

Paati was cutting betel leaves. She did not look up. 'A house has to breathe, Ravi.'

Mama laughed. 'Houses do not breathe, Paati. Air is just air.'

Meenakshi said nothing. But that evening she took one agarbatti from the puja shelf and asked Paati if she could light it. She stood at the low side door that led to the muttram and held the glowing stick in the doorway. The smoke did not rise straight up. It leaned into the hall. It drifted along near the floor, as if somebody very small was walking it inside. Then, far across the hall, it began to climb, and it climbed all the way to the tall ventilator and slipped out through the carved flowers.

'Mama, look,' she said. 'The smoke goes in low and goes out high.'

'Smoke goes where it likes,' said Mama, and he went to find his ladder.

By Friday, every ventilator was covered with glass. The air conditioner was fitted in the hall, with a long cable running to the wall. Mama switched it on that evening and the room grew cold. He was very pleased with himself. Paati sat in the courtyard with her betel leaves and said nothing at all.

On Saturday, at two o'clock in the afternoon, the electricity went off. In Karaikudi that happens often in April. The air conditioner went silent. Mama closed all the doors, to keep in the cold that was left.

In half an hour the hall was like the inside of a closed car. The air did not move. Mama's shirt stuck to his back. He opened the main door, but still the hall stayed hot, and the heat seemed to be gathered under the roof like a thick blanket. He carried a chair out to the courtyard, where Paati sat in the shade, and sat down heavily.

'It is cooler out here,' he said. 'Why is it cooler out here, and not in there? The doors are open now.'

Meenakshi had been watching the hall. 'Mama, the hot air is trapped,' she said. 'It has gone up to the roof, and the glass is the lid. It is like the idli cooker with the lid on.'

'But the cooker has a vent,' said Mama.

'So did our hall,' said Meenakshi. 'You covered it.'

Mama was too hot to argue. He fetched the ladder and, with Meenakshi holding the bottom, he climbed up and lifted the glass off one tall ventilator. It was stiff, and he said some words that Paati pretended not to hear.

Then Meenakshi lit the agarbatti again and held it at the low door. The smoke leaned in, just as before. It walked along the floor. And then it rose, faster than before, and went up and out through the open ventilator like a thread being pulled.

A cool breath touched Mama's ankles. It came in from the muttram, low and slow, along the stone floor. He stood very still, the way people do when something has just become clear.

'The hot air is going up and out,' he said slowly. 'And that is why the cool air comes in below. It is not the hot air leaving by itself. It is the cool air, which is heavier, coming in underneath and pushing it up.'

'And it only works if there is a way out at the top and a way in at the bottom,' said Meenakshi.

Paati smiled and tied up her hair. 'I told you,' she said. 'A house has to breathe.'

That week, Mama took off every sheet of glass. He kept the air conditioner for the back bedroom, where the old house had no ventilator. And on the last day of his holiday, he stood in the hall at three o'clock, in the middle of a heat wave, with the carved flowers above him, and he was cool.

**For the parent:** Warm air does not leave a room by itself. The cooler, heavier air comes in low and pushes it up and out. A room needs an opening high up and an opening low down for this loop to work.

- Ask: Why does hot air collect under a roof, and what does it need to leave?
- Ask: If the glass sheets had stayed on the ventilators, what would you expect to happen to the smoke from the agarbatti?
- Ask: How could you use this idea to keep your own home cooler on a hot day, without a fan?

## Experiments
### e1 · Colour Currents: which way does a hot or cold drop travel? (35 min)
- **question:** A drop of red water goes into the middle of a glass of still, room-temperature water. Does it rise, sink or stay put? Does the answer change if the drop is hot, warm, room-temperature or ice-cold?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess what each of the four drops will do: hot, warm, room-temperature and ice-cold. Which will move fastest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every drop is the same red water, the same amount (one straw-full), and is let go at the same depth in a glass of still water of the same height. The water in the glass is always room temperature and always still for two minutes first. Only the temperature of the drop changes, so any difference comes from the temperature.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours all the hot water. The hot drop should be hot like tea you wait to sip, about 60 degrees. Never suck on a straw that has hot water in it. The finger-over-the-end trick needs no sucking. Wipe spills at once.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each drop. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen (claim), what you saw (evidence), and why you think it happened (reasoning). Draw the glass with an arrow for each drop to show where it went.
- **results table:** Drop | Its temperature | What it did: rose, sank or stayed | Seconds to reach the top or the bottom
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the materials. Say that four drops will be let go in one glass. Ask for a guess and a reason for each drop. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will let four drops of red water go in the same glass. One is hot, one is warm, one is room-temperature, and one is ice-cold. What will each drop do? Rise, sink or stay? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four red drops go in one glass. Hot, warm, room and ice-cold. What will each one do? Why?
  - Ask: Which drop do you think will move fastest? Why? (good: The hottest, because it has the most heat.; The coldest, because it is heavy.; Maybe none will move.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Write each guess and its reason at the top of the table. Wrong guesses are useful, because they make the result a surprise.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up makes the four katoris. Put 50 millilitres of water in each and 3 drops of red colour. Make one very hot, one warm like a bath, one room-temperature, and one ice-cold with ice cubes. Take the ice out.  
  *Parent says:* The grown-up makes four katoris of red water. One is hot, like tea you wait to sip. One is warm, like bath water. One is room-temperature. One is cold from the ice. Take the ice cubes out before you use it.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes four katoris of red water. Hot, warm, room and ice-cold.
  - Note: Mix half kettle water and half cold tap water for the hot katori. It should be too hot to drink but safe to touch the outside of the katori. Use the same number of colour drops in each. If the red is too strong, use 2 drops. Too much colour can make the drop heavier than the water for a different reason. Keep the ice-cold katori in the fridge or with ice cubes for 10 minutes before you use it.
  - Check: Why do all four katoris get the same red colour and the same amount of water? → **So only the temperature is different**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the glass with room-temperature tap water, to about 8 centimetres deep. Stand it in front of the white paper. Wait two minutes. Watch the water until nothing moves.  
  *Parent says:* Fill the glass with tap water. Wait two minutes. The water is still when you cannot see anything moving. Hold the white paper behind the glass so the red will show up well.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the glass. Wait two minutes. Wait until the water is still.
  - Ask: Why do we wait for the water to be still? (good: So no stirring moves the red drop.; So the only thing moving it is its temperature.)
  - Note: If the glass has been moved, tiny swirls hide the result. Wait until a dust speck or bubble stops moving. Use the same glass and the same depth every time.
- **s4 (14 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test the drops one at a time, in this order: room-temperature, ice-cold, warm, hot. Fill a straw from the katori and press your finger on the top end. Lift it out. Lower the tip to the middle of the glass. Lift your finger and start the timer. Stop it when the red reaches the top or the bottom. Pour out the water and refill the glass. Wait two minutes between tests.  
  *Parent says:* Do the room-temperature drop first. Press a finger on the top of the straw and lift it out. Lower the tip to the middle of the glass. Let go. Start the timer. What does the red do? Write it down. Then refill the glass and wait two minutes for each next drop.

  - Ask: What does the hot drop do? What does the ice-cold drop do? (good: The hot one goes up.; The cold one goes down.; They go opposite ways.)
  - Note: The hot drop should rise to the top in about 2 to 5 seconds, then spread across the surface. The ice-cold drop should sink to the bottom in about 3 to 8 seconds. The warm drop is slower.   - Check: The hot drop rises in the glass. What does that show about the hot water? → **It is lighter than the cooler water around it**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the glass with four arrows to show where each drop went. Then ask your child to say three things: what they thought (claim), what they saw (evidence), and why it happened (reasoning).  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think the hot drop and the cold drop went opposite ways?  
  *Child mode:* Draw four arrows in the glass. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.

### e2 · Dark, White and Shiny: which wrapping lets the Sun warm a tumbler most? (45 min)

- **s1 (4 min)**

- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the temperature in each tumbler through the slit. Write the four starting temperatures in the table. Wipe the thermometer between tumblers.

  *Child mode:* Read the thermometer in each tumbler. Write the numbers down.

  *Child mode:* Put them in the Sun. Wait 15 minutes. Read each one. Wait again. Read again.
  - Ask: What is the Sun doing to the black paper? (good: Warming it up.; The paper takes in the light and gets hot.)
  - Ask: Can you feel a difference with your hand? (good: The black one feels warmest.; The foil one is cool.)

### How heat moves (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A steel ladle stands in a pot of hot sambar, and its handle slowly warms. Which way is the heat travelling up the handle?  
   Options: Conduction / Convection / Radiation  
   **Answer:** Conduction. *Why:* The heat passes from each tiny part of the steel to the part touching it. That is conduction. Convection needs a liquid or gas that moves, and radiation needs no touching at all.
2. (mcq) Why does a drop of hot water rise through cooler water?  
   Options: It is lighter than the cooler water around it / Heat itself is lighter than water / The cooler water pulls it up  
   **Answer:** It is lighter than the cooler water around it. *Why:* Hot water expands and is less dense, so the heavier cool water around it sinks underneath and pushes it up. Heat is not a thing that has weight, and the cool water pushes, not pulls.
3. (truefalse) Heat always travels upwards.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Hot liquids and gases rise, but heat in a solid spoon travels in every direction, and radiation goes out in every direction at once.
4. (fill) The Sun's heat crosses empty space by ____.  
   **Answer:** ['radiation']. *Why:* Empty space has nothing to touch or to flow, so only radiation can carry the Sun's heat across it.
5. (match) Match each way of moving heat to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Conduction → heat passes from part to touching part; Convection → heat is carried by a moving liquid or gas; Radiation → heat travels as invisible light-like waves. *Why:* Conduction needs touching. Convection needs a fluid that can flow. Radiation needs nothing.
6. (sort) Sort each thing by what it does to sunlight.  
   **Answer:** black school bag → Takes in a lot; white shirt → Sends back a lot; shiny kitchen foil → Sends back a lot; dark tar road → Takes in a lot; white-painted roof → Sends back a lot; black umbrella → Takes in a lot. *Why:* Dark, dull things take in most of the light and turn it into heat. White and shiny things send most of it back.
7. (order) Put the steps of a convection current in order, starting with the first.  
   **Answer:** ['Water near the flame is warmed', 'The warm water rises', 'At the top it spreads out and cools', 'The cooled water sinks at the sides', 'The water flows back along the bottom towards the flame']. *Why:* The loop starts with warming. The warm water rises, cools at the top, sinks at the sides and comes back to the flame to be warmed again.
8. (mcq) On a hot afternoon, a cool wind blows from the sea onto the beach. Why?  
   Options: Air rises over the warm land, and cooler sea air flows in to take its place / The sea is hotter than the land in the daytime / The Moon pulls the air towards the beach  
   **Answer:** Air rises over the warm land, and cooler sea air flows in to take its place. *Why:* The land warms faster than the sea. The air over it rises, and cooler air from over the sea slides in underneath.
9. (count) In a made-up test, a tumbler in black paper warmed from 28 to 38 degrees in the Sun. A tumbler in white paper warmed from 28 to 31 degrees. How many more degrees did the black one rise than the white one?  
   **Answer:** 7. *Why:* The black tumbler rose 38 minus 28, which is 10 degrees. The white rose 31 minus 28, which is 3 degrees. 10 minus 3 is 7. So the black one rose 7 degrees more.
10. (mcq) Why can the Sun's heat reach the Earth, when there is empty space in between?  
   Options: Radiation can cross empty space / Air in space carries the heat / Conduction works through empty space  
   **Answer:** Radiation can cross empty space. *Why:* Radiation is waves of light that need nothing to travel through. There is almost no air in space, so convection and conduction cannot work there.
11. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Warmth of a bonfire on your face, sunlight on a terrace, a lamp warming your cheek, a hot tumbler burning your fingers.  
   **Answer:** The hot tumbler. *Why:* The tumbler passes heat by conduction, because it touches your fingers. The other three are radiation, which crosses a gap without touching.
12. (write) Many old single-door fridges have the freezer box at the top. Explain why this works well.  
   **Answer:** The freezer cools the air next to it. Cold air is heavier than warm air, so it sinks and cools the food below. If the freezer were at the bottom, the cold air would stay down there.. *Why:* A strong answer says that cooled air sinks, so the cold spreads downwards, and that this is a convection current.
13. (draw) Draw a pot of water on a flame. Draw arrows to show a convection current inside it.  
   **Answer:** A pot over a flame. Arrows go up in the middle, across the top, down at the sides and back along the bottom towards the flame.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a full loop, with the water rising over the flame and sinking at the sides.
14. (label) Label this picture of a pot of dal on a stove: where conduction happens, where convection happens, and where radiation reaches the child.  
   **Answer:** Conduction is along the steel ladle handle or through the base of the pot. Convection is the loop of dal inside the pot. Radiation is the warmth reaching the child's face from the side.. *Why:* Conduction needs touching, convection needs a moving fluid, and radiation travels in straight lines through the air.
15. (truefalse) A white roof reflects more of the Sun's radiation than a black roof.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. White surfaces send most of the light back. Dark surfaces take it in and turn it into heat.
16. (write) Zoya says: 'When I open the fridge, the cold comes out.' Say what is really happening.  
   **Answer:** Heat from the warm kitchen air goes into the fridge, and the cool, heavy air from inside spills out along the floor. There is no cold stuff. Cold is just less heat.. *Why:* A strong answer says that heat moves from warm to cool, and that cool air sinks because it is heavy.

### How heat moves (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)

   **Answer:** Clouds block sunlight, so the land does not warm much. Then the air over the land does not rise as strongly, and not much cooler sea air is pulled in.. *Why:* A strong answer links the cloud to a cooler land, and a cooler land to a weaker rise and a weaker pull.
4. (mcq) A black pot and a shiny pot of the same size are filled with the same hot water and left to cool. Which cools faster by radiation, and why?  
   Options: The black pot, because dark surfaces are good at giving out radiation / The shiny pot, because it is shiny / They cool at exactly the same rate  
   **Answer:** The black pot, because dark surfaces are good at giving out radiation. *Why:* A surface that takes in radiation well also gives it out well. A shiny surface is poor at both, so it holds its heat longer.
5. (fill) When a warm fluid cools at the top of a loop, it becomes ____ and sinks.  
   **Answer:** ['heavier', 'denser']. *Why:* Cooling makes the fluid shrink a little, so each spoonful is heavier. Heavier fluid sinks.
6. (truefalse) In a vacuum flask, the empty gap between the walls stops both conduction and convection.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. With nothing in the gap, there is nothing to touch and nothing to flow. Only radiation can cross, which the silver coating reflects.
7. (perspective) A school classroom in Madurai gets very hot by 2 in the afternoon. Three people have different ideas.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and uses a fact, such as white roofs reflecting radiation or high and low openings making a loop of air. It also admits a fair point from another idea, such as cost or electricity.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Each choice uses a real idea, and a strong answer is fair about cost and who can afford what.
8. (count) In a made-up test, the top of the water in a jug with the ice at the top fell by 12 degrees. In a jug with the ice at the bottom, the top fell by 3 degrees. How many times as far did the first fall?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 12 divided by 3 is 4. So the top of the first jug fell 4 times as far.
9. (match) Match each thing to the idea that explains it.  
   **Answer:** A candle flame is a ball in space → convection needs gravity; A foil lid on a tumbler → it reflects the Sun's radiation; A tall chimney → hot air rises and draws fresh air in; A courtyard house → cool air comes in low and hot air leaves high. *Why:* Each one uses rising warm fluid, sinking cool fluid, or reflected radiation in a different place.
10. (order) Put in order how the Sun's heat warms a tumbler of water in a black wrapping.  
   **Answer:** ['Sunlight crosses empty space', 'The black paper takes it in and warms up', 'The glass passes the heat to the water', 'The water warms up and stirs in loops']. *Why:* The journey uses all three ways. Radiation crosses space and lands on the paper. Conduction carries heat through the glass. Convection stirs the water.
11. (odd) Which of these is not a real thing? A thermal, a caloric fluid, infrared light, a convection current.  
   **Answer:** A caloric fluid. *Why:* A caloric fluid was an idea from the 1700s, that heat is a weightless liquid. It was shown to be wrong. The other three are real.
12. (write) A black kite rises without flapping. Follow the energy back as far as you can. Start with the bird.  
   **Answer:** The bird is lifted by rising air. The air is warmed by the hot ground. The ground is warmed by the Sun's radiation. The Sun's energy comes from the nuclear reactions in its core.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the chain: bird, rising air, warm ground, sunlight, and the Sun itself.
13. (draw) Draw a rooftop solar water heater with a tank on top and a black panel below it. Show where the water goes as it warms.  
   **Answer:** A black panel at the bottom with water inside, a tank above it. Arrows show warm water rising from the panel into the tank, and cool water sinking from the tank back into the panel.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a loop, with the warm water rising to the top of the tank and cool water coming down.
14. (label) Label this picture of a sea breeze: the warm land, the cool sea, the rising air, and the wind along the ground.  
   **Answer:** The warm land is on the right. The cool sea is on the left. The rising air is above the land. The wind along the ground blows from the sea to the land.. *Why:* The land warms faster, so air rises over it, and cooler sea air flows in below.
15. (mcq) In 1856, Eunice Foote found that a jar of carbon dioxide warmed more in sunlight than a jar of ordinary air. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide takes in more of the Sun's heat radiation than ordinary air does / Carbon dioxide is a colder gas / The air in the other jar was too small  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide takes in more of the Sun's heat radiation than ordinary air does. *Why:* Her jars were otherwise the same. The difference suggests that the gas inside changed how much heat was held. This is an early clue to the greenhouse effect.
16. (write) You want to check whether a white roof really keeps a room cooler than a dark roof. Say what you would keep the same, what you would change, and what you would measure.  
   **Answer:** Keep the same: the size of the rooms, the walls, and the time in the Sun. Change: the colour of the roof. Measure: the temperature inside the rooms, at the start and after the same time.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing, keeps the rest the same, and measures with a thermometer.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Heat reaches your face from a bonfire across a gap of cold air. Which way is it travelling?  
   Options: Radiation / Conduction / Convection  
   **Answer:** Radiation. *Why:* The warmth crosses the air in straight lines without the air carrying it. That is radiation.
2. (mcq) A hot-air balloon rises. What makes the hot air in it rise?  
   Options: It is less dense than the cooler air around it / Heat has a lifting force / The balloon's cloth pulls it up  
   **Answer:** It is less dense than the cooler air around it. *Why:* Heated air expands, so each spoonful is lighter than the cooler air outside. The heavier cool air pushes it up.
3. (mcq) In a convection current in a pot of water, where does the water sink?  
   Options: Where it has cooled, away from the flame / Directly over the flame / It never sinks  
   **Answer:** Where it has cooled, away from the flame. *Why:* Water rises over the flame and spreads at the top, where it cools. Cooler water is heavier and sinks at the sides.
4. (mcq) At night on a beach, the land cools below the sea's temperature. Which way does the wind blow?  
   Options: From the land towards the sea / From the sea towards the land / It stops blowing  
   **Answer:** From the land towards the sea. *Why:* Now the sea is the warmer place, so air rises over the sea and cooler land air flows out to take its place.
5. (mcq) Which way of moving heat needs nothing at all to carry it?  
   Options: Radiation / Conduction / Convection  
   **Answer:** Radiation. *Why:* Radiation travels as waves that can cross empty space. Conduction needs touching, and convection needs a fluid.
6. (mcq) A city paints its rooftops white. How does this keep the rooms below cooler?  
   Options: The white paint reflects much of the Sun's radiation / The white paint makes heat disappear / The white paint cools the air  
   **Answer:** The white paint reflects much of the Sun's radiation. *Why:* Light surfaces send most sunlight back, so less of it is turned into heat in the roof.
7. (truefalse) Heat can only ever travel upwards.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Hot fluids rise, but conduction and radiation carry heat in every direction.
8. (mcq) You test hot and cold drops of coloured water in a glass. Why must the water in the glass be still first?  
   Options: So that stirring is not mistaken for an effect of temperature / So the water is clear / So the drops are the same size  
   **Answer:** So that stirring is not mistaken for an effect of temperature. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. If the glass water is swirling, the swirls could move the drop and hide what the temperature does.
9. (mcq) Milk in a pan on a stove: the pan's base is heated, and then the whole milk warms up. Which ways is the heat travelling?  
   Options: Conduction through the pan, then convection in the milk / Radiation only / Conduction only  
   **Answer:** Conduction through the pan, then convection in the milk. *Why:* Heat passes through the pan by conduction, and the warm milk rising and cool milk sinking spread it by convection.
10. (mcq) You open the fridge on a hot day. Which of these describes what is happening best?  
   Options: Heat flows in, and heavy cool air spills out along the floor / Cold flows out of the fridge / The fridge makes the room colder by itself  
   **Answer:** Heat flows in, and heavy cool air spills out along the floor. *Why:* Heat always goes from warm to cool, so it flows into the fridge. The cool air is heavy, so it spills low.
11. (mcq) You keep one tumbler in the shade during a Sun test. What is it for?  
   Options: It shows how much the warm day alone changes the water / It is the best tumbler / It protects the others from the Sun  
   **Answer:** It shows how much the warm day alone changes the water. *Why:* A control shows what happens without the thing being tested. The extra warming in the sunny tumblers is then due to the Sun.
12. (mcq) A card held between a flame and your palm stops the warmth at once. What does that tell you about the warmth?  
   Options: It travels in straight lines, like light / It was carried by moving air / It travels through the card  
   **Answer:** It travels in straight lines, like light. *Why:* Only something that moves in straight lines, like light, can be blocked at once by a thin card. This is radiation. Moving air would flow round the card.
13. (mcq) A woollen shawl keeps you warm. Which idea explains it best?  
   Options: It traps still air, which carries heat very slowly / Wool makes heat / Wool is hotter than air  
   **Answer:** It traps still air, which carries heat very slowly. *Why:* Air that cannot move cannot carry heat by convection, and it is a poor conductor. The wool holds air still next to your skin.

## Applications
- Chimneys and kitchen exhausts, which let hot air rise out and draw fresh air in.
- Birds and glider pilots that circle on thermals to gain height without power.
- Fishing and sailing with the land breeze and the sea breeze.
- Cool roofs: painting a roof white or using reflective coatings to keep a house cooler.
- Solar water heaters on rooftops, which move water by convection with no pump.
- Solar cookers, which use a shiny reflector and a black pot.
- Thermos flasks, which use a vacuum and silver walls to block all three ways.
- Thermal cameras and infrared thermometers, which read radiation to find fevers or leaks of heat.
- Courtyard houses with high ventilators, like old Chettinad homes, which keep cool without power.
- Putting heaters low in a room and coolers high up, so convection mixes the air.

## Open questions
- **Why is the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, hotter than the Sun's surface by hundreds of times?** Heat normally flows from hot to cool, so the corona should be cooler than the surface it sits on. Scientists have several ideas about waves and tiny flares, but none is settled.
- **How much do the slow currents in the Earth's mantle move the continents?** Scientists agree that the mantle convects, but they still argue about how much the currents, and how much the sinking edges of plates, move the surface.
- **Will everything in the universe one day be the same temperature?** If heat keeps evening out, there would be no differences left to drive any flow. Some physicists call this heat death. It depends on dark energy and other things we do not yet understand.
- **Why does a flowing, swirling fluid, like the air in a stormy sky, behave as it does?** The equations for moving fluids are known, but nobody can fully predict a swirling, turbulent flow. It remains one of the great unsolved problems of physics.
- **Do clouds, overall, make the Earth warmer or cooler?** Clouds reflect sunlight, which cools, and trap heat radiation, which warms. Scientists are still working out which wins as the climate changes.

## Connected maths
- The hot drop reached the top in 4 seconds. The warm drop took 12 seconds. How many times as long did the warm drop take? What does that suggest about the temperature of a drop and how fast it moves? **3 times as long. The hotter drop moved faster.**. 12 divided by 4 is 3. The warm drop took 3 times as long. The hotter drop is lighter compared with the water around it, so it is pushed up faster. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Every tumbler started at 28 degrees. After 30 minutes the black tumbler read 40, the white read 33, the foil read 30 and the shaded one read 29. How many degrees did the Sun add to each wrapped tumbler, once you take away what the shaded one rose? **Black 11 degrees, white 4 degrees, foil 1 degree.**. The shaded tumbler rose 29 minus 28, which is 1 degree. That much came from the warm day. Black rose 12, so the Sun added 11. White rose 5, so the Sun added 4. Foil rose 2, so the Sun added 1. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L4`)
- A jug starts at 40 degrees. With the ice at the top, the water cools 3 degrees every 2 minutes. If it kept cooling at that rate, what would it read after 10 minutes? Why might the real number be higher? **25 degrees. The real number may be higher because cooling slows down as the water gets closer to the ice's temperature.**. 10 minutes is 5 steps of 2 minutes. 5 times 3 is 15. 40 minus 15 is 25. The real cooling slows as the difference between the water and the ice gets smaller. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- Light travels about 300,000 kilometres every second. The Sun is about 150,000,000 kilometres away. How many seconds does the Sun's radiation take to reach us? How many minutes and seconds is that? **500 seconds, which is 8 minutes and 20 seconds.**. 150,000,000 divided by 300,000 is 500. 500 seconds is 8 minutes (480 seconds) and 20 seconds. So the Sun you see now is the Sun as it was 8 minutes and 20 seconds ago. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- Each person in a hall gives out about 100 watts of heat. A hall holds 40 people. How many watts is that? A room heater gives out 2,000 watts. How many room heaters is the crowd like? **4,000 watts. It is like 2 room heaters.**. 40 times 100 is 4,000 watts. 4,000 divided by 2,000 is 2. So a full hall is like two room heaters, which is why crowded rooms get hot. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- The air outside is 32 degrees. After 30 minutes, the black-roof box reads 44 degrees, and the white-roof box reads 36 degrees. How many times as many degrees above the outside air was the black box compared with the white box? **3 times as many.**. The black box was 44 minus 32, which is 12 degrees above the outside air. The white box was 36 minus 32, which is 4 degrees above. 12 divided by 4 is 3. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- On a hot day, a sea breeze starts at 11:30 in the morning and dies away at 7:45 in the evening. For how long did it blow? **8 hours and 15 minutes.**. From 11:30 am to 7:30 pm is 8 hours. From 7:30 to 7:45 is 15 minutes more. So the sea breeze blew for 8 hours and 15 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
