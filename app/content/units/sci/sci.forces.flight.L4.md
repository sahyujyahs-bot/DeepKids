# Steering through air: drag, balance and control in flight
`sci.forces.flight.L4`

**Summary.** A raindrop can fall from a cloud a kilometre up, yet it lands at no more than about 30 kilometres an hour. Why does it stop speeding up? The air pushes back on anything that moves through it, and pushes harder the faster the thing goes. Your child races parachutes, steers paper planes by bending their wings, and watches smoke whirl behind a card. They find out why shape matters, why a shuttlecock always lands cork first, how flyers turn, and why geese take turns at the front of a line.

**Intro.** *Hold a flat card in front of your chest and walk fast across the room. Feel the card push back. Now turn the card edge-on and walk again. The card is the same, and you walk at the same speed. What changed?* Nothing changed except how much of the card faces the air. Air is a real material, and a moving thing has to push it out of the way. The air pushes back. That push is called drag, and flyers spend most of their effort fighting it, using it and steering with it. In this unit you race parachutes, bend paper wings to steer, and watch what a moving card leaves behind in the air.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain drag as the push of air against a moving thing, and say what makes it bigger: speed, size facing the air, and shape.
1. I can explain why a falling thing stops speeding up, and why a parachute slows a fall.
2. I can run a fair test that changes one thing at a time, repeat each drop three times and use the average.
3. I can explain why a plane, a shuttlecock or an arrow flies straight, using the balance point and the tail.
4. I can explain how a flyer turns by changing the lift on one wing, and name pitch, roll and yaw.
5. I can explain how flying in a line saves energy, and how the air a flyer moves goes on moving after she has passed.
6. I can explain why small things fall gently, and why a jet flies high where the air is thin.

## Terms
- **Drag**: The push of air against anything that moves through it. Drag always pushes against the direction of motion. *e.g.* You feel drag when you open an umbrella and run into the wind.
- **Air resistance**: Another name for drag when the air is the thing pushing back. *e.g.* Air resistance slows a paper ball more slowly than a flat sheet of paper.
- **Streamlined**: Shaped so that air flows smoothly round it: round at the front, with a long, gentle slope at the back. *e.g.* A fish, a bullet train and a kingfisher's beak are streamlined.
- **Wake**: The moving, swirling air or water left behind a thing that has moved through it. *e.g.* A boat leaves a wake of waves, and a lorry leaves a wake of swirling air.
- **Terminal speed**: The top speed of a falling thing, reached when drag has grown as big as its weight. After that it stops speeding up. *e.g.* A big raindrop reaches a terminal speed of about 9 metres each second.
- **Canopy**: The wide cloth part of a parachute that catches the air. *e.g.* The canopy of a skydiver's parachute is bigger than a room.
- **Balance point**: The one spot where a thing balances. Scientists call it the centre of gravity. The whole weight of the thing seems to act there. *e.g.* A ruler balances on your finger at its middle.
- **Stable**: Stable means that after a small bump, the flyer swings back to flying straight by itself. *e.g.* A shuttlecock thrown sideways turns itself round to fly cork first.
- **Pitch**: When the nose of a plane tips up or down. *e.g.* A plane pitches up as it starts to climb after take-off.
- **Roll**: When a plane tilts to one side, with one wing going down and the other going up. *e.g.* A plane rolls left before it turns left.
- **Yaw**: When the nose of a plane swings left or right, like a door turning on its hinge. *e.g.* A boat yaws when you turn its rudder.
- **Elevator**: A flap on the tail of a plane that tips the nose up or down. *e.g.* Pilots tilt the elevator up to make the nose rise.
- **Aileron**: A flap on the back edge of each wing. One goes up while the other goes down, to roll the plane. *e.g.* To roll left, the left aileron goes up and the right aileron goes down.
- **Rudder**: An upright flap on the tail that swings the nose left or right. *e.g.* A boat and a plane both have a rudder.
- **Bank**: To lean a plane over to one side, with its wings tilted, so that it turns. *e.g.* A cyclist leans into a bend in the same way.
- **Upwash**: Air that is pushed upwards behind the wing tip of a flying bird or plane. *e.g.* A bird flying just behind and beside another can ride on her upwash.
- **Air density**: How much air is packed into a space. High up, the air is less dense, so it is thinner. *e.g.* The air on top of a mountain is thinner than the air in Chennai.
- **Anemometer**: An instrument that measures how fast the wind blows. Many have little cups that spin in the wind. *e.g.* Weather stations use an anemometer on a tall pole.

## Explanations
### Drag: the air pushes back on anything that moves through it · *defined*
**Definition:** Drag is the push of air against a thing that moves through it. It pushes against the direction the thing is moving.

Air is made of countless tiny particles called molecules. A moving thing has to shove those molecules out of the way, and they push back. You feel this when you run with an open umbrella. You also feel it when you hold a card flat in front of you and walk, and it does not push back much when you turn the card edge-on. Three things decide how big the drag is. The first is speed. The second is how much of the thing faces the air. The third is shape. A thing that goes straight through at a steady speed has drag exactly equal to the push that drives it. That is why a cyclist keeps pedalling to hold the same speed.
- e.g. A flat card walked through the air pushes back much harder than the same card turned edge-on.
- e.g. A cyclist pedals only to cancel the drag, once she is up to speed.
- e.g. An open umbrella pulls hard against the wind, and a closed one hardly does.
### Speed matters a lot: twice as fast, about four times the drag · *defined*
**Definition:** For most everyday speeds, drag grows with the square of the speed. Twice as fast gives about four times the drag.

When you go twice as fast, you meet twice as much air every second. And you hit each bit of it twice as hard. Two times two is four. Three times as fast gives three times three, which is nine times the drag. This is why riding into the wind on a cycle feels so hard, and why a car at 100 kilometres an hour uses much more fuel than at 50. A jet at full speed spends nearly all of its engine push just fighting drag. In steady, level flight, thrust equals drag, and lift equals weight. All four forces are balanced, and the plane flies straight at a steady speed.
- e.g. A cyclist at 20 kilometres an hour feels about four times the air push she feels at 10.
- e.g. A car at 100 kilometres an hour has about four times the drag it has at 50.
- e.g. A jet in level flight has thrust equal to drag and lift equal to weight.
### Why a falling thing stops speeding up: terminal speed · *defined*
**Definition:** A falling thing speeds up until its drag equals its weight. After that it falls at a steady speed called its terminal speed.

When you let go, only weight is pulling the thing down, so it speeds up. As it speeds up, drag grows. At some speed the drag grows as big as the weight. Then the forces are balanced, and the thing stops speeding up. A big raindrop falls at no more than about 9 metres each second, or about 30 kilometres an hour, though it may fall from a cloud a kilometre up. A parachute has a huge canopy, so it reaches the balance at a very low speed. A heavier thing needs more drag to balance it, so it falls faster. But four times the weight does not make four times the speed. It makes about two times the speed. A skydiver falling flat reaches about 55 metres each second, and a skydiver with a parachute open lands at about 5.
- e.g. A big raindrop stops speeding up at about 9 metres each second.
- e.g. A skydiver falling belly-down reaches about 55 metres each second, about 200 kilometres an hour.
- e.g. A skydiver tucked head-down falls faster than one with her arms spread out.
### Shape and streamlining: where drag comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** A streamlined shape has a rounded front and a long, gentle slope behind. Air flows smoothly round it and closes up neatly behind, leaving a small wake.

Drag has two main sources. The first is the air being shoved aside. Behind a blunt shape such as a flat plate, the air cannot close up smoothly. It breaks into a big, messy wake, and the low pressure in that wake pulls the shape back. The second source is that air sticks a little to any surface. A thin layer of air clings to the skin of a plane, a fish or a card, and rubs against the air moving past. A teardrop shape can have more than ten times less drag than a flat disc of the same width. This is why fish, birds, bullet trains and planes are rounded in front and tapered behind. A pointed front is not what matters. The slope of the back matters more.
- e.g. The nose of Japan's 500 Series bullet train was shaped like a kingfisher's beak, to cut drag and noise.
- e.g. A tuna and a cheel both have smooth, tapered bodies.
- e.g. A cyclist crouched low, in a smooth helmet, has less drag than one sitting upright.
### Thin air: why jets fly high · *defined*
**Definition:** Air gets thinner with height. At the height where jets cruise, the air is about a third as dense as it is near the ground.

The air near the ground is squashed by all the air above it. Higher up there is less air above, so the air is less squashed and has fewer molecules in each cubic metre. At 10 kilometres the air is about one third as dense as at the ground. On the top of Mount Everest the air is thinner still, and climbers often carry oxygen. Thin air means less drag, so a jet burns less fuel at the same speed. It also means less lift, so the wings must move faster through the air. That is why a jet cruises at about 900 kilometres an hour high up, and lands at about 250. In 2012, Felix Baumgartner jumped from a balloon 39 kilometres up. In the very thin air there he reached about 1,357 kilometres an hour before the thicker air slowed him.
- e.g. A jet cruises about 10 kilometres up, where the air is about a third as dense.
- e.g. Bar-headed geese cross the high Himalayas on their way to India, in air much thinner than at the sea.
- e.g. Baumgartner fell faster than sound because the air at 39 kilometres up is so thin.
### Why a flyer flies straight: the balance point and the tail · *defined*
**Definition:** A flyer is stable when a small bump makes forces that turn it back to where it was. A tail and a balance point near the front make a plane stable.

The balance point is the spot where the weight of a plane seems to act. If it is a little ahead of where the wing's lift acts, the nose tends to drop back down after a bump. The tail helps too. A tail is a small wing. If a bump tips the nose up, the tail swings down and meets the air at a steeper angle. The air pushes the tail up, and the nose dips back to level. A shuttlecock works in a similar way. It has a heavy cork head and a skirt of feathers with much more drag. If it flies tail-first, the drag on the feathers swings them round behind. An arrow's feathers and a kite's tail do the same job. But there is a trade-off. A very stable plane is hard to turn. Fighter jets are made less stable on purpose, and computers help the pilot keep them steady.
- e.g. A shuttlecock hit sideways turns itself round to fly cork first.
- e.g. An arrow flies straight because of its feathers at the back.
- e.g. A kite with no tail twirls and dives, and a longer tail steadies it.
### How a flyer turns: pitch, roll and yaw · *defined*
**Definition:** A flyer can tip its nose up and down (pitch), tilt its wings (roll) and swing its nose left and right (yaw). It does these by changing the lift on parts of its wings and tail.

A flap on the back edge of a wing changes how much air that part of the wing pushes down. Bend a flap down, and that part of the wing pushes more air down and lifts more. Bend it up, and it lifts less. The elevator on the tail tips the nose up or down. The ailerons on the wings work in opposite ways, one up and one down, so that one wing lifts more than the other. The wing that lifts less goes down, and the plane rolls. Once it is tilted, some of the lift pulls the plane sideways, and the plane goes round a curve. This is called banking. A cyclist leans into a bend in the same way. The rudder only fine-tunes the turn. Birds do the same. They twist their wings and fan or tilt their tails.
- e.g. A pilot rolls left by raising the left aileron and lowering the right one.
- e.g. A crow spreads and tilts her tail when she lands, so that she slows and steers.
- e.g. A cyclist leans into a bend, and a plane banks into a turn.
### Flying in each other's air: wakes and the V · *debated*
**Definition:** A flyer leaves moving air behind. A bird that flies in the right place can be lifted by that air and save energy.

A wing makes lift by pushing air down. Behind the wing tips, the air curls round into two spinning whirls. Outside the wing tips, that air goes up. This rising air is called upwash. A bird that flies just behind and to the side of another can be lifted by her. In 2014, scientists put tiny trackers on northern bald ibises that were flying in a V behind a microlight. They found that each ibis put her wing tip in the right place, and flapped in step with the bird ahead to catch the upwash. In 2001, researchers measured the heartbeat of great white pelicans flying in a line and found it was slower than when they flew alone. Early sums from 1970 said a big V could save a great deal of energy. Measurements on live birds find smaller savings, but still real. Cyclists use the same idea when they follow in a line.
- e.g. Ibises flapping in step with the bird ahead to catch her upwash.
- e.g. Cyclists riding in a line, taking turns at the front.
- e.g. Flamingos and geese flying in a V or a long slanting line.
- **Scientists who tracked ibises:** Birds in a V place themselves and time their flaps to catch the upwash of the bird ahead, and so save energy.
- **Scientists who study flock behaviour:** A V also lets every bird see the others and keep in touch, so the shape may be partly about seeing and keeping together. How much is energy and how much is seeing may be different for each kind of bird.
### Small things fall gently: size and surface · *defined*
**Definition:** A small animal has more skin for each bit of weight than a big one, so air slows her fall far more.

Imagine an animal twice as long, twice as tall and twice as wide. It has 4 times as much skin, but 8 times as much weight. So a bigger body has less skin for each bit of weight, and the air slows it less. In 1926, the biologist J. B. S. Haldane wrote that a mouse can fall down a deep mine shaft and walk away, while a person would be badly hurt. Haldane later moved to India, in 1957. A tiny insect has so much skin for her weight that she floats down like dust. This is also why you saw the small, light seed spinners fall slowly, and why big birds must flap harder than small ones. For a very small flyer, air feels thick, like water does for us. Tiny wasps have wings like feathers, made of fine bristles, and they seem to swim through the air.
- e.g. A mouse can survive a fall that would badly hurt a person.
- e.g. An ant falls from a table and walks away, because drag slows her fast.
- e.g. Tiny wasps have bristly wings that push through air which feels thick to them.
### Where flight ends: no air, no drag and no wings · *defined*
**Definition:** Without air there is no drag and no lift. Wings and flaps do nothing. A spacecraft steers by pushing on its own exhaust.

Above about 100 kilometres, the air is so thin that wings cannot make lift. A rocket pushes out burning gas, and the gas pushes the rocket the other way. When a spacecraft comes back, it hits the air at about 28,000 kilometres an hour. Drag is so strong that it turns the spacecraft's motion into heat. A shield on the front takes the heat. India's capsule SRE-1 came back this way in January 2007 and landed in the Bay of Bengal. Shooting stars glow for the same reason. Tiny bits of rock hit the air so fast that they heat up and burn. So drag does not only slow things down. It changes motion into warmth. The energy is not lost. It becomes the heat of the air and the shield.
- e.g. A shooting star is a bit of rock heated by drag as it hits the air.
- e.g. A returning capsule has a heat shield on its front.
- e.g. On the Moon, a feather and a hammer fall together, because there is no air.

## Mostly agreed
- Drag pushes against the direction of motion, and gets bigger with speed, with the size facing the air, and with a blunt shape. *How we know:* Wind tunnel tests on thousands of shapes, from planes to cyclists, give the same pattern.
- A falling thing stops speeding up when its drag equals its weight. *How we know:* Measured speeds of raindrops, parachutists and dropped balls level off at a steady value.
- A streamlined shape has far less drag than a blunt shape of the same width. *How we know:* In wind tunnels, a teardrop has more than ten times less drag than a flat disc of the same width.
- Air gets thinner with height, so both drag and lift get smaller. *How we know:* Weather balloons and aircraft instruments show the air density falling steadily with height.
- Planes turn mainly by banking, which they do by changing the lift on parts of the wings and tail. *How we know:* Pilots and flight recorders show that every turn begins with a roll of the wings.
- Some birds that fly in a line save energy by catching the upwash of the bird ahead. *How we know:* Tracking of ibises in 2014 showed each bird flapping in step with the one ahead, and a 2001 study found slower heartbeats in pelicans flying in a line.

## How we found out
**Jean le Rond d'Alembert and Ludwig Prandtl, 1752 and 1904.** In 1752, the French mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert used the best mathematics of his day to work out what happens when a smooth object moves through a perfectly smooth, slippery fluid. His sums said the fluid would push on the front and the back of the object by the same amount, so the object would feel no drag at all. Everyone knew this was wrong, because a cannonball in air slows down. For over 150 years, the sums and the real world disagreed. People called it d'Alembert's paradox. In 1904, the German engineer Ludwig Prandtl gave the answer in a talk at a maths congress in Heidelberg. Real air is slightly sticky. A very thin layer of it clings to the surface of a moving object. Behind a blunt shape, that layer peels away into a messy wake, and this wake is where much of the drag comes from. Prandtl watched the wake form in a small water channel that he built, with bits of shiny mica floating in the flow.  
*What people believed before:* The neat mathematics said that a smooth object in a smooth fluid would feel no drag. The sums left out the thin sticky layer of air, and Prandtl's experiment showed that this layer, and the wake it makes, is where drag comes from.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Heavy things fall faster than light things.** (Aristotle taught this, and many people still think so.; *debated*)  
  For: In air a stone does fall faster than a feather.  
  Against: Galileo argued in the early 1600s that without air, all things fall together. On the Moon in 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather and they landed together. In air, drag makes the difference, not weight alone.
- **Air is empty space, so a moving thing meets nothing.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: You cannot see air, and it feels like nothing when you stand still.  
  Against: A card swished through still smoke leaves whirls that keep turning. Air has weight, pushes back, and keeps moving after the thing has gone by.
- **The most streamlined shape has a sharp, pointed front.** (Many people, because arrows and rockets have pointed noses.; *rejected*)  
  For: Pointed noses are used by arrows, rockets and fast jets.  
  Against: At everyday speeds, the best shape is rounded at the front and tapered at the back, like a teardrop. A pointed nose helps mostly near the speed of sound or faster.
- **Birds fly in a V only to see each other and follow the leader.** (Some bird scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: Birds in a V can see each other well.  
  Against: Tracking showed that ibises place their wings to catch the upwash of the bird ahead and flap in step. Seeing probably matters too, so scientists still argue about how much of each.
- **A plane turns by pointing its rudder, like a boat.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: A plane has a rudder and a boat turns with one.  
  Against: A plane mostly turns by banking. It rolls its wings over, and the lift then pulls it round the curve. The rudder only fine-tunes the turn.

## Roots
We have raced parachutes, steered paper planes and watched smoke whirl behind a card. Now we ask what this tells us about the air, about flyers, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** After you swish a card through smoke, whirls of smoke keep turning where the card has been. A flyer moves the air they pass through, and that air keeps moving after the flyer has gone. In a flock, the next bird is held up by air the bird ahead has moved. So a flyer's push does not end at their wings.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture air as empty space that a flyer passes through, and a flock as separate birds. But air is a real material that gets pushed. A flyer and the air around them work as one event.  
*Evidence:* In the smoke test, the whirls kept turning after the card had stopped. Northern bald ibises flying in a V were tracked in 2014, and each one flapped in step with the bird ahead to catch the air that bird had lifted.

- *who am i:* When you walk across a room, you move the air as well as yourself. The air you push aside closes behind you, and your push goes on in it.
- *what is life:* Flamingos and geese fly in lines and take turns at the front. Many living beings save effort by using what the others around them are already doing.
- *what is this world:* Nothing moves through the world without moving a little of it. The air a plane stirs is still moving minutes after the plane has gone.

A bird and the sky around her look like two things. But in the smoke test, a card and the air moved together, and the air kept the motion after the card stopped. A flock shares that motion from one bird to the next. Her muscles move her wings, her wings move the air, and the moving air lifts the next bird. In the end the motion becomes warmth in the air. The energy is not lost. It is only handed on.

- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** Imagine a net with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel shows the reflection of every other jewel. Nothing in the net stands alone. (Each flying bird's air reaches the next bird. A flock is a net of moving air, where each push is felt by the others.)
- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes meet at the hub of a wheel, but it is the empty space in the middle that makes the wheel useful. A cup is useful because of the space inside it. (The air looks like empty space. But the air is what a wing pushes on, and what lifts every flyer.)
- Science (settled): Northern bald ibises flying in a V were tracked in 2014 (Portugal and others, Nature). Each bird flapped in step with the bird ahead, to catch the rising air from that bird's wing tip.
- Science (open): Nobody can yet work out, from the basic equations, exactly how the swirling air behind a moving thing will behave. Whether those equations always have smooth answers is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven million-dollar prize problems.
- Other view, Many physicists and engineers: A wake is only air moving according to the usual laws. Nothing is shared and nothing is used. To say a bird uses the air, or that the bird and the air are one event, is only a way of speaking, and it adds nothing to the sums.
- Practice, Feel the air go on moving (3 min): Sit still and hold a card 20 centimetres from your cheek. Wave it towards your cheek once, slowly. Feel the puff arrive. Breathe in slowly. Now breathe out onto your palm and feel your own air moving. Think about where that air goes next.
- Ask: When you walk across a room, do you move through the air, or do you move the air?
- Ask: If a bird's push goes on in the air after she has passed, where does her flight end?
- Ask: Is the air a hawk flies in the same air that you walk in?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A raindrop falls a kilometre but lands slowly. Where did the energy of its fall go? (hint: Think about what the drop had to push out of the way, all the way down.)
- *big:* Is the air a bird flies in the same air that we walk in? (hint: A hawk feels rising and sinking currents. A tiny wasp feels thick air. We feel almost nothing. Whose air is the real one?)
- *deep:* A plane that is easy to steer is hard to keep steady, and one that is steady is hard to steer. What should a pilot want? What should a bird want? (hint: Think about a bird that needs to dodge a hawk, and a plane that carries 200 sleeping passengers.)
- *big:* Geese take turns at the front of the V. Is this sharing, or does each bird look after herself? (hint: Think about who works hardest at the front, and what the others gain.)
- *deep:* After a big plane has taken off, other planes must wait a few minutes before they use the same runway. What are they waiting for? (hint: Think about what the plane leaves behind in the air.)
- *big:* Small animals can fall from heights that would kill big ones. Does that mean small is better than big? (hint: Think about what a small animal cannot do, as well as what she can.)
- *big:* Sarla Thakral flew a plane alone in 1936, at the age of 21, and earned her pilot's licence, and she is often called India's first woman pilot. Why do some stories of flight get told more than others? (hint: Think about whose names you were told, and whose you had to look up.)
- *deep:* When we say a bird uses the air, who is using whom? Could we say the air uses the bird? (hint: Think about the whirls left behind, and who catches them.)

## Story: The Wind's Shadow
*A made-up story. The place is real: the quiet road from Vedaranyam towards Kodiyakarai (Point Calimere) in Tamil Nadu, where flamingos come in winter. The science is real. A rider close behind another rider feels less push from the air, because the front rider leaves a calmer patch of air behind her. Studies of cyclists find clearly less drag for the rider behind, with the size of the saving depending on how close the riders are. Riders in a line take turns at the front.*

Thendral was the smallest girl in Class 4 at the panchayat school in Vedaranyam, and she had the oldest cycle. It had one speed, a squeaky bell, and a seat that her mother had lowered with a spanner that morning.

Every November, the school held a cycle rally to Kodiyakarai, where the flamingos come to the salt flats. The ride was twelve kilometres along a straight road, with open sky on both sides. The first team to reach the sanctuary gate would look through Mrs Selvarani's binoculars before anyone else.

Murugan, the tallest boy, looked at Thendral's cycle. 'The sea wind will blow you backwards,' he said. 'Girls at the back.'

Fathima, the strongest girl in the class, said nothing. She just tightened her scarf. Gomathi, who had a loud laugh, said, 'We will see.' The three girls made a team. The rule was that a team had to arrive together.

The boys shot off at the whistle. Fathima rode in front. Gomathi and Thendral followed.

By the second kilometre, the wind from the sea was pushing hard on Thendral's face. It whistled in her ears. Her legs began to ache. The boys were small shapes far ahead.

Then something odd happened. Thendral had been riding close behind Fathima's back, and she noticed the wind sounded different there. It had a hush in it. Her legs felt lighter.

She swung out to the side to look ahead. At once the wind slapped her cheeks so hard that her eyes watered. She tucked back in behind Fathima. The hush came back.

She did it again, to be sure. Out in the open, the wind pushed. Behind Fathima, it hardly did.

'Fathima Akka,' she called. 'Is it hard for you in front?'

'It is like pushing a wall,' Fathima panted.

Just then a line of flamingos crossed the road above them, pink and white against the blue. They flew one behind another, in a long slanting line. As Thendral watched, the front bird slid back to the end, and the next bird took her place.

'They take turns,' Thendral said, half to herself. Then she said it loudly. 'They take turns, because the one in front does the pushing!'

'What are you talking about?' said Gomathi.

'Fathima pushes the wind aside, and it leaves a quiet place behind her. We ride in her quiet. That is why it is easier back here. But she is working for all three of us.' Thendral took a breath. 'Let us each lead for two minutes. Then the front one drops to the back and rests.'

Fathima said, 'You are the smallest.'

'I will only lead for two minutes,' said Thendral. 'Then I will be in a quiet place again.'

So they tried it. Thendral went to the front. The wind hit her like a wet cloth, and she leaned over her handlebars and counted slowly to one hundred and twenty. Then she slid to the back, and the hush closed round her. Gomathi took the front, laughing. Then Fathima.

Soon they were passing the salt pans, with white heaps shining on both sides. The three girls rode in a neat line, and their legs kept turning.

At the eighth kilometre, they found Murugan sitting by the roadside with an empty bottle, breathing hard. He had ridden fast at the start, alone, and had pushed the whole wind by himself.

'How are you not tired?' he said.

Thendral stopped. 'Because the wind has a shadow,' she said. 'Behind a rider, the air is calmer. The front rider takes the hardest part. We take turns at the front.'

Murugan thought about this. 'Can I ride in your quiet?'

'Yes,' said Thendral. 'But you take your turn at the front.'

So four of them rode on together, and then Murugan took his turn, and then Thendral again. They reached the gate side by side, just as the first flamingos were landing on the shining water.

Mrs Selvarani held out the binoculars. 'Who wants the first look?'

Thendral looked at the others. Then she took the binoculars and found the line of birds. The front flamingo slid back to the end, and another took her place. Now Thendral knew exactly what they were doing.

**For the parent:** A moving body pushes the air aside and leaves calmer air behind it. A rider or bird that follows closely feels less push, so a line that takes turns at the front shares the work.

- Ask: Why was the wind quieter behind Fathima?
- Ask: Why did the girls take turns at the front, instead of Fathima always leading?
- Ask: If the wind blew from behind instead of from the front, would the quiet place behind a rider still help?

## Games
### g1 · Fix the Flyer: what is wrong, and what puts it right? (20 min)
- **rules:** One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and reads the front aloud. The reader keeps the back hidden.; Every other player puts one token face down for the choice they think is best. Then everyone turns the tokens over together.; The reader reads the back. A player whose letter matches the answer scores one point.; A player who also gives the right reason, using words such as drag, balance point, lift or thin air, scores one more point. The reader decides, using the back of the card.; After each card, the reader's job passes to the player on the left. With two players, the parent and child swap each card.; If you play alone, read the front, say your choice and your reason out loud, then turn the card over. Score yourself one point for each right choice. Try to beat your score next time.; The game ends when all 24 cards have been read. The player with the most points wins, and ties share the win.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a flyer with a problem and three choices on the front, and the answer with its reason on the back:** A paper plane dives nose-first into the floor. A: Add a clip to its nose. B: Move the clip back towards the tail. C: Make the wings smaller. (back: B. The balance point is too far forward, so the nose is too heavy. Moving weight back levels the plane.); A paper plane climbs steeply, stops in the air and then swoops down. A: Bend the back edges of the wings up. B: Bend the back edges of the wings down a little. C: Add a clip to the tail. (back: B. The nose is rising too much. Bending the back edges down pushes the tail up, and the nose comes down.); A paper plane keeps turning left in a circle. A: Bend the back edge of its left wing down a little. B: Bend the back edge of its left wing up. C: Throw it harder. (back: A. The left wing is lifting less, so it drops and the plane banks left. Bending the left flap down gives that wing more lift.); A kite twirls and dives in the wind. A: Add a longer tail. B: Cut the tail off. C: Use a heavier string. (back: A. A tail adds drag at the back. This keeps the kite pointing the right way, in the same way as an arrow's feathers.); A shuttlecock is hit so that it flies feathers-first. What happens? A: It flips round and flies cork-first. B: It flies feathers-first all the way. C: It drops straight down. (back: A. The feathers have much more drag than the heavy cork. The drag swings them round behind the cork.); A small parachute and a big parachute carry the same coin and are dropped together. A: The small one lands first. B: The big one lands first. C: They land together. (back: A. The big canopy has more drag, so the air balances the weight at a lower speed.); The same parachute is dropped with 1 coin and then with 4 coins. A: The 1-coin one is faster. B: The 4-coin one is faster, but not four times faster. C: They land together. (back: B. A heavier load needs more drag to balance it, so the parachute falls faster. But four times the weight gives only about twice the speed.); You cycle into the same wind at 10, then at 20 kilometres an hour. How does the push of the air change? A: It stays the same. B: It doubles. C: It gets about four times bigger. (back: C. Twice the speed means twice as much air each second, hit twice as hard. Two times two is four.); A big raindrop falls from a cloud a kilometre up. How fast does it land? A: Faster than a bullet. B: About 30 kilometres an hour at most. C: It hardly moves. (back: B. Drag grows with speed until it equals the drop's weight, and then the drop stops speeding up.); Which of these shapes has the least drag in air? A: A flat plate facing the wind. B: A ball. C: A teardrop with the round end in front. (back: C. A teardrop lets air flow smoothly round it and close up behind, with a small wake.); Why do jets cruise about 10 kilometres up? A: The air is thin, so drag is less. B: They are nearer to the stars. C: There is no gravity there. (back: A. At 10 kilometres the air is about a third as dense, so there is much less drag and the jet burns less fuel.); On the Moon, an astronaut drops a hammer and a feather together. What happens? A: The hammer lands first. B: They land together. C: The feather lands first. (back: B. The Moon has no air, so there is no drag. Astronaut David Scott showed this on Apollo 15 in 1971.); Why can an ant fall off a table and walk away? A: Ants have very strong bones. B: A tiny body has a lot of skin for its weight, so drag slows her fall. C: Gravity pulls less on ants. (back: B. The smaller the animal, the more skin she has for her weight. Air slows her fall much more than it slows a bigger animal.); A bird in a V flies just behind and a little to one side of the bird ahead. Why there? A: To catch air rising near the wing tip of the bird ahead. B: To be hidden from hawks. C: There is no reason. (back: A. Measurements of ibises show this. Scientists also think the V helps birds see each other, and still argue about how much each one matters.); A plane banks to the left. Where does it turn? A: To the left. B: To the right. C: It goes straight on. (back: A. Banking tilts the lift sideways, and the sideways part of the lift pulls the plane round a curve to the left.); A pilot tips the elevator up. What does the nose do? A: It goes up. B: It goes down. C: It swings left. (back: A. The elevator up pushes the tail down, so the nose rises.); A paper plane has both back wing edges bent up. What does its nose do? A: It rises. B: It dips. C: Nothing changes. (back: A. Bent-up edges push the back of the wings down, and the nose tips up.); You swish a card through still smoke and stop. What does the smoke do? A: It stops at once. B: It keeps swirling for a moment. C: It is sucked towards the card. (back: B. The card moved the air, and the moving air carries on after the card has stopped.); Two cyclists ride in a line into a headwind. Who has the easier job? A: The one in front. B: The one behind. C: Both the same. (back: B. The front rider pushes the air aside and leaves calmer air behind her. The rider behind feels less push.); One paper cup faces the wind with its open side, and another shows its rounded back. Which feels the bigger push? A: The open side. B: The rounded back. C: Both the same. (back: A. The open cup catches the air, so it has more drag. This is why cup anemometers spin.); A skydiver wants to fall faster. A: Spread out her arms and legs. B: Tuck in her arms and legs, head-down. C: Open a bigger parachute. (back: B. A smaller body facing the air has less drag, so she must fall faster before drag balances her weight.); A paper plane has its balance point too far back. What happens? A: The nose rises and the plane stalls. B: The nose dives. C: It flies perfectly. (back: A. With too little weight at the front, the nose rises and the plane stops flying. It then drops.); A big plane has just taken off. Why must the next plane wait a few minutes? A: Swirling air left behind can toss a small plane. B: The runway needs cooling. C: The pilot needs a rest. (back: A. A big plane leaves strong whirls of air behind its wing tips. They keep moving long after the plane has gone.); Japan's 500 Series bullet train has a nose shaped like which bird's beak? A: A kingfisher. B: A crow. C: A peacock. (back: A. The nose was shaped to move through air smoothly, the way a kingfisher dives into water.)
- **Printable tokens: 12 tokens: four marked A, four marked B and four marked C:** A; B; C; A; B; C; A; B; C; A; B; C
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the 12 tokens. Shuffle the cards into a pile. Give each player an A, a B and a C token.  
  *Parent says:* Each card shows a flyer with a problem. There are three choices, A, B and C. We pick one and say why.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take an A, a B and a C.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first eight cards. After each card, read the back aloud. After card four, ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* The reader reads the front. Choose a token and turn it over together. Then the reader reads the back. Who can say why?  
  *Child mode:* Read a card. Pick a token. Turn it over. Check the back.
  - Ask: What do the fixes have in common? (good: They change how much the air pushes.; They move the balance point.; They change the lift or drag on one part.)
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the next eight cards. Swap the reader after every card. Keep score on paper.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. Think about drag, weight and the balance point. What would you change to put it right?  
  *Child mode:* Play on. Think about drag, weight and the balance point.
  - Ask: Which card surprised you so far, and why? (good: The hammer and feather land together on the Moon.; Four times the weight is only twice the speed.; The ant falls and walks away.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the last eight cards. When the pile is finished, count each player's points.  
  *Parent says:* Last cards. Think about what the air does to each flyer.  
  *Child mode:* Play the last cards. Count your points.
- **s5 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say who won. Ask your child which card was hardest, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Who got the most points? Which card was hardest? What made it hard?  
  *Child mode:* Who won? Which card was hardest? Why?
  - Think: Which fix would you try first on a real paper plane?
### g2 · Pilot Says: pitch, roll and yaw with your body (15 min)
- **rules:** One player is the pilot and gives the commands. The pilot reads the front of a card aloud.; If the command starts with the words Pilot says, everyone does the move at once. If it does not start with those words, everyone stays still.; A player who moves when the pilot did not say Pilot says is out for one round, but can still play the next.; After each command, the pilot reads the back of the card. Everyone says what the real plane does.; The pilot's job passes to the next player after four cards. Play all 16 cards, or as many as you like.; Everyone who stays in is a good pilot. No one wins, but you can keep a count of the rounds you were never out.
- **Printable cards: 16 two-sided cards: a command on the front, and what the real plane does on the back:** Pilot says: Nose up! (Lean back a little, arms stay out.) (back: Pitch up. The elevator goes up, the tail is pushed down and the nose rises.); Pilot says: Nose down! (Lean forward a little.) (back: Pitch down. The elevator goes down, the tail is pushed up and the nose dips.); Pilot says: Roll left! (Tip your left arm down and your right arm up.) (back: Roll left. The left aileron goes up and the left wing lifts less, so it goes down.); Pilot says: Roll right! (Tip your right arm down.) (back: Roll right. The right aileron goes up and the right wing lifts less, so it goes down.); Pilot says: Yaw left! (Keep your arms level and turn your nose to the left.) (back: Yaw left. The rudder swings, and the nose points left without the wings tilting.); Pilot says: Yaw right! (Keep your arms level and turn to the right.) (back: Yaw right. The rudder swings the other way.); Pilot says: Bank left and turn! (Tip your left arm down and walk a small circle to the left.) (back: A banked plane turns because the lift tilts sideways and pulls it round the curve.); Pilot says: Bank right and turn! (Tip your right arm down and walk a small circle to the right.) (back: A plane banks right before it turns right, just as a cyclist leans before a bend.); Pilot says: Level out! (Arms flat, back straight, stand tall.) (back: In level flight, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag, so the plane flies straight and steady.); Pilot says: Dive! (Crouch down and lean forward.) (back: In a dive, the plane speeds up fast and the drag rises with the speed.); Pilot says: Climb! (Rise up on tiptoes with arms tilted up.) (back: A climbing plane needs extra thrust to beat both drag and the pull of gravity.); Pilot says: Spread your tail! (Spread your fingers wide behind you.) (back: A landing bird spreads her tail wide. It adds drag, which slows her, and helps her steer.); Pilot says: Stall! (Freeze with your arms up, then slowly crouch.) (back: In a stall the wing is tilted so far that the air cannot follow it. Lift drops away and the plane falls.); Pilot says: Fly in a V! (Line up behind a friend, a little to one side.) (back: Birds in a V catch the rising air from the wing tip of the bird ahead.); Pilot says: Change the leader! (The front player goes to the back.) (back: The front bird works hardest. Birds in a line take turns at the front.); Pilot says: Land! (Slowly lower your arms and crouch down.) (back: A landing plane slows down and loses height, and its flaps and wheels come out to add lift and drag.)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 16 cards. Clear a space in the room. Show your child how to hold their arms out like wings.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to be planes. Hold your arms out like wings. I will say a flying command, and you move like a plane.  
  *Child mode:* Stand with your arms out like wings. You are a plane.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the first six cards, in order. Read the front, let your child act, then read the back and ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Pilot says, nose up! Now tell me. What is the elevator doing on a real plane?  
  *Child mode:* Do each move. Then listen to what the real plane does.
  - Ask: What is different between a roll and a yaw? (good: In a roll, the wings tip.; In a yaw, the nose swings but the wings stay level.; A roll tips the wings and a yaw turns the nose.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mix the next cards. Sometimes leave out the words Pilot says, to catch your child moving. Then swap roles.  
  *Parent says:* Now listen carefully. Only move if I say the magic words. Then you give me the commands.  
  *Child mode:* Listen for the words Pilot says. Then be the pilot yourself.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child which move was hardest, and which one a bird could do easily.  
  *Parent says:* Which move was hardest to do? Which one do you think a crow does most?  
  *Child mode:* Which move was hardest? Which does a crow do most?
  - Think: Why does a bird need to roll and yaw so easily?

## Experiments
### e1 · Parachute Races: what slows a fall? (36 min)
- **question:** Does a bigger parachute fall more slowly? And does a heavier load make it fall faster, or four times as fast?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will land first: a small parachute or a big one with the same coin? Will a parachute with 4 coins fall 4 times as fast as one with 1 coin? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every parachute is made from the same polythene, the same four strings and the same coins. Each is dropped from the same height, by the same person, in the same still room. Only one thing changes at a time: the size of the canopy, or the number of coins. Each is dropped three times and the times are averaged, because one drop can be spoilt by a gust or a tangle. The scrunched-up sheet is the control. It shows what the same polythene does with no canopy.
- **safety:** A grown-up holds each parachute at the 2 metre mark and does all the dropping from a steady floor. Nobody climbs on a chair. Keep coins away from children under five.
- **record:** Write the three fall times for each parachute. Add the three times and divide by 3 to get the average. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each parachute, using the averages.
- **results table:** Parachute | Canopy and load | Fall time 1 (seconds) | Fall time 2 (seconds) | Fall time 3 (seconds) | Average (seconds)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the polythene, the strings and the coins. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guesses at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will make parachutes. Which do you think will land first, a small one or a big one? And will 4 coins fall 4 times as fast as 1 coin? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We make parachutes. Which lands first, a small or a big one? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the big parachute will fall more slowly? (good: It catches more air.; It has more drag.; It is bigger, so the air pushes on it more.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write the guess for 4 coins as a number, such as '4 times as fast' or 'twice as fast'.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut a 20 centimetre square and a 40 centimetre square from the polythene. Tape a 30 centimetre string to each corner. Tape the four strings to one coin, so the strings are even.  
  *Parent says:* Cut the squares with a ruler. Tape one string to each corner. Gather the four loose ends and tape them to a coin.  
  *Child mode:* Cut two squares. Tape a string to each corner. Tape the strings to a coin.
  - Note: Make the squares exact. Make a third parachute with the big square and 4 coins taped together. Keep the strings even, or the canopy will lean. Label them A (small, 1 coin), B (big, 1 coin) and C (big, 4 coins). For D, scrunch the big sheet into a tight ball and tape it round 1 coin.
  - Check: Why must the strings and the polythene be the same for every parachute? → **So only the size or the load changes**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark a spot about 2 metres up a wall with tape. Stand the adult underneath it. Hold parachutes A and B at the mark, one in each hand. Let go at the same moment and watch which lands first.  
  *Parent says:* Hold both at the same height. Let go together. Which lands first? Is it what you thought?  
  *Child mode:* Hold two parachutes up high. Let go together. Which lands first?
  - Ask: Both carry the same coin. Why does one land later? (good: The big one catches more air.; The big one has more drag.; The air pushes harder on the big canopy.)
  - Note: Hold each parachute by the middle of the canopy and the coin, then let go together. If a parachute folds up and drops like a stone, count it as a failed drop and do it again. Do not explain yet. Let your child say what they see.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Time each of A, B, C and D three times. The adult drops from the mark. The child starts the stopwatch at the drop and stops it at the landing. Write each time in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Start the stopwatch as I let go. Stop it when it touches the floor. Write the time. We do each one three times.  
  *Child mode:* Start the stopwatch at the drop. Stop it at the floor. Write the time.
  - Ask: What happens to the fall time as the load gets heavier? (good: The parachute falls faster.; The time gets shorter.; It lands sooner.)
  - Note: Expect roughly 1 to 2 seconds for the small parachute, 2 to 3 for the big one, a bit over 1 second for the big one with 4 coins and under 1 second for the scrunched ball. Your numbers will differ. The same person should use the stopwatch each time, because reaction time adds about a fifth of a second of error. Mark the time as a failed drop if the canopy folds.
  - Check: Parachute B takes 2.8, 2.6 and 2.7 seconds. What is the average? → **2.7 seconds**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out each average. Draw a bar chart with a bar for each of A, B, C and D. Compare C with A.  
  *Parent says:* Add the three times for each one, and divide by 3. Draw a bar for each parachute. Now look at C and A. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Find each average. Draw a bar for each one. Compare C and A.
  - Ask: Does the parachute with 4 coins fall 4 times as fast as the one with 1 coin? (good: No, it is faster but not 4 times.; About twice as fast.; The time only got a bit shorter.)
  - Note: Many families find that B takes about twice as long as C, not four times. This is because four times the weight gives only about twice the terminal speed. You may also find that C and A land at similar times. A big canopy with a heavy load can fall as fast as a small canopy with a light one.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then explain terminal speed, once they have said their ideas.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the big canopy catches more air, so the air pushes back harder, and the parachute stops speeding up at a lower speed. After they have said what they saw, explain: a falling thing speeds up until drag equals weight. A bigger canopy gives more drag at the same speed, so the balance comes at a lower speed. A heavier load needs more drag, so the balance comes at a higher speed.
  - Think: Why did the scrunched sheet fall so fast, when it is the same polythene?
### e2 · Steer a Paper Plane: which flap turns it which way? (35 min)
- **question:** If you bend the back edges of a paper plane's wings, can you steer it? Which bend makes it climb, which makes it dive and which makes it turn?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. What will a plane do if you bend the back edges of both wings up? What will it do if you bend one up and one down? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** You fold every plane the same way, from the same kind of paper, and throw each one at the same speed, level, from the same spot. Only the bends at the back of the wings change. Each plane is thrown three times, because one throw can be spoilt by a wobbly arm. The plane with no bends is the control. It shows how the plane flies with no steering.
- **safety:** Throw only when nobody is in front of you, and never at a face. Keep the pointed nose away from eyes.
- **record:** For each plane, write what the nose does, where it lands, and whether it turns. Draw the path of each plane from above, with the start line and the landing spot.
- **results table:** Bends at the back of the wings | What the nose does | Where it lands (steps from the start, and left or right) | Turn left, turn right, climb or dive?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the paper and the ruler. Ask for a guess and a reason for each bend. Write the guesses on the results table.  
  *Parent says:* We will fold a paper plane and bend the back of its wings. What will happen if both are bent up? And if one is up and one is down? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We bend the back of the wings. What will the plane do? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think a bend at the back of a wing changes the flight? (good: It changes how the air pushes on the wing.; It pushes the air up or down.; It tips the plane.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Mark the left and right wing with a pencil L and R, as seen by the pilot looking forward.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fold one plane with the steps in the pictures. Press every crease flat with your fingernail. Make four more planes exactly the same way. Mark L and R on each.  
  *Parent says:* Fold the paper in half and open it. Fold the top corners in to the middle line. Fold the slanted edges in again. Fold the plane in half. Fold the wings down.  
  *Child mode:* Fold a plane. Make four the same. Mark L and R on the wings.
  - Note: Fold carefully so the planes are the same. A plane with crooked wings will turn on its own, which spoils the test. Fold the wings so they are level and equal.
  - Check: Why must all five planes be folded the same way? → **So only the bends change**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand behind the start line. Throw plane 1, with no bends, three times, level and gently. Write what it does and where it lands each time.  
  *Parent says:* Throw it level, like putting a ball on a shelf. Do it three times. Write where it lands and which way it curves, if it curves.  
  *Child mode:* Throw the plane three times. Write where it lands.
  - Note: Plane 1 is the control. If it already curves, bend the wings level again, and refold if needed. Use the same gentle throw each time.
- **s4 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Bend both back edges of plane 2 up, by 1 centimetre. Throw it three times. Then bend both edges of plane 3 down. Then bend only the left edge of plane 4 up and the right edge down. Then do the opposite on plane 5. Write each result.  
  *Parent says:* For plane 2, bend both back edges up. Throw it three times and watch the nose. Then plane 3, both down. Then plane 4, left up and right down. Then plane 5, the opposite.  
  *Child mode:* Bend the wing edges as the table says. Throw each three times. Write what the plane does.
  - Ask: What does the nose do when both back edges are bent up? (good: It goes up.; It climbs and then falls.; The plane loops or swoops.)
  - Ask: When the left edge is up and the right edge is down, which way does the plane turn? (good: To the left.; It turns towards the up flap.)
  - Note: A common result: both edges up make the nose rise, so the plane climbs steeply, stalls and lands short. Both edges down make it dive and land early. With the left edge up and the right edge down, the left wing lifts less and drops, and the plane turns left. Yours may differ a little. If a plane does something odd, check that your throw was level and as gentle as before, and bend the edges more.
  - Check: Plane 4 has its left back edge up and its right back edge down. Which way will it most likely turn? → **To the left**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then explain pitch, roll and banking, once they have said their ideas.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think each bend would do? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that a bend up makes that part of the wing lift less, and a bend down makes it lift more. After they have said what they saw, explain: both flaps up push the back of the plane down, so the nose rises. One flap up makes one wing lift less, so it drops and the plane rolls. A rolled plane lifts sideways too, and that pulls it round a curve. Real planes do the same with elevators and ailerons, and birds twist their wings and tail.
  - Think: How does a bird steer, when she has no flaps?

## Activities
### a1 · The Card in the Fan: how hard does the wind push? (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up sets up the fan and turns it on. Keep fingers and hair away from the fan grill.
- **record:** A table with four rows: card flat to the wind, card edge-on, card bent into a cup with the open side facing the fan, and the cup with its rounded back facing the fan. In each row, write how far the card was blown sideways, in centimetres.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the thread to the top of the card. Tape the other end of the thread to a shelf, 50 centimetres in front of the fan. Lay the ruler on the floor under the card, with zero under the thread.  
  *Parent says:* The card hangs by a thread in front of the fan. The ruler on the floor will show how far the wind blows it. Which way of holding the card do you think the wind will push hardest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the card. Lay a ruler under it. Guess which way gets pushed most.
  - Ask: Which way of holding the card do you think the wind will push hardest, and why? (good: Flat to the fan, because it faces the most air.; The cup, because it catches the air.; Edge-on gets the least push.)
  - Note: Put the fan on its lowest setting. Keep the card at the same distance each time. The card should swing a clear 10 to 30 centimetres when flat to the wind.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Turn on the fan, low. Wait 10 seconds for the card to settle. Read how far it is blown, flat to the fan. Then turn it edge-on. Then bend it into a cup, open side to the fan. Then turn the cup round. Write the four readings.  
  *Parent says:* Wait until the card stops swinging. Then read the ruler. Do the flat card, the edge-on card, the open cup and the rounded back of the cup. Write each reading.  
  *Child mode:* Wait for the card to settle. Read the ruler. Test all four ways. Write each reading.
  - Ask: Which way did the wind push the card hardest? Which way least? (good: Flat or the open cup the most.; Edge-on the least.; The rounded back of the cup less than the open side.)
  - Note: The flat card and the open cup are blown about the same, and the cup's rounded back about a third as far. Edge-on is blown almost nothing. Your numbers will differ. The cup is bent from the same card, so only its shape changes.
  - Check: The open cup is blown further than the rounded back of the same cup. What does this show? → **Shape changes how hard the air pushes**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what they noticed. Compare the four readings. Ask where else they have felt the same difference.  
  *Parent says:* Which way got the most push? Which got the least? Where have you felt this before, when you ran or rode a cycle?  
  *Child mode:* Which way got the most push? Which got the least?
  - Think: Why does an open umbrella pull so hard in the wind?
### a2 · Whirls Behind a Spoon: the wake in a tray (20 min)
- **safety:** Keep pepper away from eyes and noses, and skip it if anyone is allergic to pepper or turmeric. Wipe up spilled water at once so nobody slips.
- **record:** Draw the pepper on the water behind the round spoon and behind the flat ruler. Say which one left the bigger whirls, and for how long they kept turning.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the tray with 2 centimetres of water. Wait until the water is still. Sprinkle a very thin layer of pepper over it. Ask your child to guess which shape will leave the bigger swirl behind it.  
  *Parent says:* We will pull two shapes through the water. One is round and one is a thin edge. Which do you think will leave the bigger whirl behind it? Why?

## Worksheets
### Drag and Falling (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which sentence says what drag is?  
   Options: The push of air against a thing moving through it / The pull of the Earth on a falling thing / The push that makes a plane go forward  
   **Answer:** The push of air against a thing moving through it. *Why:* Drag is the air pushing back against anything that moves through it. The pull of the Earth is weight, and the push forward is thrust.
2. (fill) Drag always pushes ____ the direction the thing is moving.  
   **Answer:** ['against', 'opposite to', 'opposite']. *Why:* Drag pushes back against the motion. That is why it slows things down.
3. (truefalse) A card held edge-on to the wind feels the same push as the same card held flat to the wind.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The flat card faces much more air, so the push is much bigger. Edge-on, it hardly faces the air at all.
4. (mcq) A cyclist rides into the wind at 10 kilometres an hour, and then at 20. About how much bigger is the push of the air at 20?  
   Options: About 4 times / About 2 times / The same  
   **Answer:** About 4 times. *Why:* At twice the speed she meets twice as much air each second, and hits each bit twice as hard. Two times two is four.
5. (match) Match each thing in the wind to how much drag it has.  
   **Answer:** An open umbrella facing the wind → a lot of drag; A closed umbrella pointing into the wind → little drag; A flat plate facing the wind → a lot of drag; A teardrop with its round end forward → very little drag. *Why:* Drag is big when a blunt shape faces the air, and small when the shape is streamlined or is turned edge-on.
6. (sort) Does each of these make the drag on a cyclist bigger or smaller?  
   **Answer:** Sitting up straight → Bigger drag; Crouching low over the handlebars → Smaller drag; Wearing a loose shirt that flaps → Bigger drag; Wearing a smooth, tight helmet → Smaller drag; Riding twice as fast → Bigger drag; Riding close behind another cyclist → Smaller drag. *Why:* A smaller, smoother body facing the air has less drag. Flapping cloth, sitting up and extra speed all add drag. A rider behind another rides in calmer air.
7. (count) A raindrop falls at a steady 6 metres each second. How many metres does it fall in 10 seconds?  
   **Answer:** 60. *Why:* 6 metres each second for 10 seconds is 6 times 10, which is 60 metres.
8. (mcq) A big raindrop stops speeding up as it falls. What does this tell you?  
   Options: The drag has grown as big as its weight / Gravity has stopped pulling it / The cloud stopped pushing it  
   **Answer:** The drag has grown as big as its weight. *Why:* When drag equals weight, the forces are balanced and the drop falls at a steady speed. Gravity is still pulling it.
9. (order) Put these in the right order, for a parachute that you let go.  
   **Answer:** ['You let go and it starts to fall', 'It speeds up and the drag grows', 'The drag becomes as big as its weight', 'It falls at a steady speed']. *Why:* A falling parachute speeds up and the drag grows with its speed. When the drag equals the weight, it falls at a steady speed.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? A parachute canopy, an open umbrella, a kingfisher's beak, a flat sheet of card.  
   **Answer:** The kingfisher's beak, because it is streamlined and cuts through air with little drag. The other three are wide and flat, with a lot of drag.. *Why:* The beak is rounded at the front and tapers to the back, so air flows smoothly round it. The others are made to catch or block the air.
11. (draw) Draw a parachute falling at a steady speed. Draw an arrow for its weight and an arrow for the drag, and say how long each arrow is.  
   **Answer:** A parachute with a down arrow for weight and an up arrow for drag. Both arrows are the same length.. *Why:* At a steady speed the forces are balanced, so the arrows are equal and opposite.
12. (write) Two parachutes carry the same coin. The big one lands later than the small one. Explain why, using the word drag.  
   **Answer:** The big canopy catches more air, so it has more drag at the same speed. The drag equals the weight at a lower speed, so it falls more slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a bigger canopy has more drag, so the forces balance at a lower speed.
13. (truefalse) A parachute with four times the weight falls four times as fast.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A heavier load falls faster, but four times the weight gives only about twice the speed.
14. (mcq) A badminton shuttlecock is hit sideways. Why does it end up flying cork-first?

   **Answer:** The feathers have more drag and trail behind the heavy cork. *Why:* The feathers catch much more air than the cork, so the drag swings them behind. This steadies the shuttlecock.
15. (perspective) Imagine a friend says: Heavy things always fall faster than light things.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says that in air, drag slows the light, wide feather more than the stone, and with no air they fall together.. *Why:* In the parachute races, the big canopy fell slowly because of drag. Weight alone is not what sets how fast things fall in air.
16. (write) Name two ways a cyclist can reduce the drag on herself, and say why each works.  
   **Answer:** She can crouch low, so less of her faces the air. She can wear a smooth, tight helmet and clothes, so the air flows round her and does not catch on flapping cloth.. *Why:* Drag depends on the size of the area facing the air and on shape. ### Steering and Staying Steady (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does a heavier parachute fall faster than a lighter one with the same canopy?  
   Options: It needs more drag to balance its weight, so it must go faster / Gravity pulls harder on heavy things in a different way / A heavier parachute has a smaller canopy  
   **Answer:** It needs more drag to balance its weight, so it must go faster. *Why:* Drag grows with speed. A heavier load needs more drag to balance it, so the drag only grows enough at a higher speed.
2. (fill) Four times the weight gives only about ____ times the falling speed.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* The speed grows with the square root of the weight. The square root of 4 is 2.
3. (match) Match each part of a plane to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Elevator → tips the nose up or down (pitch); Aileron → tilts the wings (roll); Rudder → swings the nose left or right (yaw); Propeller or engine → gives thrust to go forward. *Why:* Each part changes one thing. The elevator pitches the plane, the ailerons roll it, the rudder yaws it, and the engine pushes it forward.
4. (sort) On a paper plane, what does each bend at the back of the wings do to the plane?  
   **Answer:** Both back edges bent up → Nose rises; Both back edges bent down → Nose dips; Left edge up and right edge down → Plane rolls; Right edge up and left edge down → Plane rolls; A raised elevator on a real plane → Nose rises. *Why:* Bends that match on both wings pitch the plane. Bends that are opposite roll it.
5. (truefalse) A plane mainly turns by banking, which tilts the lift sideways.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. When the wings are tilted, part of the lift pulls the plane sideways, and it goes round a curve.
6. (count) A car at 40 kilometres an hour has some amount of air drag. At 120 kilometres an hour, how many times bigger is that drag?  
   **Answer:** 9. *Why:* 120 is three times 40. The drag grows with the square of the speed, so it is 3 times 3, which is 9 times bigger.
7. (order) Put these in order, for how a plane makes a left turn.  
   **Answer:** ['The pilot rolls the plane to the left', 'The wings tilt and the lift tilts sideways', 'The sideways part of the lift pulls the plane left', 'The plane flies round a curve to the left']. *Why:* A turn begins with a roll. The tilted lift then has a sideways part that pulls the plane round the curve.
8. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? A shuttlecock's feathers, an arrow's feathers, a kite's tail, a flat stone.  
   **Answer:** The flat stone, because it has no tail. The other three are at the back and keep the thing pointing the right way.. *Why:* Feathers and tails add drag at the back, which keeps the front pointing forward. A stone has no such part.
9. (draw) Draw a V of geese seen from above. Add small arrows to show where the air rises beside the wing tip of the goose in front.  
   **Answer:** A V shape with a goose at the front and others behind, each slightly to one side. Small upward arrows are drawn just outside the wing tip of each goose.. *Why:* A bird behind and to one side can catch the rising air from the wing tip of the bird in front. This is called upwash.
10. (write) A mouse can fall down a deep well and walk away, but a person would be badly hurt. Explain why, using the words skin and weight.  
   **Answer:** A mouse has a lot of skin for her weight, so air slows her fall far more. A person has less skin for their weight, so they fall much faster.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a small body has more skin for its weight than a big one, so drag slows it more.
11. (mcq) A jet lands at a much lower speed than it cruises. Why does it need to go faster when it flies high?  
   Options: The thin air gives less lift, so the wings must move faster / The engines are stronger at height / Gravity is stronger at height  
   **Answer:** The thin air gives less lift, so the wings must move faster. *Why:* Lift depends on how much air the wings push down. Thin air gives less lift at the same speed, so the plane must go faster to make enough.
12. (truefalse) In space, an aircraft's wing flaps could steer a spacecraft.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Flaps work only by pushing on air. With no air, they do nothing, so a spacecraft steers by firing small rockets.
13. (perspective) Scientists argue about why birds fly in a V.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view and says what could be measured, such as the birds' heart rates or their positions.. *Why:* Scientists do not yet agree how much of the V is for saving energy and how much is for seeing. Tracking the birds' wing tips and heartbeats gives evidence.
14. (write) Why must a paper plane have more weight near its nose than near its tail?  
   **Answer:** If the balance point is near the front, a bump makes forces that turn the nose back down to level. With too little weight at the front, the nose rises, the plane stalls and it falls.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the balance point should be ahead of the centre of lift, so the plane tends to come back to level flight.
15. (mcq) On an anemometer, all the cups face the same way round. Why does it spin?  
   Options: The wind pushes harder on the open sides than on the rounded backs / The cups are heavier on one side / The wind blows only on one cup

16. (fill) A spacecraft that comes back from space has a heat shield because drag turns its ____ into heat.

17. (write) A bird spreads her tail wide as she lands. Explain what that does to the forces on her.  
   **Answer:** A wide tail catches more air, so the drag is bigger and she slows down. She can also tilt the tail to steer.. *Why:* A spread tail adds drag, which slows her, and a tilted tail changes the air push on her back, which helps her turn.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these makes the drag on a moving thing bigger?  
   Options: Going faster / Turning off the engine / Making the front more rounded  
   **Answer:** Going faster. *Why:* Drag grows quickly with speed. A rounded, smooth front reduces drag, and turning off the engine does not change the push of the air.

   Options: It becomes about 4 times bigger / It stays the same / It doubles  
   **Answer:** It becomes about 4 times bigger. *Why:* Twice the speed means about four times the drag, because there is twice as much air each second, hit twice as hard.

   Options: The flat sheet has more drag / The ball is heavier / Gravity pulls the ball more  
   **Answer:** The flat sheet has more drag. *Why:* The paper is the same, so the weight is the same. The flat sheet faces more air, so its drag balances its weight at a lower speed.

   **Answer:** The 4-coin one falls faster, but not four times as fast. *Why:* A heavier load needs more drag to balance it, so it falls faster. But the speed grows only with the square root of the weight, so four times the weight gives about twice the speed.

## Open questions
- **Can anyone predict exactly how the swirling air behind a moving thing will behave?** The equations for moving air are well known, but nobody can solve them exactly for swirling, turbulent air. Whether they always have smooth answers is a million-dollar prize problem.
- **How much energy does a V really save for each kind of bird?** Measurements on ibises and pelicans show a saving, but it has been measured for very few kinds of birds, and seeing each other may matter too.
- **How do tiny insects steer so well in air that feels thick to them?** Scientists film them with very fast cameras. They still do not understand all the tricks that wings, hairs and tiny muscles use.
- **Could a plane ever steer like a bird, by changing the shape of the whole wing?** Engineers are testing wings that bend and twist smoothly, but nobody yet knows if they can be strong, safe and light enough for big planes.
- **How do bar-headed geese fly over the Himalayas, where the air is so thin?** Trackers show they fly in a swooping pattern, but scientists are still working out how their bodies make enough power in air with so little oxygen.

- In the Parachute Races, parachute B took 2.8, 2.6 and 2.7 seconds to fall. Work out the average time. **2.7 seconds.**. Add the times: 2.8 plus 2.6 plus 2.7 is 8.1. Divide 8.1 by 3 to get 2.7 seconds. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)

- If you go twice as fast, about how much bigger is the drag? → About four times bigger.
- Why does a falling thing stop speeding up? → Its drag has grown as big as its weight.
- What shape has the least drag? → A streamlined shape, rounded at the front and tapered at the back.
- Why does a shuttlecock fly cork-first? → The feathers have more drag and trail behind.
- What does an aileron do? → It rolls the plane by changing the lift on one wing.
- How does a plane turn? → It banks, and the tilted lift pulls it round the curve.
- Why do geese take turns at the front of a V? → The front bird works hardest, and the others catch her rising air.
