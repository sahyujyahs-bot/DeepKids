# How life might have begun: hot vents, the RNA world and deep time
`sci.evolution.story-of-life.L5`

**Summary.** Every living cell today comes from another living cell. So the first cell had no parent cell, and nobody saw how they began. Scientists have two main ideas about the place: hot vents on the sea floor, or warm pools on land. In this lesson you will test whether a soap skin can form in sea-salty water, which is one of the arguments between the two ideas. You will also fit the whole age of the Earth onto your own arm span.

**Intro.** *A hen comes from an egg, and the egg comes from a hen. Every cell comes from a cell. Follow that back far enough and you reach a first cell with no parent. Where could that cell have come from?* Nobody knows the answer yet. But scientists do not just guess. They look for places on Earth where the chemistry of life could start without any living being, and they test each step in the laboratory. In this lesson you will meet two such places, and test one of the arguments yourself with soap, salt and water. You will also see why the question is so hard. It happened about four billion years ago, and you will measure how long ago that is.

## Terms
- **Hydrothermal vent**: A hydrothermal vent is an opening in the sea floor where hot water comes out. The water was heated inside the rock below. *e.g.* The Kairei vent field lies nearly 2,500 metres down in the Indian Ocean. Hot water full of dissolved minerals pours out of chimneys there.
- **Black smoker**: A black smoker is a very hot vent near an undersea volcano. Its water can be hotter than 350 degrees Celsius and looks like black smoke. *e.g.* The black colour is tiny grains of metal minerals. They turn solid when the hot water meets the cold sea.
- **Alkaline**: A liquid is alkaline when it is the opposite of sour, or acid. Soap water and baking soda water are alkaline. *e.g.* Turmeric turns red in alkaline soap water. Turmeric stays yellow in acid lemon juice.
- **Alkaline vent**: An alkaline vent is a warm vent whose water is alkaline. It is heated by sea water reacting with rock, not by a volcano. *e.g.* The Lost City vents in the Atlantic Ocean are alkaline vents. Their water is 40 to 90 degrees Celsius and their chimneys are white.
- **Chemosynthesis**: Chemosynthesis is making food using energy from chemicals. Plants use energy from light, which is called photosynthesis. *e.g.* Bacteria at vents make sugar using energy from hydrogen sulfide, the gas that smells of rotten eggs.
- **Molecule**: A molecule is a group of atoms joined together. Atoms are the tiny particles that everything is made of. *e.g.* A water molecule is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.
- **RNA**: RNA is a long chain molecule made of four kinds of building blocks. They are called A, U, C and G for short. The order of the letters carries instructions. *e.g.* Your cells copy a piece of DNA into RNA, and then read the RNA to build a protein.
- **Nucleotide**: A nucleotide is one building block of RNA or DNA. Each letter in the chain is one nucleotide. *e.g.* A chain of 10 RNA letters is made of 10 nucleotides joined in a row.
- **Catalyst**: A catalyst is something that makes a chemical change go much faster. The catalyst is not used up by the change. *e.g.* The enzymes in your saliva are catalysts. They start breaking down the starch in rice while you chew.
- **Ribozyme**: A ribozyme is a piece of RNA that works as a catalyst. Most catalysts in cells are proteins, so this was a surprise. *e.g.* The part of the ribosome that joins amino acids into a protein is made of RNA. So the ribosome is a ribozyme.
- **Membrane**: A membrane is the very thin skin around a cell. It is made of fatty molecules, and it decides what goes in and out. *e.g.* Every one of your cells has a membrane. It is about 10,000 times thinner than a sheet of paper.
- **Fatty acid**: A fatty acid is a molecule with a head that mixes with water and a long tail that stays away from water. Soap is made from fatty acids. *e.g.* Coconut oil and ghee are built from fatty acids. A soap maker turns them into soap.
- **Protocell**: A protocell is a simple bubble with a skin of fatty molecules and other molecules inside. It is not alive. Scientists make protocells to study how cells might have begun. *e.g.* In laboratories, tiny fatty acid bubbles with RNA inside have been made to grow and split in two.
- **LUCA**: LUCA is short for the last universal common ancestor. LUCA was the population of early cells that every living being today descends from. *e.g.* A neem tree, a crow, a mushroom, the bacteria in curd and you all descend from LUCA.
- **Deep time**: Deep time is the huge stretch of time in Earth's history. It is measured in millions and billions of years. *e.g.* The Earth is about 4,500 million years old. Humans like us have lived for about 0.3 million years.
- **Scale model**: A scale model shows something too big or too long to see, at a size we can handle. One unit on the model always stands for the same amount of the real thing. *e.g.* On a map, 1 centimetre may stand for 10 kilometres. On your arm span, 1 centimetre may stand for 36 million years.
- **Hypothesis**: A hypothesis is an idea that explains something and can be tested. It is not yet known to be right. *e.g.* 'Life began at alkaline vents' is a hypothesis. So is 'life began in warm pools on land'.

## Explanations
### What the first cell needed · *idea*
**Definition:** Scientists who study the start of life look for three things together: a skin, instructions that can be copied, and a steady supply of energy.

Every living cell today has three things. The first is a membrane, a thin skin that keeps the cell's molecules together and apart from the water outside. The second is a set of instructions, written in DNA and RNA, which the cell copies and hands on to their young. The third is a steady supply of energy and of building materials, because a cell must keep building and repairing all the time. Why does this make the start of life hard to explain? Because in cells today, each of the three depends on the other two. The instructions say how to build the skin. The skin holds the instructions. Both need energy. So scientists ask how each one could have begun without the others, and where on the young Earth all three could have come together. This is simplified. Real cells need many more things. But these three are where most of the research is done.
- e.g. A bacterium in curd has a membrane, DNA and RNA instructions, and takes energy from the sugar in milk.
- e.g. A soap bubble has a skin but no instructions and no energy supply. So a bubble does not grow, repair or have young.
- e.g. A flame has a steady supply of energy but no skin and no instructions. So a flame is not counted as alive either.
### Hydrothermal vents: black smokers and alkaline vents · *defined*
**Definition:** A hydrothermal vent is an opening in the sea floor where water heated inside the rock comes out. Black smokers are very hot and acid. Alkaline vents are warm and alkaline.

Sea water seeps down into cracks in the sea floor. Inside the rock the water is heated, and it dissolves minerals from the rock. Hot water is lighter than cold water, so it rises and comes out again. There are two main kinds of vent. A black smoker forms near an undersea volcano, where hot melted rock lies close below. Its water can be hotter than 350 degrees Celsius, and it is acid. It does not boil, because the weight of the sea above presses on it. When the hot water meets the cold sea, metal minerals turn solid as tiny black grains. They build a dark chimney. An alkaline vent needs no volcano. Sea water reacts with a green mineral called olivine. Olivine is found in rock that comes from deep inside the Earth. The reaction itself gives out heat. It also makes hydrogen gas and makes the water alkaline. This reaction has a long name, serpentinisation, because the new rock it makes is called serpentine. The water comes out at 40 to 90 degrees Celsius and builds white chimneys of carbonate, the material of limestone and seashells.
- e.g. Black smokers were first seen in 1979 on the East Pacific Rise. The water measured about 380 degrees Celsius.
- e.g. The Lost City vents in the Atlantic are alkaline vents. The tallest chimney, called Poseidon, is about 60 metres high, as tall as an 18-storey building.
- e.g. In December 2024, Indian scientists from NCPOR in Goa and NIOT in Chennai took their first pictures of an active vent. It lies about 4,500 metres down in the Indian Ocean.
- e.g. In the Hot Vent in a Jar test, hot coloured water rises out of a small bottle through cold water. Vent water rises for the same reason.
### Life at vents lives on chemicals, not on sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** Chemosynthesis is making food using the energy in chemicals. At vents, bacteria and archaea do this, and the other animals there depend on them.

No sunlight reaches the deep sea floor, so no plant can grow there. Yet vents are crowded with life. The food makers at a vent are microbes: bacteria, and their look-alikes called archaea. They take chemicals from the vent water, such as hydrogen sulfide or hydrogen gas. They use the energy in those chemicals to build sugar from carbon dioxide. Everyone else at the vent feeds on these microbes, or keeps them inside their own body. A giant tube worm has no mouth and no gut. They are fed by billions of bacteria who live inside them. This is simplified in one way. Most vent animals and many vent microbes also use oxygen dissolved in the sea water. That oxygen was made by photosynthesis far above. So most vent life today is not completely cut off from the Sun. But some vent microbes need no oxygen at all. They live on hydrogen and carbon dioxide alone, and scientists think the earliest cells may have lived like them.
- e.g. Giant tube worms at Pacific vents grow longer than a tall adult human, and have no mouth. Bacteria inside them make their food.
- e.g. The scaly-foot snail lives at vents in the Indian Ocean. Their foot is covered in scales that hold iron.
- e.g. Inside the Lost City chimneys live archaea who feed on hydrogen and methane from the rock.
### The alkaline vent idea: energy from a difference · *idea*
**Definition:** The alkaline vent idea says that life began inside the chimneys of alkaline vents. The energy came from the difference between the alkaline vent water and the acid sea.

First, a fact about cells today. Every cell you know of gets usable energy in the same odd way. The cell pushes tiny charged particles called protons to one side of a membrane. Protons are what make a liquid acid. The protons then flow back through a tiny turning protein, and that flow is used to make ATP, the molecule that carries energy in cells. Peter Mitchell, a British scientist, worked this out in 1961. So every cell lives on a difference in acid between two sides of a thin skin. Now the idea. In 1989 the British geologist Michael Russell suggested that such a difference existed on the young Earth before any cell. Scientists think the early sea was a little acid, because the air held much more carbon dioxide than now. Alkaline vent water is the opposite. Inside a vent chimney the two are kept apart by thin mineral walls full of tiny holes. Russell, the biologist William Martin and the biochemist Nick Lane argue that this natural difference could have driven the first chemistry of life. Hydrogen from the vent and carbon dioxide from the sea could have been joined into the first carbon molecules. The tiny holes could have held the molecules together, as a cell does. The idea is not proven. In laboratory tests, hydrogen and carbon dioxide have been joined this way only in small amounts.
- e.g. In the Acid Sea, Alkaline Vent test, baking soda water and lemon juice meet on turmeric paper. You see a sharp line where alkaline meets acid.
- e.g. Your own cells make ATP by letting protons flow across a membrane inside each mitochondrion.
- e.g. Russell described alkaline vents as a likely place for life to start in 1989. The Lost City vents were found 11 years later, in 2000.
### The RNA world idea · *idea*
**Definition:** The RNA world idea says that before cells had DNA and proteins, there was a stage when RNA did both jobs: carrying the instructions and doing the work.

In cells today, DNA stores the instructions and proteins do most of the work. But proteins are built by following the instructions in DNA, and DNA can be copied only with the help of proteins. So which came first? RNA offers a way out. RNA carries instructions in the order of four letters, as DNA does. And in 1982 Thomas Cech found an RNA that cut and joined itself with no protein helping. RNA can be a catalyst too. A year later Sidney Altman found another. Such RNAs are called ribozymes. So one kind of molecule can do both jobs. In 1986 Walter Gilbert named the idea 'the RNA world'. The best evidence is inside you. The ribosome is the part of every cell that builds proteins. When scientists worked out its exact shape, they found that the part doing the joining is RNA. Venki Ramakrishnan, who was born in Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu, shared the 2009 Nobel Prize with Ada Yonath and Thomas Steitz for this work. There are real problems too. RNA is fragile and breaks up in hot water. Its building blocks are hard to make without cells. In 2009 John Sutherland's team made two of the four from simple chemicals, which helped. And nobody has yet shown an RNA that copies all of itself, again and again, without help. Scientists have made ribozymes that copy other short RNA chains, and they are getting closer.
- e.g. The ribosome in a rice plant, in a crow and in you has the same RNA core. All three inherited it from very early cells.
- e.g. ATP, the energy-carrying molecule in every cell, is built from the RNA letter A. Many scientists see this as another trace of an RNA world.
- e.g. In the game Copy or Crash, you find out why a long RNA chain is harder to copy without a mistake than a short one.
- **Supporters of the RNA world:** RNA can store instructions and act as a catalyst, and the oldest machines in the cell are built around RNA. So an RNA stage is the simplest way to explain how instructions and workers began together.
- **Scientists who think metabolism came first:** RNA is too fragile and too hard to make to be the first step. They think simple chains of chemical reactions, fed by energy at a vent, came first and RNA arose later inside them.
- **Scientists who think RNA and small proteins began together:** Short chains of amino acids form easily and help RNA to hold its shape. In this view there was never a world of RNA alone.
### The copying problem: more letters, more mistakes · *defined*
**Definition:** Every copy of a chain can have mistakes. The longer the chain, the more likely it is that a copy has at least one mistake.

Suppose a copier makes a mistake on about one letter in every six. A chain of 4 letters will often be copied perfectly. A chain of 12 letters almost never will. After a few rounds of copying, the long message is lost in mistakes. The German chemist Manfred Eigen pointed this out in 1971. It makes a puzzle for the RNA world. A good copier needs a long RNA chain, because a short chain cannot fold into a careful copying shape. But a long chain cannot be kept without a good copier. Scientists call this Eigen's paradox. A paradox is two things that both seem true but cannot both be true. There are several ideas for how early RNA got around it. Perhaps several short chains worked together. Perhaps a skin kept good copiers and their copies in the same bubble. Mistakes are not only a problem. A small number of mistakes is how new kinds of chain appear, so that some can work better than their parents. Too many mistakes lose the message. With no mistakes at all, nothing new can ever appear.
- e.g. In Copy or Crash, a 4-letter chain is copied with no mistake about half the time. A 12-letter chain manages this about one time in nine.
- e.g. Your cells copy 3 billion DNA letters with very few mistakes. They use proteins that check each letter and fix errors.
- e.g. When you copy a long phone number by hand, you are more likely to slip than with a short one.
### Skins that form by themselves, and the trouble with salt · *debated*
**Definition:** Fatty acid molecules in water line up by themselves into thin skins and hollow bubbles. Salt in the water can stop this. Scientists disagree about what that means for where life began.

A fatty acid molecule has a head that mixes with water and a long tail that stays away from water. Put many of them in water and they arrange themselves. The tails turn towards each other and the heads face the water. The result is a thin double layer, which can close up into a hollow bubble far too small to see. Nobody has to build it. Jack Szostak and other scientists have made such bubbles with RNA inside, and have seen them grow and split. These are protocells. Soap is made from fatty acids, so soap shows the same behaviour on a size you can see. The wall of a soap bubble is a thin film of water with soap molecules lined up on both faces. This is a model, not the same thing. A cell membrane is a double layer in water, and a bubble wall is a film in air. But both form because of the same head and tail. Now the trouble. Sea water holds salt, and the particles of salt pull fatty acids out of the water into solid specks. Then there are fewer left to make skins. You test this in the Salt Water Skin Test.
- e.g. In the Salt Water Skin Test, the same soap usually makes tall foam in plain water and much less in sea-salty water.
- e.g. In 2018 David Deamer's team mixed fatty acids into hot spring water and into sea water. Bubbles formed in the spring water and not in the sea water.
- e.g. In 2019 Sean Jordan and Nick Lane's team used a mixture of 12 fatty molecules. Bubbles did form in warm, salty, alkaline water.
- **David Deamer and Bruce Damer:** Fatty acid skins fall apart in sea water, so the first cells could not have formed in the sea. They needed fresh water, such as hot spring pools on land.
- **Nick Lane and Sean Jordan:** The earlier tests used only one or two kinds of fatty molecule. A vent would make a mixture, and a mixture forms skins well in warm, salty, alkaline water. So the salt argument does not rule out vents.
### Warm pools on land: wet and dry, again and again · *idea*
**Definition:** The warm pool idea says that life began in small hot spring pools on land. The pools dried out and filled again many times, and drying pushed small molecules together into chains.

In 1871 Charles Darwin wrote to his friend Joseph Hooker that life might have started in 'some warm little pond'. The modern form of the idea comes from David Deamer and Bruce Damer. To join building blocks into a chain such as RNA, a molecule of water must be removed at each join. In the open sea that is hard, because there is water everywhere, and water tends to break such chains apart again. In a pool at the edge of a hot spring, the water dries up and comes back many times a day. Each time the pool dries, the molecules are crowded together on the rock and some join into chains. Each time the pool fills, fatty skins form and wrap up some of the chains. In Pune, Sudha Rajamani's laboratory at IISER tests such wet and dry cycles. Her team has shown that they help building blocks join, and that they change the fatty skins too. The pool idea has problems as well. The young Earth had little dry land. Strong ultraviolet light from the Sun reached the ground, because there was no ozone layer, and it breaks many molecules. Some scientists answer that the same light also drives useful reactions.
- e.g. In Drying Drops, salt and turmeric in a drop of water end up crowded in a ring when the drop dries.
- e.g. When rasam boils down in the pan, the same salt is left in less water, so it tastes saltier. Drying crowds dissolved things together.
- e.g. Hot springs rise today at Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh and at Yellowstone in the United States. Small pools at the edges of such springs dry out and fill again.
### Where did life begin? The debate today · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists do not agree on where life began. The two leading ideas are alkaline vents in the deep sea and warm pools on land.

Each idea explains some things well and others badly. Vents give a steady supply of energy and of hydrogen for millions of years, and they are sheltered from sunlight and from falling space rocks. The way all cells use protons fits the vent idea well. But a vent is surrounded by sea water, which tends to break chains apart and may stop fatty skins from forming. Pools join building blocks into chains easily and let fresh water skins form. But pools are small, short-lived and open to harsh sunlight. It is possible that neither is right, or that the steps happened in different places. It is also possible that we can never know for certain, because the Earth has destroyed almost all rocks older than 4 billion years. What scientists can do is test whether each step works. That is why the work is done step by step in laboratories.
- e.g. The game Vent, Pool or Both lets you sort 16 real findings by which idea each one supports.
- e.g. Saturn's moon Enceladus has a salty ocean under ice. In 2017 scientists reported hydrogen gas in the jets that shoot from it, a sign of vents on its sea floor.
- e.g. If life began in pools on land, an ocean moon with no dry land would be a poor place to look for life.
- **Michael Russell, William Martin and Nick Lane:** Life began inside alkaline vents. The natural difference between vent water and sea water gave the energy, and all cells still use such a difference today.
- **David Deamer and Bruce Damer:** Life began in hot spring pools on land. Wet and dry cycles joined building blocks into chains, and fatty skins formed in the fresh water.
- **John Sutherland:** The building blocks of RNA can be made on the Earth's surface from simple chemicals, with ultraviolet light from the Sun driving the reactions. That light does not reach the deep sea.
- **Many other scientists:** The evidence is not strong enough to choose. Different steps may have happened in different places, and the place may never be known for sure.
### LUCA: reading the ancestor from living cells · *defined*
**Definition:** LUCA is the last universal common ancestor, the early cells from whom all living beings today descend. Scientists work out what LUCA was like from what all living cells share.

No fossil of LUCA has been found. So how can anyone say what LUCA was like? The method is the one you use in a family. If you, your cousin and your second cousin all have the same rare family recipe, the simplest explanation is that one shared ancestor had that recipe and passed it down. Biologists do this with genes. A gene found in bacteria, in archaea and in us was most likely already in the ancestor of all three. This way they find that LUCA already had a membrane, DNA, RNA, ribosomes, the genetic code and the proton way of making ATP. So LUCA was a full cell, not the first life. Much had already happened before LUCA. In 2016 William Martin's team found 355 gene families that go back to LUCA. Those genes suggest a cell who lived without oxygen, in a hot place, feeding on hydrogen, as microbes at vents do. A study in 2024 estimated that LUCA lived about 4.2 billion years ago and had about 2,600 kinds of protein. Both studies are argued over, and the date is uncertain.
- e.g. In Find the Ancestor, you compare four living beings and work out which features their shared ancestor must have had.
- e.g. The three RNA letters AUG mean 'start here' in a curd bacterium, a banana plant and a human. All inherited the code from LUCA.
- e.g. The oldest fossils that almost all scientists accept are stromatolites in Western Australia, about 3.48 billion years old. LUCA is older than any known fossil.
### Deep time on a scale model · *defined*
**Definition:** A scale model of time uses a length to stand for years. Every centimetre stands for the same number of years, so you can see how long each stretch of history was.

The Earth is about 4,500 million years old. The number is easy to say and almost impossible to picture. A scale model helps. You choose a length, such as your arm span, and let it stand for all 4,500 million years. Then you divide. If your arm span is 125 centimetres, each centimetre stands for 4,500 divided by 125, which is 36 million years. Now every event has a place. To find it, divide how long ago the event was by 36. The American writer John McPhee described this model in 1981, in the book where he first used the words 'deep time'. He pointed out that all of human history fits in the very tip of a fingernail. A scale model also shows why the start of life is so hard to study. It lies near the far fingertip. Almost no rock that old is left on Earth.
- e.g. On an arm span of 125 centimetres, the dinosaurs died out less than 2 centimetres from the fingertip.
- e.g. On the same arm span, the 5,000 years since people began to write are far thinner than a hair.
- e.g. The universe is about 13,800 million years old. On the same scale, that would need a little more than three arm spans.

## Mostly agreed
- Every living cell today comes from another living cell. *How we know:* Louis Pasteur showed in the 1860s that boiled broth stays free of microbes if dust is kept out. No one has ever seen a cell appear from non-living material.
- The Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, and life is at least 3.5 billion years old. *How we know:* Meteorites and the oldest minerals are dated by the steady change of radioactive atoms. Layered fossils made by microbes, called stromatolites, are found in rocks 3.48 billion years old in Western Australia.
- All living beings today descend from one population of early cells, called LUCA. *How we know:* Every cell uses the same four DNA letters, almost the same genetic code, ribosomes with an RNA core, and ATP. So many shared features are best explained by one shared ancestor.
- The small building blocks of life can form without living beings. *How we know:* Stanley Miller made amino acids from simple gases with electric sparks in 1952. Amino acids are also found in meteorites, and John Sutherland's team made RNA building blocks from simple chemicals in 2009.
- RNA can carry instructions and can also work as a catalyst. *How we know:* Thomas Cech found the first ribozyme in 1982. The part of the ribosome that joins amino acids is made of RNA.
- Fatty molecules form skins and bubbles in water by themselves. *How we know:* Anyone can see soap films form. Under microscopes, scientists see fatty acids form closed bubbles that can hold RNA, grow and split.
- Nobody yet knows how or where life began. *How we know:* No laboratory has made a living cell from non-living chemicals. Scientists who support vents and scientists who support pools publish evidence against each other's ideas every year.

## How we found out
**Jack Corliss and Tjeerd van Andel, geologists working in the United States, and Colleen Cavanaugh, an American biologist who was then a student, 1977 to 1981.** Until 1977, biologists thought the deep sea floor was almost empty. All food begins with plants, plants need light, and no light reaches the bottom. So deep-sea animals had to live on scraps sinking from above. On 17 February 1977, the geologists Jack Corliss and Tjeerd van Andel went down 2,500 metres in the small submarine Alvin, near the Galápagos Islands. They were looking for hot springs in the rock. They found the springs, and around them crowds of large clams and other animals. There was not one biologist on the ship. What were the animals eating? About three years later, Colleen Cavanaugh, a new student at Harvard University, heard a talk about the giant tube worms from the vents. The worms have no mouth and no gut, and their bodies hold crystals of sulfur. She said that the worms must have bacteria inside them who feed on the sulfur chemicals in the vent water. She and her colleagues looked, and found the bacteria. Their report came out in 1981. Life could be built on energy from rock and water alone. Scientists then began to ask whether life had started that way.  
*What people believed before:* That every food chain on Earth begins with sunlight, so the deep sea floor must be almost empty. The crowded vents, and the bacteria inside the tube worms, showed that food can also be made with energy from chemicals.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Life began inside alkaline vents on the sea floor.** (Michael Russell, William Martin, Nick Lane and others, from 1989; *debated*)  
  For: Vents give steady energy for a very long time. All cells still make energy from a difference in acid across a membrane, as a vent chimney has naturally. LUCA's genes look like those of a vent microbe.  
  Against: Sea water tends to break chains such as RNA apart, and salt can stop simple fatty skins from forming. Joining hydrogen and carbon dioxide has worked only weakly in laboratory vents.
- **Life began in warm pools on land that dried and filled again.** (Charles Darwin in 1871, and David Deamer and Bruce Damer today; *debated*)  
  For: Drying joins building blocks into chains. Fatty skins form well in fresh spring water. Sunlight can drive the making of RNA building blocks.  
  Against: The young Earth had little land. Pools are small and do not last. Ultraviolet light and falling space rocks made the surface a harsh place.
- **RNA came first, before DNA and proteins.** (Alexander Rich in 1962, then Carl Woese, Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel. Walter Gilbert named it the RNA world in 1986.; *debated*)  
  For: RNA can store instructions and act as a catalyst. The ribosome's working core is RNA.  
  Against: RNA is fragile and hard to make. No RNA has yet been shown to copy all of itself without help.
- **Chains of chemical reactions came first, and instructions came later.** (Günter Wächtershäuser, Michael Russell and others; *debated*)  
  For: Simple reactions fed by vent chemicals need no fragile long chains. Some of the oldest reactions in cells can run on mineral surfaces without proteins.  
  Against: Without instructions that are copied, it is hard to see how a set of reactions could improve over generations.
- **Life, or its building blocks, came to Earth from space.** (An old idea, held today by a small number of scientists for life itself; *minority*)  
  For: Amino acids and other building blocks really are found in meteorites. So space did deliver some building blocks.  
  Against: No living cell has ever been found in a meteorite. And even if life came from elsewhere, we would still have to explain how life began there.
- **All life on the sea floor depends on food that sinks from the sunlit water above.** (Most biologists before 1977; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants need light, and no light reaches the deep sea. The deep sea floor that had been sampled held few animals.  
  Against: In 1977 crowded communities of animals were found at vents. Colleen Cavanaugh showed that bacteria there make food from chemicals.

## Roots
We have learned how life might have begun, with a skin, a chain of instructions and a flow of energy. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Salt Water Skin Test, nobody built the foam. The soap molecules lined up into skins by themselves, and how well they did so depended on the water around them. Fatty molecules on the young Earth closed into hollow skins in the same way. So an inside and an outside can exist before anything is alive. The first cell did not have to build the first skin.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a skin or a wall as something a living being builds, to keep the world out. But the first skin was not built from the inside by anyone. The molecules and the water outside made it, and what the water held decided whether it could form at all.  
*Evidence:* In the Salt Water Skin Test, the same soap made tall foam in plain water and little in very salty water. In laboratories, fatty acids close into hollow bubbles that can hold RNA, grow and split. In 2018 such bubbles formed in hot spring water but not in sea water, and in 2019 a mixture formed them in salty, alkaline water.

- *who am i:* Every cell of your body is wrapped in a fatty skin of the kind that forms by itself in water. Every cell also makes energy with a flow of protons, as LUCA did about four billion years ago.
- *what is life:* A skin, a chain that can be copied and a flow of energy can each exist without life. Life may be what happened when the three began to work as one. Nobody knows yet how they joined.
- *what is this world:* The Earth was not an empty stage waiting for life to arrive. Rock, sea water and heat carried out the first steps themselves. The chemistry of the water decided what could happen next.

## Story: The City Nobody Was Looking For
*This is a true story. The ship, the date, the people, the camera sled Argo, the dive in Alvin, the white towers and what heats them are all real. Gretchen Früh-Green's two short sentences in quotation marks are her own words, as she told them afterwards. The other spoken words, and what people were thinking in the control room, are imagined.*

In December 2000, a research ship called Atlantis was working in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. Under the ship, far down in the dark, stood a mountain. The mountain is called the Atlantis Massif, and its top is still hundreds of metres below the waves.

The scientists on board had come to study the mountain's rock. Donna Blackman, who led the voyage, wanted to know how such a mountain had been pushed up from deep inside the Earth. Nobody on the ship was looking for hot vents.

There was a good reason for that. Everyone knew where vents were found. A vent needs heat, and the heat comes from melted rock under an undersea volcano. The nearest line of volcanoes was fifteen kilometres away. The mountain under the ship was old and cold.

The scientists could not go down themselves every day. So at night they lowered a sled of cameras called Argo on a long cable. Argo flew slowly above the mountain, and sent pictures up to a small crowded room on the deck. The scientists took turns to sit there in the dark and watch the screens.

On the night of the third of December, the first watch saw grey rock, pale mud and a few curious fish. At midnight the second watch took over, led by a geologist called Barbara John. Beside her sat Gretchen Früh-Green, who had come from Zurich in Switzerland. The grey rock went on sliding past.

Then something white came into the picture.

It was tall, and it had branches like fingers. More white shapes came out of the dark behind it. 'When I first saw them I thought they were corals,' Gretchen said later.

She looked again. Corals are animals, and these were not alive. They were spires and cones of pale rock, as fine and white as frost on a window. Some stood higher than the camera's lights could reach.

'Then I went and got Debbie,' she said.

Debbie was Deborah Kelley. She had spent years studying vents, and she had seen more of them than almost anyone on Earth. She came into the little room and stared at the screens.

They were chimneys. She was sure of that. But they were the wrong chimneys in the wrong place.

Every vent chimney she knew was dark, built from black grains of metal. These were white. Every vent she knew poured out water like thick smoke. Nothing here was smoking. And every vent she knew sat on top of a volcano. Here there was no volcano at all.

The room filled with people. Somebody asked the question that all of them were thinking. If there is no fire under this mountain, what is heating the water?

The next day, Debbie Kelley and Jeff Karson climbed into Alvin, a small submarine with room for three. Alvin sank down through the dark water. Then its lights found the towers.

There were many of them. The tallest stood sixty metres high, as tall as a building of eighteen floors. No one had ever seen a vent chimney so tall. Clear water shimmered out of the white walls, the way air shimmers over a hot road in May.

When the water was tested, it was warm, not scalding. And it was alkaline, like soapy water, where the water of every vent they knew was acid.

The answer to the question was in the mountain itself. The Atlantis Massif is made of a green rock from deep in the Earth. When sea water seeps into that rock, the rock and the water react. The rock swells and cracks, so more water gets in. And the reaction gives out heat.

So there was no fire to find. The rock and the sea were warming the water between them, and they had been doing so for at least thirty thousand years. The same reaction made the water alkaline. It also filled the water with hydrogen gas.

Inside the white walls, the scientists later found living microbes. They were feeding on that hydrogen.

Eleven years earlier, a geologist called Michael Russell had written that warm, alkaline vents ought to exist on the sea floor. He had never seen one, and neither had anyone else. He had also suggested that life itself might have started inside one.

Now there was a real one to study. And the green rock is not rare. When the Earth was young, far more of it lay open on the sea floor. Wherever it met the sea, it could have built warm white towers like these, with no volcano needed.

The scientists named the place Lost City. It stands on the Atlantis Massif, it was found from the ship Atlantis, and in the old Greek story Atlantis is a city lost under the sea.

Debbie Kelley was careful afterwards. Nobody could say that life began in towers like these, she told people. But she thought such places had very likely kept early life going. They are warm and steady, and they last for thousands of years.

Gretchen Früh-Green went on studying Lost City for many years. She had thought she was looking at coral. She had found a kind of place that no one had known was on the Earth.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn in this story is the question 'what is heating the water?' The scientists expected a volcano, because every vent known until then had one. The answer was that the rock and the sea water heat each other by reacting. That reaction, called serpentinisation, also makes the water alkaline and fills it with hydrogen. This is why alkaline vents matter for the start of life. They need only sea water and a common deep rock, so they could have been widespread on the young Earth. The story also shows how science often moves: by people noticing something that should not be there.

- Ask: Why was nobody on the ship looking for vents on that mountain?
- Ask: Black smokers are heated by melted rock. What heats an alkaline vent?
- Ask: Michael Russell said such vents should exist before anyone saw one. What does that tell you about a good idea in science?
- Ask: The scientists found Lost City by accident. Was it luck, or something more than luck?
- Ask: Debbie Kelley would not say that life began in such towers. Why do you think she was so careful?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Salt Water Skin Test: can a soap skin form in sea-salty water? (30 min)
- **question:** Soap molecules line up by themselves into thin skins, which we see as foam. Do they make as much foam in water as salty as the sea as they do in plain water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which bottle will have the tallest foam after shaking: plain water, sea-salty water or very salty water? Or will all three be the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three bottles are the same size and hold the same amount of water at the same temperature. Each gets the same amount of the same soap water. The same person shakes each bottle 20 times in the same way. Only one thing changes: how much salt is in the water.
- **safety:** A grown-up grates the soap. Do not drink the test water. Soap water stings the eyes, so keep the caps on while shaking.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows, one for each round. For each bottle write the height of the foam in centimetres. Add one line on what the water looks like: clear, cloudy, or with white specks.
- **results table:** Round | Soap water added in total | Foam in plain water (cm) | Foam in sea-salty water (cm) | Foam in very salty water (cm) | What the water looks like
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the soap, the salt and the three bottles. 2. Explain that foam is made of thin soap skins. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Foam is thousands of thin soap skins. Which bottle will have the tallest foam: plain, sea-salty or very salty? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the three bottles. Guess which will have the tallest foam. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think salt will, or will not, make a difference? (good: Soap does not lather well in the sea.; Salt is just dissolved, so it should not matter.; The salt might get in the way of the soap.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect no difference, because salt and soap seem to have nothing to do with each other.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Grate about half a teaspoon of soap into the small bowl. 2. Add half a tumbler of warm water. 3. Stir for two minutes until most of the soap has dissolved.  
  *Parent says:* This is our soap water. Every bottle will get exactly the same amount of this, so the test is fair.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up grates the soap. Add warm water and stir for two minutes.
  - Note: The soap water will look cloudy, and a few bits may be left. That is fine. Stir again each time before you take a spoonful.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour one tumbler of drinking water into each bottle. 2. Add no salt to the first, one level teaspoon to the second and four level teaspoons to the third. 3. Cap and shake until the salt has dissolved. 4. Label the bottles.  
  *Parent says:* One level teaspoon of salt in a tumbler of water is about as salty as the sea. The third bottle is more than three times as salty as the sea.  
  *Child mode:* Put one tumbler of water in each bottle. Add no salt, one teaspoon, and four teaspoons. Shake until the salt is gone.
  - Note: Sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in every litre. That is 7 grams in a 200 millilitre tumbler, and a level teaspoon of table salt is about 6 grams. If your tap water is hard, such as borewell water, use drinking water or boiled and cooled water for all three bottles.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir the soap water. Add two teaspoons to each bottle and cap it. 2. Shake each bottle 20 times, the same way. 3. Stand the bottles for 30 seconds. 4. Measure the foam from the water line to the top of the foam, and look at the water.  
  *Parent says:* Shake each one 20 times, just as hard each time. Then we wait 30 seconds and measure the foam.  
  *Child mode:* Add two teaspoons of soap water to each bottle. Shake each 20 times. Wait, then measure the foam.
  - Ask: What do you notice in the salty bottles, apart from the foam? (good: The water is cloudier.; There are tiny white specks.; The bubbles are bigger and pop sooner.)
  - Note: Count the shakes aloud together. Measure only the foam, not the water. If the foam top is uneven, measure to the middle of the slope.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add two more teaspoons of soap water to each bottle, shake 20 times and measure. That is round 2. 2. Do the same once more for round 3. 3. Write every height in the table.  
  *Parent says:* One round can be a fluke. Let's add more soap water and see whether the bottles stay in the same order.  
  *Child mode:* Add two more teaspoons to each bottle. Shake 20 times and measure. Then do it once more.
  - Note: With a true soap bar, the plain bottle should have clearly the tallest foam in every round. The very salty bottle should have little or none, with cloudy water or white specks. If all three foam alike, your bar is probably a detergent bar. Try a plain washing or bath soap, or add two more teaspoons of salt to the third bottle.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why. 2. Have them write the claim, the evidence and the reasoning. 3. Only then read the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your claim. What did you see that supports it? Why do you think the salt made that difference?  
  *Child mode:* Write your claim. Write what you saw. Write why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Where did the soap go in the very salty bottle? (good: It came out of the water as white specks.; The salt pushed it out, so it could not make skins.)
  - Ask: What does this say about fatty skins forming in the sea? (good: Simple fatty skins may form badly in sea water.; It is a problem for the vent idea, unless a mixture works better.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain. A soap molecule has a head that mixes with water and a tail that stays away from water. In water the molecules line up into thin skins, and nobody has to arrange them. The particles of dissolved salt push soap molecules out of the water into solid specks. Fewer are left to make skins, so there is less foam. Fatty acids, which may have made the first cell skins, behave like soap. This is a model. A bubble wall is not a cell membrane, but both form because of the same head and tail.
  - Check: Soap makes less foam in very salty water. What is the best explanation? → **Salt pushes soap out of the water**
### e2 · Hot Vent in a Jar: where does hot water go in cold water? (20 min)
- **question:** A small bottle of hot coloured water is lowered into a jar of cold water. Does the hot water stay in the bottle, sink, spread sideways or rise? Does cold coloured water do the same?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. What will the hot coloured water do when the bottle reaches the bottom? What will the cold coloured water do? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both small bottles are the same size, filled to the brim, with the same amount of colour. Both are lowered gently into the same jar of fresh cold water. Only one thing changes: the water in one small bottle is hot and in the other it is cold.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water and lowers the hot bottle. Use water that is hot but not boiling, so the small bottle does not crack.
- **record:** Draw the jar twice, once for the hot bottle and once for the cold bottle. Show with coloured pencil where the colour went. Write the time the colour took to reach the top.
- **results table:** Small bottle | What the colour did in the first 10 seconds | Seconds for the colour to reach the top | Where the colour was after 2 minutes
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the big jar with cold tap water and leave it to become still. 2. Tie a string round the neck of each small bottle. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* One small bottle will hold hot red water and one cold red water. What will each do at the bottom of this jar? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the big jar with cold water. Guess where the hot colour will go. Say why.
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Some children expect the colour to spread out evenly in all directions.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put three drops of colour in a small bottle and fill it to the brim with hot water. 2. Hold the string and lower the bottle slowly, upright, to the bottom. 3. Your child starts the timer and watches from the side.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the mouth of the bottle. Tell me what the colour does, and call out when it reaches the top.  
  *Child mode:* Watch from the side. Where does the colour go? Time how long it takes to reach the top.
  - Note: Lower the bottle slowly so the water in the jar stays still. A thin coloured stream should rise from the mouth within seconds and spread under the surface.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty the big jar and refill it with fresh cold water. Let it settle. 2. Fill the second small bottle with cold coloured water. 3. Lower it in the same way and watch for two minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same test with cold coloured water. Watch the mouth of the bottle again.  
  *Child mode:* Now lower the cold coloured bottle. Watch for two minutes. What is different?
  - Note: The cold colour should mostly stay in the bottle. A little may spill out and lie on the bottom. This second bottle shows that it is the heat, not the colour, that makes the water rise.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning. 2. Have them draw both jars. 3. Then explain, using the note below.  
  *Parent says:* What is your claim? What did you see? Why do you think hot water went up and cold water did not?  
  *Child mode:* Say your claim. Say what you saw. Draw both jars with the colour.
  - Ask: At a real vent, what would the rising hot water carry up with it? (good: Minerals dissolved from the rock.; Gases such as hydrogen.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain. When water is heated, its molecules move faster and take up a little more room. So a cup of hot water weighs slightly less than a cup of cold water. The lighter hot water is pushed up by the heavier cold water around it. At a vent, water heated in the rock rises the same way. It carries dissolved minerals up, and they turn solid in the cold sea and build the chimney.
  - Check: Why does hot water rise through cold water? → **Hot water is lighter for its size**

   **Answer:** All cells today share one ancestor → Known; RNA can act as a catalyst → Known; Fatty molecules form skins by themselves → Known; Life began inside alkaline vents → Still an idea; RNA came before DNA and proteins → Still an idea; LUCA lived 4,200 million years ago → Still an idea. *Why:* The first three have strong evidence that almost all scientists accept. The last three are hypotheses that are still being tested.
11. (perspective) Two children argue about looking for life on Enceladus, an ocean moon with no land.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that the answer depends on where life began on Earth. If life needs drying pools, an ocean moon is a poor place. If life began at vents, the moon is a good place. So finding or not finding life there would be evidence about our own beginning.. *Why:* This shows how a question about another world tests an idea about this one.
12. (draw) Draw Earth's history on a clock face of 12 hours. Mark the first animals and the first humans.  
   **Answer:** Twelve hours stand for 4,500 million years, so one hour is 375 million years. The first animals big enough to see, 570 million years ago, come about one and a half hours before the end, near half past ten. Humans like us come in about the last 3 seconds.. *Why:* 570 ÷ 375 is about 1.5 hours. For humans, 0.3 ÷ 4,500 of 12 hours is about 3 seconds.
13. (truefalse) True or false: a few copying mistakes are needed for new kinds of RNA chain to appear.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. With no mistakes, every copy is the same as its parent, so nothing new can appear. Too many mistakes lose the message. A small number allows change.
14. (odd) Three of these are problems for the pool idea. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Little dry land on the young Earth / Harsh ultraviolet light / Pools do not last long / Water breaks RNA chains apart  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Water breaking chains is a problem for vents, which never dry. The other three are problems for pools on land.
15. (write) Think of one test you could do at home or in a laboratory to learn more about how life began. Describe it.  
   **Answer:** Answers will differ. A strong answer names what is changed, what is measured and what is kept the same. An example is testing whether soap skins form better in warm or cold salty water.. *Why:* Designing a fair test is harder than answering a question. Look for one thing changed and one thing measured.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What did a first cell need, as far as scientists can tell?  
   Options: A skin, copied instructions and energy / Sunlight, leaves and roots / A heart, lungs and a brain / Only water  
   **Answer:** A skin, copied instructions and energy. *Why:* Every cell has a membrane, instructions that are copied, and a steady energy supply. Water is needed too, but water alone is not enough.
2. (mcq) What warms the water of an alkaline vent?  
   Options: Sea water reacting with rock / Melted rock from a volcano / Sunlight / Animals living in the chimney  
   **Answer:** Sea water reacting with rock. *Why:* Sea water reacts with olivine rock, and the reaction gives out heat. Melted rock is what heats a black smoker, so that choice mixes up the two kinds of vent.
3. (mcq) Who makes the food at a deep-sea vent?  
   Options: Microbes, using energy from chemicals / Seaweed, using sunlight / Fish, by hunting / Nobody, the animals do not eat  
   **Answer:** Microbes, using energy from chemicals. *Why:* Bacteria and archaea make sugar using energy from chemicals such as hydrogen sulfide. No light reaches the vent, so seaweed is not found there.
4. (mcq) What can RNA do that makes the RNA world idea possible?  
   Options: Carry instructions and act as a catalyst / Make its own building blocks from air / Last for ever in hot water / Live without any energy  
   **Answer:** Carry instructions and act as a catalyst. *Why:* RNA does both jobs that DNA and proteins share between them today. RNA does not last well in hot water, which is one of the idea's problems.
5. (truefalse) True or false: the RNA world idea has been proved.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. There is good evidence, such as ribozymes and the RNA core of the ribosome. But nobody has made RNA that copies all of itself, and other scientists think simple reactions came first.
6. (mcq) In very salty water, soap makes little foam. What does this suggest about the first cell skins?  
   Options: Simple fatty skins may form badly in sea water / Cells are made of soap / Salt is alive / The sea has no fatty molecules  
   **Answer:** Simple fatty skins may form badly in sea water. *Why:* Fatty acids behave like soap, and salt pushes them out of the water. This is an argument for fresh water pools. A later test showed that a mixture of fatty molecules can form skins in salty water.
7. (mcq) Why are three bottles of the same size, shaken 20 times each, used in the foam test?  
   Options: So that only the salt differs / So that the foam looks nicer / Because three is a lucky number / So that the soap dissolves faster  
   **Answer:** So that only the salt differs. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. Then any difference in the foam must come from the salt.
8. (mcq) A string of 100 centimetres stands for 4,500 million years. What is 1 centimetre?  
   Options: 45 million years / 100 million years / 4,500 million years / 4.5 million years  
   **Answer:** 45 million years. *Why:* 4,500 ÷ 100 = 45. Every centimetre stands for 45 million years.
9. (mcq) On a model of Earth's history, for how much of the time was all life single cells?  
   Options: Most of it / About half of it / Only a small part / None of it  
   **Answer:** Most of it. *Why:* Life is at least 3,500 million years old, and animals big enough to see appeared about 570 million years ago. So for more than four fifths of life's history there were only single cells.
10. (mcq) How do scientists work out what LUCA was like, with no fossil of LUCA?  
   Options: From features that all living cells share / From drawings made long ago / By guessing / From dinosaur bones  
   **Answer:** From features that all living cells share. *Why:* A feature shared by every branch of life was most likely inherited from the shared ancestor. So what all cells share tells us what LUCA had.
11. (truefalse) True or false: scientists agree that life began at vents.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Vents and pools on land are both leading ideas, with evidence for and against each. Nobody knows yet.
12. (mcq) Which of these is known, and not just an idea?  
   Options: Fatty molecules form skins by themselves / Life began in a warm pool / RNA came before proteins / LUCA lived at a vent  
   **Answer:** Fatty molecules form skins by themselves. *Why:* Anyone can watch fatty molecules form skins, and it has been seen under microscopes many times. The other three are hypotheses that are still being tested.

## Applications
- Space missions look for life on ocean moons such as Enceladus and Europa. Scientists choose where to look partly from ideas about where life began on Earth.
- India's Deep Ocean Mission explores vents in the Indian Ocean. Vent chimneys hold metals, and the life around them is found nowhere else.
- Microbes from hot springs make enzymes that still work when hot. One such enzyme is used all over the world to copy DNA in medical tests.
- Some medicines and vaccines are made of RNA wrapped in tiny fatty bubbles. The bubbles form by themselves, as the first skins did.
- Chemists design detergents so that salt and hard water do not push them out of the water. That is why dish liquid foams where bath soap fails.
- Geologists, map makers and model builders all use scale. One unit on the model always stands for the same amount of the real thing.

## Spotted around us
- Bath soap makes very little lather in sea water. Try it with a cup of sea water at a beach.
- Borewell water in many Indian towns is hard. Soap forms a white scum in it and lathers poorly, for a reason close to the salt effect.
- At salt pans, sea water dries in the sun. The salt is left crowded together as crystals.
- A hot spring, where water heated inside the rock comes up, as at a vent.
- When water heats in a steel pot, bits of dal or tea leaves rise in the middle and sink at the sides. Hot water rising is what you saw in the jar.
- A spoon of curd holds millions of bacteria. Each has a fatty membrane, ribosomes and the same genetic code as you, inherited from LUCA.
- Turmeric stains on a white shirt turn red when you wash them with soap. The soap is alkaline, like the water of an alkaline vent.

## Open questions
- **How did the first cell begin?** Scientists have made building blocks, RNA that does some jobs, and fatty bubbles that grow and split. Nobody has joined them into something alive.
- **Where did life begin: at vents in the deep sea, in pools on land, or somewhere else?** There is real evidence for both, and real problems with both. Almost no rock from that time is left to check.
- **Did RNA come first, or did simple reactions come before any instructions?** RNA can do two jobs, but it is fragile and hard to make. Nobody has yet shown RNA copying all of itself without help.
- **How did a skin, a chain of instructions and an energy supply come to work together?** Each has been studied alone. How the three first joined into one cell that could have young is the least understood step.
- **Did life begin only once on Earth?** All life today descends from one ancestor. Other beginnings may have happened and died out, leaving no trace we can find.
- **Is there life on ocean moons such as Enceladus and Europa?** They have liquid water and, probably, vents. No mission has yet been able to test their water for living cells.

## Connected maths
- A string of 125 centimetres stands for 4,500 million years. How many million years is 1 centimetre? Oxygen began to build up in the air 2,400 million years ago. How far from the Today end does that label go? **1 centimetre is 36 million years. The oxygen label goes about 67 centimetres from the Today end.**. 4,500 ÷ 125 = 36, so each centimetre stands for 36 million years. Then 2,400 ÷ 36 is 66 and two thirds, which is about 67 centimetres. That is just past the middle of the string. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- The Earth is about 4,500 million years old. Write that number in digits. Humans like us have lived for about 300 thousand years. How many times older is the Earth than our kind of human? **4,500,000,000. The Earth is about 15,000 times older than our kind of human.**. 4,500 million is 4,500 followed by six zeros: 4,500,000,000. 300 thousand is 300,000. Cancel five zeros from both: 45,000 ÷ 3 = 15,000. So if all of Earth's time were 15,000 days, which is about 41 years, humans would have arrived on the last day. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- In Copy or Crash, Lakshmi takes 36 turns with a 4-letter chain. Her copy is perfect 17 times. Then she takes 36 turns with a 12-letter chain. Her copy is perfect 4 times. How many points does each chain earn? Which length should she choose? **The 4-letter chain earns 68 points and the 12-letter chain earns 48 points. She should choose the 4-letter chain.**. 17 × 4 = 68 points. 4 × 12 = 48 points. The long chain wins three times as much each time, but it wins far less than one third as often. So over many turns the short chain earns more. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- Look at the table of foam heights. In each round, what fraction of the plain water foam did the sea-salty bottle make? In which round did the sea-salty bottle do best, compared with plain water? **One third in round 1, one third in round 2 and one half in round 3. The sea-salty bottle did best in round 3.**. Round 1: 1 out of 3 is one third. Round 2: 2 out of 6 is one third. Round 3: 4 out of 8 is one half. With more soap added, the salt could not push all of the extra soap out of the water. So the sea-salty bottle began to catch up. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in 1 litre. How much salt is that in a 200 millilitre tumbler? Your very salty bottle had 24 grams of salt in one tumbler. About how many times as salty as the sea was it? **7 grams in one tumbler. The very salty bottle was a little more than 3 times as salty as the sea.**. 200 millilitres is one fifth of a litre, and 35 ÷ 5 = 7 grams. Four level teaspoons of salt are about 24 grams. 24 ÷ 7 is a little more than 3. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L5`)
- Squeeze all 4,500 million years of Earth's history into one day of 24 hours. How many million years is one hour? The big dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago. About how many minutes before midnight is that? **One hour is 187.5 million years. The dinosaurs died out about 21 minutes before midnight.**. 4,500 ÷ 24 = 187.5 million years in each hour. One minute is 187.5 ÷ 60, which is about 3.1 million years. 66 ÷ 3.1 is about 21 minutes. So on this clock the dinosaurs died out at about 11:39 at night. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
