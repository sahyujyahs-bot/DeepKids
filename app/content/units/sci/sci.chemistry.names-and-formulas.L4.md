# Chemical formulas: how H₂O, CO₂ and NaCl say which atoms and how many
`sci.chemistry.names-and-formulas.L4`

**Summary.** Salt is called uppu in Chennai, namak in Delhi and nun in Kolkata. A chemist in any of these cities writes it as NaCl. A chemical formula uses letters to say which atoms are in a substance. It uses small numbers to say how many. Water is H₂O: two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom. People chose the letters, but they had to measure to find the numbers. Your child counts drops of iodine to see that substances react in fixed amounts. They also build water, carbon dioxide and a grain of salt from balls of dough.

**Intro.** *The symbol for hydrogen is H, and the symbol for oxygen is O. So why is the symbol for sodium Na? The word sodium has neither an N nor an A in it.* Chemists write every substance with a short code called a formula. Water is H₂O. Carbon dioxide, the gas you breathe out, is CO₂. Table salt is NaCl. The letters stand for kinds of atoms, and some of them come from old Latin names. Sodium was called natrium, so its symbol is Na. The small numbers say how many atoms there are. In this unit you collect the many names of one substance. You count drops of iodine to find out why the numbers are fixed. You also build molecules from dough and read the formulas of things in your kitchen.

## Terms
- **Atom**: One of the tiny particles that every substance is made of. Atoms are far too small to see. *e.g.* A single drop of water holds more than a thousand million million million atoms.
- **Element**: A substance made of only one kind of atom. 118 elements are known, and about 90 of them are found in nature. *e.g.* Gold is an element. Every atom in a pure gold ring is a gold atom.
- **Chemical symbol**: One or two letters that stand for an element. The first letter is a capital, and the second letter is small. *e.g.* O stands for oxygen, C stands for carbon and Na stands for sodium.
- **Chemical formula**: A short code for a substance. Its symbols say which atoms are in the substance, and its small numbers say how many. *e.g.* The formula H₂O says that water has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom.
- **Subscript**: The small number written low after a symbol in a formula. It says how many atoms of that element there are. No number means one atom. *e.g.* In CO₂ the small 2 belongs to the O. There are two oxygen atoms and one carbon atom.
- **Molecule**: A group of atoms joined together. A molecule is the smallest piece of many substances. *e.g.* One molecule of water is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.
- **Compound**: A substance made of two or more elements joined in fixed numbers. *e.g.* Water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen. Salt is a compound of sodium and chlorine.
- **Hydrogen (H)**: The lightest element. As a gas its atoms go about in pairs, written H₂. *e.g.* Every water molecule holds two hydrogen atoms.
- **Oxygen (O)**: The element in air that humans and other animals need to breathe. In air its atoms go about in pairs, written O₂. *e.g.* About one fifth of the air in your room is oxygen gas.
- **Carbon (C)**: The element in charcoal, pencil lead and diamond. Carbon atoms are in every living body. *e.g.* The black tip of a burnt matchstick is mostly carbon.
- **Sodium (Na)**: A soft, silvery metal that reacts fiercely with water. Its symbol comes from its old Latin name, natrium. *e.g.* Sodium is never found alone in a kitchen. It is joined to chlorine in salt.
- **Chlorine (Cl)**: A yellow-green, poisonous gas when it is alone. Joined to sodium, it makes table salt. *e.g.* A tiny amount of chlorine is added to city tap water to kill microbes.
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**: A gas with one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms in each molecule. You breathe it out, and plants take it in. *e.g.* The bubbles in a bottle of soda are carbon dioxide.
- **Sodium chloride (NaCl)**: The chemical name of table salt. It has one sodium atom for every chlorine atom. *e.g.* The salt in the sea, in rock salt and in a pickle jar is all sodium chloride.
- **Ratio**: A way to say how many of one thing there are for each of another. *e.g.* In water the ratio is two hydrogen atoms to one oxygen atom. In salt it is one to one.
- **Chemical name**: A name that chemists everywhere have agreed on. It says which elements are in the substance. *e.g.* Baking soda, cooking soda and meetha soda are everyday names. Sodium hydrogen carbonate is the chemical name.
- **Crystal**: A solid whose atoms are packed in a pattern that repeats in every direction. *e.g.* A grain of salt is a crystal. Its sodium and chlorine atoms sit in rows, one after the other.
- **Isomers**: Different substances that have the same formula. The same atoms are joined in a different arrangement. *e.g.* Glucose and fructose are two sugars. Both have the formula C₆H₁₂O₆.

## Explanations
### A symbol is one or two letters for one element · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical symbol is one or two letters that stand for an element. The first letter is always a capital, and a second letter is always small.

An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. Each element has its own symbol. Many symbols are the first letter of the English name: H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon. There are 118 elements and only 26 letters, so most symbols need a second letter. Chlorine is Cl and calcium is Ca. Some symbols come from old Latin names. Sodium was natrium, so it is Na. Iron was ferrum, so it is Fe. Gold was aurum, so it is Au. The rule about capitals matters. Co is one atom of the metal cobalt. CO is two atoms, carbon and oxygen, joined as a poisonous gas. The symbols are the same in every country and every language.
- e.g. K is potassium, from the Latin name kalium. The K on a fertiliser bag marked NPK is potassium.
- e.g. Ag is silver, from argentum. Cu is copper, from cuprum.
- e.g. No element's symbol uses the letter J or the letter Q.
### A formula says which atoms and how many · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical formula is a short code for a substance. The symbols say which kinds of atoms are in it. A small number after a symbol says how many of that atom there are.

Read a formula from left to right, one symbol at a time. A small number, called a subscript, belongs to the symbol just before it. If a symbol has no number, there is one atom. H₂O has two symbols, H and O. The small 2 comes after the H, so there are two hydrogen atoms. The O has no number, so there is one oxygen atom. One water molecule has three atoms in all. CO₂ has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms. A formula is the same wherever the substance comes from. Water from the Kaveri, from a cloud and from your tap is H₂O.
- e.g. H₂O is water: 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
- e.g. CO₂ is carbon dioxide: 1 carbon atom and 2 oxygen atoms.
- e.g. CH₄ is methane, the main gas in CNG: 1 carbon atom and 4 hydrogen atoms.
### Elements have formulas too · *defined*
**Definition:** An element's formula shows how its atoms go about. Some go about singly, and some go about in pairs or larger groups.

Oxygen gas is written O₂, because its atoms go about in pairs. The nitrogen in air is N₂, and hydrogen gas is H₂. Their molecules hold two atoms of the same kind. So a molecule does not have to be a compound. A metal such as gold has no molecules. Its atoms are packed together in one big crystal, so its formula is just Au. The air in your room is mostly two formulas. About four fifths of it is N₂, and about one fifth is O₂. Carbon dioxide is only about 4 molecules in every 10,000.
- e.g. O₂ is the oxygen you breathe: 2 oxygen atoms.
- e.g. N₂ is nitrogen gas: 2 nitrogen atoms.
- e.g. Fe is iron and Au is gold. A metal is written with its symbol alone.
### One atom more makes a different substance · *defined*
**Definition:** Two substances can be made of the same elements and still be different. If the numbers in their formulas differ, they are different substances.

Water is H₂O. Hydrogen peroxide is H₂O₂. It has one more oxygen atom in each molecule. Water is safe to drink. Hydrogen peroxide bleaches hair and kills microbes on a cut. Carbon dioxide is CO₂, and you breathe it out all day. Carbon monoxide is CO, with one oxygen atom fewer. It forms when fuel burns without enough air, and it is a deadly poison. Oxygen gas is O₂. Ozone is O₃, and it has a sharp smell and harms the lungs. So every number in a formula matters. Changing one small number gives a different substance with different behaviour.
- e.g. H₂O is water. H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide.
- e.g. CO₂ is carbon dioxide. CO is carbon monoxide.
- e.g. O₂ is the oxygen in air. O₃ is ozone, which blocks harmful rays high above the Earth.
### The formula of salt gives a ratio · *defined*
**Definition:** Salt has no separate molecules. Its formula, NaCl, says that there is one sodium atom for every chlorine atom.

A grain of salt is a crystal. Its sodium and chlorine atoms are packed in rows that alternate, like the squares of a chessboard, in every direction. Each sodium atom has chlorine atoms on all six sides. No sodium atom belongs to one chlorine atom more than to the others. So there is no small piece that you could call one molecule of salt. The formula gives the ratio. For every sodium atom in the grain there is one chlorine atom. One grain holds about a million million million atoms. The atoms in salt carry electric charges, and chemists call such charged atoms ions. You will meet ions in a later unit.
- e.g. NaCl is table salt: 1 sodium for every 1 chlorine.
- e.g. CaCO₃ is the substance of chalk, marble and eggshell: 1 calcium and 1 carbon for every 3 oxygen.
- e.g. SiO₂ is quartz, the main part of most sand: 1 silicon for every 2 oxygen.
### Why the numbers in a formula are fixed · *defined*
**Definition:** Atoms join and react in fixed whole numbers. So a pure compound always has the same proportions, wherever it comes from.

An atom cannot join with half of another atom. Atoms join one to one, one to two, two to three, and so on. In the experiment Count the Drops, you see what follows from this. Lemon juice holds vitamin C. One molecule of vitamin C changes exactly one molecule of iodine and takes away its colour. So twice as much lemon juice uses up about twice as many drops of iodine. In 1799 the French chemist Joseph Proust showed the same thing by weighing. A copper compound from a mine and the same compound made in his laboratory had the same proportions. Fixed proportions were the first strong clue that substances are made of atoms.
- e.g. Water always has 2 hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom.
- e.g. Twice the lemon juice uses up about twice the iodine.
- e.g. A cook can change a recipe. A compound has one recipe only.
### Everyday names, chemical names and formulas · *defined*
**Definition:** An everyday name is what people call a substance at home. A chemical name says which elements are in it. A formula also says how many atoms of each.

One substance can have hundreds of everyday names, one in each language. Salt is uppu, namak, nun and salt. An everyday name tells you nothing about what the substance is made of. A chemical name does. Sodium chloride holds sodium and chlorine. When a metal joins a non-metal, the metal is named first and the other name ends in ide. Small words inside a name count atoms. Mono means one, di means two and tri means three. So carbon dioxide has two oxygen atoms and carbon monoxide has one. In 1787 four French chemists, among them Antoine Lavoisier, proposed naming substances by what they are made of. Since 1919 a world body of chemists called IUPAC has agreed the rules.
- e.g. Everyday name: baking soda. Chemical name: sodium hydrogen carbonate. Formula: NaHCO₃.
- e.g. Everyday name: dry ice. Chemical name: solid carbon dioxide. Formula: CO₂.
- e.g. Everyday name: chalk. Chemical name: calcium carbonate. Formula: CaCO₃.
### A formula shows what a substance can never give · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical change rearranges atoms. It cannot make a new kind of atom. So a substance heated alone can give off only what its own atoms can build.

Salt and baking soda are both dry white powders. Salt is NaCl. Baking soda is NaHCO₃, so it holds hydrogen, carbon and oxygen atoms as well as sodium. Water is H₂O and carbon dioxide is CO₂. Baking soda has the atoms to build both. Salt has the atoms for neither. In the experiment Water from a Dry Powder, a grown-up heats each powder. Baking soda gives off water, which wets a cold steel plate. Salt gives off none. No water was hiding in the baking soda. Heat rearranged its atoms into three new substances: water, carbon dioxide and washing soda.
- e.g. Heated baking soda gives off water and carbon dioxide.
- e.g. Heated salt gives off neither, because it has no hydrogen, carbon or oxygen atoms.
- e.g. No amount of heating turns iron atoms into gold atoms.
### What a formula leaves out · *defined*
**Definition:** A formula says which atoms and how many. It does not say how the atoms are arranged.

Glucose is the sugar in your blood. Fructose is a sugar in fruit and honey. Both have the formula C₆H₁₂O₆. Each molecule has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms. But the atoms are joined in a different arrangement, so the two sugars are different substances. Fructose tastes sweeter. Substances like these are called isomers. To know a substance fully, chemists must also find its arrangement. The British chemist Dorothy Hodgkin did this with X-rays. She worked out the arrangement of the atoms in penicillin in 1945 and in vitamin B12 in the 1950s. Vitamin B12 has 181 atoms in each molecule.
- e.g. Glucose and fructose are both C₆H₁₂O₆, and they are different sugars.
- e.g. Diamond and pencil lead are both written C, and they are arranged differently.
- e.g. The formula of vitamin B12 is C₆₃H₈₈CoN₁₄O₁₄P. It took years of work to find how those atoms are joined.
### Formulas in living bodies, on the Earth and in space · *defined*
**Definition:** The same symbols can write every substance yet found, in a living body, in a rock or between the stars.

About 99 of every 100 atoms in your body are of just four kinds: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon and nitrogen. A plant takes in CO₂ and H₂O and builds glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆. You eat the glucose and breathe out CO₂ and H₂O again. Astronomers read formulas from far away, because each substance takes in its own colours of light. The air of Venus and of Mars is mostly CO₂. In 2009 an instrument on India's Chandrayaan-1 spacecraft found signs of water on the Moon. In 1969 radio telescopes found water molecules in clouds between the stars. In 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope found CO₂ in the air of a planet that orbits another star.
- e.g. Photosynthesis in words: carbon dioxide and water become glucose and oxygen.
- e.g. The air of Mars is about 95 parts CO₂ in every 100.
- e.g. Water in a comet has the same formula as water in a coconut: H₂O.
### Is a formula a fact about nature, or a tool that people made? · *debated*
**Definition:** Chemists agree on how to write and use formulas. People who study science disagree about what a formula really is.

A formula has two parts. The letters were chosen by people, and they could have been different. The numbers were found by measuring, and nobody could choose them. So is the whole formula a fact, or a tool?
- e.g. H was chosen for hydrogen. In a different history it might have been a picture or another letter.
- e.g. The 2 in H₂O was measured. Chemists who wrote HO had to give it up.
- **Most working chemists:** A formula is a fact about nature, written in agreed symbols. Water really has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom. Any careful person who measures will find the same.
- **Ernst Mach and Wilhelm Ostwald, two scientists of about 1900:** A formula is a tool for keeping track of measurements. It should not be taken as a picture of hidden things. Ostwald changed his mind a few years later, after experiments made the case for atoms much stronger.
- **A third view:** Both are partly right. A formula is a human way of writing, and what it writes down is real. It also leaves out a great deal, such as the arrangement of the atoms.

## Mostly agreed
- Every substance is made of atoms, and a formula lists which kinds and how many. *How we know:* Chemists can take a compound apart, weigh each element in it, and build the same compound again from those elements.
- Water has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom. *How we know:* In 1805 Gay-Lussac and Humboldt measured the gases that join to make water. Hydrogen took up twice as much space as oxygen.
- A pure compound has the same proportions wherever it comes from. *How we know:* In 1799 Joseph Proust compared a copper compound from a mine with the same compound made in his laboratory. The proportions were the same.
- Salt is a crystal of sodium and chlorine atoms in equal numbers. It has no separate molecules. *How we know:* In 1913 William Lawrence Bragg used X-rays to find where the atoms sit in a salt crystal. Each sodium atom has six chlorine atoms around it.
- Two different substances can have the same formula. *How we know:* In the 1820s Friedrich Wöhler and Justus von Liebig each analysed a silver compound. One was harmless and one exploded, yet both had the same atoms in the same numbers.
- Chemists in every country use the same symbols and the same rules for names. *How we know:* Since 1919 a world body of chemists, called IUPAC, has agreed the symbols and names. It also approves the name of each new element.

## How we found out
**John Dalton in England, Gay-Lussac and Humboldt in France, Avogadro and Cannizzaro in Italy, and Jöns Jacob Berzelius in Sweden, Gay-Lussac and Humboldt measured the gases in 1805. Dalton's circles were printed in 1808. Avogadro wrote in 1811, and Berzelius proposed letters in 1813. The meeting in Karlsruhe was in 1860..** Two hundred years ago, chemists had no shared way to write a substance. Alchemists had drawn secret pictures, and each country used its own names. In 1808 John Dalton, a teacher in Manchester, drew each kind of atom as a circle with a mark inside. He also guessed how atoms join. He thought nature would choose the simplest way. So he wrote water as one hydrogen atom joined to one oxygen atom. Measurements did not agree. In 1805 Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac and Alexander von Humboldt had measured the gases that form water. Two measures of hydrogen joined with one of oxygen. In 1811 Amedeo Avogadro explained what this meant. Water must hold two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom. Few chemists listened to him. In 1813 the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius proposed letters in place of circles. A number showed how many atoms there were. Dalton disliked the letters all his life. For fifty years some chemists wrote water as HO and others as H₂O. In 1860, at a meeting in Karlsruhe, Stanislao Cannizzaro argued that Avogadro had been right. In the years after, chemists came to agree on H₂O.  
*What people believed before:* That water is HO, one hydrogen atom joined to one oxygen atom. Dalton chose this because it was the simplest. The measured gases, two measures of hydrogen to one of oxygen, showed that water is H₂O.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Water is HO: one hydrogen atom joined to one oxygen atom.** (John Dalton, in 1808.; *rejected*)  
  For: It is the simplest way that two kinds of atoms could join. Dalton had no way to count atoms directly.  
  Against: When water is split, the hydrogen gas takes up twice the space of the oxygen gas. Avogadro's idea, accepted after 1860, explains this only if water is H₂O.
- **Salt is made of tiny NaCl molecules.** (A common belief, and a fair first reading of the formula.; *rejected*)  
  For: Water is made of H₂O molecules, so it seems that salt should be made of NaCl molecules.  
  Against: X-rays show that salt is one large pattern of sodium and chlorine atoms. Each sodium has six chlorine neighbours and belongs to none of them more than the others. The formula gives the ratio.
- **The proportions in a compound can vary, as they do in a mixture.** (Claude Louis Berthollet, a French chemist, in about 1803.; *minority*)  
  For: Mixtures such as salt water and alloys such as brass can be made in many proportions. In the 1900s chemists found a few solids whose proportions do vary slightly. One is an iron oxide called wüstite.  
  Against: Proust showed that pure compounds such as water and salt always have the same proportions. This is true of nearly all compounds.
- **A chemical is something made in a factory. Natural things are chemical-free.** (A common belief, and many advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many harmful substances are made in factories, so people use the word chemical to mean something harmful.  
  Against: Everything made of atoms is a chemical. Water is H₂O, salt is NaCl and the sugar in a mango is C₆H₁₂O₆. Whether a substance harms you depends on which substance it is and how much you take.
- **Vitamin C from amla is a different substance from vitamin C made in a factory.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Amla also brings fibre, water and many other substances that a tablet does not have.  
  Against: The vitamin C molecule in both is C₆H₈O₆, with the same arrangement of atoms. The body cannot tell the two apart. Only the other substances that come along with it differ.
- **If you know the formula, you know the substance.** (A common belief among people who have just learned formulas.; *rejected*)  
  For: A formula does tell you a lot: which atoms, how many, and what the substance can never give off.  
  Against: Glucose and fructose share the formula C₆H₁₂O₆ and are different sugars. Diamond and pencil lead are both C. The arrangement of the atoms matters as much as the count.
- **Pictures would make better symbols than letters.** (John Dalton, who kept his circles and disliked the letters of Berzelius.; *debated*)  
  For: A picture can show how the atoms sit beside each other, which a row of letters does not show.  
  Against: Letters can be written and printed quickly by anyone. Chemists chose letters for everyday work. They do use pictures of molecules when the arrangement matters, so Dalton was partly right.

## Roots
In this unit you read formulas such as H₂O and NaCl. You also counted drops to see why their numbers are fixed. Here we ask which part of a formula comes from people and which part comes from the substance.

**Deep truth.** In Count the Drops, twice as much lemon juice cleared about twice as many drops of iodine. Nobody chose that number, and you found it by counting. People chose the letters H and O for water, but they had to measure to find the 2. So the letters in a formula are an agreement, and the small numbers are discoveries.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat a formula as one thing, either as a fact of nature or as a label. It is two things written together. The symbols could have been different. The numbers could not.  
*Evidence:* In Count the Drops the counts rose in equal steps, whatever anyone guessed beforehand. Dalton wrote water as HO, and measurements of the two gases made chemists change it to H₂O. The letters were proposed by Berzelius in 1813, in place of Dalton's circles.

- *who am i:* About 99 of every 100 atoms in your body are hydrogen, oxygen, carbon or nitrogen. Your body is written with the same few symbols as the sea and the air.
- *what is life:* A mango tree builds sugar from CO₂ and H₂O. You eat the sugar and breathe out CO₂ and H₂O. The same atoms pass from one living being to the next.
- *what is this world:* People call water by thousands of names. The water has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom, whatever anyone calls it.

People have given salt hundreds of names, and each name belongs to one language. The formula NaCl belongs to no language, and a chemist anywhere can read it. With 118 symbols, people can write every substance yet found: a rock, a river, the air, a mango and a child. Many sages taught that the many things of the world come from one source. The science here says something close and plain. All the substances we know are built from about ninety kinds of atoms, joined in different numbers.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that by knowing one lump of clay, everything made of clay is known. A pot and a lamp differ only in name, and the truth is that both are clay. (Uppu, namak and nun differ only in name. What stays the same is sodium and chlorine, one for one. The Upanishad goes further than chemistry does. It says that all things are finally one, which no experiment in this unit tests.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 1:** The name that can be spoken is not the lasting name. (No word for water is water. A formula is also a kind of name. H₂O is not wet, and it leaves out how the atoms are arranged. Lao Tzu was speaking of the Tao, the source of all things, and not of chemistry.)
- **Confucius, Analects 13.3:** If names are not right, what people say will not match how things are, and their work will not succeed. (This is the reason chemists agreed on names and formulas. Two powders both called soda can be mixed up. NaHCO₃ and Na₂CO₃ cannot.)
- Science (settled): Water has been found on the Moon, in comets and in clouds between the stars. It has the same formula as water on the Earth.
- Science (debated): People who study science still argue about what a formula is. Some call it a fact about nature, and others call it a human tool.
- Other view, Ernst Mach, a physicist of about 1900, and people who think as he did: A formula is a tool that people made, numbers and all. It sums up measurements in a handy way. On this view we should not say that the 2 in H₂O belongs to the water. We should say only that our measurements come out in that proportion.
- Practice, A sip without a name (3 min): Hold a tumbler of water in both hands. Say every name for it that you know, slowly. Thanneer. Paani. Jol. Water. H₂O. Now take one sip and say nothing, even inside your head. Feel the water in your mouth. Swallow. Sit quietly for five breaths. Was anything missing when the names were gone?
- Ask: People chose the letter H. Could they have chosen a different number in H₂O? What would have stopped them?
- Ask: If everyone forgot the word water, would anything about water change?
- Ask: Salt has a formula. Does a river have one? Do you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* People chose the letter H for hydrogen. Could they also have chosen the number in H₂O? (hint: Think about Count the Drops. Could you have chosen how many drops the lemon juice used up?)
- *deep:* Water has thousands of names and one formula. Is any of them its real name? (hint: Was water any different before people had a word for it?)
- *deep:* One molecule of H₂O is not wet and cannot be poured. How many molecules does it take to make water? (hint: Is one grain of sand a heap? Are two?)
- *deep:* Glucose and fructose have the same formula and are different sugars. What else might a formula be leaving out? (hint: A list of the people in a family does not say who is whose mother.)
- *big:* Of 118 elements, only two are named after women: curium and meitnerium. Who gets to name things, and is that fair? (hint: Curium honours Marie and Pierre Curie. Meitnerium honours Lise Meitner. Who decided on the other names?)
- *big:* The same symbols write a rock, a river, a mango and you. Does that make you more like a rock than you thought? (hint: About 99 of every 100 atoms in your body are hydrogen, oxygen, carbon or nitrogen.)
- *big:* Children in every country learn the same chemical symbols. What else would be easier if the whole world wrote it the same way? (hint: Think about numbers, music notes and road signs. What would be lost?)

## Story: The Sack with the H
*Yazhini, Kokila Periamma, Nila Akka, Bablu and the shop are made up. These facts are real. Burrabazar is a large wholesale market in Kolkata. Bengali letters hang from a line along the top. Many Tamil speakers call baking soda soda uppu, and many Bengali speakers call it khabar soda. Nun is a Bengali word for salt. Baking soda is NaHCO₃. Washing soda, also sold as soda ash, is Na₂CO₃. Factory sacks usually print the chemical name. Only some of them print the formula as well.*

Yazhini had been in Kolkata for one day, and she could not read any of it.

The shop boards were in Bengali. The letters were round, and they hung from a line, like washing from a rope. The bus boards were the same. At home in Madurai she read everything, even the backs of biscuit packets. Here she read nothing.

She had come with Kokila Periamma, her mother's elder sister, who cooked for weddings. A Tamil family in Kolkata wanted a Madurai cook for their daughter's wedding. Tomorrow evening four hundred guests would eat vazhaikkai bajji, hot from the oil.

That morning Periamma turned a steel tin upside down over her palm. A little white dust fell out.

"Soda uppu," she said. "I packed the empty tin. Without it the bajji will be as hard as chappals. Go to the big market with the driver, kanna. Bring half a kilo."

"How will I ask? I don't know Bengali."

"Uppu is uppu," said Periamma. She believed that everybody understood Tamil if you said it slowly.

Yazhini put the empty tin in her cloth bag, to show the shopkeeper. On its lid, in red nail polish, somebody had painted NaHCO₃.

That was her sister's work. Nila Akka was in her first year of chemistry at college. She had painted a formula on every jar in the kitchen, and H₂O on the cat's water bowl. "It is only a list," Akka had said. "Na is sodium. H is hydrogen. C is carbon. O is oxygen, and the small 3 says there are three of them. Read it like a shopping list." Yazhini had not been very interested.

The market was called Burrabazar. The driver stopped at a shop with open sacks along its front. Yazhini counted nine sacks. Every one of them held a white powder.

A boy of about twelve came out with a steel scoop. The driver called him Bablu.

"Soda uppu," said Yazhini slowly.

"Soda?" Bablu dug into the third sack and held out a scoop.

Yazhini rubbed a pinch between her fingers. Periamma's soda uppu was as soft as flour. This powder was gritty. Bablu rubbed his fists together, as if he were scrubbing a shirt. It was a soda for washing clothes.

"Uppu," Yazhini tried. She pretended to sprinkle something over food.

"Nun!" said Bablu. He ran to the first sack and came back with a handful of salt.

So now there was a soda that was not hers, and an uppu that was not hers. The right powder was somewhere among seven more sacks. On each sack stood a small slate with its name in chalk, in round letters that hung from a line.

She looked at the sacks themselves. Under the dust, each one had words stamped on it by the factory, in small black print. She bent down to the washing soda.

SODA ASH, it said. Under that: Na₂CO₃.

Her stomach jumped. She knew that row of marks, or nearly. She took the tin out of her bag and held the lid beside the stamp.

Na. Both began the same way. Then the sack had a small 2, and the lid had none. Then the lid had an H, and the sack had none. Both ended with CO₃.

Two sodiums and no hydrogen, she thought. It is a different list, so it is a different powder.

She walked along the row and read every stamp. NaCl was the salt. The second sack said CaCO₃, and the fourth said C₆H₁₂O₆. She did not know what those were, and she did not need to know. They were not her list.

The seventh sack said NaHCO₃.

She checked it against the lid, mark by mark, with her finger. One sodium. One hydrogen. One carbon. Three oxygens. Nothing was extra, and nothing was missing.

It was the first thing in Kolkata she had been able to read.

"This one," she said. She held the lid against the stamp for Bablu.

He looked from one to the other. He put his finger on the H on the lid, and then on the H on the sack. He laughed.

"Khabar soda!" he said.

"Soda uppu," said Yazhini.

"Khabar soda."

"Soda uppu."

Each of them pointed at the same sack and laughed. Bablu weighed out half a kilo.

Then he picked up the chalk. Under the round letters on the slate, he began to copy the marks from the lid. He drew the 3 as tall as the letters. Yazhini shook her head. She rubbed it out with her thumb and wrote it small and low.

"Three oxygens," she said in Tamil, and held up three fingers.

Bablu held up three fingers too.

At the wedding house, Periamma rubbed a pinch of the powder between her fingers. It was as soft as flour.

"Soda uppu," she said. "I told you. Uppu is uppu."

Yazhini said nothing. On the drive back she had been looking out of the window for more. She had found one, on the side of a truck that carried gas cylinders.

"CO₂," she had told the driver. "One carbon and two oxygens."

The driver did not know Tamil either. He had nodded kindly, and driven on.

**For the parent:** Soda uppu, khabar soda and soda were three names, and they did not tell Yazhini which sack to choose. The formula on the sack listed the atoms and how many of each. By checking that list mark by mark, she found the one powder that matched her tin.

- Ask: Two sacks were both called soda. How do the formulas Na₂CO₃ and NaHCO₃ differ?
- Ask: Bablu drew the 3 as tall as the letters. Why does it matter that the 3 is small and low?
- Ask: Which words on a packet at your home could a child in another country read?

## Experiments
### e1 · Count the Drops: does twice the lemon juice use up twice the iodine? (30 min)
- **question:** Lemon juice takes the colour out of iodine solution. How many drops of iodine can 1 teaspoon of lemon juice clear? How many can 2 teaspoons and 3 teaspoons clear?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Suppose 1 teaspoon of lemon juice clears some number of drops. Will 2 teaspoons clear the same number, a few more, or twice as many? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Squeeze all the lemons into one cup and strain the juice, so that every spoonful is the same juice. Use the same teaspoon, filled level. Put the same water, 2 tablespoons, and the same flour, a quarter of a teaspoon, in every glass. Take every drop from the same bottle. Stir after every drop. Stop at the same signal each time: the colour stays for a slow count of ten. Only the amount of lemon juice changes.
- **safety:** Iodine solution is for skin only. Nobody tastes it, or the liquids in the glasses. Pour everything down the sink at the end and rinse the glasses. Iodine stains cloth and wood yellow-brown, so cover the table and wear old clothes. Do not use it if your child is allergic to iodine.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows, for 0, 1, 2 and 3 teaspoons of lemon juice. Beside each, write the number of drops before the colour stayed.
- **results table:** Teaspoons of lemon juice | Drops of iodine before the colour stayed
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover the table with newspaper. Stir a quarter teaspoon of flour into 2 tablespoons of water in a glass. Add 1 drop of iodine and stir. Then squeeze in a few drops of lemon juice and stir again.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the glass while I add one drop of iodine. What colour is the water now? Now I will add a little lemon juice. What happened to the colour?  
  *Child mode:* Add one drop of iodine to floury water. Stir it. Now add lemon juice. What happens?
  - Ask: What do you think the lemon juice did to the iodine? (good: It took the colour away.; It changed the iodine into something with no colour.; It used the iodine up.)
  - Note: Iodine stains the starch in flour. The colour may be blue-grey, purple-grey or pale brown, depending on your flour and your bottle. Whatever colour you see is your signal. Lemon juice clears it within a few seconds of stirring. Do not explain yet.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Squeeze the lemons into one cup and strain the juice. Rinse the first glass. Make four glasses of floury water, as before, and label them 0, 1, 2 and 3. Add no juice to glass 0. Add 1, 2 and 3 level teaspoons of juice to the others. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will count how many drops each glass can clear. Suppose glass 1 clears some number of drops. Will glass 2 clear the same number, a few more, or twice as many? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The glasses hold 1, 2 and 3 spoons of juice. Guess the drops for each. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do we make one glass with no lemon juice at all? (good: To see how many drops colour plain floury water.; So we can compare the others with it.; To check that the lemon juice is what clears the iodine.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add iodine to glass 0, one drop at a time, and stir. It should colour with 1 or 2 drops. Then do glass 1 the same way. Your child stirs after every drop and counts aloud. Stop when the colour stays for a slow count of ten. Write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* One drop. Stir. Has the colour gone? Then one more drop. We stop when the colour stays while you count slowly to ten.  
  *Child mode:* Add one drop and stir. Count every drop. Stop when the colour stays.
  - Note: Near the end the colour fades more slowly, so wait for the full count of ten. If glass 1 clears fewer than 4 drops, double the lemon juice in every glass. If it is still clearing after 30 drops, start again with half as much juice in every glass.
  - Check: In Count the Drops, why do you stir after every drop? → **So the iodine meets all the lemon juice**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do glass 2 and then glass 3 in the same way. Before each glass, ask your child to say how many drops they now expect. Write every count.  
  *Parent says:* Glass 1 took this many drops. How many do you expect for glass 2 now? Let us count and see.  
  *Child mode:* Now do glass 2. Then do glass 3. Count the drops each time.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the four numbers together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they found, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your four numbers. Say what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think it happened. Could you have chosen how many drops glass 2 would clear?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you found. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Glass 2 held twice as much lemon juice as glass 1. What happened to the number of drops? (good: It was about twice as many.; It nearly doubled.; Three teaspoons took about three times as many.)
  - Ask: Why do you think the numbers go up in equal steps? (good: Each bit of lemon juice can clear only so much iodine.; More juice has more of whatever clears the iodine.; They react in a fixed amount.)
  - Note: Usually 2 teaspoons clear about twice as many drops as 1 teaspoon. 3 teaspoons clear about three times as many. A count may be 1 or 2 drops out, because the end is hard to judge. Write down what you really found. The reason: lemon juice holds vitamin C. Its formula is C₆H₈O₆. Iodine goes about as molecules of two atoms, I₂. One vitamin C molecule changes exactly one iodine molecule into something with no colour. It can do this only once. So twice as many vitamin C molecules clear twice as many iodine molecules. Substances always react in fixed counts like this. That is why a formula can carry fixed numbers.
  - Think: A lemon from another tree may clear more drops, or fewer. Would 2 teaspoons of its juice still clear twice as many drops as 1 teaspoon?
### e2 · Water from a Dry Powder: which white powder can give off water? (25 min)
- **question:** Salt and baking soda are both dry white powders. The formula of salt is NaCl. The formula of baking soda is NaHCO₃. If each is heated, will either of them give off water?
- **predict:** Before the heating starts, guess. Water is H₂O. Look at the two formulas. Will salt give off water, will baking soda, will both, or will neither? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same dry pan, the same low flame and the same amount of powder, 2 level teaspoons. Cover the pan with the same steel plate, cooled under the tap and wiped dry each time. Keep the plate on for the same time, a slow count of twenty. Check at 1 minute, 3 minutes and 5 minutes. Look only at the middle of the plate, inside the ring where the pan touched it. Only the powder changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the heating and handles the hot pan and plate. Keep the flame low. Salt may crackle and jump, so keep faces away from the pan. The powder left from the baking soda is washing soda, which stings eyes and skin. Do not taste or touch it. When it is cool, wash it down the sink with plenty of water.
- **record:** Draw a table with two rows, for salt and baking soda. For each check, at 1, 3 and 5 minutes, write wet or dry. Also write what the powder looked like as it was heated.
- **results table:** The powder | Plate at 1 minute | Plate at 3 minutes | Plate at 5 minutes
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the two powders on saucers, with the formula slips in front. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Both powders are dry. Water is H₂O. Look at the two formulas. Could either powder give off water when I heat it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Read the two formulas. Water is H₂O. Which powder could give off water? Why?
  - Ask: Which atoms would a powder need in order to make water? (good: Hydrogen and oxygen.; It needs H and O.; Salt has neither of them.)
  - Note: Many children say that neither will, because both powders are dry. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat 2 level teaspoons of salt in the dry pan on a low flame. Stir now and then with the dry spoon. At 1, 3 and 5 minutes, cover the pan with the cold, dry plate for a slow count of twenty. Lift the plate with the cloth and turn it over.  
  *Parent says:* I will heat the salt. You watch the clock and tell me when each minute comes. Each time, look at the middle of the plate. Is it wet or dry?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up heats the salt. Look at the plate each time. Is it wet or dry?
  - Note: Cool the plate under the tap and wipe it dry before every check. Salt that has been open in damp weather may mist the plate faintly at the first check. After that it stays dry. Drops near the rim come from the gas flame, so look only at the middle.
- **s3 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Switch off the flame. Tip the hot salt onto a steel plate to cool. Wipe the pan with a dry cloth.  
  *Parent says:* The salt is done. I will empty the pan and wipe it. The pan must be dry before the next powder goes in. Why?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up empties the pan and wipes it dry. Why must it be dry?
- **s4 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat 2 level teaspoons of baking soda in the same way. Stir gently. Watch the powder itself as well. Check with the cold, dry plate at 1, 3 and 5 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Now the baking soda. Watch the powder as well as the plate. What is the powder doing? Now look at the middle of the plate.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up heats the baking soda. Watch the powder move. Check the plate each time.
  - Note: After a minute or two the powder seems to shiver, and small puffs lift it. That is gas leaving. The plate should come away with a mist or clear drops in the middle. By 5 minutes there is usually less.
  - Check: In Water from a Dry Powder, why is the plate cooled and dried before every check? → **So old drops are not counted again**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Switch off the flame and leave the pan to cool. Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Where could the water have come from?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: The baking soda was dry. Where did the water come from? (good: From the hydrogen and oxygen atoms in the baking soda.; The heat rearranged the atoms into water.; Its formula has H and O in it.)
  - Ask: Could any amount of heating get water out of the salt? (good: No. Salt has no hydrogen or oxygen atoms.; No. Its formula is only Na and Cl.)
  - Note: The reason: baking soda is NaHCO₃. Heat rearranges its atoms into three new substances. They are water, carbon dioxide gas and washing soda, Na₂CO₃. The water leaves as steam and turns back to drops on the cold plate. The carbon dioxide is what lifts the powder in puffs. Salt is NaCl. It has no hydrogen and no oxygen, so it has nothing to build water from. If you own a kitchen scale, weigh the baking soda before and after. About 10 grams becomes about 6 grams, which is too big a loss to be dampness.
  - Think: Sugar is C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. Does its formula hold the atoms that water needs?

### c1 · A Better Code? Invent your own way to write substances (25 min)
- **problem:** Before 1813 chemists had no shared way to write a substance. John Dalton drew circles with marks inside. Jöns Jacob Berzelius used letters and numbers. Your problem is the one they faced. Invent a code for five elements: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, sodium and chlorine. With it, write water, carbon dioxide, table salt, methane and hydrogen peroxide.

Repeat the picture once for each atom.; Use a different shape for each element, as Dalton did, and draw one shape for each atom.; Use a colour for each element and write a number in that colour.
- **creative:** Use dots, as on a dice face, to show how many atoms there are. The reader can then see the number without counting.; Use sounds as well as marks. ## Worksheets
### Reading a formula (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What does the small 2 in H₂O tell you?  
   Options: Each molecule has two hydrogen atoms / Each molecule has two oxygen atoms / There are two molecules of water / Water is twice as heavy as air  
   **Answer:** Each molecule has two hydrogen atoms. *Why:* A small number counts the atoms of the symbol just before it. The 2 comes after the H, so it counts hydrogen atoms.
2. (fill) The formula of table salt is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['NaCl']. *Why:* Na is the symbol for sodium and Cl is the symbol for chlorine. Table salt is sodium chloride.
3. (match) Match each symbol to its element.  
   **Answer:** H → Hydrogen; O → Oxygen; C → Carbon; Na → Sodium; Cl → Chlorine. *Why:* H, O and C are the first letters of the English names. Na comes from natrium, the old Latin name of sodium. Cl is the first two letters of chlorine.
4. (truefalse) In a formula, a symbol with no small number after it means one atom.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In H₂O the O has no number, so there is one oxygen atom.
5. (count) How many atoms are there in one molecule of CO₂?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* One carbon atom and two oxygen atoms make three atoms.
6. (mcq) Which of these is the formula of carbon dioxide?  
   Options: CO₂ / CO / Co / C₂O  
   **Answer:** CO₂. *Why:* Di means two. Carbon dioxide has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms. CO is carbon monoxide, and Co is the metal cobalt.
7. (sort) Sort these formulas. Does each have one kind of atom, or more than one kind?  
   **Answer:** O₂ → One kind of atom; Au → One kind of atom; N₂ → One kind of atom; H₂O → More than one kind; NaCl → More than one kind; CO₂ → More than one kind. *Why:* Count the different symbols. O₂, Au and N₂ each have one symbol, so they are elements. The others have two symbols, so they are compounds.
8. (truefalse) The symbol for sodium is So.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The symbol is Na, from natrium, the old Latin name of sodium.
9. (fill) In water there are ____ hydrogen atoms for every one oxygen atom.  
   **Answer:** ['two', '2']. *Why:* The formula H₂O has a small 2 after the H and no number after the O.
10. (mcq) What does the formula NaCl tell you about salt?  
   Options: One sodium for every chlorine / More sodium than chlorine / Sodium, carbon and iodine / Two atoms in each grain  
   **Answer:** One sodium for every chlorine. *Why:* Na is sodium and Cl is chlorine, with no numbers, so they are one to one. Cl is one symbol. It is not carbon followed by another element.
11. (write) Write what the formula CO₂ tells you, in one full sentence.  
   **Answer:** Each molecule of carbon dioxide has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.. *Why:* A strong answer names both elements and gives the number of each.
12. (write) In Count the Drops, twice the lemon juice cleared about twice the iodine. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Lemon juice holds vitamin C. One vitamin C molecule changes exactly one iodine molecule. So twice as many vitamin C molecules clear twice as many iodine molecules.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the two substances react in a fixed count, one for one. So the amounts go up together.
13. (draw) Draw one molecule of water and one molecule of carbon dioxide as circles. Label every atom.  
   **Answer:** Water: one circle marked O with two smaller circles marked H joined to it. Carbon dioxide: one circle marked C with a circle marked O joined on each side.. *Why:* A strong drawing has the right number of each atom, and every circle is labelled with its symbol.
14. (perspective) Which is the real name of water?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that a name is useful when the people you talk to understand it. Another is that only the formula says what water is made of. Another is that water does not change when its name changes.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer says what a name is for, and uses that to choose.
15. (mcq) Why is the second letter of a symbol always small?  
   Options: So Co and CO cannot be confused / Because small letters are quicker to write / Because the second atom is smaller / To save ink  
   **Answer:** So Co and CO cannot be confused. *Why:* Every capital letter starts a new symbol. Co is one element, cobalt. CO is two elements, carbon and oxygen.
### One atom more, and names that give clues (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Carbon monoxide is CO. Carbon dioxide is CO₂. What is the difference between their molecules?  
   Options: CO₂ has one more oxygen atom / CO₂ has one more carbon atom / CO₂ is two molecules of CO / There is no difference  
   **Answer:** CO₂ has one more oxygen atom. *Why:* The small 2 counts oxygen atoms. That one extra atom makes a different gas. Carbon monoxide is a deadly poison.
2. (mcq) Which pair shows two different substances made of the same two elements?  
   Options: H₂O and H₂O₂ / H₂O and NaCl / O₂ and N₂ / CO₂ and NaCl  
   **Answer:** H₂O and H₂O₂. *Why:* Water and hydrogen peroxide are both made of hydrogen and oxygen. The numbers differ, so the substances differ.
3. (mcq) Salt has no separate molecules. So what does its formula NaCl mean?  
   Options: Equal numbers of sodium and chlorine / One sodium atom in each grain / One molecule made of two atoms / Salt is half metal  
   **Answer:** Equal numbers of sodium and chlorine. *Why:* Salt is a crystal in which sodium and chlorine atoms alternate. The formula gives the ratio, which is one to one.
4. (mcq) What does 'di' mean in the name carbon dioxide?  
   Options: Two / Gas / Heavy / One  
   **Answer:** Two. *Why:* Di means two. Carbon dioxide has two oxygen atoms. Mono means one, as in carbon monoxide.
5. (truefalse) Glucose and fructose have the same formula, so they are the same substance.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both are C₆H₁₂O₆, but their atoms are arranged differently. They are two different sugars, called isomers.
6. (truefalse) Water from a cloud and water from a well have the same formula.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Pure water is H₂O wherever it comes from. Well water also has other substances dissolved in it.
7. (sort) Sort these. Was each one chosen by people, or found by measuring?  
   **Answer:** The letter H for hydrogen → Chosen by people; The word water → Chosen by people; The symbol Na for sodium → Chosen by people; The 2 in H₂O → Found by measuring; The 2 in CO₂ → Found by measuring; Equal sodium and chlorine in salt → Found by measuring. *Why:* Letters, words and symbols are agreements, and they could have been different. The numbers of atoms had to be measured, and nobody could choose them.
8. (odd) Three of these substances contain oxygen atoms. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: H₂O / CO₂ / O₃ / NaCl  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Water, carbon dioxide and ozone all have O in their formulas. Salt has only sodium and chlorine.
9. (count) How many atoms are there in one molecule of glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆?  
   **Answer:** 24. *Why:* 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms. 6 + 12 + 6 = 24.
10. (order) Put these molecules in order, from the fewest atoms to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['O₂', 'H₂O', 'CH₄', 'C₆H₁₂O₆']. *Why:* O₂ has 2 atoms, H₂O has 3, CH₄ has 5 and C₆H₁₂O₆ has 24.
11. (write) In Water from a Dry Powder, only the heated baking soda wet the plate. Explain why, using NaHCO₃ and NaCl.  
   **Answer:** Water is H₂O, so it needs hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Baking soda has both, and heat rearranged them into water. Salt has neither, so it cannot make water.. *Why:* A strong answer says that heating only rearranges atoms. A substance can give off only what its own atoms can build.
12. (write) A packet says 'chemical-free'. Can anything be chemical-free? Explain what you think.  
   **Answer:** No. Everything made of atoms is a chemical. Water is H₂O and salt is NaCl. The packet probably means that nothing harmful was added.. *Why:* A strong answer says that natural things have formulas too. Whether a substance harms you depends on which substance it is and how much.
13. (write) How could you find out whether water has one or two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom?  
   **Answer:** Split water with electricity and collect the two gases. The hydrogen takes up twice as much space as the oxygen. That points to two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom.. *Why:* A strong answer names a measurement. The number in a formula has to be found by measuring.
14. (perspective) Should every country use the same chemical symbols?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer weighs what everyone gains from one shared code. It also says what is hard or unfair about how the code was chosen.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. Shared symbols save lives and time. They also carry the history of the people who chose them.
15. (label) Label the three parts of the formula H₂O in this picture.  
   **Answer:** ['H: the symbol for hydrogen', 'Small 2: there are two hydrogen atoms', 'O: the symbol for oxygen, with no number, so one atom']. *Why:* A formula is read one symbol at a time. A small number belongs to the symbol just before it.
### What a formula can and cannot tell you (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A white powder has the formula KCl. Heated alone, could it give off water?  
   Options: No. It has no hydrogen or oxygen. / Yes. Every powder holds some water. / Yes, if it is heated long enough. / Only if the powder is white.  
   **Answer:** No. It has no hydrogen or oxygen.. *Why:* Water is H₂O. Heating only rearranges the atoms a substance already has. KCl has potassium and chlorine atoms only.
2. (mcq) Which part of a formula did people have to measure to find?  
   Options: The small numbers / The capital letters / The small letters / The order of the letters  
   **Answer:** The small numbers. *Why:* The letters were agreed by people. The numbers of atoms had to be found by measuring, as with the gases from split water.
3. (mcq) 3 teaspoons of lemon juice cleared 24 drops of iodine. About how many drops would 1 teaspoon clear?  
   Options: 8 / 24 / 72 / 3  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* The same juice clears drops in a fixed proportion. One third of the juice clears one third of the drops. 24 ÷ 3 = 8.
4. (mcq) Vitamin B12 has the formula C₆₃H₈₈CoN₁₄O₁₄P. What does Co stand for?  
   Options: One atom of cobalt / Carbon and oxygen / Carbon monoxide / A printing mistake  
   **Answer:** One atom of cobalt. *Why:* A capital letter followed by a small letter is one symbol. Co is cobalt. Carbon and oxygen would be written CO.
5. (truefalse) Heating a substance alone can add new kinds of atoms to it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Heating rearranges the atoms that are already there. It cannot make a new kind of atom.
6. (truefalse) Two substances with different formulas can look exactly alike.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Salt, NaCl, and baking soda, NaHCO₃, are both dry white powders. You need a test or a label to tell them apart.
7. (match) Match each everyday name to its chemical name.  
   **Answer:** Table salt → Sodium chloride; Baking soda → Sodium hydrogen carbonate; Washing soda → Sodium carbonate; Chalk → Calcium carbonate; Dry ice → Solid carbon dioxide. *Why:* An everyday name says how people use a substance. A chemical name says which elements are in it.
8. (count) How many oxygen atoms are there in 4 molecules of CO₂?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Each molecule has 2 oxygen atoms. 4 × 2 = 8.
9. (fill) Different substances that have the same formula are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['isomers']. *Why:* Isomers have the same atoms in the same numbers, joined in a different arrangement. Glucose and fructose are isomers.
10. (write) Two different sugars both have the formula C₆H₁₂O₆. What does a formula leave out?  
   **Answer:** A formula leaves out how the atoms are arranged. The two sugars have the same atoms, joined in different arrangements.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the count of atoms is the same and the arrangement differs.
11. (write) Many people think salt is made of tiny NaCl molecules. Explain what is wrong with this idea.  
   **Answer:** Salt is a crystal. Sodium and chlorine atoms alternate in every direction, and each sodium has six chlorine neighbours. No sodium belongs to one chlorine alone. The formula gives the ratio, one to one.. *Why:* A strong answer describes the repeating pattern and says that the formula is a ratio.
12. (write) Hydrogen gas burns. Water puts fires out. How can water be made partly of hydrogen?  
   **Answer:** In water the atoms are joined into new molecules, H₂O. A compound behaves like itself. It does not behave like the elements it is made from.. *Why:* A strong answer says that joining atoms makes a new substance with its own behaviour.
13. (write) Plan a test: does twice the baking soda need twice the vinegar before the fizzing stops?  
   **Answer:** Put 1 level teaspoon of baking soda in one glass and 2 in another. Add vinegar one teaspoon at a time and count until the fizzing stops. Keep the spoon, the vinegar and the stirring the same.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the amount of baking soda, counts something, and keeps everything else the same.
14. (perspective) Is a formula a fact about nature, or a tool that people made?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that the drop counts in Count the Drops could not be chosen. Another is that the letters could have been different, and that a formula leaves out the arrangement of the atoms.. *Why:* Scientists and philosophers still discuss this. A strong answer separates the part of a formula that was agreed from the part that was measured.
15. (draw) Draw a flat piece of a salt crystal: 3 rows of 3 atoms, in two colours. Neighbours side by side must differ.  
   **Answer:** A 3 by 3 grid of circles coloured like a chessboard. There are 5 circles of one colour and 4 of the other.. *Why:* A strong drawing alternates the colours along every row and every column. In a real grain the two kinds are equal in number, because the pattern goes on for millions of rows.

## Applications
- A medicine label lists its contents by chemical name. A pharmacist in any country can read what is in the strip.
- A fertiliser bag is marked NPK. The letters are the symbols of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, and the numbers say how much of each.
- A fire extinguisher marked CO₂ holds carbon dioxide. Fire fighters choose it for electrical fires, because the gas leaves no water behind.
- A vehicle's pollution check measures CO, carbon monoxide, in the exhaust. Too much shows that the fuel is not burning fully.
- Astronomers find H₂O and CO₂ on other worlds from the colours of light that each formula takes in.
- A food chemist checks that the salt in a packet is NaCl with a little iodine compound added.

## Spotted around us
- The letters NPK on a fertiliser bag at a nursery or a farm shop. K is the symbol for potassium.
- A red fire extinguisher on the wall of a school, a shop or a lift lobby. Its label says CO₂.
- The words sodium chloride or sodium fluoride in the small print on an ORS packet or a toothpaste tube.
- A CNG auto or bus. CNG is mostly methane, CH₄: one carbon atom and four hydrogen atoms.
- A sign for nitrogen air at a petrol pump. The nitrogen put into tyres is N₂.
- The name of a water shop or a water purifier that uses H₂O in place of the word water.

## Open questions
- **How many elements can exist?** 118 are known, and the heaviest last for less than a second. Laboratories are trying to make element 119. Nobody knows where the list ends.
- **Which molecules are floating between the stars?** Astronomers have identified more than 300 kinds of molecule in space. Starlight also shows hundreds of dark lines that no known formula explains yet.
- **Can you tell what a substance will do from its formula and arrangement alone?** For small molecules chemists can often work it out. For large ones, such as new medicines, they still have to make the substance and test it.
- **How many different substances are possible?** Chemists have recorded well over a hundred million. The number that could be made from the known elements is far larger, and nobody can say how large.
- **Did the first molecules of life form on the Earth, or did some arrive from space?** Some meteorites carry molecules that living things use. Whether they mattered for the start of life is still argued.

## Connected maths
- In Atom Market you build water, H₂O, from atom cards. How many H cards and how many O cards do you need to build 5 water molecules? How many cards is that in all? **10 H cards and 5 O cards, which is 15 cards.**. Each water molecule needs 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. 5 times 2 is 10, and 5 times 1 is 5. 10 and 5 make 15. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In Count the Drops, 1 teaspoon of lemon juice cleared 6 drops of iodine. 2 teaspoons cleared 11 drops, and 3 teaspoons cleared 17. About how many drops would 5 teaspoons clear? Explain how you decided. **About 28 or 29 drops.**. Each extra teaspoon cleared 5 or 6 more drops. 3 teaspoons cleared 17. Two more teaspoons add about 11 or 12. 17 and 11 make 28, and 17 and 12 make 29. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Here are three counts from Count the Drops. 1 teaspoon of lemon juice cleared 6 drops, 2 teaspoons cleared 7 and 3 teaspoons cleared 17. Which count looks wrong? What would you do about it? **The 7 drops for 2 teaspoons looks wrong. Make that glass again and count once more.**. From 1 to 3 teaspoons the count rose by 11, so each teaspoon adds 5 or 6 drops. The middle glass should be near 11 or 12. A count of 7 fits no pattern. The likeliest causes are a missed stir or a wrong spoonful. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In Dough Atoms you built a salt block of 8 balls, with 4 of each colour. A bigger block has 3 balls along each edge, and the colours still take turns. How many balls does it have? Can the two colours be equal? **27 balls. The colours cannot be equal. There are 14 of one colour and 13 of the other.**. 3 times 3 times 3 is 27. An odd number cannot be split into two equal whole numbers. A block with 4 balls along each edge has 64 balls, 32 of each. A real grain has millions of atoms along each edge, so one extra atom makes no difference. (queues `math.number-sense.odd-even.L3`)
- In the Name Collector you wrote the formula of table sugar, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. How many atoms are in one molecule? Now look only at the H and the O. How many water molecules could those atoms make? **45 atoms. The hydrogen and oxygen atoms could make exactly 11 water molecules.**. 12 and 22 and 11 make 45. Each water molecule needs 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. 22 divided by 2 is 11, and there are 11 oxygen atoms, so none is left over. Sugar has the atoms of 12 carbons and 11 waters. That is why sugars are called carbohydrates. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In Water from a Dry Powder, heat changes two units of baking soda, NaHCO₃, into three things. They are one unit of washing soda, Na₂CO₃, one water molecule, H₂O, and one carbon dioxide molecule, CO₂. Count each kind of atom before and after. Is any atom lost? **No atom is lost. Before and after there are 2 sodium, 2 hydrogen, 2 carbon and 6 oxygen atoms.**. Two units of NaHCO₃ have 2 sodium, 2 hydrogen, 2 carbon and 6 oxygen atoms. Na₂CO₃ has 2 sodium, 1 carbon and 3 oxygen. H₂O has 2 hydrogen and 1 oxygen. CO₂ has 1 carbon and 2 oxygen. The oxygen adds up to 3 and 1 and 2, which is 6. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What does a chemical formula tell you? → Which atoms are in a substance and how many of each.
- Read H₂O aloud as atoms. → Two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
- What is the formula of table salt? → NaCl: one sodium for every chlorine.
- A symbol has no small number after it. How many atoms is that? → One.
- What is the difference between CO and CO₂? → CO₂ has one more oxygen atom. They are different gases.
- Why is the symbol for sodium Na? → It comes from natrium, the old Latin name of sodium.
- Which part of a formula did people choose, and which part did they measure? → They chose the letters. They measured the numbers.
- Two sugars have the same formula. What is different about them? → The way their atoms are arranged.
