# What is life? Why every test for life has an exception
`sci.what-is-life.signs-of-life.L4`

**Summary.** A candle flame uses up wax, needs air, grows bigger and spreads to a new wick. Yet nobody calls a flame alive. A mule almost never has young, and a seed can wait for centuries without doing anything. Yet both are alive. So every simple test for life has an exception, and scientists still argue about the best definition. In this unit your child tests their own rule for life against hard cases, watches a beetroot cell lose its colour when it is heated, and finds that a living thing stays different from its surroundings by working at it all the time.

**Intro.** *A candle flame uses up wax. It needs air, because it goes out under a glass. It grows when you add a wick, and it spreads to a new wick. Is a flame alive? And what about a mule, who almost never has young, or a dry seed that does nothing at all?* Every simple test for life gets fooled by something. A flame passes many tests and is not alive. A seed fails many tests and is alive. Scientists know this, and they have tried for a hundred years to write a better test. In this unit you will write your own rule, break it, and mend it. You will also see what happens inside a living cell when it is heated, and why staying warm in a cool room is hard work.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain why one sign of life, such as growing or moving, cannot tell living things from non-living things, using fire and crystals as examples.
1. I can name some tests for life that scientists use, and give an exception to each one.
2. I can explain that a living body stays different from its surroundings, and that this takes energy all the time.
3. I can explain why heating a living cell makes its colour leak out, using the idea of a skin around every cell.
4. I can explain how seeds and some animals can be alive while doing almost nothing, and how long a seed can wait.
5. I can explain why scientists disagree about whether a virus is alive.
6. I can write a rule for what is alive, test it on hard cases, run a fair test, and say what the evidence supports.

## Terms
- **Alive**: A living being uses energy to build and mend their own body. They keep their inside different from the outside, and they come from other living beings. *e.g.* A neem tree, a crow and the bacteria in curd are all alive.
- **Working definition**: A meaning that scientists use for now because it is useful, even though they know it might change. *e.g.* NASA uses a working definition of life to decide what to look for on Mars.
- **Exception**: A case that does not fit a rule. *e.g.* A mule almost never has young. She is an exception to 'living things make young'.
- **Metabolism**: All the chemical changes in a living body that turn food into energy and into new body parts. *e.g.* Your body breaks down rice for energy, then uses some of it to build muscle.
- **Homeostasis**: A living body keeping something steady, such as warmth, even when the outside changes. *e.g.* You sweat when you are hot and shiver when you are cold, so you stay near 37 degrees Celsius.
- **Cell membrane**: The thin skin around every cell. It decides what goes in and what stays out. *e.g.* The skin around each beetroot cell keeps the red colour inside until the beetroot is heated.
- **Energy**: What is needed to make anything happen, such as heat, light or movement. *e.g.* Food gives your body energy, and petrol gives a scooter energy.
- **Fuel**: Something that is used up to give energy. *e.g.* Wax is the fuel of a candle, and rice is fuel for your body.
- **Crystal**: A solid whose tiny parts are stacked in a repeating pattern. *e.g.* Salt grains are tiny cubes, and mishri, the rock sugar, is made of sugar crystals.
- **Dormant**: Alive but resting, with almost nothing going on. *e.g.* A dry seed in a jar is dormant until water arrives.
- **Thermostat**: A part that senses the temperature and switches heating or cooling on and off to keep it steady. *e.g.* The thermostat inside a fridge switches the cooling on when the inside gets too warm.
- **Virus**: A tiny package of instructions inside a protein coat. It can make copies only inside a living cell. *e.g.* The virus that causes a cold.
- **Darwinian evolution**: Change over many generations. Young are a little different from their parents, and the differences that help them survive are passed on. *e.g.* Bacteria that survive an antibiotic have young who also survive it.
- **Self-sustaining**: Keeps going and repairs itself, as long as there is food and energy, without anyone rebuilding it. *e.g.* A bacterium in curd keeps going on milk sugar. A robot needs a person to build and mend it.
- **Regeneration**: Growing back a lost part. *e.g.* A money plant stem cut in two grows new roots, and a gecko can grow a new tail.
- **Sterile**: Not able to have young. *e.g.* A mule is almost always sterile, and most worker honeybees never lay eggs.
- **Combustion**: Burning. Fuel joins with oxygen from the air and gives out heat and light. *e.g.* A candle flame is combustion of wax gas.

## Explanations
### What a definition is for: a rule that is useful, not a law of nature · *defined*
**Definition:** A working definition is a rule scientists use for now, because it is useful, even though they know it may change.

A definition is a rule we write so that people mean the same thing by a word. The word 'alive' was in use for thousands of years before anyone knew what cells were. So the word came first, and the science came later. Scientists now write working definitions of life. A working definition does not claim to be the truth about nature. It only has to be useful, for example for deciding what a space probe should look for. When a hard case breaks the rule, scientists do not throw the case away. They learn something from it, and they mend the rule.
- e.g. The seven signs of life are a working definition. They work well for crows, trees and people.
- e.g. A flame breaks the seven signs, so the seven signs are not enough.
- e.g. NASA's definition is another working definition, written for the search for life on other worlds.
### Why one sign of life is never enough: fire · *defined*
**Definition:** Combustion is burning. Fuel joins with oxygen and gives out heat and light.

A candle flame does many of the things on the list of life. It takes in fuel, which is wax gas. It takes in air. It gives out heat, light, carbon dioxide and water. It grows when you add more wick. It moves, and it spreads to a new wick or a dry leaf. It even goes out when it runs out of food or air. Yet a flame is not alive. A flame is a place where fuel is joining with oxygen, a burning that is happening right now. Nothing in a flame is built, kept or mended. If you blow it out, nothing is left that could start again. A flame is only as old as its fuel.
- e.g. A forest fire eats, breathes, grows and travels far.
- e.g. A diya flame needs oil and a wick, and goes out under a katori.
- e.g. A flame can light a second wick, but both flames are made of the same burning, with no instructions passed on.
### Crystals grow by adding more of the same, living things grow by turning other things into themselves · *defined*
**Definition:** Metabolism is all the chemical changes in a living body that turn food into energy and into new body parts.

Put a grain of salt in strong salt water and it grows. Break the crystal and each piece grows too. A crystal can even copy its pattern. Each new layer is laid down just like the one below it. But look at what the crystal is doing. It only adds more salt that was already in the water. Nothing is changed into salt. A bean sprout is different. A bean sprout is made of sugar, water, air and a little soil. None of these is a bean sprout. The sprout takes them in and changes them into new roots and leaves. This turning of other stuff into your own body is called metabolism. A crystal stacks. A living thing builds.
- e.g. Salt in a pan grows by stacking more salt from the sea water.
- e.g. A coconut sprout turns rain, air and the nut's food into roots and leaves.
- e.g. You turn rice, dal and curd into muscle, blood and bone.
### Staying different: a living body works to stay unlike its surroundings · *defined*
**Definition:** Homeostasis is a living body keeping something steady, such as its warmth, even when the outside changes.

Warm water in a tumbler cools down until it is as warm as the room. Then it stays that way. Left alone, everything drifts to match its surroundings. A living body does not. Your body stays near 37 degrees Celsius whether the room is warm or cool. It does this by burning food, by shivering, by sweating and by moving blood around. Inside every cell there is also a different amount of salt, sugar and water from outside the cell. A living thing is a place where the inside is kept unlike the outside. This is called homeostasis. It takes energy every second. When a living thing dies, the work stops and the inside slowly becomes the same as the outside.
- e.g. Your body stays near 37 degrees Celsius in a 28-degree room.
- e.g. A fish keeps the salt in her blood at a steady level in river water.
- e.g. A dead leaf, a dead insect and a cold cup of tea all end up at room temperature.
### The skin of a cell: why heat makes a living beetroot bleed · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell membrane is the thin skin around a cell that controls what goes in and what stays out.

Every living cell has a thin skin around it, called the cell membrane. This skin is made of fat and protein. It works like a careful door. It lets some things in and out and keeps other things inside. In a beetroot cell, the red pigment is kept inside a bag in the cell. The membranes keep it there. Cold or room-temperature water does not hurt the membranes much, so a whole beetroot stays red inside and the water around it stays clear. Heat shakes the fat and protein of the skin out of their places. Holes open and the pigment leaks out. That is why boiled beetroot turns the cooking water red. A cell with a broken skin can no longer keep its inside different from the outside.
- e.g. Beetroot cubes in cold water stay in their colour, but cubes in very hot water turn the water red.
- e.g. A boiled potato slice no longer keeps the water balance of a fresh slice.
- e.g. Bacteria in curd are each wrapped in a skin that keeps their chemistry together.
### Exceptions that are alive: mules, worker bees and the sleepers · *defined*
**Definition:** Sterile means not able to have young.

Many people say that living things make young. But a mule almost never does, because the chromosomes from a horse mother and a donkey father do not pair up neatly. A worker honeybee almost never lays eggs either. The queen does that for the whole hive. Yet mules and worker bees are alive. So the test 'makes young' belongs to a kind of living thing across many generations, and not to each single body. Another exception is the sleeper. A dry seed, or a tiny animal dried to dust, may show no sign of life for years. Neither is dead. Each is dormant, alive but resting. So some tests have to be about what a living thing can do, and not what she is doing right now.
- e.g. A mule works hard on a farm and is alive, though she is almost always sterile.
- e.g. A worker bee flies, feeds the young and builds the comb, and does not lay eggs.
- e.g. A dormant seed does not move or eat, and yet sprouts when water arrives.
### Seeds and animals that wait: how long can life pause? · *defined*
**Definition:** Dormant means alive but resting, with almost nothing going on.

Some living things can pause their life. A seed dries out until almost nothing is going on inside. This is called dormancy. How long can it last? Most kitchen seeds are best within a couple of years. But a date palm seed found at Masada in Israel, about 2,000 years old, was sprouted by Elaine Solowey in 2005 and grew into a tree. In 2012, Svetlana Yashina's team in Russia grew whole plants of a flower called Silene from fruit tissue that had been frozen in Siberian ice for about 32,000 years. Tiny animals called tardigrades can dry up and wait, and one frozen for over 30 years woke up. Scientists call this deep pause cryptobiosis. The pause shows us that being alive is not the same as doing something right now.
- e.g. A sacred lotus seed from an old lake bed in China, about 1,300 years old, has sprouted.
- e.g. A tardigrade dries into a tiny barrel and wakes when water returns.
- e.g. Seed banks keep seeds dry and cold for many years.
### Scientists' working definitions of life · *debated*
There is no agreed definition of life. Scientists have tried many. A group at NASA, in the 1990s, wrote: life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution. This is useful for planning how to look for life on other worlds. In 2002, the American biochemist Daniel Koshland listed seven pillars of life: a program of instructions, improvisation when conditions change, compartments, energy, regeneration, adaptability and seclusion, which means keeping each chemical reaction apart from the others. Each of these definitions has a hard case that bends it. In the NASA one, a single mule is not able to evolve alone, so the definition has to be about the whole kind. In the pillars, a dormant seed is not using much energy. Scientists use these definitions as tools, not as the final answer.
- e.g. NASA's definition has three parts: chemical, self-sustaining, and able to evolve by Darwin's rules.
- e.g. Koshland's seven pillars come from his work as a biochemist.
- e.g. The seven signs of life that you may know from school are another working definition.
- **A NASA group in the 1990s:** Life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution. The key point is that living things change over generations, so a kind of life can improve at surviving.
- **Daniel Koshland, an American biochemist, 2002:** Life is seven things together: a program, improvisation, compartments, energy, regeneration, adaptability and seclusion. No one of them is enough, but a living thing shows them all.
- **Carol Cleland, an American philosopher of science:** It is too early to write a definition. People used 'water' for thousands of years before anyone knew it was H2O. We will define life well only after we find a theory of life, perhaps by finding a second kind of life that did not start on Earth.
### Viruses: alive, not alive, or something in between? · *debated*
A virus is a package of instructions in a protein coat. By itself, a virus does not take in food, grow or use energy. Inside a living cell, though, the virus takes over the cell and uses her machinery to make thousands of copies. Those copies change a little, and the changes are passed on, so viruses evolve. In 1935, Wendell Stanley made crystals of a plant virus, and the crystals still made plants sick when they were dissolved again. In 2003, scientists found a giant virus called mimivirus, big enough to see through a good light microscope. Whether viruses are alive depends on which tests you choose, and scientists honestly disagree.
- e.g. The virus that causes a cold makes copies inside the cells of a nose.
- e.g. A tobacco mosaic virus crystal can lie in a jar for years and still infect a leaf.
- e.g. A bacteriophage is a virus that infects only bacteria.
- **Scientists who say viruses are not alive:** A virus has no cells and no energy of her own. She cannot grow or repair, and she cannot copy herself alone. She is a chemical that borrows life from cells.
- **Scientists who say viruses are alive:** A virus has her own genes, she copies them, and she evolves by Darwin's rules. Needing a host does not make a virus lifeless, because many living things need a host to survive.
- **Scientists who say 'alive' is the wrong question:** Life is not a switch with two settings. A virus shows some of the traits of life and not others, and it is more useful to say which ones.
### Could life be made of other things, and could we make it? · *idea*
All the life we know uses carbon, water and cells, and passes instructions on in DNA or RNA. We have not yet found a second kind. Some scientists think that life on other worlds could use a different liquid, or a different material, and we need tests that would still work. In 2010, a team led by Craig Venter built a whole bacterial genome in a laboratory and put it into an empty bacterial cell, which then started to live and divide. In 2016, the same group built a very small cell with 473 genes and found that about 149 of those genes had no known job. So, even in the simplest known cell, scientists still do not know what everything does. Others make patterns in computers, such as the glider in John Conway's Game of Life, which moves and copies itself on a screen. Most scientists say these are models, not life. Nobody can yet say for sure.
- e.g. A cell with a lab-built genome has been made, but it was put inside a cell that already existed.
- e.g. The glider in the Game of Life is a pattern that moves across a grid, not a living thing.
- e.g. A space probe has to decide which tests to use before it lands.
### Why the line is hard to draw · *debated*
Put all these cases in a row: a rock, a crystal, a flame, a virus, a dry seed, a bacterium, an ant, a person. Each one has some of the traits of life and not others. A flame uses fuel, a crystal copies its pattern, a virus evolves, a dry seed waits, a bacterium builds and mends herself. No single trait separates the living things from the rest. Some philosophers say that this is like the word 'game'. There is no one thing that all games share, but we know games when we see them. Others say there is a real line, and that we have not yet found it. Both views are sensible, and the evidence we have does not settle it.
- e.g. No single trait is shared by all and only living things.
- e.g. Scientists often use a bundle of traits at once.
- e.g. A hard case helps us find which trait matters most.
- **Some philosophers (following Ludwig Wittgenstein's idea of 'family resemblance'):** 'Alive' names a family of traits that usually come together. In nature they sometimes come apart, so a single sharp line may not exist.
- **Many biologists:** There is a real line, which is Darwinian evolution by cells or by something like cells. The edge cases are rare and can be handled one by one.

## Mostly agreed
- No single sign of life, such as growing, moving or using fuel, separates living things from non-living things. *How we know:* A flame grows, moves and uses fuel, and a dry seed does none of these, so each test gives a wrong answer for some case.
- Every living thing we know is made of cells, or is a virus that needs cells. *How we know:* Cells have been looked at in every kind of living thing, from bacteria to trees, since Robert Hooke and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in the 1600s.
- Living cells use energy to keep their insides different from their outsides. *How we know:* Living cells hold different amounts of salt and other chemicals than their surroundings, and when a cell dies the differences fade.
- Seeds, and some tiny animals, can pause their life for years and then start again. *How we know:* A date palm seed about 2,000 years old was sprouted in 2005, and a tardigrade frozen for over 30 years woke up.
- Living things build their bodies from other materials, while a crystal grows by adding more of the same. *How we know:* A sprout lives on food stored in her seed and changes that food into new roots and leaves. A salt crystal in salt water gets bigger only by adding the salt that was already in the water.
- There is no single definition of life that every scientist accepts. *How we know:* The NASA definition, Koshland's seven pillars and the seven signs of life each have a hard case that bends them.

## How we found out
**Dmitri Ivanovsky, Martinus Beijerinck, Wendell Stanley and Rosalind Franklin, 1892, 1898, 1935 and the 1950s.** In 1892, a Russian scientist named Dmitri Ivanovsky was studying a disease of tobacco plants. At that time, everyone believed that all germs were bacteria, small living cells that a fine porcelain filter could catch. Ivanovsky squeezed the sap of sick plants through such a filter. The clear sap that came through still made healthy plants sick. In 1898, a Dutch scientist, Martinus Beijerinck, repeated this and said the cause was a new kind of tiny thing, a 'living liquid'. Then, in 1935, an American, Wendell Stanley, ground up about a tonne of sick leaves and purified the juice until it formed needle-shaped crystals. When he dissolved the crystals and rubbed them on healthy leaves, the leaves got sick. A crystal can infect a leaf. In the 1950s, Rosalind Franklin used X-rays in London to show that the virus is a hollow rod of protein, with the virus's instructions wound inside. So germs did not have to be bacteria, and a thing could be both a crystal and a source of disease.  
*What people believed before:* That every germ must be a bacterium, a small living cell big enough to be caught in a fine filter, and that a crystal could never also be a source of disease.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Fire is alive, because it eats, breathes, grows and spreads.** (Many children, and some of the oldest ideas. The Greek thinker Heraclitus, about 500 BCE, called the world an ever-living fire.; *rejected*)  
  For: A flame passes many tests: it uses fuel and air, it grows, it moves and it spreads.  
  Against: A flame builds nothing and mends nothing. It cannot keep its own warmth steady, and its new flames pass on no instructions. If the fuel stops, the flame ends, with nothing left to start again.
- **A thing that cannot have young is not alive.** (Many people who learned 'living things reproduce' as a rule.; *rejected*)  
  For: Making young is the way a kind of living thing goes on. Without it, a kind ends.  
  Against: A mule, a worker bee and an old tree that no longer flowers are all alive. The test is about a kind over many generations, not about each single body.
- **A thing that does nothing is dead.** (Many people, and any rule that depends only on what a body is doing right now.; *rejected*)  
  For: Signs of life such as moving, breathing and growing are things we can watch.  
  Against: A dry seed, a dried tardigrade and a frozen bacterium do almost nothing and wake up again. What matters is what a body can still do.
- **Viruses are alive.** (Some virologists. Some say giant viruses such as mimivirus, found in 2003, are a fourth branch of life.; *debated*)  
  For: Viruses have genes, they copy those genes, and they evolve by Darwin's rules.  
  Against: A virus cannot use energy or make anything on her own. Without a host cell, she does nothing at all.
- **Life must be made of carbon and water, in cells.** (Many biologists, because every living thing we know is built this way. This is sometimes called carbon chauvinism.; *debated*)  
  For: Carbon makes long, flexible chains, and water lets chemicals move and meet. All known life uses them.  
  Against: We have only studied one example of life, which began once on Earth. Other worlds may have other chemistries, and a test built only for our kind might miss them.
- **A computer program or robot can be alive.** (A few researchers in artificial life, such as people who study John Conway's Game of Life patterns.; *minority*)  
  For: A pattern in a computer can move, copy itself and change.  
  Against: A program needs a computer built and powered by someone, and does not turn other stuff into its own body. Most scientists say these are models of life.

## Roots
We have tested rules for life and watched what heat does to a living cell. Now we ask what these tests tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A living thing is a place that stays different from its surroundings. Warm water cooled to match the room, but your body stayed warm. A beetroot cell kept its red inside, until heat broke the skin around it. Staying different takes work every second, and dying is when the work stops.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think 'alive' means having something extra, or doing one special thing such as moving or growing. But a flame does those things, and a sleeping seed does not. What living things share is ongoing work, to keep an inside that is unlike the outside.  
*Evidence:* In the warmth test, the warm water cooled towards the temperature of the room, but a child's own temperature stayed almost the same. In the beetroot test, cubes in cold water kept their colour, and cubes in very hot water turned the water red. If your results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* You are not only a thing, you are also a doing. Every second, your body burns food to stay warm, to rebuild itself and to keep the inside of each cell apart from the outside.
- *what is life:* Life is the work of keeping an inside that is different from the outside, and of building that inside out of whatever the outside provides.
- *what is this world:* Sunlight goes into plants, plants become food, food becomes warmth, and the warmth leaves into space. Life is one of the places where this flow of energy stops for a while and builds something.

From the One come particles, stars, and then, on at least one world, living bodies. Everything is conscious, down to the smallest particle, and in a living body this shows up as sensing, choosing and mending. A bacterium senses food and swims towards food, and a root chooses where to grow. The edge of a living body is not a wall that cuts her off from the world. It is a boundary that she keeps rebuilding, from the world's own rice, water and air.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2:** The Upanishad says that beings are born from food, live by food, and in the end return to food. (A sprout, a coconut palm and a child all build their bodies from what they take in, and one day their bodies become food and soil for others.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 30 (Diels and Kranz numbering):** Heraclitus, a Greek thinker of about 500 BCE, said that the world has always been an ever-living fire, kindling in measures and going out in measures. (A flame keeps its shape while its fuel keeps changing. A living body does this too, and she also rebuilds herself while she does it.)
- **The Questions of King Milinda (Milindapanha), the chariot passage:** In this old Buddhist text, the monk Nagasena tells the king that 'chariot' is a name for the pole, the axle, the wheels and the rest put together. There is no chariot apart from the parts. ('Alive' may be like 'chariot', a name for a bundle of traits that come together, such as energy use, a skin, repair and copying. No single trait is the aliveness.)
- Science (settled): Living cells use energy to keep different amounts of salt and other chemicals inside than outside. When a cell dies, the difference fades.
- Science (debated): Nobody has an agreed definition of life. Whether life is one kind of thing with a real edge, or a bundle of traits with blurred edges, is still argued.
- Other view, Many biologists who use the NASA definition: There is a real line, and it is Darwinian evolution. A living thing belongs to a line of copies that can change over generations. Keeping warm or keeping a skin is useful, but a fridge does something like it too. Evolution is the test that no flame or fridge has passed.
- Practice, Find your own warmth (3 min): Sit still and hold one palm close to your mouth. Breathe out slowly on your palm and feel the warmth. Do this three times. Now put the same hand on your chest. Feel it rise and fall, and feel your heartbeat. Ask yourself what is working right now, without your asking.
- Ask: Is your warmth something you have, or something you are doing?
- Ask: If a thing can stay different from its surroundings without working at it, would you call it alive?
- Ask: A flame, a fridge and a bacterium each hold an inside different from the outside. What else would you need to see before you called one of them alive?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Warm water cools to match the room, and you do not. If staying different from the room is the work of being alive, what is happening to a body when she dies? (hint: Think about what you measured, and what stopped.)
- *deep:* A dry seed in a drawer shows no sign of life for a hundred years and then sprouts. Was the seed alive all that time, or is 'alive' something that only happens when a body is doing things? (hint: Think about what a seed can still do, and when we would call a seed dead.)
- *big:* A fire, a crystal and a virus each have some traits of life. If a new thing were found that had all the traits of life but was made of something else, should we call it alive? (hint: Think about which traits matter most to you, and why.)
- *big:* A bee sees a flower as a pattern of ultraviolet. A child sees it as a pink. Are they seeing the same flower differently, or two different flowers? Would a being from another world see us as alive? (hint: Think about the tests another being might use, and what they would measure.)
- *deep:* The water that turned the beetroot red was colourless a minute before. Where was the red before, and what kept it there? (hint: Think about the skin around each cell.)
- *big:* Your body replaces many of its atoms over months and years, yet you still call yourself you. What is it that stays the same, if not the atoms? (hint: Think about a flame or a river, whose pieces change but whose pattern goes on.)
- *big:* A cell with a lab-built genome has been made. If scientists one day build a whole cell from chemicals, who is its parent, and is it alive? (hint: Think about which of the tests it would pass.)
- *deep:* Scientists agreed about water only after they knew it was H2O. What might we have to discover before we can agree about life? (hint: Think about what a second kind of life, found on another world, might teach us.)

## Story: Salt That Grows
*A made-up story. Selvi, her mother Amudha and her grandmother Aachi are imagined. Sea salt really is made by letting sea water dry in shallow pans on the coast of Tamil Nadu, near Thoothukudi and in other places. Rain on salt that has not been covered is a real worry for salt workers, because it melts the salt back into water. A coconut really can sprout on the ground, with a shoot and roots coming out of the nut.*

At four in the morning, the salt pans were the colour of pewter. Selvi walked behind her mother with a wooden rake on her shoulder. On either side lay the shallow squares of sea water, and they still held the last of the stars.

By noon the squares would be white. The sun took the water away, and the salt stayed behind. Selvi's mother, Amudha, would rake the salt into long ridges. The women would carry it in flat baskets to the heaps near the road. That was the whole trick of the place. Sea in, sun on, salt out.

Selvi's grandmother, Aachi, had worked the pans for fifty years, and Aachi said salt was alive. 'It grows,' she said, sitting on an upturned basket with her feet in the warm brine. 'Drop one grain in a pan, and by the evening it is the size of a lentil. Break a lump in two, and each half grows on. Salt is the sea's child, and it has children of its own.'

Selvi liked this. But at school that week her teacher had given her a list of the signs of life. Living things take in food. They grow. They make more of their own kind. 'Salt does not eat, Aachi,' she said.

'It eats the sea,' said Aachi, and she laughed, because Selvi had no answer.

All day Selvi turned it over. That afternoon she took a steel plate of the strongest brine from the last pan. She dropped in a single grain and carried the plate home in the shade. By the next morning the grain had grown a small crown of new cubes. Selvi looked at it with her mother's magnifying glass. Every new cube was exactly like the old one. White, square, stacked on the same corners. There was nothing in that plate but salt and water, and the salt had simply picked up more salt.

'It just stacks,' she said to herself. 'Like putting one brick on another.'

That afternoon, a wind came from the east that smelled of wet tin. Amudha looked at the sky and said one word, and every woman on the pans began to run. Rain in the dry season was the thing they feared most. The heaps by the road were piled with a month of work, and rain would melt them back to water.

Selvi ran with the rest. They dragged the big palm-leaf mats over the heaps, and they weighed them down with stones. The first fat drops hit the pans like thrown pebbles. Aachi, who could not run any more, stood at the end of the dyke and called out the order of the heaps. One heap at the far end was never covered. It was too far and the rain came too fast.

The rain fell for an hour. When it stopped, the pans were silver sheets, and the uncovered heap was a grey puddle. Aachi stood looking at it for a long time. 'My salt,' she said quietly. 'Gone back to the sea.'

Next morning, Selvi walked out along the dyke to see what was left. By the side of the track lay a coconut that had fallen there months before. A week ago she had seen a pale shoot, as fat as her finger, poking out of one eye of the nut, and a few thin roots creeping into the mud. Now, after the rain, the shoot was a hand longer. And it was green.

Selvi crouched down beside it and stayed there until the sun was hot on her back.

The same rain had fallen on both. It had melted the salt, and the salt had gone into the water and stayed there as salt. The sea had taken back exactly what it had given. But the coconut had drunk the same rain, and the water had not stayed water. It had gone into a shoot. Nothing in that shoot was coconut water, or air, or the white flesh of the nut. Yet the shoot was made of all three. The salt had only added to itself. The coconut was making herself out of things that were not herself.

She ran back to the heaps. Aachi was sitting on her basket with her feet in the brine, which was already a little thicker as the sun came back.

'The salt grows,' Selvi said, out of breath. 'But it only grows with more salt. The coconut grows out of water and air and her own nut, none of which is coconut. That is the difference.'

Aachi was quiet. Then she lifted one hand from the brine and held it up in front of her face, thin and brown and creased with fifty years of sun.

'Fifty years of rice and fish and kanji,' she said slowly. 'And not one grain of it is still rice.' She laughed until she coughed. 'So I am a very old pile of rice porridge, and the salt in my tears is salt from these very pans, and it is still salt.'

'The salt just passes through you,' said Selvi. 'It does not turn into you.'

'Then perhaps,' said Aachi, 'someone will find a salt that does.' And she winked, and went back to watching the sun take the water out of the pan.

**For the parent:** A salt crystal grows by adding more salt that was already in the water. A coconut sprout grows by turning water, air and the nut's food into roots and leaves that are none of those things. Living things build their own bodies out of other stuff.

- Ask: A salt crystal in strong salt water gets bigger. A sprout in a pot gets bigger. How is the way each one gets bigger different?
- Ask: Salt can pass through a person's body and come out as salt. Rice cannot. What does that tell you about what happens to the rice?
- Ask: What would you have to see happen, to be sure that a crystal was making itself out of other things?

### g1 · Fool the Test: does the test give the right answer? (20 min)
- **rules:** One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and reads the front aloud. The reader does not show the back.; Every other player puts one token face down: Passes or Fails. Then everyone turns the tokens over together.; The reader reads the back. Every player whose token matches the first word of the back scores one point.; The reader then says if the test was fooled or fair. A player who guessed that before the reveal, and was right, scores one more point.; After each card, the reader writes F on a score sheet if the test was fooled, and a tick if it was fair. The reader's job passes to the player on the left.; If you play alone, you are every player. Guess, then read the back, and score one point for each right guess. You win if you score 30 or more out of 48.; The game ends when all 24 cards are read. The player with the most points wins. If there is a tie, the tied players share the win. Count the F marks too, to see how often tests are fooled.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a thing and a test on the front, the answer on the back:** A candle flame. Test: does it grow? (back: Passes. A flame gets bigger when you add more wick. A flame is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A candle flame. Test: does it use up food and air? (back: Passes. A flame uses up wax gas and air. A flame is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A candle flame. Test: can it spread to make more of itself? (back: Passes. A flame can light a new wick. A flame is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A salt crystal in strong salt water. Test: does it grow? (back: Passes. More salt from the water joins the crystal. A crystal is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A broken piece of a salt crystal. Test: can each piece keep growing on its own? (back: Passes. Each piece grows in strong salt water. A crystal is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A mule. Test: does she make young? (back: Fails. A mule is almost always sterile. A mule is alive, so this test is fooled.); A worker honeybee. Test: does she lay eggs? (back: Fails. A worker bee almost never lays eggs. The queen does. A worker bee is alive, so this test is fooled.); A dry moong seed in a jar. Test: does it move? (back: Fails. A dry seed does not move. A seed is alive and resting, so this test is fooled.); A dry moong seed in a jar. Test: does it use food and air? (back: Fails, almost. A resting seed uses almost nothing. A seed is alive and resting, so this test is fooled.); A date palm seed, 2,000 years old, in a drawer. Test: does it show any sign of life while it waits? (back: Fails. It showed no sign for 2,000 years, then sprouted in 2005. It was alive and resting, so this test is fooled.); A robot vacuum cleaner. Test: does it move around and sense walls? (back: Passes. It moves and senses. A robot is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A robot vacuum cleaner. Test: does it mend itself when a wheel breaks? (back: Fails. Someone has to repair it. A robot is not alive, so this test is fair.); A fridge. Test: does it keep its inside different from the outside? (back: Passes. A fridge keeps its inside cold. A fridge is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A child's body in a cool room. Test: does it stay near 37 degrees Celsius? (back: Passes. A body burns food to stay warm. A child is alive, so this test is fair.); A gecko on a cold morning. Test: is she warmer than the wall? (back: Fails. A gecko matches her surroundings. A gecko is alive, so this test is fooled.); A tumbler of warm water. Test: does it stay warm for an hour by itself? (back: Fails. The water cools to match the room. Water is not alive, so this test is fair.); A raw beetroot cube in cold water. Test: does the red stay inside the cells? (back: Passes. The cell skins hold the red inside. The cells are alive, so this test is fair.); A beetroot cube after boiling. Test: does the red stay inside the cells? (back: Fails. Heat breaks the cell skins. The cells are dead, so this test is fair.); A cold virus. Test: can it make copies of itself on its own? (back: Fails. A virus needs a living cell to copy. Whether a virus is alive is debated, so scientists disagree if this test is fair.); A bacterium in curd. Test: does she have a skin around her inside? (back: Passes. A bacterium has a cell membrane. A bacterium is alive, so this test is fair.); A forest fire. Test: does it move across the land? (back: Passes. A fire can spread fast. A fire is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A cloud. Test: does it grow? (back: Passes. A cloud can get bigger as water gathers in it. A cloud is not alive, so this test is fooled.); A tardigrade dried to a tiny barrel. Test: does she use energy? (back: Fails, almost. A dried tardigrade uses almost none. She wakes when water returns, so she is alive and this test is fooled.); A neem tree with a cut branch. Test: can the tree grow the branch back? (back: Passes. Trees regrow from the cut. A neem tree is alive, so this test is fair.)
- **Printable tokens: 8 tokens: four that say Passes and four that say Fails:** Passes; Fails; Passes; Fails; Passes; Fails; Passes; Fails
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the 8 tokens. Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile. Give each player a Passes and a Fails token.  
  *Parent says:* Each card names a thing and a test for life. You decide if the thing passes the test or fails it. Then we find out if the test gave the right answer.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take a Passes and a Fails token.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first three cards. The reader reads the front. Every other player puts one token face down. Turn the tokens over together. The reader reads the back.  
  *Parent says:* Read the front aloud. Everyone picks Passes or Fails, face down. Turn the tokens over together. Then the reader reads the back.  
  *Child mode:* Read a card. Pick a token. Turn the tokens over. Read the back.
  - Note: After the first reveal, ask your child what they notice. Keep the first rounds slow, so everyone sees how a round works.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep playing until all 24 cards are read. After each card, say if the test was fooled or fair. Write F or a tick on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. After each card, the reader says: fooled, or fair? Write down an F for fooled and a tick for fair.  
  *Child mode:* Keep playing. After each card, say fooled or fair. Write F or a tick.
  - Ask: Why did that test give a wrong answer? (good: Because a flame also does it.; Because a seed is resting.; Because the thing is an exception.)
  - Note: A test is fooled when it gives the wrong answer about whether something is alive. A thing that passes but is not alive fools the test. A thing that fails but is alive fools it too.
  - Check: A test says a mule is not alive because she makes no young. Is the test fooled or fair? → **Fooled, because a mule is alive**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count how many cards were fooled and how many were fair. Ask your child what they notice about the fooled cards. Then add up the scores.  
  *Parent says:* Count the F marks. How many tests were fooled? Which things fooled the tests most often? What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Count the F marks. Which things fooled the tests most? What do you notice?
  - Note: Flames, crystals, seeds, mules and robots fool the tests most. After your child has said what they notice, explain: that is why scientists use a bundle of tests together, and why they still argue about the hard cases.
  - Think: If most simple tests are fooled by something, what could you do to find out if something is alive?

## Experiments
### e1 · Beetroot Bleed: what does heat do to the skin of a living cell? (35 min)
- **question:** A raw beetroot keeps its red colour inside its cells. At what water temperature does the colour start to leak out?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Four glasses of water, from icy cold to very hot, will each get beetroot cubes. Which glass will turn the reddest, and which will stay the clearest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every glass has the same amount of water, the same number of cubes cut to the same size, the same rinse and the same waiting time. Only the water temperature changes, so any difference in colour comes from the temperature. The cold glass is the control. It shows what whole, unheated cells do.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the beetroot and pours the hot water. Keep children away from the kettle. Beetroot stains hands and clothes, so wear an old shirt.
- **record:** For each glass, write a colour score at 5 minutes and at 20 minutes. Zero is clear water and four is deep red. Then draw a bar for each glass, using the 20-minute score.
- **results table:** Glass | Water | Colour score after 5 minutes (0 to 4) | Colour score after 20 minutes (0 to 4)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the beetroot and the four glasses. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will put beetroot cubes in four glasses of water, from icy to very hot. Which glass will turn the reddest? Which will stay clearest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four glasses of water, from cold to hot. Which one turns reddest? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that glass will turn the reddest? (good: Hot water is stronger.; Heat makes things melt.; The red is stuck inside and heat lets it out.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write it down so your child can compare it later.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts eight cubes from a raw beetroot, each about one centimetre wide. Rinse the cubes under the tap for one minute.  
  *Parent says:* A knife breaks the cells on the cut faces, and red juice leaks out. Rinse the cubes until the water runs nearly clear. Then any red we see later comes from whole cells.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts eight small cubes. Rinse them under the tap for one minute.
  - Note: Cubes about one centimetre wide are best. If they are very small, much of the red leaks at once from cut cells. Pat the cubes dry on a tissue before dropping them in.
  - Check: Why do we rinse the cubes before the test? → **The cut cells leaked juice, and rinsing washes it off so we only see red from whole cells**
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Label four glasses A, B, C and D. Pour half a cup of water into each. A is cold from the fridge. B is tap water at room temperature. C is hand-hot, from a kettle mixed with tap water. D is very hot, from a just-boiled kettle.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up pours the hot water. A is cold, B is room water, C is as warm as a bath, and D is very hot. Each has the same amount.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours the water. A is cold. B is room water. C is warm. D is very hot.
  - Note: C should feel comfortably hot on the back of the wrist, about 50 degrees Celsius. D is hot enough to hurt, so keep your child's hands away. Put the white paper behind the glasses so the colours are easy to see.
  - Check: Why do all four glasses get the same amount of water and the same number of cubes? → **So only the temperature changes**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drop two cubes into each glass at the same moment. Start the timer. At 5 minutes and again at 20 minutes, look at each glass. Score each glass from 0 to 4. Zero is clear water. Four is deep red.  
  *Parent says:* Drop the cubes in all together. Watch the water. After 5 minutes, give each glass a score. Look again at 20 minutes. Which glass changed the most?  
  *Child mode:* Drop two cubes in each glass. Watch for 20 minutes. Give each glass a score from zero to four.
  - Ask: What do you notice in each glass? (good: The hot one went red fast.; The cold one stayed clear.; The warm one went a little pink.)
  - Note: Hold the white paper behind each glass to judge the colour. Typical results are glass A and B clear to very pale pink, glass C light pink, and glass D deep red within minutes. If every glass looks the same, your cubes were too small or not rinsed enough. Start again with bigger cubes and fresh water.
  - Check: Glass A stays clear and glass D turns deep red. What does this suggest? → **Heat damages whatever kept the red colour inside the cells**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar for each glass, using the 20-minute score. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each glass. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the red is kept inside each cell, and that heat damaged the skin around the cell so the red leaked out. After they have said what they saw, explain: every cell has a thin skin, called the cell membrane, made of fat and protein. Heat shakes it out of shape, and holes open. A living cell keeps its inside different from the outside, and a heated cell cannot.
  - Think: Boiled beetroot turns the cooking water red. Now you know why. What had happened to the cells?
### e2 · Staying Warm: who keeps their own temperature? (40 min)
- **question:** After half an hour in the same room, which stays warm: a tumbler of warm water, a bottle of warm water wrapped in a thick sock, or you?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Over half an hour, which will cool down the most, and which will cool down the least? Do you think your own temperature will change? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The tumbler and the bottle are filled from the same jug of warm water at the same time, and all three sit in the same room. You measure them at the same times, using the same thermometer. Only what is being measured changes: open water, wrapped water and a living child. The open tumbler is the control.
- **safety:** Never use a glass thermometer with silver liquid. Use water no hotter than a warm bath, about 42 degrees Celsius at most. A grown-up pours the warm water. Wipe the thermometer tip with a clean tissue between uses, and wash it with soap and water at the end.
- **record:** Write the temperature of the tumbler and the bottle every 5 minutes for 30 minutes. Write your child's temperature at the start, at 15 minutes and at 30 minutes. Then draw one line for each.
- **results table:** Time | Tumbler (degrees Celsius) | Wrapped bottle (degrees Celsius) | Child (degrees Celsius)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the tumbler, the wrapped bottle and the thermometer. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will watch warm water and you for half an hour in the same room. Who will cool the most? Who will cool the least? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Warm water in a tumbler. Warm water in a wrapped bottle. And you. Who cools most? Why?
  - Ask: Do you think your temperature will go down, up, or stay about the same? Why? (good: It will stay the same because I am alive.; It will go down like the water.; My body makes heat.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit your child down quietly. Wipe the thermometer tip. Put it under one armpit with the arm pressed down. Wait for the beep. Write the number as time zero.  
  *Parent says:* Sit quietly and press your arm down on the thermometer. Wait for the beep. This is your starting temperature.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Put the thermometer under your arm. Press down. Wait for the beep.
  - Note: A normal temperature under the arm is about 36 to 37 degrees Celsius. Children are often a little warmer after running about, so let your child sit for five minutes first.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up pours warm water from one jug into the tumbler and into the bottle. Screw the cap on the bottle and wrap it in the sock. Measure both and write the starting numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Warm water goes into both from the same jug, so they start the same. The sock goes round the bottle. Measure each one and write the numbers.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours warm water in both. Wrap the bottle in a sock. Measure both.
  - Note: The water should be about 40 to 42 degrees Celsius, as warm as a comfortable bath. To measure the bottle, open the cap, put the thermometer tip in, then close the cap again and keep it wrapped. Wrap the thermometer tip in cling film if it is not made for water.
  - Check: Why do we fill the tumbler and the bottle from the same jug at the same time? → **So both start at the same temperature**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At 5, 10 and 15 minutes, measure the tumbler and the bottle and write the numbers. At 15 minutes, measure your child under the arm too.  
  *Parent says:* Measure the tumbler and the bottle every five minutes. At 15 minutes, measure yourself too. Sit quietly in between.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the tumbler and the bottle every five minutes. At 15 minutes, measure yourself.
  - Note: Keep the room still, away from a fan pointing at the tumbler. Your child must sit still and not run about, because exercise warms the body.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At 20, 25 and 30 minutes, measure the tumbler and the bottle and write the numbers. At 30 minutes, measure your child under the arm again.  
  *Parent says:* Keep measuring every five minutes. At 30 minutes, measure yourself one last time. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Keep measuring every five minutes. At 30 minutes, measure yourself again.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the three sets of numbers? (good: The water went down.; The sock bottle went down more slowly.; My number stayed about the same.)
  - Note: Typical results in a room near 28 to 30 degrees Celsius are the tumbler falling by 5 to 8 degrees, the wrapped bottle falling by 2 to 4 degrees, and the child changing by half a degree or less. In a very hot room the water falls less. Move to the coolest room, because the test needs a room cooler than your body.
  - Check: Imagine the water went from 41 to 34 degrees, and the child went from 36.6 to 36.7 degrees. What does this show? → **The water drifted towards the room's temperature, and the child's body worked to stay warm**
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw one line for each on the same graph. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then ask the last question.  
  *Parent says:* Draw three lines. Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line for each. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says the water drifted towards the room's warmth, the sock slowed the drift but did not stop it, and the child stayed steady. After they have said what they saw, explain: a living body burns food to make heat, and uses sweat, shivering and blood flow to keep steady. A gecko, a frog, a fish and a plant mostly match their surroundings, and they are alive. So 'stays warmer than the room' is a test that works for birds and mammals, and gives the wrong answer for many living things. Cold-blooded animals still use energy every second to keep other things steady inside their cells.
  - Think: A gecko on a cold morning is as cold as the wall. If you measured her, what would the thermometer say? Would you call her not alive?
### e3 · Waking Up: do older seeds sprout less often? (33 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Seeds can wait a long time. Does a seed that has waited longer sprout less often, and how much less?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. You will have groups of seeds of different ages. Which group will have the most sprouts, and how many out of 10 will sprout in the oldest group? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every group has the same kind of whole seed, the same number of seeds, the same plate, the same damp tissue and the same place and time. Only the age of the seeds changes. The youngest seeds are the control. Use the date on the packet for the age.
- **safety:** Whole seeds are a choking risk for children under five. Throw away any seeds with mould. Wash your hands after touching damp seeds.
- **record:** Count the sprouts in each group on day 3 and day 4. Then draw a bar for each group.
- **results table:** Group | Age of the seeds | Sprouted on day 3 (out of 10) | Sprouted on day 4 (out of 10)
- **schedule:** day 1: Find seeds of different ages, guess, and set up the plates. (18 min); day 3: Count the sprouts on each plate. Keep the tissue damp. (4 min); day 4: Count again, draw the bars, and say what the evidence shows. (11 min)
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find whole seeds of the same kind in 3 or 4 packets or jars of different ages. Write the date on a label for each. Ask your child for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Look through the kitchen for the same kind of whole seed, bought at different times. Write the date on each label. Which will sprout best? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Find seeds of the same kind from different times. Label each. Which will sprout best? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the oldest seeds will do worst? (good: They have been waiting too long.; They are dried out.; They have used up their food.)
  - Note: Use whole seeds, never split dal, because split dal cannot sprout. If you only have one packet, borrow seeds from a neighbour or buy a fresh packet of the same kind. Some shop seeds are heated or treated, so a group may not sprout at all. That is a result too. Write down what you find.
  - Check: Why do all four groups use the same kind of seed? → **So only the age changes**
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wet the tissue and lay it on each plate. Count out ten seeds from the first packet onto the first plate. Do the same for each packet. Label each plate.  
  *Parent says:* Wet the tissue so it is damp but not dripping. Put ten seeds on each plate, spread out. Check each plate has the right label.  
  *Child mode:* Wet the tissue. Put ten seeds on each plate. Label each plate.
  - Note: Damp, not soaking. Seeds that sit in water can rot instead of sprouting. Keep the plates in the shade, in the same place, and away from the fridge. Rewet the tissue each day if it dries.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the plates. Count the sprouts on each plate and write the number in the table. Keep the tissue damp.  
  *Parent says:* Look at each plate. A sprout is a seed with a small white root. Count them and write the numbers. Put the plates back.  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouts on each plate. Write the numbers. Keep the tissue damp.
  - Note: A sprout means a small white root, at least as long as the seed is wide. Do not remove sprouts from the plate.
  - Wait: Day 3
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the sprouts on each plate again. Write the numbers. Draw a bar for each group.  
  *Parent says:* Count the sprouts again. Draw a bar for each group. Which group did best? Which did worst?  
  *Child mode:* Count again. Draw a bar for each group. Which did best?
  - Check: The youngest seeds sprouted 9 out of 10 and the oldest only 2 out of 10. What is the best claim? → **In this test, the older seeds sprouted less often**
  - Wait: Day 4
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then ask the last question.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that more of the older seeds did not wake, that a few did, and that the seeds are alive but resting. After they have said what they saw, explain: a dormant seed uses almost no energy, but the seed's tiny store of repair work still runs, and over time damage builds up inside the seed. In the right conditions, such as very dry and cool, damage builds up slowly. The 2,000-year-old date palm seed was kept dry in a dry place. Most kitchen seeds are kept in warm, humid kitchens, so they lose their ability to wake much faster.
  - Think: A date palm seed about 2,000 years old sprouted. How could one seed wait so long when your old seeds did worse?
  - Wait: Day 4

## Activities
### a1 · Break Your Own Rule: write a test for life and fool it (25 min)
- **record:** A table with eight rows, one for each case. In each row write what your rule says, what is really true, and whether they match. Then write your rule before and after you mended it.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Give your child a sheet of paper and a pencil. Ask them to finish the sentence 'Something is alive if it...'. Write their rule at the top.  
  *Parent says:* Finish this sentence in your own words. 'Something is alive if it...'. Write the rule you think is best.  
  *Child mode:* Finish this sentence. Something is alive if it... Write your best rule.
  - Ask: Why did you pick that rule? (good: Because living things always do it.; Because it is what animals do.; Because it is what my teacher said.)
  - Note: Take any rule they give. Common rules are 'it moves', 'it grows', 'it eats', 'it breathes' or 'it has babies'. Write the rule exactly as they say it.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the eight cases one at a time. The cases are a candle flame, a salt crystal growing in brine, a mule and a dry seed in a jar. The rest are a worker bee, a robot vacuum cleaner, a virus and a person asleep. For each, ask what the rule says. Then say what is really true.  
  *Parent says:* Here are eight cases. For each one, use your rule to say alive or not alive. Then I will tell you what scientists say. Do they match?  
  *Child mode:* Use your rule on each case. Alive or not alive? Then check if you were right.
  - Ask: Which case was the hardest for your rule? (good: The seed.; The flame.; The mule.)
  - Note: What is really true: the flame is not alive. It uses fuel and air, grows and spreads. The salt crystal is not alive. It grows by adding salt from the brine. The mule is alive, though almost never able to have young. The dry seed is alive but resting. The worker bee is alive, though workers almost never lay eggs. The robot vacuum cleaner is not alive. It moves and senses, but someone built it and charges it. A virus is debated. It copies and evolves, but only inside a living cell. The sleeping person is alive. Count how many cases the rule got right.
  - Check: Your rule says 'alive means it moves'. A dry seed does not move, but is alive. What does this show? → **The rule has an exception, so it needs mending**

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A test with an exception is still useful for most cases. Scientists keep it, mend it or use it together with other tests.
6. (write) Explain why a fridge is not alive, even though it keeps its inside different from the outside, using the words 'build' and 'energy'.  
   **Answer:** A fridge needs energy from electricity to stay cold, and so does a living body. But a fridge cannot build its own parts from other materials or mend itself. People built it and people repair it.. *Why:* Keeping an inside different takes energy in both. Only a living thing builds and mends itself.
7. (match) Match each term to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Metabolism → chemical changes that turn food into energy and body parts; Homeostasis → keeping something, such as warmth, steady; Dormant → alive but resting; Regeneration → growing back a lost part. *Why:* These four words describe different parts of how a living thing keeps going.
8. (fill) Koshland listed seven pillars of life. The pillar for the skin around a cell, which keeps each chemical reaction in its own place, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['compartmentalization', 'compartments', 'compartment', 'compartmentalisation']. *Why:* A compartment is a space with its own boundary. A cell membrane makes the compartment that keeps the inside different from the outside.
9. (sort) Sort each case by what the test 'it moves' says about it.  
   **Answer:** a crow → Moves, and alive; a forest fire → Moves, and not alive; a dry seed in a jar → Does not move, and alive; a robot vacuum cleaner → Moves, and not alive; a dried tardigrade → Does not move, and alive; a neem tree in still air → Does not move, and alive. *Why:* 'It moves' is wrong for the fire and the robot, which move and are not alive. It is also wrong for the seed, the tardigrade and the tree, which are alive and do not move much.
10. (mcq) Carol Cleland says it may be too early to define life. What is her reason?  
   Options: Nobody has ever studied life / People used the word 'water' for thousands of years before anyone knew water was H2O, so a good definition of life may need a theory we do not yet have / Life cannot be measured  
   **Answer:** People used the word 'water' for thousands of years before anyone knew water was H2O, so a good definition of life may need a theory we do not yet have. *Why:* Cleland compares life with water. A good definition of water had to wait for chemistry. A good definition of life may have to wait for a theory of life.
11. (draw) Draw a salt crystal growing, and a bean sprout growing, side by side. Use arrows to show what each one takes in and what it becomes.  
   **Answer:** The crystal has arrows from salt in the water into new cubes on the outside. The sprout has arrows from water, air and food into new roots and leaves, which are different from what went in.. *Why:* A crystal adds more of the same stuff. A sprout turns other stuff into new roots and leaves. A strong drawing shows the arrows going into the same thing for the crystal, and into a new thing for the sprout.
12. (mcq) Which of these would be the strongest evidence that a pattern in a computer was alive?  
   Options: It moves across the screen / It makes a copy of itself / It builds its own parts from raw materials, repairs itself, and its copies change in ways that are passed on and help it survive  
   **Answer:** It builds its own parts from raw materials, repairs itself, and its copies change in ways that are passed on and help it survive. *Why:* Moving and copying are passed by flames and crystals too. Building and repairing itself from other materials, and evolving, are much harder to fake.
13. (write) Predict: you heat beetroot cubes to only 40 degrees Celsius instead of boiling water. What would the water look like after 20 minutes? Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The water would be only a little pink, much less than from boiling water. At 40 degrees the cell skins are not badly damaged, so only a little red leaks out. Boiling damages most of them.. *Why:* Heat damages cell skins more as it gets hotter. A moderate temperature damages a few, and a very high temperature damages most.
14. (truefalse) A dried tardigrade uses a lot of energy while it waits.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A dried tardigrade uses almost no energy. When water returns, she begins to use energy again.
15. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Thermostat, cell membrane, skin sensing cold, brick.  
   **Answer:** Brick, because a brick does nothing to keep anything steady. The other three each help to keep something steady.. *Why:* A thermostat, a cell membrane and skin each play a part in keeping an inside steady or different. A brick does not.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A candle flame uses fuel, needs air and grows. Why do scientists still say it is not alive?  
   Options: It builds nothing, mends nothing and passes on no instructions. It is fuel burning / It is too hot / It does not move  
   **Answer:** It builds nothing, mends nothing and passes on no instructions. It is fuel burning. *Why:* A flame passes several tests for life, but it is only a burning. Nothing in it is built or mended, and a new flame carries no inherited instructions.
2. (mcq) A mule almost never has young, but a mule is alive. What does this show about the test 'living things make young'?  
   Options: Mules are not alive / It applies to a whole kind across many generations, not to each single body / The test is perfect  
   **Answer:** It applies to a whole kind across many generations, not to each single body. *Why:* A single body can be alive without having young. The test describes a kind of living thing over time.
3. (mcq) Warm water in a tumbler sits in a cool room for half an hour next to a child. What happens?  
   Options: Both stay as warm as they began / The child cools more than the water / The water's temperature drifts towards the room's, while the child's temperature stays almost the same  
   **Answer:** The water's temperature drifts towards the room's, while the child's temperature stays almost the same. *Why:* Left alone, warm water matches the room. A living body keeps working to stay warm.
4. (mcq) You heat a raw beetroot cube in very hot water and the water turns red. What happened to the cells?  
   Options: The cells grew larger / Heat damaged the skin of the cells, so the red leaked out / The beetroot produced more red  
   **Answer:** Heat damaged the skin of the cells, so the red leaked out. *Why:* The cell membrane keeps the pigment inside. Heat shakes it out of shape, and the pigment leaks out.
5. (mcq) What does the cell membrane do?  
   Options: It makes food for the cell / It stops the cell from moving / It controls what goes into the cell and what stays in  
   **Answer:** It controls what goes into the cell and what stays in. *Why:* The membrane is a thin skin around the cell. It lets some things pass and keeps other things in.
6. (mcq) A dry seed shows no sign of life for months. What is the best way to describe the seed?  
   Options: Dormant: alive but resting / Dead / Neither alive nor dead, like a stone  
   **Answer:** Dormant: alive but resting. *Why:* A dormant seed uses almost no energy but can wake. A stone cannot.
7. (mcq) Why did people find the tobacco mosaic virus crystals so surprising?  
   Options: They were the biggest crystals ever seen / They were red like beetroot / The crystals could still make plants sick, even though they were crystals  
   **Answer:** The crystals could still make plants sick, even though they were crystals. *Why:* Stanley's crystals infected plants when they were dissolved again. It showed that a germ could be a chemical that forms crystals.
8. (mcq) Which is the best reason that scientists disagree about whether viruses are alive?  
   Options: No one has seen a virus / A virus copies and evolves, but cannot use energy or copy itself without a host cell / Viruses are too large to study  
   **Answer:** A virus copies and evolves, but cannot use energy or copy itself without a host cell. *Why:* A virus passes some tests and fails others, so the answer depends on which tests you choose.
9. (mcq) A salt crystal grows in strong salt water. How is its growth different from a sprout's?  
   Options: The crystal adds more of the salt that was already in the water, and the sprout turns other things into new roots and leaves / The crystal grows more slowly / The sprout does not use energy  
   **Answer:** The crystal adds more of the salt that was already in the water, and the sprout turns other things into new roots and leaves. *Why:* A crystal stacks more of the same. A living thing builds its body out of other materials.
10. (mcq) You test your rule 'alive means it moves' on a dry seed, and the rule gives the wrong answer. What is the best next step?  
   Options: Decide the seed is dead / Never write another rule / Mend the rule and test it again  
   **Answer:** Mend the rule and test it again. *Why:* Scientists mend a rule after a hard case breaks it, and then test again.
11. (mcq) Two groups of seeds are tested. 9 of 10 fresh seeds sprout, and 2 of 10 old seeds sprout. What is the best claim?  
   Options: All old seeds are dead / In this test, the older seeds sprouted less often / Fresh seeds are always better  
   **Answer:** In this test, the older seeds sprouted less often. *Why:* A claim should only say what the evidence shows. Two old seeds sprouted, so not all old seeds are dead.
12. (mcq) A fridge keeps its inside colder than the kitchen. Why is a fridge still not alive?  
   Options: Someone built it and powers it, and it cannot build or mend its own parts from other materials / A fridge is too cold / A fridge does not use energy  
   **Answer:** Someone built it and powers it, and it cannot build or mend its own parts from other materials. *Why:* A fridge and a living body both hold an inside different from the outside using energy. Only a living thing builds and mends itself.

## Applications
- Space agencies decide which tests to put on a probe that looks for life on Mars or on moons such as Europa.
- Doctors use heat to kill germs, because heat breaks the skin of bacterial cells in the same way as it breaks beetroot cells.
- Seed banks keep seeds dry and cold so they stay alive for many years.
- Hospitals use fridges and thermostats to keep medicines and blood at a steady, safe temperature.
- Food makers heat milk and juice to kill microbes, a process named after Louis Pasteur.
- Farmers test the seed they buy by sprouting a few, to check how many will wake.
- Gardeners grow new plants from cuttings, using the way a stem can start again.
- Biologists build cells with lab-made genomes, to learn which genes a cell really needs.

## Open questions
- **Is there one definition of life that works for every case?** Every definition so far has a hard case that bends it. Some scientists think there is a real answer to find, and others think there is only a bundle of traits.
- **Are viruses alive?** Viruses copy and evolve but need a host cell for energy and copying. Scientists disagree about which tests should count most.
- **Could there be life that is not made of cells, carbon or water?** We know only one kind of life, from one start on Earth. We cannot yet say what else would count.
- **What does every gene in the simplest cell do?** In a very small lab-built cell with 473 genes, about 149 of them had no known job. Even the simplest known cell is not fully understood.
- **How long can a seed or a tiny animal wait and still wake up?** Plants have woken from thousands of years in ice and in dry places, but nobody knows the limit, or what exactly limits it.
- **How did the first living thing begin from non-living chemicals?** Many ideas exist, but no one has shown how it happened, or made it happen in a laboratory.

## Connected maths
- In the warm-water test, the tumbler falls from 41 to 34 degrees Celsius in 30 minutes. A child's temperature goes from 36.6 to 36.7 degrees. How many degrees did each change? Which changed more, and by how many degrees? **The water fell by 7 degrees. The child rose by 0.1 degrees. The water changed 6.9 degrees more.**. 41 take away 34 is 7. 36.7 take away 36.6 is 0.1. 7 take away 0.1 is 6.9. So the water changed 6.9 degrees more than the child. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.L4`)
- A tumbler of water falls 2 degrees every 5 minutes for the first 30 minutes, from 41 to 29 degrees. The room is 29 degrees. Would you expect it to fall another 12 degrees in the next 30 minutes? Explain. **No. It would stay close to 29 degrees.**. The water has reached the room's temperature of 29 degrees. Warm water drifts towards the room's temperature and stops there. It cannot go on falling 2 degrees every 5 minutes, because a pattern in numbers can stop when something stops it. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In the beetroot test, the colour scores after 20 minutes are glass A 1, glass B 1, glass C 2 and glass D 4. Glass D scores how many times as much as glass A? If you added the four scores, what is the total? **4 times as much. The total is 8.**. 4 divided by 1 is 4, so glass D scored 4 times as much as glass A. 1 plus 1 plus 2 plus 4 is 8. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In the seed test, a group of 10 fresh seeds has 9 sprouts, a group of 10 seeds from last year has 8, a group from three years ago has 5, and the oldest group has 2. What percentage of each group sprouted? Between which two groups did the number fall the most? **90 percent, 80 percent, 50 percent and 20 percent. The biggest fall was between the group from last year and the group from three years ago.**. Each group has 10 seeds, so 9 out of 10 is 90 out of 100, or 90 percent. 8 is 80 percent, 5 is 50 percent and 2 is 20 percent. The falls are 10, 30 and 30 percentage points. The 30-point falls happen from last year to three years ago, and from three years ago to the oldest. Both are the biggest. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A student wants to show the seed results from the Waking Up test. Should she use a bar chart or a line graph for four groups of seeds of different ages? Which tells the story better? Explain your choice. **A bar chart shows each group clearly. A line graph can also show the fall with age. Both work, but a line graph shows the trend.**. A bar chart has one bar for each group, so it is easy to compare the groups. A line graph joins the points, and shows how the number falls as the seeds get older. Because age is a number that goes up, the line shows the trend best. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- Each seed has about a 50 percent chance of sprouting. Out of 20 seeds, how many would you expect to sprout? One group gets 7. Is that surprising? Explain. **You would expect 10. Getting 7 is not very surprising.**. Half of 20 is 10. Chance means the real number can be a bit more or less, so 7 or 13 would not be surprising. Getting 0 or 20 would be very surprising. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- A date palm seed that was about 2,000 years old sprouted in the year 2005. About which year was it from? Why do we say 'about'? **About the year 5 of the Common Era. We say about because the age was measured, not written down.**. 2005 take away 2,000 is 5. The age came from measuring a kind of carbon in the seed, which has a margin of error of a few decades. So the seed's real age could be a little more or less. (queues `math.time.calendar.L4`)
- Imagine a crystal grows 2 millimetres thicker every day. A bacterium divides every 20 minutes, and none die. In 3 hours, how many times does the bacterium divide, and how many bacteria are there? How many millimetres does the crystal grow in the same 3 hours? **The bacterium divides 9 times and there are 512 bacteria. The crystal grows 0.25 millimetres.**. 3 hours is 180 minutes. 180 divided by 20 is 9, so the count doubles 9 times. 2 times itself 9 times is 512. 3 hours is one eighth of a day, so the crystal grows 2 divided by 8, which is 0.25 millimetres. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
