# Using the Sun's heat and light: the solar cooker, the solar water heater and the solar cell
`sci.energy.using-the-sun.L3`

**Summary.** A steel tumbler of water left in the noon sun gets warm, but it never boils. Put the same tumbler in a black box under a clear lid, and the water can get hot enough to cook rice. The box has no flame and no wire, so where does the extra heat come from? In this lesson you build a simple solar cooker and find out how a solar cooker, a solar water heater and a solar cell each use sunlight. Then you follow the same sunlight into a basking lizard, a leaf, the whole Earth and your own body.

**Intro.** *Hold the back of your hand in a patch of sunlight for ten slow breaths. Then move your hand into the shade. Nothing touched your hand. So what warmed it, and where did the warmth go?* Sunlight crossed 15 crore kilometres of empty space and ended its journey on your skin. There it turned into heat. In the shade the heat left your hand again, into the air. People, lizards, butterflies and plants all use this arriving energy. Today you will build a box that lets sunlight in easily and lets heat out slowly. Then you will ask an odd question about every hot thing you know: is it hot because heat is coming in fast, or because heat is leaving slowly?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that sunlight carries energy, and name three ways my family uses the Sun's heat and light on an ordinary day.
1. I can build a simple solar cooker from a box, black paper, a clear cover and foil, and say what each part does.
2. I can explain why water in the box gets hotter than water in the open, by comparing the heat coming in with the heat leaving.
3. I can explain how a solar water heater heats water and why its tank sits above its tubes.
4. I can say how a solar cell differs from a solar cooker: a cell turns light straight into electricity and does not need heat.
5. I can give one strength and one limit of using the Sun, and name one question about sunlight or energy that nobody can answer yet.

## Terms
- **energy**: A number that scientists work out for light, heat, movement and electricity. When things change, the total stays the same. Nobody can yet say what energy is, only how it behaves. *e.g.* Sunlight warms your hand. The light's energy turns into the hand's heat, and the two add up to the same total.
- **heat**: Energy that moves from a hotter thing to a cooler thing. *e.g.* A steel tumbler of hot tea warms your hands, because heat flows from the tumbler into them.
- **infrared**: A kind of light that our eyes cannot see. We feel it as warmth. *e.g.* Hold your hand beside a warm tava without touching it. The warmth you feel is infrared.
- **watt**: A measure of how fast energy arrives or is used. *e.g.* A small LED bulb uses about 10 watts. Noon sunlight brings about 1,000 watts to every square metre of ground.
- **square metre**: The size of a square that is 1 metre long and 1 metre wide. *e.g.* Four floor tiles, each 50 centimetres wide, laid in a big square cover one square metre.
- **vapour**: Water that has turned into a gas that mixes with the air. We cannot see it. *e.g.* Wet clothes on a line dry because their water slowly leaves as vapour.
- **absorb**: To take in. A surface that absorbs light keeps its energy, and the energy turns into heat. *e.g.* Black tar absorbs most of the sunlight that falls on it, so it is too hot to walk on at noon.
- **reflect**: To send light away again without keeping it. *e.g.* Kitchen foil reflects most of the sunlight that lands on it, so it stays cooler than black paper.
- **temperature**: A number that says how hot or cold something is. *e.g.* Bath-warm water is about 40 degrees Celsius. Water boils at about 100 degrees Celsius.
- **thermometer**: A tool that measures temperature. *e.g.* A kitchen thermometer dipped in a steel tumbler of tea shows how hot the tea is.
- **solar box cooker**: A box that cooks food with sunlight. It has a black inside, a clear cover and a shiny flap. *e.g.* The box you build for the Sun Box test is a small solar box cooker.
- **insulator**: A material that heat passes through only slowly. *e.g.* A woollen sweater and a lunch bag lined with foam are insulators. They keep a warm thing warm for longer.
- **focus**: The one small place where a curved mirror brings light together. *e.g.* The pot of a dish cooker sits at the focus of its curved mirror, where the light of the whole dish gathers.
- **parabola**: A smooth curve, open on one side, like the path of a thrown ball. A mirror in this shape sends all straight rays to one point. *e.g.* A satellite television dish is a parabola. Its receiver sits at the focus.
- **steam**: Water that has turned into a very hot gas, which forms when water boils. *e.g.* Steam rises from a rice pot and from idlis in a steamer.
- **solar water heater**: A rooftop device that heats water with sunlight and keeps the hot water in a wrapped tank. *e.g.* Many homes in Bengaluru and Pune get their bathing water from a solar water heater on the roof.
- **electricity**: Energy carried by tiny charged particles that move along a wire. *e.g.* The electricity that lights a bulb is a flow of tiny particles through the wire.
- **electron**: A tiny charged particle found in every atom. Electrons moving along a wire are what we call electric current. *e.g.* In the wire of a lit bulb, billions upon billions of electrons are moving at the same time.
- **solar cell**: A thin slice of material, usually silicon, that gives an electric current when light falls on it. *e.g.* The dark strip above the screen of a pocket calculator is a solar cell.
- **silicon**: A grey material made from quartz sand. It is used in solar cells and in computer chips. *e.g.* Most sand on the beach holds quartz, the mineral that silicon is made from.
- **solar panel**: Many solar cells joined side by side under a sheet of glass. *e.g.* Solar panels on the roof of Kochi airport in Kerala make electricity from sunlight all day.
- **photon**: A tiny packet of light energy. A beam of light is a stream of photons. *e.g.* When you switch on a torch, an enormous number of photons leave it every second.
- **current**: The flow of electrons along a wire. *e.g.* A fan runs while current flows through its wire, and stops when you switch the current off.
- **battery**: A container that stores electric energy to use later. *e.g.* A solar lantern's battery fills up by day and lights the lantern in the evening.
- **solar park**: A large area covered with solar panels that send electricity into the wires of a region. *e.g.* Bhadla Solar Park in the Thar desert of Rajasthan covers about 56 square kilometres.
- **basking**: Sitting in the Sun to warm the body. *e.g.* A garden lizard basks on a wall in the morning and goes into the shade once warm enough.
- **molecule**: A tiny group of atoms joined together. Every kind of thing is made of molecules or atoms. *e.g.* One molecule of water has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom joined together.
- **hydrogen**: The lightest kind of atom. The Sun is mostly hydrogen. *e.g.* Every drop of water has hydrogen in it, joined to oxygen.
- **helium**: The second lightest kind of atom. It is made when hydrogen joins together in the Sun's centre. *e.g.* Helium fills the balloons that float up at a fair, because it is lighter than air.
- **neutrino**: A very tiny particle made in the Sun's centre that passes through almost everything. *e.g.* About 6,000 crore neutrinos from the Sun pass through your fingernail every second, and you feel none of them.
- **eclipse**: A time when the Moon or the Earth hides the Sun or the Moon from view. *e.g.* In a total solar eclipse, the Moon covers the whole of the Sun for a few minutes and the day goes dim.
- **corona**: The Sun's outermost layer of thin, glowing gas. It is hotter than 10 lakh degrees Celsius. *e.g.* During a total solar eclipse, the corona shows as a pearly white halo around the dark Moon.
- **fossil fuel**: A fuel such as coal, oil or gas that formed from the remains of living beings buried for millions of years. *e.g.* Cooking gas in a cylinder is a fossil fuel. It holds sunlight that plants caught long ago.
- **ultraviolet**: A kind of light that our eyes cannot see. It has more energy than violet light. *e.g.* Ultraviolet in sunlight tans and burns skin. It also helps to kill germs in clear water.
- **chloroplast**: A tiny green part inside a plant cell. It uses light to make sugar from air and water. *e.g.* The green of a leaf is the colour of the chloroplasts inside its cells.
- **pigment**: A coloured substance that absorbs some colours of light and reflects the rest. *e.g.* A green leaf reflects green light and absorbs mostly red and blue light. Its pigment is called chlorophyll.

## Explanations
### Sunlight carries energy from the Sun to the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Sunlight is energy that travels from the Sun to the Earth as light. Some of it we can see, and some we cannot see.

The Sun is about 15 crore kilometres away, and the space in between is almost empty. Sunlight crosses that space in about 8 minutes. When it lands on your skin, your skin gets warm. That warming shows that sunlight carries energy. Part of sunlight is the light our eyes see. Part is infrared, a kind of light our eyes cannot see. Infrared carries about half of the Sun's energy that reaches the ground. Our skin feels sunlight only as warmth, after the light has been taken in. So what do people do with all this arriving energy?
- e.g. A black scooter seat parked in the sun at noon is too hot to sit on. No flame and no wire ever touched it.
- e.g. On a clear winter morning in Delhi or Shillong the air is cold, yet your face feels warm the moment the Sun's light falls on it.
### How much energy arrives: about 1,000 watts on every square metre at noon · *defined*
**Definition:** A watt measures how quickly energy arrives or is used. At noon on a clear day, about 1,000 watts of sunlight fall on each square metre of ground that faces the Sun.

A watt tells you how quickly energy is arriving or being used. A small LED bulb uses about 10 watts. Now mark a square on a sunny terrace, 1 metre long and 1 metre wide. At noon on a clear day, sunlight brings about 1,000 watts to that square. That is as much as 100 of those bulbs use. In the early morning, or under cloud, far less arrives, because the light passes through more air or is scattered by the cloud. Every way of using the Sun starts from this number. A bigger catching area catches more watts. What happens to those watts when they land?
- e.g. A rooftop solar panel is about 2 square metres. At noon about 2,000 watts of sunlight fall on it.
- e.g. A newspaper spread open in the sun covers about half a square metre, so about 500 watts fall on it at noon.
### People have used the Sun's heat and light directly for thousands of years · *defined*
**Definition:** Using the Sun directly means letting sunlight itself do a job, such as drying, warming or lighting, with no fuel and no machine.

Long before solar panels, families used sunlight to do work. Wet clothes on a line dry because sunlight gives the water the energy to leave as vapour. Vadam, papad, red chillies and fish are dried in the sun so that they keep for months. At the salt pans of Thoothukudi and Kutch, sunlight dries sea water until only salt is left. A window is a way of using the Sun's light, so no lamp is needed by day. None of these needs a box or a wire. They all need time, open sky and someone who knows the Sun's hours. Could sunlight also do a hotter job, such as cooking rice?
- e.g. One litre of sea water holds about 35 grams of salt. In a salt pan the Sun removes the water and leaves the salt behind.
- e.g. Mango pickle jars are set in the sun for some days in many homes, from Andhra Pradesh to Punjab.
- **Engineers who design solar machines:** They often begin the story of solar energy with the first solar cell in 1954. On that view, solar technology is new.
- **Women who dry food and salt workers across India:** They choose the days, spread the food thin, turn it, and cover it from dew and crows. This is skilled solar work that is thousands of years old, and it is seldom called technology.
### Sunlight turns into heat only where it is absorbed · *defined*
**Definition:** To absorb light is to take it in. To reflect light is to send it away again. Light that a surface absorbs turns into heat in that surface.

Light that lands on a surface can do two things. The surface can absorb it, which means take it in. Or the surface can reflect it, which means send it away again. Absorbed light turns into heat. Reflected light keeps travelling as light and heats nothing here. A dull black surface absorbs nearly all the light that falls on it. A shiny surface, such as kitchen foil, reflects most of it. So a person building a solar cooker uses both. Black goes where the heat is wanted. Shiny goes where light must be sent somewhere else. But a black pot in the open sun still does not boil. Why not?
- e.g. Walk barefoot from a white painted line on a road to the black tar beside it at noon. The tar is far hotter under the same sunlight.
- e.g. Many terraces in Chennai and Ahmedabad are painted white so that the rooms below stay cooler.
- **A bee or a butterfly:** Many insects see ultraviolet light, which we cannot see. A flower that looks plain white to us may absorb ultraviolet in patches and look patterned to a bee. So 'black' and 'white' describe what our eyes receive, and other eyes sort surfaces differently.
- **Physicists measuring infrared:** Fresh snow reflects most visible light, yet it absorbs infrared very well. A surface can be 'white' for one kind of light and 'black' for another.
### A thing gets hotter only while heat comes in faster than it leaves · *defined*
**Definition:** The temperature of a thing rises while more heat enters it each second than leaves it. When the two are equal, the temperature stays steady.

A black tumbler of water in the open sun warms up, and then it stops at about the warmth of bath water. The Sun is still shining on it. So why does it stop? As the water warms, it loses heat faster. Moving air carries heat away. The warm tumbler also sends out its own infrared. Soon heat is leaving as fast as sunlight brings it in, and the temperature stays where it is. This gives two ways to make the water hotter. You can bring more sunlight in. Or you can make the heat leave more slowly. A solar cooker does both.
- e.g. Fill a bucket from a tap while its drain hole is open. The water level rises only until water leaves as fast as it enters.
- e.g. A parked car with its windows shut gets much hotter inside than the air outside, because sunlight comes in through the glass and the warm air cannot get out.
### A solar box cooker does three jobs: it gathers sunlight, absorbs it and keeps the heat in · *defined*
**Definition:** A solar box cooker is a box that cooks food with sunlight. It has a shiny flap to send in extra light, a black inside to turn light into heat, and a clear lid and thick walls to slow the heat's escape.

A solar box cooker can be made from a wooden or cardboard box. Each part has one job. The inside and the pot are black, so they absorb sunlight and turn it into heat. A clear cover of glass or plastic lets sunlight in and stops the warm air from blowing away. A mirror or a flap covered in aluminium foil reflects extra sunlight down into the box. Thick walls, or crumpled newspaper packed around the box, slow the heat passing out through the sides. With all three jobs done, a good box cooker reaches about 100 to 150 degrees Celsius. It cooks rice and dal in about two to three hours.
- e.g. Take away the lid, and wind carries the warm air off. Take away the black, and the light bounces out again. Take away the foil flap, and less light comes in. Each missing part makes the box cooler.
- e.g. A box cooker is aimed so that its own shadow falls straight behind it. Then the Sun is shining straight in.
### What the clear lid really does: it mostly stops the warm air from escaping · *defined*
**Definition:** The clear lid of a hot box lets sunlight in. It keeps the box hot mainly by holding the warmed air inside, and partly by absorbing the infrared that the hot pot sends out.

For a long time books said one thing about a glass lid. Sunlight passes through glass, they said, but the infrared from the hot pot cannot pass back out, so the heat is caught. In 1909 the American physicist Robert Wood tested this. He made two small hot boxes. One had a lid of glass. The other had a lid of rock salt, which lets infrared pass straight through. Both boxes reached nearly the same temperature. So the main job of a lid is to stop the warmed air from rising away and being replaced by cool air. Blocking infrared helps too, by a smaller amount.
- e.g. A clear plastic sheet lets a lot of infrared pass, yet a box covered with it still gets hot. This fits what Wood found.
- e.g. Lift the lid of a hot box for ten seconds and the temperature inside drops at once, because the warm air leaves.
- **Horace-Bénédict de Saussure of Geneva, in the 1760s and 1770s:** He built the first well-tested hot boxes, with several layers of glass, and found each extra layer made the box hotter. He took this to mean that glass holds back the heat.
- **Robert W. Wood, in the Philosophical Magazine, 1909:** His glass box and his rock-salt box came out almost equally hot. He concluded that trapped air matters far more than trapped infrared in a small box.
- **Physicists who have repeated the test since:** With more careful boxes they find that blocking infrared does add some warmth. Both effects are real. In a small box the trapped air is the larger one. For the whole Earth, which has no lid and loses heat to space only as infrared, the infrared effect is the one that matters.
### A dish cooker uses a curved mirror to bring a wide area of sunlight to one spot · *defined*
**Definition:** A dish cooker is a bowl-shaped mirror that reflects all the sunlight falling on it towards one small place, called the focus, where the pot sits.

A box cooker keeps heat in. A dish cooker works the other way and brings more light in. Its mirror is shaped like a bowl, in a curve called a parabola. Every ray of sunlight that hits the bowl is reflected to one small place, the focus. A dish 1.5 metres wide gathers the sunlight of nearly 2 square metres onto the bottom of one pot. The pot gets hot enough to fry and to boil water quickly. The dish must be turned every few minutes to follow the Sun. In Shirdi in Maharashtra, rows of large dishes on a kitchen roof make steam that cooks food for tens of thousands of people a day.
- e.g. The inside of a steel serving spoon is a small curved mirror. In sunlight it throws a bright patch onto your palm that is smaller and brighter than the spoon.
- e.g. The bright spot of a dish cooker can burn skin and harm eyes, so nobody looks at the focus or puts a hand there.
- **Diocles, a Greek mathematician, about 200 BCE, and Ibn Sahl of Baghdad, about 984 CE:** Diocles proved that a parabola-shaped mirror sends all the Sun's rays to one point. Ibn Sahl wrote a book on burning mirrors and lenses that carried the mathematics further.
- **Bronze workers of ancient China, more than 2,000 years ago:** They cast bowl-shaped bronze mirrors called yangsui and used them to light fires from the Sun. An old book of court duties, the Zhouli, names an official whose work was to take fire from the Sun with such a mirror. They found the shape by making and trying, without the mathematics.
- **Wolfgang Scheffler, with Deepak and Shirin Gadhia of Valsad, Gujarat, from the 1980s:** Scheffler designed a dish that turns with the Sun while its hot spot stays in one place. That lets the cook stand indoors, in the shade. The Gadhias helped build such dishes across India. Women trained at the Barefoot College in Tilonia, Rajasthan, build dish cookers too.
### A solar cooker has real limits, and people disagree about how useful it is · *debated*
**Definition:** A solar cooker needs clear sunshine and cooks slowly in the middle of the day. Whether that fits real kitchens is still argued.

A solar cooker burns nothing, so it costs nothing to run and makes no smoke. Smoke from wood and dung fires harms the lungs of the people who cook, who are mostly women, and of the children near them. Yet many families who were given solar cookers stopped using them. A box cooker is slow. It cooks at noon, and many families eat their hot meal after dark. It cannot make a roti or a dosa, which need a very hot pan. It does little in the monsoon. So is the solar cooker a good answer that needs better design, or a poor fit for how people cook?
- e.g. A family that leaves for the fields at 7 in the morning cannot turn a cooker towards the Sun at 11.
- e.g. A large kitchen that cooks rice and dal at fixed hours every day, such as a hostel or a temple kitchen, fits solar steam well.
- **India's government solar programmes and many engineers, from the 1980s onward:** They point to the lakhs of box cookers sold with government help, the fuel saved and the smoke avoided. They hold that better designs and stored heat will widen their use.
- **Theresa Beltramo and David Levine, who ran a careful trial of solar ovens with about 1,000 households in Senegal, published in 2013:** Families who were given solar ovens did not burn less wood or breathe less smoke over the following months. The oven was too small for their large shared meals, so the fire stayed lit beside it.
- **Cooks who use them every sunny day:** Many say the cooker is worth having beside a stove, though it cannot replace one. Food does not burn or need stirring, so it frees an hour for other work.
### A solar water heater: black tubes under glass, with a wrapped tank above · *defined*
**Definition:** A solar water heater is a rooftop device that heats water with sunlight in black tubes or a black plate, and stores the hot water in a tank that is wrapped to keep heat in.

A solar water heater is a solar hot box with water running through it. On the roof sits a slanted panel that faces the Sun. Inside it, water flows through black tubes, or behind a black metal plate, under a glass cover. The black absorbs sunlight and turns it into heat, and the heat passes into the water. Many heaters use glass tubes with two walls and no air between the walls, like a thermos flask. With no air there, very little heat can leak out. The hot water collects in a tank wrapped in a thick insulator, which is a material that heat passes through only slowly. The water reaches about 60 to 80 degrees Celsius and stays hot until the next morning.
- e.g. A garden hose left full of water in the sun gives hot water for the first few seconds. A solar water heater is that hose, made black, covered and connected to a tank.
- e.g. Rooftops in Bengaluru, Pune and Shimla carry rows of slanted glass tubes with a round tank across the top.
### Why the tank sits above the tubes: warm water rises without a pump · *defined*
**Definition:** Warm water is a little lighter than the same amount of cold water. In a solar water heater, the warmed water rises into the tank and colder water sinks into the tubes to take its place.

Most rooftop solar water heaters have no pump, yet water keeps moving through them all day. When water warms, it spreads out a little. A litre of warm water weighs slightly less than a litre of cold water. So the warmed water in the tubes is pushed upward by the heavier cold water beside it. It rises into the tank. Cold water from the bottom of the tank sinks down into the tubes to be heated in its turn. The water goes round and round with only the Sun driving it. This works only if the tank is higher than the tubes. That is why the tank is always at the top.
- e.g. Slide into a pond or a still swimming pool on a sunny afternoon. The top layer is warm, and your feet find colder water below.
- e.g. Drop a little ink into the bottom of a clear tumbler of water that stands on a warm surface. The ink climbs up the middle and drifts down the sides.
### A solar cell turns light straight into electricity, with no heating and no moving part · *defined*
**Definition:** A solar cell is a thin slice of material, most often silicon, that gives an electric current when light falls on it. Electric current is a flow of tiny charged particles called electrons through a wire.

A cooker and a water heater turn sunlight into heat. A solar cell does something different. It turns light straight into electricity. Electricity in a wire is a flow of electrons, which are tiny charged particles found in every atom. A solar cell is a thin, dark blue or black slice, usually of silicon. Silicon is made from quartz, the hard mineral in most sand. When light falls on the cell, electrons start to move through the wires joined to it. Nothing in the cell turns, burns or boils. Many cells joined side by side under glass make a solar panel. How can light push electrons?
- e.g. A pocket calculator with a small dark strip above its screen runs on a solar cell. Cover the strip with your thumb and the numbers fade.
- e.g. A solar street lamp has a panel on top that fills a battery by day, and the battery lights the lamp at night.
### Inside a solar cell: each packet of light knocks one electron loose · *defined*
**Definition:** Light arrives in tiny packets of energy called photons. In a solar cell, a photon that is absorbed frees one electron, and the two layers of the cell push the freed electrons in one direction.

Light arrives as a stream of tiny packets of energy called photons. In silicon, most electrons are held in place between the atoms. When a photon is absorbed, its energy knocks one electron loose. A solar cell is made of two thin layers of silicon. Each layer has a tiny amount of a different element mixed in. Where the two layers meet, loose electrons are pushed one way and cannot go back. So they travel out through a wire, through a bulb or a fan, and round to the other side of the cell. More light means more photons, more loose electrons and a larger current. In the dark no electrons are freed, and the current stops.
- e.g. Shade half of a small solar cell with a card. About half as many photons land, and the current drops to about half.
- e.g. At noon, many thousand crore crore photons land on a fingernail-sized piece of a cell every second, which is why the push of single photons adds up to a steady current.
- **Edmond Becquerel, in Paris in 1839:** At the age of 19 he found that light falling on metal plates in a liquid made a small electric current. He could measure it and could not explain it.
- **Physicists of the 1800s, who had shown that light is a wave:** A wave spreads its energy out smoothly. On that view a dim light should need some time to shake an electron loose, and any colour should do if it is bright enough. Experiments did not show this.
- **Albert Einstein, in 1905:** He proposed that light delivers its energy in separate packets, and that one packet frees one electron. This fitted the measurements. Light still behaves as a wave in other tests, so physicists use both descriptions.
### A solar cell uses the light and not the heat, and it turns about one fifth of the light into electricity · *defined*
**Definition:** A good rooftop solar panel turns about 20 parts in every 100 of the sunlight energy that lands on it into electricity. A hot panel gives less electricity than a cool one in the same light.

Here is a surprise. A solar cooker wants to be hot. A solar panel works better cool. Of every 100 parts of sunlight energy that land on a good rooftop panel, about 20 come out as electricity. A few are reflected. The rest turn into heat in the panel and do no useful work. The heat even harms the output, because warm silicon pushes its electrons less strongly. A panel at 65 degrees Celsius on a May afternoon gives roughly 15 parts in 100 less than the same panel at 25 degrees. So panels are mounted with a gap underneath for air to pass. A cold, clear, bright day is a solar panel's best day.
- e.g. Solar panels in the cold, high desert of Ladakh give more electricity for the same sunlight than panels on the hot plains.
- e.g. The first working silicon solar cell, made in 1954, turned about 6 parts in 100 of the light into electricity.
### Solar electricity in India, and the problem of the night · *defined*
**Definition:** A solar park is a large area of solar panels that sends electricity into the wires of a region. Solar panels give electricity only in daylight, so power for the night must be stored or made another way.

India gets strong sunlight on most days of the year, and now makes a growing share of its electricity with solar panels. The Bhadla Solar Park in the Thar desert of Rajasthan covers about 56 square kilometres. The airport at Kochi in Kerala has run on its own solar panels since 2015. Modhera in Gujarat, a village with a Sun temple built in about 1026 CE, was declared India's first village to run on solar power day and night, in 2022. The night is the hard part. Panels give nothing after sunset, when homes switch their lights on. So the day's electricity must be kept in large batteries, or another power station must take over.
- e.g. A solar lantern charges for a whole day and then gives light for a few hours. Its battery sets how long the evening lasts.
- e.g. In the monsoon a panel under thick cloud may give only a tenth to a quarter of what it gives on a clear day.
### Living beings used sunlight first: basking bodies, bowl-shaped flowers and leaves · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings take in sunlight on purpose. Some use its heat to warm their bodies, and plants, algae and some bacteria use its light to make food.

A garden lizard climbs a wall in the early morning and turns their side to the Sun. They are warming up, and they move to the shade when warm enough. A butterfly opens their dark wings to the Sun before flying, because flight muscles work only when warm. In the Arctic, some flowers are shaped like small bowls and turn to face the Sun all day. The middle of the flower is a few degrees warmer than the air, and insects come to sit in the warmth. Plants do something no cooker does. Their leaves catch light and use it to build sugar from air and water. A field of crops stores about 1 part in 100 of the sunlight that falls on it.
- e.g. Crows, mynas and sparrows sometimes spread their wings flat on a sunny roof and stay still for a minute. Scientists think the heat helps them deal with tiny animals living in their feathers.
- e.g. A cat or a street dog moves through the day from one sunny patch to the next in winter and from shade to shade in summer.
- **Solar engineers:** A panel turns about 20 parts in 100 of sunlight into electricity and a crop stores about 1 part in 100. By this count the panel uses sunlight far better.
- **Robert Blankenship and a group of plant and solar scientists, in the journal Science, 2011:** They wrote that the two numbers measure different things. A panel gives electricity that must be used at once or stored in something else. A plant builds, repairs and copies themself, and stores the energy as food, all from the same light.
- **Peter Kevan, who measured Arctic flowers in 1975:** He found that bowl-shaped Arctic flowers that follow the Sun are warmer inside than the air, and that insects warm themselves there. He described the flowers as dishes that gather the Sun's warmth. Whether the plant gains mainly by warming their own seeds or by drawing insects is still studied.
### Is solar energy clean and free? It has costs, and people disagree about who should bear them · *debated*
**Definition:** Sunlight costs nothing and using it makes no smoke. Making the machines and finding the land for them has costs to people, other living beings and places.

Sunlight arrives free, and a working solar panel gives off no smoke. Many people call solar energy clean for that reason. The whole story is longer. Silicon for panels is purified in very hot furnaces, and most of those run on electricity from coal. A panel repays that energy in about one to three years, and then works for about 25 years. After that, the old panel must be taken apart or it becomes waste. Large solar parks need land. In western Rajasthan that land was grazing ground for herders' animals, and it is the home of the great Indian bustard, a large bird with fewer than 200 left. Bustards die when they fly into power lines.
- e.g. A panel on the roof of a house, a school or a railway station uses no extra land at all.
- e.g. In 2021 the Supreme Court of India ordered that power lines in the main bustard areas be laid underground where possible. The order was later changed after power companies said this could not be done everywhere.
- **Climate scientists and India's energy planners:** Burning coal for electricity heats the whole Earth and dirties the air people breathe. Solar parks replace coal faster and more cheaply than any other choice, and deserts have the most sun.
- **Herding communities of Jaisalmer and wildlife scientists of the Wildlife Institute of India:** The desert is not empty land. Shared grazing grounds called orans have fed animals for centuries, and the bustards have nowhere else to go. They ask for panels on roofs, canals and land that is already built on.
- **Engineers who study the whole life of a panel:** Counting mining, making, transport and waste, solar electricity still causes many times less harm to the air than coal electricity. They add that panel waste will become a large problem unless recycling is planned now.
### Where the Sun's energy comes from: hydrogen joining into helium · *defined*
**Definition:** In the centre of the Sun, the centres of hydrogen atoms are pressed together so hard that they join to make helium. Each joining gives out energy, which leaves the Sun as light.

Every solar cooker is fed from one place, the centre of the Sun. The Sun is mostly hydrogen, the lightest kind of atom. The astronomer Cecilia Payne showed this in 1925, when most astronomers thought the Sun was made much like the Earth. At the Sun's centre the temperature is about 1.5 crore degrees Celsius. There the centres of hydrogen atoms are pressed together and join to make helium. The helium weighs a tiny bit less than the hydrogen that made it. The missing matter has become energy. The Sun turns about 40 lakh tonnes of matter into energy every second. It has enough hydrogen to keep shining for about 500 crore years more.
- e.g. The Earth catches less than one part in 200 crore of the light the Sun sends out. The rest goes past, out into space.
- e.g. The sunlight that reaches the Earth in under two hours carries more energy than all the people of the world use in a year.
- **Anaxagoras, a Greek thinker, about 450 BCE:** He taught that the Sun is a huge red-hot stone, larger than the southern part of Greece. It was a careful guess from what a glowing coal looks like. The people of Athens put him on trial for saying the Sun was not a god.
- **Scientists of the 1800s, such as Hermann von Helmholtz and Lord Kelvin:** They worked out that the Sun could shine by slowly shrinking under its own weight. That would last some crores of years. Rocks and fossils showed the Earth to be far older, so the idea failed.
- **Cecilia Payne in 1925, and Hans Bethe in 1939:** Payne found the Sun is mostly hydrogen. Bethe worked out the steps by which hydrogen joins into helium. Tiny particles called neutrinos, which come straight from the Sun's centre and are caught in detectors on the Earth, confirm it.
### What is energy? Science can count it and cannot yet say what it is · *idea*
**Definition:** Energy is a number that scientists can work out for anything that moves, glows, is hot or is stored. The total never changes. Science has working definitions of energy and no final one.

This lesson has used the word energy on almost every card. Here is how scientists use it. They work out a number for light, a number for heat, a number for electricity and a number for movement. When sunlight heats water or drives a current, the numbers before and after add up to the same total, every time. That lets an engineer say how big a panel a house needs. But no one can point to energy itself. The physicist Richard Feynman wrote that in physics today we have no knowledge of what energy is. Confusion like this is how science grows. What do you think is travelling from the Sun to your hand?
- e.g. You can measure how hot the water got and how bright the light was. You cannot take the energy out and look at it apart from the water or the light.
- e.g. Money in a bank account is also a number that is moved and never seen. Is energy like that, or is it more real than that?
- **Richard Feynman, in The Feynman Lectures on Physics, volume 1, chapter 4 (1963):** Energy is a number that does not change when nature goes through its changes. He called this an abstract idea and wrote that we do not know what energy is.
- **Emmy Noether, a mathematician, in 1918:** She proved that the total energy stays the same because the laws of nature are the same today as they were yesterday. This explains why the number is kept. It does not say what the number is a number of.
- **The Prashna Upanishad, 1.4 and 1.5:** The text says that everything comes as a pair, prana and rayi. Prana is the life-force or energy, and rayi is matter or food. It says the Sun is prana and the Moon is rayi. This is a teaching reached by reflection and not by measuring, and it is not the physicists' energy. It asks the same question from another side: is the world made of one kind of thing or two?
### The Sun as the life of the world: what old texts in India, Egypt and China said · *idea*
**Definition:** Many old traditions taught that the Sun is the source of life and movement on the Earth. These are teachings from reflection and long watching, and are not measurements.

In India the Prashna Upanishad says that the rising Sun gathers the life of every direction into its rays. The Chandogya Upanishad calls the Sun the honey of the gods, with its rays as the honeycomb. In Egypt, about 1340 BCE, a hymn to the Sun was carved in a tomb at Amarna. It says that when the Sun rises, birds fly, fish leap and every being wakes to their work. In China the bowl-shaped mirror that takes fire from the Sun was named yangsui, from yang, the bright and warm side of things. Science today agrees that nearly all life on the Earth's surface runs on sunlight. It does not test whether the Sun is alive or aware.
- e.g. Wind, rain and rivers are also driven by the Sun's heat. So is the food of nearly every animal, including us, through the plants that caught the light.
- e.g. Some living communities do not run on sunlight. Deep on the sea floor, bacteria near hot vents live on chemicals from inside the Earth.
- **Prashna Upanishad, 1.6 to 1.8:** When the Sun rises and enters the east, the text says, it holds the life-breaths of the east in its rays, and so for every direction. It calls the rising Sun the life of all beings.
- **Chandogya Upanishad, 3.1.1:** The Sun is the honey of the gods. The sky is the crossbeam the comb hangs from, the air between is the comb, and the rays are the young bees.
- **The Great Hymn to the Aten, in the tomb of Ay at Amarna, Egypt, about 1340 BCE:** The hymn says the Sun is far away, yet its rays are on the Earth, and that all beings live when it rises and rest when it sets.
- **Biologists who study deep-sea vents, first seen in 1977:** They found whole communities of living beings in permanent darkness, fed by bacteria that use chemicals from hot rock. So sunlight is the source of most life on Earth, and not of all of it.

## Mostly agreed
- Solar cells work in space, where there is no air at all. *How we know:* The small satellite Vanguard 1, launched in 1958, carried six solar cells. They kept its radio sending signals for about six years, long after its ordinary battery had run out. Almost every satellite since, including India's, has been powered by solar cells.
- Without its layer of air, the Earth's surface would be frozen. *How we know:* The Earth takes in sunlight and sends infrared back to space. Working out that balance for a bare Earth gives an average of about 18 degrees Celsius below zero. The measured average is about 15 degrees above zero. Gases in the air, mainly water vapour and carbon dioxide, absorb some of the outgoing infrared and make up the difference. Eunice Foote in 1856 and John Tyndall in 1859 first measured this absorbing by gases.
- Strong sunlight can make clear water safer to drink. *How we know:* In the 1980s Aftim Acra and his team in Beirut showed that sunlight kills many disease-causing germs in water. Later tests found that clear water in a clear plastic bottle, laid in full sun for about six hours, has far fewer living germs, through the ultraviolet light and the warmth together. The World Health Organization lists this method for places with no other way to treat water. It does not remove chemicals or mud.
- Heat from the Sun can be stored in a material that melts by day and sets again at night. *How we know:* In 1948 the scientist Mária Telkes, the architect Eleanor Raymond and the sculptor Amelia Peabody, who paid for it, built a house at Dover in Massachusetts heated only by the Sun. Sun-warmed air melted a salt kept in drums, and the salt gave the heat back as it set. The house stayed warm through cloudy days for about two winters, and then the salt stopped setting properly. Melting-and-setting heat stores are used today in buildings and in some solar cookers.
- The price of solar cells has fallen to less than one hundredth of what it was in the 1970s. *How we know:* Records of panel prices kept since 1976 show the price for each watt falling by more than 99 parts in 100. Each time the world's total number of panels doubled, the price fell by about a fifth, as makers learned to use less silicon and waste less.

## How we found out
**Bronze workers of ancient China, Diocles, Horace-Bénédict de Saussure, William Adams, M. K. Ghosh, M. L. Ghai, More than 2,000 years ago to the 1950s.** For most of history people thought a thing in the sun gets hot because the air around it is hot. It fitted what everyone felt: sunny days are warm days. More than 2,000 years ago, bronze workers in China and mathematicians in Greece knew that curved mirrors could light fires. Ordinary sunshine without a mirror seemed too weak for real heat. In 1767 Horace-Bénédict de Saussure of Geneva nested glass boxes inside one another and set them in the sun. The innermost passed the temperature of boiling water. Then he tested the hot-air idea. In 1774 he carried a hot box up a mountain, where the air was only about 6 degrees Celsius. The box got about as hot as it did on the warm plain below. So the heat came from the sunlight and not from the air. In the 1870s William Adams cooked meals in a mirror-sided box in Bombay. In 1945 M. K. Ghosh designed the first box cooker made for sale in India. In the 1950s M. L. Ghai at the National Physical Laboratory in Delhi built a dish cooker.  
*What people believed before:* A thing left in the sun gets hot because the air around it is hot, so ordinary sunshine can never cook.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Archimedes set Roman ships on fire with mirrors at Syracuse in 212 BCE.** (Later Greek and Byzantine writers, such as Anthemius of Tralles in the 500s CE; *minority*)  
  For: In 1747 CE the Comte de Buffon in Paris aimed 168 flat mirrors at a plank about 45 metres away and set it alight. In 2005 a class at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology lit a wooden model of a ship's side with 127 small mirrors, after about ten minutes of steady sun.  
  Against: The writers nearest to the siege, Polybius, Livy and Plutarch, describe Archimedes' other machines and never mention mirrors. The story first appears hundreds of years later. A real ship moves, is wet, and is further away than the test planks. Most historians think it did not happen.
- **The Oriental hornet turns sunlight into electricity in their own body.** (Marian Plotkin, Jacob Ishay, David Bergman and colleagues at Tel Aviv University, in 2010; *debated*)  
  For: Most wasps are busiest in the morning, and this hornet digs most at midday, when sunlight is strongest. The brown parts of the hornet's outer covering have fine grooves that take in light. The yellow band holds a pigment called xanthopterin. The team built a small solar cell from that pigment and it gave a current.  
  Against: The home-made cell turned less than half of one part in 100 of the light into electricity. Nobody has measured a living hornet using such a current. The midday digging may simply be because the Sun's heat warms the muscles.
- **Some sea slugs live on sunlight, like plants.** (Mary Rumpho and colleagues, studying the green sea slug Elysia chlorotica, from the 1990s; *debated*)  
  For: These slugs eat algae and keep the algae's green food-making parts, called chloroplasts, alive inside their own bodies. Slugs kept in light with no food have lived for nine months or more, and their chloroplasts kept working.  
  Against: In 2014 Gregor Christa, Sven Gould and colleagues kept two other kinds of such slugs in the dark, or with their chloroplasts chemically switched off. The slugs lived about as long as slugs in the light. The team proposed that the chloroplasts are mainly a larder that is slowly digested.
- **Giving families solar cookers cuts the smoke they breathe and the wood they burn.** (Many aid groups and government programmes since the 1980s; *debated*)  
  For: Smoke from cooking fires is linked to about 30 lakh early deaths a year across the world, by the World Health Organization's count. A solar cooker makes no smoke. Tests in kitchens show a box cooker can cook a family's rice and dal on a clear day.  
  Against: In the Senegal trial by Theresa Beltramo and David Levine, published in 2013, families with solar ovens burned as much wood and breathed as much smoke as families without them. The ovens were too small and too slow for the main meal. A machine that works in a test does not always fit a household.

## Roots
You have built a box that cooks with sunlight, and seen how a heater and a cell use the same light. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Sun Box test, two tumblers of water stood in the same sunlight. The one in the open stayed lukewarm, and the one in the box grew hot. The box, the foil and the glass made no heat at all. They only slowed the heat on its way out. So a thing can be made hotter by slowing the heat's way out, with no extra heat added.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the cooker cooks', 'the heater heats' and 'the panel makes electricity', as if each machine were a source. That way of speaking hides that nothing on the terrace makes any energy. Every one of them only meets energy that is already passing, and holds it or turns it for a while.  
*Evidence:* Both tumblers received the same sunlight for the same time. The only difference was the box, which has no flame, no wire and no fuel, and the water inside it ended much hotter.

- *who am i:* Your body stays at about 37 degrees Celsius, day and night. Your food brings energy in, and your skin lets heat out, and the two keep pace. Your food was grown with sunlight. So you are warm in the way the box is warm: sunlight is passing through you, and you slow it down.
- *what is life:* A leaf holds sunlight for a season as sugar. A lizard holds it for a morning as warmth. A mango tree holds it for years as wood. Every living being takes in energy that is passing and keeps it for a while by their own skill, and then it goes on.
- *what is this world:* The Earth is a sun box too. Sunlight comes in, the air slows the heat's leaving, and all day and all night the Earth sends out to space about as much energy as it received. Its warmth, its winds and its rain are that passing energy, held for a while. Nothing on the Earth owns the energy it uses.

The cooker, the water heater, the solar cell, the leaf and your own body do not differ in where their energy comes from. They differ in what each does with it before it leaves. Even the Sun is passing on energy that was held in its hydrogen since the universe was young. Follow the heat of one pot of rice back or forward, and you never reach a thing that the energy belongs to. Consciousness is the ground that the Sun, the box and the one who watches arise from, and the one flow through all three is that ground in motion. Many scientists would agree only that the flow of energy is one, and would say that what it is remains unknown.

- **Rig Veda 1.115.1:** The hymn to Surya, the Sun, says that the Sun has filled the sky, the earth and the space between, and calls the Sun the atman, the self, of all that moves and all that stands still. (Science finds that wind, rain, rivers and the food of nearly every animal are driven by sunlight. The hymn says more than that. It says 'self', where science says 'source of energy', and science has no test for the larger claim.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 16:** The speaker asks the Sun to gather in its rays so that its most beautiful form can be seen. Then the speaker says: the Person who is there in the Sun, I am that. (This lesson shows that the energy working in your body was sunlight a short time ago. The verse speaks of the one who is aware in you and says that one is the same as in the Sun. The first is measured. The second is a teaching to think about.)
- **The Great Hymn to the Aten, tomb of Ay at Amarna, Egypt, about 1340 BCE:** The hymn says to the Sun: you are far away, yet your rays are on the Earth. When you rise, every being lives, and when you set, they rest. (The writers had no way to measure 15 crore kilometres. They had noticed the same puzzle as this lesson's first card: something very far away does its work right here, on the skin.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or destroyed. It is only moved or changed in form. Every solar machine obeys this, and no exception has ever been measured.
- Science (settled): Satellites that measure the Earth from above find that it sends out to space very nearly as much energy as it takes in from the Sun. The small difference is what is slowly warming the planet.
- Science (open): What energy is, apart from the numbers used to count it, has no agreed answer in physics.
- Other view, The Charvaka thinkers of ancient India, Anaxagoras of Greece about 450 BCE, and most physicists today: The Charvakas taught that only what the senses can check is real, and that the body is matter and nothing more. Anaxagoras said the Sun is a hot stone and no kind of self. Most physicists today would say that the Sun is hydrogen joining into helium, with no awareness that anyone has detected. On this view, 'you are made of sunlight' is a true statement about where your body's energy came from, and it says nothing about who you are.
- Practice, Arriving and leaving (3 min): Sit near a window where morning sunlight falls on the floor. Never look at the Sun itself. Lay the back of one hand in the sunlight and keep the other hand in the shade. Close your eyes and take ten slow breaths. Notice the warmth arriving on one hand. Now slide that hand into the shade and take ten more breaths. Notice the warmth leaving. Ask quietly: where was that warmth eight minutes before it reached me, and where is it going now?
- Ask: The box made no heat and the panel made no energy. Is there anything you know of that truly makes energy, and does not only pass it on?
- Ask: If the warmth in your hand was in the Sun eight minutes ago and will be in the air a minute from now, at which moment is it yours?
- Ask: A leaf, a lizard and you all slow sunlight down for a while. What would be left of each of you if the flow stopped?

## Story: The Pot That Was Ready Too Early
*A made-up story set in a salt-making and fishing village on the coast near Thoothukudi, Tamil Nadu. The people are imagined. What is real: sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in a litre, salt pans dry it with sun and wind, fish is sun-dried into karuvadu, a box cooker cooks rice in about two hours of clear sun, and a hot pot packed in straw or cloth stays hot for hours. What a lizard feels while basking is not known to anyone.*

The salt pans behind Selvi's house were so white at noon that she had to squint. Selvi was nine. Her periamma, Arokiamary, had raked salt there for thirty years, and in the afternoons she dried fish on the long racks by the lane.

On Saturday their neighbour Nasreen came home from her engineering college in Madurai with a flat wooden box under her arm. It was black inside. It had a glass lid and a mirror on a hinge. 'A solar cooker,' Nasreen said. 'No firewood, no gas, no smoke in your eyes, Periamma. The Sun does everything.'

Arokiamary looked at the box for a while. 'The Sun already does everything here,' she said. 'But show me.'

They put rice and water in a black pot, shut the lid and tilted the mirror until the box's shadow lay straight behind it. Then Arokiamary went to the pans, Nasreen went to visit her aunt, and Selvi was left to watch with Anthony, who was six and lived next door.

Nothing happened that Selvi could see. A garden lizard was watching too, from the compound wall. In the morning the lizard had lain flat and side-on to the Sun. Now, at noon, they had turned to face it, so that hardly any light fell on their body. A little later they slipped into the crack behind the drainpipe.

'The lizard is switching off,' said Anthony.

By half past twelve the glass lid was wet on the inside. By one o'clock Selvi could smell rice. She was so pleased that she ran all the way to the pans to tell her periamma.

Arokiamary laughed. 'Ready at one! And we eat at eight, when the boats are in and the salt is covered. Will your box keep it hot for seven hours?'

It would not. By four the Sun had gone behind the neem tree, and by six the pot was barely warm. Selvi felt as if the cooker had played a trick on her.

Nasreen came back and felt the pot. 'It worked,' she said, a little stiffly. 'The rice is cooked. It reached almost a hundred degrees.'

'It worked for the Sun's day,' said Arokiamary. 'Not for ours.' She was not unkind about it. She sat down on the step and pointed with her chin at the pans. 'Come. Look at my cooker.'

'That is not a cooker,' said Anthony. 'That is a puddle.'

'A very wide puddle, one hand deep,' said Arokiamary. 'Why do you think we make it wide and shallow, and open to the wind?'

Selvi thought about the wet glass lid. In the box, the water had tried to leave the rice and the lid had stopped it. 'So that the water can get away,' she said slowly. 'The Sun's heat goes into the water, and the water leaves with it. You want it to leave.'

'I want it to leave,' said Arokiamary. 'If I put a glass lid on my salt pan I would have warm sea water for ever, and no salt. Your box and my pan are fed by the same Sun. In the box you shut the door behind the heat. In the pan I hold the door open.'

Nasreen had stopped being stiff. 'And the fish racks?'

'The same. Thin, and high, and where the wind passes. And the lizard knows a third way.' She nodded at the drainpipe. 'They have no lid and no door. They turn their own body. Side-on to take the Sun in, nose-on to let it pass, and into the crack when they have enough.'

Selvi looked at the box, the pan and the drainpipe. Three users of one Sun, and each had settled the same matter differently: what the heat should do after it arrived. Stay, or go, or be let in only a little.

'There is a fourth thing,' said Nasreen. 'It can wait. Periamma, do you have a basket and some old straw?'

The next day they cooked the rice again. At one o'clock, with cloths round her hands, Nasreen lifted the hot pot straight out of the box and pushed it deep into a basket packed with straw and an old cotton sari, and put a pillow on top. At eight that night Arokiamary dug the pot out. Steam rose when she lifted the lid.

'Now it works for our day,' she said, and served Anthony first because he was the loudest.

Selvi ate slowly. The rice in her mouth was warm with heat that had arrived at noon and had been kept waiting in straw for seven hours. Before that it had waited eight minutes in the dark between the Sun and the Earth. And the fish beside the rice had been dried by yesterday's Sun, and the salt on the fish by last month's.

'Periamma,' she said, 'the Sun gives everyone the same. So what is it that we do?'

Arokiamary considered this while she ate. 'We decide how long it stays,' she said. 'I think that is all anyone does.' Then she added, 'But I rake salt. Ask the lizard as well.'

**For the parent:** A solar cooker, a salt pan, a fish rack and a basking lizard all receive the same sunlight. They differ in what each one does next with the heat: keep it in, let it leave with the water, let in only some, or hold it for later.

- Ask: Arokiamary says that all anyone does with the Sun is decide how long its heat stays. Can you think of a use of sunlight that does not fit her idea?
- Ask: The lizard turned side-on, then nose-on, then went into the crack. Is the lizard using a solar heater, or being one? Does the difference matter?
- Ask: The cooker worked for the Sun's day and not for the family's day. When a machine does not fit people's lives, who should change: the machine, the people, or neither?

   **Answer:** Both lines start at the same temperature. The open tumbler's line rises a little and flattens early at a lower temperature. The box tumbler's line rises more and flattens later at a higher one.. *Why:* Both lines flatten when the heat leaving equals the heat coming in. The box's line flattens higher because its heat leaves more slowly.
9. (perspective) Is energy a real thing, or only a number that scientists calculate?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer accepts that the number is solid and useful, and says that whether it is a thing is still open.. *Why:* Scientists agree on what the number does. They do not agree on what it is.
10. (count) A panel gives 400 watts. Heat cuts its output by 15 of every 100. About how many watts does it give then?  
   **Answer:** 340. *Why:* 15 of every 100 of 400 is 60. 400 − 60 = 340 watts.
11. (mcq) A family cooks at 8 in the evening. Their cooker works at noon. Which change helps most?  
   Options: Keep the cooked food hot in a packed, insulated container / Paint the box white / Take off the lid / Use a smaller mirror  
   **Answer:** Keep the cooked food hot in a packed, insulated container. *Why:* Packed straw or cloth slows the heat's leaving for hours. The other changes bring less light in or let heat out.
12. (write) Write one question about sunlight or energy that nobody can answer yet. Say how someone might start to find out.  
   **Answer:** Any real open question, such as what energy is, or how the Sun's corona is hotter than its surface. A strong answer names a first step, such as a measurement or a test.. *Why:* A good question can be tested or compared, even if nobody knows the answer yet.
13. (match) Match each way heat leaves a tumbler to a thing you can see or feel.  
   **Answer:** Moving air carries it off → Wind blowing over a tumbler; It is sent out as invisible infrared → A warm tumbler warms your hand from a distance; It passes into what the tumbler touches → A tumbler on a cold steel table; Trapped air slows all of these → A tumbler packed in crumpled paper. *Why:* Heat leaves by moving air, by infrared and by touch. Trapped air slows all three.
14. (truefalse) A solar panel gets more electricity from the Sun's heat than from its light.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A panel uses the light. Heat harms it, which is why a hot panel gives less.
15. (sort) Sort each claim by how firmly we know it.  
   **Answer:** The Sun's energy comes from hydrogen joining into helium → Well tested; A hot panel gives less electricity than a cool one → Well tested; Solar cookers are a good answer to cooking smoke → Still argued; Big solar parks should use desert grazing land → Still argued; A clear cover mostly stops warm air leaving → Well tested. *Why:* Some claims are measured many times. Others depend on how people cook, who owns land and what we value.
16. (write) Your family lives where the sky is cloudy for months. How could you still use the Sun, and what would you add?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer uses sunny hours with a heat store or a battery. It says what else is needed, such as another power source for cloudy weeks.. *Why:* No single method fits every place. Good designs fit the place and the weather.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A rooftop panel is 2 square metres. About how many watts of noon sunlight land on it?  
   Options: 200 / 1,000 / 2,000 / 20,000  
   **Answer:** 2,000. *Why:* 1,000 watts fall on each square metre, and the panel covers 2, so 2 × 1,000 = 2,000. Choosing 1,000 counts one square metre only. Multiply by the area.
2. (mcq) A black scooter seat is too hot to sit on, though nothing touched it. What made it hot?  
   Options: Sunlight that the seat absorbed turned into heat / The seat made its own heat / Hot air from under the seat rose into it  
   **Answer:** Sunlight that the seat absorbed turned into heat. *Why:* Light that a dark surface absorbs turns into heat in that surface. The seat has no flame or wire and makes no energy of its own.
3. (mcq) A friend colours the inside of a box cooker white. What happens to the heat inside?  
   Options: It rises, because white is bright / It is lower, because white sends light away / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It is lower, because white sends light away. *Why:* White reflects much of the light, so less is absorbed and less turns into heat. Black is the colour that absorbs.
4. (mcq) Which part of a box cooker mostly stops warm air from blowing away?  
   Options: The foil flap / The black lining / The clear cover / The box's shadow  
   **Answer:** The clear cover. *Why:* The clear cover lets light in and holds the warm air in. The foil flap sends more light in, and the black lining absorbs light.
5. (mcq) In the Sun Box test, the box adds no heat. Why is its water hotter?  
   Options: The box makes extra heat / Heat leaves the water in the box more slowly / More air reaches the box water / The foil adds heat  
   **Answer:** Heat leaves the water in the box more slowly. *Why:* The box slows the heat's leaving. Both tumblers get the same sunlight, but the box tumbler loses heat more slowly. Foil sends light in and makes no heat.
6. (mcq) A black tumbler in open sun stops at 45 degrees Celsius while the Sun still shines. Why?  
   Options: Sunlight has stopped arriving / Heat leaves as fast as sunlight brings it in / Black stops absorbing when warm / The water is full of heat  
   **Answer:** Heat leaves as fast as sunlight brings it in. *Why:* As the tumbler warms, it loses heat faster. At 45 degrees the heat leaving equals the heat arriving, so the temperature stays steady.
7. (mcq) You want to test only the clear cover. Which test is fair?  
   Options: Two matching boxes side by side, one with a cover and one without / One box with a cover on Monday and without on Tuesday / A large box with a cover against a small box without  
   **Answer:** Two matching boxes side by side, one with a cover and one without. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and keeps the Sun the same. Different days or different boxes change more than one thing.
8. (mcq) Why is the tank of a solar water heater above its tubes?  
   Options: Warmed water rises, so it flows up into the tank / A heavy tank needs to be high / Warm water is heavier and sinks into the tank / The Sun is higher up  
   **Answer:** Warmed water rises, so it flows up into the tank. *Why:* Warm water is a little lighter, so cold water pushes it up and it moves into the tank with no pump. Warm water sinking is the wrong way round.
9. (mcq) Many solar heater tubes have two glass walls with no air between them. What does this do?  
   Options: Slows heat leaking out, like a flask / Lets in more light / Makes the water flow faster / Keeps the tubes cool  
   **Answer:** Slows heat leaking out, like a flask. *Why:* With no air between the walls, very little heat can pass through. The water stays hot. The tubes are made to hold heat, not to cool.
10. (mcq) A rooftop panel gives less electricity on a hot afternoon than on a cold clear day. Why?  
   Options: Hot silicon pushes its electrons less strongly / A hot day has less sunlight / Cold air has more photons / Panels need heat to start  
   **Answer:** Hot silicon pushes its electrons less strongly. *Why:* A solar cell uses light, not heat. Warm silicon gives a smaller push to the freed electrons, so the panel gives less electricity.
11. (mcq) Which statement is true?  
   Options: A solar cell heats water to make electricity / A solar cooker turns light into electricity / A solar cell turns light into electricity without heating anything first / Both a cooker and a cell need a mirror  
   **Answer:** A solar cell turns light into electricity without heating anything first. *Why:* A solar cell frees electrons with light and needs no heating step. A cooker turns light into heat. Neither needs a mirror, though a flap helps a cooker.
12. (mcq) A solar cooker is slow and fails in the monsoon. What is the fairest conclusion?  
   Options: Solar cookers are useless / They suit some kitchens and need better design for others / Families who stopped using them were wrong / Only the mirror matters  
   **Answer:** They suit some kitchens and need better design for others. *Why:* People disagree about solar cookers. They fit some families well and others poorly, so the honest answer names who they suit and what to improve.
13. (mcq) Which question about energy has no agreed answer yet?  
   Options: How many watts fall on a square metre at noon? / What energy finally is / Whether sunlight crosses empty space / Whether black paper absorbs light  
   **Answer:** What energy finally is. *Why:* Physicists calculate energy and trust the sums. They have no final answer to what it is. The other questions have been measured.

## Open questions
- **What is energy?** Physicists can calculate the energy of light, heat, movement and electricity, and the total always stays the same. Emmy Noether showed why it stays the same. Nobody has shown what the number is a number of, and Richard Feynman wrote that physics has no knowledge of what energy is.
- **How can the Sun's energy be kept cheaply for the night and for the monsoon?** Batteries can hold a few hours of electricity, and they need mined metals. Melted salts, water pumped uphill and gases made with solar electricity are all being tried. Nobody yet knows which will hold weeks of energy at a price most people can pay.
- **Can people copy the leaf, and make fuel from sunlight, water and air?** Laboratories have made small devices that split water or build simple fuels using light. They are costly, and many wear out within days or months. A leaf repairs their own light-catching parts all day long, and nobody has yet built a device that mends itself.
- **How much of the sunlight can a solar cell turn into electricity?** In 1961 William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser worked out that a cell made of one material can never pass about one third. Cells made of two stacked materials have passed that mark in laboratories. The newer top layers, called perovskites, break down in heat and damp, and nobody has yet made them last 25 years on a roof.
- **Why is the Sun's thin outer layer far hotter than its surface?** The Sun's surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius. The thin gas above it, called the corona, is more than 10 lakh degrees. Heat should not flow from cooler to hotter, so something else carries energy up. Magnetic waves and countless small explosions are the leading ideas. India's Aditya-L1 spacecraft, launched in 2023 with Nigar Shaji as project director, is measuring the corona to help decide.
- **Does any animal truly live on sunlight?** The green sea slugs and the Oriental hornet are the best-known cases, and the evidence for each is disputed. It is hard to prove that a living animal is using light for energy and not only carrying parts that can.

## Connected maths
- A tray on a terrace is 0.5 metre long and 0.4 metre wide. At noon about 1,000 watts of sunlight fall on each square metre. How many watts fall on the tray? **200 watts**. The tray's area is 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 square metres. Then 0.2 × 1,000 = 200 watts. A tray one fifth of a square metre catches one fifth of 1,000 watts. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
- A tumbler holds 150 grams of water. It takes roughly 630 joules to warm it by 1 degree Celsius. How many joules does it take to warm it by 30 degrees? A heater of 100 watts gives 100 joules each second. How many seconds would it need if none of the heat leaked out? **18,900 joules. 189 seconds, about 3 minutes.**. 630 × 30 = 18,900 joules. At 100 joules each second, 18,900 ÷ 100 = 189 seconds, about 3 minutes. The real Sun Box takes about 60 minutes, nearly 20 times longer. Most of the light misses the tumbler, and much of the heat leaks out. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A panel of 2 square metres sits in noon sun of 1,000 watts on each square metre. It turns 20 of every 100 parts into electricity. How many watts of electricity does it give? How many 10-watt LED bulbs could it run? **400 watts. 40 bulbs.**. The sunlight on the panel is 2 × 1,000 = 2,000 watts. Then 20 of every 100 parts of 2,000 is 2,000 × 20 ÷ 100 = 400 watts. 400 ÷ 10 = 40 bulbs. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- These are imagined readings from a box tumbler. At 0, 15, 30, 45 and 60 minutes it read 28, 38, 47, 54 and 59 degrees Celsius. Find the rise in each 15 minutes. What do the rises tell you about heat coming in and heat leaving? **10, 9, 7 and 5 degrees. The rises shrink, so the heat leaving grows as the water gets hotter, and it is getting closer to the heat coming in.**. 38 − 28 = 10, 47 − 38 = 9, 54 − 47 = 7 and 59 − 54 = 5. A hotter tumbler loses heat faster, so less of the arriving heat is left to raise the temperature each time. The graph bends and flattens. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Light travels about 3 lakh kilometres each second. The Sun is about 15 crore kilometres away. How many seconds does sunlight take to reach the Earth? How many minutes is that? **500 seconds, about 8 minutes.**. 15 crore is 15,00,00,000 and 3 lakh is 3,00,000. 15,00,00,000 ÷ 3,00,000 = 500 seconds. 500 ÷ 60 is about 8 minutes and 20 seconds. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- The Earth catches less than 1 part of every 200 crore of the Sun's light. Write 200 crore in figures. How many zeros does it have after the 2? **2,00,00,00,000, which has 9 zeros after the 2.**. 1 crore is 1,00,00,000. 200 crore is 200 × 1,00,00,000 = 2,00,00,00,000. That is a 2 followed by 9 zeros, or 2 thousand million. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- These are imagined readings. In the Sun Box test the open tumbler rose 14 degrees and the box tumbler rose 34 degrees. How many degrees more did the box tumbler rise? About how many times bigger was its rise? **20 degrees more. About 2.4 times.**. 34 − 14 = 20 degrees. 34 ÷ 14 is about 2.4. The box's rise was a little more than twice the open tumbler's rise. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
