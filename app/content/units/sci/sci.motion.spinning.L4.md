# Going round: what keeps a thing in a circle, and why it flies off straight when the pull stops
`sci.motion.spinning.L4`

**Summary.** Whirl a small bag on a string, and your fingers feel the string pulling inward the whole time. Whirl it twice as fast, and the pull is not twice as big but about four times as big. Let go, and the bag does not curve away. It flies off in a straight line. A stone on a string, a car on a bend, wet clothes in a spin dryer and the Moon going round the Earth all need a pull towards the middle, every moment. The pull must grow with the mass, with the square of the speed, and as the circle gets tighter. The 'outward push' you feel on a bend is your own body trying to go straight, and scientists still argue about whether to call it a force.

**Intro.** *Whirl a small bag on a string. Your fingers feel a pull, and the faster you whirl, the stronger the pull gets. Now let go. Does the bag fly straight on, curve round, or go outward? Nobody pushes the bag outward, so why do you feel a pull at all?* The string pulls the bag towards your hand all the time. That pull bends the bag's path into a circle. When the pull stops, the bag goes straight on. In this unit you measure how the pull changes with speed, you slide coins off a turntable, and you watch a spin dryer throw water out through its holes. You find out why a car slows for a bend, why a lab spins blood and a dairy spins milk, and why the Moon keeps falling towards the Earth without ever getting closer.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that a thing going round needs a pull towards the middle all the time, and that without it, the thing flies off in a straight line.
1. I can run a fair test with a whirler, record the turns in a table, and say how the speed of the circling changes when the pull changes.
2. I can say how the pull needed changes when the speed doubles, when the circle gets wider, and when the mass gets bigger.
3. I can explain why coins on a turntable slide off from the edge first, and why a heavy coin and a light coin slide off together.
4. I can explain how a spin dryer, a cream separator and a laboratory centrifuge use the same idea.
5. I can explain why a car slows for a bend, why roads and railway lines are tilted at bends, and why a cyclist leans.
6. I can explain that the Moon keeps falling towards the Earth without getting closer, and say what scientists argue about when they say 'centrifugal force'.

## Terms
- **Circular motion**: Moving round and round in a circle. A thing in circular motion changes its direction every moment. *e.g.* A stone whirled on a string, and a car going round a roundabout, are in circular motion.
- **Centre**: The middle point of a circle. Every point on the circle is the same distance from it. *e.g.* The centre of a merry-go-round is the post in the middle.
- **Radius**: The distance from the centre of a circle to its edge. A small radius means a tight circle, and a big radius means a wide circle. *e.g.* A string 40 centimetres long makes a circle with a radius of 40 centimetres.
- **Tangent**: A straight line that just touches a circle at one point, and points the way a thing on the circle is moving at that moment. *e.g.* Sparks from a grinding wheel fly off along tangents.
- **Centripetal force**: The pull or push towards the centre that keeps a thing going round. The word means 'centre-seeking'. It is not a new kind of force. It is a name for whatever force points to the middle. *e.g.* The string pulling a whirled bag, and the Earth's gravity pulling the Moon, are both centripetal forces.
- **Centrifugal force**: The name many people give to the feeling of being pushed outward when you turn. Scientists agree that something is felt, and they still discuss whether it should be called a force. *e.g.* On a sharp bend in a bus, you feel pressed against the window on the outside of the turn.
- **Inertia**: The way every thing keeps doing what it is doing. A moving thing goes in a straight line at the same speed until a force changes it. *e.g.* A coin on a spinning turntable wants to go straight on, and slides off when the friction is not enough.
- **Tension**: The pull along a string, a rope or a wire when it is stretched tight. *e.g.* The string of a whirled bag has tension.
- **Friction**: The grip between two surfaces that touch. It works against sliding. *e.g.* Friction between a tyre and the road gives a car the pull it needs to take a bend.
- **Gravity**: The pull between things because they have mass. The Earth's gravity pulls everything towards the ground, and holds the Moon in its path. *e.g.* Gravity pulls a dropped tumbler down, and holds the Moon near the Earth.
- **Orbit**: The path of one thing going round another because of gravity. *e.g.* The Moon is in orbit round the Earth, and the Earth is in orbit round the Sun.
- **Mass**: How much matter a thing has. More mass means a bigger pull is needed to turn it. *e.g.* A heavy stone on a string pulls harder on your hand than a light one at the same speed.
- **Turn**: One full trip round a circle, back to the start. Scientists also say a revolution. *e.g.* A bag that goes round 10 times in 10 seconds makes one turn every second.
- **Centrifuge**: A machine that spins things fast, so that heavier parts of a mixture move to the outside and lighter parts stay near the middle. *e.g.* A laboratory centrifuge spins tubes of blood to separate the blood cells from the liquid.
- **Banked bend**: A bend in a road or a rail that is tilted, with the outside edge higher than the inside edge, so that part of the push of the ground points towards the middle. *e.g.* On many railway curves, the outer rail is raised a little.
- **Speed**: How far a thing moves in a certain time, such as metres every second. *e.g.* A bag that goes round a circle 2 metres long in 1 second has a speed of 2 metres per second.

## Explanations
### Going round needs a pull towards the middle, all the time · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing moving in a circle changes its direction every moment. A force that points towards the middle of the circle makes this happen. Scientists call it the centripetal force.

Think of the bag on the string. Because of inertia, the bag wants to go straight on. The string pulls it towards your hand at every moment, and that bends its path into a circle. The pull always points at a right angle to the way the bag is moving. So it changes the direction of the bag, but it does not make the bag faster or slower. Different things give the pull. A string gives tension. A road gives friction to a car on a bend. The Earth gives gravity to the Moon. The wall of a spin dryer gives a push to the clothes. The word 'centripetal' only names what the pull does: it seeks the middle. It is not a new kind of force.
- e.g. The string pulls a whirled bag towards your hand.
- e.g. The friction of the tyres pulls a car round a bend.
- e.g. The Earth's gravity pulls the Moon, so that the Moon goes round the Earth.
### Without the pull, a thing flies off in a straight line · *defined*
**Definition:** When the pull towards the middle stops, a thing moves in a straight line, along the way it was going at that moment. This line just touches the circle, and is called a tangent.

A common guess is that a whirled stone, when let go, flies outward from the middle, like the spoke of a wheel. But it does not. It goes along the tangent, in the direction it was moving. Sparks from a grinding wheel fly off in long straight lines. Mud thrown from a tyre leaves in a straight line. Drops fly off a spun wet umbrella in straight lines. A hammer thrower in an athletics match lets go of the hammer when it is moving towards the field, not when it is pointing at it. Each time, the thing is doing what the first law says: going straight, because no force is bending its path any more.
- e.g. Sparks fly off in straight lines from a grinding wheel.
- e.g. Mud flies off a fast tyre along the way the tyre was turning.
- e.g. Water drops leave a spun wet umbrella in straight lines.
### A faster circle needs a much bigger pull · *defined*
**Definition:** In the same circle, going twice as fast needs four times the pull. Going three times as fast needs nine times the pull.

A faster thing has to turn round more quickly, and it also has more speed to bend every second. The two effects multiply, so the pull grows with the square of the speed. In the whirler test, you used 4 coins, 8 coins and 16 coins as the pull. Four times the coins did not need four times the turns. It needed only about twice the turns. So twice the speed needed four times the pull. A car going round a bend at 40 kilometres an hour needs four times the grip of the same car at 20 kilometres an hour. That is why a small increase in speed can suddenly be too much for a bend.
- e.g. Four times the coins held the whirled bag at about twice the turns in the same time.
- e.g. A car at 40 kilometres an hour needs four times as much grip on a bend as at 20 kilometres an hour.
- e.g. A merry-go-round that turns twice as fast needs four times the holding force on the riders.
### A tighter circle needs a bigger pull · *defined*
**Definition:** At the same speed, a circle with half the radius needs twice the pull.

A tight circle turns the direction more sharply at every moment than a wide one, so it needs more pull. A car can take a wide bend at 40 kilometres an hour, but a sharp hairpin bend on a ghat road needs a much lower speed, such as 15 or 20. On the same turntable, a coin near the middle goes round a small circle, and needs less friction to stay on than a coin near the edge, which goes round a bigger circle and also moves faster. That is why the coin at the edge slides off first.
- e.g. Road signs at hairpin bends on ghat roads tell drivers to slow down.
- e.g. A coin near the edge of a turntable slides off before a coin near the middle.
- e.g. A cycle taking a sharp turn must lean more than one taking a wide turn at the same speed.
### More mass needs a bigger pull, and the rule in one line · *defined*
**Definition:** The pull needed for a circle is the mass times the speed times the speed, divided by the radius. A bigger mass needs a bigger pull in the same proportion.

This comes from Newton's second law. A bigger mass needs a bigger force to change its motion. A stone of 0.2 kilograms whirled at 5 metres per second on a 1 metre string needs a pull of 0.2 x 5 x 5 divided by 1, which is 5 newtons. A child of 30 kilograms on a roundabout of radius 2 metres, going at 2 metres per second, needs a holding force of 30 x 2 x 2 divided by 2, which is 60 newtons. The rule says three things in one line: more mass needs more pull, more speed needs much more pull, and a bigger radius needs less.
- e.g. A stone of 0.2 kilograms at 5 metres per second on a 1 metre string needs 5 newtons.
- e.g. A child of 30 kilograms at 2 metres per second on a roundabout of radius 2 metres needs 60 newtons.
- e.g. A heavy lorry on a bend needs more grip from its tyres than a car at the same speed.
### The outward feeling on a bend is your own body going straight · *defined*
**Definition:** When a vehicle turns, your body tries to keep going straight. The seat, door or rail pushes you inward. What you feel is that push on your body, and your body pressing back because of its inertia.

In a bus going round a bend, you feel pressed against the window on the outside of the turn. No force is pushing you outward. Your body was going straight, and the bus turned under you. The window is the thing that pushes you inward, and bends your path into the curve. If the window suddenly vanished, you would not fly outward. You would carry on in a straight line, while the bus turned away. On a merry-go-round, the bar pushes inward on your back, and what you feel is your body pressing on the bar.
- e.g. In a bus on a left bend, you lean towards the right side.
- e.g. On a merry-go-round, you press on the bar that holds you in.
- e.g. In a car that turns sharply, a school bag slides towards the outside door.
### A spin sorts things: the dryer, the cream separator and the centrifuge · *defined*
**Definition:** In a spinning container, each part needs a pull towards the middle to stay in the circle. Parts that are held go round. Parts that are not held, or are heavier, move to the outside.

In a spin dryer, the wall of the drum holds the clothes in the circle, but the holes in the wall let the water go. Each drop goes straight on and leaves through a hole, so the water leaves and the clothes stay. Milk is a mixture of watery milk and fat, called cream. The watery milk is a little heavier. In a cream separator, the heavier watery milk is pushed to the outside, and the lighter cream gathers nearer the middle. The two leave by different pipes. In a hospital laboratory, a centrifuge spins tubes of blood. The heavier blood cells settle at the bottom of the tube, which is its outer end, and the lighter liquid stays above. Nobody has to choose which part goes where. The spin does it.
- e.g. A spin dryer throws water out through the holes in the drum, and leaves the clothes behind.
- e.g. A cream separator spins milk, so that the cream gathers nearer the middle.
- e.g. A laboratory centrifuge spins blood, so that the heavier blood cells settle at the outer end of the tube.
### Bends in roads, rails and cycles: friction, tilt and lean · *defined*
**Definition:** On a flat bend, the friction between the tyres and the road is the pull towards the middle. If the friction is not enough, the vehicle slides straight on. Tilting the road, or leaning the vehicle, helps.

A car on a flat road needs the friction of its tyres to pull it round a bend. On a wet or oily road, there is less friction, so the car can only take the bend at a lower speed. If it goes too fast, it slides straight on, off the road. Road builders tilt bends so that the outside is higher than the inside. Then part of the push of the road points towards the middle, and the car needs less friction. Railways raise the outer rail of a curve for the same reason. A cyclist leans into a bend so that the push of the ground points towards the middle. The faster the speed, or the tighter the bend, the more the cyclist must lean.
- e.g. A car on a wet road slides straight on if it takes a bend too fast.
- e.g. On many railway curves the outer rail is a little higher than the inner rail.
- e.g. A cyclist leans more in a tight turn, and more in a fast turn.
### Circles that go up and down: buckets, loops and giant wheels · *defined*
**Definition:** In a circle that goes up and over, the thing must be going fast enough at the top that gravity alone gives all the pull it needs. If it is slower, it leaves the circle and falls.

Swing a bucket of water in a circle over your head. At the top, the water is upside down. It stays in the bucket because the bucket moves down faster than the water would fall. The water presses on the bottom of the bucket, and the bottom pushes back. If you swing too slowly, the water falls out. For a circle with a radius of about 70 centimetres, the slowest safe swing takes about 1.7 seconds for a full turn. In a giant wheel at a mela, you feel lighter at the top, and heavier at the bottom. A roller-coaster train with a loop needs enough speed at the top, so the cars stay on the track. A marble on a loop usually needs to start about 2.5 to 3 times the loop's radius above the bottom of the loop.
- e.g. Water stays in a bucket swung fast enough in a vertical circle.
- e.g. A rider on a giant wheel feels lighter at the top and heavier at the bottom.
- e.g. A marble run with a loop needs the marble to start high enough.
### The Moon and the satellites: falling and always missing · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon is pulled towards the Earth by gravity. It is also moving sideways. It keeps falling towards the Earth, and keeps missing it, so it goes round.

Newton asked what would happen if a cannon on a very high mountain fired a ball sideways. A slow ball falls to the ground quite near. A faster ball lands further away. A ball fired fast enough keeps falling, but the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls, so the ball never lands. The Moon is like that ball. It goes round the Earth once in about 27 days, at about 1 kilometre every second. The International Space Station goes round the Earth about once every 90 minutes, at about 8 kilometres every second. Newton also compared the Moon's fall with the fall of an apple. The Moon is about 60 times as far from the Earth's middle as an apple on the ground. He reasoned that the pull weakens with the square of the distance, so it is about 3,600 times weaker there. The Moon's sideways speed does the rest.
- e.g. The Moon goes round the Earth once in about 27 days.
- e.g. The International Space Station goes round the Earth about once every 90 minutes.
- e.g. The Earth goes round the Sun at about 30 kilometres every second.
### Is centrifugal force real? · *debated*
Everyone agrees on what happens on a turntable or a bus. Scientists differ on what to call the outward feeling. Many teachers say there is no outward force at all, only inertia and an inward pull. Many engineers, who work inside spinning machines such as centrifuges, use 'centrifugal force' every day because it makes their sums easy. The old argument is about what spinning is measured against. Does a spinning bucket of water show spin in empty space, or only spin compared with the stars? Nobody has settled it.
- e.g. In a bus on a bend, you are pushed inward by the window, and you press back on it.
- e.g. Engineers use centrifugal force to design a laboratory centrifuge.
- **Isaac Newton, in 1687:** Newton spun a bucket of water and noticed that the water climbs the sides. He argued that this shows real spin, which is not just spin compared with other things.
- **Ernst Mach, an Austrian scientist, in 1883:** Mach suggested that spin might be spin compared with all the matter in the universe, such as the far stars. Nobody has shown whether he was right.
- **Many physics teachers today:** They teach that there is no outward force. There is the inertia of the thing, which goes straight, and the inward pull. The outward feeling is the push of the inward pull on you.
- **Many engineers today:** They use centrifugal force as a handy way to describe what happens inside a spinning machine. It is useful, even if it is not a force in the way a push or a pull is.

## Mostly agreed
- A thing moving in a circle needs a force pointing towards the middle of the circle, all the time. *How we know:* A bag whirled on a string stays in its circle only while the string pulls it in. A car stays on a bend only while the tyres grip. Satellites stay in orbit only because of gravity.
- When that force stops, the thing moves off in a straight line along the way it was moving at that moment. *How we know:* Sparks from a grinding wheel, mud from a tyre, drops from a wet umbrella and a released hammer in an athletics match all leave in straight lines.
- The force needed equals the mass times the speed times the speed, divided by the radius of the circle. *How we know:* This follows from Newton's second law and has been tested with whirlers like yours, with centrifuges and with satellites, for more than 300 years.
- The Moon and satellites are held in orbit by gravity. They keep falling towards the Earth, and keep missing it. *How we know:* The path of the Moon, the International Space Station and the satellites launched by ISRO can all be worked out from gravity and from their sideways speed.
- Spinning a mixture moves its heavier parts to the outside and its lighter parts nearer the middle. *How we know:* Dairies use cream separators, and hospital laboratories use centrifuges to separate the cells in blood from the liquid. Both work every day.
- On a flat bend, the friction of the tyres supplies the pull. If the friction is not enough, the car slides straight on. *How we know:* Skid marks on wet road bends run in straight lines off the curve. Roads and railways are tilted at bends to help.

## How we found out
**René Descartes, a French thinker, Christiaan Huygens, a Dutch scientist, and Isaac Newton, an English scientist, 1644 for Descartes's sling, 1659 for Huygens's rule, and the 1680s for Newton's Moon.** For about two thousand years, many thinkers followed Aristotle in saying that the heavens move in circles because circles are perfect and natural. They said a circular motion needs no cause. Some historians say that even the great Galileo, in his book of 1632, still treated circular motion as one that could go on by itself, though others disagree. In 1644, René Descartes looked at a stone whirled in a sling. He saw that the stone always tries to fly off in a straight line, and that the sling must keep pulling it back. So going in a circle is not natural. It needs a pull. In 1659, Christiaan Huygens worked out how big the pull must be. Then Isaac Newton asked what pulls the Moon. The Moon is about 60 times as far from the Earth's middle as an apple on the ground. He reasoned that the pull weakens with the square of the distance, so it is about 3,600 times weaker there, and later checks fitted the Moon's motion.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle and many thinkers after him taught that moving in circles is natural for heavenly things, and needs no cause. A stone in a sling showed that a thing in a circle is always trying to go straight, so something must pull it inward all the time.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A whirled stone, when let go, flies outward away from the middle.** (A common guess among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: While you whirl a stone, you feel it pulling outward on your hand.  
  Against: When you let go, the stone goes straight along the way it was moving, along the tangent, and not outward along the string. What you felt was the stone's inertia pulling on the string, not a push outward on the stone.
- **Something must push a thing forward all the time to keep it going round.** (A common idea among children, and an old idea from teachers who followed Aristotle; *rejected*)  
  For: A cycle slows down when you stop pedalling, so it feels as if pushing is needed to keep going.  
  Against: The force that makes a thing go round points towards the middle, at a right angle to its motion. It changes the direction but not the speed. A thing going round on smooth ice needs no forward push, only the pull inward.
- **A heavier coin on a turntable slides off before a lighter one.** (A very common guess, because heavier things seem harder to hold; *rejected*)  
  For: A heavier coin needs a bigger pull to be turned in a circle.  
  Against: A heavier coin also presses harder on the turntable, so the friction that holds it grows in the same proportion. The two cancel. A heavy coin and a light coin at the same distance slide off at the same speed. Only the distance from the middle, and the roughness of the surface, matter.
- **The Moon does not fall, because the Earth's gravity does not reach that far.** (A common idea among children and adults; *rejected*)  
  For: The Moon is far from the Earth, and astronauts seem to float up there.  
  Against: Gravity does reach the Moon. If it did not, the Moon would fly off in a straight line. At the Moon's distance, the Earth's pull is about 3,600 times weaker than here, but it is still enough to bend the Moon's path into a ring. Astronauts float because they are falling together with their spacecraft, not because gravity is missing.
- **A big spinning space station could give its crew a feeling like gravity.** (Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, a Russian scientist, in the early 1900s, and many engineers since; *accepted*)  
  For: On a spinning ring, the floor pushes the crew inward, as the wall pushes the clothes in a dryer. The push feels the same as weight.  
  Against: Nobody has built a large spinning station yet. If it is small, the floor would move faster at the head than at the feet, and people could feel dizzy. A big ring turning slowly works much better.
- **Centrifugal force is a real outward force, like a push or a pull.** (Many engineers, and many textbooks; *debated*)  
  For: Inside a spinning machine, everything behaves as if it were pushed outward, and it is easy to calculate with.  
  Against: Seen from outside, there is no outward force. There is only inertia, and the inward pull. Many physicists call it a helpful pretend force that only appears when you are inside the spin.

## Roots
We have measured the pull that keeps a bag going round, and seen what happens when it stops. Now we ask what going round tells us about the world and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** The bag on your string was pulled inward the whole time, yet it never came closer. It kept moving sideways while it was pulled. So going round is falling inward and sideways together, without ever arriving. The Moon moves like this round the Earth.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat falling and going round as opposites. A fall ends on the ground, and going round seems calm and steady. But a thing in a circle is pulled inward every moment, and never arrives only because its sideways motion keeps carrying it past.  
*Evidence:* In the whirler test, the bag kept circling only while the coins pulled on the string, and a faster circle needed many more coins. Without that pull, things leave in straight lines. If your results differ, write them down as they are.

- *who am i:* You are riding on a spinning Earth, and the Earth is going round the Sun. Gravity holds you to the ground while you are carried round, and you feel nothing of it.
- *what is life:* A crow turning in the air tilts her body into the turn, and a girl on a cycle leans into the bend. Living bodies learn how much to lean for the speed and the circle.
- *what is this world:* A stone on a string, wet clothes in a dryer and the Moon going round the Earth all follow one rule. A thing goes round only while something pulls it towards the middle.

Look at the stone on the string, the clothes in the dryer and the Moon together. Each is pulled towards a middle, and each would go straight on if the pull stopped. The circle is what you get when those two meet. The same simple rule holds from a toy to a planet: the pull needed grows with the square of the speed. So the many circles of the world come from one pull and one straight-line habit.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes meet in one hub, but it is the empty space in the hub that makes the wheel useful. (At the middle of the whirler there is only a hole in a pen. Yet the pull on the bag points to it all the time. The empty middle holds everything in its circle.)
- **Rig Veda 1.164.11:** The ancient singers of the Rig Veda spoke of a wheel with twelve spokes that turns round the sky and does not wear out. They saw the turning of the sky as a wheel of order. (Today we know that a pull towards a middle keeps the Moon and the planets going round, year after year, just like the bag on a string.)
- Science (settled): A thing in a circle needs a force towards the middle that equals its mass times its speed times its speed, divided by the radius. This holds from whirled bags to planets.
- Science (debated): Whether the outward feeling on a bend should be called a force, and what spin is measured against, are still discussed by physicists and philosophers.
- Other view, Albert Einstein's theory of general relativity: In Einstein's theory, nothing pulls the Moon at all. The Earth bends space and time around it, and the Moon follows the straightest path through that bent space. 'Falling' is then another way to describe the same thing, and 'a pull' is a picture that works very well but is not the last word.
- Practice, The slow turn (3 min): Stand with your arms hanging loose. Turn slowly on the spot, once all the way round. Notice that your arms drift outward a little. Now stand still, and breathe out slowly three times. Feel the floor pushing up on your feet. Remember that the Earth is turning, and you are riding it.
- Ask: If going round is falling sideways, what are you falling around right now?
- Ask: Is the feeling of being pushed outward on a bend real, if nobody is pushing you?
- Ask: Is a pull a thing in the world, or a picture we draw to describe how things move?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* On a bus bend, you feel pushed outward, yet nobody is pushing you. Where does the feeling come from, and is the push real? (hint: Think about what your body is doing, what the window is doing, and whether a feeling needs a push from outside.)
- *deep:* The Moon is falling towards the Earth all the time, yet it never gets closer. How can something fall and never arrive? (hint: Think of a ball fired sideways from a very high mountain, faster and faster.)
- *deep:* If you were in a closed room with no windows, how could you tell whether the room was sitting on the ground in gravity, or spinning in space? (hint: Think of the clothes pressed against the wall of the dryer. What would a ball do if you dropped it?)
- *deep:* If you whirl a bag twice as fast, the pull is four times as big. Why would nature make the pull grow faster than the speed? (hint: Think of two things happening at once when you go twice as fast in the same circle.)
- *big:* Many things in the sky go round: the Moon, the Earth, whole galaxies. Why do almost all of them spin? (hint: Think of a big cloud of gas that shrinks and begins to turn. What happens to a turn when something gets smaller?)
- *big:* Would you like to live in a spinning space town? What would 'down' mean there, and what would be strange? (hint: Think of the floor of a giant wheel, and what happens if you walk along it or throw a ball.)
- *big:* Who decides how fast a car may take a bend, and how do they know it is safe for every driver? (hint: Think of a road builder, a wet day, a heavy lorry and an old tyre.)
- *big:* A spin separates milk from cream and blood cells from liquid without anyone choosing what goes where. What other sorting happens in nature without a sorter? (hint: Think of heavy pebbles and light sand in a river, or the layers of oil on water.)

## Story: The Wheel That Threw Its Clay
*Thulasi, Kamatchi Paati, Mani Anna, Bala and this morning are made up. Manamadurai, near Madurai, is a real town known for its clay pots, and Karthigai Deepam is a real festival of lamps in Tamil Nadu. The science in the story is real: potters slow the wheel for wide pots, and wet clay leaves a wheel that spins too fast in straight lines.*

In Manamadurai, a town known for its clay pots, every morning began with the smell of wet earth. Thulasi was ten. She sat beside her grandmother, Kamatchi Paati, at the old wooden wheel in the courtyard, and watched her hands.

Paati's wheel was a heavy stone disc on a pin. She turned it with a stick, and it hummed round and round. On top of it sat a ball of wet clay. With two wet thumbs, Paati opened the ball like a flower. In a minute, it was a pot.

Before Karthigai Deepam, the festival of lamps, the whole street made diyas. This year, a shop in Madurai had asked for five hundred, and the temple had asked for forty wide water bowls. Paati's arms were tired. Her eyes were tired too.

On Monday, a man called Mani Anna came with a box and a motor. He fitted a belt to the wheel and plugged it in. 'Now it turns by itself,' he said. 'Faster than any stick. Turn the knob to the right, and it goes quicker, and quicker still. You will finish your order in half the time.'

Thulasi's little brother Bala clapped. Paati looked at the knob with narrow eyes. 'A wheel is a friend,' she said slowly. 'But a friend who is asked to go too fast will let go of you.'

Mani Anna laughed. He turned the knob all the way to the right. The disc hummed. Paati put a ball of clay in the middle and opened it with her thumbs. At once the clay began to climb up and out. Then flick, flick, flick, lumps of clay and drops of water flew off the wheel. They spattered across the wall, and across Bala's face. The pot sagged and tore.

'Aiyo!' said Bala. Mani Anna wiped his cheek. 'Maybe too fast,' he said. 'But what is the right speed?'

Nobody answered. Thulasi looked at the wall. The spots on it were not in a ring round the wheel. They were in long, straight streaks, like arrows. Each streak began at the edge of the wheel and pointed along the way the wheel had been turning.

'Paati,' she said. 'The drops did not fly outward. They flew straight on.' 'Go on,' said Paati.

'The clay at the edge goes round and round,' said Thulasi. 'Something must hold it in. The wet clay holds on to the wheel, and holds on to the clay beside it. When the wheel is slow, the holding is enough. When the wheel is fast, the holding is not enough. Then the drop is let go, and it goes straight, the way it was already moving.'

Mani Anna scratched his head. 'But how fast is too fast?'

'It depends where you are,' said Thulasi. 'Clay near the middle goes round a tiny circle. Clay at the edge goes round a big one, and moves much faster. So the edge lets go first.'

She dipped her finger in water and put a drop near the middle of the disc. Then she put another near the edge. 'Turn it very slowly, Anna,' she said. He turned the knob one notch. Both drops stayed. Two notches, and the drop at the edge rolled off. The drop in the middle stayed until the fourth notch.

'The wider the pot, the closer the clay is to the edge,' said Paati softly. 'My mother told me, the wider the pot, the slower the wheel. I have known it in my hands for sixty years. Now this girl knows it in words.'

They tried again. For the narrow diyas, Mani Anna turned the knob to the fourth notch. For the wide water bowls, only to the second. The clay stayed on the wheel. The pots rose smooth and round, one after another, like lotus buds.

When the order was done, Mani Anna painted four little marks on the knob, and wrote 'Thulasi's rule' beside them. 'Wide pot, slow wheel,' he said. 'Narrow pot, quicker wheel.' Thulasi looked at the wall, where the streaks of clay still pointed along the way the wheel had turned. The clay had never wanted to go round, she thought. It only wanted to go straight, and the wheel had kept asking it to turn.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a circle needs a pull towards the middle at every moment, and in the wheel that pull is the grip of the wet clay. The pull needed grows with the square of the speed, and with the distance from the middle, so the edge of the wheel is the first place where the grip fails. When the grip fails, the clay does not move outward. It goes straight, along the way it was moving, which is why the streaks on the wall were straight. Wide pots reach out to the edge, so they need a slower wheel. This is the same rule as the whirler and the coins on the turntable.

- Ask: Why were the streaks on the wall straight, and not curved?
- Ask: Why did the drop at the edge of the wheel roll off before the drop near the middle?
- Ask: Why does a wide pot need a slower wheel than a narrow one?
- Ask: Thulasi tested with drops of water before she told anyone her idea. Why might that have been better than just saying it?
- Ask: How could you test Thulasi's rule with a turntable and some coins?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Whirler: how fast must a bag go round for each size of pull? (35 min)
- **question:** A small bag whirls in a circle on a string, and the pull on the string comes from coins hanging at the other end. When the pull is bigger, how much faster must the bag go round to stay in the same circle?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If you use four times as many coins, will the bag need to go round four times as fast, or less than four times as fast, or more? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same bag, the same string, the same pen, and the same length of string from the pen to the bag every time. A piece of tape on the string marks the length. Only the number of coins at the hanging end changes: 4, 8, then 16. Keep the tape at the top of the pen while you whirl. The helper counts how many turns the bag makes in 10 seconds. Do 3 tries for each number of coins, because one try can be a fluke, and use the middle result.
- **safety:** Use only the soft bag, never a hard object. Whirl it in front of you at arm's length, away from faces and from other people. A grown-up stays close. Keep coins away from children under five.
- **record:** Fill in the table, one row for each number of coins. Then draw a bar chart of the middle results. Under the chart, write how the turns changed when the coins became four times as many.
- **results table:** Coins hanging | Turns in 10 seconds, try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | Middle result
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the pen, the string, the bag and the pouch of coins. Explain that the coins pull the string, and the bag must go round fast enough to hold the string still. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* The coins hang below and pull on the string. The bag must go round fast enough to hold them up. If we use four times as many coins, how much faster must the bag go? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Coins pull the string. The bag goes round to hold them. Four times the coins. How much faster must the bag go? Why?
  - Ask: If the coins are four times as many, how many times as fast must the bag go round? Why? (good: Four times as fast, because the pull is four times as big.; Less than four times as fast.; Twice as fast.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children say four times as fast. Write down the guess and the reason.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up takes the ink refill out of the pen and checks the ends are smooth. 2. Push the string through the pen. 3. Tie the bag to one end. 4. Tie the pouch to the other end. 5. Stick the tape on the string 50 centimetres from the bag. 6. Put 4 coins in the pouch.  
  *Parent says:* We are building the whirler. The string goes through the pen. The bag is on top and the coins hang below. The tape shows how long the string is above the pen.  
  *Child mode:* Thread the string through the pen. Tie on the bag. Tie on the pouch. Stick on the tape.
  - Note: The bag should be as light as you can make it, just 2 coins in a knotted handkerchief. If the pouch is lighter than the bag, the bag will hang down instead of whirling. Check that no sharp edge of the pen can rub the string.
  - Check: Why is a piece of tape stuck on the string at 50 centimetres from the bag? → **So the circle is the same size every time**
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the pen upright at arm's length, in front of you. Whirl the bag in a circle. Find the speed at which the pouch stays still and the tape stays at the top of the pen. Practise until you can keep it steady for 10 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Whirl the bag. If the pouch drops, whirl a little faster. If the pouch rises, slow down a little. Find the speed that keeps the tape still.  
  *Child mode:* Whirl the bag. Pouch drops? Go faster. Pouch rises? Go slower. Keep the tape still.
  - Note: A grown-up stands close. If the bag will not whirl and hangs low, add 2 coins to the pouch or use a lighter bag. It takes most children a few tries. The speed does not need to be exact. A small band of speeds keeps the tape steady, so aim for the middle of it.
- **s4 (13 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put 4 coins in the pouch. Whirl the bag steadily. The helper starts the stopwatch and counts the turns in 10 seconds. Do 3 tries. Then repeat with 8 coins, then with 16 coins.  
  *Parent says:* Start whirling. When the tape is steady, say go. Count every time the bag passes in front of you for 10 seconds. Write down the number.  
  *Child mode:* Whirl the bag steadily. The helper counts the turns in 10 seconds. Do it three times for each pouch.
  - Note: Use the middle of the three results. With more coins, the bag should go round faster and faster. With 16 coins the bag goes round nearly twice every second, so the helper can count in twos or film it. Expect roughly 9, 13 and 18 turns for 4, 8 and 16 coins. Four times the coins should need about twice the turns, not four times. If counting is hard, film the bag with a phone and count the turns when you play it back.
  - Check: With 16 coins, the bag went round faster than with 4 coins. Why did it need to? → **A bigger pull on the string needs a faster circle to match it**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: their claim, their evidence and their reason. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? What numbers show it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say what numbers show it. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: When the coins became four times as many, how many times as many turns did the bag need? (good: About twice as many turns.; Less than four times as many.; The turns grew more slowly than the coins.)
  - Note: After your child has said their ideas, tell them: this fits the rule. Twice the speed needs four times the pull. Four times the coins needed only about twice the turns. If the results were messy, say that scientists see that too, and that is why we do each setting three times.
  - Think: The pouch of coins is the thing that pulled the bag inward. If the string snapped, where would the bag go?
### e2 · Coins on the Turntable: which one slides off first? (30 min)
- **question:** Coins sit at different distances from the middle of a plate that turns faster and faster. Which coin slides off first? Does a heavy stack of coins slide off before a single coin?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which coin will slide off first, the one near the middle or the one near the edge? Will a heavy stack of coins slide off before a single coin? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same plate and the same type of coin every time. In Part A, put three coins on rings marked at 5, 10 and 15 centimetres from the middle, on different sides of the plate. Turn the plate faster in steps: one turn in 4 seconds, in 3 seconds, in 2 seconds, and then as fast as you safely can. In Part B, put a single coin and a stack of 3 coins taped together on the same ring, on opposite sides. Do 3 tries of each part, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Nobody sits on the turntable. Keep fingers away from the moving seat, and turn it by hand only. Stand a metre back, because a coin can fly off. Keep coins away from children under five.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each try. Draw a picture of the plate from above, with the three coins on their rings, and number them in the order that they slid off.
- **results table:** Try | First coin to slide off | Second coin | Third coin | Speed when the first coin slid off
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the plate, the coins and the turntable. Ask for a guess and a reason for each question. Write the guesses on a sheet of paper.  
  *Parent says:* We will put coins on a plate and turn it faster and faster. Which coin do you think will slide off first? Will a heavy stack slide off before a single coin? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Coins on a plate that turns faster and faster. Which slides off first? Will a heavy stack go before one coin? Why?
  - Ask: Which coin do you think will slide off first, and why? (good: The one near the edge, because it goes round a bigger circle.; The one near the middle.; The heavy one, because it is harder to hold.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say a heavy stack will slide off first. Write down what your child says and why.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw three rings on the plate, at 5, 10 and 15 centimetres from the middle. 2. Put the plate on the turntable, with its middle over the middle of the turntable. 3. Put one coin on each ring, on different sides of the plate. 4. Tape three coins into a stack.  
  *Parent says:* We will draw three rings on the plate. The coins will sit on different rings and on different sides, so they do not bump each other.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three rings on the plate. Put one coin on each ring, on different sides.
  - Note: The plate must be flat and centred on the turntable. The smoother the plate, the easier it is for a coin to slide. If your plate is rough, put a sheet of smooth plastic on it. Wipe the coins and the plate clean first.
  - Check: Why do we put the coins on different sides of the plate, and not in a line? → **So that a coin that slides off does not knock the others**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Part A. The helper turns the plate by hand. First one turn in 4 seconds, counting 'one-and, two-and, three-and, four-and'. Then one turn in 3 seconds, then in 2 seconds, then as fast as is safe. After each speed, stop and look at which coins are left. Do 3 tries.  
  *Parent says:* Turn the plate slowly, one turn for each count of four. Keep it steady for three turns. Then go a little faster. Stop each time and look. Which coin has gone?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the plate slowly. Keep it steady. Then go faster. Stop and look. Which coin has gone?
  - Note: On a smooth steel plate, the coin at 15 centimetres usually slides when a turn takes about 1.5 to 2 seconds. The coin at 10 centimetres goes at a quicker turn, and the coin at 5 centimetres may only go at about one turn a second, which may be as fast as a hand can turn it. If nothing slides, use a smoother plate. If all of the coins slide at the slowest speed, use a rougher plate. Write down the order each time.
  - Check: The coin at the edge of the plate slid off before the coin near the middle. What is the best reason? → **The coin at the edge goes round a bigger circle, and needs more friction to stay on**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Part B. Take the coins off. Put a single coin and the stack of 3 coins on opposite sides of the 10 centimetre ring. Turn the plate faster and faster as before. Watch which one slides first. Do 3 tries.  
  *Parent says:* Now a single coin and a heavy stack sit on the same ring. Which will slide off first? Turn the plate faster, and watch.  
  *Child mode:* A single coin and a stack on the same ring. Turn faster. Which slides first?
  - Note: The stack and the single coin should slide off at about the same time. If the stack slides a little before or after, the tape or the plate may be uneven, so repeat the try. The stack is three times as heavy, so it needs three times the pull. It also presses three times as hard on the plate, so the friction is three times as big.
  - Check: The single coin and the stack of three coins slid off at about the same time. What is the best explanation? → **The stack needs three times the pull, but the plate also grips it three times as hard**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write their claim, their evidence and their reason. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? Which coin went first? Did the heavy stack go first? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the coin at the edge go first, and why did the stack and the single coin go together? (good: The edge goes round a bigger circle, so it needs more friction.; The stack is heavier, but it also grips harder.; Only the distance from the middle and the plate mattered.)
  - Note: After your child has said their ideas: friction is the pull towards the middle. A coin slides off when the speed is too high for the friction to hold it. The pull needed grows with the distance from the middle and with the square of the speed. The friction available grows with the weight, and so does the pull needed. So the weight cancels, and only the distance and the roughness are left.
  - Think: The friction of the plate was the only pull towards the middle. What would happen with a rougher plate?

  *Child mode:* The marble seems to push outward on the wall. What is pushing it inward?

  - Note: After your child has answered: the marble presses on the wall because it tries to go straight on. The wall pushes back inward, and that push is what bends the marble's path into a circle. The push on the marble is inward, and the press on the wall is the marble's own pressing.

### c1 · The Coin Keeper: keep three coins on a plate at one turn a second (40 min)
- **problem:** Make a plate that keeps three coins on it, starting at 15 centimetres from the middle, while the turntable turns once every second for 5 turns. Make it keep the coins at as fast a speed as you can.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the cardboard and any sandpaper. Stand a metre back from the turntable, because a coin can fly off. Keep coins away from children under five.

- **constraints:** The three coins must start on the ring 15 centimetres from the middle.; You may change the plate's surface, tilt it, or add a rim. You may not tape, glue or stick the coins down.; The turntable must turn once every second, counted aloud, for 5 turns.; The coins must all stay on the plate for the whole test.; Test it 3 times, and the best of the three counts.
- **general:** Put a rough surface on the plate, such as a rubber mat or sandpaper, to give more friction.; Cover the plate with a towel or a cloth, so the coins grip.; Use a plate whose surface is more sticky than a smooth steel thali.
- **creative:** Add a low cardboard rim round the edge of the plate. The rim can push the coins inward, and the push does not depend on friction.; Tilt the plate into a shallow dish shape, by laying a bowl under a rubber mat, so the dish wall pushes the coins inward.; Raise the plate's outer edge a little, so the coins lean inward towards the middle.

  *Child mode:* Coins at the edge. A fast turn. How can you keep them on? Draw some ideas.

## Worksheets
### Going Round (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Priya whirls a stone on a string, and the string snaps. Which way does the stone go?  
   Options: Straight on, along the way it was moving / Straight outward, away from her hand / Round in a curve  
   **Answer:** Straight on, along the way it was moving. *Why:* When the pull stops, nothing bends the stone's path. It goes straight on, along the line that just touches the circle. Picking 'outward' is the most common mix-up: the stone does not go along the string.
2. (fill) A force that points towards the middle of a circle and keeps a thing going round is called the ____ force.  
   **Answer:** ['centripetal']. *Why:* Centripetal means centre-seeking. It is not a new kind of force. It is the name for whatever pull or push points to the middle, such as a string, friction or gravity.
3. (truefalse) Karthik says, 'When you let go of a whirled stone, it flies outward along the string.' Is he right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The stone goes straight on, along the way it was moving at that moment, which is at a right angle to the string. Your fingers felt the stone pulling outward on the string, but the stone itself was never pushed outward.
4. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Radius → the distance from the centre of a circle to its edge; Tangent → a straight line that just touches a circle; Centripetal force → a pull towards the middle of the circle; Turn → one full trip round a circle; Orbit → the path of one thing going round another because of gravity. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit. A strong answer could also give an example of each, such as the Moon for orbit and sparks from a grinding wheel for tangent.
5. (sort) Divya lists six things that go round. Sort each one by what gives the pull towards the middle.  
   **Answer:** A bag whirled on a string → A string or wire; A car on a flat bend → Friction; The Moon going round the Earth → Gravity; A coin on a turning plate → Friction; A hammer on a wire, before it is thrown → A string or wire; A satellite going round the Earth → Gravity. *Why:* Whatever goes round needs a pull towards the middle, and something has to give it. A string gives tension, tyres and plates give friction, and the Earth gives gravity. A strong answer can say what would happen to each thing if its pull stopped.
6. (count) A bag goes round 12 times in 10 seconds. How many turns does it make in 30 seconds, if it keeps the same pace?  
   **Answer:** 36. *Why:* 30 seconds is 3 times as long as 10 seconds. 12 times 3 is 36 turns.
7. (mcq) A stone is whirled on a string in a circle. The stone is whirled twice as fast in the same circle. How does the pull needed change?  
   Options: It becomes twice as big / It becomes four times as big / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It becomes four times as big. *Why:* The pull grows with the square of the speed. Twice the speed needs 2 times 2, which is 4 times the pull. Picking 'twice as big' is the natural guess, and it is the one the whirler test showed was too small.
8. (order) Put the steps of the Whirler test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Guess how the turns will change with more coins', 'Build the whirler with the pen, string, bag and pouch', 'Practise until the tape stays steady', 'Count the turns in 10 seconds with each number of coins', 'Say what you found and why']. *Why:* First guess, so the test can surprise you. Then build and practise, count with each number of coins, and last say what you found and why.
9. (write) In the Whirler test, four times the coins made the bag go round only about twice as fast. Explain why four times the pull does not make it four times as fast.  
   **Answer:** A faster circle needs a pull that grows with the square of the speed. Twice the speed needs four times the pull, so four times the pull only makes the bag go about twice as fast.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the pull grows with the square of the speed, so the speed grows more slowly than the pull.
10. (odd) Three of these happen because a thing goes straight on when its pull stops. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Sparks leave a grinding wheel / Mud flies off a fast tyre / Drops fly from a spun wet umbrella / The Moon keeps going round the Earth  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The Moon is still being pulled by the Earth's gravity, so it keeps going round. In the other three, the pull that held the spark, the mud or the drop was too small, so each of them went straight on.
11. (label) Here is a picture of a bag whirled on a string. Label the four parts.  
   **Answer:** ['the bag: the thing going round', 'the string: gives the pull towards the middle', 'the hand at the middle: the centre of the circle', 'the straight line: along the way the bag was moving at that moment']. *Why:* The string gives the pull that holds the bag in its circle. If the string snapped, the bag would go along the straight line that just touches the circle.
12. (mcq) In a spin dryer, why do the clothes stay in the drum while the water leaves?  
   Options: The wall holds the clothes in the circle, but the holes let the water go straight on / The water is lighter than the clothes / The holes suck the water out  
   **Answer:** The wall holds the clothes in the circle, but the holes let the water go straight on. *Why:* The wall pushes the clothes inward and keeps them in the circle. Where there is a hole, nothing holds the water, so it goes straight on and leaves. Nothing sucks.
13. (mcq) Coins sit on a plate that turns faster and faster. Which coin slides off first?  
   Options: The coin nearest the edge / The coin nearest the middle / The heaviest coin  
   **Answer:** The coin nearest the edge. *Why:* The coin at the edge goes round the biggest circle and moves fastest, so it needs the biggest pull from friction. Weight does not settle it, because a heavier coin is also gripped harder.
14. (perspective) Two friends in Mysuru talk about a bus turning left.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with Imran: the body's inertia keeps it going straight, and the window gives the inward push that bends its path. It is fair to Pooja: she really did feel pressed against the window, and the feeling is real. Many engineers call that feeling centrifugal force, and scientists still discuss how to describe it.. *Why:* Both children describe the same thing. What differs is whether the outward feeling is a force on the body, or the body's own tendency to go straight.
15. (draw) Draw a stone going round on a string. Mark the point where the string snaps, and draw the straight path the stone takes afterwards.  
   **Answer:** A circle with a stone on it, and a straight line leaving the circle at the point where the string snaps, touching the circle.. *Why:* After the string snaps, the stone goes along the way it was moving, so its path is a straight line that just touches the circle. A strong drawing does not show the line going outward from the middle.
16. (mcq) A car takes a bend on a wet road and slides straight off the road. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: The tyres did not have enough grip to give the pull towards the middle / The car became heavier on the bend / The road pulled the car outward  
   **Answer:** The tyres did not have enough grip to give the pull towards the middle. *Why:* On a flat bend, friction gives the pull towards the middle. A wet road gives less friction, so the car could not be held in the curve and went straight on.
### Pull, Speed and Size (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (count) The pull needed to hold a stone in a circle is the mass times the speed times the speed, divided by the radius. A stone of 0.5 kilograms goes round at 4 metres per second on a string 2 metres long. What is the pull, in newtons?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 0.5 times 4 times 4 is 8. 8 divided by 2 is 4. The pull is 4 newtons.
2. (count) A stone of 0.5 kilograms needs a pull of 4 newtons to go round at 4 metres per second on a string 2 metres long. How many newtons does it need at 8 metres per second on the same string?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* Twice the speed needs four times the pull. 4 times 4 is 16. The pull is 16 newtons.
3. (mcq) A bag needs a pull of 3 newtons to go round at a steady speed in a circle. It is whirled twice as fast in the same circle. What pull does it need now?  
   Options: 6 newtons / 12 newtons / 9 newtons  
   **Answer:** 12 newtons. *Why:* Twice the speed needs four times the pull. 3 times 4 is 12. Picking 6 doubles the pull when the speed doubles, and forgets that the pull grows with the square of the speed.
4. (mcq) A stone on a 1 metre string needs a pull of 8 newtons at a certain speed. The string is replaced by one 2 metres long, and the stone goes at the same speed. What pull does it need?  
   Options: 4 newtons / 8 newtons / 16 newtons  
   **Answer:** 4 newtons. *Why:* A circle that is twice as wide needs half the pull at the same speed. Half of 8 is 4. Picking 16 reverses the rule.
5. (truefalse) Heena says, 'A heavy coin and a light coin at the same distance from the middle of a turntable slide off at the same speed of turning.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A heavier coin needs a bigger pull, but it also presses harder on the plate, so the friction that holds it grows in the same proportion. The two cancel, and only the distance and the roughness of the plate matter.
6. (sort) Sort each change: does it make the pull needed bigger or smaller?  
   **Answer:** The same bag whirled twice as fast → Needs a bigger pull; The same bag in a circle twice as wide, at the same speed → Needs a smaller pull; A heavier bag at the same speed in the same circle → Needs a bigger pull; A car taking the same bend at half the speed → Needs a smaller pull; A coin moved from the edge of a turntable to the middle, with the turntable turning at the same rate → Needs a smaller pull; A lorry in place of a car, at the same speed on the same bend → Needs a bigger pull. *Why:* More speed, more mass and a tighter circle all need a bigger pull. A slower speed, a wider circle and a smaller mass need a smaller pull. A coin near the middle goes round a smaller circle and moves more slowly.
7. (fill) A machine that spins things fast, so that heavier parts move to the outside, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['centrifuge']. *Why:* A centrifuge spins a mixture, so the heavier parts move to the outer end and the lighter parts stay nearer the middle. Hospitals use them to separate blood.
8. (mcq) The Moon keeps going round the Earth and never crashes into it. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: It keeps falling towards the Earth, but it moves sideways fast enough to keep missing / It is too far away for gravity to reach / It is held up by the air  
   **Answer:** It keeps falling towards the Earth, but it moves sideways fast enough to keep missing. *Why:* Gravity pulls the Moon towards the Earth all the time. The Moon also moves sideways at about 1 kilometre every second, so as it falls, the Earth's surface curves away. It keeps missing. Gravity does reach the Moon, and there is no air to hold it.
9. (write) A car takes a bend at 60 kilometres an hour. The driver slows to 30 kilometres an hour. How does the grip needed from the tyres change? Explain.  
   **Answer:** The grip needed becomes one quarter as big. Half the speed needs one quarter of the pull towards the middle, because the pull grows with the square of the speed. That is why slowing down for a bend helps so much.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the grip needed falls to one quarter, and links it to the square of the speed.
10. (draw) Draw a cyclist taking a bend to the left. Show which way the cyclist leans, and draw an arrow for the push of the ground on the cycle.  
   **Answer:** A cyclist leaning to the left, towards the inside of the bend, with an arrow from the ground pointing inward and up through the cycle.. *Why:* A cyclist leans into the bend, so that the push of the ground points towards the middle of the turn. The faster the speed or the tighter the bend, the more the cyclist leans.
11. (perspective) Two friends in Pune talk about the Moon.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with Sana: gravity holds the Moon in its ring, and if it stopped, the Moon would fly off in a straight line. It is fair to Neel: the Moon is far away and its pull is much weaker there, about 3,600 times weaker than at the ground, and astronauts in space do feel weightless because they are falling together with their spacecraft.. *Why:* Neel has noticed that gravity is weaker far away and that astronauts float, which is true. But weaker is not zero. The Moon needs a pull towards the middle, and gravity gives it.
12. (match) Match each moving thing to what gives its pull towards the middle.  
   **Answer:** A whirled bag → the tension in the string; A car on a flat bend → the friction between tyres and road; The Moon → the Earth's gravity; Clothes in a spin dryer → the push of the drum wall; A rider on a giant wheel → the push of the seat and the safety bar. *Why:* Whatever goes round needs a pull towards the middle. In each case something different supplies it, but its job is the same.
13. (mcq) A bucket of water is swung in a circle over a person's head, and the water stays in at the top. This happens only if  
   Options: the bucket is moving downward faster than the water would fall / the water is very cold / the bucket is pushed up by the air  
   **Answer:** the bucket is moving downward faster than the water would fall. *Why:* At the top, the bucket's bottom must push down on the water faster than gravity alone would. If it moved more slowly, the water would fall away from it.
14. (count) A bag makes 5 turns in 10 seconds. How many seconds does it take for one turn?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 10 seconds divided by 5 turns is 2 seconds for one turn.
15. (mcq) A cream separator spins milk. Where does the cream, the lighter part, gather?  
   Options: Nearer the middle / At the outer wall / It stays evenly spread through the milk  
   **Answer:** Nearer the middle. *Why:* The heavier watery milk is pushed to the outside, and the lighter cream gathers nearer the middle. The two can then leave through different pipes.
16. (label) Here is a top view of a bus turning left with a passenger standing in it. Label what each arrow shows.  
   **Answer:** ["the arrow pointing straight ahead: the way the passenger's body wants to go", 'the arrow from the window: the push of the window on the passenger', 'the curved arrow: the path the bus takes as it turns', 'the passenger: pressed against the window on the outside of the turn']. *Why:* The passenger's body wants to go straight on. The window pushes inward, and bends the passenger's path into the turn. The pressing feeling is the passenger's own body pressing on the window.
17. (odd) Three of these need a pull towards the middle all the time. Which one does not?  
   Options: The Moon going round the Earth / A car taking a bend / A coin sliding straight off a turntable / A stone whirled on a string  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A coin sliding straight off has lost its pull towards the middle, so it goes straight on. The other three are going round, and each needs a pull towards the middle at every moment.
### Puzzles of the Spin (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (count) The pull needed is the mass times the speed times the speed, divided by the radius. A child of 40 kilograms sits at the edge of a roundabout of radius 2 metres, going at 3 metres per second. How many newtons of holding force does the child need?  
   **Answer:** 180. *Why:* 40 times 3 times 3 is 360. 360 divided by 2 is 180. The child needs 180 newtons.
2. (count) A stone of 1 kilogram needs a pull of 9 newtons on a string 1 metre long, at a certain speed. How many newtons does it need on a string 3 metres long, at the same speed?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A circle 3 times as wide needs one third of the pull at the same speed. 9 divided by 3 is 3 newtons.
3. (mcq) A road bend is rebuilt in a circle twice as wide, with the same speed limit. What is the effect on the grip each car needs?  
   Options: It needs half as much grip / It needs the same grip / It needs twice as much grip  
   **Answer:** It needs half as much grip. *Why:* A circle twice as wide needs half the pull at the same speed. That is one reason why road builders try to make bends wide.
4. (truefalse) In a spin dryer, the drum wall pushes the water outward through the holes. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The wall pushes the clothes inward, which holds them in the circle. The wall does not push the water. Where there are holes, nothing holds the water, so it goes straight on and leaves.
5. (write) On a bus bend, you feel pressed against the window on the outside. Explain what is really happening, using the words 'inertia' and 'inward'.  
   **Answer:** Your body has inertia, so it keeps going straight while the bus turns. The window pushes you inward, which bends your path into the turn. The feeling of being pressed outward is your own body pressing on the window.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the body tries to go straight, that the window pushes it inward, and that the outward feeling is the body pressing on the window.
6. (perspective) Two friends in Guwahati talk about a coin that slides off a spinning plate.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with Rohit, using evidence: the coin at the edge slid off first, because friction was not enough to give the pull it needed, and the coin went straight on. It is fair to Mina: in the spinning plate, things do seem to be pushed outward, and engineers use the idea of a centrifugal force to describe it.. *Why:* Both friends describe what happens on a spinning plate. Rohit describes it as seen from outside, where nothing pushes outward. Mina describes it as it appears from the plate.
7. (mcq) A satellite goes round the Earth at a steady speed. If the Earth's gravity suddenly vanished, what would the satellite do?  
   Options: Go off in a straight line / Fall straight down / Keep going round in a circle  
   **Answer:** Go off in a straight line. *Why:* Gravity is the pull that bends the satellite's path into a circle. With it gone, the satellite would go straight on, along the way it was moving.
8. (order) The same stone goes round on the same string at three speeds. Put the speeds in order, from the one that needs the smallest pull to the one that needs the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['1 metre per second', '2 metres per second', '3 metres per second']. *Why:* The pull grows with the square of the speed. At 1 metre per second the pull is 1 unit, at 2 it is 4 units and at 3 it is 9 units.
9. (draw) A wet umbrella is spinning, with its rim going round anticlockwise. Draw the umbrella from above, and show the path of a drop of water as it leaves the rim.  
   **Answer:** A circle for the umbrella, and a drop leaving the rim along a straight line that touches the circle, in the direction the rim was moving.. *Why:* The drop goes straight on, along the way the rim was moving at that moment. Its path is a straight line that touches the circle, not a spoke going outward.
10. (sort) Does each thing keep going round, or go straight on?  
   **Answer:** A satellite with the Earth's pull still on it → Keeps going round; A coin that has just lost its grip on a turntable → Goes straight on; Mud leaving a tyre → Goes straight on; Clothes in a spin dryer, held by the wall → Keeps going round; A hammer after the thrower lets go → Goes straight on; A car on a bend with enough grip → Keeps going round. *Why:* A thing keeps going round only while the pull towards the middle is enough. When the pull stops or is not enough, the thing goes straight on.
11. (mcq) A hospital centrifuge spins a tube of blood. Where do the heavier blood cells gather?  
   Options: At the outer end of the tube / Near the middle / At the top of the liquid  
   **Answer:** At the outer end of the tube. *Why:* The heavier cells need more pull to stay in the circle, so they move to the outside. In the tube, the outside is the bottom end. The lighter liquid stays above.
12. (fill) In a circle, the distance from the centre to the edge is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['radius']. *Why:* The radius tells you how tight or wide the circle is. A string 40 centimetres long makes a circle with a radius of 40 centimetres.
13. (mcq) A giant wheel turns steadily. At which point does a rider feel lightest?  
   Options: At the top / At the bottom / At the side  
   **Answer:** At the top. *Why:* At the top, the seat has to push up less than the rider's weight, because part of the weight is used to give the pull towards the middle. At the bottom, the seat must push up more.
14. (write) Gravity pulls the Moon all the time, but the Moon never gets closer. Explain how it can keep falling and never arrive.  
   **Answer:** The Moon is moving sideways very fast as well as being pulled towards the Earth. As it falls, the Earth's surface curves away beneath it, so it keeps missing the Earth. It falls round the Earth instead of into it.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the Moon has a sideways speed, and that the pull bends its path round the Earth, so it keeps falling and keeps missing.
15. (label) Here is a picture of a cannon on a very tall mountain, firing three balls. Label the three paths.  
   **Answer:** ['the slowest ball: lands close to the mountain', 'a faster ball: lands further away', 'the ball fired fast enough: keeps missing the Earth and goes round it']. *Why:* Newton imagined faster and faster shots. A slow ball falls near. A faster ball lands further. A ball fast enough keeps falling but the ground curves away, so it never lands. That is an orbit.
16. (count) A satellite goes round the Earth once every 90 minutes. How many times does it go round in 24 hours?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* 24 hours is 24 times 60, which is 1,440 minutes. 1,440 divided by 90 is 16 times.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A thing that was going round in a circle suddenly has nothing pulling it towards the middle. What does it do next?  
   Options: It goes straight on, along the way it was moving / It goes outward from the middle / It stops at once  
   **Answer:** It goes straight on, along the way it was moving. *Why:* With no pull, nothing bends the path, so the thing goes straight on. It does not go outward along the radius, and it does not stop.
2. (mcq) What is needed to keep a thing going round in a circle?  
   Options: A pull towards the middle, all the time / A push forward, all the time / A push outward, all the time  
   **Answer:** A pull towards the middle, all the time. *Why:* The pull towards the middle bends the path round. A forward push would change the speed, and an outward push would make the thing leave the circle.
3. (mcq) In the Whirler test, what happened as more coins were hung on the string?  
   Options: The bag had to go round faster / The bag could go round more slowly / The bag went round at the same speed  
   **Answer:** The bag had to go round faster. *Why:* More coins mean a bigger pull on the string, and the bag must go round faster to hold the same circle. The speed went up more slowly than the pull.
4. (mcq) A car goes through a bend at 20 kilometres an hour, and then through the same bend at 40 kilometres an hour. How does the grip the tyres need change?  
   Options: It needs four times as much grip / It needs twice as much grip / It needs the same grip  
   **Answer:** It needs four times as much grip. *Why:* Twice the speed needs four times the pull towards the middle. A small rise in speed can be too much for a bend.
5. (mcq) A bag is whirled at a steady speed in a circle. It is replaced by a bag that is twice as heavy, and whirled at the same speed in the same circle. What happens to the pull needed?  
   Options: It doubles / It halves / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It doubles. *Why:* A bigger mass needs a bigger pull in the same proportion. Twice the mass needs twice the pull.
6. (mcq) Coins sit at different distances from the middle of a turntable that turns faster and faster. Which coin slides off last?  
   Options: The one nearest the middle / The one nearest the edge / The heaviest one  
   **Answer:** The one nearest the middle. *Why:* Nearer the middle, the circle is smaller and the coin moves more slowly, so it needs less friction. It stays on the longest.
7. (fill) In a cream separator, the heavier part of the milk moves to the ____ of the spinning bowl.  
   **Answer:** ['outside', 'outer wall', 'edge']. *Why:* The heavier watery milk is pushed to the outside, and the lighter cream gathers nearer the middle.
8. (mcq) In a spin dryer, what lets the water leave the clothes?  
   Options: The holes let the water go straight on, while the wall holds the clothes / The drum squeezes the clothes dry / The lid sucks the water up  
   **Answer:** The holes let the water go straight on, while the wall holds the clothes. *Why:* The wall holds the clothes in the circle, and the holes let the water go. The spin does the sorting.
9. (mcq) Why are railway curves built with the outer rail a little higher than the inner rail?  
   Options: So that part of the push of the rails points towards the middle of the curve / So that the train goes faster / So that the rails last longer  
   **Answer:** So that part of the push of the rails points towards the middle of the curve. *Why:* A tilted track helps the train go round, because the push of the rails then points partly towards the middle. The train needs less side grip from its wheels.
10. (mcq) A car slides straight off a wet road at a bend. What was missing?  
   Options: Enough friction to give the pull towards the middle / Enough engine power / Enough weight  
   **Answer:** Enough friction to give the pull towards the middle. *Why:* On a flat bend, friction is the pull towards the middle. A wet road gives less friction. Engine power and weight do not make the bend easier.
11. (mcq) Which is the best description of the Moon going round the Earth?  
   Options: It keeps falling towards the Earth and keeps missing it / It floats where there is no gravity / It is pushed round by the air  
   **Answer:** It keeps falling towards the Earth and keeps missing it. *Why:* Gravity pulls the Moon all the time, and the Moon is moving sideways, so it keeps falling and keeps missing. There is no air to push it.
12. (count) A bag makes 24 turns in 20 seconds. How many turns does it make in 10 seconds, at the same pace?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 10 seconds is half of 20 seconds. Half of 24 is 12 turns.
13. (mcq) What do scientists still discuss about 'centrifugal force'?  
   Options: Whether the outward feeling should be called a force / Whether a bag on a string goes round / Whether the Moon goes round the Earth  
   **Answer:** Whether the outward feeling should be called a force. *Why:* Everyone agrees on what happens. The argument is about how to describe the outward feeling, as a real force or as the body's tendency to go straight.

## Applications
- Spin dryers and salad spinners dry clothes and leaves by letting the water go straight on through the holes.
- Dairies use cream separators, and hospitals use centrifuges to separate blood cells from the liquid in blood.
- Road and railway engineers tilt bends and set speed limits, so vehicles can take them safely.
- Cyclists, motorcyclists and skiers lean into turns, so that the push of the ground points towards the middle.
- Engineers design giant wheels, merry-go-rounds and roller-coaster loops with the pull in mind, so that riders are held safely.
- Potters set the speed of the wheel to suit the width of the pot, because clay at the edge needs more grip.
- Satellites for weather, television, phones and navigation, launched by ISRO and other space agencies, are put into orbits at the speed that matches their height.
- Pilots and astronauts train in big centrifuges, to get used to strong pulls when a spacecraft or plane turns or speeds up.
- Hammer and discus throwers in athletics spin round, and let go at the moment when the hammer or discus points where they want it to go.
- Fan blades, flywheels and grinding wheels are made strong enough to hold themselves in the circle, because the pull needed grows with the square of the speed.

## Open questions
- **Why do almost all the things in space spin?** Stars, planets and galaxies nearly all spin. Scientists know that a cloud of gas spins faster as it shrinks, but exactly how the first spin began in the early universe is still being worked out.
- **When something spins, what is the spin measured against?** Newton thought the spin of a bucket of water shows spin in empty space. Ernst Mach thought it might be spin against the far-away stars. Nobody has settled the question.
- **Is gravity a pull, or a bend in space and time?** Einstein's theory describes gravity as a bend in space and time, and it has passed every test so far. Newton's idea of a pull gives the same answers for everyday things. A theory that joins gravity with quantum physics is still missing.
- **How fast can a star spin before it breaks apart?** Some small, dense stars called pulsars spin hundreds of times every second. Scientists are still finding out how fast a star can possibly spin and what holds it together.
- **Could people live in a big spinning space town, and would it be comfortable?** The science is clear that a spinning ring can give a feeling like weight. But nobody has built a large one, and scientists are still working out how people would feel in one.
- **Do the tiny particles inside atoms go round like tiny planets?** No. Quantum physics describes them in a different way, and it does not give them simple circular paths. How to picture them is still debated.

## Connected maths
- In the made-up example for the Whirler test, the middle results for 4, 8 and 16 coins were 9, 13 and 18 turns in 10 seconds. How many more turns did 16 coins give than 4 coins? How many times as many coins is 16 than 4? About how many times as many turns is 18 than 9? **9 more turns. 16 coins is 4 times as many as 4 coins. 18 turns is 2 times as many as 9 turns.**. 18 take away 9 is 9. 16 divided by 4 is 4. 18 divided by 9 is 2. Four times the coins gave only twice the turns. That is the sign that the pull grows with the square of the speed. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- A bag makes 13 turns in 10 seconds. How many turns would it make in 1 minute, at the same pace? About how many seconds does one turn take, to the nearest tenth of a second? **78 turns in a minute. One turn takes about 0.8 seconds.**. 1 minute is 6 times 10 seconds. 13 times 6 is 78. One turn takes 10 divided by 13 seconds, which is about 0.77. To the nearest tenth, that is 0.8 seconds. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A stone needs a pull of 4 newtons to go round at 2 metres per second on a string. The pull grows with the square of the speed. What pull does it need at 1, 3 and 4 metres per second? What kind of numbers are 1, 4, 9 and 16? **1 newton at 1 metre per second, 9 newtons at 3 metres per second, 16 newtons at 4 metres per second. They are square numbers.**. At 2 metres per second the pull is 4, which is 2 times 2. At 1 it is 1 times 1, which is 1. At 3 it is 3 times 3, which is 9. At 4 it is 4 times 4, which is 16. The pull is the speed times itself, so the numbers 1, 4, 9 and 16 are square numbers. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- A car needs a grip of 5 units to take a bend at 10 kilometres an hour. The grip needed grows with the square of the speed. How much grip does it need at 20, 30 and 40 kilometres an hour? **20 units at 20 kilometres an hour, 45 units at 30, and 80 units at 40.**. At 20 kilometres an hour the speed is 2 times as big, so the grip is 2 times 2, which is 4 times 5, which is 20. At 30, it is 3 times 3, which is 9 times 5, which is 45. At 40, it is 4 times 4, which is 16 times 5, which is 80. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A satellite goes round the Earth once every 90 minutes. How many times does it go round in 3 hours? How many times in one day of 24 hours? **2 times in 3 hours, and 16 times in a day.**. 3 hours is 180 minutes. 180 divided by 90 is 2. A day is 24 times 60, which is 1,440 minutes. 1,440 divided by 90 is 16. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- A turntable turns once every 2 seconds. What fraction of a turn does it make in 1 second? How many turns does it make in 3 seconds? **One half of a turn in 1 second. One and a half turns in 3 seconds.**. One turn takes 2 seconds, so in 1 second it makes half a turn. In 3 seconds, that is 3 halves, which is 1 and a half turns. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- The Earth takes 1 year to go round the Sun, and Jupiter takes about 12 years. How many times does the Earth go round while Jupiter goes round 3 times? Why do far planets take longer? **The Earth goes round 36 times. Far planets have a bigger circle to travel and a weaker pull from the Sun, so they go round more slowly.**. 3 times 12 is 36 years, and the Earth goes round once every year, so 36 times. The bag on the longer string also went round more slowly in the Planets on a String project. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A coin on a turntable moves through a quarter of a turn, and then through a half of a turn more. What angle has it turned through in all? How many right angles is that? **270 degrees, which is 3 right angles.**. A quarter of a turn is 90 degrees. A half of a turn is 180 degrees. 90 plus 180 is 270 degrees. A right angle is 90 degrees, so 270 divided by 90 is 3 right angles. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
