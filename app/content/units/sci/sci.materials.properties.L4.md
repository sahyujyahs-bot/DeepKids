# Properties of materials: why the same paper tears straight one way and crooked the other
`sci.materials.properties.L4`

**Summary.** Tear a sheet of newspaper from top to bottom and the tear runs straight. Tear it from side to side and the tear wanders. It is the same paper, so why does the way you pull matter? Your child finds the hidden direction in paper, cardboard and cloth with three tests. Then they bend a paper clip until it snaps, to see that a metal can wear out. Along the way they learn why wood and bamboo split along their length. They learn why a tailor cuts cloth on the slant, and why plywood is made of crossed layers. They also find out why a folded sheet of paper can carry more than a flat one. The unit ends with what nobody can yet predict: a material's properties from its atoms alone.

**Intro.** *Take a sheet of newspaper. Tear it from top to bottom, along the columns of print. Now take another sheet and tear it from side to side. One tear runs straight as a ruled line. The other wanders and rips. It is the same newspaper, the same hands and the same effort. What is different?* Nothing was added to the paper and nothing was taken away. Only the direction of the pull changed. Paper has a hidden direction built into it, and so do wood, bamboo, cloth and many other materials. In this unit you find that direction with your own hands. You test paper, cloth and a paper clip, and you learn why a material can be strong one way and weak another, and why a property can wear out with use.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that some materials have a direction, which is called a grain, and name at least three of them.
1. I can run a test that shows the same material behaving differently along its grain and across it.
2. I can explain that the direction comes from how fibres, cells or threads lie inside the material.
3. I can tell a property of a material from a property of an object, and show that shape can make a thing stiffer without changing the material.
4. I can explain how crossed layers, as in plywood and cardboard, cancel out a material's direction.
5. I can explain that stiff, strong and tough are three different properties, and give an example of each.
6. I can predict that a wire bent back and forth will snap, and explain that a property can wear out with use.
7. I can say one thing designers can already do with a material's inside, and one thing nobody can yet predict.

## Terms
- **Grain**: The direction in which the fibres or cells of a material mostly lie. A material with a grain behaves differently along it and across it. *e.g.* A wooden plank splits easily along its grain and hardly at all across it.
- **Fibre**: A long, thin strand. Many natural materials, such as wood, paper, cotton and bamboo, are made of fibres. *e.g.* Paper is a mat of tiny plant fibres pressed flat.
- **Along and across**: Along means in the same direction as the grain. Across means at a right angle to the grain. *e.g.* You split a bamboo stick along its length and snap it across its length.
- **Anisotropic**: Having a property that is different in different directions. The word comes from Greek and means not equal in all ways. *e.g.* Wood is anisotropic: it is much stronger to pull along the grain than across it.
- **Isotropic**: Having a property that is the same in every direction. *e.g.* A sheet of glass is almost the same in every direction, so it is isotropic.
- **Woven**: Made by crossing two sets of threads over and under each other, at right angles. *e.g.* A cotton shirt and a cotton dupatta are woven.
- **Warp and weft**: In woven cloth, the warp threads run the length of the cloth and the weft threads run across it. *e.g.* On a handloom, the weaver passes the weft thread across the warp threads.
- **Bias**: The diagonal direction of woven cloth, at 45 degrees to the threads. Woven cloth stretches the most along the bias. *e.g.* A tailor cuts a strip of cloth on the bias to bend smoothly round a curved neckline.
- **Knitted**: Made from one thread looped into rows of loops. Knitted cloth stretches in all directions. *e.g.* A cotton T-shirt and a woollen sweater are knitted.
- **Layer**: One thin sheet in a stack. Materials can be built by gluing layers together. *e.g.* Plywood is made of thin sheets of wood glued in layers.
- **Composite**: A material made of two or more different materials joined, so that each does a job the other cannot. *e.g.* Bone is a composite of a hard mineral and a bendy protein.
- **Stiff**: Hard to bend or stretch. A stiff thing changes shape very little when you push or pull it. *e.g.* A steel ruler is stiff. A rubber band is not.
- **Tough**: Able to take in a lot of energy before breaking. A tough material bends or stretches a long way instead of snapping. *e.g.* A bicycle tyre is tough. A glass tumbler is stiff but not tough.
- **Stress**: How hard a material is being pulled, pushed or bent, shared across the area it acts on. The same weight on a thin string is a bigger stress than on a thick rope. *e.g.* A heavy bag hung from a thin string puts more stress on the string than the same bag on a thick rope.
- **Fatigue**: Damage that builds up in a material when it is bent, pulled or pushed again and again, until it breaks at a force that it could easily take once. *e.g.* A paper clip bent back and forth snaps, though one bend does not break it.
- **Work hardening**: A metal becoming harder and stiffer in the place where it has been bent. *e.g.* Where a paper clip has been bent, the metal is harder than the wire next to it.
- **Crack**: A thin break that starts in a material and can grow. A tiny crack can end in a snap. *e.g.* A crack in a glass bangle can grow across it from one small knock.
- **Cell wall**: The stiff outer layer of a plant cell. In wood, the cell walls are made of fibres of cellulose held together by a glue called lignin. *e.g.* The long, thin cell walls in a bamboo stick are what make it hard to snap along its length.

## Explanations
### A property can have a direction · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is anisotropic when a property, such as strength or stiffness, is different in different directions. It is isotropic when the property is the same in every direction.

In the first unit on properties, you saw that a property tells you what a material is like, such as hard, flexible or waterproof. Now look closer. Pull a strip of newspaper from top to bottom and it tears straight. Pull it from side to side and it tears crookedly. The property is not only in the material. It also depends on which way the force acts. So when someone says 'wood is strong', a careful scientist asks 'strong in which direction?' Many materials are the same in every direction. Glass, steel balls and still water are close to this. But many natural materials, and many made ones, have a direction built in. Knowing the direction lets you use the material well. You split wood along the grain, you hang cloth from its threads and you fold a card along its grain.
- e.g. Wood is many times stronger to pull along the grain than across it.
- e.g. A sheet of paper tears straight along its grain and crookedly across it.
- e.g. Woven cloth stretches very little along its threads and a lot on the slant.
### Paper has a grain, and where it comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** Paper grain is the direction in which most of the paper's fibres lie. It is set when the paper is made.

Paper is a thin mat of plant fibres. Each fibre is a tiny strand, a fraction of a millimetre wide and a few millimetres long. In a paper mill, a watery mix of fibres is poured onto a fast-moving belt. As the water drains away, the long fibres tend to line up in the direction the belt is moving. That direction is the grain of the paper. Along the grain, a tear has to cut through the gaps between the lines of fibres, so it runs straight. Across the grain, a tear must break fibres and slip between them, so it wanders. Paper is also stiffer along the grain. And when paper gets wet, the fibres swell mostly in width, not in length, so a wet sheet curls and grows more across the grain than along it.
- e.g. A newspaper tears straight along its columns because that is usually the grain direction.
- e.g. A sheet of card folds more cleanly when the fold runs along the grain.
- e.g. A wet strip of paper bends or curls because the fibres swell across, not along.
### Wood: bundles of long tubes · *defined*
**Definition:** Wood is made of long, thin cells lying side by side along the trunk. Their walls are strong fibres of cellulose, held together by a glue called lignin.

Cut a tree trunk across and look closely. You see rings and tiny holes. Each hole is the end of a long, narrow cell. These cells run up and down the tree, like a bundle of drinking straws. They carry water up from the roots and hold the tree upright. Their walls are tough, so pulling a plank along the grain pulls on the walls themselves. But the glue that holds the cells side by side is weaker, so a force across the grain can slip them apart, and the wood splits. That is why a carpenter or a woodcutter splits logs along the grain with a wedge. It is also why a plank with the grain running across a short shelf can snap, while the same plank turned the right way holds many books. In a living tree, the same cells carry water up from the roots. So the wood that holds a tree upright is also their plumbing.
- e.g. A woodcutter splits a log with one wedge, because the wood opens along its grain.
- e.g. A wooden ruler snaps easily if you bend it across a knot, where the grain turns.
- e.g. A stick of firewood is hard to pull apart along its length but easy to split.
### Bamboo: a grass that is stronger than it looks · *defined*
**Definition:** Bamboo is a giant grass with a hollow stem. Its strong fibres run along its length, so it splits easily lengthwise and is hard to snap across when it is whole.

Bamboo is not a tree. The grass family is where bamboo belongs, with rice and sugarcane. Its stem is a hollow tube, divided by solid rings called nodes. The wall of the tube is packed with strong fibres that run along the length of the stem. This makes bamboo strong to pull and to bend, and light for its size. It also means that bamboo splits easily along its length, because there are no fibres holding the sides together. Basket makers in Assam and Kerala use this on purpose. They split a stem into thin strips with a knife, then weave the strips into baskets, mats and hats. Some kinds of bamboo grow very fast, up to about 90 centimetres in a day in the best conditions, so bamboo is also a material that can be harvested again and again.
- e.g. Basket makers split bamboo into long strips, because the strips come off cleanly along the fibres.
- e.g. A bamboo scaffold on a building site holds workers because the whole tube is stiff.
- e.g. A broken bamboo stick shows long splinters, not a neat edge.
### Cloth: threads, the weave and the slant · *defined*
**Definition:** Woven cloth is made of two sets of threads crossing at right angles. It is stiff along the threads and stretches most on the bias, which is the diagonal.

A woven cloth, such as cotton, has warp threads running one way and weft threads running the other. Each thread is strong and does not stretch much. So if you pull the cloth along either set of threads, you are pulling on the threads, and the cloth hardly stretches. But if you pull on the diagonal, the threads do not stretch. Instead the squares between them squeeze into long thin diamonds. The cloth gets longer in one way and thinner in the other. This is why tailors say 'cut on the bias' when they want cloth to stretch round a curve, such as a neckline or a sleeve edge. In the 1920s, a French dress designer named Madeleine Vionnet became famous for cutting whole dresses on the bias so that they hung and moved softly along the body. Knitted cloth, such as a T-shirt, works differently. Its threads are looped, and loops can open, so it stretches in all directions.
- e.g. A strip of cotton cut on the bias bends smoothly round a curve, and the same strip cut along the threads does not.
- e.g. A woven dupatta stretches little along its length but pulls into a diamond shape if you tug a corner.
- e.g. A knitted sock stretches round your foot in all directions.
### Crossed layers cancel the direction · *defined*
**Definition:** A laminate is a material built from thin layers stuck together. If the layers are turned so their grains cross, the whole material is strong in more than one direction.

If a material has a weak direction, you can use two sheets of it and turn one sheet to cover the weakness of the other. Plywood is made like this. It is built from thin sheets of wood called veneers. Each veneer is glued to the next with its grain turned a quarter-turn. So where one sheet is weak, the next is strong. Plywood almost never splits, and it does not shrink or swell as much as a solid plank. The number of layers is usually odd, such as three, five or seven, so that the board is balanced and does not curl. Cardboard works in a similar way. A corrugated sheet has a wavy middle layer, glued between two flat layers. The waves hold the flat layers apart, and that makes the sheet stiff. Bone is also a layered material, made of a hard mineral and a stretchy protein. The mineral resists squashing and the protein stops the bone from snapping like chalk.
- e.g. Plywood is built of thin wood layers, each turned across the one below it.
- e.g. A corrugated cardboard box is stiffer along its flutes than across them.
- e.g. Bone is a composite of a hard mineral and a flexible protein.
### Shape makes a stiffer thing: material and object are different · *defined*
**Definition:** A property of a material is the same whatever the shape. A property of an object depends on the shape too. The same material can make a floppy object or a stiff one.

A flat sheet of paper laid across a gap sags and falls. Fold the same sheet into a zigzag, or roll it into a tube, and it can carry a pile of coins. The paper has not changed. Only the shape has. A thin sheet bends easily because its two surfaces are close together. Fold it, and some of the paper lies far from the middle. Stretching and squashing happen at the edges of a bend, so material far from the middle does most of the work. This is why a bicycle frame is made of tubes and a steel girder has an I-shape, with thick flanges top and bottom. Thickness matters hugely. If you double the thickness of a plank, it becomes about eight times harder to bend. That is why a thick plank does not sag under your weight, and a thin one of the same wood does.
- e.g. A folded paper bridge holds many coins, and a flat sheet holds almost none.
- e.g. A bamboo tube is far stiffer than a solid stick of the same weight.
- e.g. A thick plank is about eight times as stiff as a plank half as thick.
### Stiff, strong and tough are three different things · *defined*
**Definition:** Stiff means hard to bend. Strong means able to take a large force before breaking. Tough means able to take in a lot of energy before breaking.

People often use these words as if they meant the same thing. They do not. A steel ruler is stiff: it bends only a little when you push it. Glass is also stiff, but it is not tough, because a small knock can shatter it. A rubber band is not stiff at all, but it is tough: you can stretch it a long way before it breaks, and it takes in a lot of energy doing so. Strong is about the biggest pull a material can take. A thin thread of steel is far stronger than a thick thread of cotton. But a cotton shirt can take a hard tug without tearing, and a glass thread of the same size would snap. Engineers choose from all three. A car body should be tough, so it crumples and soaks up energy in a crash. A bridge cable should be strong. A floor should be stiff.
- e.g. Glass is stiff but not tough.
- e.g. A rubber band is tough but not stiff.
- e.g. A steel beam is stiff, strong and tough.
### Fatigue: a property can wear out with use · *defined*
**Definition:** Fatigue is damage that builds up in a material when it is loaded again and again. After enough repeats, it breaks at a force far below the force that would break it once.

Bend a paper clip once and nothing happens. Bend it back and forth, and after ten or twenty bends it snaps. The force on each bend was the same, so why did it break? Inside a metal are tiny crystals. Each bend pushes layers of atoms inside the crystals to slide over each other by a tiny amount, and tiny cracks begin at the places where this happens most. Each bend lengthens the crack a little, until the wire is too thin to hold and snaps. Feel the bent place: it is warm, because the bending itself makes heat. In the 1850s and 1860s, a German railway engineer named August Wöhler showed that train axles broke after millions of turns under loads far smaller than their breaking strength. Fatigue is why machine parts are checked and replaced, and why aircraft windows have rounded corners. A sharp corner makes the stress bigger, so a crack starts there first.
- e.g. A paper clip bent back and forth snaps after a few bends.
- e.g. Railway axles and aircraft parts are checked for tiny cracks.
- e.g. A plastic spoon or ruler bent back and forth shows a pale line where it has been bent.
### Work hardening: where the metal was bent, it gets harder · *defined*
**Definition:** Work hardening is the way a metal becomes harder and stiffer in a place where it has been bent or hammered.

Straighten a paper clip into a wire. Bend it at one place, then straighten it again. The wire keeps a kink there, and the metal in the kink is harder than the wire beside it. That is work hardening. When a metal is bent past its springy limit, tiny faults inside its crystals, called dislocations, are made and moved. They pile up and block each other, so the metal gets harder where it was bent. Blacksmiths have used this for thousands of years. They hammer a blade to make its edge harder. The price is that the metal becomes less bendy and more likely to crack, so a smith heats the metal again when it gets too stiff. Bending a metal back and forth many times uses up this hardened metal, because each bend also does a little damage, and the damage ends in a snap.
- e.g. A paper clip is harder in the kink where it was bent than in the wire beside it.
- e.g. A blacksmith hammers a blade edge to make it harder.
- e.g. A coin pressed between steel dies is harder than the blank it was cut from.
### Why materials have a direction: the inside story · *defined*
**Definition:** A material has a direction when its fibres, layers or crystals are lined up, so that the bonds along one direction are not the same as the bonds along another.

A force along a fibre pulls on the fibre itself, and the fibre is made of atoms joined by very strong bonds. A force across a fibre pulls the fibres apart from each other, and the glue or weak bonds between them let go sooner. The same idea works at every size. Wood cells have walls of cellulose fibre, held together by weaker glue. Mica, a shiny mineral found in India, is a stack of very thin sheets of atoms. Atoms inside a sheet are bound tightly, and the sheets are held to each other weakly, so a piece of mica peels into thin, clear leaves. Graphite, the black stuff in a pencil, is layered the same way. A plastic carry bag has long chains of molecules that were stretched into line when the plastic was made, so it stretches more one way than the other. Whenever a material is built of long things lying in line, or flat things lying in layers, it will have a direction.
- e.g. Mica peels into thin sheets because its layers are held together weakly.
- e.g. Pencil graphite flakes off in layers onto the paper.
- e.g. A plastic bag is easier to tear in one direction than the other.
### Designing the inside: new properties from structure · *idea*
People have always shaped materials from outside, by cutting and bending. Today, designers also shape the inside. They arrange fibres, holes and layers so the material does something that no ordinary material does. One example is an auxetic material. If you stretch an ordinary rubber band, it gets thinner. An auxetic material gets fatter when stretched, because its inside is built of hinged shapes that open out. In 1987, an American scientist named Roderic Lakes made a foam that did this. Another example is a material designed to bend light or sound around an object. These are called metamaterials, and scientists have made small working samples. Nature has been doing this much longer. A butterfly's wing is coloured by tiny ridges that act on light, and a gecko's foot grips a wall through millions of tiny hairs. Designing the inside is a young field, and many ideas are still at the laboratory stage.
- e.g. An auxetic foam gets fatter when you stretch it.
- e.g. A butterfly's wing is coloured by tiny ridges, not by paint.
- e.g. A gecko sticks to a wall with millions of tiny hairs on each foot.
### Can we predict a material's properties from its atoms? · *debated*
In principle, a material's properties come from its atoms and how they are joined. If we knew exactly how the atoms sit, could we calculate how hard, bendy or strong the material is? For simple crystals, computers can calculate some properties quite well. But for most real materials, which have cracks, crystal edges, gaps and mixed ingredients, the calculation is too hard, and people still make and test many samples. In 2023, a research group at Google DeepMind reported that a computer program had predicted 2.2 million new crystal structures, about 380,000 of them stable. Other scientists replied that many of the predictions were not new, or not useful, and that making and testing them is the real work. So the question is open. Some scientists think we are close to designing materials with a computer. Others think real materials will always hold surprises.
- e.g. Computers can calculate how stiff a perfect diamond crystal is.
- e.g. No computer can yet predict how long a particular steel part will last in a bridge.
- e.g. Wood from two trees of the same kind can differ in strength.
- **Optimists in materials science:** With enough data and faster computers, we will learn the rules that link atoms to properties and design new materials on screen before making them.
- **Careful scientists:** Real materials are full of flaws and mixtures that are very hard to model. Tests on real samples will always be needed, and a prediction is only as good as the test that checks it.

## Mostly agreed
- Many materials, including paper, wood, bamboo and woven cloth, have a grain or a direction, and their strength and stiffness differ along it and across it. *How we know:* Strips cut along and across the grain, hung with the same weight, bend or break differently, and the result repeats.
- Wood is made of long cells lying along the trunk, and it is much stronger to pull along the grain than across it. *How we know:* A microscope shows the long cells, and strength tests on cut samples show the difference.
- Woven cloth stretches the most on the bias, because its threads can swing to a new angle without stretching. *How we know:* A strip cut on the diagonal stretches many times more than a strip cut along the threads under the same weight.
- A thin sheet can be made much stiffer by folding, rolling or layering it, without changing the material. *How we know:* A paper bridge folded into a zigzag carries many times the weight of a flat sheet.
- Stiff, strong and tough are different properties. A material can be high in one and low in another. *How we know:* Glass is stiff but cracks from a small knock, and a rubber band is soft but takes a lot of energy before breaking.
- Metals can fail by fatigue: repeated loading that is smaller than their breaking force can grow cracks until they snap. *How we know:* Wöhler's axle tests in the nineteenth century, the investigation of the 1954 Comet airliner crashes and any paper clip bent back and forth.

## How we found out
**August Wöhler, a German railway engineer (1819 to 1914), From the 1850s to about 1870.** In the 1800s, trains carried more and more people. The iron axles under the carriages sometimes snapped, even though they had been tested with loads far above what they carried. Many engineers thought the shaking changed the iron inside, turning it from long fibres into brittle crystals. The broken ends did look bright and grainy, which seemed to prove it. A German railway engineer named August Wöhler did not accept this. From the 1850s, he built machines that spun axles under a fixed load for millions of turns, and counted the turns until each one broke. He found that an axle lasted more turns when the load was smaller, and that below a certain load it might not break at all. The grainy look was simply what a fresh break looks like in iron. The iron had not changed. A small crack had grown a little with every turn. Counting the turns to failure is still how engineers test materials today.  
*What people believed before:* Many engineers thought that shaking turned iron into brittle crystals. Wöhler showed that tiny cracks grow a little with each repeated load, and that a smaller load means more turns before the break.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A material has one strength, and a table of materials can say what it is.** (Many people who have only used a table of numbers.; *rejected*)  
  For: Textbooks often give one number for the strength of wood, steel or paper.  
  Against: A strip of wood is many times stronger along the grain than across it, and a sheet of paper tears straight one way and crooked the other. A single number hides the direction, the wetness and the age of the sample.
- **Doubling the thickness of a plank makes it twice as stiff.** (Many people, by guessing.; *rejected*)  
  For: Twice as much material seems to mean twice as much stiffness.  
  Against: For a beam bending under a load, the stiffness grows with the cube of the thickness. Double the thickness and the plank is about eight times stiffer. Two sheets laid loosely on each other are only twice as stiff, because they slide.
- **Bamboo is a kind of wood.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: It is hard, woody and used like timber.  
  Against: Bamboo is a grass, in the same family as rice and sugarcane. Bamboo stems have no growth rings and no bark layer, and they are hollow. A bamboo stick feels like wood because its walls are packed with strong fibres.
- **A strong material is also a tough material.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Strong things usually seem hard to damage.  
  Against: Glass is stiff and quite strong when pulled with no scratches, yet it shatters from a small knock. Rubber is not strong, but it is tough. Strength and toughness measure different things.
- **Plywood is a cheap, weaker copy of solid wood.** (Some carpenters and many buyers.; *debated*)  
  For: Solid timber looks richer and is made of one piece.  
  Against: Plywood is made of crossed layers, so it does not split along one direction, and it shrinks and swells much less than a solid plank. For many jobs, such as a door or a floor, a plywood sheet is better than a single board.
- **Computers will soon be able to design any material we want.** (Some materials scientists and technology writers.; *debated*)  
  For: Computer programs have predicted millions of possible crystals and found some that worked.  
  Against: Other scientists point out that many predictions repeat known materials, and that real materials have flaws and mixtures that are hard to model. Testing and making a sample remains the hard part.

## Roots
We have found that paper, wood, bamboo and cloth have a direction. Now we ask what that tells us about the things we call the same all the way through, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** A sheet of newspaper tears straight one way and crooked the other. The paper did not change. Its fibres lie mostly one way, so it has a direction built in. One number for how strong paper is leaves out which way you pull.  
*Habit it breaks:* We write one value for a material, such as 'paper is weak' or 'wood is strong', as if a property pointed nowhere. That hides the direction of the pull.  
*Evidence:* In the tear test, the same newspaper tore straight along its grain and crookedly across it. In the droop test, strips cut along the grain drooped less than strips cut across. Cloth cut on the slant stretched far more than cloth cut along its threads. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your bones are built of layers, and the struts inside them tend to line up with the way you stand and walk. Your body is built with directions in it.
- *what is life:* A tree that is swayed by the wind grows a thicker trunk. A leaning tree makes special wood on one side to stand up again. A living body keeps a record of the pushes it has felt.
- *what is this world:* Paper still carries the direction of the belt it was made on, and wood carries the direction of the cells that the tree grew. Things carry their past inside them as a direction.

Zoom into a sheet of paper and you see thin fibres lying mostly one way. Zoom into wood and you see long tubes. Zoom into a metal and you see tiny crystals made of rows of atoms. At every size, a material is made of smaller parts placed in an arrangement. The direction comes from the arrangement. So what a material is depends on how its parts are placed, as well as on what they are.

- **Tao Te Ching 76:** People are born soft and bendy, and they die stiff and hard. Green plants are soft and full of sap, and they die dry and brittle. So the stiff and hard belong to death, and the soft and yielding belong to life. A tree that cannot bend is easily broken. (Bamboo bends a long way in a storm and springs back, because its fibres run unbroken. The brittle strips in our tests snapped.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** Uddalaka teaches his son that when you know one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay. The pots and plates are only names, and the truth of each is clay. (A spoon, a tumbler and a bowl of steel are one material in different shapes. Our paper bridge showed that a shape changes how stiff a thing is, and the material stays the same.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 4:** A carpenter walks past a huge old oak and calls it useless, because its wood is twisted and cannot be sawn into planks. That night the oak speaks to the carpenter in a dream and says that this is why they have lived so long. (A carpenter reads the grain of wood before cutting. Twisted grain means a crooked plank, and the tree was left alone.)
- Science (settled): Trees that are swayed or shaken grow shorter, thicker trunks, and leaning trees build special wood to straighten up again.
- Science (open): Exactly how a living cell senses a push and decides where to lay down new fibres is still being worked out.
- Other view, Many materials scientists: The direction is not a surprise or a flaw. A full description of a material's stiffness already includes every direction, in a table of numbers. A single number such as 'paper is strong' was only ever a shorthand, and nothing deeper is hidden in it.
- Practice, Feel the grain (3 min): Pick up a wooden spoon or a bamboo stick. Close your eyes. Run your thumb along it, and then across it. Breathe in slowly three times. Notice how the two directions feel different. Picture the long threads inside, lying side by side, holding on to each other.
- Ask: Is paper one thing, or is it many different things depending on which way you hold it?
- Ask: A tree grows thicker where the wind pushes. Is the wind part of what builds the tree?
- Ask: If a thing carries its past in its direction, what do you carry in yours?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A newspaper tears straight one way and crooked the other. So is 'the strength of paper' a fact about the paper, or a fact about the paper and the way you pull it? (hint: Think about what you would write in a table of materials, and what is missing from one number.)
- *deep:* A bent paper clip carries a record of every bend it has had. What other things carry a record of what has happened to them, such as a tree, a bone or a rock? (hint: Think about tree rings, a healed cut and layers of rock.)
- *deep:* A tree that sways in the wind grows a thicker trunk, and a tree that leans grows special wood on one side to straighten up. How does a tree know where the forces are? (hint: Think about how a living thing can sense a push and answer it, without a brain.)
- *deep:* Plywood crosses its layers to be the same in every direction, and it gives up some strength along the grain to do it. When is it better for a thing to be strong in one direction, and when in all? (hint: Think about a rope, a floor and a bone.)
- *big:* Scientists can now build the inside of a material so that it does something new, such as getting fatter when stretched. Is a property that we designed less real than one we found? (hint: Think about the colour of a butterfly's wing, which comes from tiny ridges and not from paint.)
- *big:* Engineers check aircraft and bridges for tiny cracks long before anything breaks. Why is it better to find a problem when it is small? (hint: Think about how a crack grows a little with each bend.)
- *big:* Mica is dug from mines in India and used in shiny paints and make-up. Who should know where the things that we buy come from, and how they were made? (hint: Think about a thing you own, and try to list the people who helped to make it.)
- *big:* In the Zhuangzi, a carpenter calls a huge, crooked oak useless, because its grain twists and it cannot be sawn into planks. The oak lives to a great age. Useful for what, and useful to whom? (hint: Think about who decides what a thing is for, and who is not asked.)
- *deep:* When you break a snapped paper clip, the metal looks bright and grainy inside. What do you think you are seeing, and how could you find out? (hint: Think of a broken biscuit and a broken stick. What do their insides look like?)

## Story: The Spine of the Kite
*A made-up story. Uttarayan in Ahmedabad and its kites are real. Whether kite makers choose the direction of their tissue paper, as Ba does here, is imagined. The science in it is real: bamboo splits along its fibres, a saw that wanders cuts fibres across, and paper tears straight along its grain.*

In the old city of Ahmedabad, the week before Uttarayan is the busiest week of the year. Every roof has a family on it. Every shop sells paper and string. Hiral was nine, and this year she wanted to make her own kite, one that would climb above all the others.

Her grandmother, Ba, had made kites for fifty Januaries. Ba's kites were thin, light and almost never wobbled. Hiral sat on the floor beside her, with a bundle of tissue paper, a pot of paste and a bamboo stick from the market.

Hiral sawed the stick lengthwise into thin strips, because that was how she thought sticks were made. Her saw wandered a little, but she did not mind. She glued the tissue to the frame, tied on the string, and carried the kite up to the roof.

The kite spun twice and dived. A gust came, and the tissue tore from one corner right across the middle. Then the bow, the curved stick, cracked with a small, sharp snap.

Her cousin Imran was twelve, and he was flying a bought kite from the next roof. 'Bamboo is bamboo,' he called. 'Bad luck. Buy a better one.' Hiral said nothing. She picked up the broken bow and looked at the break. It was not a clean line. It was a tangle of long splinters.

Downstairs, Ba did not say what had gone wrong. She gave Hiral a sheet of tissue. 'Tear it once from top to bottom,' she said. 'Then tear another sheet from side to side.'

The first tear ran straight, like a line drawn with a ruler. The second wandered off to the left and ended in a ragged scrap. 'Same paper,' said Hiral. 'Same hands.' Ba only waited.

'Now look at the bamboo,' said Ba. She set the blade of a small knife on the end of a fresh stick and tapped it with a stone. The stick opened along its whole length in one smooth line, as if it had been waiting to. Then Ba tried to cut across it. That took ten times as long, and the edge came out fuzzy.

'Bamboo grows in threads,' said Ba. 'Long threads, all lying one way. When you split it, the knife follows the gaps between the threads, and every thread stays whole from one end of the bow to the other. When the wind bends the bow, the whole thread bends with it. Your saw wandered. Wherever it wandered, it cut the threads, and left little ends sticking out along the edge. A crack loves a place like that.'

Then Ba held up the torn tissue. 'Paper is made of threads too, very tiny ones. The machine leaves them lying mostly one way. Find the way a tear runs straight. That is the way your threads run. Paste the tissue so they lie along the spine. Then the wind pulls along the threads, and not between them.'

Hiral spent four evenings doing it Ba's way. She split a new stick, and split the pieces again, and shaved them with the knife while Ba watched with her hands folded. She bent each strip a little against another strip, and kept the two that bent in the same way. 'One stiff wing and one floppy wing,' said Ba, 'and the kite will turn in circles all day.'

On the morning of Uttarayan, the roofs were full of music, and the sky was full of colour. Hiral let out the string. Her kite rose without a wobble and kept climbing, and it hung there, steady as a held breath.

Imran's kite, made in a hurry the night before, went up beside it. A gust came. The tissue tore from the corner, and the bow cracked. He stood holding the string, looking at the long splinters. Then he looked at Hiral's kite, which had not moved.

'How?' he said. Hiral did not make a speech. She handed him a scrap of tissue. 'Tear it that way,' she said. 'Now that way.' His eyebrows went up. 'Bamboo is not the same all the way round,' she said. 'It has a way it runs.' Then she gave him a spare bow that she had split herself.

By noon the two kites hung side by side in the blue. Hiral felt the string pulling gently at her fingers. She thought of the broken bow with its long splinters. The break had not been bad luck. The bamboo had been telling her which way it ran.

**For the parent:** Bamboo and paper both have a way they run, like threads lying side by side. Work along them and they hold. Work across them and they split or tear.

- Ask: Why did Hiral's sawn bamboo crack, when a strip split along the stick would have bent?
- Ask: Hiral's tissue tore straight one way and crooked the other. What does that tell you about how the paper was made?
- Ask: Think of a thing at home that you always cut, tear or fold in one direction. Why do you do it that way?

## Experiments
### e1 · Which Way Does It Tear? Paper has a grain (45 min)
- **question:** Is a sheet of paper the same in every direction? Does it tear in the same way, and droop the same amount, when it is cut along one direction and across it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will a sheet of newspaper tear in the same way from top to bottom and from side to side? Will two strips cut the other ways droop the same amount? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The two strips for each paper come from the same sheet, with the same width and the same length. They stick out over the table by the same amount, with the same book on the end. Only the direction of the cut changes. So any difference in the droop comes from the direction, and not from the paper, the width or the table.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the strips, or stays close while an older child does it. Keep the scissors closed when you walk with them.
- **record:** For each paper, write which way the tear ran straight. Then write the droop of each strip, three tries each. Last, find the average droop of each strip and draw a bar for each strip.
- **results table:** Paper | Which way the tear ran straight | Droop of strip A, cut along the arrow (cm, three tries) | Droop of strip X, cut across the arrow (cm, three tries) | Which drooped more
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the newspaper, the printer paper and the ruler. Ask for a guess and a reason before anything is touched. Write the guess at the top of the results table.  
  *Parent says:* We have newspaper and plain paper. First we tear. Then we cut strips and see which droops more. Will paper act the same in every direction? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Is paper the same in every direction? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think paper will be the same, or different, in different directions? (good: It is flat and smooth, so it is the same.; Newspaper has lines of print, so it might tear along them.; Paper is made of fibres, and they might lie one way.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write your child's own words. With 2 to 4 children, give each child one paper to test. One holds the strip and the book, one reads the ruler, and one writes. Swap roles for the next strip, then put all the tables side by side.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold a newspaper sheet by its top edge. Tear a strip down it from top to bottom for about 20 centimetres. Take a second sheet and tear from side to side, for the same distance. Look at the two tears.  
  *Parent says:* Tear one sheet from top to bottom. Tear another from side to side. Which tear is straighter? Which one wanders?  
  *Child mode:* Tear one sheet downward. Tear another sideways. Which tear is straighter?
  - Note: Newspaper usually tears straight from top to bottom, along the columns of print. If yours tears straight the other way, that is fine. Use whichever way tears straight in your paper.
  - Check: Which tear ran straighter, and what does that tell you? → **The straight tear shows the way most fibres lie, so it marks the grain**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a pencil arrow along the way the newspaper tore straight. Now find the grain of the printer paper. Tear a short strip from a corner in each direction, and draw an arrow along the straighter tear.  
  *Parent says:* Draw an arrow along the straight tear. Now find the straighter tear on the printer paper. Draw an arrow there too.  
  *Child mode:* Draw an arrow along the straight tear. Do the same on the printer paper.
  - Note: Printer paper shows the difference less clearly than newspaper. If you cannot tell, use the fold test. Fold the sheet in half one way and press the crease. Do the same the other way. The fold along the grain is smoother, and the fold across it looks rougher and a little cracked. If printer paper shows no clear difference, write that down. It is an honest result, and newspaper shows the grain best.
- **s4 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Cut two strips from the newspaper and two from the printer paper, each 3 centimetres wide and 20 centimetres long. Cut one strip along the arrow and one across it. Write A on the strip along the arrow and X on the strip across it.  
  *Parent says:* Cut one strip along the arrow and one across it. Mark them A and X. Keep the strips the same width.  
  *Child mode:* Cut a strip along the arrow. Cut another across it. Mark them A and X.
  - Note: Make the two strips exactly the same width. A strip 3 centimetres wide is easy to measure. Cut four strips in all, two from the newspaper and two from the printer paper. Cut along the arrow means the long side of the strip runs the same way as the arrow.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put strip A on the table with 10 centimetres sticking out over the edge, or 6 for newspaper. Put a heavy book on the other end. Hold the ruler upright beside the tip. Read how far the tip droops. Do it three times. Then do strip X.  
  *Parent says:* Slide the strip so exactly ten centimetres stick out, or six for newspaper. Put the book on the end. Measure how far the tip drops. Do each strip three times.  
  *Child mode:* Let 10 centimetres stick out, or 6 for newspaper. Measure how far the tip drops. Do each strip three times.
  - Note: Newspaper is floppy, so let 6 centimetres stick out for newspaper and 10 for printer paper. Use the same length for both strips of one paper. If a printer-paper strip also folds right down, use 6 for it too. Typical droop for printer paper is about 2 to 5 centimetres, and the strip cut along the grain usually droops less. The difference can be small with thin paper, so measure carefully to the millimetre and write down what you see.
  - Check: The two strips are the same width, length and paper. Why do you measure each one three times? → **One try can be a fluke, and three tries show whether the result repeats**
- **s6 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the average droop for each strip. Add the three numbers and divide by 3. Draw a bar for each strip. Then ask your child to say or write three sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Write three sentences. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three sentences. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Ask: What did you notice about the strips cut in the two directions? (good: The strip cut along the arrow drooped less.; The strips were different even though they came from one sheet.; Newspaper showed a bigger difference than printer paper.)
  - Note: A good reason mentions fibres lying mostly one way, a tear running between them, and a strip being stiffer along the fibres. Do not give the reason first. Let your child try, and then read the Paper has a grain card together. An extra for later: wet one strip of each kind and watch which way it curls.
### e2 · The Tailor's Trick: which way does cloth stretch? (40 min)
- **question:** Does woven cloth stretch the same amount in every direction? How much does a strip cut along the threads stretch, compared with a strip cut on the slant?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. A strip of cloth cut along the threads and a strip cut on the slant each carry the same bottle of water. Will they stretch the same amount? Which will stretch more, and why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both strips are cut from the same piece of cloth. They have the same width, and the same length between the pencil marks. The same bottle hangs from each for the same time. Only the direction of the cut changes. So any difference in stretch comes from the direction.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting. Hang the bottle low over a bed or a rug, in case the strip slips.
- **record:** For each strip, write the length between the marks without weight and with the bottle hanging. Subtract to find the stretch. Do each strip twice, and write all the numbers.
- **results table:** Strip | Length between marks with no weight (cm) | Length between marks with the bottle hanging (cm) | Stretch (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the cloth, the bottle and the clips. Ask for a guess and a reason before anything is cut. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will cut two strips from one cloth. One strip runs along the threads. The other runs on the slant. Each carries the same bottle. Which will stretch more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Which strip will stretch more? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think one strip might stretch more than the other? (good: The threads are in the way on the slant.; They are the same cloth, so they will be the same.; The threads cross, so the slant might give.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write your child's own words. With 2 to 4 children, each child cuts a pair of strips from a different cloth, such as a kitchen cloth, a dupatta and an old shirt. One holds the bag steady, one reads the ruler at eye level, and one writes. Then compare which cloth stretched most.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Pull one thread out near the edge of the cloth. The gap it leaves is a straight line along the threads. Cut along the gap. Do the same on another side to make a square about 40 centimetres each way.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the cloth. You can see threads in two directions. Pull out one thread. The gap shows the straight line of the threads.  
  *Child mode:* Pull out one thread near the edge. The gap shows the way the threads run.
  - Note: Use woven cloth, not a knitted T-shirt. Knitted cloth is made of loops and stretches both ways. If the cloth has a printed stripe or check, you can also follow that. Do not worry if the square is not perfect. If a tight weave will not let you pull out a thread, follow a printed stripe or the woven edge of the cloth. The threads run in two straight directions at right angles.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Fold the square corner to corner, so the fold crosses the cloth at a slant. Press the fold and draw a pencil line along it. Cut one strip 5 centimetres wide and 35 centimetres long along the threads. Cut a second strip of the same size centred on the fold line.  
  *Parent says:* Fold the cloth from corner to corner. That fold is the slant, which tailors call the bias. Cut one strip along the threads and one along the bias.  
  *Child mode:* Fold the cloth corner to corner. That fold is the bias. Cut one strip each way.
  - Check: Why are the two strips the same width and length? → **So that only the direction of the cut is different**
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay strip 1 flat and draw two pencil marks 20 centimetres apart. Clip the top of the strip to the hook. Clip a carry bag to the bottom. Measure between the marks. Then put the bottle in the bag, wait ten seconds, and measure again.  
  *Parent says:* Mark two points 20 centimetres apart. Hang the strip, with the bag clipped to the bottom. Measure. Then add the bottle. Wait ten seconds and measure again.  
  *Child mode:* Mark 20 centimetres. Hang the strip. Add the bottle. Measure again.
  - Note: Hold the bag steady while someone reads the ruler, because a swinging bag gives wrong numbers. Along the threads, the marks may stay almost 20 centimetres apart. On the slant, a typical thin cotton stretches about 1 to 4 centimetres. A very old or loose cloth may stretch more, and a stiff new cloth less. If the slant strip stretches only a few millimetres, try a thinner cloth or a 1 litre bottle. Read the ruler at eye level.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the other strip in the same way. Then do each strip a second time. Write the stretch for each try. The stretch is the length with the bottle minus 20 centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* Now test the other strip in the same way. Then test both strips again. Subtract 20 from each hanging length to find the stretch.  
  *Child mode:* Test the other strip. Then test both again. Work out the stretch.
  - Note: If both strips stretch the same amount, check that the cloth is woven and not knitted. Then check that the second strip really was cut on the slant.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Then look closely at the cloth and its threads in bright light.  
  *Parent says:* Write three sentences. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Then look closely at the threads.  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three sentences. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Ask: What do you think the threads did on the slant? (good: They turned, so the squares became diamonds.; They did not stretch, but they moved.; They swung to a new angle.)
  - Note: A good reason says that threads hardly stretch. Along the threads, the pull goes straight into them. On the slant, the threads swing to a new angle, so the cloth gets longer without stretching its threads. Let your child try first. Then read the Cloth card together.
### e3 · Bend Until It Snaps: how many bends does a paper clip survive? (35 min)
- **question:** How many bends does it take to snap a paper clip? Does a big bend or a small bend snap it sooner?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many big bends will it take to snap a paper clip? Will small bends take more bends, or fewer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the clips are the same kind, from the same box. You straighten each one in the same way and bend it at the middle. Three clips get big bends and three clips get small bends. You count every bend in the same way. Only the size of the bend changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays close and straightens the clips. The wire can spring and the snapped ends are sharp. Hold the wire low and away from your face, and wear glasses if you have them. Put the pieces in the bowl at once, then in the bin, and keep them away from children under five.
- **record:** For each clip, write the size of bend and the number of bends before it snapped. Then find the average for the big bends and for the small bends.
- **results table:** Clip | Size of bend | Number of bends until it snapped
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the paper clips and the bowl. Ask for a guess and a reason before bending anything. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will bend a paper clip back and forth until it snaps. How many bends will it take? Will small bends take more or fewer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. How many bends will snap a paper clip? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think a clip might snap after only a few bends? (good: The metal gets tired.; Each bend hurts the metal a little.; It breaks when it cannot bend any more.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write your child's own words. With 2 to 4 children, each child bends 3 or 4 wires. One bends, one counts aloud, and one writes. Swap roles. Put every count in one table. A box of 100 clips is enough for four children.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Unfold 8 paper clips into straight wires. Show your child the corner of the notebook. Say that this is a right angle. A big bend makes a right angle. A small bend makes half of it.  
  *Parent says:* Straighten each clip into a wire. A big bend turns the wire to a right angle. A small bend turns it only half as far.  
  *Child mode:* Straighten the clips. A big bend is a right angle. A small bend is half of that.
  - Note: Each clip is straightened in the same way. Keep two for spares. Mark the three for big bends with B and the three for small bends with S on the results table. Fold the notebook corner in half, corner to corner, to make a guide for the small bend.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**

Bring it back through straight and over to the right angle on the other side. That is bend 2. Keep counting until it snaps. Do this with three wires.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the wire low. Bend the end to one side. Count one. Bend it over to the other side. Count two. Keep going until it snaps.  
  *Child mode:* Bend the wire to one side. Count one. Bend it back. Count two. Keep going.
  - Note: In many tests a big bend snaps a clip in about 5 to 15 bends. This is typical, not a promise. Clips vary a lot with the maker, so write down what you get. If the fingers of a young child are too weak to bend the wire, a grown-up does the bending while the child counts.

## Worksheets
### Properties and direction (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A strip of newspaper tears straight when you pull it from top to bottom, and crookedly from side to side. What does this show?  
   Options: The paper has been damaged / The paper has a grain, so it behaves differently in different directions / Tearing is always random / The top of the paper is thicker than the sides  
   **Answer:** The paper has a grain, so it behaves differently in different directions. *Why:* A tear follows the direction in which most fibres lie. Straight one way and crooked the other shows that the paper is not the same in every direction. Picking 'damaged' or 'random' suggests that the result would change each time, but it repeats.
2. (fill) The direction in which the fibres of wood or paper mostly lie is called its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['grain']. *Why:* The grain is the direction of the fibres or long cells. Along it, a material behaves differently from across it.
3. (truefalse) A glass marble has a grain, like a plank of wood.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Glass has no lines of fibres or layers, so it is almost the same in every direction. A plank has long cells lying along it, so it has a grain.
4. (match) Match each material to what lies in lines or layers inside it.  
   **Answer:** Paper → Fibres pressed flat in a mat; Woven cloth → Threads crossing at right angles; Wood → Long cells lying side by side; Plywood → Thin layers with the grain turned across; Mica → Stacked thin sheets of atoms. *Why:* In each material, something is arranged in lines or layers. That arrangement gives the material its direction.
5. (sort) Which of these have a clear grain? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** Newspaper → Has a clear grain; A bamboo stick → Has a clear grain; A wooden plank → Has a clear grain; Woven cotton cloth → Has a clear grain; A glass tumbler → No clear grain; A steel ball → No clear grain; A lump of clay → No clear grain; A glass of still water → No clear grain. *Why:* Paper, bamboo, wood and woven cloth are built of fibres or threads lying in lines. Glass, steel balls, clay and water have no lines in them that stay in place, so they are almost the same in every direction.
6. (mcq) A tailor wants a strip of cloth to bend smoothly round a curved neckline. How should she cut it from a woven cloth?  
   Options: Along the threads / It makes no difference / Across the threads / On the slant, along the bias  
   **Answer:** On the slant, along the bias. *Why:* Woven cloth stretches most on the slant, because the threads swing to a new angle without stretching. Along the threads, the strip hardly stretches and would not bend round a curve.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the one that stretches LEAST to the one that stretches MOST under the same pull: a woven cotton strip cut along its threads, a woven cotton strip cut on the slant, a rubber strip.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Along the threads, the pull goes straight into strong threads, so the stretch is tiny. On the slant, the threads swing, so the strip stretches more. Rubber stretches the most of the three.
8. (label) Here is a square of woven cloth with three arrows drawn on it. Label each arrow with the word stretches least or stretches most.  
   **Answer:** ['Arrow along the left edge: stretches least', 'Arrow along the top edge: stretches least', 'Arrow corner to corner: stretches most']. *Why:* Along either set of threads, the cloth hardly stretches. On the slant, the squares of the weave turn into diamonds, so the cloth stretches most.
9. (mcq) Why is a plywood board much less likely to split along one line than a plank of the same wood?  
   Options: Plywood is made of a stronger kind of wood / Each layer has its grain turned across the one below, so the layers cover each other's weak direction / Plywood is thicker than a plank / Plywood has more glue than wood  
   **Answer:** Each layer has its grain turned across the one below, so the layers cover each other's weak direction. *Why:* A layer is easiest to split along its grain. In plywood, the next layer has its grain turned across, so it resists the split. The wood is the same kind, and the glue only holds the layers together.
10. (write) Explain why a basket maker splits a bamboo stick lengthwise on purpose, and why a saw would be a worse tool for making thin strips.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that bamboo has strong fibres running along its length, and few things holding the sides together. A split follows the gaps between the fibres, so every fibre stays whole from end to end. A saw wanders and cuts fibres across, leaving cut ends that are weak spots where a crack can start.. *Why:* A split keeps the fibres whole. A saw cuts them. That is why the strips from a split are stronger.
11. (mcq) A paper clip is bent back and forth and snaps after a few bends. Each bend is the same size. Which idea explains this?  
   Options: The metal changes into a different metal / Each bend is bigger than the one before / Tiny cracks grow a little with every bend, until the wire is too thin to hold / The paper clip gets heavier with each bend  
   **Answer:** Tiny cracks grow a little with every bend, until the wire is too thin to hold. *Why:* Each bend does a small amount of damage inside the metal. Tiny cracks start and lengthen until the wire snaps. This is called fatigue. The bends are the same size, and the metal does not become another metal.
12. (truefalse) A paper clip snaps after a few bends because each bend is stronger than the one before.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each bend is the same size. What changes is the inside of the metal, because tiny cracks grow a little with every bend.
13. (draw) Draw a sheet of newspaper with a straight tear and a crooked tear. Draw an arrow for the grain direction, and write how you could tell which way it runs.  
   **Answer:** A sheet with a straight tear running one way and a ragged tear running the other way. A grain arrow along the straight tear, and a note such as: I tear the paper both ways and see which tear runs straight.. *Why:* The straight tear shows the way the fibres lie, so the grain arrow goes along it. Anyone can find the grain with a tear.
14. (mcq) Two bridges are made of identical sheets of paper. One is flat. One is folded into a zigzag. The zigzag one carries many more coins. What changed?  
   Options: The amount of paper / The shape, not the paper / The kind of paper / The grain of the paper  
   **Answer:** The shape, not the paper. *Why:* The paper is the same, and so is its amount. The fold puts some paper far from the middle, where it does most of the work against a bend. So the object is stiffer, though the material is not different.
15. (odd) Three of these have a clear grain. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A glass marble / A sheet of newspaper / A bamboo stick / A wooden plank  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* Bamboo, a plank and newspaper are built of fibres or cells lying in one direction. A glass marble has no lines of fibres or layers.
16. (count) A strip of cloth has two marks 20 centimetres apart. When a bottle hangs from it, the marks are 24 centimetres apart. How many centimetres did the strip stretch?  
   **Answer:** 4 centimetres. *Why:* The new length is 24 centimetres and the old length is 20 centimetres. 24 take away 20 is 4. So the strip stretched 4 centimetres.
17. (mcq) Look at the table. Strip A was cut along the grain and strip X across it. What does the table show?  
   Options: Strip A drooped more than strip X / The table does not tell us anything / Both strips drooped the same amount / Strip X, cut across the grain, drooped about twice as far as strip A  
   **Answer:** Strip X, cut across the grain, drooped about twice as far as strip A. *Why:* Strip A drooped about 2 centimetres and strip X about 4 centimetres. A strip that droops more is more floppy, so the strip cut across the grain is less stiff. Picking strip A means reading the table the wrong way round.
### Shape, layers and wear (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A wooden plank is made twice as thick, with the same length and width. About how much harder is it to bend?  
   Options: About a hundred times as hard / About the same / About eight times as hard / About twice as hard  
   **Answer:** About eight times as hard. *Why:* For a plank bending under a load, the stiffness grows with the cube of the thickness. Double the thickness is two times two times two, which is eight. So the plank is about eight times stiffer. Picking twice as hard counts the extra material, but forgets how much further it sits from the middle.
2. (mcq) A rubber band can stretch a long way and soak up a lot of energy before it breaks, yet it is not stiff. Which word fits the rubber band best?  
   Options: Brittle / Stiff / Tough / Hard  
   **Answer:** Tough. *Why:* Tough means able to take in a lot of energy before breaking. A rubber band does that by stretching. Stiff means hard to bend, which a rubber band is not. Brittle means breaking suddenly with little bending, which is the opposite.
3. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Stiff → Hard to bend or stretch; Strong → Takes a large force before breaking; Tough → Takes in a lot of energy before breaking; Brittle → Breaks suddenly with little bending. *Why:* These four words describe different things. A material can be stiff and brittle, like glass, or soft and tough, like rubber.
4. (truefalse) A material that is strong must also be tough.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Glass can be strong when it is pulled gently, yet a small knock shatters it, so it is not tough. Strength is about the largest force. Toughness is about the energy taken in before it breaks.
5. (fill) A thin sheet of paper can be made much stiffer by changing its ____, without changing the paper.  
   **Answer:** ['shape']. *Why:* Folding, rolling or building up a sheet puts some of the paper far from the middle. That makes the object stiffer, though the paper itself is the same.
6. (order) Put these in order from the LEAST stiff to the MOST stiff. All are made of the same paper, with the same width and length: one sheet, four sheets glued together, two sheets glued together.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Glued sheets act like one thicker sheet, and stiffness grows with the cube of the thickness. Two sheets are about eight times as stiff as one, and four sheets are about sixty-four times as stiff.
7. (sort) Which of these are properties of a material, and which are properties of an object? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** Steel is hard → Property of a material; Rubber can stretch → Property of a material; Glass lets light through → Property of a material; This tumbler holds 250 millilitres → Property of an object; This beam is 2 metres long → Property of an object; The folded bridge is stiffer than the flat sheet → Property of an object. *Why:* A property of a material stays the same whatever the shape. How much a tumbler holds, how long a beam is and how stiff a folded sheet is all depend on the shape that was made.
8. (mcq) A woven cloth is pulled along its threads. Why does it hardly stretch?  
   Options: The cloth is too thick to stretch / The cloth is made of rubber / The threads are not joined at all / The pull goes straight into strong threads that hardly stretch  
   **Answer:** The pull goes straight into strong threads that hardly stretch. *Why:* Each thread is strong and does not stretch much. Pulling along the threads pulls on the threads themselves. On the slant, the pull turns the threads instead, and the cloth gets longer without stretching its threads.
9. (mcq) A plank is used as a short shelf, but with its grain running across the shelf, not along it. It snaps under a few books. Why?  
   Options: The cells are too strong / A pull across the grain pulls the cells apart, and the glue between the cells is weak / The wood is the wrong colour / Planks cannot hold books  
   **Answer:** A pull across the grain pulls the cells apart, and the glue between the cells is weak. *Why:* Wood is made of long cells held side by side by a weaker glue. A force along the cells pulls on the strong walls. A force across them pulls on the weak glue, so the wood splits.
10. (perspective) Kavya wants a long shelf for heavy books. Three people in her family give advice.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer chooses one option and gives a reason about grain. For example, plywood is even in every direction, so it does not split along one line. A solid plank is fine if its grain runs along the shelf. It then admits a fair point from another side, for example that a plank can be as good as plywood when it is cut and used the right way.. *Why:* Nobody is wrong here. A solid plank, plywood and a plank with the grain turned correctly can all work. What matters is which way the grain runs and what load the shelf carries.
11. (write) A ship's engineer finds that a metal part broke after months of use, though the load on it was never more than half of what it could carry once. Use what you learned from the paper clip to explain what may have happened.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that the part was loaded again and again, and each load did a small amount of damage. Tiny cracks started and grew a little each time, until the part was too thin to hold, and it broke. This is called fatigue. A force that is safe once can break something that is loaded many times.. *Why:* The paper clip showed that a force too small to break a metal once can break it after many repeats.
12. (draw) Draw the inside of a plywood sheet with three layers, seen from the side. Draw a small arrow or a dotted line on each layer to show which way its grain runs.  
   **Answer:** Three thin layers, drawn as three bands. The top and bottom layers have the grain drawn as lines along the board, and the middle layer has dots or circles, to show grain coming towards the viewer, or a different pattern to show it is turned across.. *Why:* Turned layers cover each other's weak direction. A picture helps you remember how plywood is built.
13. (label) Here is a picture of a banana leaf with parallel veins. Label two places where you could try to tear the leaf.  
   **Answer:** ['Tear along the veins: easy and straight', 'Tear across the veins: harder and ragged']. *Why:* A leaf splits easily between its parallel veins, where only thin leaf tissue holds the sides together. To tear across the veins you must break them.
14. (odd) Three of these are built from crossed or stacked layers. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Bone / A corrugated cardboard box / A solid wooden plank / Plywood  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Plywood, corrugated cardboard and bone are all built from layers or stacked parts. A solid plank is one piece of wood with its cells all lying the same way.
15. (mcq) A railway axle breaks after a million turns, under a load far below what would break it once. Which idea explains this?  
   Options: Metal never changes / The load was too heavy to carry once / Fatigue: tiny cracks grow a little with each turn / The axle was too thick  
   **Answer:** Fatigue: tiny cracks grow a little with each turn. *Why:* Each turn bends the axle slightly, and tiny cracks grow. After a million turns they can reach all the way through. August Wöhler counted turns to failure in the 1800s and showed that smaller loads take many more turns.
16. (truefalse) A tree that is swayed by the wind every day tends to grow a thicker, shorter trunk.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Experiments since the 1970s, first on small plants and later on trees, have shown that plants which are shaken or swayed grow shorter and thicker stems and trunks. A living thing builds its body in answer to the pushes it feels.
17. (mcq) Look at the bar chart. It shows made-up results from the paper clip test. What does it suggest?  
   Options: Clips bent through a big angle took more bends to snap / The chart does not show anything / Clips bent through a small angle took more bends to snap than clips bent through a big angle / Both kinds of bend took the same number of bends  
   **Answer:** Clips bent through a small angle took more bends to snap than clips bent through a big angle. *Why:* The tall bar is for the small bends, about 28 on average, and the short bar is for the big bends, about 9. A smaller bend does less damage each time, so it takes more bends to build up. Picking the big bend means reading the bars the wrong way round.
### Thinking like a materials engineer (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Board A is made of four layers of newspaper, all with the grain the same way. In board B, every second layer is turned across. Which statement is most likely?  
   Options: Board A droops about the same in both directions / Board B cannot be tested / Both boards droop exactly the same in every direction / Board B droops about the same along and across, and board A droops differently along and across  
   **Answer:** Board B droops about the same along and across, and board A droops differently along and across. *Why:* In board B, the turned layers are strong across where the other layers are weak, so the board is more even. In board A, all the layers share the same weak direction, so the board stays uneven.
2. (write) A friend says, 'Wood is strong.' Rewrite the sentence so that it is more careful, and say what you added and why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer adds the direction and, if possible, a measure or a comparison. For example, 'Wood is many times stronger when you pull it along the grain than across the grain.' It says that the direction matters because the cells lie in lines, and the weak glue between them lets go sooner.. *Why:* One word such as strong can hide the direction of the pull. A careful sentence names the direction.
3. (mcq) A shelf of one kind of wood is 2 centimetres thick and sags 8 millimetres under a load. A shelf of the same wood, length and width, but 4 centimetres thick, carries the same load. About how much will it sag?  
   Options: About 1 millimetre / About 4 millimetres / About 8 millimetres / About 16 millimetres  
   **Answer:** About 1 millimetre. *Why:* Doubling the thickness makes the shelf about eight times stiffer. So it sags about eight times less. 8 millimetres divided by 8 is 1 millimetre.
4. (count) A fatigue machine bends a wire 1,000 times a minute. How many hours and minutes will it take to reach 100,000 bends?  
   **Answer:** 1 hour 40 minutes. *Why:* 100,000 divided by 1,000 is 100 minutes. 100 minutes is 1 hour and 40 minutes.
5. (mcq) Which question is the best one to ask before you choose a plank for a small wooden bridge?  
   Options: Which way does the grain run, and which way will the load pull? / How old is the plank's label? / What colour is the plank? / Is the plank shiny?  
   **Answer:** Which way does the grain run, and which way will the load pull?. *Why:* The strength of a plank depends on the direction of the pull compared with the direction of the grain. Colour and shine tell you nothing about how it will carry a load.
6. (perspective) A shop sells a table of material strengths. It gives one number for each material. Three people talk about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer decides, and gives a reason from the tests: the same newspaper tore straight one way and crooked the other, and woven cloth stretched far more on the slant. So one number can mislead for these materials. It then admits that one number can still be a useful start, as Anita and Farhan say, if the direction is also given.. *Why:* A single number is a summary. It is useful, but for materials with a grain it leaves out the direction.
7. (order) A paper clip is bent back and forth until it snaps. Put these steps in the order they happen.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Fatigue is a story in steps. Each bend starts or lengthens cracks. When too little sound metal is left, the next bend snaps it.
8. (truefalse) Bamboo is a kind of wood.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bamboo is a giant grass, in the same family as rice and sugarcane. A bamboo stick feels like wood because its walls are packed with strong fibres.
9. (match) Match each design to the idea behind it.  
   **Answer:** Plywood → Crossed layers cancel the direction; An I-shaped steel beam → Material far from the middle makes a bend harder; A cloth strip cut on the bias → Threads swing to a new angle, so the cloth stretches; Counting turns to failure → Cracks grow a little with each repeat. *Why:* Each design uses a fact about a material's inside or shape. Knowing the fact lets a designer choose the right design.
10. (sort) Which parts should be checked often for tiny cracks? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** An aircraft wing joint → Checked often for fatigue cracks; A railway axle → Checked often for fatigue cracks; A bicycle pedal arm → Checked often for fatigue cracks; A stone statue in a garden → Rarely needs this check; A brick wall holding up a roof → Rarely needs this check; A concrete step → Rarely needs this check. *Why:* Fatigue comes from being loaded again and again. Wings, axles and pedal arms flex with every journey or turn. A statue or a wall carries the same steady load all the time, so fatigue is not the main worry.
11. (mcq) The window of an aircraft is a rounded shape, not a square with sharp corners. Why?  
   Options: Sharp corners let in more light / A sharp corner makes the stress bigger, so a fatigue crack would start there / Round windows look better / Round windows are easier to clean  
   **Answer:** A sharp corner makes the stress bigger, so a fatigue crack would start there. *Why:* Stress piles up at a sharp corner, so a crack can begin there after many flights. Rounded corners spread the stress out. Engineers learned this after the 1954 Comet airliner accidents.
12. (write) Two planks are cut from the same tree and are the same size. One snaps under a load much sooner than the other. Give two reasons why this could happen, using ideas from this unit.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives two reasons, such as: one plank has its grain running across the span, and the other along it. Or one plank has a knot or a tiny crack, and a crack can grow and break it. It may also say that one plank was damp. It must be clear that the reasons come from the inside of the wood.. *Why:* The inside of a plank decides how it breaks: the direction of the grain, tiny cracks and knots. Two planks that look alike can differ inside.
13. (draw) Draw an I-shaped steel beam from the end. Mark where the thick parts are, and write one sentence on why this shape is stiff.  
   **Answer:** An I shape with a thick bar at the top, a thick bar at the bottom and a thin web joining them. A sentence such as: Most of the steel is far from the middle, where it does the most work against a bend.. *Why:* An I-shape puts material where it helps most. The thin web in the middle saves weight.
14. (mcq) An ordinary rubber band gets thinner when you stretch it. An auxetic material is built differently. What would an auxetic rubber band do when stretched?  
   Options: Snap at once / Get fatter / Stay exactly the same / Get thinner  
   **Answer:** Get fatter. *Why:* An auxetic material has hinged shapes inside that open out when pulled, so the material gets fatter. This is a property of the structure of the inside, not just of what it is made of.
15. (fill) A material that has the same property in every direction is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['isotropic']. *Why:* Isotropic comes from Greek words meaning equal in all ways. Glass and still water are close to this. A material that differs by direction is called anisotropic.
16. (mcq) Which of these can scientists NOT yet do reliably?  
   Options: Measure how far a strip of cloth stretches / Split bamboo along its length / Make plywood / Predict from its atoms alone exactly how long a particular steel bridge part will last  
   **Answer:** Predict from its atoms alone exactly how long a particular steel bridge part will last. *Why:* Real materials have tiny cracks, mixed ingredients and crystal edges that are very hard to calculate. People can make plywood and measure a stretch, but a full prediction from atoms alone is still beyond us.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which sentence best describes a material that has a grain?  
   Options: It behaves differently when pulled in different directions / It is always brown / It is always made of wood / It is always weak  
   **Answer:** It behaves differently when pulled in different directions. *Why:* A grain is a direction in the material, set by fibres, cells, threads or layers lying in lines. Colour and kind of material do not decide it. Picking 'weak' mixes up direction with strength.
2. (mcq) How could you find out which way the fibres lie in a sheet of newspaper?  
   Options: Hold it up to the light / Weigh it / Smell it / Tear it in two directions and see which tear is straighter  
   **Answer:** Tear it in two directions and see which tear is straighter. *Why:* A tear runs most easily between the lines of fibres, so the straighter tear shows the grain. Weight, light and smell do not show a direction.
3. (mcq) Why does a bamboo stick split so easily along its length?  
   Options: Bamboo is soft all through / Its strong fibres run along it, and little holds the sides together / Bamboo is full of water / Bamboo has no fibres  
   **Answer:** Its strong fibres run along it, and little holds the sides together. *Why:* Bamboo is packed with fibres lying along the stem. A split follows the gaps between them. A force across the fibres has to break them. Picking 'soft all through' misses that the direction matters.
4. (mcq) Two bridges are made from identical sheets of paper. One is flat and sags under a coin. The other is rolled into a tube and carries many coins. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Coins weigh less on a tube / The tube is made of a stronger paper / The tube has more paper / The shape of the tube is stiffer, though the paper is the same  
   **Answer:** The shape of the tube is stiffer, though the paper is the same. *Why:* The paper is the same. Rolling it puts the paper far from the middle, where it does the most work against a bend. A property of the object, its stiffness, changed. The property of the material did not.
5. (mcq) Why is plywood less likely to split along one line than a plank?  
   Options: The layers cross, so each covers the weak direction of the next / It is made without glue / It is made of harder wood / It is lighter  
   **Answer:** The layers cross, so each covers the weak direction of the next. *Why:* Each layer of plywood has its grain turned across the one below. Where one layer would split, the next holds. Glue holds the layers together, but it is not the reason for the evenness.

   Options: Elastic / Tough and flexible / Brittle, not tough / Soft

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Glass has no lines of fibres or layers, so it is almost the same in every direction. Wood, paper and woven cloth are not.

## Applications
- Building boats, furniture and roofs with wood cut so that the grain runs the strongest way.
- Making plywood and laminated timber for doors, floors, boat hulls and school desks.
- Weaving, stitching and cutting cloth, such as cutting on the bias for a neckline or a drape.
- Packing goods in corrugated cardboard boxes that are stiff along the flutes.
- Making baskets, mats and furniture from split bamboo and cane strips.
- Designing bicycle frames, scaffolds and tent poles from hollow tubes.
- Building bridges and cranes from steel beams with an I-shape.
- Checking aircraft, trains and bridges for tiny fatigue cracks before they grow.
- Choosing the paper direction for books, so that pages open and turn easily.
- Designing materials with layers, fibres and holes, such as carbon-fibre tennis rackets and cricket helmets.

## Open questions
- **How does a living tree decide which way to lay down new fibres as she grows?** Scientists know that trees sense bending and lean, and build special wood in answer. How a cell senses a push and chooses where to put new fibres is still being studied.
- **Can we ever predict all the properties of a material from its atoms alone?** For simple crystals, computers can calculate some properties well. For real materials, with cracks, mixtures and crystal edges, the calculation is too hard, and scientists disagree about how close we are.
- **How can we tell, before it happens, exactly when a metal part will fail by fatigue?** Engineers have good rules for how many bends a part can take on average. Predicting when one particular part will crack is still uncertain, because tiny flaws vary from part to part.
- **Is there a limit to how strong a material can be?** Calculations give a very high limit for a perfect crystal, and real materials are far weaker because of flaws. Whether we can make large pieces that come close to the limit is not known.
- **Can a material be built so that it heals its own cracks?** Living things heal their wounds, and bone repairs a break. Scientists have made small samples of plastics and concrete that heal tiny cracks, but making this work in large, everyday parts is still an open problem.
- **How did the first people learn to split wood and bamboo along the grain?** No written records survive from that time. We know that stone tools, wedges and split bamboo are very old, but not who first used the grain on purpose.

## Connected maths
- In the droop test, strip A drooped 3 centimetres and strip X drooped 6 centimetres. How many times as far did strip X droop as strip A? **2 times.**. Strip X drooped 6 centimetres and strip A drooped 3 centimetres. 6 divided by 3 is 2. So strip X, cut across the grain, drooped twice as far. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A plank is doubled in thickness and becomes about 8 times as stiff, because 2 times 2 times 2 is 8. About how many times as stiff would it be if its thickness were tripled? **About 27 times.**. Tripling the thickness is 3 times 3 times 3. 3 times 3 is 9, and 9 times 3 is 27. So the plank would be about 27 times as stiff. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- Five loose sheets of paper laid on each other are only 5 times as stiff as one sheet, because they slide. Five sheets glued into one act like a sheet 5 times as thick, so they are 5 times 5 times 5 times as stiff. How many times stiffer is the glued stack than the loose stack? **25 times.**. The glued stack is 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125 times as stiff as one sheet. The loose stack is 5 times as stiff. 125 divided by 5 is 25. So the glued stack is 25 times stiffer than the loose stack. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A strip of cloth cut along the threads stretched 0.2 centimetres out of a 20 centimetre length. A strip cut on the slant stretched 4 centimetres out of 20 centimetres. What percentage of its length did each strip stretch? How many times more did the slant strip stretch? **1 percent and 20 percent. The slant strip stretched 20 times more.**. 0.2 out of 20 is 1 out of 100, which is 1 percent. 4 out of 20 is 1 out of 5, which is 20 percent. 20 divided by 1 is 20. So the slant strip stretched 20 times more. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A fatigue machine bends a wire 500 times each minute. How many hours and minutes will it take to reach 100,000 bends? **3 hours 20 minutes.**. 100,000 divided by 500 is 200 minutes. 200 minutes is 3 hours and 20 minutes, because 3 hours is 180 minutes and 20 minutes are left over. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Plywood boards are made with an odd number of layers. A maker has stacks of 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 layers. Which stacks can she use? If each layer is 2 millimetres thick, how thick is the 7-layer board? **The stacks of 3, 5 and 7 layers. The 7-layer board is 14 millimetres thick.**. A number is odd if it cannot be split into two equal groups. So 3, 5 and 7 are odd. 7 layers of 2 millimetres is 7 times 2, which is 14 millimetres. (queues `math.number-sense.odd-even.L3`)
- In the paper bridge test, the flat sheet carried 2 coins. The zigzag bridge carried 34 coins. How many times as many coins did the zigzag carry? The tube carried 51 coins. How many more coins did the tube carry than the zigzag? **17 times as many. The tube carried 17 more.**. 34 divided by 2 is 17. So the zigzag carried 17 times as many coins. 51 take away 34 is 17. So the tube carried 17 more coins than the zigzag. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Three clips snapped after 8, 10 and 9 big bends. Three more snapped after 26, 31 and 27 small bends. Find the average for each group. About how many times more small bends than big bends did it take? **9 big bends and 28 small bends. About 3 times as many.**. 8 plus 10 plus 9 is 27, and 27 divided by 3 is 9. 26 plus 31 plus 27 is 84, and 84 divided by 3 is 28. 28 divided by 9 is a little more than 3. So small bends took about 3 times as many. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A shelf plank is 2 centimetres thick and holds a load with a sag of 16 millimetres. You swap it for a plank of the same wood that is 4 centimetres thick. Using the rule that doubling the thickness makes a plank 8 times as stiff, what sag do you expect? Why can you not simply halve it? **About 2 millimetres. Halving would give 8 millimetres, but the stiffness grows eight times.**. The thicker plank is about 8 times as stiff, so it sags about 8 times less. 16 divided by 8 is 2 millimetres. Halving would be right only if the stiffness doubled, but it grows by 2 times 2 times 2. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a grain in wood or paper? → The direction in which the fibres or cells mostly lie.
- How can you find the grain of newspaper? → Tear it two ways. The straighter tear runs along the grain.
- Does glass have a grain? → No. It is almost the same in every direction.
- Which way does woven cloth stretch most? → On the slant, along the bias.
- Why does plywood have crossed layers? → So each layer covers the weak direction of the next.
- What does stiff mean? → Hard to bend or stretch.
- What does tough mean? → Able to take in a lot of energy before breaking.
- What is fatigue? → Damage that builds up when a thing is bent or loaded again and again.
- How much stiffer is a plank when it is twice as thick? → About 8 times.
