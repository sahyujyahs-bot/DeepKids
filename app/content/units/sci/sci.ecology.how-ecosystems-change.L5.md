# How ecosystems change and heal: storms, wolves, mangroves and the patchwork of a landscape
`sci.ecology.how-ecosystems-change.L5`

**Summary.** A storm fells trees in a forest every few years. A fire burns a hillside. A volcano buries a whole island in ash. So why is the world not bare by now? You find out with dice and a paper forest of 36 patches. Storms hit your forest 60 times, and you count how much old forest is left. You roast a pot of soil to find out where new plants come from. You follow the wolves who were brought back to Yellowstone, and sort what scientists are sure of from what they still argue about. Then you see how people in Tamil Nadu helped a dying mangrove forest grow back, mostly by letting the sea back in.

**Intro.** *Somewhere near your home, a plant is growing where nobody planted them. It may be a peepal seedling on a wall, grass in a crack, or moss under a dripping pipe. How did they get there? And what would that spot look like in 50 years if nobody touched it?* In the first activity, Nobody Planted Me, you hunt for these plants and work out how each one arrived. They are the first step of a change that goes on in every bare place on Earth. In this unit you follow that change from a crack in a wall to a whole forest, and then to the question of how a damaged place heals.

## Terms
- **Ecosystem**: An ecosystem is all the living beings of one place, together with the soil, water, air and sunlight they use. *e.g.* A pond with its fish, plants, mud, water and the sunlight falling on it is one ecosystem. So is a patch of forest.
- **Disturbance**: A disturbance is an event that kills or removes many living beings from a place in a short time. *e.g.* A cyclone that fells trees is a disturbance. So is a fire, a flood, a landslide, or a bulldozer.
- **Succession**: Succession is the slow change in which living beings live in a place, one group after another, after a disturbance. *e.g.* A paddy field left alone grows weeds in the first year, bushes after a few years, and young trees after that.
- **Pioneer species**: Pioneer species are the first living beings to settle in a bare or cleared place. *e.g.* Lichens on bare rock, grasses on a landslide and the fast-growing Macaranga tree in a forest gap are all pioneers.
- **Primary succession**: Primary succession is succession that starts on ground with no soil and no life, such as new lava or bare rock. *e.g.* The island of Surtsey rose from the sea near Iceland in 1963. Everything living there had to arrive from somewhere else.
- **Secondary succession**: Secondary succession is succession that starts where the soil is still there after a disturbance. *e.g.* After a forest fire, the soil still holds seeds, roots and fungi. So new plants appear within weeks.
- **Seed bank**: A seed bank is all the living seeds that lie waiting in the soil of a place. *e.g.* A handful of soil from under a weedy patch can hold dozens of seeds that you cannot see.
- **Old forest**: An old forest is a forest that has grown for a very long time without being cleared. Scientists also call it old-growth forest. *e.g.* The great hornbill nests in holes in very large old trees. A young forest has no such trees yet.
- **Patch**: A patch is one area of land that differs from the land around it, in its age or in who lives there. *e.g.* A gap where one giant tree fell is a small patch. A burnt hillside is a large one.
- **Patchwork (mosaic)**: A patchwork, or mosaic, is a landscape made of many patches of different kinds and ages lying side by side. *e.g.* From a hilltop in the Western Ghats you can see tea, grassland, young forest and old forest in one view.
- **Apex predator**: An apex predator is a predator at the top of a food web. No other animal hunts the adults for food. *e.g.* Tigers in Nagarhole and wolves in Yellowstone are apex predators.
- **Trophic cascade**: A trophic cascade is a chain of changes that passes down a food web, from predators to plant-eaters to plants. *e.g.* Where sea otters eat sea urchins, fewer urchins eat the kelp, and the kelp forest grows.
- **Ecosystem engineer**: An ecosystem engineer is a living being who changes the shape of a place, and so changes who else can live there. *e.g.* A beaver builds a dam across a stream and makes a pond. Elephants push over trees and make clearings.
- **Restoration**: Restoration is work done by people to help a damaged ecosystem recover. *e.g.* At Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu, people dug channels so that the tide could reach a dying mangrove forest again.
- **Natural regeneration**: Natural regeneration is a place growing back by itself, from the seeds and roots already there and the seeds that arrive. *e.g.* A field next to a forest is fenced off from cattle. In a few years young trees come up without anyone planting them.
- **Reference site**: A reference site is a healthy place of the same kind nearby. People study it to learn what a damaged place could become. *e.g.* Before restoring a rainforest fragment in the Anamalai hills, scientists list the trees of the protected forest next to it.
- **Wetland**: A wetland is land that is covered with shallow water, or soaked with water, for all or part of the year. *e.g.* Pallikaranai marsh in Chennai, the mangroves of Pichavaram and a village tank edge full of reeds are all wetlands.
- **Tipping point**: A tipping point is the point where a small extra change makes an ecosystem switch to a very different state that then keeps itself going. *e.g.* A clear lake that gets more and more sewage can turn green and cloudy quite suddenly, and then stay that way.

## Explanations
### Disturbance: places get cleared all the time · *defined*
**Definition:** A disturbance is an event that kills or removes many living beings from a place in a short time.

Forests, grasslands, rivers and reefs are disturbed again and again. A big tree falls and takes the neighbours down. A cyclone flattens a mangrove. A river in flood scrapes its banks. A fire runs through dry grass. People add more: they cut, burn, plough, mine and build. Disturbances differ in three ways. They differ in size, from one fallen tree to a whole island. They differ in how often they come. And they differ in what they leave behind. A fire may leave the soil with its seeds and roots. A lava flow leaves nothing living at all. What is left behind decides how recovery starts, as your roasted soil shows in The Soil Remembers. A disturbance is not always harm. The gap left by a fallen tree is the only place where many seedlings get enough light to grow.
- e.g. In a rainforest in the Western Ghats, one giant tree falling can open a gap as wide as a house plot.
- e.g. On 18 May 1980 the volcano Mount St Helens in the United States blew out sideways and flattened forest for many kilometres.
- e.g. A village tank that dries out fully in a drought year is a disturbance for the fish and the water plants.
### Succession: who comes back, and why in that order · *defined*
**Definition:** Succession is the change in which living beings live in a place, one group after another, after a disturbance.

The first to settle are the pioneer species. They are good at arriving and good at living in the open. Their seeds or spores are small and many, and travel on wind, water or birds. They grow fast in full sun. But why do they not stay for ever? The reason is that they change the place. They shade the ground. Their dead leaves and roots add to the soil and hold water in it. Some, such as lupines and other plants of the bean family, house bacteria in their roots who take nitrogen from the air. Plants need nitrogen to build their bodies. Now the place suits other plants better than before. Many pioneers need full sun to sprout, so their own seedlings do poorly in the shade they made. Slower plants whose seedlings bear shade grow up under them and, in time, over them. Animals follow the plants they need. This order is a tendency, not a timetable. Which seeds happen to arrive first also matters, and two patches cleared on the same day can go different ways.
- e.g. On an abandoned field in South India, grasses and touch-me-not come first, then shrubs such as lantana and Calotropis, then trees.
- e.g. In a gap in the Western Ghats rainforest, Macaranga trees shoot up in the sun. Slow, shade-bearing trees rise beneath them over many years.
- e.g. On new ash at Mount St Helens, prairie lupines were the first plants. Their fallen leaves added nitrogen, and their stems trapped other seeds blown by the wind.
### Starting with nothing, and starting with soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Primary succession starts on ground with no soil and no life. Secondary succession starts where the soil, with its seeds, roots and tiny beings, is still there.

Primary succession is slow, because soil has to be made first. On bare rock, lichens are often the first to settle. A lichen is a fungus and an alga, or a bacterium, living as one body. They make weak acids that slowly dissolve the rock's surface. Dust and dead lichen collect in cracks, and mosses root in that. On a new island, the sea and birds do much of the work. On Surtsey, the first plant with roots and leaves was a sea rocket, found on the shore in 1965. Her seed had floated there. Later, seabirds nested on the island, and their droppings fed the soil. Plants grew thickest where the gulls lived. Secondary succession is far quicker. The soil already holds a seed bank, living roots, fungi and bacteria. In The Soil Remembers you compare the two starts in two pots. One pot keeps its seed bank and one has had it killed by heat.
- e.g. Krakatau, a volcanic island in Indonesia, blew apart in August 1883. Nine months later a visitor found no plants, and only one tiny spider. By 1886 there were mosses, ferns and beach plants.
- e.g. A burnt grass slope in the Nilgiris is green again within weeks of the first rain, because the grass roots lived.
- e.g. A new concrete wall has no soil. Its first settlers are algae, mosses and lichens, where water drips.
### Does succession have an end? · *debated*
For a long time textbooks said that every place moves toward one final, stable community, called the climax, and then stops changing. Today most ecologists say something different. Late in succession, change does slow down, and old forests can look the same for centuries. But they are not finished. Trees die and gaps open. The climate shifts. New species arrive. So an old forest is better described as changing slowly than as complete.
- e.g. Pollen in lake mud shows that after the last ice age, each kind of tree spread north across North America at a different speed and by a different route.
- e.g. A sacred grove in Kerala may look unchanged for 200 years, yet every big tree in it will fall and be replaced.
- e.g. Lantana, brought to India from the Americas in the 1800s, now grows in many old forests that had no such shrub before.
- **Frederic Clements, American botanist, 1916:** A plant community is like one giant living being. It is born, grows up through fixed stages and reaches an adult form, the climax, which the climate of the region decides.
- **Henry Gleason, American botanist, 1926:** Each species spreads and grows according to their own needs and their own luck in arriving. A community is whoever happens to be living together in a place. There is no fixed end.
- **Most ecologists today:** Gleason was closer to the truth about how communities form. But Clements was right that living beings change a place for each other, and that late stages change slowly.
### A landscape is a patchwork, and the mix can stay steady · *defined*
**Definition:** A landscape is a patchwork of patches at different ages since their last disturbance. If disturbances come at a steady rate, the share of young, middle and old patches stays about the same, while each single patch keeps changing.

Walk through a large old forest and you cross gaps of every age. Here is last year's treefall, bright and full of weeds. There is a thicket of thin poles twenty years old. Beyond are giants. Each patch is moving through succession, and sooner or later each is knocked back. Why does the whole not end up all young or all old? You find the reason in the Patchwork Test. Each round, storms clear a few patches, and the same number of older patches grow up a step. When what is cleared equals what grows back, the mix stops changing. The American ecologists Herbert Bormann and Gene Likens described this for a forest in 1979. The mix matters to the residents. Some need open sun, some need thickets and some need giant old trees. A patchwork gives each a place, and lets them move when their own patch changes.
- e.g. In your Patchwork Test, about 22 of 36 patches were old forest from round 3 onward, though different squares were old each time.
- e.g. In Kaziranga in Assam, floods, grazing and the yearly burning of old grass keep a patchwork of short grass, tall grass, wetland and woodland. Rhinos, deer and birds use different parts.
- e.g. Elephants push over trees and open clearings. The clearings fill with young plants that deer and gaur feed on.
### How much disturbance is best? · *debated*
If patches are almost never cleared, the sun-loving pioneers have nowhere to live. If patches are cleared very often, nothing has time to grow old. So it seems that a middle amount of disturbance should keep the most kinds of living beings. Your Patchwork Test, played with no storms, 6 storms and 12 storms, lets you check this on paper. Real studies give a mixed answer.
- e.g. Part 2 of the Patchwork Test: doubling the storms cut the old patches from about 22 to about 13.
- e.g. A lawn mown every week holds very few kinds of plant. An unmown corner of the same park holds many more.
- e.g. Rocks on a seashore that waves roll over every few months carry more kinds of seaweed than rocks that never move.
- **Joseph Connell, American ecologist, 1978:** The number of species is highest where disturbance is neither rare nor frequent. With too little, a few strong competitors crowd out the rest. With too much, only fast settlers live.
- **Jeremy Fox, Canadian ecologist, 2013:** When real places are measured, the hump in the middle shows up only rarely. The reasoning behind it has gaps too. The idea should be dropped.
- **Douglas Sheil and David Burslem, replying in 2013:** The idea still explains real patterns, especially among forest trees. It must be tested with care, comparing the same kind of place under different amounts of disturbance.
### The wolves of Yellowstone: what happened · *defined*
**Definition:** Wolves were removed from Yellowstone National Park in the United States by 1926 and brought back in 1995 and 1996. Elk numbers then fell, and some plants that elk eat began to grow taller in some places.

Park rangers and hunters killed the last wolves of Yellowstone in 1926. People then thought predators were harmful. With no wolves, the elk, who are large deer, became many. They ate young willow, aspen and cottonwood trees down to stubs. Beavers need willow for food and for their dams, and beavers became rare. In January 1995, 14 wolves caught in Canada were brought to the park. Another 17 came in 1996. The wolves bred and spread. On the park's northern range, the winter count of elk fell from about 17,000 in 1995 to about 8,000 in 2004, and it has fallen further since. In some valleys young aspen and willow have since grown taller than an elk can reach. Beaver colonies on the northern range rose from 1 in 2001 to 9 in 2011. A chain of changes like this, from predator to plant-eater to plant, is called a trophic cascade. That much is agreed. How much of it the wolves caused is argued about, and the next card tells you why.
- e.g. By the end of 2023 there were 124 wolves in 11 packs in Yellowstone, all descended from the wolves brought in and from wolves who walked in later.
- e.g. In India, where tigers are protected in a reserve, the number of deer and wild pigs they hunt is held down in the same way.
- e.g. On the coast of North America, sea otters returning to a bay were followed by fewer sea urchins and more kelp.
### The wolves of Yellowstone: what scientists argue about · *debated*
A short film from 2014, How Wolves Change Rivers, says that the wolves changed where elk dared to feed, that the trees came back, and that the rivers themselves changed their shape. It has been watched many millions of times. It is a clear and moving story. Most scientists who work in Yellowstone say it claims too much. Three things make the true answer hard to find. First, wolves were not the only change. In the same years, people outside the park hunted many elk, the numbers of cougars and grizzly bears grew, and there were dry years. Second, there is only one Yellowstone. Nobody can run the same park again without wolves and compare. Third, the answer depends on where you measure and what you measure. Notice what kind of argument this is. Nobody is arguing about whether wolves belong in Yellowstone. They are arguing about how strongly one returned animal can repair a whole place.
- e.g. Two teams measured the same willows from 2001 to 2020. One measured height and found a weak effect. The other measured the volume of the leafy crown and found a strong one.
- e.g. Bison in northern Yellowstone have become many more since the 1990s, and they eat young trees too.
- e.g. In the activity The Wolf Chain you sort eight claims from this story into agreed, argued and claimed too much.
- **William Ripple, Robert Beschta and colleagues, Oregon State University:** The return of wolves, with other predators, lowered the pressure of elk on young trees. Aspen and willow have recovered strongly in many places. It is a clear trophic cascade.
- **Tom Hobbs, David Cooper and colleagues, Colorado State University, 2024:** In a 20-year experiment, willows grew tall only where they were both fenced from browsing and given a raised water level by a small dam. Wolves alone have not restored the streams. In the first ten years, human hunters lowered the elk more than wolves did.
- **The film How Wolves Change Rivers, 2014:** Wolves made elk avoid risky valleys. Trees returned, banks held firm, and rivers ran straighter. Scientists on both sides above say this part goes beyond the evidence.
### Why putting one piece back does not always rewind a place · *idea*
**Definition:** An ecosystem can settle into more than one lasting state. Once it has switched, undoing the first cause may not switch it back.

Think about the streams of northern Yellowstone. When the wolves went, elk ate the willows. With no willows, the beavers left. With no beaver dams, the streams ran faster and cut down into their beds. As a stream cuts deeper, the water under the ground beside it sinks too. Willow roots then cannot reach water easily, even if no elk ever bites them. So the place now holds itself in a new state: deep stream, low water, short willows, no beavers. Bringing back the wolves removes the first cause, but the later causes are still there. Lakes show the same thing. A clear lake that receives too much sewage turns green with algae. The algae shade out the bottom plants, who used to hold the mud down and shelter the tiny animals that eat algae. Cutting the sewage back to the old amount is then not enough. The Dutch scientist Marten Scheffer and others have studied such switches since the 1990s. How common they are in nature is still being worked out.
- e.g. In the Yellowstone experiment, willows behind small man-made dams grew taller, because the dam raised the water under the ground.
- e.g. Some shallow lakes in the Netherlands stayed green for years after the sewage was cut. They cleared only when people also took out many of the fish who stir up the mud.
- e.g. A coral reef that loses its plant-eating fish can become covered in seaweed, and young corals then find no bare rock to settle on.
### Restoration: find what is blocking recovery · *defined*
**Definition:** Restoration is work done by people to help a damaged ecosystem recover. Its first question is what is stopping this place from growing back by itself.

Most damaged places are trying to recover all the time. Seeds arrive, roots resprout and animals wander in. So a restorer asks what is in the way. Sometimes cattle or fire keep killing the seedlings. Then a fence or a fire line is enough, and the land regrows by itself. This is called natural regeneration. A review of 133 studies of tropical forests, published in 2017, found that naturally regrown forests held more kinds of living beings, on average, than planted ones. Sometimes the seeds cannot arrive, because the nearest forest is far away. Then people must bring them. In the Anamalai hills of Tamil Nadu, Divya Mudappa, T. R. Shankar Raman and their team have restored rainforest fragments among tea estates since about 2001. They remove weeds such as lantana and plant many kinds of local trees raised from wild seed. And sometimes the block is in the ground or the water. At Pichavaram near Chidambaram, mangroves had died over about half the forest. Villagers and scientists found that sea water was standing still in sunken ground and turning far too salty. From 1996 they dug channels to let the tide flow in and out. Six years later, most of that damaged ground had young mangroves on it. A restored place is not the same as one that was never damaged. In the Anamalai study, the planted fragments were well ahead of the unplanted ones after 7 to 15 years, and still behind the old forest beside them.
- e.g. Pichavaram: about 375 hectares were damaged before the work. About 65 hectares were still damaged six years after it began.
- e.g. Anamalai hills: replanted fragments stored about half as much carbon again as fragments left alone, yet less than the old forest nearby.
- e.g. A hillside beside a healthy forest, closed to cattle for ten years, usually needs no planting at all.
### Rivers and wetlands: give the water back its path · *defined*
**Definition:** A river or a wetland is made by moving water. To restore one, people restore how the water flows: where it goes, how fast, and when it rises and falls.

A river is more than a channel. It floods and shrinks with the seasons, carries sand and silt, and lets fish swim up and down it. A dam blocks all three. On the Elwha river in the United States, two old dams were taken down between 2011 and 2014. It was the largest dam removal in that country. Salmon, who are fish that swim up from the sea to breed, were seen above both dam sites by 2016. The first years were hard: the mud that poured out killed most small river animals downstream, and some kinds of salmon have still not recovered. A wetland is land soaked with shallow water. It works like a towel on a sloping board, as you find in Wetland on a Board. It soaks up a rush of water and lets it out slowly. Pallikaranai marsh in Chennai covered about 5,500 hectares in 1965. Roads, buildings and a rubbish dump have left about 600 to 700 hectares. Many scientists link Chennai's worst floods, as in December 2015, partly to the loss of marshes and lakes like this one. The marsh that remains was made a protected wetland of world importance in 2022.
- e.g. Pichavaram: the cure was to bring back the tide's flow in and out, twice a day.
- e.g. A tank bund in Tamil Nadu repaired so that the tank fills in the monsoon brings back reeds, fish and water birds within a year or two.
- e.g. On the Elwha, sand carried down the freed river rebuilt beaches at the river's mouth.
### The ecosystem that belongs to the place is not always a forest · *defined*
**Definition:** The goal of restoration is the kind of ecosystem that the place's own climate, soil and history support. In many places that is grassland, scrub, desert or marsh, not forest.

Many people think healing land means planting trees. But large parts of India were never forest. The dry plains of the Deccan, parts of Rajasthan and Gujarat, and the high grasslands of the Western Ghats have been open country for many thousands of years. Blackbuck, wolves, great Indian bustards, larks and thousands of kinds of grass and herb live there and nowhere else. Old government maps mark much of this land as wasteland. A study by M. D. Madhusudan and Abi Vanak, published in 2023, found that nearly 70 in every 100 parts of India's dry open ecosystems lay on land marked as wasteland. Fewer than 5 in every 100 parts were protected. Planting trees there does not restore anything. It replaces one old ecosystem with a plantation, and the grassland beings lose their home. So a restorer first asks what this place was, and looks for a reference site. A reference site is a healthy place of the same kind nearby. The same care applies to mangroves. Seedlings planted on open mudflats where mangroves never grew mostly die.
- e.g. On the Nilgiri plateau, the natural cover is a patchwork of shola forest in the folds and grassland on the slopes. Wattle and eucalyptus planted on the grass have harmed it.
- e.g. The great Indian bustard needs wide, open, dry grassland to feed and to nest. A plantation of trees takes that away.
- e.g. In the Rann of Kutch, salt desert is the natural ecosystem, and wild asses live there.
### Following the idea further: atoms, energy and deep time · *idea*
**Definition:** Succession can be followed down to atoms and energy, and back through the history of the Earth.

Atoms first. Bare rock has almost no nitrogen that a plant can use, though the air above it is mostly nitrogen. The first settlers that matter most are often those who house nitrogen-catching bacteria, such as lupines and alder trees. Lichens dissolve minerals out of rock, a few atoms at a time. A soil is rock, air and dead bodies that living beings have mixed. Now energy. A young patch grows fast and adds wood quickly. An old forest adds wood slowly but holds far more carbon in total, in its trunks and its soil. Seeds sense all this. Many pioneer seeds lie in the dark soil for years. They sprout when they sense the fuller light and the bigger swings of day and night temperature that a gap brings. Now time. After the last ice age, forests moved north over thousands of years, each tree species at their own speed. And 66 million years ago an asteroid struck the Earth and most forests died. In the rocks of North America laid down just after it, the pollen of trees almost vanishes and fern spores take over. Ferns were the pioneers then, as they are on fresh landslides today. Some scientists read all this as plain chemistry and chance. Seeds sense, wait and choose their moment, and that is part of how a place heals.
- e.g. Wild tobacco seeds in North America sprout when they sense chemicals from smoke, which tell them a fire has cleared the ground.
- e.g. Near a retreating Himalayan glacier, the ground freed from ice first grows mosses and small herbs, and shrubs only decades later.
- e.g. The layer of fern spores above the asteroid layer is called the fern spike.

## Mostly agreed
- After a disturbance, living beings return in a rough order, and the early settlers change the place for the later ones. *How we know:* Scientists have followed this year by year on Surtsey since 1963, on Mount St Helens since 1980, and on thousands of abandoned fields.
- Recovery is much faster where the soil with its seeds and roots is still there. *How we know:* Burnt and logged land greens within a season. New lava and bare rock take decades to grow more than lichens and scattered plants. Your roasted soil shows the same thing in a pot.
- A large natural landscape is a patchwork of patches of different ages. *How we know:* Aerial photographs, satellite pictures and the ages of trees counted from their rings all show it, in every kind of forest and grassland studied.
- Wolves were brought back to Yellowstone in 1995 and 1996, and the number of elk on the northern range then fell by more than half. *How we know:* Wolves and elk have been counted there every year. Hunters, bears, cougars and dry years also lowered the elk, as well as wolves.
- Where the cause of damage is removed and seeds can arrive, most damaged ecosystems begin to recover by themselves. *How we know:* A 2017 review of 133 studies of tropical forests, and the regrowth at Pichavaram after the tide was let back in from 1996.
- A restored ecosystem is usually poorer than one that was never damaged, for decades at least. *How we know:* In the 2017 review and in the Anamalai hills study of 2019, restored forests had fewer kinds of living beings, or less carbon, than old forest nearby.

## How we found out
**Henry Cowles, Frederic Clements, Henry Gleason, Robert Whittaker, John Curtis and Margaret Davis, all in the United States., 1899 (the dunes), 1916 (the climax theory), 1926 (Gleason's reply), the 1950s (the forest measurements) and the 1960s and 1970s (the pollen maps).** In 1899 Henry Cowles walked inland from the shore of Lake Michigan in the United States. Near the water the sand dunes were bare. Further back, older dunes carried grass, then shrubs, then forest. He saw that walking inland was like walking forward in time. In 1916 Frederic Clements built a grand theory on this. He said a plant community is like one giant living being. It grows up through fixed stages to an adult form, the climax, and then stays. This idea filled textbooks for forty years. In 1926 Henry Gleason disagreed. He said each species spreads separately, wherever the seeds land and the plant finds what is needed. Almost nobody listened to him. In the 1950s Robert Whittaker and John Curtis measured real forests and found Gleason's pattern. Then Margaret Davis read the pollen buried in lake mud, layer by layer. She showed that after the ice age each kind of tree had moved north at a different speed, by a different path. The forests of today were never one being. They were put together, kind by kind.  
*What people believed before:* That a community of plants is like one living being that grows up to a fixed adult form, the climax, and then stops changing. Measurements of real forests, and pollen in lake mud, showed that each species comes and goes separately and that communities keep changing.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Nature is in balance. Left alone, every place returns to the way it was.** (A very old and common belief, and the climax idea of Frederic Clements, 1916; *rejected*)  
  For: Many places do return to something like their earlier state. A burnt grassland looks the same two years later.  
  Against: Some places switch to a new state and stay there. Climates change, species arrive and species are lost. Pollen records show that the mix of trees in a region has kept changing for thousands of years.
- **Bringing back the wolves healed Yellowstone, and even changed its rivers.** (The film How Wolves Change Rivers, 2014, and many news stories; *debated*)  
  For: Elk fell by more than half. Aspen and willow have grown taller in a number of places. Beaver colonies increased.  
  Against: Hunters, bears, cougars and drought also cut the elk. In a 20-year experiment, willows on most streams stayed short unless the water level was raised as well. Careful measurements have not shown rivers changing course.
- **The best way to heal land is to plant as many trees as possible.** (Many tree-planting drives around the world; *debated*)  
  For: Where the nearest forest is far away, seeds cannot arrive, and planting local trees speeds recovery. The Anamalai rainforest work shows this.  
  Against: Natural regrowth often does better and costs less. Trees planted on natural grasslands, marshes or mudflats destroy the ecosystem that was there. Rows of one kind of tree are a plantation, not a forest.
- **A tiny, very dense planted forest can become like a natural forest in about 20 or 30 years.** (Followers of the planting method of the Japanese botanist Akira Miyawaki; *debated*)  
  For: Saplings planted close together in rich soil grow tall quickly, and a bare city plot becomes green and shady within a few years.  
  Against: Many ecologists in India say there is little long-term measurement, that the method costs a great deal, and that it is used in dry places where dense forest never grew. Tall trees are only one part of a forest.
- **Animals from another country can stand in for animals that have died out here.** (Supporters of India's Project Cheetah, begun in 2022; *debated*)  
  For: India's own cheetahs died out about 70 years ago. Eight African cheetahs were brought from Namibia to Kuno in Madhya Pradesh in September 2022 and twelve from South Africa in 2023. Cubs have been born there since.  
  Against: Critics such as the tiger scientist Ullas Karanth say Kuno is too small, that many of the first animals died, and that India's grasslands should be protected first. African cheetahs are also a different subspecies from the Asian one.

## Roots
We have learned how places are cleared, how they grow back, and how people help them heal. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Patchwork Test, storms cleared patches in every round, and no square stayed the same for long. Yet from round 3 the forest kept about 22 old patches. A forest does not stay the same by being left alone. It stays the same because patches fall and grow back at matching rates. So a healed place is one where falling and regrowing are in step again, even if it looks different from before.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a forest as a thing that stands still, and of damage as something done to it from outside. So we think healing means putting it back exactly as it was. But the forest was never still. What we call 'the forest' is a steady mix made by endless change.  
*Evidence:* In the Patchwork Test the old forest count stayed near 22 while every square changed. At Pichavaram the mangroves regrew once the tide flowed, without being put back tree by tree. In Yellowstone the wolves returned, and the streams still did not go back to what they had been.

- *who am i:* Your skin, your blood and the lining of your gut are replaced again and again. You stay yourself because loss and regrowth keep step, as the patches did.
- *what is life:* Life in a place is not a fixed list of beings. It is beings taking turns, each making room for others and then giving way.
- *what is this world:* The world is not a finished picture that gets damaged and repaired. It is always being cleared somewhere and always growing back somewhere.

A bare patch after a fire looks like nothing. But the soil is full of seeds, roots and spores, so the bare patch holds everything that could grow there. One lichen or one blade of grass arrives, and from that start come many kinds, each making room for the next. The young patch, the thicket and the old trees look like different things. They are one forest at different moments of the same change.

- **Thirukkural 15 (paraphrased):** “கெடுப்பதூஉம் கெட்டார்க்குச் சார்வாய்மற் றாங்கே / எடுப்பதூஉம் எல்லாம் மழை.” It is rain that ruins people, and it is rain again that lifts up those who were ruined. (Valluvar saw that the same thing can clear and restore. A flood strips a river bank, and the silt it leaves grows the next forest. At Pichavaram, standing sea water killed the mangroves and flowing sea water brought them back.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment B12 (Diels-Kranz):** “ποταμοῖσι τοῖσιν αὐτοῖσιν ἐμβαίνουσιν ἕτερα καὶ ἕτερα ὕδατα ἐπιρρεῖ” On those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow. (The river stays the same river because its water keeps changing. The forest in the Patchwork Test stayed the same forest because its patches kept changing.)
- **Dhammapada 277 (paraphrased):** “Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā, / yadā paññāya passati; / Atha nibbindati dukkhe, / esa maggo visuddhiyā.” Everything that is put together from parts keeps changing. Seeing this clearly is the path to freedom from sorrow. (The Buddha taught this about all things. Ecologists found it of forests: even the oldest one is being taken apart and put together all the time.)
- Science (settled): A landscape under steady disturbance holds a steady mix of patches of different ages, while each patch keeps changing (Bormann and Likens, 1979).
- Science (debated): An ecosystem can have more than one lasting state, so removing the cause of damage may not bring the old state back (Scheffer and others, from 1993).
- Other view, Many conservationists, and Frederic Clements before them: Not all change is equal. An old forest holds beings that no young patch can, and some losses cannot be undone in a thousand years. To say 'everything always changes' can become an excuse for damage. There are better and worse states, and people should defend the better ones. Some go further and say that a forest that cannot return to what it was has not healed at all.
- Practice, Ending, beginning, staying (3 min): Sit where you can see one plant: a tree from the window, or a pot. Look for one thing that is ending, such as a yellow leaf. Look for one thing that is beginning, such as a bud. Now look at the whole plant. Take three slow breaths. Each breath out is an ending, and each breath in is a beginning. The plant is still the plant, and you are still you.
- Ask: The forest on your grid stayed the same though every square changed. Is there anything in you that stays the same while everything in you changes?
- Ask: When a place heals, does it go back, or does it go forward to something new?
- Ask: A fallen tree is an ending and a beginning at once. Can you think of something in your own life that was both?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the Patchwork Test, every square changed, but the count of old squares stayed near 22. So what is 'the forest': the squares or the mix? (hint: Could you point to the forest on your grid? Is it in any one square?)
- *deep:* When a mangrove grows back after people dig a channel, who healed the forest: the people, the tide or the mangroves? (hint: What did each one do? Could any one of them have done it alone?)
- *deep:* A restored forest has the same trees as before, but all of them are young. Is it the same forest? (hint: Think of what an old tree has that a young tree of the same kind has not.)
- *big:* If a place has changed many times over 10,000 years, which of its past states should people restore it to? (hint: Who should choose? The people living there now, scientists, or nobody?)
- *big:* A fallen tree is a death for the tree and a beginning for a hundred seedlings. Is a disturbance bad, good or neither? (hint: Does your answer depend on who you ask: the tree, the seedlings or the hornbill?)
- *big:* People are a cause of disturbance and also the ones who restore. Are we outside the ecosystem, or one of its animals? (hint: Beavers flood valleys and elephants push down trees. Is what people do different in kind, or only in size?)

## Story: The Water That Stood Still
*A made-up story about real work. The Pichavaram mangrove forest near Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu is real. By the 1980s mangroves had died over about half of it. From 1996, the M. S. Swaminathan Research Foundation, the Tamil Nadu Forest Department and people of the nearby villages, among them Irular families, dug tidal channels in the sunken, stagnant ground and planted seedlings. Villagers helped to find the cause. Six years later most of the damaged ground had young mangroves. Irular women of Pichavaram do catch prawns by feeling for them with their hands. Thenmozhi, her mother Valli, her brother Murugan, Kamala akka and everything they say and do are imagined.*

The seedlings were dying again.

Thenmozhi could tell from the boat, before anyone stepped out. Three hundred small mangroves stood in rows across the grey flat, each one planted by hand in the cool month of Thai. In Thai they had been green. Now, in the heat of Chithirai, their leaves were the colour of old paper.

"That is the third time," said her brother Murugan, who was seven and counted everything.

Kamala akka did not answer. She was the scientist who had come from Chennai two years ago, with a notebook, a floppy hat and more patience than anyone in the village had expected. She climbed out, squatted by the nearest seedling and rubbed a leaf between her fingers. It crumbled.

Thenmozhi's amma, Valli, was already walking away along the creek with her basket tied at her waist. Seedlings did not feed a family. Prawns did. Thenmozhi followed her, because that was her work on days without school. She waded in beside her mother, bent down and felt along the soft mud with both hands, the way her amma's amma had done. The tide was going out. The water slid past her wrists, cool, pulling gently toward the sea.

Here, along the creek, mangroves grew thick. Nobody had planted these. Their roots stood out of the mud like the fingers of a hand pressed down. And between the roots, Thenmozhi saw as she bent, there were babies. Dozens of them, each with two leaves, each sitting in mud that the creek had wet that morning and would wet again by evening.

"Amma. Who planted these small ones?"

"Nobody. The seeds float. The water brings them and leaves them." Valli pulled up a prawn, dropped it in the basket and moved on.

By noon the basket was a quarter full and Thenmozhi was thirsty. She walked back across the flat toward the boat. The ground here was different. It crunched. A white crust lay over the mud and broke under her feet into sharp flakes. In the middle of the flat the ground dipped, and in the dip lay a wide sheet of water, ankle deep, flat and bright as a steel plate.

Murugan was standing at its edge. "I dare you to taste it."

"It is sea water. I know what sea water tastes like."

"Then taste it."

She dipped a finger and licked it. Her whole mouth pulled tight. She spat, and spat again. It was sea water the way pickle is a mango. Murugan laughed so hard that he sat down in the crust.

Thenmozhi did not laugh. She stood looking at the bright water. A dragonfly crossed it. Nothing else moved. No pull at her ankles. No ripple going anywhere.

"When did this water come?" she asked.

"It is always here," said Murugan. "Since the big tide at the new moon. It just gets smaller."

Smaller. She looked at the white rim around the pool, a hand wide, where the water had been last week. The water was leaving, then. Only not the way it left the creek. It was leaving upward, into the sun. And the salt was staying behind.

She thought of her amma's rasam on the stove, forgotten once, boiled down to a dark spoonful that nobody could eat.

She ran.

Kamala akka was still by the dead rows, writing. Thenmozhi arrived without breath and pointed back at the pool, then at the creek, and then found some words. "In the creek the water comes and goes. Two times every day. The babies there live. Here it comes one time in a month and then it stands. It stands and the sun cooks it. Akka, taste it."

Kamala akka walked to the pool and tasted it. She was polite and did not spit, but her eyes watered.

"We have been bringing plants," she said slowly.

"Don't bring plants," said Thenmozhi. "Bring the creek."

It was Valli who told the others, that evening, in fewer words than her daughter had used. The old men said the ground had sunk since the big trees were cut, in their fathers' time, when the cutting was done one whole block at a time. Sunk ground holds water. Nobody had thought of that as a reason before, because nobody had asked them.

They dug in the month of Aadi, forty people with spades, the women carrying the mud away in headloads. One long channel ran from the creek to the middle of the flat, and short ones branched off it to both sides. From the bund it looked like the bones of a fish. Murugan counted the side channels and got nineteen.

Then the tide came. Thenmozhi stood in the main channel and felt it arrive, a cool push against her shins, brown and quick, carrying leaves and bits of stick. It filled the dip. Six hours later she came back and stood there again, and felt it pulling the other way, taking the old bright water with it out to the sea.

They planted that year too, along the channels. Kamala akka said they should do both. But in the next Thai, when Thenmozhi walked the flat with a stick to count for Murugan, she found more seedlings than anyone had put there. They stood in no rows at all. They grew wherever the tide had set them down.

She counted the ones in rows and the ones not in rows, and the ones not in rows won.

"Who planted those?" asked Murugan.

"Nobody," said Thenmozhi. "We only opened the door."

**For the parent:** For the parent: the story turns when Thenmozhi stops asking why the seedlings die and asks what the water is doing. The mangroves were never short of seeds. The standing water had become too salty for them. Once the tide could flow in and out, the forest began to plant itself. Restoration is often the removal of whatever blocks a place from recovering.

- Ask: Why did the mangrove seedlings die on the flat, when wild ones lived beside the creek?
- Ask: The standing water was much saltier than the sea. Where did the extra salt come from?
- Ask: What is the difference between bringing plants to a place and bringing back what the plants need?
- Ask: The old men knew the ground had sunk. Why had nobody used what they knew before?
- Ask: Think of a damaged place you know. What might be blocking that place from growing back?

### e1 · The Patchwork Test: after 60 storms, how much old forest is left? (45 min)
- **question:** Storms clear 6 of the 36 patches every ten years. After 100 years and 60 storms, how many patches are old forest? Does that number keep falling?
- **predict:** Guess first. After 10 rounds and 60 storms, how many of the 36 patches will be old forest: none, about 10, about 20, or all 36? Will the number go on falling round after round? Say why you think so, and write it down.

- **record:** Fill in the table for every round of both parts. Draw the old forest counts of both parts on one line graph, in two colours. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.

  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a square 18 centimetres wide. 2. Divide it into 6 columns and 6 rows, each 3 centimetres wide. 3. Number the columns 1 to 6 along the top and the rows 1 to 6 down the side. 4. In the notebook draw a table with the five columns listed above.

- **s2 (3 min)**

- **s6 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a fresh grid of 36 empty squares. 2. Ask for a new guess and a reason. 3. Play 5 rounds with 12 storms in each round. 4. Count after each round and fill in a second table.  
  *Parent says:* Now there are 12 storms a round. People have added their fires and cutting to the storms. Will the old forest go to zero this time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a new grid. Now roll 12 storms each round. Guess first, and say why. Play 5 rounds.
  - Note: With 12 storms the old forest settles at 10 to 16 patches, usually about 13, and bare ground at about 10. It still levels off, at a lower number.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two tables side by side. 2. Add a third forest in your head: no storms at all, so 36 old patches. 3. Use this made-up rule: grasshoppers need at least 3 grass patches, and a hornbill family needs at least 15 old patches. 4. Ask who can live in each forest.  
  *Parent says:* Here are three forests. Who can live in the forest with no storms? With 6? With 12? Which forest has room for both? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your two tables. Grasshoppers need 3 grass patches. Hornbills need 15 old patches. Who can live in each forest?
  - Note: The rule for grasshoppers and hornbills is made up for this test. Real animals do need a least amount of the right kind of patch, but the numbers differ for each.
  - Check: A forest is hit by storms at the same rate for hundreds of years. What happens to its mix of young and old patches? → **It stays about the same**
### e2 · The Soil Remembers: where do the first plants come from? (50 min, 21 days)
- **question:** If you plant nothing, will plants come up in a pot of soil? And will they still come up if the soil has been roasted first?
- **predict:** Guess first. On day 21, how many plants will each pot have? If any plants grow in the roasted pot, where could their seeds have come from? Say why you think so, and write it down.
- **fair test:** Mix all the soil well before you split it, so that both pots start with the same soil. Use pots of the same size. Give both the same water and the same windowsill. The only difference is the roasting.
- **safety:** A grown-up roasts the soil and pokes the holes in the cups. Hot soil smells strongly, so open a window. Let it cool fully before a child touches it. Wash your hands after touching soil.
- **record:** Fill in the table on days 4, 7, 14 and 21. On day 21 draw both pots from above. Then write what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Day | Plants in the plain pot | Plants in the roasted pot | Anything else I see (moss, green film, fungus, ants)
- **schedule:** day 1: Collect and mix the soil. Roast half of it. Fill the two pots, water them and write your guess. (25 min); day 4: Look closely at both pots. Count any seedlings. (3 min); day 7: Count the seedlings in each pot. Note anything else you see. (5 min); day 14: Count again. Are there different kinds of plant? (5 min); day 21: Count for the last time, compare the pots and explain. (12 min)
- **s1 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Collect about 4 tumblers of soil from a place where weeds grow. 2. Tip it on a newspaper and pick out stones and plants. 3. Mix it well with a spoon. 4. Divide it into two equal heaps.  
  *Parent says:* We will plant nothing in this soil. Do you think anything will grow? Where would it come from? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Mix the soil well. Make two equal heaps. Will anything grow if you plant nothing? Say why.
  - Note: Shop-bought potting mix often has few seeds. Soil from under weeds, from an old pot or from a roadside tree base works best. Take the top three or four centimetres, where most seeds lie.
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open a window. 2. Put one heap of soil in an old kadai on a medium flame. 3. Stir for 15 minutes, until the soil is dry and too hot to touch all through. 4. Let it cool for 30 minutes. 5. Fill one cup with plain soil and one with roasted soil. 6. Label them and give each 3 tablespoons of water.  
  *Parent says:* Heat kills seeds, as cooking kills a grain of rice. Now one pot has living seeds and one does not. What do you guess for day 21? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up roasts one heap. Fill two pots and label them. Water both. Write your guess and your reason.
  - Ask: If plants grow in the roasted pot, where could they have come from? (good: Seeds blown in on the wind.; A seed that the heat missed.; Seeds carried by ants or dropped by a bird.)
  - Note: Roasting also kills most fungi and bacteria in the soil and changes its smell. Say so if your child asks. The difference you will see is mostly about seeds.
- **s3 (13 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep both pots on the same bright sill. 2. Give each 2 tablespoons of water a day, so the soil stays damp. 3. On days 4, 7, 14 and 21, count the plants in each pot and fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Count carefully. What do you notice about the two pots today? Is anything growing that is not a plant with leaves?  
  *Child mode:* Water both pots the same. Count the plants on days 4, 7, 14 and 21. Fill in the table.
  - Note: The plain pot usually shows its first seedlings between day 4 and day 10. If neither pot has any by day 14, the soil had few seeds. That is a result too. Start again with soil from a weedier place.
  - Wait: Come back on days 4, 7, 14 and 21
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count both pots for the last time. 2. Draw each pot from above. 3. Ask for the claim, the evidence and the reason. 4. Only then give the explanation below.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think is true about where new plants come from? Which numbers show it? Why did the two pots differ? Then I will tell you the scientists' words.  
  *Child mode:* Count both pots. Where do new plants come from? Which numbers show it? Say why the pots differ.
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain: the plain pot started with a seed bank, like land after a fire or a storm. That is secondary succession. The roasted pot is like new lava. Every plant there had to arrive from outside, which is primary succession.
  - Check: A forest burns, but the soil is not badly heated. Where will most of the first new plants come from? → **From seeds and roots already in the soil**

## Worksheets
### How places change and recover (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a disturbance?  
   Options: A noisy animal / An event that removes many living beings quickly / Any change in the weather / A new kind of plant  
   **Answer:** An event that removes many living beings quickly. *Why:* A disturbance clears living beings from a place quickly: a storm, a fire, a flood, a bulldozer. Choosing 'a noisy animal' uses the everyday meaning of the word.
2. (fill) The first living beings to settle on bare ground are called ____ species.  
   **Answer:** ['pioneer']. *Why:* Pioneers arrive easily and can live in the open. Lichens, mosses and grasses are common pioneers.
3. (order) Put these in the order they usually appear on an abandoned field.  
   **Answer:** ['Grasses and small herbs', 'Shrubs', 'Sun-loving young trees', 'Tall shade-bearing trees']. *Why:* Each group changes the place for the next. Grasses hold the soil. Shrubs give shade and perches. Sun-loving trees make the shade that slower trees' seedlings can bear.
4. (mcq) Why do pioneer plants not keep a patch for ever?  
   Options: They are weak plants / Animals eat them all / Their seedlings grow badly in the shade they make / They get tired  
   **Answer:** Their seedlings grow badly in the shade they make. *Why:* Pioneers change the place. Their own seedlings need full sun and do badly in the shade the pioneers made. Choosing 'they are weak' is not right: they are the only ones able to start on bare ground.
5. (truefalse) True or false: after a forest fire, new plants must wait for seeds to blow in from far away.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The soil usually still holds seeds and living roots, so plants return within weeks. This is secondary succession.
6. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Primary succession → starting on ground with no soil and no life; Secondary succession → starting where the soil is still there; Seed bank → living seeds waiting in the soil; Patchwork → many patches of different ages side by side; Restoration → people helping a damaged place recover. *Why:* These are the main words of the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
7. (write) In The Soil Remembers, plants came up in a pot where nothing was planted. Where did they come from?  
   **Answer:** From seeds that were already in the soil, called the seed bank. In the roasted pot the heat had killed them, so few or no plants grew.. *Why:* A strong answer names the seed bank and uses the roasted pot as the evidence.
8. (sort) Sort each start into primary or secondary succession.  
   **Answer:** new lava on a volcano → Primary succession; a paddy field no longer farmed → Secondary succession; a burnt grass slope → Secondary succession; a new island of ash in the sea → Primary succession; a forest gap after a cyclone → Secondary succession; bare rock left by a melting glacier → Primary succession. *Why:* Primary starts with no soil. Secondary starts with the soil, its seeds and its roots still there.
9. (mcq) In the Patchwork Test, why did the old forest count stop falling?  
   Options: Losses and gains became equal / The storms stopped / The dice ran out of squares / Old patches cannot be hit  
   **Answer:** Losses and gains became equal. *Why:* About as many patches grew old each round as were cleared, so the count stayed level. Choosing 'the storms stopped' is not right: 6 storms came in every round.
10. (draw) Draw a forest from above as a patchwork. Show patches of four ages and label each one.  
   **Answer:** A map-like drawing with at least four patches labelled, for example, bare, grass, young trees and old forest.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the ages side by side in one landscape. A drawing of one even forest has missed the idea of a patchwork.
11. (truefalse) True or false: wolves were brought back to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 and 1996.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. 14 wolves came in 1995 and 17 in 1996, all caught in Canada. The last Yellowstone wolves had been killed in 1926.
12. (mcq) At Pichavaram, what was killing the mangrove seedlings on the sunken ground?  
   Options: Too few seeds / Too little sunlight / Crabs eating them / Standing water that had become too salty  
   **Answer:** Standing water that had become too salty. *Why:* Sea water stood still there and dried in the sun, so the salt built up. Channels let the tide flow in and out again. Choosing 'too few seeds' is the mistake the planters first made.
13. (fill) A marsh soaks up flood water and lets it out ____.  
   **Answer:** ['slowly']. *Why:* A wetland holds a rush of water and releases it over hours or days, as the towel did in Wetland on a Board.
14. (write) Why is planting trees on a natural grassland not restoration?  
   **Answer:** Because the grassland is the ecosystem that belongs there. The grasses, blackbuck, larks and bustards need open country. Trees replace their home with a plantation.. *Why:* A strong answer says that restoration aims at what the place naturally is, and that a grassland is not damaged forest.
15. (mcq) What should a restorer ask first about a damaged place?  
   Options: How many trees can I plant? / What is stopping this place from recovering? / Which plants look prettiest? / How can I make it tidy?  
   **Answer:** What is stopping this place from recovering?. *Why:* Most places regrow by themselves once the block is gone. Choosing 'how many trees can I plant' assumes the problem is always a lack of trees.
### Patches, wolves and wetlands (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (count) A Patchwork Test round has 6 bare, 5 grass and 4 young patches. How many of the 36 are old?  
   **Answer:** 21. *Why:* Add the three: 6 + 5 + 4 = 15. Then 36 − 15 = 21 old patches.
2. (mcq) With 12 storms a round, not 6, what happened to the old forest count?  
   Options: It stayed at about 22 / It levelled off at a lower number / It fell to zero / It rose  
   **Answer:** It levelled off at a lower number. *Why:* It settled at about 13, down from about 22. It still levelled off. Choosing 'zero' forgets that some patches always escape for three rounds.
3. (write) After 60 storms on 36 patches, more than half the forest was still old. How can that be?  
   **Answer:** Storms fall on patches at random, so many hit bare or young patches that had been hit before. Meanwhile every patch that escapes for three rounds becomes old again.. *Why:* A strong answer has both parts: storms overlap, and patches regrow. An answer that says 'the storms missed' has only the first part.
4. (fill) Lupines can grow on bare volcanic ash because bacteria in their roots take ____ from the air.  
   **Answer:** ['nitrogen']. *Why:* Plants need nitrogen to build their bodies. New ash has almost none that a plant can use, so plants with these bacteria get a head start.
5. (sort) Sort each being into the stage where you would expect to find them.  
   **Answer:** lichen on rock → Pioneer stage; great hornbill → Old forest; grass with wind-blown seeds → Pioneer stage; orchid on a mossy branch → Old forest; Macaranga tree in a sunny gap → Pioneer stage; lion-tailed macaque → Old forest. *Why:* Pioneers travel light and need sun. Old forest beings need what only time gives: huge trees, a closed canopy and damp shade.
6. (mcq) On the new island of Surtsey, where did plants grow thickest?  
   Options: On the highest point / On the newest lava / Where seabirds nested / Where it rained least  
   **Answer:** Where seabirds nested. *Why:* Seabird droppings carry nitrogen and other plant food from the sea to the land. Choosing 'the highest point' forgets that new rock has almost no plant food.
7. (write) Elk fell after wolves returned to Yellowstone. Give two other causes that could have lowered the elk.  
   **Answer:** Any two of: hunting by people outside the park, more grizzly bears, more cougars, and dry years.. *Why:* A strong answer shows that several things changed in the same years, so the fall cannot be put down to wolves alone.
8. (mcq) Two teams measured the same Yellowstone willows and disagreed. What did they do differently?  
   Options: They measured different things / One team made up numbers / They studied different parks / One team counted wolves  
   **Answer:** They measured different things. *Why:* One team measured height and the other the volume of the leafy crown. What you choose to measure can change the answer. Choosing 'one team made up numbers' is not what happened.
9. (draw) Draw a stream with a beaver dam, and without one. Show the water level under the ground in both.  
   **Answer:** Two cut-away drawings. With the dam: a pond, water just under the bank, tall willows. Without: a deep narrow channel, water far below the bank, short willows.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the water under the ground at two different levels. That hidden water is why willows did not recover with wolves alone.
10. (odd) Three of these remove a block to recovery. Which one does not?  
   Options: Fencing cattle out of a regrowing field / Digging channels to let the tide in / Taking down an unused dam / Planting one kind of tree in rows  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Fencing, channels and dam removal each take away something that stops regrowth. Planting one kind of tree in rows adds a plantation and removes no block.
11. (count) At Pichavaram, 375 hectares were damaged. Six years into the work, 65 were. How many hectares had recovered?  
   **Answer:** 310. *Why:* 375 − 65 = 310 hectares had young or grown mangroves again.
12. (truefalse) True or false: a forest restored 15 years ago is as rich as an old forest beside it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the Anamalai hills, restored fragments were ahead of unrestored ones but still behind the old forest. Big trees, nest holes and deep soil take far longer.
13. (mcq) In Wetland on a Board, what did the third tumbler of water show?  
   Options: Wetlands stop all floods / Towels do not hold water / A full wetland cannot hold more / Water runs uphill  
   **Answer:** A full wetland cannot hold more. *Why:* A towel that is already full lets new water run through. A marsh works the same way. Choosing 'wetlands stop all floods' claims too much.
14. (perspective) Three people look at a bare, grazed hillside next to a forest.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and uses the idea of a block to recovery. Seeds can arrive from the forest, so the block is grazing, and Lakshmi's fence may be enough. It can grant that planting helps where seeds cannot arrive.. *Why:* All three want the hill green. They differ on what is stopping it now.
15. (write) Why did the forest in the Patchwork Test need storms for grasshoppers to live there?  
   **Answer:** Grasshoppers need grass patches. With no storms every patch became old forest, and there was no grass. Storms kept making new young patches.. *Why:* A strong answer links the animal to a stage, and the stage to disturbance.
### Steady mixes and stuck states (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Every square in the Patchwork Test changed, yet the old count stayed near 22. How can both be true?  
   **Answer:** Each round some old squares were cleared and some young squares grew old. The squares swapped roles, but the numbers lost and gained were about equal, so the total stayed level.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the single patch, which keeps changing, from the whole mix, which stays steady.
2. (count) Six storms hit 6 different patches out of 36. What is the chance, in sixths, that one named patch escapes?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* 30 of the 36 patches escape. 30 out of 36 is the same as 5 out of 6. So the chance is 5 sixths.
3. (mcq) A patch needs to escape storms three rounds running to become old. What is that chance, roughly?  
   Options: About 5 in 6 / A little over 1 in 2 / About 1 in 6 / About 1 in 36  
   **Answer:** A little over 1 in 2. *Why:* 5/6 × 5/6 × 5/6 = 125/216, which is a little over one half. Choosing '5 in 6' forgets that the patch must escape three times, not once.
4. (truefalse) True or false: most ecologists today think every place moves to one fixed final community and then stops changing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. That was the climax idea of 1916. Old communities change slowly, but they keep changing as trees die, the climate shifts and species arrive.
5. (write) After the ice age, each tree species spread north at a different speed. What does this say about forests?  
   **Answer:** That a forest is not one unit that moves together. Each species goes their own way, so the mix of trees in a place keeps being put together afresh.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that this supports Gleason's view: a community is whoever lives together at the time.
6. (draw) Draw the Yellowstone chain: wolf, elk, willow, beaver, stream. Make agreed links solid arrows and argued links dotted.  
   **Answer:** Wolf to elk is solid (wolves kill elk). Elk to willow is solid (elk eat willow). Willow to beaver is solid (beavers need willow). 'Wolves made willows recover everywhere' and 'wolves changed the rivers' are dotted or crossed out.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the single links are well known. What is argued is how strongly the whole chain ran after 1995.
7. (mcq) Why did willows on many Yellowstone streams stay short even with fewer elk?  
   Options: Wolves eat willows / The winters were too warm / The water under the ground had sunk too low / Willows had died out  
   **Answer:** The water under the ground had sunk too low. *Why:* With beavers gone, streams had cut deep and the water under the ground had sunk below the willow roots. Choosing 'wolves eat willows' mixes up the links of the chain.
8. (order) Put these steps in the order they happened on Yellowstone streams after wolves were removed.  
   **Answer:** ['Elk became many', 'Elk ate the willows down', 'Beavers left', 'Streams cut deeper and ground water sank']. *Why:* Each step caused the next. The last step then kept willows short by itself, which is why bringing wolves back did not simply undo it.
9. (write) A lake stays green after its sewage is cut back. Why might the water not turn clear again?  
   **Answer:** The green algae shade out the bottom plants. Without those plants the mud is stirred up and the tiny animals who eat algae lose their shelter. So the green state keeps itself going.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the lake has switched to a new state that holds itself in place. Removing the first cause is then not enough.
10. (perspective) Two people talk about a restored forest of young trees.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer can take either side with reasons. The same kinds of tree are back, and animals are returning. But huge trees, nest holes, deep soil and the beings who need them are still missing, so it is not yet the same.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. It asks what makes a forest the forest it is.
11. (count) Pallikaranai marsh shrank from about 5,500 hectares to about 600. Roughly how many parts out of 9 are left?  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* 5,500 ÷ 600 is a little over 9. So about 1 part in 9 is left, and about 8 parts in 9 are gone.
12. (mcq) Salmon passed the old Elwha dam sites within two years. Why do scientists still say recovery will take decades?  
   Options: River, banks and fish numbers rebuild slowly / The dams were rebuilt / Salmon live for decades / The river dried up  
   **Answer:** River, banks and fish numbers rebuild slowly. *Why:* The river bed, the banks and the fish numbers all need many salmon generations to rebuild. Choosing 'the dams were rebuilt' is not right.
13. (odd) Three of these are natural ecosystems of India that should not be planted with trees. Which one is not?  
   Options: Deccan dry grassland / The salt desert of Kutch / A cleared rainforest fragment / Nilgiri mountain grassland  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A cleared rainforest fragment was forest, so local trees belong there. Dry grassland, salt desert and high mountain grassland are natural open ecosystems.
14. (fill) A healthy place of the same kind, studied to guide a restoration, is called a ____ site.  
   **Answer:** ['reference']. *Why:* A reference site shows which living beings belong and what the damaged place could become.
15. (write) People, beavers and elephants all cause disturbance. Name one way human disturbance is different, and one way the same.  
   **Answer:** Same: all three clear patches and change who can live there. Different: people clear far larger areas, far more often, and often leave no soil or seeds behind, as under concrete.. *Why:* A strong answer compares size, how often, and what is left behind. Those are the three ways disturbances differ.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a disturbance?  
   Options: A tree growing a new leaf / A bird building a nest / A cyclone felling trees / Rain on a pond  
   **Answer:** A cyclone felling trees. *Why:* A cyclone removes many living beings in a short time. The others are slow, everyday events.
2. (mcq) Why do lichens and mosses come before trees on bare rock?  
   Options: They are older kinds of life / They can live without soil / Trees dislike rock / Birds plant them first  
   **Answer:** They can live without soil. *Why:* They need no soil, and they help to make the first soil. Choosing 'they are older kinds of life' explains nothing about this rock.
3. (mcq) A landslide and a lava flow each clear a slope. Which regrows faster, and why?  
   Options: The landslide: some soil and seeds remain / The lava: it is warmer / Both the same / Neither will regrow  
   **Answer:** The landslide: some soil and seeds remain. *Why:* A landslide scar keeps some soil and lies next to living forest. Lava leaves no soil and no seeds, so succession there is primary and slow.
4. (mcq) You plant nothing in a pot of soil from under weeds, and water it. What is most likely to happen?  
   Options: Nothing grows / Only moss grows / Seedlings come up from seeds in the soil / The soil turns to rock  
   **Answer:** Seedlings come up from seeds in the soil. *Why:* The soil holds a seed bank, so seedlings appear within a week or two. Choosing 'nothing' forgets the seeds you cannot see.
5. (mcq) Storms hit a large forest at a steady rate for centuries. What is the forest like?  
   Options: All bare / All young / All old / A steady mix of patches of all ages  
   **Answer:** A steady mix of patches of all ages. *Why:* Patches are cleared and regrow all the time, so the forest holds every age at once, in a steady mix.
6. (truefalse) True or false: in a steady forest, each single patch stays the same.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each patch keeps changing. Only the mix of young and old across the whole landscape stays about the same.
7. (mcq) Which statement about the wolves of Yellowstone is well shown by counting?  
   Options: Wolves changed the course of rivers / Elk numbers fell after wolves returned / Wolves alone made the elk fall / All willows have recovered  
   **Answer:** Elk numbers fell after wolves returned. *Why:* Wolves and elk are counted every year, and elk fell by more than half. The other three go beyond the evidence.
8. (mcq) A missing predator is brought back, but the streams do not recover. What is the most likely reason?  
   Options: Predators do not matter / Not enough time, and nothing else / Later changes now keep the damage going / The plants forgot how to grow  
   **Answer:** Later changes now keep the damage going. *Why:* After the predator was lost, later changes took hold, such as deep-cut streams and sunken ground water. Those now hold the place in its new state.
9. (mcq) Mangroves are dying where sea water stands still on sunken ground. What is the best plan?  
   Options: Dig channels so the tide flows in and out / Plant more seedlings each year / Add fresh soil on top / Build a wall against the sea  
   **Answer:** Dig channels so the tide flows in and out. *Why:* The block is standing salty water. Channels bring back the tide's flow. This is what worked at Pichavaram.
10. (mcq) A city fills in its marshes and builds on them. What is the likely result in heavy rain?  
   Options: Less flooding / Faster, worse flooding / No change / Less rain  
   **Answer:** Faster, worse flooding. *Why:* The marsh no longer soaks up the rush of water, so it reaches the streets quickly and in one burst.
11. (mcq) A dry grassland with blackbuck is marked 'wasteland'. What would restore it?  
   Options: Planting trees in rows / Flooding it to make a lake / Covering it with lawn grass / Protecting it as grassland  
   **Answer:** Protecting it as grassland. *Why:* An open grassland is a natural ecosystem. It needs protection from ploughing, building and tree planting, not a forest on top of it.

## Applications
- Foresters and restorers fence damaged land from cattle and let trees return by themselves, which costs far less than planting.
- Mangrove restorers in Tamil Nadu dig channels to bring back the tide before they plant anything.
- City planners keep marshes and lakes as places for flood water to spread and soak in.
- Rainforest restorers in the Anamalai hills raise seedlings of many local trees from wild seed, to plant where seeds can no longer arrive.
- Engineers take down old dams that are no longer needed, so that fish can reach their breeding places again.
- Wildlife managers plan parks as patchworks of grass, scrub, forest and water, because different animals need different patches.
- Farmers in the hills of the Northeast rest a jhum field for years, so that bamboo and trees rebuild the soil before the next crop.

## Spotted around us
- A peepal or banyan seedling growing out of a wall or a roof gutter. A bird left the seed there.
- An empty plot in a city. In its first monsoon it is all grass and weeds. A few years on, it is a thicket.
- A dried tank bed in summer, in Tamil Nadu or Telangana. Within weeks of drying it is covered in short grass, and cattle graze there.
- A landslide scar beside a hill road in Kerala or Uttarakhand, a few years old, now green with grass, ferns and young trees.
- The hills above Munnar, Ooty or Kodaikanal, where dark shola forest sits in the folds and grassland covers the slopes.
- A gap where a big tree fell in a park or on a campus after a cyclone. Look at what has grown in the light since.
- Mangroves along a creek in Mumbai, Kochi or Pichavaram, with their breathing roots standing out of the mud at low tide.
- A damp wall under a leaking pipe: a green film first, then moss, then a small fern. This is primary succession on your own building.

## Open questions
- **How much of the change in Yellowstone was caused by the wolves?** Two teams of scientists measure the same valleys and reach different answers. There is only one Yellowstone, so no fair test with and without wolves can be run.
- **Can we tell in advance that an ecosystem is close to a tipping point?** Some lakes have shown warning signs before switching, such as slower recovery from small upsets. Nobody yet knows whether such signs can be trusted in forests, reefs or grasslands.
- **How long does a restored rainforest take to become like an old one, and does it ever?** The oldest careful restoration projects are only a few decades old. A rainforest tree can live for centuries, so nobody has watched for long enough.
- **When the climate is changing, should a place be restored to what it was, or to what will suit the future?** Some trees that grew in a place a hundred years ago may not thrive there in a warmer century. Restorers argue about whether to copy the past or plan for the new climate.
- **Does a middle amount of disturbance really keep the most kinds of living beings?** Some studies find it and many do not. Scientists disagree about whether the idea needs repair or should be dropped.

## Connected maths
- In the Patchwork Test, 6 storms hit 6 different patches out of 36. What is the chance that one patch escapes for three rounds running? About how many of the 36 patches is that? **125 out of 216, which is a little over half. That is about 21 patches.**. In one round, 30 of 36 patches escape, which is 5 out of 6. For three rounds, multiply: 5/6 × 5/6 × 5/6 = 125/216. That is about 0.58. And 0.58 × 36 is about 21. So about 21 patches should be old at any time. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- With 12 storms a round, the same sum gives 2/3 × 2/3 × 2/3 of 36, which is about 11 old patches. Real games give about 13. Why is the real number higher? **Because 12 storms do not hit 12 different patches. Some land on a patch already hit in that round, so fewer patches are cleared.**. The sum assumes that 12 different patches are cleared each round, leaving 24 of 36, which is 2 out of 3. But dice repeat. With 12 rolls on 36 squares, about 10 different squares are hit on average. So more patches escape than the sum says, and more grow old. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L5`)
- Look at the graph of old forest patches over 10 rounds. In which round did the count stop falling? How far does it wander after that? **It stopped falling in round 3. After that it stays between 21 and 22.**. The count goes 36, 30, 26, 22, and then 21, 22, 21, 22, 22, 21, 22. The fall ends at round 3, because a patch cleared in round 1 first becomes old again in round 4. From then on, gains match losses. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
- The winter count of elk on Yellowstone's northern range was 16,791 in 1995 and 8,335 in 2004. Did the elk fall by more than half, or by less? **By a little more than half.**. Half of 16,791 is about 8,396. The 2004 count, 8,335, is a little below that. So the elk fell by slightly more than half in nine years. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L5`)
- At Pichavaram, 375 hectares of mangrove were damaged. Six years into the work, 65 hectares still were. About what fraction of the damaged area had recovered? **310 out of 375, which is about 5 parts in every 6.**. 375 − 65 = 310 hectares recovered. 375 ÷ 6 is about 62, and 5 × 62 = 310. So about 5 sixths recovered, which is about 83 in every 100. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- Pallikaranai marsh covered about 5,500 hectares in 1965 and about 600 hectares in 2013. About how many hectares were lost each year, on average? **About 100 hectares a year.**. 5,500 − 600 = 4,900 hectares lost. From 1965 to 2013 is 48 years. 4,900 ÷ 48 is about 102. So about 100 hectares went each year, which is 1 square kilometre a year. (queues `math.big-ideas.change-and-motion.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What do we call an event that clears many living beings from a place quickly? → A disturbance.
- What are the first living beings to settle on bare ground called? → Pioneer species.
- Which is faster: regrowth on burnt soil, or on new lava? → On burnt soil, because seeds and roots are still in it.
- What do we call the living seeds waiting in the soil? → The seed bank.
- In what years were wolves brought back to Yellowstone? → 1995 and 1996.
- What did people dig at Pichavaram to help the mangroves? → Channels, so that the tide could flow in and out.
- What does a marsh do with a rush of flood water? → It soaks the water up and lets it out slowly.
- Is every natural place in India meant to be forest? → No. Grasslands, deserts and marshes are natural ecosystems too.
