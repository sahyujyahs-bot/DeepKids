# Colour and rainbows: how sunlight splits into colours
`sci.light.colour.L3`

**Summary.** Sunlight looks white, but it is really many colours mixed together. A glass of water or a prism spreads them into red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet, because each colour bends by a different amount. Raindrops do the same, and that is how a rainbow forms. Things look coloured because they absorb some colours of light and reflect the rest. Mixing coloured lights can make white, but mixing paints makes a dark, muddy colour. Nobody yet knows whether the red you see looks the same as the red someone else sees.

**Intro.** *Fill a clear glass to the top with water. Stand it at the edge of a windowsill where the morning or evening Sun shines in. Now look at the floor below it. What do you notice? What do you wonder?* Sunlight looks plain and white. It lights up the kitchen and nobody thinks about it. But sometimes, after the monsoon rain, a giant curve of colours hangs in the sky. Where were those colours before the rain came? Were they in the clouds? In the water? Or were they hiding inside the sunlight all along? In 1666, about 360 years ago, a young man in England called Isaac Newton darkened his room to find out. He made a small hole in the window shutter and put a glass prism in the beam of sunlight.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that sunlight is many colours mixed together, and that things look coloured because they absorb some colours and reflect the rest.
1. I can use a prism or a glass of water to split light into colours, and name the colours in order.
2. I can explain that a rainbow forms when sunlight goes into raindrops, bends, bounces and comes out as colours.
3. I can say where to stand to see a rainbow: with the Sun behind me and rain in front of me.
4. I can mix the colours back into white with a spinning colour wheel, and say why mixing paints gives a dark colour instead.
5. I can run a fair test, write what I saw, and finish with claim, evidence and reasoning.
6. I can say what scientists agree about colour, and what nobody knows yet, like whether your red is the same as mine.
7. I can talk about light past the ends of the rainbow, what starlight tells us, and animals that see colours we cannot.

## Terms
- **Light**: Light is what lets us see. It comes from things like the Sun, a bulb or a flame. *e.g.* When you switch off the light at night, the room goes dark.
- **White light**: White light is light that has no single colour of its own. Sunlight is white light. It is really many colours mixed together. *e.g.* The light from the noon Sun on a white wall looks white.
- **Colour**: Colour is how light looks to our eyes. Red, green and blue are colours. *e.g.* A ripe mango looks yellow. A leaf looks green.
- **Prism**: A prism is a clear block, often glass, with flat sides at an angle. It splits white light into colours. *e.g.* A triangle-shaped glass prism on a sunny table throws a small rainbow.
- **Spectrum**: A spectrum is the band of colours you get when white light is split. The colours always come in the same order. *e.g.* A glass of water in sunlight can throw red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet in a line on the floor.
- **Rainbow**: A rainbow is a curve of colours in the sky. It forms when sunlight shines into raindrops. *e.g.* After a monsoon shower in the late afternoon, a rainbow appears in the east.
- **Bend (refraction)**: When light goes from air into water or glass, it changes direction a little. We say it bends. Scientists call this bending refraction. *e.g.* Light bends as it goes into a raindrop.
- **Reflect**: To reflect is to bounce back. Light reflects off a mirror and off the inside of a raindrop. We see most things by the light they reflect. *e.g.* A mirror reflects your face back to you.
- **Violet**: Violet is the purple-blue colour at one end of the rainbow. It bends the most in a prism. *e.g.* Violet is the colour of some brinjal flowers.
- **Indigo**: Indigo is a deep blue. Isaac Newton named it as a rainbow colour, between blue and violet. The word means 'from India', because the blue dye came from an Indian plant. *e.g.* Blue jeans and some block-printed cloth are dyed indigo.
- **Filter**: A colour filter is a clear coloured sheet. It lets some colours of light through and absorbs most of the others. *e.g.* Red cellophane from gift wrap, or a see-through red sweet wrapper, is a red filter.
- **Wavelength**: Light travels in tiny waves. The wavelength is the length of one wave. Red light has longer waves than violet light. *e.g.* Red waves are almost twice as long as violet waves.
- **Infrared**: Infrared is light just past the red end of the rainbow. Our eyes cannot see it, but we can feel it as warmth. *e.g.* A TV remote sends infrared light. A phone camera can sometimes see it.
- **Ultraviolet**: Ultraviolet is light just past the violet end. Our eyes cannot see it, but bees can. *e.g.* Ultraviolet from the Sun can burn your skin on a beach.
- **Mixing light**: Mixing colours of light means sending them into your eye together. All the rainbow colours together look white. Red, green and blue light together look white too. *e.g.* A spinning rainbow wheel looks pale grey, nearly white.
- **Fair test**: A fair test changes only one thing at a time. Then we know what made the difference. *e.g.* We use the same glass and the same water. We only move where the glass stands.
- **Claim, evidence and reasoning**: Three parts of a scientist's answer. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened. *e.g.* I think sunlight has colours in it. I saw six colours on the floor. The water spread them apart.
- **Photon**: A photon is a tiny packet of light, far too small to see. Light comes in these packets. *e.g.* A small torch sends out more than a million billion photons every second.
- **Absorb**: To absorb light is to take it in, so that it does not bounce back. The light's energy usually turns into warmth. *e.g.* A black dosa tawa absorbs almost all the light that falls on it, so it looks black.
- **Pigment**: A pigment is a substance that gives paint, dye, skin or a leaf its colour. It absorbs some colours of light and reflects the rest. *e.g.* Turmeric has a yellow pigment. Leaves have a green pigment called chlorophyll.
- **Mixing paint**: Mixing paints puts their pigments together. Each pigment absorbs some colours, so together they absorb more. Many paints mixed make a dark, muddy brown. *e.g.* Yellow and blue paint make green, because green is the main colour that both of them reflect.

## Explanations
### Sunlight is many colours mixed together · *defined*
**Definition:** White light, like sunlight, is a mixture of all the colours of the rainbow. A prism or a raindrop can spread them apart.

Sunlight looks white, so we think it is one plain colour. It is not. It is a mixture of red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet light, all arriving together. When they arrive together, our eyes see white. When something spreads them apart, we see each colour on its own. Nothing paints the colours on. They were in the sunlight all along.
- e.g. A glass of water on a sunny windowsill throws a strip of colours on the floor.
- e.g. A drop of water on a leaf in the morning can flash red, then green, as you move your head.
- e.g. A cut-glass bottle stopper or a chandelier bead throws tiny rainbows round a room.
### What a prism does · *defined*
**Definition:** A prism splits white light into colours because each colour bends by a different amount as it goes in and out.

A prism is a clear block with flat sides at an angle. When light goes from air into glass, it bends a little. It bends again when it comes out. This bending is called refraction. Each colour bends by a different amount. Violet bends the most and red bends the least. So colours that went in together come out in slightly different directions. A little way off, they have spread into a band. The unit on bending light explains why light bends at all.
- e.g. Red always lands at one edge of the band, and violet at the other.
- e.g. A glass of water works like a round prism.
- e.g. A thick glass paperweight or a diamond can split light too.
### How a rainbow is made · *defined*
**Definition:** A rainbow forms when sunlight goes into raindrops, bends, bounces off the back of each drop, and comes out split into colours.

A rainbow is made by millions of raindrops, each one working like a tiny prism. Sunlight goes into a drop and bends. It bounces off the back of the drop. Then it bends again as it comes out. Each colour comes out at a slightly different angle. So from any one drop, only one colour reaches your eye. Drops higher up send you red. Drops lower down send you violet. Together, all those drops make the whole bow.
- e.g. After a monsoon shower, when the Sun comes out behind you, you see a rainbow on the dark clouds ahead.
- e.g. The spray of a waterfall, like Jog Falls in Karnataka, often holds a rainbow.
- e.g. A spray bottle set to a fine mist makes a small rainbow on a sunny balcony, with the Sun behind you.
### Where to stand to see a rainbow · *defined*
**Definition:** A rainbow is a circle centred on the point exactly opposite the Sun, which is where the shadow of your head falls.

You can only see a rainbow with the Sun behind you and rain in front of you. Look at the shadow of your own head. The rainbow is a circle around that shadow. The bow is always the same size around it. That is why rainbows come in the early morning or late afternoon, when the Sun is low. At noon the Sun is too high, and the bow is below the ground. From an aeroplane, people sometimes see the whole rainbow as a full circle.
- e.g. Morning rainbows appear in the west. Evening rainbows appear in the east.
- e.g. At noon in summer, you will almost never see a rainbow in India.
- e.g. The higher the Sun, the lower the rainbow sits in the sky.
### Everyone sees their own rainbow · *defined*
**Definition:** Because each raindrop sends each colour in one direction, every person sees a rainbow made by a different set of drops.

Each drop sends one colour to one place. If you take one step to the side, different drops send colours to your eyes. So you and your friend, standing apart, see light from different drops. Each person sees their own rainbow. That is also why you can never reach the end of a rainbow. When you move, the rainbow moves with you.
- e.g. Walk towards a rainbow. It never comes nearer.
- e.g. Two cameras a few steps apart photograph two slightly different rainbows.
- e.g. Your left eye and your right eye see slightly different rainbows too.
### Double rainbows · *defined*
**Definition:** A double rainbow has a second, fainter bow outside the first, made by light bouncing twice inside each drop, with its colours in the opposite order.

Sometimes there is a second, fainter rainbow outside the first. In the second one, the colours are the other way round. Red is on the inside. It forms when light bounces twice inside each drop, not once. Each bounce loses some light, so the second bow is dimmer. The sky between the two bows looks darker than the rest. It is called Alexander's dark band, after Alexander of Aphrodisias, a Greek thinker who wrote about it about 1,800 years ago.
- e.g. In the main bow, red is on the outside. In the second bow, red is on the inside.
- e.g. The sky inside the main bow looks brighter than the sky outside it.
- e.g. Very rarely, people see a third bow.
### How many colours are in a rainbow? · *debated*
Most books say seven colours. But look closely and there are no lines between the colours. Red fades into orange, and orange into yellow. People have counted the colours very differently. So the number of colours is partly about the light, and partly about the names we choose.
- e.g. Some children count five colours. Some count eight.
- e.g. Many languages name blue and green with one word.
- e.g. A spectrum seen through a good instrument shows a smooth band with no gaps.
- **Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,350 years ago:** He said the rainbow has three colours: red, green and a purple-blue. He also thought it was made by clouds working like mirrors.
- **Isaac Newton, an English scientist, in his book Opticks in 1704:** He named seven colours: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. At first he named five. He added orange and indigo partly to make seven, like the seven notes of a musical scale.
- **Scientists today:** The colours change smoothly, with no steps. There are thousands of shades our eyes can tell apart. Seven is a useful way of naming them, not a fact about the light. Scientists who study languages still argue about how much our colour words change what we notice.
### What makes red different from violet · *defined*
**Definition:** The colour of light depends on its wavelength, the length of its waves. Red has the longest waves we can see and violet the shortest.

Light travels as tiny waves, much too small to see. The colour depends on how long the waves are. Red light has the longest waves we can see. Violet has the shortest. Red waves are almost twice as long as violet waves. Laid end to end, about 150 red waves or 250 violet waves would fit across the thickness of one sheet of paper. Light also comes in tiny packets called photons. A violet photon carries more energy than a red one.
- e.g. Red, orange and yellow have longer waves. Blue and violet have shorter waves.
- e.g. Violet and ultraviolet light carry more energy, which is why ultraviolet can burn skin.
- e.g. Red light from a bicycle reflector is made of long waves.
### Colours past the ends of the rainbow · *defined*
**Definition:** Infrared is invisible light beyond red, and ultraviolet is invisible light beyond violet. They are light just like the colours we see, with longer or shorter waves.

The rainbow does not really stop at red and violet. It goes on, but our eyes cannot see the rest. In 1800, William Herschel, an astronomer in England, put thermometers in the colours of a spectrum. One just past the red end, where there was no colour at all, got the warmest. He had found infrared light. In 1801, Johann Ritter in Germany found invisible light past the violet end. It darkened a special chemical. We call it ultraviolet. Bees can see ultraviolet light. Many flowers have ultraviolet patterns that bees can see but we cannot.
- e.g. A TV remote talks to the TV with infrared light.
- e.g. Sunscreen protects skin from ultraviolet light.
- e.g. Sunflowers and many other flowers have ultraviolet patterns that guide bees to the nectar.
### Why things have colours · *defined*
**Definition:** Things look coloured because they absorb some colours of the light falling on them and reflect the rest to our eyes.

A leaf does not make green light. Sunlight falls on it with all its colours. The leaf's green pigment, chlorophyll, absorbs most of the red and blue light, and the leaf uses that energy to make its food. Much of the green light is reflected, and some of it reaches your eyes. So the leaf looks green. A ripe tomato absorbs most colours and reflects red. A white shirt reflects nearly all the colours. A black bag absorbs nearly all of them, which is why it gets hot in the sun. The unit on how we see tells you more about light bouncing into our eyes.
- e.g. Under red light, a green leaf looks dark, because there is no green light for it to reflect.
- e.g. A black umbrella gets hotter in the sun than a white one.
- e.g. Turmeric looks yellow because it absorbs blue light and reflects the other colours.
### Putting the colours back together · *defined*
**Definition:** Mixing all the colours of light together makes white light again.

If white light can be split into colours, can the colours be put back into white? Isaac Newton did it in the 1660s and 1670s. He split sunlight with one prism. Then he caught the colours with a lens and a second prism, and white light came out. You can do it with a spinning wheel. Colour a disc with rainbow slices and spin it fast. Your eye cannot keep up, so it mixes the colours. The wheel looks pale grey or nearly white. It is grey, not bright white, because each slice reflects only part of the light that falls on it.
- e.g. A spinning rainbow top looks pale while it spins fast.
- e.g. Phone and TV screens make white from tiny red, green and blue dots very close together.
- e.g. A second prism, turned the other way, can join the colours again.
### Is colour in the light or in us? · *debated*
Light has wavelengths, but the light itself is not 'red'. Red is what happens when that light reaches an eye and a brain. Most people have three kinds of colour-sensing cells in their eyes. Each kind is best at a different part of the rainbow. The brain compares them and makes the colours we feel. Some people have only two kinds working. They mix up some colours, like red and green. The pink-purple called magenta is not in the rainbow at all. Your brain makes it when red and blue light arrive together, with no green.
- e.g. Magenta, the colour of many bougainvillea flowers, has no place of its own in the rainbow.
- e.g. A mantis shrimp has 12 kinds of colour cells, but tests show they are worse than us at telling close colours apart.
- e.g. John Dalton, an English scientist, described his own colour blindness in 1794.
- **Many physicists:** Colour is wavelength. Red light is light with long waves. The rest is just how eyes report it.
- **Many brain scientists:** Colour is made by the brain. The same light can look different colours depending on what is around it. Magenta exists only in brains.
- **Philosophers:** Even if we know everything about waves and brains, we may not know what red feels like to you. Nobody knows if your red and my red are the same. This is still an open question.
### The rainbows of stars · *defined*
**Definition:** Every kind of atom makes its own pattern of lines in a spectrum, so the split light of a star shows what the star is made of.

When scientists split the light from the Sun very finely, they see thin dark lines across the rainbow. Joseph von Fraunhofer, a German lens-maker, mapped hundreds of them in 1814. Later, scientists found that each kind of atom makes its own lines, like a fingerprint. In 1868, astronomers came to Guntur in Andhra Pradesh to watch a total eclipse of the Sun. Pierre Janssen and others saw a bright yellow line in the Sun's light that no one could explain. Norman Lockyer in England said it came from an element nobody had found on Earth yet. It was named helium, after Helios, the Greek word for the Sun. In 1925, Cecilia Payne, a young woman astronomer, used the split light of stars to show that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
- e.g. An old-style tubelight or a CFL bulb, not an LED, seen on the shiny side of a CD shows a few bright coloured lines, not a smooth rainbow.
- e.g. Astronomers can tell what a star is made of without ever going there.
- e.g. Annie Jump Cannon sorted hundreds of thousands of stars by their rainbows, at Harvard, more than 100 years ago.
### Why the sky is blue and sunsets are red · *defined*
**Definition:** Air scatters blue light more than red, so the sky looks blue. At sunset the light crosses so much air that mostly red and orange reach us.

Sunlight passes through the air on its way to us. The tiny particles of gas that make up air scatter blue light more than red light. Scatter means to send off in all directions. So blue light comes at you from every part of the sky, and the sky looks blue. At sunset, sunlight travels a long way through the air. Most of the blue has been scattered away before it reaches you. Red and orange are left. In 1921, on a sea voyage between India and England, C.V. Raman wondered why the sea was so blue. He showed that water scatters blue light too. His later work on scattered light won the Nobel Prize in 1930.
- e.g. The noon sky is blue. The sky near the horizon at sunset is orange and red.
- e.g. On the Moon there is no air, so the sky is black even in daytime.
- e.g. 28 February, the day Raman announced his discovery in 1928, is National Science Day in India.
### Mixing light is not like mixing paint · *defined*
**Definition:** Mixing coloured lights adds colours, so the mix gets brighter and can look white. Mixing paints puts pigments together, and between them they absorb more colours, so the mix gets darker.

Shine a red light and a green light on the same patch of white wall, and the patch looks yellow. Add a blue light, and it looks white. Each light adds more colours to what reaches your eye. Paint works the other way. Each paint has a pigment that absorbs some colours and reflects the rest. When you mix paints, each pigment absorbs its own colours, so less and less light is reflected. Mix many paints, and very little light comes back, so you get a dark, muddy brown. The spinning rainbow wheel is different. Its slices sit side by side, so the light they reflect mixes in your eye, as coloured lights do. That is why it looks pale grey, not brown.
- e.g. Phone and TV screens make white from tiny red, green and blue lights.
- e.g. Yellow and blue paint make green, because green is the main colour that both paints reflect.
- e.g. Colouring the same spot with every felt pen makes a dark brownish blot, not white.

## Mostly agreed
- Sunlight is a mixture of many colours. *How we know:* A prism splits it into colours. Newton showed that a second prism could join them back into white light.
- Each colour bends by a different amount in glass or water. Violet bends most, red least. *How we know:* In every prism, every glass of water and every rainbow, the colours come in the same order.
- Rainbows form when sunlight bends and bounces inside raindrops. *How we know:* Around 1300, Kamal al-Din al-Farisi in Persia and Theodoric of Freiberg in Germany each made a model raindrop, a glass ball full of water, and made rainbow colours with it. In 1637, Rene Descartes worked out the rainbow's angle.
- There is light our eyes cannot see, past both ends of the rainbow. *How we know:* Herschel's thermometer warmed up beyond the red in 1800. TV remotes, sunburn and bee vision all use this invisible light.
- Each kind of atom makes its own pattern of lines in a spectrum. *How we know:* Helium was found in the Sun's light in 1868, 27 years before it was found on Earth in 1895.

## How we found out
**Isaac Newton, with Robert Hooke arguing against him, 1666 to 1672.** For a long time, people thought white light was pure and simple. They thought a prism added colours to it, perhaps by mixing in some darkness. Starting in 1666, a young Isaac Newton tested this. He darkened his room and let one thin beam of sunlight in through a small hole in the shutter. A prism spread the beam into a long strip of colours on the far wall. Then he let only one colour, such as red, pass through a small hole in a board. He sent that one colour through a second prism. If prisms added colour, the red should have changed again. It did not. It stayed red, and it bent by the same amount as before. Newton also used a lens to bring all the colours back together, and they made white again. He published his results in 1672. Some scientists, such as Robert Hooke, argued against him for years. But anyone could repeat the test, and it kept working. White light is not pure. It is a mixture of colours.  
*What people believed before:* A prism adds colours to pure white light. Newton sent a single colour through a second prism and it did not change, so the colours must already be in the light.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A rainbow is made by clouds working like a mirror.** (Aristotle, about 2,350 years ago. Many people accepted it for more than 1,500 years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rainbows do appear on clouds, and they look like a reflection of the Sun.  
  Against: Al-Farisi and Theodoric showed, around 1300, that a single ball of water makes the colours. It is the drops, not the cloud as a whole.
- **A prism adds colour to white light, like a stain.** (Many people before Newton, including some scientists of his time.; *rejected*)  
  For: Thick glass often looks a little coloured, so it seemed the glass was colouring the light.  
  Against: Newton passed one colour, like red, through a second prism. It stayed red and did not change. If glass added colour, the second prism would have changed it again. And the colours could be joined back into white.
- **Colours come from light meeting darkness, not from white light being split.** (Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, a German poet, in his book Theory of Colours, 1810.; *rejected*)  
  For: Goethe noticed true things about how colours look, like coloured shadows and the colours at the edges of dark shapes. Artists still learn from him.  
  Against: As physics, his idea does not work. Measurements of light show that white light really is a mixture.
- **There are exactly seven colours in the rainbow.** (Many school books, following Isaac Newton.; *debated*)  
  For: Seven names are easy to remember, and they match the main bands most people notice.  
  Against: The colours change smoothly with no edges. Newton chose seven partly to match the seven notes of music. Many people today leave out indigo.
- **Our colour words change which colours we can see.** (Some scientists who study languages. Others disagree.; *debated*)  
  For: In some studies, people whose language has two words for light blue and dark blue, like Russian, tell those blues apart a little faster.  
  Against: The difference is small. People everywhere can see the same colours, even if they name them differently.
- **Some women can see more colours than other people, because they have four kinds of colour cells.** (Gabriele Jordan and other scientists, in a study published in 2010.; *debated*)  
  For: Tests found at least one woman who could tell apart colours that look the same to most people.  
  Against: Such people seem to be very rare. Scientists still argue about how many there are and how much more they see.

## Roots
We have learned that sunlight holds many colours, and that things look coloured by absorbing some colours and reflecting the rest. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A glass of water split sunlight into a band of colours, from red to violet. White sunlight is all those colours together. A green leaf sends back green and takes in the rest, so under red cellophane it looked dark. So a thing can only show colours that are in what shines on it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk as if colour belongs to a thing, like its size: 'the leaf is green'. But a thing can only send back colours that are in the light that falls on it.  
*Evidence:* In the glass of water test, sunlight split into a band of colours. In Catch a Rainbow, a green leaf under a torch with red cellophane looked dark, and it was green again in daylight.

- *who am i:* Your eye is part of every rainbow you see. When you step sideways, different drops send you their colours.
- *what is life:* Life is fed by the colours in sunlight. A leaf absorbs red and blue light and uses its energy to make food.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of light we cannot see, beyond both ends of the rainbow. Bees see some of it.

Sunlight comes to us as one white light. A glass of water spreads it into many colours, and a spinning wheel mixes them back into white. The water adds no colour of its own. A leaf, a tomato and a shirt each send back a different part of the same sunlight. So every colour around us is one light, met by different things. The many things of the world come from one source in the same way: different on the outside, one underneath.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.4.1:** The teacher Uddalaka tells his son that the red colour of a fire belongs to heat, its white to water, and its dark to food. 'Fire' is only a name, he says. The three colours underneath are what is real. (Uddalaka looked under one name and found a mix of colours. A prism does the same with sunlight: under the one name 'white' are all the colours of the rainbow. His three colours are not the rainbow's, but the way of looking is the same.)
- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** Over the palace of the god Indra hangs a great net with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel reflects every other jewel, so each one holds all the rest. (Things around us do this with colour. Put a red cloth next to a white wall in sunlight, and the wall near it glows faintly red. So the light from the wall carries a little of the cloth too, as each jewel holds the others.)
- **Jain teaching of anekantavada, many-sidedness, and the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Each blind man touches one part of the elephant and says what the elephant is like. Each is partly right. No one view holds the whole truth. (Each person sees their own rainbow from their own place. No one sees the rainbow from everywhere at once.)
- Science (settled): White light is a mixture of all the colours we can see, and the colours can be split apart and joined back together.
- Science (open): Colour as we feel it is made by eyes and brains working together. Whether your red feels like my red is unknown.
- Other view, Many physicists: White light is a mixture of waves of different lengths, and a rainbow is geometry in raindrops. They say this explains it fully, with no need for one source behind everything. That is an honest view.
- Practice, Colours with closed eyes (3 min): Let's sit facing a bright window or the open sky, but not the Sun itself. Close your eyes gently. Notice that it is not black behind your eyelids. What colour is it? Maybe orange or red. That is sunlight coming through your eyelids. Your skin and blood absorb most of the other colours and let mostly red through. Now cup your hands over your eyes. Did the colour change? Take three slow breaths. Then open your eyes and look at a white wall. Remember, all the colours are in that white light.
- Ask: A bee's eyes do not sense red, so a red tomato looks dark to a bee. You see it red. Are you seeing the tomato differently, or are you seeing different things?
- Ask: If white light is all the colours together, is it one thing or many things?
- Ask: Where is a rainbow? In the sky, in the drops, or in you?
- Ask: A leaf looks dark under red light and green in sunlight. Which one is its real colour?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Where were the rainbow's colours before the rain came? (hint: Think of the glass of water on the windowsill. Did the water make the colours, or show them?)
- *big:* If you and your friend each see your own rainbow, is there one rainbow or many? (hint: Each drop sends each colour to one place only.)
- *big:* Is your red the same as my red? How could we ever find out? (hint: We both call a tomato red. But can you show me what red feels like to you?)
- *big:* If nobody is looking, is there still a rainbow in the sky? (hint: The light is still bending in the drops. But where would the bow be centred?)
- *deep:* Why do we say a rainbow has seven colours, when the colours have no edges? (hint: Try drawing a line where green ends. Does everyone draw it in the same place?)
- *big:* A bee sees ultraviolet. What colours might there be that no human has ever seen? (hint: Think of the flowers with patterns that only bees can see.)
- *deep:* How can a star's light tell us what the star is made of, from so far away? (hint: Each kind of atom draws its own lines in the rainbow, like a fingerprint.)
- *deep:* Mixing coloured lights makes white, but mixing paints makes a dark brown. Why would mixing do opposite things? (hint: Coloured lights each add colours that reach your eye. What does each pigment in a paint do to the light?)
- *deep:* A leaf absorbs red and blue light and reflects green. So is green the colour the leaf keeps, or the colour it gives away? (hint: Think of the leaf in red light. With almost no green light to reflect, how did it look?)
- *big:* Kamal al-Din al-Farisi explained the rainbow 350 years before Newton. Why do so many books tell only Newton's story? (hint: Think about which books were read in which countries, and which languages they were written in.)

## Story: The Glass Raindrop
*Kamal al-Din al-Farisi was a real Persian scientist. Around 1300 he explained the rainbow using a glass ball filled with water inside a dark room, in his book Tanqih al-Manazir, a revision of Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics. Zainab, the glassblower's daughter, her friend Maryam, and everything that happens to them are made up.*

The glassblower's daughter was called Zainab, and she was not supposed to be in the dark room. Nobody was supposed to be in the dark room, except the scholar.

The scholar's name was Kamal al-Din. He had a grey beard and ink on all ten fingers. He had come to Zainab's father with a strange order. He did not want a lamp, or a bottle, or a bowl for sherbet. He wanted a ball of glass, perfectly round, as big as a pomegranate, and hollow inside, with a tiny neck for filling.

"What is it for?" asked Zainab's father.

"It is a raindrop," said the scholar. "A very large one. One that will hold still."

Zainab's father laughed. But he made the ball. And because Zainab had the steadiest hands in the workshop, it was Zainab who carried it through the streets, wrapped in wool, and Zainab who was told to stay and hold it.

The dark room was a storeroom with its shutters nailed closed. In one shutter there was a single hole, no bigger than a lentil. A thin rod of sunlight came through it and lay across the dust in the air, as straight as a spear.

"Fill it," said Kamal al-Din. Zainab filled the glass ball with water from the well, slowly, so that no bubble stayed inside. Then she held it up in the rod of light.

Nothing happened. The water glowed. A bright spot of light shone on the far side of the ball. That was all.

"Higher," said the scholar. "Now turn. Slowly." He was not looking at the ball. He was crouched on the floor with his back to the hole, peering up at the ball from below, the way you might look at a bird.

They did this for three days. Kamal al-Din moved the ball, moved his head, wrote in his book, crossed out what he wrote, and moved again. He talked as he worked, mostly to himself. He spoke of a great scholar called Ibn al-Haytham, who had lived nearly three hundred years before. He spoke of the Greek, Aristotle, who said the rainbow was the cloud acting like a mirror. "But a cloud is not a mirror," he muttered. "A cloud is drops. Only drops. So the secret must be inside one drop."

Zainab did not understand most of it. But she understood that her arms ached. On the third afternoon, when the scholar went out to eat, Zainab set the glass ball on a stool in the light. She sat down on the floor near the shutter, with her back to the hole, just to rest.

And there, glowing on the glass, was a spot of red.

It was not a dull red. It was red like the inside of a pomegranate, red like the first coal in the morning fire. It sat on the edge of the ball like a jewel.

Zainab held her breath. Where had it come from? The sunlight was white. The water was clear. The glass was clear. She had helped to make the glass herself. There was no red in it anywhere.

She leaned to the side to look more closely. The red slid away, and a yellow took its place. She leaned a little more. Green. Then a blue, deep as indigo cloth. Then a violet that was almost not there. She leaned back. Yellow. Red.

She moved her head up and down, side to side, until she was dizzy. Each place she put her eye, the same drop gave her a different colour. It was as if the ball were throwing colours into the room in many directions, red this way, green that way, and her eye could only catch one at a time.

When Kamal al-Din came back, Zainab was lying flat on the floor, laughing.

"It is not in the ball," Zainab said. "It is not in the ball, sir. It is where you put your eye."

The scholar looked at her for a long time. Then he lay down on the dusty floor beside the glassblower's daughter, his grey beard in the dust, and put his own eye where Zainab's had been.

"Red," he said quietly. He moved. "Green." He did not say anything for a while.

That night he wrote for many hours. Zainab heard the pen scratching through the wall where she slept. The scholar wrote that the light goes into the drop and bends. It strikes the back of the drop and comes back, and bends again on its way out. And each colour leaves at its own angle, so that one drop gives one colour to one eye. A rainbow, he wrote, is not one thing hanging in the sky. It is a great crowd of drops, and each drop sends you only the colour that leaves it towards the place where you stand. The drops higher up give you red. The drops lower down give you violet. Together they make the bow.

He did not write the name of the glassblower's daughter. People did not write the names of glassblowers' daughters.

Some weeks later the rains came. Zainab ran out of the workshop with ash still on her hands, because the Sun had come out low behind her and the sky ahead was dark and full of drops. There it was, a great bow across the city, over the domes and the drying cloth and the donkeys in the lane.

Her friend Maryam came running up beside her, pointing. "Look! Look at it!"

Zainab looked at Maryam. Then she looked at the rainbow. She thought of the millions of drops, each one a little glass ball, each one throwing its colours out in every direction. Some of those colours were falling on her eyes. Different ones, from different drops, were falling on Maryam's.

"Yes," said Zainab. "I see mine. Do you see yours?"

Maryam frowned at her, as if she had gone mad. But Zainab only smiled, and stood very still, so that her rainbow would not move.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the colours were never in the glass, the water or the sky. They were in the sunlight all along, and the drop only spread them apart. Each drop sends each colour off at its own angle, so one drop gives one colour to one eye, and every person sees a rainbow made by different drops. The story also quietly asks who gets written into the history books.

- Ask: Zainab helped to make the glass ball, and the water was clear. So where did the red come from?
- Ask: When Zainab moved her head, the colour changed. Why?
- Ask: Why did Kamal al-Din want a glass ball as big as a pomegranate, instead of looking at real raindrops?
- Ask: What did Zainab mean when she said, "I see mine. Do you see yours?"
- Ask: The scholar did not write down Zainab's name. Whose names do you think are missing from the stories of discoveries?

  *Parent says:* Let's say the whole rainbow together, red to violet.  
  *Child mode:* Say your rainbow aloud: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet.
  - Think: Why must the colours go in this order, and never another?
### g2 · The Bending Race (20 min)
- **rules:** Draw a start line, a big triangle for the glass, and a finish wall beyond it.; Each player pins on a colour badge. All the colours stand together on the start line, holding hands, as white light.; On 'Go', everyone walks together in a straight line to the glass.; Inside the glass, everyone lets go and walks slowly. Each colour turns a little to one side, by the number of small turns on its badge.; When you come out of the glass, walk straight to the finish wall.; Mark where each colour lands. Did the colours spread into a rainbow, in the right order?
- **Printable cards: 7 colour badges to pin on:** RED: take 1 small turn; ORANGE: take 2 small turns; YELLOW: take 3 small turns; GREEN: take 4 small turns; BLUE: take 5 small turns; VIOLET: take 6 small turns; WHITE: the Sun, who starts the race
- **Printable board: A chalk court:** Start line: the Sun; Glass zone: a big chalk triangle; Finish wall: the white screen
- **Printable score sheet: Where did each colour land?:** Colour; Where it reached the finish wall; Was the order right?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the court in chalk. 2. Give each player a colour badge. 3. Line up the colours on the start line, holding hands.  
  *Parent says:* Holding hands, all together, you are white light. Let's see what happens when white light goes through glass.  
  *Child mode:* Pin on your colour badge. Hold hands with the others. Together, you are white light.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say 'Go'. 2. Everyone walks to the glass. 3. In the glass, each colour makes its number of small turns to one side. 4. Then each walks straight to the finish wall.  
  *Parent says:* Into the glass! Slow down. Red, one small turn. Violet, six. Now out, and walk straight!  
  *Child mode:* Walk to the glass. Let go of hands. Turn your number of small turns. Walk straight to the wall.
  - Note: The game is a picture, not the full science. Real light slows down in glass, and each colour slows a slightly different amount, which makes each colour bend differently. Violet bends most.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark where each colour landed on the finish wall. Read the order aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Who landed where? Read the order from one end. Is it a rainbow?  
  *Child mode:* Where did you land? Say the order of colours from one end.
  - Check: In the Bending Race, the colours started together but landed spread out. Why? → **Each colour turned by a different amount in the glass**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play once more, but this time make every colour take the same number of turns.  
  *Parent says:* What if every colour turned the same amount? Let's try.  
  *Child mode:* Play again. This time everyone takes 3 turns. What happens?
  - Think: If all colours bent the same, would we ever see a rainbow?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Water Prism: do the colours ever change order? (25 min)
- **question:** When we split sunlight in different ways, do the colours always come in the same order?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the colours come in a different order with the mirror in water? Will red ever be in the middle? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same sunlight, at the same time of day, and the same white paper. Only change the thing that splits the light. Look at each one twice, because a quick look can fool you.
- **safety:** Never look straight at the Sun, or at its reflection in the mirror. Point the mirror's light at the wall, never at anyone's face. A glass mirror's edges can be sharp, so a grown-up handles it.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the three strips of colours, one under the other, with red on the left each time.
- **results table:** What split the light | Colour at one end | Colours in the middle, in order | Colour at the other end | How many colours I counted
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the glass, the mirror in the tray of water and the prism. 2. Ask them to guess whether the colours will come in the same order every time.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to split sunlight in three different ways. Do you think the colours will always come in the same order? Could red ever be in the middle?  
  *Child mode:* You will split sunlight three ways. Guess first. Will the colours always be in the same order?
  - Ask: Why do you think that? (good: The colours are inside the sunlight, so they might always be the same.; The mirror is different, so maybe it will be different.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the full glass at the edge of the sunny sill. 2. Catch the colours on white paper on the floor. 3. Write the colour at each end and the colours between them in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which colour is at one end? Which is at the other? Let's write down the order, colour by colour.  
  *Child mode:* Catch the colours on paper. Which colour is at each end? Write the order in the table.
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill a tray with water and put it in direct sunlight. 2. Lean the mirror inside it at a slant, facing the Sun, so the sunlight hits the mirror under the water. 3. Move the mirror slowly until a rainbow strip lands on a white wall or card. 4. Record the order.  
  *Parent says:* This time the light goes into the water, bounces off the mirror, and comes back out. That's a little like what happens inside a raindrop. Let's see the order.  
  *Child mode:* Lean the mirror in the water, facing the Sun. Find the colours on the wall. Write their order.
  - Note: This works best with the Sun quite low and a white wall in the shade nearby. Tilt the mirror slowly. The water must be still, so wait a moment after moving it. The light goes in through the water, bounces and comes out, like light in a raindrop.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If you have a prism, hold it in a sunbeam. Turn it slowly until a strip of colours falls on white card. Record the order.  
  *Parent says:* One more. A real glass prism. Turn it slowly. Same order or different?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the prism slowly in the sunlight. Catch the colours on card. Same order?
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the three rows of the table together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all three rows. Did the order ever change?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Did the order ever change?
  - Check: In the water prism test, the glass, the mirror in water and the prism all gave red at one end and violet at the other. What does this tell us? → **The colours and their order come from the sunlight itself**
- **cer (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Now say three things. Say what you think. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: sunlight always splits into the same colours in the same order. What I saw: with the glass, the mirror in water and the prism, red was at one end and violet at the other every time. Why: the colours are in the sunlight, and each one bends by its own amount.
### e2 · Make Your Own Rainbow with a Spray (20 min)
- **question:** Where must the Sun be for you to see a rainbow in the spray?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will you see the rainbow better with the Sun in front of you, behind you, or beside you? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same spray, the same fine setting and the same time of day. Only change which way you face. Try each direction for one full minute.
- **safety:** Do this outside with a grown-up. Never look straight at the Sun. Spray away from people's faces and away from electric sockets and wires.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw yourself, the Sun, your shadow and the rainbow in one picture.
- **results table:** Where the Sun was | Did I see a rainbow? (yes or no) | Where in the spray was it? | Which colour was on the outside?
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go to a sunny balcony, terrace or doorstep in the early morning or late afternoon. 2. Set the spray bottle or hose to its finest mist. 3. Ask your child to guess where the Sun must be, and why.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to make a rainbow. Where should the Sun be, do you think? In front of us, beside us, or behind us?  
  *Child mode:* You will make a rainbow with spray. Guess first. Where should the Sun be?
  - Note: You need direct sunlight on you and the spray. If your balcony gets no direct sun at these times, use the terrace or the building's entrance. Many homes already have a spray bottle for plants or ironing.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Face the Sun, but look down, never at it. Spray in front of you for one minute. 2. Turn so the Sun is to your side. Spray for one minute. 3. Write yes or no in the table each time.  
  *Parent says:* Let's try facing the Sun first. Don't look at it! Look at the spray. Any rainbow? Now let's turn sideways.  
  *Child mode:* Face the Sun but look only at the spray. Any rainbow? Now turn sideways. Write yes or no.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn so the Sun is right behind you. 2. Spray the mist in front of you, a little higher than your head. 3. Look for the rainbow. Find the shadow of your head on the ground. Notice that the bow curves around it.  
  *Parent says:* Now the Sun is behind us. Spray up high. What do you see? Look down at your shadow. Where is your shadow's head?  
  *Child mode:* Turn your back to the Sun. Spray high. Do you see the rainbow now? Find your shadow's head.
  - Note: With the Sun behind you, a bow appears in the spray. It is part of a circle centred on the shadow of your head. Stretch out your arm: the bow is about two hand-spans from your shadow's head. Red is on the outside of the bow. If you see nothing, make the mist finer, spray in a different spot, or try a darker background.
  - Check: In the spray rainbow test, when did you see the rainbow best? → **With the Sun behind me**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to walk two steps to the side and look again.  
  *Parent says:* Take two steps to the side. Did the rainbow stay where it was, or come with you?  
  *Child mode:* Take two steps sideways. Did the rainbow move with you?
  - Think: If your friend stands next to you, do you both see the same rainbow?
- **cer (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Now say three things. Say what you think. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: you only see a rainbow with the Sun behind you. What I saw: facing the Sun there was no rainbow, but with the Sun behind me there was a bow around my shadow's head. Why: the drops bounce the light back towards the side the Sun is on, split into colours.
### e3 · Newton's Wheel: spin the colours back into white (30 min)
- **question:** If we spin all the rainbow colours together, will they look white?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess what colour each disc will look while spinning: the rainbow disc, the half-red half-green disc, and the all-red disc. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, make all the discs the same size, and spin them the same way and just as fast. Only change the colours on them. Spin each one three times.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the hole in the middle of each disc. Spin the discs on a table, away from faces.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Stick your three discs on a page and write what each looked like while spinning.
- **results table:** Disc | My guess: colour while spinning | Colour I saw while spinning | Same as my guess? (yes or no)
- **s1 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw three circles round a steel tumbler on white card. 2. Cut them out. 3. Divide the first into six equal slices, like cutting a round uttapam for six. Colour them red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet. 4. Colour the second half red and half green. 5. Colour the third all red.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make three spinning wheels. This one is like Isaac Newton's colour wheel, with all the rainbow colours.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three circles. Cut them out. Colour one with six rainbow slices. Colour one half red, half green. Colour one all red.
  - Note: To get six equal slices, fold the disc in half, then into three like a fan. Colour boldly and evenly. Felt pens work better than crayons. A grown-up pokes the hole in the centre with a sharp pencil.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to write a guess for each disc in the table.  
  *Parent says:* What colour do you think each one will look when it spins fast?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. What colour will each disc look when it spins? Write it down.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push a short pencil through the hole in each disc. 2. Spin each one like a top on the table, as fast as you can. 3. Spin each disc three times. 4. Write what you saw.  
  *Parent says:* Spin! What colour is it now, while it's spinning fast? Is it the same as your guess?  
  *Child mode:* Spin each disc fast. What colour do you see? Spin each one three times.
  - Note: The rainbow disc looks pale grey or creamy white, not bright white. Each slice reflects only part of the light that falls on it, so the mix is dimmer than white paper. It is still clearly paler than any of its colours. Red and green spun together look a dull yellowish brown. The all-red disc stays red.
  - Check: In Newton's Wheel, the rainbow disc looked pale grey-white when it spun fast. What does this show? → **When all the colours reach your eye together, they look white**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stop the rainbow disc and look at it again. Then compare all three discs.  
  *Parent says:* Look, the colours are still there! So where did the white come from?  
  *Child mode:* Stop the disc. Are the colours still there? So where did the white come from?
  - Think: Sunlight is all the colours together. Is the white in sunlight one colour or many?
- **cer (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Now say three things. Say what you think. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: all the rainbow colours together look white. What I saw: the rainbow disc looked pale white when it spun, but the all-red disc stayed red. Why: when all the colours reach my eye together, my eye mixes them into white, like sunlight.

## Activities
### a1 · Catch a Rainbow on the Floor (14 min)
- **safety:** Never look straight at the Sun. Look only at the paper on the floor. Wipe up any spilt water.
- **record:** Draw what you see on the paper. Colour it in the right order. Draw the leaf in red light and in room light. Write one thing you notice and one thing you wonder.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill a clear glass to the very top with water. 2. Stand it at the edge of a sunny windowsill or table, so sunlight shines through it and down to the floor.  
  *Parent says:* Let's put this glass of water right here in the sunshine. Now don't tell me anything yet. Just look at the floor and the walls.  
  *Child mode:* Fill a glass to the top with water. Put it at the edge of a sunny windowsill. Look at the floor below it.
  - Note: If you see no colours, move the glass right to the edge, so light passes through it and down. Morning or late afternoon sun works best.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put white paper on the floor where the light lands. 2. Move the paper slowly back and forth until a strip of colours appears.  
  *Parent says:* What do you notice? What do you wonder?  
  *Child mode:* Put white paper on the floor. Move it slowly. Can you see colours? Which colour is at each end?
  - Ask: What do you notice? (good: There are colours on the paper.; Red is at one end and blue or violet is at the other.; The colours are in a row.)
  - Ask: What do you wonder? (good: Where do the colours come from?; Is the water coloured?; Why are they in that order?)
  - Note: The strip is small, about the size of a finger, and often lies at the edge of the glass's shadow. Look along the edges of the shadow. If no window gets direct sun, put the glass on a step or wall in the sun outside, with the paper on the ground below.
  - Check: The glass and water are clear. Sunlight looks white. So where do you think the colours on the paper came from? → **The colours were hiding in the sunlight**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to draw the colours in order, and to write one thing they notice and one thing they wonder.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw what we saw, in the right order. Then tell me one question you want to find out.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the colours in the order you saw them. Write one question you have.
  - Think: If you put the glass in the shade, would you still see colours? Why or why not?
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go to a dim room. Put a green leaf on white paper. 2. Fold red cellophane or a red sweet wrapper two or three times, and hold it over a phone torch. If you have neither, open a plain red picture on a phone at full brightness. Use that screen as the light. 3. Ask your child to guess, then shine the red light on the leaf and the paper. 4. Switch the room light on again.  
  *Parent says:* This leaf is green and this paper is white. What colour do you think the leaf will look in red light? Why do you think so? Now look. Is the leaf brighter or darker than the paper?  
  *Child mode:* Put a leaf on white paper in a dim room. Guess how it will look in red light. Shine the red light on it. Is the leaf brighter or darker than the paper?
  - Ask: How did the leaf look next to the paper in red light? (good: The paper looked red, but the leaf looked dark.; The leaf did not look green any more.; When the light came on, the leaf was green again.)
  - Note: The white paper looks bright red. The leaf looks much darker, a dull dark red or almost black. It may not look fully black, because cellophane lets a little other light through and a leaf reflects a little red. A darker room and folded cellophane make it clearer. Don't explain yet. The explanation cards come later.
  - Think: The leaf was green again in room light. Did the leaf change, or did the light?
### a2 · Colour Filters: let only one colour through (15 min)

- **s1 (3 min)**

  *Parent says:* Let's make our strip of colours again. Today we'll test the colours with these coloured sheets.

## Worksheets
### Rainbow Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at a white part of a phone screen through a magnifying glass. You see tiny red, green and blue dots, but no white dots. Why does the screen look white from far away?  
   Options: Red, green and blue light reaching your eye together look white / The glass of the screen is white / The dots turn white when you step back / White dots hide behind the coloured ones  
   **Answer:** Red, green and blue light reaching your eye together look white. *Why:* From far away, the light from many tiny dots arrives in your eye together. Red, green and blue light added together look white, just like the spinning rainbow wheel. Picking 'the glass is white' forgets that the glass is clear: switch the phone off and it looks black.
2. (fill) A clear block of glass with flat sides that splits white light into colours is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['prism']. *Why:* A prism splits white light because each colour bends by a different amount as it goes in and out of the glass.
3. (truefalse) A rainbow in the sky is made by millions of raindrops, not by one cloud.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each drop works like a tiny prism and sends one colour to your eye. Millions of drops together make the whole bow.
4. (mcq) In Newton's Wheel, one disc was coloured all red. What do you predict it looks like when it spins fast?  
   Options: Still red / White / Black / Rainbow stripes  
   **Answer:** Still red. *Why:* Spinning mixes the colours on a disc. An all-red disc has only red to mix, so it stays red. This shows that spinning does not remove colour. It is the rainbow disc, with every colour, that looks pale white.
5. (sort) Sort these. Does each one split white light into colours, or not?  
   **Answer:** a glass prism → Splits light into colours; a raindrop → Splits light into colours; a glass of water in sunlight → Splits light into colours; a wooden block → Does not; a cotton towel → Does not; a CD under a torch → Splits light into colours. *Why:* Clear glass and water bend light and spread the colours. The tiny grooves on a CD spread them too. Wood and cloth do not let light through, so they cannot spread it.
6. (fill) A blue school bag looks blue because it reflects ____ light to our eyes and absorbs most of the other colours.  
   **Answer:** ['blue']. *Why:* Sunlight has all the colours. The bag's dye absorbs most of them and sends the blue back to our eyes. In red light, the same bag would look almost black.
7. (mcq) You hold red cellophane in the sunbeam, before the light reaches the glass of water. Which colour reaches the paper on the floor?  
   Options: Mostly red / All the colours / Mostly green / None  
   **Answer:** Mostly red. *Why:* A red filter lets red light through and absorbs most of the others. Picking 'all the colours' means thinking the cellophane is just clear. Picking 'mostly green' mixes up which colour passes.
8. (match) Match each thing to what it does to light.  
   **Answer:** Prism → splits white light into colours; Spinning rainbow wheel → mixes the colours back into white; Red cellophane → lets mostly red light through; Raindrop → sends one colour to your eye, as one tiny part of a rainbow. *Why:* Splitting, mixing and filtering are three things we can do with the colours in light.
9. (truefalse) Moonlight is sunlight that has bounced off the Moon. True or false: a rainbow can only ever form in the daytime.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bright moonlight shining on rain can make a faint 'moonbow' at night, because moonlight still has all the colours of sunlight. It usually looks pale or white to us, because our eyes see very little colour in dim light.
10. (draw) Draw yourself seeing a rainbow. Draw the Sun, the rain and the rainbow in the right places.  
   **Answer:** The Sun is behind the person. The rain and the rainbow are in front of them. Red is on the outside of the bow. A strong drawing also shows the person's shadow pointing towards the rainbow.. *Why:* To see a rainbow, the Sun must be behind you and the rain in front.
11. (write) Tell someone in two sentences why a glass of water makes colours on the floor.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that sunlight is all the colours mixed together, and that the water bends each colour by a different amount, so they spread apart on the floor.. *Why:* Two ideas make a full answer: the colours are in the sunlight, and the water spreads them.
12. (write) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, has a little brother. He says the colours on the floor are painted on by the glass. Kavya says they were in the sunlight all along. How could they test who is right? What would they find?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer plans a real check. For example: pour the water into a white cup and see that it is clear. Or put the glass in the shade, where no colours appear. Or use a different clear glass, which gives the same colours. Each shows the colours come from the sunlight, so Kavya is right.. *Why:* A good test lets the evidence decide. If the glass made the colours, they would appear in the shade too.
13. (truefalse) A red shirt looks red because the cloth makes red light.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The shirt makes no light of its own. It absorbs most of the colours in sunlight and reflects the red back to your eyes. In a dark room, it gives out no red at all.
14. (mcq) In a room with no light at all, what colour is a ripe mango?  
   Options: You cannot see any colour, because no light reaches your eyes from the mango / Bright yellow, as always / Green / White  
   **Answer:** You cannot see any colour, because no light reaches your eyes from the mango. *Why:* Colour is the light a thing reflects to your eyes. With no light, there is nothing to reflect, so you see no colour. Many people think colour stays in the mango, but you need light to see it.
15. (odd) Which one is the odd one out? A raindrop, a glass prism, a glass of water in sunlight, a mirror lying on a dry table. Say why.  
   **Answer:** a mirror lying on a dry table. *Why:* A mirror on its own just bounces light back, with no colours. The other three bend light as it passes through, so the colours spread apart. A mirror only makes colours when it sits under water, as in the water prism test.
16. (mcq) Why does a white shirt look white in sunlight?  
   Options: It reflects nearly all the colours of sunlight together / It absorbs all the colours / It makes its own white light / It has no colour at all  
   **Answer:** It reflects nearly all the colours of sunlight together. *Why:* When all the colours reach your eye together, you see white. A white shirt reflects them all. A black shirt absorbs nearly all of them, which is why it looks dark.
### Bending, Bouncing and Blue Skies (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Each colour bends by its own amount in a prism. Predict: if every colour bent by exactly the same amount, what would you see on the floor?  
   Options: A patch of white light, with no colours / A brighter rainbow / Only red / The colours in the opposite order  
   **Answer:** A patch of white light, with no colours. *Why:* The colours spread apart only because they bend by different amounts. If they all bent the same, they would stay together and look white. This is why a flat window pane makes no rainbow: light comes out at the same angle it went in.
2. (mcq) In the water prism test, the glass, the mirror in water and the prism all gave the same order of colours. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: The order comes from the light itself / All three were painted the same way / It was luck / Mirrors make rainbows  
   **Answer:** The order comes from the light itself. *Why:* Three different things gave the same order, so the order must come from the light. Each colour has its own bending amount. Picking 'luck' would need three lucky results in a row.
3. (mcq) A rainbow is part of a circle. Where is the centre of that circle?  
   Options: At the shadow of your head / At the Sun / At the top of the cloud / At the end of the rainbow  
   **Answer:** At the shadow of your head. *Why:* The rainbow is a circle around the point exactly opposite the Sun, which is where your head's shadow falls. Picking 'the Sun' is a common mix-up, but the bow is on the other side of the sky from the Sun.
4. (write) Why do we hardly ever see a rainbow at noon in summer? Use the shadow of your head in your answer.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that the rainbow is a circle around the shadow of your head. At noon the Sun is high, so your shadow is short and points nearly straight down. Then the whole circle is below the ground, where you cannot see it.. *Why:* A low Sun makes a long shadow and a high rainbow. That is why rainbows come in the morning and late afternoon.
5. (truefalse) From an aeroplane, you can sometimes see a rainbow as a full circle.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. On the ground, the ground hides the bottom half of the circle. From high up, with drops below you, the full circle can show.
6. (truefalse) In the second, fainter bow of a double rainbow, the colours are in the same order as in the main bow.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The second bow comes from light bouncing twice inside each drop. The extra bounce flips the order, so red is on the inside. Each bounce also loses some light, which is why it is fainter.
7. (mcq) In a dark room, you shine a torch through red cellophane onto a green leaf. The leaf looks very dark, almost black. Why?  
   Options: There is almost no green light for the leaf to reflect / Red light kills leaves / The leaf changes colour / Our eyes stop working  
   **Answer:** There is almost no green light for the leaf to reflect. *Why:* A leaf looks green by reflecting green light. The red light has almost no green in it, so the leaf reflects very little and looks dark. Picking 'the leaf changes colour' forgets that it is green again in sunlight.
8. (perspective) People disagree about how many colours a rainbow has.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and uses the family's counts as evidence, for example that everyone counted a different number. It then admits a fair point from another side, for example that names like 'red' and 'green' are still useful. It may say that the number is about how we name colours, not about the light.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. The spectrum has no lines in it, so the number of colours depends on the names we choose. Newton himself first counted five, then seven.
9. (fill) Light just past the red end of the rainbow, which we cannot see but can feel as warmth, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['infrared']. *Why:* Infra means below: it is below red. William Herschel found it in 1800 with a thermometer, just past the red end of a spectrum.
10. (sort) Sort these. Is light of different colours being added together, or are some colours being taken away?  
   **Answer:** a red torch and a green torch shining on one spot of a white wall → Colours of light added together; a rainbow disc spinning fast → Colours of light added together; red cellophane held in a sunbeam → Some colours taken away; mixing yellow and blue paint → Some colours taken away; a green leaf in sunlight → Some colours taken away; a blue shirt in sunlight → Some colours taken away. *Why:* Adding lights makes things brighter, and all the colours together look white. Red and green light together even look yellow. Filters, paints, leaves and cloth all absorb some colours, so less light comes back. This is why mixing paints gives mud, while mixing lights gives white.
11. (mcq) Why is the sky blue in the daytime?  
   Options: Air scatters blue light much more than other colours, sending it all over the sky / The sea reflects onto it / The sky is made of blue gas / Space is blue  
   **Answer:** Air scatters blue light much more than other colours, sending it all over the sky. *Why:* The tiny particles of gas in air scatter blue light much more than red. So blue comes at us from every part of the sky. Picking 'the sea reflects onto it' is a very old idea. But the sky is blue over deserts too.
12. (match) Match each person to what they found.  
   **Answer:** Isaac Newton → split sunlight with a prism and joined it back into white; Kamal al-Din al-Farisi → explained the rainbow with a glass ball of water, around 1300; William Herschel → found infrared, past the red end, in 1800; Cecilia Payne → used the split light of stars to show they are mostly hydrogen and helium, in 1925. *Why:* Many people in many countries, over many centuries, worked out the story of colour.
13. (mcq) A white shirt and a black shirt hang on the same line in the sun. Which one gets hotter, and why?  
   Options: The black one, because it absorbs almost all the colours of light / The white one, because it reflects light / Both the same / Neither, because light has no energy  
   **Answer:** The black one, because it absorbs almost all the colours of light. *Why:* Black absorbs nearly all the colours, and the energy of that light turns into warmth. White reflects most of the light. Picking 'light has no energy' forgets that sunlight warms you.
14. (write) In a sari shop, the shopkeeper often carries a sari to the door to show a customer the colour in daylight. Why might the colour look different under the shop's tube lights?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that a thing's colour comes from the light it reflects. Sunlight has all the colours in it, but a tube light gives out more of some colours and less of others. So the cloth has different light to reflect, and can look a little different. Daylight shows the colour the cloth has in the sun.. *Why:* Colour is not only in the thing. It depends on the light falling on the thing too. That is also why a green leaf looks dark under red light.
15. (draw) Draw a prism with a white beam going in. Draw the colours coming out, in the right order. Show which colour bends most.  
   **Answer:** A white beam enters one side. Colours fan out of the other side, with red bent least and violet bent most.. *Why:* Each colour bends by its own amount. Violet bends most, so it ends up furthest from the straight path.
16. (write) Ishita, a girl in Class 4, holds green cellophane in the sunbeam, and then holds red cellophane in front of it too. Predict what reaches the floor, and explain.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer predicts that almost no light reaches the floor, so the patch goes dark. The green filter lets only green through. The red filter absorbs green and lets only red through. So almost nothing passes both.. *Why:* A filter does not add colours. It only takes some away. Two different filters together take away nearly all of them.
### Colour Beyond the Rainbow (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Newton passed red light from one prism through a second prism. What happened?  
   Options: It stayed red / It split into more colours / It turned white / It disappeared  
   **Answer:** It stayed red. *Why:* A single colour does not split again. This showed that the prism does not add colours. It only separates the ones already in white light. Picking 'split into more colours' is what people who thought prisms make colours would expect.
2. (write) In 1800, William Herschel found infrared light with a thermometer, not with his eyes. Why could he not see it? How did the thermometer show it was there?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that our eyes cannot see light past the red end. Herschel put thermometers in each colour and just beyond the red. The one past red, where there was no colour at all, got warmest. So some light he could not see was landing there.. *Why:* Instruments let scientists find things our senses miss. Infrared is the warmth you feel from the Sun or a hot tava.
3. (mcq) A tube light seen through a spectroscope shows bright lines instead of a smooth rainbow. What do the lines come from?  
   Options: The atoms inside the tube, which each give out their own colours / Dust on the tube / A broken CD / The wall paint  
   **Answer:** The atoms inside the tube, which each give out their own colours. *Why:* Each kind of atom gives out its own colours, like a fingerprint. Tube lights contain a little mercury vapour. Picking 'dust' is a fair guess, but a clean tube shows the same lines.
4. (mcq) Helium was found in the Sun before anyone found it on Earth. How?  
   Options: A yellow line in the Sun's split light did not match any element known on Earth / Astronomers went to the Sun / It fell from the sky / It was found in a balloon  
   **Answer:** A yellow line in the Sun's split light did not match any element known on Earth. *Why:* In 1868, during an eclipse seen from Guntur in India, Pierre Janssen and other astronomers saw a yellow line in the Sun's spectrum. It matched no element known on Earth. Helium was found on Earth in 1895. Balloons came much later.
5. (truefalse) Magenta, the pink-purple of a bougainvillea flower, has its own place in the rainbow.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Magenta is not in the rainbow. Your brain makes it when red and blue light arrive together, with no green in between.
6. (mcq) A mantis shrimp has 12 kinds of colour cells in their eyes. Humans have 3. What did scientists find in 2014?  
   Options: Mantis shrimp were worse than us at telling close colours apart / Mantis shrimp see every colour much better than us / Mantis shrimp are colour-blind / Humans have 12 too  
   **Answer:** Mantis shrimp were worse than us at telling close colours apart. *Why:* Hanne Thoen and her team found that mantis shrimp seem to recognise colours quickly, but are poor at telling close shades apart. More colour cells does not always mean seeing more colours. Picking 'see every colour much better' is what most people guess.
7. (write) Is your red the same as my red? Write what we could test, and what we might never be able to test.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer separates the two. We could test whether we both pick the same red from a set of colour cards, and whether our eyes have the same colour cells. We might never be able to test what red feels like inside another person.. *Why:* This is an open question. Scientists can measure light and eyes, but not yet what a colour feels like to someone.
8. (mcq) Why are sunsets red and orange?  
   Options: Sunlight crosses so much air that most of the blue is scattered away, leaving red and orange / The Sun cools down in the evening / Clouds are red / The Sun is on fire  
   **Answer:** Sunlight crosses so much air that most of the blue is scattered away, leaving red and orange. *Why:* At sunset, sunlight takes a long path through the air. Air scatters blue more than red, so by the time the light reaches you, mostly red and orange are left. Picking 'the Sun cools down' is a common idea, but the Sun is just as hot at sunset.
9. (write) Kamal al-Din al-Farisi used a glass ball of water as a model raindrop. Why was a big model useful? What is one thing a model cannot tell you?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that a real drop is tiny and falls in a moment, while a big glass ball holds still, so you can study where each colour comes out. It then says that a model can be wrong in some way, for example real raindrops are tiny and get squashed out of round as they fall, so you must check the model against real rainbows.. *Why:* Models let scientists study what is too small, too fast or too far. But a model must be checked against the real thing.
10. (draw) Draw a big raindrop with a ray of sunlight going in. Show the light bending as it enters, bouncing off the back of the drop, and bending again as it comes out. Show red and violet coming out at different angles.  
   **Answer:** The ray enters the upper part of the drop and bends. It bounces off the back inside the drop. It comes out lower down, bending again, and spreads into colours. Red and violet leave at slightly different angles, so they go to different places.. *Why:* Bend in, bounce off the back, bend out. Because each colour bends by its own amount, they leave the drop at different angles. That is why a viewer sees red from higher drops and violet from lower ones.
11. (fill) Light comes in tiny packets. A packet of ultraviolet light can burn skin, because it carries more ____ than a packet of red light.  
   **Answer:** ['energy']. *Why:* Violet packets carry more energy than red ones, and ultraviolet packets carry more still. That is why sunscreen blocks ultraviolet. Red looks warm, but each red packet carries the least energy of all the colours.
12. (mcq) Plants are green. What does that tell us about which light they use most to make food?  
   Options: They absorb mostly red and blue light, and reflect the green / They use mostly green light / They use no light / They use only ultraviolet  
   **Answer:** They absorb mostly red and blue light, and reflect the green. *Why:* A plant reflects the light they use least. Green comes back to our eyes, so plants use mostly red and blue. Picking 'mostly green' is the most common mistake.
13. (write) Anjali, a girl in Class 4, says: 'The rainbow is out there in the sky, in one place, and everyone sees the same one.' Give two pieces of evidence against this.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives two pieces of evidence. First, when you walk towards a rainbow, it moves with you, so it is not in one place. Second, in the spray rainbow test, when I took two steps sideways, the rainbow moved too. It explains why: each drop sends each colour in only one direction, so a person standing somewhere else gets colours from different drops.. *Why:* A rainbow depends on where the viewer stands. Each person sees their own.
14. (write) Design a fair test to find out whether the depth of water in the glass changes the strip of colours on the floor. Say what you will change, what you will keep the same, and how many times you will try.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer changes only the water: a full glass, then half full. It keeps the same glass, the same spot on the sill and nearly the same time of day. It tries each one at least twice, because one try can be a fluke. It says what it will look at, such as how wide the strip is.. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Changing the glass or the time too would mix up the causes.
15. (perspective) For over 2,000 years, people argued about where the colours of a rainbow come from.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names an experiment, such as Newton's Wheel, where the rainbow disc spun back into pale white, or the water prism, which split sunlight into the same order every time. It then gives the other side a fair point, for example that Goethe was right that colours appear at the edges of dark shapes through a prism.. *Why:* Scientists today agree with Newton about light. But Goethe noticed real things about how we see colour, and those still interest scientists who study the eye and brain.
16. (write) Make up a question about colour or rainbows that nobody in your family can answer. How could you try to find out?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer asks a real question and gives a sensible way to find out. For example: do dogs see rainbows? I could read about which colour cells dogs have, or test whether our dog can find a red ball on green grass.. *Why:* Good scientists collect questions they cannot yet answer, and then look for a way to test them.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is white sunlight made of?  
   Options: One colour, white / Many colours mixed together / Only yellow / Heat only  
   **Answer:** Many colours mixed together. *Why:* Sunlight is all the colours mixed. A prism or a drop spreads them apart. Picking 'one colour, white' is what sunlight looks like, not what it is.
2. (mcq) What does a prism do to white light?  
   Options: Paints colours onto it / Spreads apart the colours already in it / Makes it darker / Turns it into heat  
   **Answer:** Spreads apart the colours already in it. *Why:* Each colour bends by a different amount, so they spread apart. Picking 'paints colours' was an old idea. Newton showed a single colour does not change in a second prism.
3. (order) Put these rainbow colours in order from red: blue, yellow, red, violet, green, orange.  
   **Answer:** ['red', 'orange', 'yellow', 'green', 'blue', 'violet']. *Why:* The order is always the same: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet. Red bends least and violet most.
4. (mcq) Which colour bends the most in a prism or raindrop?  
   Options: Red / Yellow / Violet / All the same  
   **Answer:** Violet. *Why:* Violet bends most. If all bent the same, there would be no rainbow. Picking red is common, because red is on top.
5. (mcq) What happens to sunlight inside a raindrop to make a rainbow?  
   Options: It goes in, bends, bounces off the back and comes out split into colours / It gets painted / It stops / It turns into rain  
   **Answer:** It goes in, bends, bounces off the back and comes out split into colours. *Why:* Bend in, bounce off the back, bend out. Each colour leaves at its own angle. Picking 'it stops' forgets that the light comes back out to your eye.
6. (mcq) You want to see a rainbow after an evening shower. Which way should you face?  
   Options: Towards the setting Sun / Away from the Sun, towards the east / Straight up / Down at the ground  
   **Answer:** Away from the Sun, towards the east. *Why:* The Sun must be behind you. In the evening it is in the west, so you face east. Picking 'towards the Sun' is the most common mistake.
7. (mcq) You spin a rainbow-coloured disc fast. It looks pale white. What does this show?  
   Options: All the colours together look white / Spinning removes colour / The disc was white / Your eyes were closed  
   **Answer:** All the colours together look white. *Why:* When all colours reach the eye together, we see white, as in sunlight. Picking 'spinning removes colour' is tested by the all-red disc, which stays red.
8. (mcq) A girl did the water prism test once and saw red at one end of the strip. Why should she do it again before saying red is always at that end?  
   Options: Because one try can be a fluke / To use up time / Because the first try is always wrong / To make the colours brighter  
   **Answer:** Because one try can be a fluke. *Why:* Repeating a test makes sure the result is real. Picking 'the first try is always wrong' is not true. It may be right, but we cannot be sure from one try.
9. (mcq) In the main rainbow, which colour is on the outside?  
   Options: Violet / Red / Green / Blue  
   **Answer:** Red. *Why:* Red is on the outside and violet on the inside. In the fainter second bow, the order flips.
10. (mcq) Which of these do scientists NOT know yet?  
   Options: That sunlight is many colours / Whether your red looks the same to you as my red does to me / That rainbows are made by raindrops / That violet bends more than red  
   **Answer:** Whether your red looks the same to you as my red does to me. *Why:* The other three are settled by experiments. What a colour feels like inside someone is still an open question.
11. (mcq) A bee looks at a flower. What can they see that we cannot?  
   Options: Infrared heat / Ultraviolet patterns / Nothing at all / Radio waves  
   **Answer:** Ultraviolet patterns. *Why:* Bees see ultraviolet, just past violet. Many flowers have ultraviolet patterns that guide bees to nectar. Picking 'infrared' mixes up the two ends of the rainbow.
12. (mcq) How do astronomers know what stars are made of?  
   Options: They visit them / They split starlight into a rainbow and read the lines, which are like atom fingerprints / They guess / From the colour of the night sky  
   **Answer:** They split starlight into a rainbow and read the lines, which are like atom fingerprints. *Why:* Each kind of atom makes its own lines in a spectrum. Cecilia Payne used this in 1925 to show stars are mostly hydrogen and helium. Nobody can visit a star. Stars are much too far away and too hot.
13. (mcq) You walk towards a rainbow over a field. What happens?  
   Options: The rainbow moves away as you move, so you never reach the end / You reach the end and touch the colours / The rainbow gets brighter and stays still / The rainbow turns white  
   **Answer:** The rainbow moves away as you move, so you never reach the end. *Why:* Each drop sends each colour to one place. When you move, different drops make your rainbow, so the bow moves with you. Even a rainbow in a spray moves when you move.
14. (mcq) You mix red, yellow, green and blue paint together in a cup. What colour do you get?  
   Options: White, like sunlight / A dark, muddy brown / A bright rainbow / Clear, like water  
   **Answer:** A dark, muddy brown. *Why:* Each paint's pigment absorbs some colours. Mixed together, they absorb nearly all of them, so very little light is reflected. Picking 'white' mixes up paint with light. Coloured lights add up to white, but paints do not.

## Applications
- Scientists split starlight into rainbows to find out what stars and planets are made of.
- Doctors and laboratories use instruments called spectrometers to test blood, water and medicines by their colours.
- Phone, TV and computer screens make every colour, and white, from tiny red, green and blue dots.
- Stage lights at plays and weddings use colour filters to light a scene in one colour.
- Traffic signals use red, amber and green lights that are easy to tell apart.
- Prisms inside binoculars and cameras turn the light so the picture is the right way up.
- The width and colours of a rainbow show how big the raindrops are. Very tiny drops, as in fog, make a pale, almost white bow called a fogbow.
- Painters and dyers in India have used colours like indigo, turmeric and madder for thousands of years.
- Remote controls use infrared light, just past the red end of the rainbow.
- Sunscreen and sunglasses block ultraviolet light, just past the violet end.
- Farmers and scientists use cameras that see infrared to check whether crops are healthy.
- C.V. Raman's discovery about scattered light is used today to identify chemicals, even in old paintings.

## Open questions
- **Is the red you see the same as the red I see?** We can measure light and eyes. But nobody knows how to measure what a colour feels like inside another person.
- **Why do colours feel like anything at all?** Scientists know a lot about eyes and brains. But how brain signals become the feeling of red is one of the biggest puzzles in science, called the hard problem of consciousness.
- **How many women can see extra colours, and how much more do they see?** A few people may have four kinds of colour cells. They are rare and hard to find, and tests are difficult.
- **How does the mantis shrimp's colour vision really work?** They have 12 kinds of colour cells but are poor at telling close colours apart. Scientists have ideas but are not sure.
- **Do our colour words change what we see?** Some studies find small effects. Others find none. Scientists still argue.
- **What colours would we see if our eyes had more kinds of colour cells?** Most people find they cannot imagine a truly new colour. We can only guess from animals that see more.
- **What is light, really, if it acts like a wave and also like tiny packets?** Physicists can predict what light does very well. But what it is underneath is still argued about.
- **Exactly how did the first eyes that could see colour evolve?** Eyes are soft and rarely leave fossils, so the early story is pieced together from living animals and genes.

## Connected maths
- In the Where Does Green End? activity, Amma counted 7 colours, Appa counted 5, the child counted 6 and Akka counted 6. Look at the chart. How many more colours did Amma count than Appa? Which number did most people count? **Amma counted 2 more than Appa. Most people counted 6.**. Amma counted 7 and Appa counted 5. 7 take away 5 is 2. So Amma counted 2 more. Two people counted 6, and only one person counted each of the other numbers. So 6 is the number most people counted. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In Newton's Wheel, you divided a disc into 6 equal slices, one for each colour. What fraction of the disc is red? If you made the wheel with Newton's 7 colours instead, would each slice be bigger or smaller? **Red is one sixth of the disc. With 7 colours, each slice would be smaller, one seventh.**. The disc is cut into 6 equal parts, and red is 1 of them. So red is one sixth. If the same disc is shared between 7 colours, there are more slices, so each slice must be smaller. One seventh is smaller than one sixth. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- Rainbows only appear when the Sun is low. On one monsoon day, the Sun was low enough from 6 o'clock to 9 o'clock in the morning, and from 4 o'clock to 6:30 in the evening. How many hours in all could you have seen a rainbow, if it rained? **5 and a half hours.**. From 6 to 9 in the morning is 3 hours. From 4 to 6:30 in the evening is 2 and a half hours. 3 hours and 2 and a half hours make 5 and a half hours. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In the Biggest Rainbow Indoors challenge, a family measured their rainbow on the wall three times. With the wall 1 metre away, it was 4 cm wide. At 2 metres, it was 8 cm. At 3 metres, it was 12 cm. Look at the table. What is the pattern? How wide might it be at 4 metres? **It gets 4 cm wider for every extra metre. At 4 metres it might be about 16 cm wide.**. 4, 8, 12. Each time the wall is 1 metre further away, the rainbow is 4 cm wider. 12 + 4 = 16. So at 4 metres it might be about 16 cm. It would also be dimmer, because the same light is spread wider. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Red light waves are about 700 tiny steps long. Violet waves are about 400 of the same tiny steps. How many steps longer is a red wave than a violet wave? **300 steps longer.**. 700 take away 400 is 300. So a red wave is 300 tiny steps longer than a violet wave. The tiny steps are called nanometres. A nanometre is so small that a million of them fit in one millimetre. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
