# Festivals and the mela: how India celebrates, and why turmeric turns red
`sci.community.festivals.L3`

**Summary.** Yellow turmeric turns red when it meets soap or lime, and that is how aarathi water and kumkum get their colour. India's festivals are full of science like this, and full of people. At a mela you find stalls of bamboo and cloth, sellers, cooks, toy makers, police and volunteers. Each festival has its own food, clothes and customs, and one January harvest festival has five names across India. Some festivals follow the Sun and come on almost the same date each year. Others follow the Moon and move. India also has three national festivals that mark its history.

**Intro.** *Stir a pinch of turmeric into a little water. It is yellow. Now add a drop of soapy water. Why does yellow turmeric turn red?* Turmeric is yellow in sambar and red in aarathi water, yet it is the same turmeric. In this unit you find out why, with a test of your own. You also visit a mela in your mind, meet the people who make it work, and see what it is built from. You learn the foods, clothes and customs of festivals from Kerala to Assam. You find out why Pongal comes on almost the same date every year, while Diwali and Eid move. And you learn what India's three national festivals remember.

## Terms
- **Festival**: A special day or days that people celebrate together every year, often with special food, clothes, prayers, music and decorations. *e.g.* Onam is a festival of Kerala, with a big feast called a sadya served on a banana leaf.
- **Mela**: A fair: a big gathering in an open ground with stalls, rides, food, shows and many people. Many melas are held at festival time. *e.g.* At the Mysuru Dasara exhibition, families shop at stalls and ride the giant wheel.
- **Stall**: A small shop set up for a short time, often from bamboo poles, wooden boards and cloth or plastic sheets. *e.g.* A bangle seller's stall at a mela has bangles hanging on strings from a bamboo frame.
- **Custom**: Something people of a place have done in the same way for a long time, passed on from older people to younger ones. *e.g.* Drawing a kolam with rice flour at the door every morning is a custom in many Tamil homes.
- **Harvest**: The time when ripe crops, such as rice or sugarcane, are cut and gathered from the fields. *e.g.* Pongal is celebrated after the rice harvest in Tamil Nadu.
- **National festival**: A day the whole country celebrates together, whatever their religion or state, to remember something in the country's history. *e.g.* On Independence Day, 15 August, schools and offices hoist the national flag.
- **Constitution**: The book of rules for how a country is run, and of the rights every person in it has. *e.g.* India's Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950. We remember that day as Republic Day.
- **Solar calendar**: A calendar that follows the Sun. Its year is about 365 days, the time Earth takes to go round the Sun once. *e.g.* Pongal follows the Sun, so it comes on 14 or 15 January almost every year.
- **Lunar calendar**: A calendar whose months follow the Moon, from one new moon to the next, about 29 and a half days. *e.g.* Diwali falls on a new moon night, so its date moves between October and November.
- **Acid**: A substance that tastes sour and turns some plant colours a different shade. Lemon juice, tamarind and vinegar are weak acids. *e.g.* Lemon juice is an acid, so turmeric stays yellow in it.
- **Base**: A substance that is the chemical opposite of an acid. Bases feel slippery, like soap. Baking soda, soap and lime are bases. *e.g.* Soapy water is a base, so turmeric turns red in it.
- **Indicator**: A substance that changes colour to show whether something is an acid or a base. Turmeric and red hibiscus flowers are indicators. *e.g.* A turmeric stain on a white shirt turns red when you wash it with soap, because turmeric is an indicator.
- **Lime (chuna or sunnambu)**: A white paste made from limestone or shells, used in paan and in whitewash. Its chemical name is slaked lime. It is a strong base and burns the eyes. *e.g.* A pinch of sunnambu turns turmeric water deep red.
- **Kolam**: A pattern drawn on the floor at the doorway, made with rice flour or white stone powder. In the North and West a similar pattern, often coloured, is called a rangoli. *e.g.* At Pongal, families draw big kolams with a pot shape in the middle.
- **Volunteer**: A person who helps others without being paid. *e.g.* At a big mela, volunteers at the Lost and Found booth look after lost children until their families come.

## Explanations
### What you find at a mela · *defined*
**Definition:** A mela is a fair held in an open ground for a few days, with stalls, rides, food and shows. Many are held at festival time.

A mela is built quickly and taken down quickly, so it is made of light things. Stalls are bamboo poles tied with rope, with cloth or plastic sheets for a roof. Toys are made of clay, paper, wood, bamboo, tin and plastic. Many kinds of people work there. Sellers run the stalls. Potters, bangle makers and toy makers sell what they made. Cooks fry bajji, vadai and jalebi. Electricians light the rides. Police, first-aid workers and volunteers keep people safe, and cleaners clear the ground every night. People come to shop, eat, ride the giant wheel, watch shows and meet friends.
- e.g. At the Mysuru Dasara exhibition, the stalls stay open for weeks after Dasara.
- e.g. At the Thrissur Pooram festival in Kerala, crowds come to see decorated elephants and fireworks, and stalls sell food and toys.
- e.g. At the Pushkar fair in Rajasthan, camel herders come to buy and sell camels.
### Food, dress and customs at festivals · *defined*
**Definition:** Each festival has its own special foods, clothes and customs, which families pass on from older people to younger ones.

Festival food often uses the crop that has just been harvested. At Pongal, families cook new rice with moong dal, jaggery and ghee in a new pot, and let it boil over as a sign of plenty. At Onam in Kerala, families eat a sadya, a feast of many dishes on a banana leaf. They wear kasavu, white cotton with a gold border, and make a pookalam, a carpet of flower petals. At Ugadi in Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, people eat Ugadi pachadi. It mixes neem flowers, jaggery, tamarind, raw mango, salt and chilli, for all the tastes of the year ahead. At Eid, families make sweet seviyan and visit friends. At Christmas, homes in Kerala hang paper star lanterns.
- e.g. In Assam, families make rice cakes called pitha for Bihu.
- e.g. In Karnataka, people share ellu bella, a mix of sesame, jaggery, coconut and groundnut, at Sankranti.
- e.g. At Durga Puja in Kolkata, people visit pandals, big decorated tents built for the festival.
### One harvest festival with many names · *defined*
**Definition:** In mid-January, people across India celebrate the end of the winter harvest. The festival has different names and customs in different states.

In Tamil Nadu it is Pongal, four days long. On the third day, Mattu Pongal, families wash and decorate their cows and bulls, who help with the farm work. In Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Maharashtra and many other states it is Makara Sankranti. In Punjab it is Lohri, with a bonfire on the night before. In Assam it is Magh Bihu, with feasts and bonfires. In Gujarat it is Uttarayan, and the sky fills with kites. The names differ, but each one thanks the Sun, the land and the animals for the harvest.
- e.g. In Gujarat, families fly kites from their rooftops at Uttarayan.
- e.g. In Punjab, people throw sesame seeds and popcorn into the Lohri bonfire.
- e.g. In Tamil Nadu, the cows wear painted horns and bells on Mattu Pongal.
### Festivals that follow the Sun, and festivals that follow the Moon · *defined*
**Definition:** Some festivals are fixed by the Sun's place in the sky and fall on nearly the same date every year. Others are fixed by the Moon and move.

Our school calendar follows the Sun. Its year is about 365 days, the time Earth takes to go round the Sun. Pongal and Makara Sankranti follow the Sun too, so they come on 14 or 15 January almost every year. Vishu in Kerala and Puthandu, the Tamil New Year, come in mid-April for the same reason. Diwali falls on a new moon night, when no Moon can be seen, so its date moves. Twelve Moon months are only about 354 days, 11 days short of a Sun year. The Hindu calendar adds an extra month about every three years to catch up. That keeps Diwali in October or November. The Islamic calendar adds no extra month. So Eid comes about 11 days earlier each year, and moves through every season.
- e.g. Christmas follows the Sun calendar and is always on 25 December.
- e.g. Eid-ul-Fitr begins when the new crescent Moon is seen at the end of Ramzan.
- e.g. Buddha Purnima falls on a full moon day in April or May.
### Our national festivals · *defined*
**Definition:** National festivals are days the whole country celebrates, whatever their religion or state. India has three.

Independence Day, on 15 August, remembers the day in 1947 when India became free from British rule. The Prime Minister hoists the flag at the Red Fort in Delhi. Republic Day, on 26 January, remembers the day in 1950 when India's Constitution came into force. The Constitution is the book of rules for how India is run, and of every person's rights. Republic Day has a big parade in New Delhi, with floats from each state. Gandhi Jayanti, on 2 October, is the birthday of Mahatma Gandhi, born in 1869 in Porbandar, Gujarat. He led millions of people to struggle for freedom without violence. These three days follow the Sun calendar, so their dates never move.
- e.g. Schools and housing societies hoist the flag and sing the national anthem on Independence Day.
- e.g. On Republic Day, each state sends a decorated float called a tableau to the parade in New Delhi.
- e.g. Many people clean their streets and parks on Gandhi Jayanti.
### Why turmeric turns red · *defined*
**Definition:** Turmeric is an indicator: it stays yellow in acids and in plain water, and turns red-brown in bases.

Turmeric gets its yellow colour from a substance called curcumin. In plain water or in an acid, such as lemon juice, curcumin looks yellow. In a base, such as soap, baking soda or lime, the tiny particles of curcumin change shape a little. The changed particles take in different colours of light, so we see red-brown. Nothing red has been added. Add enough lemon juice, and the particles change back, and the colour turns yellow again. A substance that changes colour like this is called an indicator. That is why a turmeric stain on a shirt turns red when you wash it with soap.
- e.g. A turmeric stain turns red with soap, then fades back to yellow as it dries and rinses.
- e.g. A pinch of lime in turmeric water makes the deep red water used for aarathi.
- e.g. Red hibiscus juice is an indicator too. It turns pink in acids and green in bases.
### Where festival colours come from · *defined*
**Definition:** Festival colours come from plants, minerals and chemicals made in factories.

Kumkum, the red powder worn on the forehead, has long been made by drying turmeric with a little lime. The lime is a base, so the turmeric turns red. A kolam is drawn with rice flour, or with powdered white stone. Rice flour kolams are also food for ants and small birds. Holi colours were once made from flowers, such as the orange flowers of the palash tree, and from turmeric and leaves. Today many colours are made in factories. Some cheap colours have been found to hold harmful chemicals that hurt the skin and eyes. So it is safest to buy colours from a trusted maker, or to make your own from kitchen things.
- e.g. In Tamil Nadu and Kerala, people use turmeric and lime to make kumkum and aarathi water.
- e.g. Families in many cities make Holi colours from beetroot, spinach and turmeric.
- e.g. Rangoli powder sold in shops is often ground stone, coloured with dyes.
### Why do people celebrate festivals? · *debated*
Every group of people in the world has festivals. People who study festivals give different reasons for them, and each reason fits some festivals better than others. Most festivals do more than one of these jobs at once.
- e.g. Pongal marks the harvest, thanks the Sun and brings families together.
- e.g. Republic Day remembers a day in history.
- e.g. Eid-ul-Fitr ends a month of fasting and is a time to give to people in need.
- **A farmer:** Festivals mark the seasons of farm work. We celebrate when the crop is in and the hard work is done, and we thank the Sun, the rain and the animals.
- **A person of faith:** Festivals remember stories of gods, saints and teachers, and help us live the way they taught.
- **A scientist who studies people:** Sharing food, music and work on the same days makes people feel they belong together. That helps a big group trust and help each other.

## Mostly agreed
- Turmeric stays yellow in acids and plain water, and turns red-brown in bases such as soap, baking soda and lime. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with turmeric and kitchen liquids. Chemists have studied the colour change of curcumin, the yellow substance in turmeric, for over a hundred years.
- The January harvest festival is celebrated in most of India under different names. *How we know:* Pongal, Makara Sankranti, Lohri, Magh Bihu and Uttarayan all fall in mid-January and all celebrate the winter harvest.
- Festivals that follow the Sun fall on nearly the same date each year. Festivals that follow only the Moon move by about 11 days each year. *How we know:* Pongal falls on 14 or 15 January every year. Eid-ul-Fitr comes about 11 days earlier each year, because twelve Moon months are about 354 days.
- India's national festivals are Independence Day on 15 August, Republic Day on 26 January and Gandhi Jayanti on 2 October. *How we know:* India became independent on 15 August 1947. The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950. Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869.

## How we found out
**Robert Boyle in England, Antoine and Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier in Paris, and Humphry Davy in London, 1664 (Boyle's book on colours), the 1770s and 1780s (Lavoisier's oxygen idea), and 1810 (Davy's work on the acid from salt).** In 1664, Robert Boyle in England wrote that some plant juices change colour with sour things and with soapy things. He used these colour changes to sort substances into acids and alkalis, which we now call bases. About a hundred years later, in Paris, Antoine Lavoisier and his wife Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier worked together in their laboratory. She kept the records, drew the equipment and translated English science papers for him. Lavoisier noticed that every acid he made had oxygen in it. So he decided that oxygen is what makes a substance an acid. He even named the gas oxygen from Greek words meaning 'acid maker'. In 1810, Humphry Davy in London tested the strong acid made from salt. He showed that it is made of hydrogen and chlorine, with no oxygen at all. One acid without oxygen was enough to show that Lavoisier's rule was wrong. Later chemists found that acids share hydrogen instead. The acid Davy studied is the same acid your stomach makes to break down food.  
*What people believed before:* Lavoisier thought every acid must contain oxygen, and named oxygen 'acid maker'. Davy showed that the acid made from salt contains only hydrogen and chlorine, so oxygen is not what makes an acid.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Turmeric turns red because the soap has burnt it or spoilt it.** (A common first guess among children; *rejected*)  
  For: The red looks like a stain, and red-brown can look like something burnt.  
  Against: Add lemon juice to the red turmeric, and it turns yellow again. Something burnt or spoilt cannot be changed back. The curcumin particles only change shape in a base, and change back in an acid.
- **Makara Sankranti is the day the Sun starts moving north in the sky.** (A common saying, and the meaning of the word Uttarayan; *rejected*)  
  For: About 1,700 years ago the two days nearly fell together, so the saying was close to true then.  
  Against: Today the Sun starts moving north on about 21 or 22 December, the shortest day of the year. Earth's tilted axis slowly wobbles, so the two dates have drifted about 24 days apart.
- **Colours from plants are always safe, and colours from factories are always harmful.** (A common idea at Holi time; *rejected*)  
  For: Some cheap factory colours do contain harmful chemicals. Turmeric and beetroot are foods.  
  Against: Lime is natural, but it is a strong base and burns the eyes. Some plants make the skin itch. Many factory colours are tested and safe. What matters is what the colour is, not where it came from.
- **Every festival belongs to one religion.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Many festivals, such as Diwali, Eid and Christmas, began in one religion and tell its stories.  
  Against: Harvest festivals and national festivals belong to everyone who lives there. And many people join their neighbours' festivals, sharing seviyan at Eid or cake at Christmas.

## Roots
We have turned turmeric red and yellow again, and learned how festivals mark the Sun, the Moon and the harvest. Now we ask what the turmeric tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Turmeric water turned red with soap, and yellow again with lemon juice. You added no red colour, and took nothing out. Turmeric shows yellow among acids and red among bases. So the colour you see belongs to turmeric and its surroundings together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We give each thing one fixed colour, as if 'turmeric is yellow' says everything about it. The test shows that what a thing looks like also depends on what it is with.  
*Evidence:* In the Turmeric Test, the same turmeric water turned red-brown with soap and baking soda, stayed yellow with lemon, tamarind, salt and sugar, and went back to yellow when lemon was added to a red bowl.

- *who am i:* Like turmeric, you show different sides with different people: one way with your grandmother, another way with your friends, and you are still you.
- *what is life:* Curd bacteria make the acid that makes curd sour. Living things change the world around them, and that changes how other things look and act.
- *what is this world:* A world where yellow turmeric and white lime together make red is a world where meetings make new things.

Turmeric on its own looks yellow. Lime on its own looks white. Put them together, and a red appears that neither had alone. A festival works the same way. Rice, jaggery, a pot, the Sun and many people are each ordinary on their own. Brought together on one morning, they make Pongal.

- **Jain teaching of anekantavada (many-sidedness), and the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Several blind men touch one elephant. One feels the trunk and says it is a rope. One feels the leg and says it is a pillar. Each is partly right, and none sees the whole. (Someone who has only seen turmeric in sambar would say it is yellow. Someone who has only seen aarathi water would say it is red. Each is partly right. The Turmeric Test shows both sides of one turmeric.)
- **Tao Te Ching 2:** Long and short shape each other. High and low lean on each other. Each is what it is because of the other. (Turmeric looks red only next to a base, and yellow next to an acid. What it looks like depends on what it is with.)
- Science (settled): Curcumin, the yellow substance in turmeric, changes shape in a base and looks red-brown. In an acid it changes back.
- Science (open): How festivals began, and why every human group has them, is still studied and argued about.
- Other view, A chemist who keeps to the facts: The colour change is curcumin gaining and losing tiny particles called hydrogen ions. That is all there is to it. Comparing it with people, or with festivals, is a nice picture, but it tells us nothing new about turmeric.

- Ask: Do you act differently with different people? Are you still the same person?

## Story: The Red Water at the Door
*The story is made up. The science is true: turmeric turns red in a base such as lime or soap, and yellow again in an acid such as lemon juice. Lime paste is a strong base and must be kept away from the eyes.*

It was the morning of Thai Pongal in Chennai. Meenakshi was eight, and she had been awake since five. Her grandmother, Paati, had already drawn a big kolam at the door of their flat. It had a pot in the middle, with lines of steam curling out of it. Meenakshi had filled in the steam with red kolam powder.

"Arjun is coming today," said Paati. "And his amma, your Athai. They have been on the bus all night from Bengaluru. We will welcome them with aarathi." Arjun was Meenakshi's cousin. He was seven, and he thought he knew everything.

Paati put a brass plate on the floor. She poured in a glass of water and stirred in a big spoon of turmeric. The water turned cloudy yellow, like sambar without the dal. Then Paati opened her old paan box. In one corner was a little white paste.

"That is sunnambu," said Paati. "Lime. It goes in paan. It is strong, so you must never touch it or let it near your eyes." She took a tiny bit on the tip of a matchstick and swirled it in the plate. A red cloud spread through the yellow water. In a few seconds the whole plate was deep red, like a hibiscus flower.

The doorbell rang. There stood Athai with a big bag, and Arjun, sleepy, with his hair standing up on one side. Paati lifted the plate and moved it in circles in front of them, three times. Then she dipped her thumb in the red water and touched it to their foreheads. "Now you are home," she said.

Arjun looked at the plate. "How did you make it red?" he asked. "Did you put in red colour?"

"No," said Meenakshi. "I watched. It was yellow turmeric, and then Paati added lime, and it went red."

"That is impossible," said Arjun. "Yellow cannot just turn into red. She must have put red powder in when you weren't looking."

Meenakshi thought about it. Then she remembered something. Last week she had dropped sambar on her white school shirt. When Amma scrubbed it with soap, the yellow stain went red. Amma had laughed and said, "Turmeric does that."

"I can show you without any lime," said Meenakshi. "Come." She took Arjun into the kitchen. She put a pinch of turmeric in a steel bowl with a little water. It was yellow. She rubbed the bar of soap in a cup of water until it was cloudy. Then she let Arjun pour in one spoon of the soapy water himself.

The yellow turned orange, then red. Arjun stared. "There is no red in soap," he said slowly. "Soap is white."

"So the red was waiting in the turmeric," said Meenakshi. "The soap only helped it show."

Athai had come in behind them. She was cutting lemons for the lemon rice. "Try this," she said, and handed Meenakshi half a lemon.

Meenakshi squeezed the lemon over the red bowl. Drop by drop, the red faded. The water turned orange, and then yellow again, the same yellow as at the start.

"It went back!" said Arjun. "So it isn't burnt or spoilt. If you burn a roti, it never comes back."

"Lemon is sour," said Athai. "Sour things like lemon and tamarind are called acids. Soap and lime are the opposite. They are called bases. Turmeric is yellow with acids and red with bases. Your great-grandmother made her kumkum this way, by drying turmeric with lime."

Arjun went quiet. Then he ran to the kitchen shelf. "What about salt? What about sugar? What about curd?" he asked. "Can we test all of them?"

"After the pongal," said Paati, laughing. "The pot is on the stove."

They all crowded onto the balcony, where Paati had put a new clay pot on a small gas stove. Inside, milk and new rice were bubbling. Sugarcane stood against the wall, and the sun was coming up over the buildings. Everyone watched the white foam climb up the pot. It reached the top, and then it spilled over the side.

"Pongalo pongal!" they all shouted, as the neighbours shouted it on their balconies too.

That afternoon, Meenakshi and Arjun made a row of bowls on the kitchen floor, each with a pinch of turmeric. Into one went salt water. Into one went sugar water, then curd, then tamarind water. Last came baking soda, from the tin Athai used for bajji. Only the baking soda bowl turned red, like the soap.

"Write it down," said Meenakshi, handing Arjun a pencil. "That is what scientists do." And for once, Arjun did exactly what she said.

**For the parent:** For the parent: turmeric's yellow colour comes from curcumin. In a base, such as lime, soap or baking soda, curcumin particles change shape a little and look red-brown. In an acid, such as lemon juice, they change back and look yellow. Nothing red is ever added, and the change can go back and forth. Salt, sugar and curd are not bases, so turmeric stays yellow in them.

- Ask: Why did Arjun think Paati had put red powder in the water?
- Ask: How did Meenakshi show him that the red came from the turmeric and the soap?
- Ask: Why did the lemon juice make Arjun sure the turmeric was not burnt or spoilt?
- Ask: Which bowls stayed yellow in the afternoon test, and which one turned red?
- Ask: Why did Paati say never to let lime near your eyes?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Turmeric Test: which kitchen liquids turn turmeric red? (30 min)
- **question:** Which liquids from our kitchen turn turmeric water red, and which leave it yellow? Can the red be turned back?
- **predict:** Look at the eight liquids. Which ones do you think will turn the turmeric red? Why do you think so? Then guess: if a bowl turns red, can lemon juice make it yellow again, and why?
- **fair test:** Every bowl gets the same amount of turmeric water and the same spoonful of liquid. We wait the same one minute before we look. One bowl gets plain water, so we can see what no change looks like. We look at every bowl on the same white paper, in the same light.
- **safety:** Soapy water and baking soda water sting the eyes, so keep hands away from the face and wash them afterwards. Do not taste the test liquids. Turmeric stains clothes and counters, so wear old clothes and work on an old cloth or a steel plate.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw your row of bowls in colour, and write 'acid', 'base' or 'neither' under each one.
- **results table:** Liquid | My guess: red or yellow? | Colour after 1 minute | Acid, base or neither? | Lemon added: what happened?
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir half a teaspoon of turmeric into one cup of water. 2. Pour 2 tablespoons into each of the 8 bowls. 3. Stand the bowls in a row on the white paper.  
  *Parent says:* This is turmeric water. It stays a bit cloudy, because turmeric does not dissolve fully. That is fine. Look at the colour of every bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Stir turmeric into water. Pour some into each bowl. What colour is it?
  - Note: The water looks cloudy yellow, and some turmeric settles at the bottom. Stir each bowl before you add a liquid.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the test liquids, about 3 tablespoons each. 2. Salt, sugar and baking soda: half a teaspoon each in 3 tablespoons of water. 3. Soapy water: rub the soap bar in water for 10 seconds. 4. Curd: one spoon of curd stirred into 2 spoons of water.  
  *Parent says:* These are the eight liquids we will test. Lemon and tamarind are sour. Soap feels slippery. Look at each one and smell it, but do not taste.  
  *Child mode:* Help make the eight liquids. Smell them, but do not taste.
  - Note: Lemon juice can be squeezed straight into a cup. Tamarind water is a small piece of tamarind soaked in warm water for 5 minutes. No tamarind? Leave it out and test seven liquids.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a small paper label in front of each bowl. 2. Ask your child to guess red or yellow for each. 3. Ask why. 4. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which of these liquids do you think will turn the turmeric red? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess for each bowl: red or yellow? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will turn red? (good: Because soap turned a turmeric stain red.; Because it is slippery, like soap.; Because the lemon is sour and the soap is not.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Any reason is good if your child says it.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add one teaspoon of the first liquid to its bowl. 2. Stir once. 3. Wait one minute. 4. Write the colour. 5. Go on to the next bowl.  
  *Parent says:* One teaspoon in, one stir, then we wait a whole minute. Compare it with the plain water bowl. Has it changed?  
  *Child mode:* Add one spoon. Stir. Wait one minute. Is it red or yellow?
  - Note: Soapy water and baking soda water turn the turmeric orange-red or red-brown. If the baking soda bowl is only orange, add a pinch of dry baking soda and stir. Lemon, tamarind, curd, salt and sugar leave it yellow, like the plain water. Lemon may make it a little paler.
  - Check: Which bowl did you keep with only plain water added? → **One bowl, so we can see what no change looks like**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Squeeze lemon juice into the reddest bowl, a few drops at a time. 2. Stir after each few drops. 3. Keep going until the colour changes.  
  *Parent says:* Before we start, what do you think will happen? Now watch closely as I add lemon, drop by drop.  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze lemon into the red bowl, a few drops at a time. Watch!
  - Note: The red fades to orange and then back to yellow. You may need a whole teaspoon or more of lemon juice. This shows the turmeric was not burnt or spoilt. The change goes both ways.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the results. Ask for a claim, the evidence and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Which liquids turned the turmeric red? What did they have in common? What did the lemon do?  
  *Child mode:* Which ones went red? How are they alike? What did lemon do?
  - Ask: What is the same about the liquids that turned it red? (good: Soap and baking soda are both slippery when wet.; They are not sour.; They are bases.)
  - Ask: What does the lemon test show? (good: The red can go back to yellow, so the turmeric was not spoilt.; Lemon is an acid, and it undoes what the base did.)
### e2 · The Hibiscus Test: is a festival flower an indicator too? (25 min)
- **question:** Does red hibiscus juice change colour with lemon juice and with soap, the way turmeric does?
- **predict:** Guess what colour hibiscus juice will turn with lemon, and what colour with soapy water. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same hibiscus juice in three bowls, the same spoonful of liquid, and wait the same one minute. Keep one bowl with plain water, to compare.
- **safety:** Keep soapy water away from the eyes. Wash hands after. Do not drink any of the juices.
- **record:** Draw the three tumblers in colour. Write one sentence on how hibiscus is like turmeric, and one on how it is different.
- **results table:** Liquid added | My guess | Colour after 1 minute | Same as turmeric, or different?
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tear the petals of two flowers into small pieces. 2. Put them in half a cup of warm water. 3. Squeeze and rub them with your fingers for 3 minutes. 4. Strain the juice.  
  *Parent says:* Rub the petals hard. The colour comes out into the water. What colour is the juice?  
  *Child mode:* Tear the petals. Rub them in warm water. What colour is the water?
  - Note: The juice is pale purple or dull red. The darker it is, the clearer the result. If it is very pale, add more petals.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the juice into three tumblers. 2. Ask your child to guess the colour with lemon and with soap, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Turmeric went red with soap. What do you think hibiscus will do with soap? And with lemon? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess: what colour with lemon? What colour with soap? Why?
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: I think it will go red like turmeric.; Lemon and soap did different things to turmeric, so maybe they will here too.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add one teaspoon of lemon juice to the first tumbler. 2. Add one teaspoon of soapy water to the second. 3. Add plain water to the third. 4. Wait one minute and compare.  
  *Parent says:* Lemon in the first, soap in the second, plain water in the third. Now we wait one minute.  
  *Child mode:* Lemon in one. Soap in one. Plain water in one. Wait. Look!

- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare with the turmeric results.  
  *Parent says:* Turmeric changed colour only with the base. Hibiscus changed with both. What does that tell you?  
  *Child mode:* Turmeric changed with soap only. Hibiscus changed with both. Why might that be?
  - Ask: Which is the better indicator for telling an acid from plain water? (good: Hibiscus, because it turns pink in acid, and turmeric stays yellow in acid and in water.)

### Festivals and the Turmeric Test (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (match) Match each festival to its state.  
   **Answer:** Pongal → Tamil Nadu; Onam → Kerala; Lohri → Punjab; Magh Bihu → Assam; Uttarayan → Gujarat. *Why:* Each of these is a festival of one state. A strong answer also notices that four of them are the January harvest festival.

   **Answer:** People eat festival food, ride the giant wheel, watch shows, meet friends and relatives, and buy things for the home.. *Why:* A strong answer names three different kinds of things, such as eating, riding and meeting people.
4. (fill) A thin pattern drawn at the door with rice flour is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['kolam']. *Why:* A kolam is drawn with rice flour or white stone powder. Rice-flour kolams also feed ants and small birds.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. New moons fall on different dates of our Sun calendar each year, so the festival moves. Diwali is an example.

   **Answer:** ['Pour turmeric water into each bowl', 'Add one spoon of a liquid', 'Wait one minute and look at the colour', 'Write the colour in the table']. *Why:* The turmeric water goes in first, then the liquid. Waiting the same minute for every bowl makes the test fair.

### Think Like a Chemist and a Calendar Maker (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Some people say Makara Sankranti is the day the Sun starts moving north in the sky. Today that happens on about 21 or 22 December. How could both be true about 1,700 years ago, but not today?

## Open questions
- **When and where did people first notice that turmeric turns red with lime?** Turmeric and lime have been used in India for thousands of years, but nobody wrote down who first saw the change.

- **Which was the first festival people ever celebrated?** Festivals are much older than writing, so no record of the first one is left.
- **Is there any one reason why some plant colours, like hibiscus, change with acids and others, like spinach green, hardly do?** Chemists know how many plant colours change, but why plants make colours that change in this way is still studied.

## Connected maths
- Twelve Moon months are about 354 days. A Sun year is about 365 days. How many days shorter is the Moon year? **11 days.**. 365 minus 354 is 11. Count up from 354: 6 more makes 360, and 5 more makes 365. 6 and 5 make 11. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Eid moves about 11 days earlier every year. About how many years does it take to move 33 days, more than a whole month? **3 years.**. Each year it moves 11 days. 11, 22, 33: that is 3 years. So in 3 years Eid moves back by more than a month. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Turmeric Test, Lakshmi tested 8 liquids. 2 turned red. Write the fraction that turned red, and the fraction that stayed yellow. **2 out of 8, which is 1 quarter, turned red. 6 out of 8, which is 3 quarters, stayed yellow.**. 2 out of 8 is the same as 1 out of 4, a quarter. The rest, 6 out of 8, is 3 out of 4. Most kitchen liquids were not bases. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- India's Constitution came into force in 1950. How many years ago was that, this year? How old was Gandhi in 1947, if he was born in October 1869 and India became free in August 1947? **This year minus 1950. For 2026, that is 76 years. Gandhi was 77 years old in August 1947.**. From 1869 to 1947 is 78 years. But in August 1947 he had not yet had his birthday in October, so he was still 77. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- Pongal is on 14 January and Republic Day is on 26 January. How many days after Pongal is Republic Day? Predict: will the gap be the same next year? Why? **12 days. Yes, the gap stays about the same, because both follow the Sun calendar.**. 26 minus 14 is 12. Both dates are fixed by the Sun, so they stay together. If Pongal falls on 15 January in some year, the gap is 11. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)

- NCERT Our Wondrous World Class 3, Chapter 2 Going to the Mela and Chapter 3 Celebrating Festivals (app/content/ncert/sci-L3-02.json, sci-L3-03.json): coverage only, no text copied: the mela, its people and safety, seasonal festivals and foods, Moon calendars
- NIOS Environmental Studies Level A, Lesson 3 Our Festivals (app/content/nios/sci-L3-03.json): coverage only: festivals of many religions, Makara Sankranti under many names, national festivals, natural colours
- Priyadarsini, 'The Chemistry of Curcumin: From Extraction to Therapeutic Agent', Molecules 19, 2014 (curcumin is yellow in acid and neutral solution and turns red above about pH 8)
- Boyle, Experiments and Considerations Touching Colours, London, 1664 (plant juices that change colour with acids and alkalis)
- Lavoisier, Traité élémentaire de chimie, Paris, 1789 (oxygen as the 'acid former')
- Davy, 'On the oxymuriatic acid, and its combinations', Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society 100, 1810 (chlorine is an element, and muriatic acid contains no oxygen)
- NCERT Science Class 7, Acids, Bases and Salts (turmeric and China rose as indicators)
- Wikipedia, Kumkuma (kumkum made from turmeric with slaked lime)
- Government of India, national festivals: Independence Day 15 August 1947, Republic Day 26 January 1950, Gandhi Jayanti 2 October (born 1869, Porbandar)
- Precession of the equinoxes: about 1 degree in 72 years, so sidereal Makara Sankranti and the December solstice drift apart by about one day every 70 years
