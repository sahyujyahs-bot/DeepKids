# What plants need, and how they grow towards the light
`sci.plants.what-plants-need.L2`

**Summary.** Turn a pot of young sprouts around, and by the next day they have bent back towards the window. A plant cannot walk to the light, so they grow towards it. Plants need light, water, air, warmth and soil to live and grow. Your child grows rai (mustard) sprouts by a window, turns one cup round, and watches what the sprouts do.

**Intro.** *Look at a plant that lives indoors, near a window. Which way are the leaves and stems leaning?* Most plants indoors lean towards the nearest window. Nobody pulled them that way. The plant grew that way. What happens if we turn the pot round, so the plant leans away from the window? In this unit we find out. We also look at the five things a plant needs: light, water, air, warmth and soil.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name five things a plant needs: light, water, air, warmth and soil.
1. By the end I can say what young sprouts do when the light comes from one side: they bend towards it.
2. By the end I can say what happened when we turned the cup round, and why.
3. By the end I can say that seeds can sprout without soil, but plants need soil, or minerals, to keep growing.
4. By the end I can say why roots need air as well as water.
5. By the end I can make a guess, give a reason, test it, and say what I saw.

## Terms
- **Phototropism**: Phototropism is a plant growing towards light. 'Photo' means light, and 'tropism' means turning. *e.g.* Rai sprouts on a windowsill bend towards the window. That is phototropism.
- **Shoot**: The shoot is the part of a young plant that grows up out of the seed: the stem and the first leaves. *e.g.* A rai sprout's shoot is a thin white or green stem with two small leaves on top.
- **Germinate**: A seed germinates when they start to grow. First a tiny root comes out, then a shoot. *e.g.* Rai seeds on wet tissue germinate in one or two days in warm weather.
- **Soil**: Soil is the top layer of the ground. It is made of tiny bits of rock, bits of dead plants and animals, water and air. *e.g.* The soil in a flower pot holds the plant up and holds water for the roots.
- **Minerals**: Minerals are tiny bits from rocks that dissolve in water. Plants take them in through their roots to stay healthy. *e.g.* Farmers add manure to a field, because it puts minerals back into the soil.

## Explanations
### Plants grow towards light · *defined*
**Definition:** A young shoot bends towards the brightest light. This is called phototropism.

A plant makes their food in their green leaves, using light. So getting the leaves into light matters a great deal. A shoot senses where the light is coming from. Then the side of the stem away from the light grows a little faster than the side facing it. A stem that grows faster on one side bends, just as a line of marching children curves if the outside row takes bigger steps. Turn the plant round, and the bend starts again the other way. The shoot tip is the part that senses the light best.
- e.g. Rai sprouts by a window bend towards the glass within a day.
- e.g. A money plant in a dim room grows long stems towards the window.
- e.g. Young sunflower plants turn their heads east to west through the day, following the Sun.
### Plants need water · *defined*
**Definition:** Every plant needs water to live and grow. Roots take in water from the soil.

Water keeps a plant's stems and leaves firm. It carries minerals up from the roots. The leaves use water, with light and air, to make food. A plant short of water wilts: the leaves go soft and droop. Different plants need different amounts. A cactus needs very little. Rice grows in fields full of water.
- e.g. A tulsi plant droops on a hot afternoon and stands up again after a drink.
- e.g. Rice fields in Thanjavur and in Assam are kept flooded while the rice grows.
- e.g. A cactus in Rajasthan can go weeks without rain.
### Plants need air · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants need air for two jobs. Leaves take in a gas from the air called carbon dioxide to make food. Every part of a plant, roots too, takes in oxygen from the air to breathe.

Air is a mix of gases. Leaves take in carbon dioxide through tiny holes, too small to see. With light and water, they turn it into food. Roots breathe too. They take in oxygen from the air in the gaps between bits of soil. If soil stays soaking wet for days, water fills those gaps. Then the roots cannot get air, and they can rot. That is why most pots have a hole at the bottom.
- e.g. Bubbles rise when you pour water on dry soil. That is the air from the gaps in the soil.
- e.g. Flower pots have a hole in the bottom so extra water can drain away.
- e.g. Mangrove trees in the Sundarbans grow roots that stick up out of the mud, to take in air.
### Plants need warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants grow only when it is warm enough for them. Each kind of plant has the warmth they grow best in.

In cold weather, seeds germinate slowly, and plants grow slowly. Frost, which is frozen dew, can kill the leaves of many plants. Too much heat can dry a plant out. Different plants like different warmth. Rice and bananas like hot, wet weather. Mustard and wheat are sown in the cool winter months in the north of India. Apples grow in cold hill places like Himachal Pradesh and Kashmir.
- e.g. Rai seeds sprout in a day or two in a warm Chennai kitchen. In a cold Delhi winter they can take several days longer.
- e.g. Mustard fields in Punjab and Rajasthan turn yellow with flowers in winter.
- e.g. Coconut palms grow along the warm coasts of Kerala and Goa, but not high in the Himalayas.
### Plants need soil, or what is in it · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil holds a plant up, holds water and air for the roots, and gives the plant minerals.

A seed carries a small store of food inside. So a seed can germinate on wet tissue, with no soil at all. But the store runs out after some days. To keep growing, a plant needs minerals, which come from the soil. Plants do not eat soil. They make their food in their leaves. Farmers can even grow plants with their roots in water, if they add minerals to the water. This is called hydroponics.
- e.g. Rai seeds sprout on wet tissue as well as in soil.
- e.g. A money plant can live for months in a bottle of water, getting minerals from the water.
- e.g. Some farms in Indian cities grow lettuce in pipes of water with minerals, without soil.
### Does a sprout decide to turn? · *debated*
Scientists agree on what happens. Light falls on one side of the shoot. A messenger chemical called auxin moves to the shady side. There it makes the stem grow faster, so the stem bends. People describe this in different ways.
- e.g. A rai sprout turned away from the window bends back within a day.
- e.g. A potato sprouting in a dark cupboard grows a long pale shoot towards a crack of light.
- **Scientists who study how plants sense and act:** A sprout feels where the light is and grows towards it. Sensing, then acting on what you sensed, is what a mind does. A sprout does this with chemicals and cells, not a brain.
- **Many other plant scientists:** The bend is a response, set off by light and carried out by auxin. The sprout does the same thing every time, so they do not call it a decision.

## Mostly agreed
- Young shoots of most plants grow towards light. *How we know:* Seedlings on a windowsill bend towards the window. Charles and Francis Darwin showed in 1880 that the shoot tip senses the light. Since then, thousands of experiments have shown the same thing.
- Plants need light, water, air, a suitable warmth and minerals to grow. *How we know:* A plant missing any one of these grows weak or dies. Farmers and gardeners everywhere see this, and scientists have tested each one.
- Plants make their food in their leaves. They do not eat soil. *How we know:* A plant grows heavy, yet the soil in the pot hardly gets lighter. Plants grow in water with minerals, with no soil at all.

## How we found out
**Charles Darwin and his son Francis Darwin, in England (1880); Frits Went, a Dutch scientist (1926), The Darwins published their tests in 1880, in their book The Power of Movement in Plants. Went's agar test was in 1926..** People had long seen plants bend towards light. Many thought the part of the stem that bends is the part that senses the light. In 1880, Charles Darwin and his son Francis, in England, tested this. They grew canary grass seedlings, which have a thin shoot with a pointed tip. They put tiny black caps over the tips of some shoots, so no light could reach the tips. Those seedlings hardly bent at all. When the caps let light through, the seedlings bent towards the light as usual. When the tip was in the light but the lower stem was covered, the shoots still bent. So the tip senses the light, and something travels down from the tip to the part that bends. Nobody knew what that something was. In 1926, a young Dutch scientist, Frits Went, cut off shoot tips and stood them on small blocks of agar. Agar is the seaweed jelly sold in India as China grass. A block that had held tips made a cut shoot bend, all by itself. The messenger was a chemical. Today it is called auxin.  
*What people believed before:* That the part of the stem that bends is the part that senses the light. The Darwins showed that the tip senses it, and a message travels down to the part that bends.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Plants eat soil, so the soil is their food.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants grow in soil, and they grow badly in poor soil.  
  Against: Plants make their food in their leaves, from air and water, using light. From the soil they take water and small amounts of minerals. Plants can grow in water with minerals added and no soil at all.
- **A sunflower in full bloom turns to follow the Sun all day.** (A common belief, in many countries; *rejected*)  
  For: Young sunflower plants really do turn their heads from east to west during the day, and back again at night.  
  Against: Once the flowers open, most sunflowers stop turning and face east. In 2016, Stacey Harmer and her team in the United States showed that young sunflowers time their turning with an inside clock. East-facing flowers warm up faster in the morning, and bees visit them more.
- **A plant bends towards the window because the light pulls the stem.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: The stem does end up pointing at the light, as if pulled.  
  Against: Light does not pull anything. The stem bends because the shady side grows faster than the sunny side. A pulled stem would bend at once. A growing stem takes hours, and the bend stays.

## Roots
We have seen rai sprouts turn back towards the window after we turned their cup. Now we ask what that tells us about plants, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** When we turned cup A round, the rai sprouts bent back to the window by the next day. A plant cannot walk to the light, so they move by growing. Every bend stays in the stem. So a plant's shape shows where their light has come from.  
*Habit it breaks:* We split living things into those who move, like humans and other animals, and those who stay still, like plants. But plants move all the time, by growing, too slowly for us to see.  
*Evidence:* In the turning cup test, cup A's sprouts curved back towards the window overnight while cup B stayed as it was. In the Straight or Bent project, the tray turned every day grew upright, and the tray never turned leaned to the window.

- *who am i:* Your body grows too, and your shape also shows how you have lived: the hand you write with, the way you stand.
- *what is life:* Being alive means sensing what is around you and acting on it. The rai sprouts felt where the light was and grew towards it.
- *what is this world:* The world gives every plant light, water, air, warmth and soil. Each plant grows into a shape that fits the place they found.

A rai seed holds a tiny plant. Around that plant is the whole world: sunlight from far away, water from the tap and the rain, air from the window, warmth from the day, and soil from the ground. The sprout senses all of this and grows to meet the world. So each sprout's shape is made by the seed and the world together.

- **Acharanga Sutra, Book 1, Lecture 1, Lesson 5 (a Jain scripture):** As a human is born, so is a plant born. As a human grows, so does a plant grow. As a human needs food, so does a plant need food. (Our sprouts needed light, water, air, warmth and soil, as we need food, water, air and warmth. Jain teachers saw plants as living beings with needs, like us.)
- **Tao Te Ching 76:** Plants are born soft and supple. When they die, they are dry and brittle. (Only a young, soft, growing shoot can bend towards the light. The bend in our rai sprouts happened near the tip, where the stem was youngest and still growing.)
- Science (settled): Plants have light sensors called phototropins, mostly sensitive to blue light. These tell a shoot which side the light comes from.
- Science (open): Plants feel where the light is and act on it. What that feeling is like for a plant, nobody knows yet.
- Other view, Many plant scientists: Many plant scientists say a sprout bends because light moves a chemical, auxin, to the shady side, and that makes those cells grow longer. It happens the same way every time, so they call it a response, with no choosing.
- Practice, Grow towards the light (3 min): Stand in the middle of the room, facing away from the window. Keep your feet still, like roots. Close your eyes. Can you feel which side is brighter or warmer? Now lean your top half towards the window, as slowly as you can. Take five slow breaths while you turn. Open your eyes. Did you face the light?
- Ask: A plant cannot walk out of a dark corner. What else can a plant do about the dark?
- Ask: Your shape and a plant's shape both show how you have lived. What does your body show about your life?
- Ask: If you could only move by growing, what would you grow towards?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* We turned the cup, and the sprouts bent back to the window. How did they know where the window was? (hint: Think about which part of the sprout faced the light. What can you sense without eyes?)
- *deep:* A plant moves by growing. Can you move by growing? (hint: Think about your hair, your nails, and how tall you were last year.)
- *big:* If a plant could walk, would they still need to bend towards light? (hint: Animals walk to their food. Plants grow towards theirs. Which way is better? Is one better?)
- *big:* Every plant on a windowsill leans the same way. What would plants look like on a planet with two Suns? (hint: Think about what the sprouts did when the light came from one side, and then from the other.)
- *big:* Light, water, air, warmth and soil all come from outside the plant. So what part of a plant is only the plant? (hint: Think about the seed. What did the seed bring with them?)

## Story: Meenakshi Turns the Pot
*Meenakshi, Anu and Appa are made up. Mustard sprouts really do bend towards a window, and really bend back within about a day when the pot is turned.*

Meenakshi lived in a flat in Chennai, on the fifth floor. She was seven. Her little sister Anu was five. Their kitchen had one small window, above the sink.

One morning, Meenakshi took a pinch of kadugu, the small black mustard seeds, from the masala box. She sowed them in an old steel tumbler full of soil. She pressed the seeds into the soil with her finger. She sprinkled water on top. Then she put the tumbler on the kitchen counter, beside the window.

Every morning she gave the soil one spoon of water. On the second day, tiny shoots came up. By the fourth day, each one had two small round leaves at the top.

On the fifth day, Appa was making coffee. He looked at the tumbler. 'These mustard plants are all falling over,' he said. 'The fan must have blown them.'

Meenakshi looked. All the little shoots were leaning the same way. They were not falling. They were leaning towards the window.

'The fan blows round and round, Appa,' she said. 'But they are all leaning one way. They are leaning to the window.'

'Plants cannot move,' said Appa. 'They stay where they are planted.'

Meenakshi thought about this. Then she had an idea. She put a small red bindi on the side of the tumbler that faced the window. Then she turned the tumbler round. Now the bindi faced the room, and all the shoots leaned towards the stove.

'What are you doing?' asked Anu.

'I am asking the mustard sprouts a question,' said Meenakshi. 'If they were blown by the fan, they will stay like this. If they lean to the light, they will turn back.'

'Which one will it be?' asked Anu.

'I don't know yet,' said Meenakshi. 'That's why I'm asking.'

That night, Anu peeped at the tumbler before bed. The shoots still leaned towards the stove. 'Nothing happened,' she said.

'Plants are slow,' said Meenakshi. 'Wait till morning.'

In the morning, Meenakshi ran to the kitchen. The shoots had changed. Each one had a little bend near the top. The bottom of each stem still leaned towards the stove. But the top had curved round, and the leaves pointed at the window again.

'Appa!' she called. 'Come and see!'

Appa came with his coffee. He looked at the bindi, which still faced the room. He looked at the bent shoots. 'Nobody touched them?' he asked.

'Nobody,' said Meenakshi. 'They did it themselves, in one night. A plant cannot walk to the window. So they grow towards it.'

'But how?' said Appa. 'They have no legs, and no eyes.'

'I think the side away from the window grew a bit more,' said Meenakshi. 'If one side of you grows longer, you bend. Look, the bend is right at the top, where the shoot is growing.'

Appa put his cup down. 'So the plant was moving the whole time,' he said. 'Just too slowly for me to see.'

'Can I turn it now?' asked Anu.

'Yes,' said Meenakshi. 'Turn it all the way round. And tomorrow, we'll see if the mustard turns too.'

Anu turned the tumbler very carefully. Then she leaned her own head towards the window, slowly, slowly, like a mustard shoot. Meenakshi laughed, and leaned with him.

**For the parent:** Meenakshi works out that the shoots were not blown over. When the light came from the other side, they bent back to it, by growing. A plant moves by growing.

- Ask: Appa thought the fan blew the plants over. How did Meenakshi's test show this was not right?
- Ask: Why did Meenakshi put a bindi on the tumbler?
- Ask: Why did nothing seem to happen by bedtime?
- Ask: What do you think the mustard shoots felt when the light came from behind them?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Turning Cup: will the sprouts turn back? (25 min, 6 days)
- **question:** Rai sprouts by a window lean towards the light. If we turn the cup round, what will the sprouts do?
- **predict:** Guess first. After we turn the cup round, will the sprouts stay leaning into the room, fall over, or turn back to the window? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both cups get the same seeds from one packet, the same soil or tissue, the same water and the same window. The only difference is that we turn cup A round and leave cup B as it is. So if cup A changes and cup B does not, the turning made the change.
- **record:** On day 4, draw both cups with an arrow for the way the sprouts lean. On day 5, draw them again. Draw the window in both drawings.
- **results table:** Cup | Day 4: which way do the sprouts lean? | Did we turn the cup? | Day 5: which way do the sprouts lean?
- **schedule:** day 1: Fill both cups. Scatter the rai seeds and wet them. Put both cups by the window. (5 min); day 2: Give each cup 1 spoon of water. Look for tiny white roots. (2 min); day 3: Give each cup 1 spoon of water. Look for tiny shoots. (2 min); day 4: See which way the sprouts lean. Mark the window side of each cup. Guess, then turn cup A round. (8 min); day 5: Look at both cups. Draw them. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (8 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write A on one cup and B on the other. 2. Fill each cup with 2 cm of soil, or wet folded tissue. 3. Scatter 1 teaspoon of rai seeds on each. 4. Sprinkle 2 spoons of water on each. 5. Put both cups side by side, by the window.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to grow rai sprouts in these two cups. Rai is the mustard we put in sambar and poriyal. Help me scatter the seeds.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the two cups. Scatter the rai seeds on top. Sprinkle water. Put both cups by the window.
  - Note: Use a window where light comes from one side, not from all around. A bright window with no hot sun on the cups is best. Seeds just lying on the soil are fine.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Give each cup 1 spoon of water. Look closely at the seeds together.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely. What has changed since yesterday?

  - Ask: What do you see? (good: Tiny white roots.; The seeds have split open.)
  - Note: In warm weather rai germinates in a day or two. In a cold room it may take 3 or 4 days. Keep watering 1 spoon a day, and wait until the shoots are about 2 cm tall.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow, on day 2.

  - Ask: Which way are the sprouts leaning? (good: Towards the window.; Towards the light.)

  - Wait: Come back when the shoots are about 2 cm tall, usually on day 4.
- **s4 (3 min)**

  - Check: We turned cup A round. The next day, which way did the tops of the sprouts point? → **Back towards the window**

- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and the reason. Then read the explanation card on light together.

### e2 · Soil or No Soil? Two cups of rai (20 min, 12 days)
- **question:** Do rai seeds need soil to sprout? Do the sprouts need soil to keep growing?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the seeds on wet tissue sprout? After ten days, which cup will have the bigger plants? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both cups get the same seeds, the same water, and the same place by the window. The only difference is soil or tissue. We sow 10 seeds in each cup, so we can count fairly.
- **record:** Draw both cups on day 3 and on day 10. Write how many seeds sprouted in each.
- **results table:** Cup | Day 3: how many of the 10 seeds sprouted? | Day 10: how tall? How many leaves?
- **schedule:** day 1: Fill one cup with soil and one with wet tissue. Put 10 seeds on each. Water both. (6 min); day 3: Count the sprouts in each cup. (4 min); day 6: Water both cups. Look at the leaves. (3 min); day 10: Compare the two cups. Count the leaves on one plant in each. (7 min)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill cup 1 with soil. 2. Fold tissue into cup 2 and wet it. 3. Count out 10 rai seeds onto each cup. 4. Sprinkle 2 spoons of water on each.  
  *Parent says:* This cup has soil. This one has only wet tissue. Will the seeds sprout without soil? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Fill one cup with soil. Fill one with wet tissue. Put 10 seeds on each. Guess: will both sprout? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Seeds need soil to grow.; Seeds only need water to sprout.)
  - Note: Keep the tissue damp, not swimming in water. Many children guess that the tissue seeds will not sprout.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the sprouts in each cup. Write the numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* How many seeds sprouted in the soil cup? How many on the tissue? Were you right?  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouts in each cup. Which cup has more?
  - Ask: Did the tissue seeds sprout? (good: Yes, almost as many as in the soil.)
  - Note: Both cups usually sprout about the same number. A seed needs water, air and warmth to germinate, but not soil.
  - Check: Rai seeds on wet tissue, with no soil, did what? → **They sprouted**
  - Wait: Come back on day 3.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Water both cups with 1 spoon each. Look at the leaves.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the leaves in each cup. Do they look the same?  
  *Child mode:* Give each cup one spoon of water. Look at the leaves.
  - Wait: Come back on day 6. Keep both cups damp in between.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two cups side by side. 2. Pick one plant from each cup and count their leaves. 3. Draw both cups.  
  *Parent says:* Which cup has the bigger plants now? Why might that be? Remember, the seed brought a little food store with them.  
  *Child mode:* Which cup has bigger plants now? Count the leaves. Why are they different?
  - Ask: Why are the soil plants bigger? (good: The soil gave them something more.; The seed's food ran out on the tissue.; Soil has minerals.)
  - Note: By day 10 the soil plants usually have new, jagged leaves growing between the first two round ones. The tissue plants often stay small, and their leaves may turn pale. If the two cups still look the same, keep going a few more days. The claim: seeds sprout without soil, but plants need the minerals in soil to keep growing.
  - Wait: Come back on day 10.

## Challenges
### c1 · The Maze Box: can a shoot find the way out? (25 min)
- **problem:** Make a box that is dark inside except for one hole. Put a sprouting potato at the far end, behind a wall. Can the shoot find a way round the wall to the hole?
- **your turn:** Now design your own maze. Draw it first. Check it every 3 days, and close the lid quickly. How many days did your shoot take to reach the light?
- **constraints:** Use a shoebox or any cardboard box with a lid.; Only one hole may let light in.; Put at least one cardboard wall between the potato and the hole.
- **general:** Cut a hole about the size of a coin in one end of the box. Tape a cardboard wall halfway across the middle, with a gap at one side. Put the potato at the far end and close the lid.; Stand the box with the hole facing a bright window.
- **creative:** Add a second wall with a gap on the other side, so the shoot must zig-zag.; Use a long box, like a shoe box for boots, to see how far the shoot will grow in the dark.; Make a box with two holes, one big and one small, and see which hole the shoot chooses.
- **c1s1 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts a coin-sized hole in one end of the box. 2. Tape a cardboard wall across the middle, with a gap on one side. 3. Put the potato in a cup of damp soil at the far end. 4. Close the lid and tape any gaps.  
  *Parent says:* The shoot will be in the dark. Only one hole lets light in, and there is a wall in the way. Do you think the shoot can find the hole? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Build the box with a grown-up. One hole, one wall. Put the potato at the far end. Guess: will the shoot find the hole?
  - Note: A potato already showing small shoots works best. It does not need much water: the potato carries their own food and water.
- **c1s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every 3 days, open the lid for a moment and look. Close it again quickly.  
  *Parent says:* Where is the shoot now? Which way is the shoot growing?  
  *Child mode:* Open the lid quickly. Where is the shoot? Close the lid.
  - Note: The shoot will be long, thin and pale, because it is dark inside. That is normal. The shoot grows towards the faint light coming round the wall.
  - Wait: Check every 3 days for 1 to 3 weeks.
- **c1s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When the shoot reaches the hole, open the box and look at the whole shoot together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the path the shoot took. How did the shoot know which way to grow?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the shoot's path. How did the shoot find the way?
  - Ask: How did the shoot find the way? (good: The shoot grew towards the light.; A little light came round the wall, and the shoot followed it.)

## Applications
- Gardeners turn pots on a windowsill every few days, so the plants grow straight.
- Farmers sow each crop in the season with the right warmth: rice in the warm monsoon, and mustard and wheat in the cool winter in the north.
- Pots have a hole at the bottom so extra water drains out and roots get air.
- Some farms grow lettuce and herbs with their roots in water with minerals added, without soil. This is called hydroponics.
- E. K. Janaki Ammal, a botanist from Kerala, bred sugarcane at Coimbatore in the 1930s that grew well in India's warmth and soils.

- A potato in the kitchen basket growing pale shoots towards a gap of light.

## Open questions
- **What is it like for a sprout to feel where the light is?** Plants have light sensors, and they act on what they sense. Nobody knows what that feeling is like for a plant.
- **How does a sprout know which side of their stem is shady?** Scientists know that auxin moves to the shady side. How the sprout compares the two sides so exactly is still being studied.
- **Why do roots of some plants grow away from light?** Many roots grow away from light, and scientists are still working out how much light really reaches roots in soil, and why this matters.

## Connected maths
- Uma, a girl in Kochi, sowed 10 rai seeds in a cup. By day 3, 7 had sprouted. How many had not sprouted yet? **3 seeds had not sprouted yet.**. 10 take away 7 is 3. Seeds do not all wake up at the same time, so some may sprout tomorrow. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- In the soil cup, 9 of the 10 seeds sprouted. On the tissue, 8 of the 10 seeds sprouted. Did soil make a big difference to sprouting? Why do you think so? **No, not a big difference. 9 and 8 are very close.**. 9 is only 1 more than 8. One seed can fail by chance, so a difference of 1 does not show that soil matters for sprouting. A big difference, like 9 and 0, would. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- Cup A's sprouts bent back to the window in 1 night. Nisha turned the cup round every night for a week. How many times did the sprouts turn back? **7 times, one for each night of the week.**. A week has 7 nights. If the sprouts turn back each night, that is 7 turns. The stem may end up wiggly, with a bend for each turn. (queues `math.time.calendar.L2`)
- On day 4, rai sprouts were 2 cm tall. On day 8 they were 6 cm tall. How many centimetres did they grow in those 4 days? **They grew 4 cm.**. 6 cm take away 2 cm is 4 cm. That is about 1 cm each day. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L2`)

- Phototropism: shoots bend towards one-sided light because auxin is moved to the shaded side, where cells elongate faster (the Cholodny-Went model, widely accepted, details still studied). Any plant physiology textbook, for example Taiz and Zeiger, Plant Physiology.
- Charles Darwin, assisted by Francis Darwin, The Power of Movement in Plants (London, 1880): canary grass (Phalaris canariensis) seedlings with their tips covered did not bend towards light.
- Frits Went (1926 to 1928): diffusion of a growth substance from oat coleoptile tips into agar blocks, which caused curvature when placed on one side of a decapitated coleoptile. The substance was later named auxin.
- Phototropins are blue-light receptors that start phototropism (Winslow Briggs and colleagues, 1990s).
- Atamian, Creux, Brown, Garner, Blackman and Harmer, 'Circadian regulation of sunflower heliotropism, floral orientation, and pollinator visits', Science 353 (2016): young sunflowers track the Sun, mature flower heads face east, warm faster and get more pollinator visits.
- Seeds germinate using their stored food; seedlings need mineral nutrients to keep growing (hydroponics shows soil itself is not needed).
- Roots need oxygen; waterlogged soil leads to root rot. Pots have drainage holes for this reason.

- E. K. Janaki Ammal worked at the Sugarcane Breeding Institute, Coimbatore, from 1934, breeding sugarcane varieties suited to Indian conditions.
