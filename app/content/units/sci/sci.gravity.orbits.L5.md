# Gravity, orbits and satellites: how a thing can fall all the time and never land
`sci.gravity.orbits.L5`

**Summary.** The space station is only about 400 kilometres above us, and gravity pulls it almost as hard as it pulls you. So why does it not fall down? In this lesson you will whirl a pouch of rice on a thread to find out what an orbit needs, and send a stream of water past an upturned bowl. You will find that a satellite is falling all the time, and that it moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing the ground. You will also follow India's own road to orbit, from Aryabhata to Chandrayaan and Mangalyaan.

**Intro.** *Draw the Earth as a circle as wide as a steel plate. How far from the edge of the circle would you draw the space station: a hand away, a step away, or across the room?* Most people put the station far away, because astronauts float there. On a drawing where the Earth is about 13 centimetres wide, the station is only 4 millimetres above the line. Gravity there is almost nine tenths as strong as on the ground. Something else must be keeping the station up. In How High Is Space you will draw this, and then you will test what an orbit needs.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what a satellite is, and tell a natural satellite from an artificial one.
1. By the end I can explain an orbit as falling round: sideways speed and the pull of gravity working together.
2. By the end I can say what happens to a launched thing that is too slow, fast enough to orbit, or faster than escape speed.
3. By the end I can explain why astronauts float although gravity still pulls on them.
4. By the end I can use my own test to show that a stronger pull, or a closer orbit, needs a quicker trip round.
5. By the end I can name three kinds of orbit and the jobs that satellites in them do, with examples from India.
6. By the end I can tell how India reached orbit, the Moon and Mars, and name people the usual telling leaves out.
7. By the end I can give both sides of one open debate about orbits, and say what I think and why.

## Terms
- **Satellite**: A satellite is a thing that goes round a bigger thing, held by the bigger thing's gravity. *e.g.* The Moon is a satellite of the Earth. INSAT-3DS is a satellite of the Earth too.
- **Natural satellite**: A natural satellite is a satellite that nobody made. A moon is a natural satellite of a planet. *e.g.* The Earth has one natural satellite, the Moon. Saturn has more than 270 known moons.
- **Artificial satellite**: An artificial satellite is a machine that people build and send into orbit to do a job. *e.g.* Aryabhata, launched on 19 April 1975, was India's first artificial satellite.
- **Orbit**: An orbit is the path of a thing that keeps falling round another thing without landing on it. *e.g.* The space station's orbit takes it once round the Earth in about 92 minutes.
- **Orbital speed**: Orbital speed is the sideways speed a thing needs to stay in an orbit at a given height. *e.g.* Just above the air, the orbital speed is about 7.8 kilometres every second.
- **Escape speed**: Escape speed is the speed at which a thing with no engine, thrown from a world, never falls back. *e.g.* The escape speed from the Earth's surface is about 11.2 kilometres every second.
- **Free fall**: Free fall is moving with only gravity acting on you, and nothing pushing or holding you. *e.g.* A mango dropping from a branch is in free fall. So is a satellite in orbit.
- **Weightlessness**: Weightlessness is the feeling of having no weight. It comes when you and everything round you fall together, so nothing pushes on you. *e.g.* Astronauts in the space station float because the station and the astronauts fall together.
- **Ellipse**: An ellipse is a stretched circle, like the outline of an egg seen from the side. Most orbits are ellipses. *e.g.* Chandrayaan-3 went round the Earth in longer and longer ellipses before it left for the Moon.
- **Period**: The period of an orbit is the time for one full trip round. *e.g.* The Moon's period is about 27 days. The space station's period is about 92 minutes.
- **Low Earth orbit**: A low Earth orbit is an orbit less than about 2,000 kilometres above the ground. *e.g.* The space station and India's Cartosat mapping satellites are in low Earth orbit.
- **Polar orbit**: A polar orbit is an orbit that passes over or near the North Pole and the South Pole. *e.g.* A satellite in a polar orbit sees every part of the Earth, strip by strip, as the Earth turns below it.
- **Geostationary orbit**: A geostationary orbit is the orbit 35,786 kilometres above the equator. A satellite there goes round once a day, in step with the turning Earth. *e.g.* From the ground, an INSAT weather satellite stays in one place in the sky all day and all night.
- **Launch vehicle**: A launch vehicle is a rocket that carries a satellite up above the air and gives it sideways speed. *e.g.* India's PSLV and LVM3 are launch vehicles. They lift off from Sriharikota in Andhra Pradesh.
- **Space debris**: Space debris is anything in orbit that no longer works: dead satellites, used rocket parts and broken pieces. *e.g.* A fleck of paint in orbit moves so fast that it can chip a space station window.
- **Perigee and apogee**: Perigee is the point of an orbit nearest to the Earth. Apogee is the point farthest from it. *e.g.* A satellite moves fastest at perigee and slowest at apogee.

## Explanations
### A satellite is anything that goes round a bigger thing · *defined*
**Definition:** A satellite is a thing that goes round a bigger thing, held by gravity. A natural satellite was made by nobody. An artificial satellite is built and launched by people.

The Moon is the Earth's natural satellite. The Earth and the other planets go round the Sun, so they are natural satellites of the Sun. Artificial satellites are machines. In 2025 there were more than 10,000 working ones round the Earth, and more than half belonged to one internet company. A planet and a satellite differ in what they go round: a planet goes round a star, and a moon goes round a planet. Here is a closer look. The Earth does not stay still while the Moon goes round it. Both go round a balance point between them. The Earth has 81 times the Moon's mass, so that point lies inside the Earth, about 1,700 kilometres below the ground. The Earth makes a small circle round it each month.
- e.g. The Moon is 3,84,400 kilometres away and takes about 27 days to go round the Earth.
- e.g. Jupiter has more than 90 known moons, and Saturn has more than 270.
- e.g. India's first artificial satellite was Aryabhata, launched on 19 April 1975.
- e.g. The Earth is a satellite of the Sun. One trip round takes one year.
- **Astronomers who name the worlds (the International Astronomical Union):** The Moon goes round the Earth, so the Moon is the Earth's satellite. The balance point of the two lies inside the Earth, and that settles which one is the satellite.
- **The writer and biochemist Isaac Asimov, in an essay of 1963:** The Sun pulls the Moon more than twice as hard as the Earth does. Seen from the Sun, the Moon's path never loops backwards. It always bends towards the Sun. So the Earth and the Moon can also be called a double planet, going round the Sun together.
- **India's astronomers, in the Sanskrit tradition:** The old word is graha, which means 'one that seizes'. The Sun and the Moon were both counted as grahas, because both move against the stars. The newer word for a satellite, upagraha, means a companion graha. What we call a thing depends on what we choose to watch.
### An orbit is falling round · *defined*
**Definition:** An orbit is the path of a thing that moves sideways so fast that, as it falls, the ground curves away beneath it. It keeps falling and keeps missing.

Throw a ball level and it curves down to the ground. Throw it faster and it lands farther away. The Water Cannon shows this with a stream of water. Now think of a very high mountain, above the air. The Earth is round. Over every 8 kilometres, its surface curves down by about 5 metres. A dropped thing falls about 5 metres in its first second. So a thing that moves 8 kilometres sideways every second falls 5 metres, and the ground has also dropped away by 5 metres. It is no closer to the ground than before. This goes on, second after second, all the way round. Isaac Newton drew this picture of a cannon on a mountain. A satellite needs no engine to keep going, because above the air nothing slows it. It needs a rocket only to get up there and to gain its sideways speed. In Orbit in Your Hand, the thread pulled the pouch inwards all the time, and the pouch still never came in. The pull did not shorten the circle. It bent the path.
- e.g. The space station moves about 7.7 kilometres every second. That is Chennai to Bengaluru in about 40 seconds.
- e.g. A satellite with its engine off stays in orbit. Vanguard 1 was launched in 1958 and is still going round, silent since 1964.
- e.g. The Moon is in orbit too. It falls towards the Earth by about 1.4 millimetres every second, while it moves 1 kilometre sideways.
- **Isaac Newton, in 1687:** A moving thing goes straight unless a force acts on it. Gravity is a force that pulls the satellite towards the Earth's centre. That pull bends the straight path into a closed curve.
- **Albert Einstein, in 1915:** No force pulls the satellite at all. The Earth's mass bends space and time round it. The satellite moves along the straightest path there is in that bent space and time, and to us the path looks like a circle.
- **Aristotle and most thinkers for about 2,000 years after him:** Things in the sky are made of a different, perfect stuff, and circling is simply what that stuff does. Things on the Earth fall. This fitted what people saw, and it was given up only when one rule explained both the falling mango and the circling Moon.
### Too slow, just right, too fast · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing launched sideways above the air falls back if it is too slow. It orbits if it reaches orbital speed. It never comes back if it reaches escape speed.

Just above the air, the orbital speed is about 7.8 kilometres every second. Slower than that, the path meets the ground. At that speed, the path is a circle. Faster than that, the thing swings out into a stretched loop called an ellipse and comes back to where it started. Johannes Kepler found in 1609 that the planets move in ellipses. At about 11.2 kilometres every second, the loop never closes. This is the escape speed. The word needs care. A thing that escapes has not left gravity behind, because the Earth's pull never ends. It only grows weaker with distance. Escape means the thing will never fall back. A rocket with its engine running does not have to reach 11.2 kilometres a second near the ground. That number is for a thing thrown with no engine. ISRO used ellipses to save fuel. Chandrayaan-3 and Mangalyaan went round the Earth several times. At the lowest and fastest point of each loop, the engine gave a short push, and the far end of the loop swung farther out. The last push sent the spacecraft away.
- e.g. A cricket ball hit for six leaves the bat at about 40 metres every second. Orbit needs about 200 times that speed.
- e.g. Before leaving for Mars, Mangalyaan's loops reached about 1,93,000 kilometres from the Earth.
- e.g. The Moon is a smaller world, and its escape speed is about 2.4 kilometres every second.
- e.g. A thing that escapes the Earth is still in orbit round the Sun.
### Higher orbits are slower and take longer · *defined*
**Definition:** The farther a satellite is from the Earth, the weaker the pull on it, the slower it needs to go, and the longer one trip round takes.

In Near and Far, the pouch on the short thread went round sooner than the pouch on the long thread. When you added the Earth's own rule, a four times stronger pull at half the distance, it went round much sooner still. Real satellites follow the same pattern. The space station, 400 kilometres up, goes round in about 92 minutes. Navigation satellites of the GPS system, about 20,200 kilometres up, take 12 hours. A satellite 35,786 kilometres above the equator takes one day, so it keeps step with the turning Earth. The Moon, 3,84,400 kilometres away, takes about 27 days. Kepler found the exact rule in 1619 from the planets. Newton showed in 1687 that the rule follows from gravity growing weaker with distance. At twice the distance from the Earth's centre, the pull is one quarter as strong.
- e.g. The space station moves 7.7 kilometres every second. The Moon moves only about 1 kilometre every second.
- e.g. Mercury, close to the Sun, goes round it in 88 days. Neptune, far out, takes about 165 years.
- e.g. Jupiter's moon Io, close in, goes round in under 2 days. Callisto, far out, takes almost 17 days.
### Astronauts float because they are falling · *defined*
**Definition:** Astronauts in orbit feel weightless because they, their station and everything in it fall together. Gravity has not gone away.

At the height of the space station, each kilogram is pulled with about 8.7 newtons. On the ground it is about 9.8 newtons. So gravity up there is almost nine tenths as strong. If the station stood on a tower 400 kilometres tall, the astronauts would walk about and feel nearly their normal weight. They float because nothing holds them up. The floor falls exactly as fast as their feet, so the floor never pushes on them. The weight you feel is the push of the floor. Scientists call the state in orbit free fall, or microgravity. An astronaut still has all of her mass. A heavy box still takes a hard push to start moving and a hard push to stop. Bodies of humans and other animals are built for the push of the ground. In orbit, bones lose about 1 part in 100 of their strength each month. So astronauts exercise for about two hours every day, strapped down with stretchy cords.
- e.g. Rakesh Sharma spent almost 8 days in orbit in April 1984, on the Salyut 7 station.
- e.g. Sunita Williams ran a full marathon on the space station's treadmill in 2007, held down by cords.
- e.g. Shubhanshu Shukla became the first Indian on the International Space Station in June 2025.
- e.g. Water in orbit does not pour. It gathers into floating balls.
- **Isaac Newton's way of saying it:** Gravity pulls the astronaut down with almost her full weight. She feels nothing because no floor pushes back. Her weight is still there, and only the feeling of it has gone.
- **Albert Einstein, who called this his happiest thought, in 1907:** A person in free fall feels no force because no force acts on her. She is the one at rest. The person standing on the ground is the one being pushed, upwards, by the floor, every moment.
- **Doctors who look after astronauts:** For the body, what matters is the missing push. Bones, muscles, the heart and the balance sensors in the ear all change in orbit, whatever name we give the state.
### The Moon is falling too, and it pulls back · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon is in free fall round the Earth, held by the same gravity that pulls a mango down. The Moon's gravity pulls on the Earth too, and that makes the tides.

The Moon is about 60 times as far from the Earth's centre as you are. Gravity grows weaker with distance: at 60 times the distance it is 60 times 60, or 3,600 times, weaker. A mango falls about 5 metres in its first second. Divide that by 3,600 and you get about 1.4 millimetres. That is how far the Moon falls towards the Earth each second, and it is just enough to keep bending its path into an orbit. Newton did this sum and found that it matched. The Moon pulls the near side of the Earth a little harder than the far side. This stretches the oceans into two bulges, and the turning Earth carries each coast through them. That is why most coasts have two high tides a day. The tides also slowly push the Moon outwards. It moves away from us by about 3.8 centimetres every year.
- e.g. Scientists bounce laser light off mirrors left on the Moon and time its return. Chandrayaan-3's lander carries one such mirror.
- e.g. At Chandipur in Odisha, the sea goes out by up to 5 kilometres at low tide and comes back at high tide.
- e.g. The same side of the Moon always faces us, because the Earth's pull slowed the Moon's spin long ago.
### Kinds of orbit and the jobs they do · *defined*
**Definition:** Engineers choose an orbit for the job. Low orbits give sharp pictures. A polar orbit covers the whole Earth. A geostationary orbit watches one side of the Earth all the time.

A satellite in low Earth orbit is close, so its cameras see small things, such as a single road. But it rushes past a town in a few minutes. A low satellite in a polar orbit goes over the poles while the Earth turns below, so it sees every place, strip by strip. India's Resourcesat, Cartosat and Oceansat work this way. They map crops, floods, forests and the colour of the sea. A geostationary satellite is 35,786 kilometres up, above the equator. It goes round once a day, so it stays over one spot. It sees about a third of the Earth all the time, though with less detail. INSAT and GSAT satellites there carry television and telephone signals and watch the weather. This is why a dish on a roof never has to turn. Navigation satellites tell a phone where it is. The phone needs signals from at least four of them. India's system is called NavIC.
- e.g. INSAT-3DS, launched in February 2024, takes a picture of the clouds over India every half hour or sooner.
- e.g. Fishers along the coast get messages on where fish are likely to be, made in Hyderabad from Oceansat's pictures of the sea.
- e.g. In October 2013, satellites tracked Cyclone Phailin for days. About ten lakh people in Odisha and Andhra Pradesh moved to safety in time.
- e.g. From anywhere in India, dishes point to the southern half of the sky, because geostationary satellites sit above the equator.
### Getting up there: rockets · *defined*
**Definition:** A rocket throws hot exhaust out of its back at great speed, and that pushes the rocket forward. It lifts a satellite above the air, then tips over to give it sideways speed.

Reaching space is the smaller part of a rocket's work. Space begins only about 100 kilometres up. The larger part is reaching 7.8 kilometres every second sideways. So a rocket goes straight up for only a short time, and then it leans over and speeds along above the sea. A rocket must carry all its fuel with it, and also the oxygen to burn it. About nine tenths of a rocket's mass at lift-off is fuel and oxygen. To save mass, a rocket is built in stages. Each stage drops away when it is empty. The Earth helps a little. The ground at Sriharikota moves east at about 450 metres every second as the Earth spins. A rocket launched towards the east starts with that speed. This is one reason India's launch site is on the east coast, with the Bay of Bengal under the rocket's path.
- e.g. India's first rocket rose from Thumba, a fishing village in Kerala, on 21 November 1963. Its parts came to the launch pad by bicycle.
- e.g. PSLV, the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle, has four stages. LVM3 carried Chandrayaan-3 in July 2023.
- e.g. Mysore's army under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan used rockets with iron cases in the 1780s and 1790s. They flew farther than any rockets in Europe at that time.
- e.g. The Russian teacher Konstantin Tsiolkovsky worked out in 1903 how much fuel a rocket needs to reach orbit.
### India's road to orbit, the Moon and Mars · *defined*
**Definition:** India built its space programme to serve people on the ground first: weather warnings, television for villages, maps and telephones. Later it went to the Moon, Mars and the Sun.

Vikram Sarabhai began the work in 1962, and ISRO was formed on 15 August 1969. Aryabhata, India's first satellite, was built in sheds at Peenya in Bengaluru by a team led by U. R. Rao. A Soviet rocket launched it on 19 April 1975. On 18 July 1980, India's own rocket, SLV-3, put the Rohini satellite into orbit from Sriharikota. A. P. J. Abdul Kalam led that rocket's team. INSAT-1B, launched in 1983, began the INSAT system for television, telephones and weather. Chandrayaan-1 went into orbit round the Moon in 2008 and helped to find water on the Moon. Mangalyaan left for Mars on 5 November 2013 and went into orbit there on 24 September 2014, at India's first try. Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole on 23 August 2023. Aditya-L1 reached its place for watching the Sun in January 2024. Thousands of people did this work, and many who led it were women.
- e.g. Ritu Karidhal and Nandini Harinath helped to run Mangalyaan's flight. B. P. Dakshayani led the team that worked out its path. Moumita Dutta and Minal Rohit built its instruments.
- e.g. M. Vanitha was project director of Chandrayaan-2, and Ritu Karidhal was its mission director.
- e.g. N. Valarmathi from Ariyalur in Tamil Nadu led the radar satellite RISAT-1. T. K. Anuradha led the communication satellite GSAT-12.
- e.g. Nigar Shaji from Sengottai in Tamil Nadu led Aditya-L1. Kalpana Kalahasti was associate project director of Chandrayaan-3.
- e.g. The fishing families of Thumba and their bishop, Peter Bernard Pereira, gave their church and land for the first rocket station.
- **Vikram Sarabhai, who founded the programme:** Some people ask why a developing country should work in space. Sarabhai answered that India was not racing anyone to the Moon. It had to be second to none in using the newest tools for the real problems of people and society.
- **The economist Jean Drèze, speaking about the Mars mission in 2013:** A country where many children are underfed should put food, health and schools first. A Mars mission looks like a wish to be seen as a great power.
- **People on the coast who use the satellites:** For a fisher who gets a cyclone warning two days early, or a village that got its first television lessons by satellite in 1975, space work is not about pride. It is a tool they use.
### A crowded sky: space debris and bright satellites · *debated*
**Definition:** Low Earth orbit is filling with satellites and with debris. People disagree about how many it can hold safely, and about who should decide.

Everything in low orbit moves at about 7 to 8 kilometres every second. At that speed, a loose bolt hits as hard as a speeding lorry. Radars track about 40,000 objects more than 10 centimetres across, and there are far more small pieces. On 10 February 2009, a working satellite and a dead one crashed into each other for the first time and made thousands of new pieces. Very low orbits clean themselves. A trace of air still reaches a few hundred kilometres up. It slows low satellites, and they sink and burn up. The space station sinks about 2 kilometres a month and must be pushed back up. Higher orbits keep their debris for hundreds of years. Satellites also reflect sunlight. Thousands of them leave streaks across astronomers' pictures, and they change the night sky that every being on the Earth has always had.
- e.g. Aryabhata sank slowly and burned up in February 1992, almost 17 years after its launch.
- e.g. In March 2019, India tested a weapon on its own satellite, about 300 kilometres up. Most of the pieces fell within months because the orbit was so low.
- e.g. ISRO moves its satellites out of the way of debris many times each year.
- e.g. Dung beetles steer by the band of the Milky Way, and many birds that fly at night steer by the stars.
- **Donald Kessler, a NASA scientist, in 1978:** Past a certain crowding, each crash makes pieces that cause more crashes. The debris then grows by itself, even if nobody launches anything more. Some orbits could become unusable.
- **Companies that fly thousands of internet satellites:** Their satellites fly low, steer round debris by themselves, and burn up within about five years when they stop working. They bring the internet to villages, ships and mountains that cables never reach.
- **Astronomers, through the International Astronomical Union:** Bright satellites spoil pictures of the sky and drown faint radio signals from space. The dark and quiet sky belongs to everyone and needs rules to protect it.
- **Sky-watching peoples and scientists who study other animals:** Many peoples keep calendars, stories and sea routes in the stars. Night-flying birds, moths and dung beetles steer by the sky too. Nobody asked any of them before the sky was changed.
### How far does gravity reach, and what is it? · *idea*
**Definition:** Gravity has no edge. The Earth's pull grows weaker with distance, and it never becomes zero. What gravity is, deep down, is still being worked out.

Here is what we know so far. Every thing with mass pulls every other thing. The pull between two things grows with their masses and falls quickly with distance. This rule, from Newton, is good enough to fly a spacecraft to Mars. Newton himself said he did not know how the pull crosses empty space. Einstein gave a deeper picture in 1915: mass bends space and time, and things move along the bends. That picture explains small things Newton's rule gets wrong, such as the slow turning of Mercury's orbit. It is used every day, because clocks on navigation satellites run faster than clocks on the ground by about 38 millionths of a second each day, and the system must correct for that. In 2015, detectors caught ripples in space and time from two black holes that fell together. A detector of this kind is being built at Aundha in Maharashtra. What is still missing is how gravity fits with the rules of the smallest particles.
- e.g. The Sun's gravity holds comets that are a thousand times farther away than Neptune.
- e.g. Our whole galaxy is held together by gravity. The Sun takes about 23 crore years to go once round its centre.
- e.g. The Voyager spacecraft, launched in 1977, are still slowing down a little because the Sun still pulls on them.
- **Isaac Newton:** Gravity is a pull between any two masses, across any distance. Newton gave the rule for its strength. He refused to guess at its cause.
- **Albert Einstein:** Gravity is the shape of space and time. Mass bends them, and that bend tells things how to move.
- **Brahmagupta of Bhinmal, in 628 CE:** It is the nature of the Earth to attract and hold things, as it is the nature of water to flow. The scholar al-Biruni reported this view of his about 400 years later.
- **Physicists who work on quantum gravity today:** At the smallest scales, gravity should come in tiny packets, like light does. Nobody has detected such a packet, and no one theory of it is agreed.

## Mostly agreed
- The oldest machine still circling above us has been silent for sixty years. *How we know:* Vanguard 1 has been in orbit since 17 March 1958. Its radio went silent in 1964, and it is still tracked today by radar.
- Orbits low enough to touch the thin upper air do not last. *How we know:* The Skylab station sank and broke up over the Indian Ocean and Australia on 11 July 1979. Aryabhata burned up in February 1992. The space station is boosted several times a year.
- Water on the Moon was first mapped by a spacecraft circling overhead, and no lander was needed. *How we know:* In 2009, an instrument on Chandrayaan-1 read the sunlight bouncing off the Moon's soil and found the mark of water in it. Chandrayaan-1's own probe, dropped near the south pole in November 2008, had sensed water on its way down.
- Thousands of planets circling distant stars keep Kepler's timing. *How we know:* The moons of Jupiter and Saturn, and thousands of planets found round other stars, all fit the rule that links distance and time for one trip.
- Things of different mass share the same orbit at the same speed. *How we know:* An astronaut, a spanner and a 400-tonne station move round together. In 1971 on the Moon, David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather, and they landed together.
- A star wobbles when a planet circles it, and that wobble is how many planets were discovered. *How we know:* In 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz saw the star 51 Pegasi swing to and fro every 4 days. A planet and the star were circling a balance point between them, as the Earth and the Moon do.

## How we found out
**Robert Hooke, Isaac Newton and Émilie du Châtelet, and the builders of Sputnik 1, led by Sergei Korolev., Hooke's letter was written in 1679. Newton's book came out in 1687, and du Châtelet's translation in 1759. Sputnik 1 flew in 1957..** For about 2,000 years, most scholars followed the Greek thinker Aristotle. He taught that the sky and the Earth obey different rules. Things on the Earth fall and stop. The Moon and the stars are made of a perfect stuff that circles for ever. This fitted what everyone saw, because mangoes fall and the Moon does not. In 1679, Robert Hooke wrote to Isaac Newton with a new idea. An orbit could be a straight movement that is bent, all the time, by a pull towards the centre. Newton did the sums. The Moon is 60 times as far from the Earth's centre as a mango is. He found that the Moon falls towards the Earth 3,600 times more gently than the mango, which is exactly what one rule for both would give. He published this in 1687, and he drew a cannon on a mountain firing a ball right round the world. Émilie du Châtelet translated and explained his book in French, and France still uses her version. On 4 October 1957, the Soviet satellite Sputnik 1 did what Newton had drawn.  
*What people believed before:* That the sky and the Earth follow different rules: sky things circle for ever by their nature, and earthly things fall. It was believable because nobody had ever seen a sky thing fall or an earthly thing circle. It was shown wrong when Newton's sum for the Moon's fall matched the fall of things on the ground, and again when people put a thing made on the ground into orbit.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Astronauts float because they are too far from the Earth for gravity to reach them.** (Many films, toy boxes and older school books; *rejected*)  
  For: Astronauts do float, and on the ground nothing floats. Space also looks very far away, so it seems natural that gravity runs out before it.  
  Against: The station is only 400 kilometres up, and gravity there is almost nine tenths of its strength on the ground. Without gravity, the station would fly off in a straight line and never come round again. Astronauts float because they fall together with the station.
- **A satellite needs its engine running to stay in orbit.** (A common belief, because every vehicle on the Earth needs an engine to keep going; *rejected*)  
  For: A car, a boat and an aeroplane all slow down and stop when the engine stops. On the Earth, friction and air are always there.  
  Against: Above the air, almost nothing slows a satellite. The Moon has had no engine for more than 400 crore years. Satellites use small engines only to change orbit, or to make up for the thin air that reaches low orbits.
- **Aryabhata taught, in 499 CE, that the Earth and the planets go round the Sun.** (The mathematician B. L. van der Waerden, and some popular books in India; *minority*)  
  For: Aryabhata said plainly that the Earth spins, and that the stars only seem to move. Some of the numbers in his planet tables match the planets' trips round the Sun.  
  Against: In his book the planets, the Sun and the Moon all go round the Earth. Most historians of Indian astronomy, such as Kim Plofker, read his numbers as part of an Earth-centred model. A spinning Earth is a different idea from an Earth in orbit.
- **The stars at the edges of galaxies go round too fast for Newton's rule, so the rule for gravity must be changed when the pull is very weak.** (The physicist Mordehai Milgrom, since 1983, and a small group of astronomers; *minority*)  
  For: Vera Rubin and Kent Ford found in the 1970s that the outer stars of galaxies do not slow down with distance, as the planets do. Milgrom's changed rule predicts the speeds in most galaxies from the visible stars alone.  
  Against: Most astronomers keep the rule and say there is unseen matter, called dark matter, adding its pull. Pictures of colliding clusters of galaxies and the oldest light in the universe fit dark matter better. But nobody has caught a particle of dark matter.
- **A country with many poor people should not spend money on space.** (The economist Jean Drèze and other writers, about the Mars mission of 2013; *debated*)  
  For: Money is limited, and children's food, health and schools are urgent. Mangalyaan cost about 450 crore rupees.  
  Against: Weather and cyclone satellites have saved many thousands of lives, and satellites serve farmers, fishers and schools every day. The Mars mission cost each Indian about 4 rupees. But the benefit of a Moon or Mars mission is harder to count than the benefit of a weather satellite.
- **Low orbit can safely hold tens of thousands more satellites.** (Companies building satellite internet, and some space agencies; *debated*)  
  For: Low orbits clean themselves through the drag of the thin air. New satellites steer away from danger by themselves. No runaway chain of crashes has happened so far.  
  Against: Kessler's sums say crowding can pass a tipping point without warning. One failed satellite cannot steer. Astronomers and sky-watching peoples already count a cost, and no single body has the power to set a limit.

## Roots
In this lesson you drew the space station 4 millimetres above the Earth and held an orbit on a thread. Here we ask what a fall that never lands says about the sky, the ground and us.

**Deep truth.** On your drawing, the space station was 4 millimetres above the Earth, and gravity pulls it almost as hard as it pulls you. It stays up only because it never stops falling. The Moon falls by the same rule as a dropped mango. So the sky has no rules of its own. 'Up there' and 'down here' are one place.  
*Habit it breaks:* We split the world into the ground, where things fall, and the sky, where things float or hang. We also treat falling and staying up as opposites. An orbit is both at once, so neither split holds.  
*Evidence:* In How High Is Space, the station sat just above the line of the Earth, where gravity is almost nine tenths as strong as on the ground. In Orbit in Your Hand, the pouch stayed out in its circle only while the thread pulled it inwards. Newton's sum gives the Moon a fall of 1.4 millimetres each second, which is a mango's fall made 3,600 times weaker by distance.

- *who am i:* You are in orbit now. With the Earth, you fall round the Sun at about 30 kilometres every second, and you feel nothing, just as an astronaut feels nothing. The matter of your body follows the rule that the Moon follows.
- *what is life:* Life on the Earth grew up with the Moon's orbit. Corals, crabs and many other sea animals time their eggs by the Moon and the tides. The bodies of humans and other animals are shaped by the push of the ground, and they begin to change when that push is gone.
- *what is this world:* It is a world where one rule runs from a pebble to a galaxy. Nothing in it hangs in place by itself. Each thing is held on its path by the others.

A mango, the Moon, the Earth and the Sun look like separate things with separate ways of moving. This lesson's tests show one fall, at different speeds and distances. An orbit is not something the Moon has by itself. It is something the Earth and the Moon do together, round a point they share. Everything is one thing showing up as many, and gravity is among the plainest places to see it. Some sages said this long ago and some thinkers disagree, and both are given here.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 8.1.3:** As far as this space reaches, so far reaches the space within the heart. Both heaven and earth are held within it. (The sage does not put heaven in one place and earth in another. Both are held in one space. This lesson found the same from the outside: one rule of falling holds the mango and the Moon.)
- **The Emerald Tablet, in Isaac Newton's own translation of about 1680 (Keynes MS 28, King's College, Cambridge). The text first appears in Arabic, in the Book of the Secret of Creation, around the 800s CE.:** That which is below is like that which is above, and that which is above is like that which is below. (Newton copied out this old saying by hand, a few years before he showed that the Moon falls as a stone does. The saying proved nothing. His sums and measurements did. But it shows that the question was asked long before him, in Arabic and in Latin.)
- **Tao Te Ching 25:** Humans follow the Earth. The Earth follows heaven. Heaven follows the Way. The Way follows what is natural to it. (Laozi's lines join the ground and the sky in one chain, each following the next, with one Way running through all of them. An orbit is a small case of it: you follow the Earth, and the Earth follows the Sun.)
- Science (settled): One rule of gravity describes a falling mango, the Moon, the planets and every satellite. Each spacecraft flight tests it again.
- Science (settled): A thing in free fall feels no weight, whether it is a child in mid-jump or an astronaut in orbit.
- Science (debated): At the edges of galaxies, stars move faster than the rule predicts from the matter we see. Most astronomers add unseen dark matter. A few change the rule.
- Science (open): What gravity is at the smallest scales, and why the universe follows one rule at all, science has not answered.
- Other view, Steven Weinberg, a physicist, and Madhvacharya, the teacher of Dvaita: Weinberg held that one rule everywhere shows only that matter behaves alike everywhere. It does not show that things are one, or that the universe means anything. He wrote in 1977 that the more the universe seems comprehensible, the more it also seems pointless. Madhvacharya of Udupi taught in the 1200s that the differences between one thing and another are real and lasting. In his view, the mango and the Moon may obey one law and still be truly two.
- Practice, Sit still at 30 kilometres a second (3 min): Sit on the floor and close your eyes. Feel the floor push up on you. Now remember that the floor, the building and the whole Earth are falling round the Sun. You are falling with them, about 30 kilometres every second. Breathe slowly ten times. Look for any feeling of that movement. Then open your eyes, and tell me what you found.
- Ask: If the Moon and a mango fall by one rule, is there any real line between the sky and the ground?
- Ask: You are falling round the Sun and you feel nothing. What else might be happening to you that you do not notice?
- Ask: The Earth and the Moon go round a point they share. Is an orbit something one thing does, or something two things do?
- Ask: Weinberg says one rule everywhere means nothing more than that. What do you say?

## Story: The Rocket That Leaned Over
*A made-up story. Selvi, Arul, Paatti and Mariam are imagined. These things are real: rockets rise from Sriharikota island, people watch them from Pazhaverkadu across the lake, the sea under the rocket's path is closed to boats on launch days, and every rocket leans over soon after lift-off.*

On launch days, nobody in Pazhaverkadu went to sea. The notice had come three days before. The water under the rocket's path was closed until evening. So the boats lay in a row on the sand, and Selvi's grandmother, Paatti, had no catch to dry.

Selvi was eleven. Her cousin Arul was eight, and he had come all the way from Madurai to see the rocket. The two of them sat on an upturned boat beside Paatti and looked north across the lake, to the long green line of Sriharikota island.

"Mariam akka is over there," said Selvi. Mariam lived two doors away. She was a technician at the spaceport, and she checked the pipes that fill the rocket. "She won't even see it go. She'll be indoors, watching numbers."

"How high will it go?" asked Arul.

"This one is taking a satellite about 500 kilometres up."

Arul worked it out on his fingers. "That's like here to Madurai."

"Nearly the same," said Selvi.

"Then space is not far at all! I came that far on a bus!"

"Paatti's sister in Kochi lives farther away than space," said Selvi, and Paatti snorted.

A light came first, low on the island, brighter than a welding torch. There was no sound for a long time. Then the sound arrived all at once, a rumble that Arul felt in his chest. The rocket rose on a white pillar, straight up.

Then it leaned.

Arul jumped to his feet. "It's tipping over! Akka, it's going wrong! It will fall in the sea!"

"It leans every time, kanna," said Paatti. "I have watched thirty of them lean. Ask her why. I only know that they do."

Selvi picked up a flat pebble and put it in Arul's hand. "Throw this out over the water. Level, not up."

He threw. The pebble curved down and went in with a plop, a few boat-lengths out.

"Harder."

He threw another one with his whole body. It curved down and went in, twice as far out.

"Mariam akka made me do this last year until my arm hurt," said Selvi. "What did the second pebble do that the first one didn't?"

"Nothing. It just went farther before it came down."

"So both of them fell. But the faster one landed farther off. Now look at the far edge of the sea. When a boat comes home, what do you see first?"

Arul knew this one. "The top of the mast. Then the rest."

"Because the sea is not flat. It bends away from us. So think. If you could throw a pebble so hard that the sea bent away under it as fast as the pebble dropped..."

Arul stood very still. "It would never reach the water."

"It would keep on falling, right round the world, and hit you on the back of the head." Selvi pointed at the bent white trail. "That is what the rocket is doing. Going up is the small part. It has to throw the satellite sideways, nearly 8 kilometres every second. So it leans over and runs."

"Then the satellite is falling?"

"The whole time. It falls and it misses."

Paatti had been listening with her eyes on the sky. "Then tell me this," she said. "When your grandfather's anchor fell from the boat, it went down and it stopped at the bottom. Things that fall, land. That is what falling means."

Selvi opened her mouth and shut it again. Mariam akka had never said anything about that. "Maybe landing is not a part of falling, Paatti," she said slowly. "Maybe landing is only the ground getting in the way."

Paatti did not say yes or no. The trail was a thin scratch now, bent by the high winds, and the spark at its tip went out over the Bay of Bengal.

Mariam came home after dark, tired and smiling. The satellite was in its orbit, she said, and its rocket had been switched off for hours. They all went down to the sand. A steady point of light was crossing among the stars, with no sound and no flame.

"That is one," said Mariam. "It isn't ours. Ours is too small to see tonight. But it is doing the same thing."

"With its engine off?" said Arul. "What keeps it going?"

"What is up there to stop it?" said Mariam.

Arul watched the light until it faded. The Moon was coming up over the lake, orange and slow. He looked at it for a while.

"Akka. Is the Moon falling too?"

"What do you think?" said Mariam.

He did not answer. After a minute he said, more quietly, "And us? Is the Earth falling round the Sun?"

Nobody spoke. Paatti pressed her palm flat on the cool sand. "Sixty-eight years I have sat on this beach," she said, "and I thought I was sitting still."

**For the parent:** For the parent: both pebbles fell, and the faster one only landed farther away. On a round Earth, a thing thrown fast enough falls for ever without landing. That is an orbit, and it is why a rocket leans over to gain sideways speed. Paatti's question is left open on purpose. Ask your child what they think falling means.

- Ask: Paatti says that things that fall, land. Selvi says landing is only the ground getting in the way. Who do you agree with, and why?
- Ask: Why does a rocket lean over instead of going straight up all the way?
- Ask: The satellite's engine was off, and it kept going. What would stop a boat whose engine is off? Why is orbit different?
- Ask: Arul asks whether the Moon is falling. What would you tell him, and how would you show it?
- Ask: Paatti thought she was sitting still. Was she? What would you need to know to decide?

### e1 · Orbit in Your Hand: does a stronger pull need a faster circle? (30 min)
- **question:** A pouch circles on a thread, pulled inwards by a hanging load. When the load is 2 times and 4 times as heavy, how does the time for ten rounds change?

### Satellites, orbits and falling round (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a natural satellite of the Earth?  
   Options: The Moon / INSAT-3DS / The space station / The Sun  
   **Answer:** The Moon. *Why:* The Moon goes round the Earth and nobody made it. INSAT-3DS and the space station were built by people. The Earth goes round the Sun, not the other way.
2. (mcq) A satellite's engine is switched off. Why does it keep going round?  
   Options: The air pushes it along / Almost nothing is there to slow it / The Earth's spin drags it / It slows down and stops within a day  
   **Answer:** Almost nothing is there to slow it. *Why:* Above the air there is almost no friction, so a moving thing keeps moving. On the ground, things stop because friction and air slow them. Gravity bends the satellite's path but does not slow it in a circular orbit.
3. (mcq) A thing is launched level, above the air, at 5 kilometres every second. What happens?  
   Options: It orbits in a circle / It falls back to the ground / It escapes from the Earth / It hangs still  
   **Answer:** It falls back to the ground. *Why:* Orbit just above the air needs about 7.8 kilometres every second. At 5, the thing falls faster than the ground curves away, so its path meets the ground. It does travel a very long way first.
4. (mcq) Why do astronauts in the space station float?  
   Options: Gravity does not reach that high / They fall together with the station / The station spins them / There is no air to hold them down  
   **Answer:** They fall together with the station. *Why:* The station and everything in it fall round the Earth together, so the floor never pushes on anyone's feet. Gravity is almost as strong there as on the ground. Air does not hold people down anywhere.
5. (mcq) Which orbit lets a satellite stay above one place in India?  
   Options: A low polar orbit / A geostationary orbit / Any orbit, if the engine is on / The Moon's orbit  
   **Answer:** A geostationary orbit. *Why:* A geostationary satellite goes round once a day above the equator, in step with the turning Earth. A low satellite takes about 90 minutes for a trip, so it rushes past every place.
6. (truefalse) True or false: gravity at the height of the space station is almost as strong as on the ground.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The station is only 400 kilometres up. Each kilogram there is pulled with about 8.7 newtons, and on the ground with about 9.8.
7. (truefalse) True or false: the hardest part of a rocket's work is lifting the satellite very high.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Space begins only about 100 kilometres up. The harder part is giving the satellite its sideways speed of nearly 8 kilometres every second. That is why a rocket leans over soon after lift-off.
8. (fill) The speed needed to orbit just above the air is about ____ kilometres every second.  
   **Answer:** ['8', '7.8', '7.9']. *Why:* It is about 7.8. At that speed a thing moves about 8 kilometres sideways while it falls 5 metres, and the ground curves down by 5 metres too.
9. (fill) India's first satellite, launched in 1975, was named ____.  
   **Answer:** ['Aryabhata']. *Why:* It was named after the astronomer Aryabhata, who wrote in 499 CE that the Earth spins. A team led by U. R. Rao built it in Bengaluru.
10. (match) Match each satellite or spacecraft to its job.  
   **Answer:** INSAT-3DS → watches clouds and cyclones; Cartosat → makes sharp maps of the ground; NavIC → tells boats and phones where they are; Chandrayaan-3 → landed near the Moon's south pole; Aditya-L1 → watches the Sun. *Why:* Each job needs its own orbit or path. A strong answer could also say which of these are in orbit round the Earth.

### Speeds, heights and the people who got us there (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In a whirling test, the hanging load is made four times as heavy. About how long do ten rounds take now?

   Options: It moves more slowly / It moves faster / Its speed is unchanged / It must keep its engine on

   Options: It has left the Earth's gravity behind / It will not fall back to the Earth / Its engine must stay on for ever / It stops when its fuel ends

### The Moon's fall, crowded skies and open debates (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) The Moon is 60 times as far from the Earth's centre as you are. How much weaker is the Earth's pull there?  
   Options: 60 times weaker / 120 times weaker / 3,600 times weaker / It is not weaker  
   **Answer:** 3,600 times weaker. *Why:* The pull falls with the distance multiplied by itself. 60 times 60 is 3,600. Choosing 60 forgets that the pull spreads out in every direction, like light from a lamp.
2. (mcq) An astronaut in orbit pushes a floating box with a mass of 100 kilograms. What does she find?  
   Options: It moves at the lightest touch / It is hard to start and hard to stop / It drops to the floor / It has lost its mass  
   **Answer:** It is hard to start and hard to stop. *Why:* The box is weightless, but it still has 100 kilograms of mass. Mass is what makes a thing hard to speed up or slow down. Astronauts have been bruised by forgetting this.
3. (mcq) Stars at a galaxy's edge move faster than the rule for orbits predicts. What do most astronomers think?  
   Options: Unseen matter adds its pull / The stars push themselves along / The telescopes are faulty / Gravity stops at the edge  
   **Answer:** Unseen matter adds its pull. *Why:* Most astronomers say unseen dark matter adds extra pull. A smaller group says the rule for gravity changes when the pull is very weak. Nobody has yet caught a particle of dark matter, so the question is open.
4. (count) The Earth curves down 5 metres every 8 kilometres. A satellite covers 8 kilometres each second. How many metres must it fall?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* It must fall as much as the ground curves away, which is 5 metres. A dropped thing falls about 5 metres in its first second, so 8 kilometres every second is the speed that keeps missing.
5. (count) Mangalyaan cost 450 crore rupees. India had 125 crore people. How many rupees is that each, to the nearest rupee?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 450 divided by 125 is 3.6, which rounds to 4 rupees. A sum like this helps in the debate, but it does not settle whether the money was best spent this way.
6. (truefalse) True or false: a heavy satellite must move faster than a light one to stay in the same orbit.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Gravity pulls a heavier satellite harder, but a heavier satellite is also harder to turn. The two cancel exactly. An astronaut, a spanner and the whole station share one orbit at one speed.
7. (order) Put these events in order, earliest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Aryabhata is launched on a Soviet rocket', "Rohini goes up on India's own rocket, SLV-3", 'INSAT-1B begins the INSAT system', 'Mangalyaan goes into orbit round Mars', 'Chandrayaan-3 lands on the Moon']. *Why:* The years are 1975, 1980, 1983, 2014 and 2023. India first built a satellite, then its own rocket, then satellites that served people, and then went to other worlds.
8. (sort) Sort each job by the orbit that suits it best.  
   **Answer:** mapping every paddy field in sharp detail → Low polar orbit; watching a cyclone hour by hour → Geostationary orbit; sending television to fixed dishes → Geostationary orbit; photographing a glacier's cracks → Low polar orbit; measuring the colour of every ocean → Low polar orbit. *Why:* Sharp detail and whole-Earth cover need a low orbit over the poles. A steady watch on one region, or a signal to dishes that never turn, needs a satellite that stays above one spot.
9. (odd) Three of these would bring the space station down. Which one would not?  
   Options: Stopping its sideways speed / Thin air dragging on it for years / Switching its engines off for a day / A crash that slows it greatly  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The station's engines are off nearly all the time. It needs sideways speed, not thrust. Anything that takes away much of that speed lets the fall meet the ground.
10. (fill) A dead satellite or a broken piece left in orbit is called space ____.  
   **Answer:** ['debris', 'junk']. *Why:* Space debris moves at 7 to 8 kilometres every second, so even a small piece can wreck a working satellite.
11. (write) Some books say Aryabhata taught that the Earth goes round the Sun. How would you check a claim like this?  
   **Answer:** Look for what he wrote in his own book, in a careful translation. Ask what historians who read Sanskrit astronomy say, and whether they agree. Keep apart the two ideas: he clearly wrote that the Earth spins, which is not the same as the Earth going round the Sun.. *Why:* A strong answer goes to the source and to the people who study it, and separates a spinning Earth from an orbiting Earth. It may add that praise beyond the evidence does not honour a scientist.
12. (write) Is the space station falling, or is it staying up? Give your own answer, with reasons.  
   **Answer:** Either answer can be defended. It is falling, because only gravity acts on it and it curves towards the Earth all the time. It is staying up, because it never gets closer to the ground. A good answer says what the writer means by falling.. *Why:* A strong answer says clearly what 'falling' means to the writer and uses the idea of sideways speed. There is no single right answer to this question.
13. (draw) Draw the Earth and the Moon going round their shared balance point. Mark where the point is.  
   **Answer:** A large Earth and a small Moon on opposite sides of a point. The point is inside the Earth, about three quarters of the way from its centre to its surface. The Earth makes a small circle round it and the Moon a large one.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the point inside the Earth, on the side facing the Moon, and keeps the Earth and the Moon on opposite sides of it.
14. (perspective) Two scientists explain why an astronaut in orbit floats.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer chooses one and says why. It also notes that both give the same result for every measurement in orbit, and that Einstein's picture explains a few things Newton's does not.. *Why:* Both ways give the right numbers for the space station. Newton's is simpler and is used to plan most flights. Einstein's goes deeper and is needed for very exact clocks. Neither is 'the' true one for every purpose.
15. (label) On a rocket's path to orbit, label lift-off, the lean, a stage dropping away, and the satellite's release.  
   **Answer:** ['Lift-off: at the launch pad on the coast', 'Leans over: soon after lift-off, while still in the air', 'A used stage drops: over the sea, part of the way up', 'Satellite released: at the top, moving level at orbital speed']. *Why:* The rocket goes straight up only at first. It then leans over to build sideways speed. Empty stages drop into the sea so that the rest is lighter. The satellite is let go when it is moving level and fast enough.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is an artificial satellite?  
   Options: The Moon / Titan / Cartosat / The Earth  
   **Answer:** Cartosat. *Why:* Cartosat is a mapping satellite built by ISRO. The Moon and Titan are moons, and the Earth is a planet. Nobody made them.
2. (mcq) What does a planet go round, and what does a moon go round?  
   Options: A planet: a star. A moon: a planet / Both go round a star only / A planet: a moon. A moon: a star / Neither goes round anything  
   **Answer:** A planet: a star. A moon: a planet. *Why:* A planet is a satellite of a star, and a moon is a satellite of a planet. The Moon also travels round the Sun, along with the Earth.
3. (mcq) Why does a satellite in orbit not hit the ground?  
   Options: Gravity does not reach it / It falls, and the ground curves away as fast / Thin air holds it up / Its engine holds it up  
   **Answer:** It falls, and the ground curves away as fast. *Why:* The satellite falls all the time. It moves sideways so fast that the round Earth curves away beneath it by the same amount. Gravity does reach it, and its engine is off.
4. (mcq) A satellite is in orbit above the air. What would it do if gravity vanished?  
   Options: Stay where it is / Go straight on, away from the Earth / Drop to the ground / Carry on in its circle  
   **Answer:** Go straight on, away from the Earth. *Why:* A moving thing goes straight unless something bends its path. Gravity is what bends a satellite's path into a circle. Without it, the satellite would leave along a straight line.
5. (mcq) A thing above the air moves sideways at 9 kilometres every second. What is its path?  
   Options: A circle / A stretched loop that comes back / Straight down / A path that never comes back  
   **Answer:** A stretched loop that comes back. *Why:* It is faster than the 7.8 needed for a circle and slower than the 11.2 needed to escape. So it swings out on an ellipse and returns.
6. (truefalse) True or false: a spacecraft that reaches escape speed has gone beyond the reach of the Earth's gravity.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Earth's pull never ends. It only grows weaker. Escape speed means the spacecraft will never fall back, although the pull goes on slowing it a little for ever.
7. (mcq) Imagine the space station standing still on a tower 400 kilometres tall. What would the astronauts feel?  
   Options: They would float / Nearly their normal weight / No weight at all / One sixth of their weight  
   **Answer:** Nearly their normal weight. *Why:* Gravity at that height is almost nine tenths as strong as on the ground, and the tower's floor would push up on them. They float in orbit only because the floor is falling with them. One sixth is the Moon's surface.
8. (mcq) Which of these satellites takes the longest to go once round the Earth?  
   Options: One that is 400 kilometres up / One that is 20,000 kilometres up / One that is 36,000 kilometres up / They all take the same time  
   **Answer:** One that is 36,000 kilometres up. *Why:* The higher the orbit, the weaker the pull, the slower the satellite and the longer its way round. The three trips take about 92 minutes, 12 hours and 1 day.
9. (mcq) You whirl a pouch on a thread and double the hanging load. To keep the same circle, how must you whirl?  
   Options: Faster / More slowly / At the same speed / It makes no difference  
   **Answer:** Faster. *Why:* A stronger pull bends the path more sharply, so the pouch must go faster to stay out on the same circle. If you whirl at the old speed, the load pulls the pouch in.
10. (mcq) Which satellite would you choose to photograph every village in India in sharp detail?  
   Options: One in geostationary orbit / One in a low polar orbit / One in orbit round the Moon / A navigation satellite  
   **Answer:** One in a low polar orbit. *Why:* A low satellite is close enough to see detail. In a polar orbit, the Earth turns beneath it, so in time it passes over every village. A geostationary satellite is about 60 times farther away.
11. (mcq) What did Mangalyaan do in September 2014?  
   Options: It landed on the Moon / It went into orbit round Mars / It became India's first satellite / It carried astronauts to a station  
   **Answer:** It went into orbit round Mars. *Why:* Mangalyaan went into orbit round Mars on 24 September 2014, at India's first try. The Moon landing was Chandrayaan-3 in 2023, and the first satellite was Aryabhata in 1975.
12. (mcq) Why do some scientists worry about tens of thousands of new satellites?  
   Options: Crashes can make debris that causes more crashes / Satellites use up the Earth's gravity / They make the Earth much heavier / They block the Sun's light by day  
   **Answer:** Crashes can make debris that causes more crashes. *Why:* Donald Kessler showed in 1978 that in a crowded orbit, each crash makes pieces that can cause further crashes. Gravity cannot be used up, and satellites are tiny beside the Earth.
13. (truefalse) True or false: scientists all agree on why stars at the edges of galaxies move so fast.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most think unseen dark matter adds its pull, but no particle of it has been found. A smaller group thinks the rule for gravity needs changing when the pull is very weak.

## Applications
- Weather satellites such as INSAT-3DS track cyclones over the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea, so that people on the coast can move in time.
- Navigation satellites tell phones, buses, trains, ships and fishing boats where they are. India's own system is NavIC.
- Television, village telephone links and bank machines in far-off places send their signals through geostationary satellites.
- Mapping satellites such as Cartosat and Resourcesat measure crops, floods, forest cover, city growth and the shrinking of glaciers.
- Oceansat's pictures of the sea are turned into daily advice for fishers on where fish are likely to gather.
- Space agencies plan every flight to the Moon and Mars as a chain of orbits, using gravity to save fuel.
- Astronomers weigh a planet, a star or a whole galaxy by timing whatever goes round it.
- Doctors use what astronauts' bodies do in orbit to understand bone loss and balance problems in people on the ground.

## Open questions
- **Why do the outer stars of galaxies go round so fast?** Either there is unseen dark matter, or the rule for gravity changes when the pull is very weak. Nobody has found a dark matter particle, and no changed rule fits every measurement.
- **How exactly did the Moon form?** Most scientists think a world the size of Mars hit the young Earth, and the Moon gathered from the wreck. But Moon rocks are almost the same as Earth rocks, and a simple crash should have left more of the other world in them.
- **How much water ice is in the dark craters at the Moon's poles, and can it be used?** Chandrayaan-1 and other orbiters saw signs of ice from above. No machine has yet dug into a dark crater floor and measured it.
- **How many satellites and pieces of debris can low orbit hold before crashes begin to multiply?** It depends on how well satellites steer, how often they fail, and how fast the thin air clears each height. The models disagree, and the only full test would be the disaster itself.
- **Can humans and other animals stay healthy for years without the push of the ground?** The longest single stay in orbit is about 14 months. Nobody knows what three years, the time for a trip to Mars and back, does to bones, eyes and the heart, or whether a baby could grow normally.
- **Is there a large unseen planet far beyond Neptune?** In 2016 Konstantin Batygin and Michael Brown showed that some small far-off worlds have orbits that line up strangely. A hidden planet could explain it. So could chance, or the places where telescopes happened to look. Nobody has seen the planet.

## Connected maths
- On your drawing, 1 centimetre stands for 1,000 kilometres. A geostationary satellite is 35,786 kilometres above the ground. How many centimetres from the circle is that, to the nearest centimetre? **36 centimetres.**. Divide by 1,000. 35,786 divided by 1,000 is 35.786, which rounds to 36. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- The space station moves 7.7 kilometres every second. How far does it go in one minute? **462 kilometres.**. One minute is 60 seconds. 7.7 times 60 is 462. That is farther than Chennai to Madurai, in one minute. (queues `math.time.speed.L5`)
- The space station takes 92 minutes for one trip round the Earth. How many full trips does it finish in 24 hours, and how many minutes are left over? **15 full trips, with 60 minutes left over.**. 24 hours is 1,440 minutes. 15 times 92 is 1,380, and 16 times 92 is 1,472, which is too many. 1,440 minus 1,380 is 60. (queues `math.division.remainders-and-cycles.L5`)
- In Orbit in Your Hand, three tries for ten rounds gave 10.4, 9.8 and 10.9 seconds. What is the middle time? How long did one round take at that time? **The middle time is 10.4 seconds. One round took 1.04 seconds.**. Put the times in order: 9.8, 10.4, 10.9. The middle one is 10.4. Divide by 10 to get the time for one round, which is 1.04 seconds. (queues `math.data.averages.L5`)
- The Moon moves away from the Earth by 3.8 centimetres every year. How far will it move in 100 years? Give the answer in metres. **3.8 metres.**. 3.8 times 100 is 380 centimetres. 380 centimetres is 3.8 metres. That is a tiny part of 3,84,400 kilometres. (queues `math.fractions.decimal-operations.L5`)
- A satellite is moved to 3 times its distance from the Earth's centre. The Earth pulled it with 90 newtons before. How hard does the Earth pull it now? **10 newtons.**. The pull falls with the distance multiplied by itself. 3 times 3 is 9. 90 divided by 9 is 10. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- The space station's circle round the Earth is about 42,500 kilometres long. One trip takes 92 minutes. About how many kilometres does it cover each minute? **About 462 kilometres each minute.**. 42,500 divided by 92 is about 462. This matches 7.7 kilometres every second, because 7.7 times 60 is 462. (queues `math.shapes-2d.circles.L5`)
