# Diseases and medicines: what causes an illness, what a medicine does, and how we know it works
`sci.health.diseases-and-medicines.L5`

**Summary.** Limp coriander stems stand up just as well in plain water as in sugar water. A cold also ends in a week with or without a remedy. So how do we know a medicine works? In this lesson you will run a fair trial on wilted stems, and track doses of tea in a glass.

**Intro.** *A cold goes away about a week after you take a remedy. A cold also goes away about a week after you take nothing. So how can anyone find out whether a remedy works?* People have used remedies for thousands of years, and many of them did nothing. For about two thousand years, doctors let blood out of ill people, and it harmed far more than it helped. Nobody could tell, because many ill people get better whatever is done to them. In this lesson you find out how people learned to tell a real medicine from a useless one. First you learn that every disease has a cause, and that a treatment must fit the cause. You use a glass of water and spoons of tea as a model of a body. You see how a medicine builds up there and leaves. You run a fair trial on wilted coriander stems, with a remedy group and a comparison group. You test whether a label changes how sweet a drink tastes. No real medicine is used anywhere in this lesson. Medicines are only for a grown-up to give, in the way a doctor says. By the end you can say what a medicine can do and what only the body can do. You can also say what counts as evidence that a medicine works.

## Terms
- **Disease**: A condition in which part of the body, or the mind, does not work as it should, for some time. *e.g.* Malaria, anaemia, diabetes and asthma are all diseases, and each has a different cause.
- **Cause**: The thing that makes a disease begin. Take the cause away early enough, and the disease does not happen. *e.g.* The cause of malaria is a tiny parasite passed on by a mosquito. The cause of goitre is too little iodine.
- **Symptom**: A change that an ill person feels or shows, such as fever, pain, a cough or tiredness. *e.g.* A fever is a symptom. Malaria, flu and typhoid can each cause it, so a fever alone does not name the disease.
- **Diagnosis**: Finding out which disease a person has, and what caused it. A doctor asks questions, examines the body and may order tests. *e.g.* A doctor sends a drop of blood to be tested for malaria parasites before choosing a medicine.
- **Deficiency disease**: A disease caused by too little of one nutrient for a long time. *e.g.* Too little iron causes anaemia. Too little vitamin C causes scurvy.
- **Long-term disease**: A disease that lasts for years, and that medicines can control but usually cannot end. Doctors also say 'chronic disease'. *e.g.* Diabetes and asthma are long-term diseases. A person takes medicine for them for many years and lives a full life.
- **Lifestyle disease**: A disease that grows slowly over years, helped along by daily habits such as too much sugar and salt, too little movement, tobacco and alcohol. *e.g.* Type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease are lifestyle diseases.
- **Blood pressure**: How hard the blood pushes on the walls of the arteries as the heart pumps it round the body. *e.g.* A nurse wraps a cuff round your arm to measure your blood pressure.
- **Medicine**: A substance used to treat, ease or prevent a disease. A medicine is also called a drug. *e.g.* An antibiotic, a vaccine, an iron tablet and an asthma inhaler are all medicines, and each does a different job.
- **Antibiotic**: A medicine that kills bacteria or stops them multiplying. An antibiotic does nothing to viruses. *e.g.* A doctor may give an antibiotic for typhoid, which bacteria cause. A virus causes a cold, so an antibiotic cannot help a cold.
- **Dose**: The amount of a medicine to be given at one time. The doctor or the label also says how often. *e.g.* A label may say 5 millilitres, three times a day. The 5 millilitres is the dose.
- **Course**: All the doses of a medicine that a doctor orders for one illness, from the first to the last. *e.g.* A course may be one tablet twice a day for five days. That is ten tablets in all.
- **Half-life**: The time a body takes to remove half of a medicine that is in the blood. *e.g.* A medicine has a half-life of 4 hours. Half of a dose is left after 4 hours, and a quarter after 8 hours.
- **Side effect**: An effect of a medicine that nobody wanted, such as sleepiness, a rash or a stomach ache. *e.g.* Some cold medicines make people sleepy. Sleepiness is a side effect of those medicines.
- **Prescription**: A doctor's written order for a medicine. It names the medicine, the dose, the times and the number of days. *e.g.* The chemist reads the prescription before selling an antibiotic.
- **Generic name**: The name of the substance in a medicine that does the work. Many companies sell the same substance under their own brand names. *e.g.* Paracetamol is a generic name. It is sold in India under dozens of brand names.
- **Self-limiting illness**: An illness that the body ends by its own defences and repair, usually within days. *e.g.* A common cold is self-limiting. Most colds are over in seven to ten days, with or without a remedy.
- **Clinical trial**: A test of a medicine on people who agree to take part. Its results are counted and compared. *e.g.* In a trial in 1948, patients with tuberculosis were put into two groups by chance. One group was given a new medicine.
- **Control group**: The group in a trial that does not get the new treatment. The treated group is compared with this group. *e.g.* In the Wilted Stems Trial, the stems in plain water are the control group.
- **Random**: Decided by chance alone, such as a coin toss, so that nobody's choice can tilt the result. *e.g.* A coin toss sends each stem to glass A or glass B. So the fresher stems cannot all end up in one glass.
- **Placebo**: A dummy treatment that looks like the real one and holds no medicine. Trials use it for the control group. *e.g.* In a trial, half the people may get the real tablet and half a tablet made of plain starch. The starch tablet is the placebo.
- **Blind test**: A test in which the people being tested do not know which treatment they got. Often the people who measure do not know either. *e.g.* In the Label Test, the second round is blind, because the cups carry no labels.
- **Evidence**: Facts, counts or measurements that show whether a claim is true. *e.g.* 'My cousin took it and got well' is weak evidence. 'Of 55 people who took it, 51 lived' is stronger evidence.
- **Prevention**: Stopping a disease before it starts, by blocking its cause. *e.g.* Iodised salt prevents goitre. A mosquito net prevents malaria. A vaccine prevents measles.

## Explanations
### Every disease has a cause, and treatment starts by finding it · *defined*
**Definition:** A symptom is what an illness shows. A cause is what makes the illness begin. A treatment works only if it fits the cause.

Fever, tiredness, cough and stomach ache are symptoms. One symptom can come from many causes. A fever may come from malaria, typhoid, dengue, flu or an infected tooth. Each of these needs a different treatment, and some need no medicine at all. So a doctor first finds the cause. This is called diagnosis. The doctor asks when the illness began, what the person ate and where they travelled. The doctor looks, listens and feels, and may order a test of blood, urine or spit. A medicine chosen without a diagnosis is a guess. A wrong guess wastes days, and the real cause goes on working. A medicine that only hides a symptom can also hide the clue the doctor needs.
- e.g. A fever with shivering every second day points to malaria, and a blood test confirms it.
- e.g. Tiredness may come from anaemia, poor sleep, diabetes or tuberculosis. Each has a different treatment.
- e.g. A cough that lasts more than two weeks needs a test for tuberculosis. Cough syrup will not show the cause.
### Five kinds of cause · *defined*
**Definition:** A disease may be caused by a germ, a missing nutrient or a body part that stops working well. Harm from outside and an inherited fault are causes too.

First, germs. Bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites enter the body and multiply. Cholera, malaria and tuberculosis begin this way. Second, a missing nutrient. A body short of iron, iodine or a vitamin for months falls ill. These are deficiency diseases. Third, a body part that stops working well. In type 1 diabetes, the cells of the pancreas that make insulin are destroyed. In a cataract, the lens of the eye turns cloudy with age. Fourth, harm from outside and from the way we live. Tobacco smoke, dirty air, alcohol, too little movement and too much sugar and salt damage the body slowly. Fifth, an inherited fault. A child gets it from the parents in their genes, as in sickle cell disease. Many diseases have two causes working together. Tuberculosis needs the germ, and the disease strikes hardest where people are short of food.
- e.g. Diseases caused by germs include typhoid, dengue, measles and ringworm.
- e.g. Diseases caused by a missing nutrient include anaemia, goitre, rickets and scurvy.
- e.g. Diseases caused by a body part that stops working well include type 1 diabetes and cataract.
- e.g. Diseases caused by harm from outside include lung disease from tobacco smoke and many heart attacks.
- e.g. Diseases that are inherited include sickle cell disease and thalassaemia.
### Common infectious diseases: the germ, the road and the protection · *defined*
**Definition:** An infectious disease is caused by a germ. The germ reaches a person by a certain road, and blocking that road prevents the disease.

Cholera, typhoid and most diarrhoea come from germs in dirty water and food. Safe water, cooked food, a toilet and soap stop them. A child with loose motions needs oral rehydration solution, called ORS, and a doctor if they cannot drink. Malaria is caused by a parasite, and female Anopheles mosquitoes carry the parasite. Dengue is caused by a virus, carried by Aedes mosquitoes, who bite in the day. Filariasis is caused by thin worms, also carried by mosquitoes, and it can make a leg swell greatly. Nets, covered water and no standing puddles stop all three. Tuberculosis, flu, colds, measles and pneumonia travel in the air from coughs and sneezes. Fresh air, covered coughs and vaccines stop many of them. HIV is a virus passed on by infected blood, and from a mother to her baby. HIV is not passed on by touching, sharing food or mosquitoes. Medicines now keep people with HIV well for a full life.
- e.g. Cholera and typhoid are caused by bacteria, and they spread through water and food. Typhoid has a vaccine and is treated with an antibiotic.
- e.g. Malaria is caused by a parasite, and mosquitoes carry the parasite from person to person. A blood test confirms malaria, and antimalarial medicines treat it.
- e.g. Tuberculosis is caused by a bacterium and spreads through the air. Several medicines taken together for at least six months cure the disease. They are free at government centres.
- e.g. Measles and polio are caused by viruses. Both are prevented by vaccines given to babies.
### Deficiency diseases: the cure is the missing nutrient · *defined*
**Definition:** A deficiency disease is caused by too little of one nutrient for a long time. It is cured and prevented by supplying that nutrient.

Too little iron causes anaemia. The blood carries less oxygen, and the person is pale, tired and breathless. Too little vitamin A causes night blindness. Too little vitamin D or calcium causes rickets, in which a child's leg bones bend. Too little iodine causes goitre, a swelling at the front of the neck. Too little vitamin B1 causes beriberi, which weakens the legs and the heart. Too little vitamin C causes scurvy, with bleeding gums. Small children who get far too little protein and energy develop kwashiorkor or marasmus. Kwashiorkor shows as swollen feet, and marasmus as extreme thinness. For centuries people looked for a germ or bad air behind these diseases, and found none. The cause was something absent. The treatment is the nutrient itself, from food, from fortified food such as iodised salt, or from tablets. Iron and vitamin tablets are medicines too. Too many can poison a child, so a doctor decides the dose.
- e.g. Anaemia is prevented by iron from ragi, bajra, chana and greens, eaten with lemon. A doctor may add iron tablets.
- e.g. Night blindness is prevented by vitamin A from drumstick leaves, carrot, papaya and mango.
- e.g. Goitre is prevented by iodine from iodised salt.
- e.g. Beriberi is prevented by vitamin B1 from unpolished rice, whole grains and dals.
### Long-term diseases: medicines that control and do not cure · *defined*
**Definition:** A long-term disease stays for years. Its medicine does the work of a failing part, or holds the symptoms down. This lasts only while the medicine is taken.

In type 1 diabetes the pancreas stops making insulin. Insulin is the hormone that lets sugar pass from the blood into the cells. Before 1922, a child with this disease lived only a year or two. In January 1922 doctors in Toronto, Canada, injected insulin into Leonard Thompson, a boy of 14, and he lived. Insulin does not repair the pancreas. It replaces what the pancreas no longer makes, every day. In type 2 diabetes the body makes insulin but responds to it poorly. Food, exercise and tablets control it. About ten crore people in India have diabetes. In asthma, the airways of the lungs swell and tighten. One inhaler opens the airways within minutes. Another, used daily, keeps the swelling down. High blood pressure is controlled by daily tablets, which lowers the chance of a heart attack or a stroke. Cancer is treated by surgery, by medicines and by radiation. Not smoking, moving every day and eating well lower the chance of many long-term diseases.
- e.g. Insulin injections replace the insulin that the pancreas no longer makes.
- e.g. A reliever inhaler opens tight airways in an asthma attack. A preventer inhaler is used every day.
- e.g. A person with high blood pressure may take one tablet daily for decades and stay well.
### Diseases of the way we live · *defined*
**Definition:** A lifestyle disease grows slowly over many years, and daily habits help bring it on. Obesity, high blood pressure and type 2 diabetes are examples. Habits are only part of the cause, because the body and the family we are born into matter too.

Obesity means carrying far more body fat than is healthy. It builds up when a body takes in more energy from food than it uses, year after year. Sugary drinks, fried snacks and long sitting add up. Sleep, family and the food a family can afford play a part too. High blood pressure means the blood pushes too hard on the walls of the arteries, the tubes that carry blood from the heart. Too much salt, too little movement and extra weight raise it. It usually gives no signs, so only a check with a cuff can find it. Type 2 diabetes has signs you can notice. A person feels very thirsty, passes urine often, sees things blurred and finds that wounds heal slowly. A healthy weight, daily movement, and less sugar and fried food lower the chance. It can also run in families, so a blood test is the way to be sure. Heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and some cancers all become more likely with too much sugar and fat and too little movement. More likely does not mean certain. Some people with good habits fall ill, and some with poor habits do not.
- e.g. A father with a cuff on his arm at a health camp finds that his blood pressure is high, though he feels well. He eats less salt and walks every evening.
- e.g. A woman who is very thirsty, passes urine often and has a foot wound that will not heal gets a blood test. It shows type 2 diabetes.
- e.g. A boy swaps a sugary drink for water or buttermilk each day. Over the years this helps keep his weight healthy.
### Tobacco and alcohol: harm to the whole family · *defined*
**Definition:** Tobacco and alcohol damage the body over years. Their harm reaches children, unborn babies and the whole family.

Smoking bidis or cigarettes raises the risk of lung cancer and of cancer of the lip and mouth. It causes long coughs and other breathing diseases. Children who live in a smoky home get pneumonia more often. A woman who smokes, or breathes a lot of smoke, while she carries a baby can harm her baby before the baby is born. Drinking a lot of alcohol for years damages the liver, which can scar and harden. This is called cirrhosis. It harms the whole family, not just the person who drinks. Money meant for food and school fees may go on drink, and the home can become an unsafe place. These habits can change. Stopping tobacco at any age lowers the risk, and the sooner the better.
- e.g. A bidi worker in a small town coughs every morning. A doctor tells him that quitting now will still help his lungs.
- e.g. A girl whose uncle smokes indoors has a cough every winter. Her family asks him to smoke outside, and then to stop.
### What a medicine does, and what only the body can do · *defined*
**Definition:** A medicine removes a cause, replaces something missing, eases a symptom or prevents a disease. The body does the repair.

An antibiotic kills bacteria or stops them multiplying. The body's own defenders then clear what is left, and the body mends the damaged tissue. An iron tablet supplies iron, and the bone marrow builds the new blood cells. ORS replaces lost water and salts, while the gut clears the germ and regrows the gut lining. Paracetamol lowers a fever and dulls pain, so a child can rest and drink. It does nothing to the cause. A vaccine shows the defenders a harmless part of a germ, and the defenders build the protection. A doctor's stitches hold the edges of a deep cut together. The skin cells then join the edges, and no medicine does that for them. A plaster cast holds a broken bone still, and the bone knits itself. So in every treatment, the medicine does one exact job. The body does the mending.
- e.g. Stitches hold a cut closed. New skin cells join the edges in about a week.
- e.g. An antibiotic lowers the number of bacteria. White blood cells clear the rest.
- e.g. A cast holds a child's broken arm still for four to six weeks while bone cells bridge the break.
### How a medicine reaches the place where it works · *defined*
**Definition:** A swallowed medicine dissolves in the gut, passes into the blood and is carried to every part of the body.

A tablet breaks up in the stomach and dissolves. In the small intestine the dissolved medicine passes through the gut wall into the blood. The blood goes first to the liver, and then the heart pumps it everywhere. This takes about half an hour to an hour. The medicine reaches the sore throat, and also the toes, the skin and the brain. A medicine acts only where its molecules fit. Each kind of medicine molecule fits one kind of molecule in the body or in a germ. A key fits a lock in a similar way. Some medicines are put straight where they are needed. An inhaler sends a mist into the lungs, so its dose can be about thirty times smaller than a tablet's. Eye drops go on the eye and ointment on the skin. An injection puts medicine into a muscle or a vein, for medicines that the stomach would destroy. Insulin is one of these.
- e.g. A syrup acts a little sooner than a tablet, because it is already dissolved.
- e.g. A puff from an asthma inhaler holds a tiny dose, and nearly all of it goes to the lungs.
- e.g. Insulin is a protein. The stomach would digest it like food, so it is injected.
### How a medicine leaves the body, and why doses are spaced · *defined*
**Definition:** The liver changes a medicine and the kidneys pass it out in urine. The time taken to remove half of it is called its half-life.

The body starts to remove a medicine as soon as it arrives. The liver changes the molecules, and the kidneys filter them into the urine. For most medicines, the body removes about half of whatever is there in a fixed time. This time is the half-life. It is about an hour for some medicines and several days for others. After one half-life, half is left. After two, a quarter is left. So one dose fades below the level that works, and another dose is needed. In the Half-Glass Test, each pour removed half of the tea, and each dose added 2 teaspoons. The tea in the glass rose to nearly 4 teaspoons and stayed there. A fuller glass loses more at each pour, so the level stops rising. Makers of a medicine use this sum to set the dose and the gap between doses. The gap keeps the level between too little and too much.
- e.g. In the Half-Glass Test, the glass held 2 teaspoons of tea after the first dose, then 3, then 3 and a half. It never went above 4 teaspoons.
- e.g. A medicine with a short half-life may be given three or four times a day.
- e.g. A medicine with a long half-life may be given once a day, or once a week.
### The dose: enough to work, and too little to harm · *defined*
**Definition:** A dose is the amount given at one time. The right dose keeps the medicine in the blood above the level that works and below the level that harms.

With too little medicine in the blood, nothing happens. With too much, the medicine harms the body. Too much paracetamol damages the liver, and too many iron tablets can kill a small child. For some medicines the safe range is wide, and for others it is narrow. The right dose depends on the body. A child weighs less than a grown-up, so the same tablet makes a stronger mixture in a child's blood. A child's liver and kidneys also clear some medicines at a different speed. So a child's dose is worked out from the child's weight, and it is never a guess. In the Half-Glass Test, a double dose at every turn took the tea up towards 8 teaspoons. That is twice the level of the single doses. Taking doses closer together does the same. This is why nobody takes two doses at once to make up for a missed one. The grown-up reads the label, or asks the doctor or the chemist.
- e.g. A child of 15 kilograms and a grown-up of 60 kilograms may take the same medicine. Their doses differ.
- e.g. In the Half-Glass Test, when every dose was doubled, the level of tea in the glass doubled too.
- e.g. Iron tablets look like sweets to a toddler, so they are kept locked away.
### Side effects: a medicine goes everywhere · *defined*
**Definition:** A side effect is an effect of a medicine that nobody wanted. It happens because the blood carries the medicine to every part of the body.

The blood cannot deliver a tablet to the throat alone. The medicine reaches every organ, and its molecules may fit a lock in more than one place. Some medicines for a runny nose also act on the brain and cause sleepiness. An antibiotic kills the harmful bacteria, and also many helpful bacteria in the gut. So the antibiotic may cause loose motions. A few people are allergic to a medicine. Their defenders attack it, and the result can be a rash or trouble breathing. Every medicine that has an effect can have a side effect. So a doctor weighs the good against the harm for each patient. A medicine that is right for one person may be wrong for a pregnant woman or a baby. Anyone who gets a rash, swelling or trouble breathing after a medicine must tell a grown-up at once.
- e.g. A medicine for a blocked nose may make a person sleepy.
- e.g. An antibiotic may cause loose motions, because helpful gut bacteria die too.
- e.g. A person allergic to penicillin carries a card that says so.
### Getting better anyway: why a remedy seems to work · *defined*
**Definition:** Many illnesses end by the body's own defences and repair. A remedy taken during such an illness gets the credit, whether it did anything or not.

A cold lasts about seven to ten days. A sore throat, a small cut, a bruise and most stomach upsets also end without treatment. Such an illness is called self-limiting. Now think about when people take a remedy. They take it on the day they feel worst. After the worst day, the illness is already on the way down. So the next day feels better, and the remedy seems to have worked. People also remember the times a remedy was followed by recovery, and forget the other times. In the Wilted Stems Trial, sugar water alone would have looked like a cure. The glass of plain water showed that the stems recovered just as well without sugar. For about two thousand years, doctors bled ill people, and both doctors and patients were sure that it helped. Most of the patients who recovered would have recovered anyway.
- e.g. A child drinks pepper rasam on the worst day of a cold and feels better two days later. The cold was already ending.
- e.g. A wart often disappears by itself within two years, so many wart remedies seem to work.
- e.g. In the Wilted Stems Trial, plain water and sugar water revived about the same number of stems.
### The fair trial: how doctors find out whether a medicine works · *defined*
**Definition:** A fair trial compares people who get a treatment with similar people who do not. Chance decides who is in which group.

A trial needs five things. First, two groups, a treated group and a control group, so that getting better anyway happens in both. Second, chance decides who joins which group. If the doctor chose, the doctor might give the new medicine to the stronger patients without meaning to. Third, the control group gets a placebo. This is a dummy that looks and tastes like the medicine, so nobody can tell the groups apart. Fourth, the test is blind. The patients do not know which they got, and the people who measure do not know either. Fifth, many people take part, because a few can mislead by luck. At the end the two groups are counted and compared. Every person must agree to take part, and a trial is stopped early if one group is clearly being harmed. In India, new medicines and vaccines must pass such trials before they are sold.
- e.g. In 1948, 107 patients with tuberculosis were put into two groups by random numbers. Streptomycin clearly saved lives.
- e.g. From 1956, a trial in Madras, now Chennai, compared treatment at home and in hospital. Tuberculosis medicines worked as well at home.
- e.g. A trial of India's Rotavac vaccine gave it or a placebo to 6,799 babies in Delhi, Pune and Vellore.
### The placebo effect: how much can a dummy do? · *debated*
**Definition:** A placebo is a dummy treatment with no medicine in it. People given one often say they feel better. Scientists disagree about how much a placebo really changes.

In trials, many people in the placebo group report less pain or feel less ill. Part of this is getting better anyway. Part of it may be real: expecting relief can change how the brain handles pain. In the Label Test, a label alone may have changed how sweet a drink tasted to your tasters. In 2011, doctors tested this on people with asthma. A dummy inhaler made them feel as much better as the real inhaler did. But when their breathing was measured, only the real inhaler had opened their airways. So a placebo can change what a person feels and reports. Whether a placebo can change a disease is much less sure. This is why a trial measures the body as well as asking the patient, and why it is blind.
- e.g. In the 2011 asthma trial, people felt better after a dummy inhaler, but their measured breathing did not improve.
- e.g. A plaster on a small scrape often stops a child's crying before any healing has happened.
- e.g. In the Label Test, write here whether the label changed your tasters' scores.
- **Henry Beecher, an American doctor, in 1955:** He added up fifteen trials and wrote that about one patient in three got real relief from a placebo. He called the placebo powerful.
- **Asbjørn Hróbjartsson and Peter Gøtzsche, Danish doctors, in 2001:** They studied 114 trials that also had a group given nothing at all. People given nothing improved nearly as much. They found only a small effect, mostly on pain and on what patients said about how they felt.
- **Ted Kaptchuk, an American researcher, in 2010:** He gave people with a painful gut disease dummy pills and told them truthfully that the pills were dummies. They still reported more relief than people given nothing. He thinks the care and the routine of treatment matter in themselves.
### Old remedies: which ones work? · *debated*
**Definition:** An old remedy is a treatment handed down in a medical tradition or a family. Some work, some do nothing and a few do harm. People disagree about how to judge them.

Some of the best modern medicines began as old remedies. In India, the root of the sarpagandha plant was given for centuries to calm people. In 1931 two Indian doctors, Gananath Sen and Kartick Chandra Bose, reported that it lowered blood pressure. In 1949 Dr Rustom Jal Vakil of Bombay tested the root on patients. A medicine made from the root was soon used across the world. In 1972 Tu Youyou in China found the malaria medicine artemisinin. She had tested a plant named in a book 1,600 years old. In about 1916 Alice Ball, a young chemist in Hawaii, is credited with a way to change chaulmoogra oil so that the body could absorb it from an injection. The oil was an old Indian remedy for leprosy, and her method was used for decades. She died young, and for some years her name was left off the work. Other old remedies failed when tested, and bloodletting was one of them. A few do harm. In 2004 a study found lead, mercury or arsenic in one in five Ayurvedic products bought in shops in Boston. So age does not show that a remedy works, and 'natural' does not show that a remedy is safe.
- e.g. Sarpagandha root gave the world a medicine for high blood pressure, after it was tested.
- e.g. Honey eases a child's night cough a little, as a trial in 2007 showed. Honey is never given to a baby under one year.
- e.g. Datura is a wild plant, and their seeds are a strong poison.
- **Many doctors and medicine scientists:** Every remedy, old or new, must pass the same fair trial. Until it does, nobody knows whether it works or what harm it does. A remedy that passes is simply called a medicine.
- **Many physicians of Ayurveda and Siddha:** These systems treat the whole person with food, daily routine and mixtures of many plants, chosen for each patient. A trial that gives one extract to everyone does not test what they really do. Centuries of careful practice are evidence too.
- **Scientists who search old texts, as Tu Youyou did:** Old remedies are a list of good guesses made by careful observers. Most will fail a trial, and a few will become important medicines. So the old books should be read and tested, and neither worshipped nor thrown away.
### Other animals use medicines too · *idea*
**Definition:** Some animals eat or apply particular plants when they are ill. Scientists call this self-medication. They are still working out how the animals know what to take.

People are not the only animals who use medicines. In 1987, at Mahale in Tanzania, a chimpanzee named Chausiku was ill, with a bad stomach and little strength. She chewed the bitter pith of a shrub that chimpanzees normally avoid, and she swallowed only the juice. A day later she was eating and travelling again. Mohamedi Seifu Kalunde, a Tanzanian park worker, was watching with the scientist Michael Huffman. He knew the shrub as a strong medicine. In 2022, in Sumatra, an orangutan named Rakus had a deep wound on his face. He chewed the leaves of a climbing plant. He put the juice and then the mash on the wound. The wound closed in about a week. People in Southeast Asia use the same plant as a traditional medicine. Woolly bear caterpillars with parasites inside them eat more of certain bitter leaves, and more of them survive. Scientists agree on what these animals did. They still argue about how the animals know what to take.
- e.g. Chausiku the chimpanzee chewed a bitter pith when she had a stomach illness.
- e.g. Rakus the orangutan put chewed leaves on his own wound.
- e.g. Many dogs and cats eat grass. Nobody is yet sure whether grass is their medicine.
- **Michael Huffman and other scientists who study animal self-medication:** Apes choose a particular plant when ill, and prepare the plant in a particular way. They avoid the same plant when well. Young apes watch their mothers do it. This looks like knowledge that is learned and passed on.
- **Other animal scientists:** For caterpillars and many insects, the taste for bitter leaves when ill is probably inherited, and no learning is needed. For apes there are still only a few clear cases, so more watching is needed before anyone can be sure.
### Protection: stopping a disease before it starts · *defined*
**Definition:** Prevention means blocking the cause of a disease before the disease starts. It costs less than a cure and spares the person the illness.

Each kind of cause has its own protection. Against germs, the protections are safe drinking water, cooked food, a toilet, hand-washing with soap, mosquito nets, fresh air and vaccines. India's free vaccination programme protects children against about a dozen diseases. Among them are polio, measles, tetanus, diphtheria, whooping cough, tuberculosis and hepatitis B. Smallpox was wiped out across the world by 1980. India had its last case of polio in 2011. Against a missing nutrient, the protections are a varied plate, iodised salt, sunlight, and iron tablets for those a doctor says need them. Against the slow harms of the way we live, the protections are no tobacco, clean air, daily exercise, enough sleep, and not too much sugar, salt and fried food. Against poisoning, medicines and cleaning liquids must be locked away from small children. A check-up can find a disease early, when it is easier to treat. A health worker called an ASHA brings many of these protections to village homes.
- e.g. A mosquito net costs a few hundred rupees. It blocks the night-biting mosquitoes who carry malaria and filariasis.
- e.g. Two drops of polio vaccine cost very little. A child paralysed by polio needs care for a whole lifetime.
- e.g. The iodine in iodised salt costs only a few paise a day, and it prevents goitre.
### Medicines at home: the rules · *defined*
**Definition:** A medicine is given only by a grown-up, in the way a doctor or the label says. A child never takes, tastes, mixes or shares a medicine alone.

First: only a grown-up gives a medicine. If you find a tablet or a bottle, do not taste it. Take it to a grown-up. Second: the right medicine for the cause. A doctor chooses it, and a chemist may sell an antibiotic only against a prescription. In India a red line on the back of a strip marks such medicines. Third: the right dose at the right times, measured with the cup or the spoon that came with the bottle. A kitchen spoon is not a measure. Fourth: never two doses at once to make up for a missed dose. Fifth: never give one person's medicine to another person. Sixth: check the expiry date, and return old medicines to a chemist. Seventh: keep every medicine in its own packet, locked or high up, away from small children. Nobody should call a medicine a sweet. Eighth: tell the doctor about every medicine, tonic and home remedy the person already takes. If a child swallows a medicine by mistake, a grown-up takes child and packet to a hospital at once. The ambulance number in most of India is 108.
- e.g. A red vertical line on a tablet strip means that the medicine is sold only with a doctor's prescription.
- e.g. A syrup is measured in its own cup, because a kitchen teaspoon may hold 3 millilitres or 7.
- e.g. A grandmother's blood pressure tablets can badly harm a toddler who finds them.

## Mostly agreed
- Most colds end in seven to ten days, whatever remedy is taken. *How we know:* Doctors have followed thousands of people with colds, some given remedies and some given none. The colds lasted about the same time in both. In trials, antibiotics did not shorten a cold either.
- A fair trial, with a comparison group chosen by chance, is the reliable way to test a medicine. *How we know:* In 1948, random numbers put 107 patients with tuberculosis into two groups. After six months, 4 of the 55 given streptomycin had died, and 14 of the 52 given bed rest alone.
- A deficiency disease is cured by the missing nutrient, and no germ-killing medicine helps. *How we know:* In 1897 Adolphe Vorderman compared the prisons of Java. Where prisoners ate mostly polished white rice, beriberi was common. Where they ate brown rice, it was very rare.
- Some medicines replace what the body has lost or cannot make, and they save lives without curing the disease. *How we know:* Before insulin, children with type 1 diabetes died within a year or two. Since 1922, people who inject insulin every day live for many decades. In 1971 Dr Dilip Mahalanabis gave ORS to cholera patients in refugee camps at Bongaon in West Bengal. Deaths fell from about 30 in 100 to under 4 in 100.
- The right medicines work as well at home as in a hospital, if they are taken properly. *How we know:* From 1956, a trial in Madras, now Chennai, studied about 190 tuberculosis patients. Chance sent each one to home treatment or to a year in a sanatorium. After a year, the home group had done nearly as well as the sanatorium group.
- Vaccines and clean water prevent more illness than medicines cure. *How we know:* Smallpox once killed about three in ten of the people who caught it. Vaccination ended it everywhere by 1980. India has had no case of polio from the wild virus since January 2011.

## How we found out
**Pierre Louis, a doctor in Paris. Austin Bradford Hill, a British scientist of statistics, with the doctors Marc Daniels and Philip D'Arcy Hart., Louis published his counts in 1835. The streptomycin trial was published in October 1948..** For about two thousand years, doctors in Europe and Asia treated fevers by bloodletting. They opened a vein and let out a bowl of blood. Patients often felt calmer afterwards, and most recovered, so nobody doubted the treatment. In 1835 a doctor in Paris, Pierre Louis, did something new. He counted. He compared 77 patients with pneumonia who had been bled early or late in their illness. More of those bled early died than of those bled late. But Louis could not decide who got which treatment. In 1948 the British scientist Austin Bradford Hill solved that problem. He planned a trial of streptomycin, a new medicine for tuberculosis. Random numbers in sealed envelopes put 107 patients into two groups. After six months, 4 of the 55 patients given streptomycin had died. Of the 52 given bed rest alone, about 14 had died. The doctors who read the chest X-rays did not know which group each patient was in. Since then, a new medicine is trusted only after such a trial.  
*What people believed before:* That bloodletting cures fevers, because patients who were bled usually recovered. They recovered in spite of it. Louis's counts showed no clear benefit, and the 1948 trial showed how to test any treatment fairly: two groups, chosen by chance, counted by people who do not know the groups.

## Challenging perspectives
- **I took the remedy and got better, so the remedy worked.** (A very common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: The recovery did come after the remedy, and a person knows how their own body felt.  
  Against: Most colds, coughs and aches end by themselves. People take remedies on their worst day, after which they improve anyway. Only a comparison with people who took nothing can show what the remedy added.
- **A remedy that has been used for centuries must work.** (Many people in every country believe this.; *rejected*)  
  For: Useless remedies should have been dropped long ago. Some old remedies, such as sarpagandha root, did become medicines.  
  Against: Bloodletting was used for about two thousand years and did harm. A useless remedy can last for centuries, because most of its patients get better anyway.
- **Natural remedies are always safe, because they come from plants.** (Many advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many plant foods and spices are safe in kitchen amounts.  
  Against: Plants make chemicals to defend themselves. Datura seeds and oleander leaves can kill. A plant strong enough to act as a medicine is strong enough to have side effects.
- **An injection is stronger than a tablet.** (Many people in many clinics across India believe this.; *rejected*)  
  For: An injection acts faster, and a few medicines work only when injected.  
  Against: For most illnesses the same medicine by mouth works as well. An injection given without need adds pain, cost and a small risk of infection.
- **A costly branded medicine works better than a cheap generic one.** (Many buyers believe this, and so do some sellers.; *rejected*)  
  For: A known brand may check its quality more carefully, and quality does differ between factories.  
  Against: The generic and the brand hold the same substance in the same dose. Studies that compared them found the same results in patients. India's Jan Aushadhi shops sell tested generics at much lower prices.
- **A dummy tablet can heal real disease, if the patient believes in it.** (Some doctors believe this, and many writers do too.; *debated*)  
  For: People given a placebo often report less pain and feel better.  
  Against: When the body is measured, a placebo changes little. In the 2011 asthma trial, people felt better after a dummy inhaler, but their airways had not opened.

## Roots
In this lesson you ran a trial on wilted stems and watched a cut potato grow a new skin. Now we ask what healing tells us about ourselves and about living beings.

**Deep truth.** In the Wilted Stems Trial, stems in plain water stood up as well as stems in sugar water. The stems recovered by drawing water back into their own cells. A body ends most colds and closes most cuts in the same way, by its own repair. A medicine removes a cause or supplies what is missing, and the body does the mending.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the medicine cured me', and give the bottle all the credit. People have done this for thousands of years, which is how useless remedies such as bloodletting lasted so long. A medicine does one exact job. The living cells of the body do the rest, and in many illnesses they do all of it.  
*Evidence:* In the Wilted Stems Trial, sugar water and plain water revive about the same number of stems. Stems on a dry plate stay limp. In the Potato's New Skin, a cut potato grows a tough new layer with nothing put on the cut. In trials, colds last about a week with a remedy or without one. If your own stems or your potato behaved differently, write down what you saw and try again.

- *who am i:* You are a body that repairs itself all day. Scrapes close, colds end and bruises fade, mostly without your noticing or deciding.
- *what is life:* Every living being mends themselves. A potato grows a new skin, a stem refills their cells, and an orangutan treats his own wound. Healing is something living beings do, and a medicine is help that they find or make.
- *what is this world:* One event follows another, and people easily take the first for the cause of the second. The world shows what caused what only when we compare, as the two glasses of stems did.

A cut potato, a limp stem and a child with a cold recover by the work of their own cells. Some other animals search out plants that help them when they are ill. People have learned to make medicines that each do one exact job. So a medicine is one living being's help to another being's own repair. The same power of repair runs through every living body. A doctor's skill is to know when a body needs help and when it needs only time.

- **Tirukkural 948, by Thiruvalluvar, in the chapter on medicine:** “நோய்நாடி நோய்முதல் நாடி அதுதணிக்கும் / வாய்நாடி வாய்ப்பச் செயல்.” Find out the disease. Find out its root cause. Find out the way to remove it. Then act in the way that fits. (This couplet is the first rule of this lesson, written in Tamil more than 1,500 years ago. Five tired people had five causes, and each needed a different answer.)
- **Charaka Samhita, Sutra Sthana, chapter 9:** Treatment stands on four legs: the physician, the medicine, the attendant and the patient. All four must be sound for the treatment to succeed. (Charaka counts the patient as one of the four supports of a cure, beside the medicine. In this lesson, too, the medicine does one job and the patient's own body does the mending.)
- **Hippocratic writings, Epidemics, book 6, section 5:** The body's own nature is the healer of diseases. (The Greek doctors saw that many illnesses end by themselves. But they had no fair trial, so they could not tell which of their own treatments helped. Bloodletting came from the same tradition.)
- Science (settled): Common colds and many other everyday illnesses are ended by the body's own defences, and remedies do not shorten them.
- Science (settled): A wound is closed by the body's own cells. Medicines can keep germs out, but no medicine builds new skin or bone.
- Science (debated): Whether a dummy treatment can change a disease, and not only how a person feels, is still argued over.
- Science (open): Chimpanzees and orangutans have been seen treating their own illnesses and wounds with plants. How they know which plant to use is not yet understood.
- Other view, Many hospital doctors: They would say that this gives the body too much credit. A child with type 1 diabetes dies without insulin. A person with tuberculosis usually dies without medicines. Before vaccines and antibiotics, a great many children died before they grew up. These doctors warn that people who trust the body to heal itself often wait too long. For them, a medicine is often the whole difference between living and dying.

## Story: Across the Stream
*Ponnu, Ajji, Kaveramma, Somanna, Chinni and the estate are made up. These facts are real. Kodagu in Karnataka is a hill district of coffee estates, and its June rains are among the heaviest in India. Streams there rise in hours, and footbridges made of areca logs are sometimes carried away. Kashaya is a hot drink of pepper, dry ginger, tulsi and other herbs, taken for colds across South India. A common cold lasts about seven to ten days with or without any remedy. In a trial in 2007, honey at bedtime eased children's night coughs a little. Honey is never given to a baby under one year. A fever that lasts more than three days, or fast and difficult breathing, needs a doctor.*

The rain came to Kodagu on the first of June, and by the fourth, half the estate school was coughing.

Ponnu was eleven, and she was not worried. Her grandmother's kashaya was known on every hill around. Ajji roasted pepper and dry ginger, pounded them with tulsi, and boiled the powder black. She stirred a spoon of honey into each tumbler. 'Seven days of my kashaya,' people said, 'and the cold is gone.'

But Ajji had left for a wedding in Mangaluru, and she would be away for twelve days. She had given her jar of powder to Kaveramma, who cooked the noon meal at the school.

'Brew one kettle each morning,' Ajji had said. 'One small tumbler for each child who coughs. Ponnu will carry.'

So on Monday morning Kaveramma brewed, and Ponnu carried. Twenty-three children went to the school. Twelve lived in the upper lines, on Ponnu's side of the stream. Eleven lived in the lower lines, on the far side, and crossed every day on a bridge made of two areca logs.

That Monday every child who coughed drank a tumbler and pulled a face.

On Monday night the stream rose. By morning it was brown and loud, and the two logs were gone.

Ponnu stood on the bank with the kettle in her hand. On the far bank stood Somanna, who sat beside her in class, and his small sister Chinni. The water was too loud for shouting. Chinni coughed, and Ponnu saw her shoulders shake, though she heard nothing.

'Nobody crosses until the water falls,' said Kaveramma. 'Not you, not the kettle. Come away.'

Every morning that week, the twelve children of the upper lines drank their kashaya. Every night Ponnu lay awake and listened to the rain on the tiles. She thought of the eleven on the far bank, coughing with nothing to help them. She counted the days for them on her fingers.

By the next Monday, the coughs on her side had gone. It had taken seven days, as people always said. Only little Muthu still sniffed.

On Wednesday the water fell. The men laid two new logs before noon. Ponnu did not wait for the meal. She filled the kettle to the top and ran across the bridge. The kashaya slopped hot over her wrist.

She had her words ready. I am sorry. We could not come. Drink this now, it is not too late.

In the yard of the lower lines, eleven children were playing lagori. Somanna had just knocked down the seven stones, and he was running, and he was not coughing. Chinni was shouting the score. Nobody was coughing.

'When did it stop?' Ponnu asked. She was still out of breath.

'What?'

'Your cough. All of you.'

Somanna thought. 'Sunday. Monday, for Chinni.' He looked at the kettle. 'Is that for us? We are well now.'

Ponnu walked back over the logs slowly. The kettle was still full. Seven days on this bank, with the kashaya. Seven days on that bank, with nothing. She poured the kettle out under a coffee bush and watched the ground drink it.

Ajji came home on Friday, with wedding sweets wrapped in a banana leaf. Ponnu did not touch them.

'Your kashaya does nothing,' she said. Her voice shook. 'They had none, and they got well on the same day as us. Everyone says seven days of your kashaya. It is seven days of nothing, too.'

She waited for Ajji to be angry. Ajji sat down on the step and untied her bundle.

'How did they sleep?' Ajji asked.

'What?'

'The eleven. In the first nights. Did you ask?'

Ponnu had not asked. So on Saturday the two of them crossed the logs together, and Ajji asked the mothers. The mothers said the first four nights had been bad. The children had coughed until the cocks crowed, and nobody in the lines had slept. On Ponnu's side, the children had drunk the warm tumbler and slept.

'I never said my kashaya sends a cold away,' said Ajji, on the way home. 'People said that. A cold goes when the body has finished with it. The body takes about seven days, and I cannot make it six. I only make the nights softer.'

'Then why does everyone say it cures?'

'Because they drink, and afterwards they are well. They tie the two together. Nobody ever saw what you saw.' Ajji stopped on the bridge and looked down at the water. 'I have brewed for thirty years, child, and I never had two banks. The stream gave you one bank with the kashaya and one bank without. That is the only way to know what a remedy adds.'

They walked on. 'And one more thing,' said Ajji. 'When Kaveramma's boy had a fever for five days, and his chest pulled in at every breath, did I brew for him?'

Ponnu remembered. 'You sent for the jeep. They took him to the hospital in Madikeri.'

'A cold is a waiting illness. His was not. The body could not finish that one alone, and the doctor had the medicine that would help. A healer must know which is which. That matters more than any powder.'

On Monday, Chinni caught a new cold from somebody. Ajji brewed that evening, and Ponnu carried a tumbler across the logs.

'This will not make your cold go faster,' she told Chinni. 'It will take seven days anyway. This is for tonight, so that you sleep.'

Chinni drank it and pulled a face.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Ponnu believed what everyone said, that seven days of kashaya cures a cold. The flooded stream made a comparison by accident. One group of children drank the kashaya and one group had none. Both were well in the same seven days. So the kashaya did not shorten the cold. The body ended the cold. What the remedy added could be found only by comparing the two banks: it eased the nights. Ajji also names the limit of home remedies. Some illnesses do not end by themselves, and those need a doctor.

- Ask: A cold ended seven days after a remedy. What else do you need to know before you say that the remedy worked?
- Ask: Why can a comparison show something that thirty years of careful practice did not show?
- Ask: A remedy does not shorten an illness, but it helps a child sleep. Is that remedy worth taking? Why?
- Ask: How can a family tell an illness that needs waiting from an illness that needs a doctor?

### e1 · The Half-Glass Test: does a medicine keep building up when dose follows dose? (40 min)
- **question:** A body removes half of a medicine every few hours. A new dose is given each time. Does the amount in the body keep rising, dose after dose? Or does it stop rising?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Each dose is 2 teaspoons of tea. Before each new dose, half the glass is poured away and replaced with water. After six doses, how much tea will be in the glass: about 4 teaspoons, about 8, or about 12? Will the glass be darker than the TWO DOSES glass? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same glass for the body every time, with the same FULL line and HALF line. Use level teaspoons of the same tea. Pour away exactly to the HALF line, add the dose, and fill with water exactly to the FULL line. Stir before you compare. Compare colours side by side on white paper, in the same light. Only one thing changes: how many doses have gone in.
- **safety:** The tea is a pretend medicine, and no real medicine is used. A grown-up boils and strains the tea, and cuts any bottles. Nobody drinks from the test glasses.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for each dose. Write the teaspoons of tea in the glass before and after each dose, as worked out by halving. Beside each, write which colour glass the BODY glass matches.
- **results table:** Dose number | Teaspoons of tea in the glass just before the dose | Teaspoons of tea in the glass just after the dose | Colour after the dose, against the three colour glasses
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up boils 2 heaped teaspoons of tea leaves in 2 cups of water for 3 minutes. They strain the tea and cool it. 2. Pour 1 tea cup of water into the BODY glass. Mark the water level with the pen. This is the FULL line. 3. Pour from the BODY glass into an empty matching glass until the two levels are equal. Mark this level on the BODY glass. This is the HALF line. Pour the water back. 4. Pour 1 tea cup of water into each of the other three glasses. 5. Stir 1 teaspoon of tea into the LOW glass, 2 into ONE DOSE and 4 into TWO DOSES.  
  *Parent says:* This glass of water is a pretend body, and the tea is a pretend medicine. The three colour glasses show one, two and four teaspoons of tea. We will compare the BODY glass with them.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes strong tea. Mark FULL and HALF on the BODY glass. Make three colour glasses.
  - Note: The tea should be dark. One teaspoon in a cup of water must still show a pale colour against white paper. If the LOW glass looks like plain water, boil the tea longer or use 2 teaspoons as the unit throughout.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child the plan: one dose is 2 teaspoons, and half the glass is poured away before every new dose. 2. Ask for the guess and the reason. 3. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* A body removes half of a medicine every few hours. Then the next dose arrives. After six doses, how much tea will be in the glass? Will it be darker than TWO DOSES? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how the glass will look after six doses. Say why.
  - Ask: Six doses of 2 teaspoons make 12 teaspoons. Will all 12 be in the glass at the end? (good: No, some is poured away each time.; I think half of it will go.; I think most of it stays, because we keep adding.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect the glass to grow darker without limit.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add one dose, 2 teaspoons, to the BODY glass and stir. Compare with ONE DOSE. 2. Pour away to the HALF line and fill to FULL with water. Compare with LOW. 3. Do this twice more, with no new dose. 4. Write the teaspoons left each time: 2, then 1, then a half, then a quarter.  
  *Parent says:* First we give one dose only. Then we pour half away three times. Watch the colour against the LOW glass. How long does one dose last?  
  *Child mode:* Add one dose. Pour half away and refill. Do it three times. Watch the colour fade.
  - Ask: After which pour was the BODY glass paler than the LOW glass? (good: After the second pour.; After two pours only half a teaspoon was left.)
  - Note: After one pour the glass matches LOW. After two it is paler. Say that LOW stands for the smallest amount that still works. One dose falls below it, so another dose is needed. Rinse the BODY glass and fill it with fresh water to FULL.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add dose 1 and stir. Write 2 teaspoons. 2. Pour away to HALF. Add dose 2. Fill to FULL and stir. 3. Work out the tea now in the glass: half of 2 is 1, and 1 plus 2 is 3. Write it. 4. Compare the colour with the three colour glasses. 5. Repeat until six doses have gone in.  
  *Parent says:* Now a dose goes in every time. Before each dose, half is poured away. Work out the teaspoons each time, then check the colour. Is it still getting darker?  
  *Child mode:* Add a dose. Pour to HALF. Add the next dose and refill. Work out the teaspoons. Do six doses.
  - Note: The amounts go 2, 3, 3 and a half, 3 and three-quarters, and then just under 4. The colour comes close to TWO DOSES by the third or fourth dose, and then stops changing. Then empty and rinse the BODY glass.
  - Check: In the Half-Glass Test, the glass holds 3 teaspoons of tea. Half is poured away and a dose of 2 is added. How much tea is in the glass now? → **3 and a half teaspoons**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the rinsed BODY glass with water to the FULL line. 2. This time each dose is 4 teaspoons. Do three doses, pouring to HALF before each. 3. Work out the teaspoons: 4, then 6, then 7. 4. Compare the colour with the TWO DOSES glass, which the six single doses never passed.  
  *Parent says:* Suppose someone takes two doses at once, every time, to get well faster. Work out the teaspoons, and compare with the TWO DOSES glass. What happened to the level?  
  *Child mode:* Now each dose is 4 teaspoons. Do three doses. Compare with the TWO DOSES glass.
  - Ask: The single doses stopped rising near 4 teaspoons. Where will the double doses stop? (good: Near 8 teaspoons.; Twice as high, because every dose is twice as big.)
  - Note: After three double doses the glass holds 7 teaspoons and is clearly darker than TWO DOSES. If TWO DOSES stood for the highest safe level, this body is now above it.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your numbers. What did you guess, and what did you see?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Does the level keep rising?
  - Ask: Why did the tea stop rising near 4 teaspoons? (good: When the glass holds 4 teaspoons, each pour removes 2 teaspoons, and each dose adds 2 teaspoons.; The more tea in the glass, the more each pour takes away.)
  - Ask: What does this tell you about taking two doses at once after a missed dose? (good: The level goes higher than it was meant to go.; It could go above the safe level, so a grown-up asks the doctor instead.)
  - Note: The reason: each pour removes half of whatever is there. A fuller glass loses more. When the glass holds about 4 teaspoons, the pour removes 2 and the dose adds 2, so the level stays. A body treats most medicines this way. The makers choose the dose and the gap so that the level stays between too little and too much. Real bodies remove medicines little by little, and the pour does it all at once. So the glass is a model.
  - Think: A medicine label says 'every 8 hours'. What might happen to the level if the doses were taken every 4 hours instead?
### e2 · The Wilted Stems Trial: does sugar in the water revive limp coriander? (45 min)
- **question:** Do limp coriander stems recover better in sugar water than in plain water? And what would you have thought if you had tried only the sugar water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Six limp stems go into sugar water, six into plain water, and three stay on a dry plate. After two hours, how many in each group will stand up again? Which group will do best? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the stems come from the same bunch and wilt on the same plate for the same time. A coin decides which glass each stem joins, so nobody can pick the fresher stems for one glass. Both glasses hold 1 cup of water and stand side by side. The person who counts does not know which glass has the sugar. Only one thing differs between the glasses: the sugar.
- **safety:** Use scissors with care. The stems are food: rinse them afterwards and use them in tonight's cooking.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for each time: start, 30 minutes, 1 hour and 2 hours. Write how many stems have recovered in glass A, in glass B and on the dry plate.
- **results table:** Time | Glass A: stems recovered, out of 6 | Glass B: stems recovered, out of 6 | Dry plate: stems recovered, out of 3
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take 15 coriander stems of about the same length, with their leaves. 2. Lay them apart on a plate. 3. Leave the plate in a warm room, out of the sun, for 2 to 3 hours.  
  *Parent says:* These coriander stems are fresh now. We will leave them without water until they go limp. Later we will test a way of reviving them.  
  *Child mode:* Lay fifteen coriander stems on a plate. Leave them until they are limp.
  - Note: The stems are ready when they hang down from your fingers but are still green and soft. In a hot, dry room this may take one hour. If the leaves have turned dry and crisp, they have gone too far, so start again with a shorter time.
  - Wait: Leave the stems for 2 to 3 hours, until they are limp
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child the claim: a spoon of sugar in the water revives wilted greens. 2. Ask the first question below, and wait for the answer. 3. Then ask for the guess about three groups, with the reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Some people say sugar in the water revives wilted greens. Suppose we put every stem in sugar water, and they all stand up. Would that show that the sugar works? What else would we need?  
  *Child mode:* People say sugar water revives limp greens. How could you test that fairly? Guess the result. Why?
  - Ask: If all the stems in sugar water stand up, why is that not enough? (good: They might stand up in plain water too.; We need some stems without sugar to compare.; Maybe the water did it and the sugar did nothing.)
  - Note: Let your child reach the idea of a second glass by themselves if they can. Do not say what you expect to happen.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour 1 cup of water into each glass and stand them on the slips A and B. 2. Your child looks away. Stir 1 level teaspoon of sugar into one glass until it has dissolved. Write which glass on the folded slip. 3. Your child keeps 3 stems on the plate. 4. They cut a fingernail's width off the bottom of the other 12. 5. They toss a coin for each stem: heads goes to A, tails to B. When one glass has 6, the rest go to the other.  
  *Parent says:* I will put sugar in one glass while you look away. Then you toss a coin for each stem to choose its glass.  
  *Child mode:* Look away while the sugar goes in. Toss a coin for each stem. Six in each glass.
  - Ask: Why does a coin choose the glass for each stem? (good: So I cannot put the better stems in one glass.; Chance is fair to both glasses.)
  - Ask: Why should the person counting not know which glass has the sugar? (good: They might count more kindly for the glass they expect to win.)
  - Note: A fresh cut helps the stem take up water. Stand the glasses side by side, away from the sun, the stove and the fan.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 30 minutes, lift each stem out by the bottom end and hold the stem upright. 2. If the top stays up or out, count the stem as recovered. If the top hangs down, count it as limp. 3. Put the stem back in the same glass. 4. Write the count for A, for B and for the plate. 5. Do the same after 1 hour.  
  *Parent says:* Hold each stem upright by the bottom end. Does the top stay up, or hang down? Count the recovered stems in each glass, and on the plate.  
  *Child mode:* Hold each stem upright. Does the top stay up? Count for A, B and the plate.
  - Note: Use the same rule for every stem. If you are unsure about a stem, count it as limp in both glasses.
  - Wait: Count after 30 minutes and again after 1 hour
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 2 hours, count all three groups once more. 2. Now open the folded slip and read out which glass had the sugar. 3. Write 'sugar water' and 'plain water' at the top of the right columns.  
  *Parent says:* This is the last count. Now I will open the slip. Which glass do you think had the sugar? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count once more after two hours. Then open the secret slip.
  - Check: In the Wilted Stems Trial, which group shows what happens with no sugar? → **The stems in plain water**
  - Wait: Do this 2 hours after the stems went into the glasses
- **s6 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your counts. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. What did the sugar add?  
  *Child mode:* Which group did best? Tell your grown-up why.
  - Ask: Suppose you had only the sugar glass. What would you have believed? (good: That sugar revives wilted stems.; I would have given the sugar the credit.)
  - Ask: What did the stems on the dry plate show? (good: Stems do not recover without water.; The water was what the stems needed.)
  - Ask: How is this like a cold and a remedy? (good: A cold ends anyway, so the remedy gets credit it has not earned.; You need people who took nothing, to compare.)
  - Note: In most kitchens, both glasses recover about equally and the plate stems stay limp. Write what you really found. If one glass did better by a stem or two, that can be luck with only six stems. Run the trial again before you believe it. The reason: a limp stem's cells have lost water. In the glass, water moves up the stem and refills the cells, and the stem stiffens. The stems do this with plain water. One teaspoon of sugar in a cup adds nothing they can use quickly. If fewer than half the stems recovered in both glasses, they were too dry. Try again with a shorter wilting time, or leave them overnight.
  - Think: A coin sent each stem to a glass. In a medicine trial, what does chance decide, and why?
### e3 · The Label Test: does a label change how sweet a drink tastes? (30 min)
- **question:** Two cups hold the same drink. Do tasters score the cup labelled EXTRA SWEET as sweeter? What do they score when the cups have no labels?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In round 1 the two cups have labels. In round 2 they have none. Will the tasters give the EXTRA SWEET cup a higher score in round 1? Will the two cups score the same in round 2? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Make one jug of the drink and stir it well, so that every cup holds the same drink. Give every taster the same small amount, in the same kind of cup. Each taster scores alone and does not hear the others. Say the same words to each taster. Only one thing changes between the rounds: the labels.
- **safety:** Wash your hands before you make the drink. Each taster drinks from their own cup. Afterwards, tell every taster the truth about the two cups.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for each taster and four columns of scores. Add up each column. Write the difference between the two cups in round 1, and in round 2.
- **results table:** Taster | Round 1: ORDINARY | Round 1: EXTRA SWEET | Round 2: cup with one dot | Round 2: cup with two dots
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stirs 3 level teaspoons of sugar and the juice of half a lemon into 3 cups of water. 2. They fill two serving cups from the jug and label them ORDINARY and EXTRA SWEET. 3. They fill two more cups, and mark one dot under one and two dots under the other. 4. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* All four cups hold the same drink from one jug. Two have labels, and two have only dots underneath. Will the labels change the scores? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make one jug of lemon drink. Fill four cups. Label two. Guess what the tasters will say.
  - Ask: The drinks are the same. So why might the scores differ? (good: People may taste what they expect to taste.; The label tells them it is sweeter, so they believe it.; I think they will not differ, because tongues do not read.)
  - Note: Do not say what you expect. Your child is the tester and knows the secret, so they cannot also be a taster.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Call one taster at a time. 2. Pour a little from the ORDINARY cup into their cup. They sip and write a sweetness score from 1 to 5 on a slip. 3. Do the same from the EXTRA SWEET cup. 4. Collect the slip. Write the two scores in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Please taste these two drinks, one after the other. Score how sweet each one is, from 1 for hardly sweet to 5 for very sweet. Please do not tell anyone your scores.  
  *Child mode:* Call one taster at a time. They sip both drinks. They write a score for each.
  - Note: Your child must keep a plain face and say the same words to everyone. Tasters should not hear each other's scores.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When every taster has done round 1, start round 2. 2. Call each taster again. Pour from the one-dot cup, then from the two-dot cup. 3. They score each from 1 to 5. 4. Write the scores in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Here are two more drinks. These have no names. Please score how sweet each one is, from 1 to 5.  
  *Child mode:* Now serve from the two cups with dots. No labels this time. Collect the scores.
  - Check: In the Label Test, why is round 2 called a blind test? → **The tasters cannot tell which cup is which**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up each of the four columns. 2. Find the difference between the two cups in round 1, and in round 2. 3. Tell every taster kindly that all the cups held the same drink. 4. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Add up each column. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Then let us tell the tasters the truth.  
  *Child mode:* Add up the columns. Say what you thought, what you saw and why. Tell the tasters the truth.
  - Ask: What did the label change, the drink or the taster? (good: The taster. The drink was the same.; What they expected changed what they tasted.)
  - Ask: A patient knows that she got the new medicine and not the dummy. How might that change what she tells the doctor? (good: She may say she feels better because she expects to.; So trials hide who got which.)
  - Note: In many homes the EXTRA SWEET cup scores higher in round 1. The two cups then score about the same in round 2. In some homes the label makes no difference. Write what you really found. With four tasters, a difference of one or two points can be luck, so add more tasters if you can. The reason: the brain builds a taste from the tongue's signal and from what it expects. The same happens with pain and with feeling ill. This is why a medicine trial gives one group a dummy that looks the same, and hides who got which. Nobody was foolish. Everyone's brain works this way.
  - Think: Your tasters were honest, and yet the label may have changed their scores. Can a person always trust what they feel?

## Worksheets
### Causes, medicines and fair tests (core, 18 questions)
1. (mcq) What is the cause of a disease?  
   Options: The thing that makes the disease begin / The symptom that feels worst / The medicine that treats it / The day the illness started  
   **Answer:** The thing that makes the disease begin. *Why:* A cause is what makes a disease begin, such as a germ or a missing nutrient. A symptom is only what the disease shows. If you chose the symptom, remember that one symptom can come from many causes.
2. (sort) Sort these diseases by their kind of cause.  
   **Answer:** malaria → Germ; typhoid → Germ; anaemia → Missing nutrient; goitre → Missing nutrient; type 1 diabetes → A body part stops working; cataract → A body part stops working. *Why:* Malaria and typhoid are caused by germs. Anaemia comes from too little iron, and goitre from too little iodine. In type 1 diabetes the pancreas stops making insulin, and in cataract the lens of the eye clouds.
3. (match) Match each disease to the protection that prevents it.  
   **Answer:** Malaria → A mosquito net; Cholera → Safe drinking water; Measles → A vaccine; Goitre → Iodised salt; Anaemia → Iron-rich food with lemon; Lung disease from smoke → No tobacco. *Why:* Each protection blocks one cause. A net blocks the mosquito, safe water blocks the cholera germ, and iodised salt supplies the missing iodine.
4. (match) Match each habit to the harm it can bring.  
   **Answer:** Smoking bidis or cigarettes → Lung cancer and cancer of the lip and mouth; Drinking a lot of alcohol for years → Cirrhosis of the liver; Too much salt and too little movement → High blood pressure; Too much sugar and sitting still → Weight gain and type 2 diabetes. *Why:* Each habit harms one part of the body slowly. Tobacco harms the lungs and mouth, alcohol the liver, salt the arteries, and sugar with little movement leads to weight gain and diabetes.
5. (fill) A medicine that kills bacteria and does nothing to viruses is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['antibiotic']. *Why:* Antibiotics act on parts that bacteria have and viruses lack. So they cannot help a cold or flu.
6. (truefalse) Paracetamol lowers a fever by killing the germ that caused the fever.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Paracetamol eases the symptom. The germ is untouched, and the body's defenders or another medicine must deal with it.
7. (truefalse) A person with high blood pressure always feels ill, so they will know it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. High blood pressure usually gives no signs for years. A check with a cuff is the only way to find it, and it can still cause a stroke or heart attack.
8. (mcq) What does insulin do for a person with type 1 diabetes?  
   Options: Replaces what the pancreas no longer makes / Kills the diabetes germ / Repairs the pancreas / Eases the pain  
   **Answer:** Replaces what the pancreas no longer makes. *Why:* In type 1 diabetes the pancreas stops making insulin. The injected insulin does that missing job each day. No germ causes diabetes, and insulin does not repair the pancreas.
9. (truefalse) A swallowed tablet travels only to the part of the body that hurts.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The medicine passes into the blood, and the blood carries it to every part. This is why side effects happen.
10. (fill) The time a body takes to remove half of a medicine is called its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['half-life']. *Why:* After one half-life, half the medicine is left. After two, a quarter is left. The half-life decides how far apart the doses are.
11. (count) A glass holds 4 teaspoons of tea. Half is poured away, twice. How many teaspoons are left?  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* Half of 4 is 2, and half of 2 is 1. A medicine in the body falls in the same way, by half in each half-life.
12. (mcq) In the Wilted Stems Trial, why did a coin choose the glass for each stem?  
   Options: So nobody could pick the fresher stems / So the stems recovered faster / Because coins measure wilting / To save time  
   **Answer:** So nobody could pick the fresher stems. *Why:* If a person chose, they might put the perkier stems in the sugar glass without meaning to. Chance treats both glasses alike. A coin does nothing to the stems themselves.
13. (write) In the Wilted Stems Trial, what would the sugar glass alone have made you believe? Why is that a mistake?  
   **Answer:** I would have believed that sugar revives wilted stems, because they stood up. That is a mistake, because the stems in plain water stood up just as well. The water revived them and the sugar added nothing.. *Why:* A strong answer says that recovery after a treatment needs a comparison group. It uses the plain water glass as the evidence.
14. (write) A cold ended a week after a remedy. Give two reasons why this does not show that the remedy worked.  
   **Answer:** First, a cold ends by itself in about a week. Second, people take a remedy on the worst day, and after the worst day a cold improves anyway. Only a comparison with children who took nothing could show what the remedy added.. *Why:* A strong answer names getting better anyway, and says what evidence would be needed.
15. (draw) Draw the path of a swallowed tablet through the body, from the mouth to the urine. Label five stops.  
   **Answer:** Mouth, stomach, small intestine, blood and liver, then the heart pumps it everywhere. The liver changes it, the kidneys filter it out, and it leaves in urine.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the medicine passing into the blood and reaching the whole body. It also shows the liver and kidneys removing it.
16. (perspective) A child has an ordinary cold. Should the family give a home remedy?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. The grandmother is right that easing a symptom is a real help. The neighbour is right that the cold is not shortened. The doctor adds the limit: some illnesses do not end by themselves.. *Why:* All three speakers say something true. A strong answer keeps apart two things: shortening the illness and easing the symptoms.
17. (order) Put the steps of a fair trial in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Find many people who agree to take part', 'Chance puts the people into two groups', 'One group gets the medicine and one gets a dummy', 'Measure everyone without knowing their group', 'Compare the two groups']. *Why:* People must agree first. Chance makes the groups alike. The dummy and the blind measuring keep hopes from changing the result. The comparison comes last.
18. (mcq) You find a loose tablet on the floor at home. What should you do?  
   Options: Take it to a grown-up / Taste it to find out what it is / Put it back in any bottle / Leave it where it is  
   **Answer:** Take it to a grown-up. *Why:* A child never tastes or takes a medicine alone. A tablet in the wrong bottle could be given to the wrong person. A tablet left on the floor could be found by a smaller child.
### Doses, journeys and evidence (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Why is a dose of a medicine repeated after some hours?  
   Options: The body removes the medicine all the time / The germs return every night / The first dose is always too weak / Medicine evaporates from the blood  
   **Answer:** The body removes the medicine all the time. *Why:* The liver and kidneys start removing a medicine as soon as it arrives. After a few hours too little is left to work, so another dose is needed.
2. (mcq) In the Half-Glass Test, why did the tea stop rising near 4 teaspoons?  
   Options: Loss at each pour equalled one dose / The glass was full / Dark tea dissolves less / The spoon held less each time  
   **Answer:** Loss at each pour equalled one dose. *Why:* A pour removes half of what is there. At 4 teaspoons a pour removes 2, and the dose adds 2, so the level stays. The glass was filled to the same line every time.
3. (count) A glass holds 6 teaspoons of tea. Half is poured away, and 4 are added. How many teaspoons now?  
   **Answer:** 7. *Why:* Half of 6 is 3. Adding the dose of 4 makes 7.
4. (truefalse) Taking two doses together is a safe way to make up for a missed dose.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Two doses together push the level in the blood higher than was planned. A grown-up asks the doctor or the chemist what to do.
5. (fill) A very thirsty person who passes urine often, sees things blurred and has wounds that heal slowly may have type 2 ____.  
   **Answer:** ['diabetes']. *Why:* These are the signs of too much sugar in the blood. A blood test from a doctor is the way to be sure. A healthy weight and daily movement lower the chance.
6. (truefalse) An inhaler needs a smaller dose than a tablet, because its medicine goes straight to the lungs.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A swallowed medicine is spread through the whole body. A breathed-in medicine lands where it is needed, so far less is enough.
7. (odd) Three of these are needed for a fair trial. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A comparison group / Chance choosing the groups / Blind measuring / A famous person's support  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A trial needs a comparison group, chance and blind measuring. A famous person's support tells us nothing about whether a medicine works.

   **Answer:** Antibiotic → Kills bacteria; Iron tablet → Supplies a missing nutrient; Insulin → Replaces what the pancreas no longer makes; Paracetamol → Eases fever and pain; Vaccine → Prevents a disease; ORS → Replaces lost water and salts. *Why:* Each medicine does one exact job.    **Answer:** killing bacteria → The treatment; holding the edges of a cut together → The treatment; supplying iron → The treatment; growing new skin across a cut → The body; knitting a broken bone → The body; building new red blood cells → The body. *Why:* An antibiotic, stitches and an iron tablet each do one job. Growing skin, bone and blood cells is done only by living cells.
10. (fill) A dummy treatment with no medicine in it, used in trials, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['placebo']. *Why:* A placebo looks and tastes like the real treatment. It lets the control group expect the same as the treated group.
11. (write) In a trial, the doctor chooses which patients get the new medicine. Explain how this could spoil the trial.  
   **Answer:** Without meaning to, the doctor might give the new medicine to the patients who look stronger. Then the groups differ before the trial starts. A difference at the end could come from the choosing and not from the medicine.. *Why:* A strong answer says the groups must be alike at the start, and that only chance makes them alike.
12. (write) In the Label Test, both cups held the same drink. Explain why their scores can still differ.  
   **Answer:** The label told the tasters to expect a sweeter drink. The brain builds a taste from the tongue's signal and from what it expects. So an honest taster can score the labelled cup higher.. *Why:* A strong answer says that expecting something changes what a person reports, and links this to why trials are blind.
13. (write) A swallowed medicine for a blocked nose can make a person sleepy. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The medicine passes into the blood, and the blood carries it to every part of the body. In the brain its molecules also fit, and there they cause sleepiness.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a swallowed medicine goes everywhere. A side effect is the same medicine acting in another place.
14. (write) Write three ways in which one person's drinking alcohol can harm the whole family.  
   **Answer:** Money meant for food and school fees may go on drink. The home may become unsafe or full of fights. Children may feel afraid and may not study well. The person's liver may also fail, and the family then has a sick person to care for.. *Why:* A strong answer names harm to more than the person who drinks: money, safety, the children, and the care of a sick person.
15. (perspective) In a trial of a new medicine, should half of the ill people get a dummy?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For the patient: wanting the best chance is fair. For the doctor: an untested medicine may be worse than the dummy, as bloodletting was. A strong answer notices that the new medicine is not yet known to be better.

   **Answer:** ['Tablet: the mouth, then the gut and the blood', 'Inhaler: the lungs', 'Eye drops: the eye', 'Ointment: the skin']. *Why:* A tablet reaches the whole body through the blood. The other three put the medicine at the place where it is needed.

### Testing claims about remedies (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Twenty people took a syrup and all recovered. What is the most important thing still unknown?  
   Options: How many recover without the syrup / The colour of the syrup / The price of the syrup / The names of the patients  
   **Answer:** How many recover without the syrup. *Why:* If twenty similar people who took nothing also recovered, the syrup added nothing. Without that comparison the twenty recoveries show very little.
2. (mcq) A trial has only six people in each group. Why is that weak evidence?  
   Options: Luck can decide the result in small groups / Six people cannot swallow tablets / Small groups recover faster / Six is an even number  
   **Answer:** Luck can decide the result in small groups. *Why:* With six in a group, one or two people who recover by luck change the result a great deal. With hundreds, luck evens out.
3. (mcq) In the 2011 asthma trial, what did a dummy inhaler change?  
   Options: How patients felt, and not their measured breathing / Their measured breathing only / Both their feeling and their breathing / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** How patients felt, and not their measured breathing. *Why:* Patients said they felt better after the dummy inhaler. A breathing meter showed that their airways had opened only after the real one.
4. (truefalse) A remedy that has been used for a thousand years must work.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bloodletting was used for about two thousand years and did harm. A useless remedy can last for centuries, because most of its patients get better anyway.
5. (truefalse) A fair trial can show that a treatment does not work.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. If the treated group does no better than the control group, the treatment added nothing. Trials showed this for antibiotics against colds.
6. (count) In a trial, 4 of the 55 patients given a new medicine died. How many of the 55 lived?  
   **Answer:** 51. *Why:* 55 take away 4 is 51. In the group of 52 given bed rest alone, 14 died and 38 lived.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the weakest evidence to the strongest.  
   **Answer:** ["One person's story", 'Twenty recovered patients and no second group', 'Two groups chosen by the doctor', 'Two groups chosen by chance, measured blind']. *Why:* One story is the weakest. Twenty counted patients are better, but have no comparison. A doctor's choice gives a comparison that may be tilted. Chance and blind measuring remove the tilt.
8. (fill) An illness that the body ends by itself within days is called a ____ illness.  
   **Answer:** ['self-limiting']. *Why:* A cold is self-limiting. Any remedy taken during such an illness seems to work.
9. (write) An advertisement says nine in ten users felt better in three days. Write two questions to ask the makers.  
   **Answer:** How many people who used nothing felt better in three days? Who counted, and did they know who had used the product? Other good questions: how many people were asked, and who paid for the count?. *Why:* A strong answer asks for a comparison group and asks who measured.
10. (write) Doses of 2 teaspoons, halved each time, level off near 4. Predict the level for doses of 3. Explain.  
   **Answer:** Near 6 teaspoons. At 6 teaspoons a pour removes 3, and the dose adds 3, so the level stays. The level settles at twice the dose.. *Why:* A strong answer finds the level at which the amount removed equals the dose.
11. (write) An ill chimpanzee chews a bitter plant and recovers. Is this evidence that the plant is a medicine? Explain.  
   **Answer:** It is a hint, and not strong evidence. She might have recovered anyway, and it is one case. It is a good reason to test the plant. The hint is stronger if ill chimpanzees choose the plant and healthy ones avoid the same plant.. *Why:* A strong answer treats the case as a hint worth testing, and says what more would be needed.
12. (write) A stitched cut joins in a week. Say what the stitches did, and what the skin did.  
   **Answer:** The stitches held the two edges together and kept dirt out. The skin cells divided and grew across the gap, and joined the edges. The stitches could not make new skin.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the treatment one exact job and gives the repair to the living cells.
13. (perspective) How should old remedies, such as those of Ayurveda and Siddha, be judged?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For the first: bloodletting lasted two thousand years. For the second: a trial can compare whole treatments too, if two groups are made by chance. For the third: sarpagandha and artemisinin both came from old texts, after testing.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement in India today. A strong answer says what evidence each side would accept.
14. (draw) Draw a graph of the tea in the Half-Glass Test over six doses, before and after each dose.  
   **Answer:** A zigzag line. It jumps up by 2 teaspoons at each dose and falls by half before the next. The tops go 2, 3, 3 and a half, 3 and three-quarters, and then just under 4. The bottoms go 1, 1 and a half, and then just under 2.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the jumps staying the same size and the falls growing bigger. The zigzag levels off below 4.
15. (match) Match each observation to what it shows.  
   **Answer:** Stems in plain water recovered as well as stems in sugar water → The sugar added nothing; Stems on the dry plate stayed limp → The stems needed water; A labelled cup scored sweeter than the same drink unlabelled → Expecting changes what people report; The tea levelled off near 4 teaspoons → A fuller glass loses more at each pour; A cut potato face turned dry and tough → Living cells repair a wound. *Why:* Each observation is evidence, and each conclusion says only what that evidence supports.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Three people have a fever. Why might each one need a different treatment?  
   Options: One symptom can have different causes / Fevers differ in colour / Doctors like variety / Each person needs the newest medicine  
   **Answer:** One symptom can have different causes. *Why:* A fever is a symptom. Malaria, typhoid and flu each cause one, and each needs a different treatment. So the cause must be found first.
2. (mcq) Which of these protects a sleeping family against malaria?  
   Options: A mosquito net / Iodised salt / Boiled drinking water / An antibiotic kept at home  
   **Answer:** A mosquito net. *Why:* Malaria parasites are carried by mosquitoes who bite at night. A net blocks them. Boiled water blocks the germs of cholera and typhoid.
3. (mcq) Which disease is cured by a nutrient, and not by a medicine that kills germs?  
   Options: Goitre / Typhoid / Malaria / Tuberculosis  
   **Answer:** Goitre. *Why:* Goitre comes from too little iodine, and iodised salt prevents and treats it. The other three are caused by germs.
4. (mcq) Why do doctors say that a child living in a home full of tobacco smoke is at risk?  
   Options: The child breathes the smoke and gets pneumonia more often / The smoke gives the child a deficiency disease / Smoke harms only the person who is smoking  
   **Answer:** The child breathes the smoke and gets pneumonia more often. *Why:* Smoke harms everyone who breathes it, and children's lungs are small and still growing. They get pneumonia more often when they live with smoke. A deficiency disease comes from a missing nutrient and not from smoke.
5. (mcq) A man drinks a lot of alcohol for many years. Which part of his body is most likely to be damaged, and who else is harmed?  
   Options: His liver, and his whole family / His lungs only, and nobody else / His bones only, and nobody else  
   **Answer:** His liver, and his whole family. *Why:* Alcohol is broken down in the liver, and years of heavy drinking can scar the liver. This is called cirrhosis. The family is harmed too, because money, safety and peace at home suffer. Smoke, not alcohol, is what harms the lungs.
6. (mcq) Which of these is a sign of type 2 diabetes?  
   Options: Cuts and wounds that heal very slowly / A rash that comes only in the rains / A sore throat that ends in two days  
   **Answer:** Cuts and wounds that heal very slowly. *Why:* Too much sugar in the blood slows healing. It also makes a person very thirsty, makes them pass urine often and can blur their sight. A rash in the rains or a sore throat that ends quickly is usually from another cause.
7. (mcq) What does an antibiotic do to a cold virus?  
   Options: Nothing / It kills the virus slowly / It weakens the virus / It stops the virus spreading  
   **Answer:** Nothing. *Why:* An antibiotic acts on parts that bacteria have and viruses lack. A cold is caused by a virus, so the antibiotic has nothing to act on.
8. (mcq) A broken arm is put in a plaster cast. What mends the bone?  
   Options: The bone's own living cells / The plaster / A medicine for pain / The bandage under the plaster  
   **Answer:** The bone's own living cells. *Why:* The cast only holds the bone still. Bone cells bridge the break over some weeks. No medicine builds bone.
9. (mcq) A swallowed medicine passes into the blood. Where does the blood carry it?  
   Options: To every part of the body / Only to the part that is ill / Only to the stomach / Only to the heart  
   **Answer:** To every part of the body. *Why:* Blood reaches every organ, so the medicine does too. That is why a medicine can have effects in places it was not meant for.
10. (mcq) A medicine has a half-life of 4 hours. How much of one dose is left after 8 hours?  
   Options: A quarter / A half / None / All of it  
   **Answer:** A quarter. *Why:* After 4 hours half is left. After 4 more hours, half of that is left, which is a quarter.
11. (mcq) A cough ended five days after a child took a syrup. What can we say?  
   Options: We cannot tell whether the syrup helped / The syrup cured the cough / The syrup made the cough last longer / The cough was not real  
   **Answer:** We cannot tell whether the syrup helped. *Why:* Most coughs end by themselves. Without a group that took no syrup, nobody can say what the syrup added.
12. (mcq) In a trial of a medicine, what is the control group for?  
   Options: To show what happens without the medicine / To control the doctors / To take a double dose / To keep the medicine cold  
   **Answer:** To show what happens without the medicine. *Why:* The control group gets a dummy. Whatever happens to them would have happened anyway, so the medicine's effect is the difference between the groups.
13. (truefalse) In a blind trial, the patients know whether they got the real medicine or the dummy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Blind means the patients do not know. This keeps their hopes from changing what they report.
14. (mcq) Who should give a child a medicine?  
   Options: A grown-up, in the way a doctor says / The child, if she can read the label / An older brother or sister / Anyone who had the same illness  
   **Answer:** A grown-up, in the way a doctor says. *Why:* A medicine needs the right cause, the right dose and the right times. A grown-up follows the doctor and the label. A child never takes a medicine alone.
15. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: How much a dummy can change a disease / Whether germs cause cholera / Whether iron prevents anaemia / Whether vaccines prevent measles  
   **Answer:** How much a dummy can change a disease. *Why:* The other three are settled by strong evidence. How much a placebo can do, beyond changing what people feel and report, is still argued over.

## Applications
- Every new medicine and vaccine sold in India must first pass fair trials on people who agreed to take part.
- Doctors use the half-life of a medicine to decide the dose and the gap between doses.
- Health workers give iron tablets, vitamin A doses and iodised salt to prevent deficiency diseases before they start.
- People with diabetes, asthma or high blood pressure take medicines for decades and live full lives.
- Jan Aushadhi shops across India sell tested generic medicines at much lower prices than the brands.
- A red line on a strip of tablets tells the buyer that the medicine needs a doctor's prescription.
- Scientists study the plants that ill chimpanzees and orangutans choose, in the search for new medicines.

## Open questions
- **Can a dummy treatment change a disease, or only how a person feels?** Trials that measure the body find small effects or none. Trials that ask patients find larger ones. Scientists disagree about what the difference means.
- **Why does the same dose help one person and harm another?** People differ in the liver enzymes that clear medicines, and these differences are inherited. Doctors can test for only a few of them so far.
- **How do chimpanzees and orangutans know which plant to take when they are ill?** They may learn from their mothers, or find out by trying. They may be born with a taste for bitter plants when ill. There are too few clear cases to decide.
- **How does paracetamol dull pain?** It has been used for more than a hundred years. It seems to act mainly in the brain and spinal cord. The full way it works is still not agreed.
- **How many useful medicines are still hidden in old remedies and in plants that nobody has tested?** Only a small share of the world's plants have been tested, and many old texts have never been searched carefully.

## Connected maths
- In the Half-Glass Test, the tea after each dose was 2, 3, 3.5 and 3.75 teaspoons. How far below 4 is each number? What is the pattern? Will the tea ever reach 4? **The gaps are 2, 1, 0.5 and 0.25. Each gap is half the one before. The tea gets closer and closer to 4 and never reaches it.**. 4 take away 2 is 2. 4 take away 3 is 1. 4 take away 3.5 is 0.5. 4 take away 3.75 is 0.25. Each pour halves the gap. Half of something is never nothing, so the gap never closes completely. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the Half-Glass Test, one dose of 2 teaspoons goes in, and no more. Each pour removes half. How much tea is left after 1, 2, 3 and 4 pours? What fraction of the dose is that last amount? **1 teaspoon, a half, a quarter and an eighth of a teaspoon. An eighth of a teaspoon is one-sixteenth of the dose.**. Half of 2 is 1. Half of 1 is a half. Half of a half is a quarter. Half of a quarter is an eighth. The dose was 2 teaspoons, and an eighth is one-sixteenth of 2. Four halvings leave one-sixteenth. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- In the Half-Glass Test, doses of 2 teaspoons levelled off near 4, and doses of 4 teaspoons near 8. Where would doses of 3 teaspoons level off? What is the rule? **Near 6 teaspoons. The level settles at twice the dose.**. The level stops rising when a pour removes exactly one dose. A pour removes half. So the glass must hold twice the dose: 2 times 3 is 6. Check: half of 6 is 3, and 3 plus 3 is 6 again. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the trial of 1948, 4 of 55 patients given streptomycin died, and 14 of 52 given bed rest alone. Write each as a percentage, to the nearest whole number. About how many times greater was the second? **About 7 percent of the first group died and about 27 percent of the second. The second share is nearly 4 times the first.**. 4 divided by 55 is about 0.07, which is 7 percent. 14 divided by 52 is about 0.27, which is 27 percent. 27 divided by 7 is nearly 4. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In a made-up run of the Wilted Stems Trial, glass A got 7 stems and glass B got 5. Six recovered in A and 4 in B. Which glass did better? Would you trust the difference? **Glass A did better, with about 86 percent recovered against 80 percent. I would not trust the difference, because one stem more or less would change the answer.**. 6 divided by 7 is about 0.86. 4 divided by 5 is 0.80. But if one more stem in B had recovered, B would show 100 percent. A result that one stem can reverse is luck, and the trial needs more stems. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L5`)
- In a made-up Label Test, four tasters gave the ORDINARY cup 11 points in all. They gave the EXTRA SWEET cup 15. In the blind round, the two cups got 13 and 12. Find the average score for each cup. What did the label add? **With labels, the average for ORDINARY was 2.75 and for EXTRA SWEET 3.75. Without labels, the averages were 3.25 and 3. So the label added about 1 point for each taster.**. 11 divided by 4 is 2.75. 15 divided by 4 is 3.75. The difference is 1. In the blind round, 13 divided by 4 is 3.25 and 12 divided by 4 is 3. The difference is only 0.25, which is small enough to be luck. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In Read the Label, the made-up syrup may be given every 6 hours, at most 4 times in 24 hours. The first dose is at 7.30 in the morning. Write the earliest times of the next three doses. Another label says 'three times a day'. How many hours apart is that, spread evenly? **The next three doses are at 1.30 in the afternoon, 7.30 in the evening and 1.30 at night. Three times a day, spread evenly, means every 8 hours.**. Add 6 hours each time: 7.30, 1.30, 7.30, 1.30. For three doses in a day, divide 24 hours by 3, which is 8. Doses bunched into the daytime leave a long gap at night, when the level falls low. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is the difference between a symptom and a cause? → A symptom is what an illness shows, such as fever. A cause is what makes the illness begin, such as a germ.
- Name three kinds of cause of disease. → Any three of these five. A germ. A missing nutrient. A body part that stops working well. Harm from outside. An inherited fault.
- Name the four jobs a medicine can do. → Remove a cause, replace something missing, ease a symptom, or prevent a disease.
- What is a half-life? → The time a body takes to remove half of a medicine.
- Why does a cold remedy seem to work? → A cold ends by itself in about a week, so the remedy gets the credit.
- What is a control group? → The group in a trial that does not get the treatment. The treated group is compared with it.
- What is a placebo? → A dummy treatment that looks like the real one and holds no medicine.
- Who gives a child a medicine? → Only a grown-up, in the way a doctor says.
