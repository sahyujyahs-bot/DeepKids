# Being aware: how you notice the world, and what it is like to be you
`sci.what-is-real.consciousness.L2`

**Summary.** The fan hums all evening, yet most of the time you do not hear it. Being aware means knowing or feeling that something is there. Your attention picks out a few of the many sounds, sights and touches around you. In this unit you test how many sounds you notice when you are busy and when you sit still. You also taste the same lemon as your family and find out whether everyone feels the same sour. Then you meet the other beings who notice their world, and the big question nobody has answered: how does being aware happen at all?

**Intro.** *Can you hear the fan right now? Could you hear it a minute ago?* Stop and listen for ten seconds. You may hear the fan, the fridge, a horn on the road or a bird. Most of these sounds were there before you stopped. You were not noticing them. Being aware of something means you know or feel that it is there. So what decides which sounds you are aware of?

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what being aware of something means.
1. By the end I can show, with my own test, that I notice more sounds when I pay attention.
2. By the end I can explain why I stop noticing a steady sound, and why I notice when it stops.
3. By the end I can say why nobody else can have my experience, and how we share experiences in words.
4. By the end I can tell being awake, dreaming and deep sleep apart.
5. By the end I can name other beings who notice their world, and say what scientists still argue about.

## Terms
- **Aware**: Being aware of something means you know or feel that it is there. *e.g.* When you feel a mosquito on your arm, you are aware of the mosquito.
- **Consciousness**: Consciousness is being aware of anything at all: sights, sounds, feelings or thoughts. *e.g.* When you wake up in the morning, your consciousness of the room comes back.
- **Notice**: To notice something is to become aware of it. *e.g.* You notice the pressure cooker when it whistles.
- **Attention**: Attention is choosing what to notice, out of everything around you. *e.g.* When you read a story, your attention is on the story, so you may not hear your name.
- **Experience**: An experience is what something is like for you: how it looks, sounds, tastes or feels to you. *e.g.* The sour of a lemon on your tongue is your experience. Nobody else can have it for you.
- **Senses**: Your senses are how your body takes in the world: seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting and touching, and more. *e.g.* Your ears take in the sound of rain on the window.
- **Getting used to (habituation)**: Getting used to something means you stop noticing it because it goes on and on. Scientists call this habituation. *e.g.* After a few minutes in a room, you stop noticing the smell of the incense.
- **Dream**: A dream is what you are aware of during sleep: pictures, sounds and stories your sleeping mind makes. *e.g.* You might dream you are riding an elephant through Mysuru.

## Explanations
### Being aware · *defined*
**Definition:** Being aware of something means knowing or feeling that it is there. Scientists call being aware of anything at all consciousness.

Right now you are aware of these words. You may also be aware of your feet on the floor, now that you read this. A moment ago you probably were not. Being aware is the most familiar thing there is, because it is there in every moment you are awake. Yet nobody can see your awareness from the outside. Other people can only see what you do and hear what you say.
- e.g. You are aware of a horn when you hear it on the road.
- e.g. You are aware of feeling hungry before lunch.
- e.g. You are aware of a thought, such as 'I want to play'.
### Attention picks what you notice · *defined*
**Definition:** Attention is choosing what to notice. Your senses take in far more than you notice, and attention picks out a small part.

In the sound test, the room made the same sounds in the busy minutes and the still minutes. When you were racing to sort beans, your attention was on the beans. So you noticed only a few sounds. When you sat still and listened, your attention was on sounds. So you noticed many more. Your ears were working the whole time. What changed was where your attention was.
- e.g. While you watch cartoons, you may not hear Amma call you.
- e.g. In a busy market, you hear your own name even through all the noise.
- e.g. When you look for a red pencil, red things seem to jump out at you.
### Getting used to steady sounds · *defined*
**Definition:** When a sound, a smell or a touch goes on without changing, you stop noticing it. This is called getting used to it, or habituation. A change makes you notice again.

The fan hums all evening, and after a while you stop hearing it. The sound still reaches your ears. Your mind lets a steady sound fade into the background, so you can notice new things. When the fan suddenly stops, the change is new, so you notice it at once. This is why a power cut makes a flat feel suddenly quiet.
- e.g. You stop smelling your own home, but visitors notice its smell.
- e.g. You stop feeling your watch or bangle on your wrist.
- e.g. You notice the fridge only when its hum stops.
### What it is like to be you · *defined*
**Definition:** Your experience is what something is like for you. Only you have your experience. Others learn about it from your words, your face and what you do.

In the lemon test, everyone tasted the same lemon juice. Yet people often showed different numbers of fingers for the sour. Even when two people both show four fingers, nobody can be sure their sours feel the same. You cannot step inside another person's tongue to check. So we share our experiences by telling each other about them.
- e.g. You and your friend both say a mango is sweet, but each of you has your own sweet.
- e.g. A song can make one person happy and another person sad.
- e.g. A cold floor may feel nice to you and too cold to an older person.
### Awake, dreaming and deep sleep · *defined*
**Definition:** When you are awake, you are aware of the world around you. When you dream, you are aware of a world your sleeping mind makes. In deep sleep you are aware of very little, and later you remember almost nothing of it.

Every night you go through deep sleep and dreaming sleep several times. In a dream, strange things can feel normal, such as flying or talking to a fish. While you dream, the dream usually feels real. Almost everyone dreams every night, even people who remember no dreams. Doctors can also make a person unaware for an operation, with medicines called anaesthetics. Afterwards the person remembers nothing of the operation.
- e.g. Awake: you hear Amma call and you answer.
- e.g. Dreaming: you swim through the sky among the clouds.
- e.g. Deep sleep: the night passes, and in the morning it feels as if no time went by.
### Who else is aware? · *debated*
Every living being notices their world in their own way. An ant notices the smell of food. A crow notices you at the window. A tulsi plant notices which way the light comes from, and grows towards it. Awareness does not need a brain, and it runs through everything, down to the smallest particle. Scientists still argue about which beings are aware, and how.
- e.g. A touch-me-not plant folds up when touched. In 2018, scientists found the medicines that make people unaware for an operation also stop the touch-me-not folding.
- e.g. Young bumblebees rolled small balls again and again with no food reward, in tests at Queen Mary University of London, led by Samadi Galpayage.
- e.g. A dog notices the sound of your footsteps before you open the door.
- **Many scientists who signed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, 2024:** There is strong evidence that mammals and birds are aware, and a real chance that fish, octopuses, crabs and insects are too.
- **Most brain scientists:** Awareness is something brains do. Beings without brains, such as plants, sense and respond, but they call this automatic.
- **Plant scientists such as Monica Gagliano, and thinkers such as Philip Goff:** Sensing, choosing and remembering happen in plants too. Some thinkers say every bit of the world has some awareness.

## Mostly agreed
- You notice only a small part of what your senses take in. Attention picks out what you notice. *How we know:* In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris asked people to count ball passes in a short film. About half of them did not notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the middle.
- You stop noticing a sound, smell or touch that goes on without changing, and you notice when it changes. *How we know:* Many people say that they stop smelling their own home and notice the quiet when a fan or fridge stops. Experiments on habituation show the same in many animals, including us.
- Almost everyone dreams every night, even people who say they never dream. *How we know:* In sleep laboratories since the 1950s, people woken during dreaming sleep usually say they were having a dream, including people who remember no dreams at home.
- Doctors can make a person unaware for an operation and then bring their awareness back. *How we know:* Anaesthetics have been used for operations since the 1840s. People wake up afterwards and remember nothing of the operation.

## How we found out
**Colin Cherry, Donald Broadbent and Neville Moray in England, and Anne Treisman at the University of Oxford, From 1953 to 1960.** In 1953, Colin Cherry, a British scientist, played two different spoken messages to people at once, one in each ear. He asked them to repeat one message aloud. Afterwards they could say almost nothing about the other message. In 1958, Donald Broadbent explained this with an idea: the mind shuts out the ear you are not listening to, like closing a door. Then the door idea began to crack. In 1959, Neville Moray found that some people heard their own name in the shut-out ear. In 1960, a young student at Oxford, Anne Treisman, tried a clever test. She played a story into one ear and a different story into the other. Halfway through, she swapped the stories over. Many people followed their story into the wrong ear for a word or two, without meaning to. So the ignored ear had not been shut out. Its sounds were turned down, and some of their meaning still got through. Treisman went on to become one of the best-known scientists of attention in the world.  
*What people believed before:* That the mind shuts out everything you are not paying attention to, like a closed door. Moray's own-name finding and Treisman's swapped-story test showed that ignored sounds are turned down, not shut out.

## Challenging perspectives
- **We see and hear everything that is in front of us.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: The world looks full and complete to us, so it feels as if we take in all of it.  
  Against: In the gorilla film test, about half the people did not see a gorilla walking past. In your sound test, you named far fewer sounds when you were busy.
- **Sounds you are not paying attention to are shut out completely, like a closed door.** (Donald Broadbent, a British scientist, in 1958; *rejected*)  
  For: In Colin Cherry's tests in 1953, people could say almost nothing about a message in the ear they were not listening to.  
  Against: Neville Moray found in 1959 that some people heard their own name in that ear. Anne Treisman showed in 1960 that some meaning gets through.
- **Only humans are really aware.** (Many people in the past, and some today; *rejected*)  
  For: Only humans can tell us in words what they experience.  
  Against: Other animals play, feel pain and learn. In 2024, many scientists signed the New York Declaration, saying there is strong evidence that mammals and birds are aware.
- **Awareness needs a brain.** (Most brain scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Medicines and injuries that change the brain change a person's awareness.  
  Against: Plants sense, remember and choose. The medicines that make people unaware also stop a touch-me-not folding, though she has no brain.

## Roots
We have tested what we notice when busy and when still, and what the same lemon is like for each of us. Now we ask what this tells us about being aware.

**Deep truth.** While you were busy, you noticed only a few sounds. Sitting still in the same room, you noticed many more. The sounds were the same, and only your attention changed. So what you are aware of depends on where you put your attention.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think we are aware of everything around us, as if our ears and eyes were open windows. The sound test shows that attention picks out only some of it.  
*Evidence:* In the sound test, each room gave far more sounds in the still round than in the busy round, in either order.

- *who am i:* You are the one who notices. Right now you can choose to notice your breath, a sound or your feet.
- *what is life:* Being alive means noticing your world and answering it: an ant notices a crumb, a plant notices the light, you notice a horn.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of sounds, smells and sights at every moment. Each being notices a different part of it.

At any moment there is one world of sound in your flat. You, a grown-up at home, a crow on the ledge and an ant on the floor each notice a different part of it. Each of them is aware in their own way. Awareness runs through all of them, and through everything, down to the smallest particle. So one world is being noticed in countless ways at once.

- **Mandukya Upanishad, verses 3 to 7:** This Upanishad describes waking, when we are aware of the world outside, dreaming, when we are aware of a world inside, and deep sleep, when we are aware of almost nothing. It names a fourth, turiya: the awareness that is there through all three. (In this unit you sorted moments into awake, dreaming and deep sleep. Sleep scientists also find that awareness changes through a night.)
- **Satipatthana Sutta, the Buddha's teaching on mindfulness (Majjhima Nikaya 10):** The Buddha taught: when walking, know that you are walking. When breathing in, know that you are breathing in. (This is attention on purpose. It is what you did in the still round of the sound test, when you noticed sounds you had been missing.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2:** The Chinese thinker Zhuangzi dreamt he was a butterfly, happily flying about. When he woke, he wondered: was he Zhuangzi who had dreamt of being a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming of being Zhuangzi? (A dream feels real while you are in it. Only you have your experience, so it is hard for anyone else to check what it was like.)
- Science (settled): Attention picks out a small part of everything your senses take in. Many experiments since the 1950s show this.
- Science (open): Nobody knows how or why being aware happens at all. The philosopher David Chalmers called this the hard problem in 1995.
- Other view, Most brain scientists today, and the Charvaka thinkers of ancient India: Most brain scientists think awareness is something brains do, so beings without brains are not aware. The Charvaka thinkers taught long ago that awareness comes from the body and ends with it.
- Practice, The Last of the Sound (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. I will tap this steel tumbler once with a spoon. Listen as the sound gets softer and softer. Raise your hand when it is gone. Keep your eyes closed. What is the next sound you notice? And the next?
- Ask: When a sound fades from your notice, where does it go?
- Ask: Is the ant on the kitchen floor noticing you, the way you notice the ant?
- Ask: Is your sour the same as someone else's sour at home? How could anyone ever find out?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* When you and a grown-up at home hear the same pressure cooker whistle, do you hear the same sound? (hint: The same sound reaches both of you. But each of you has your own experience of it. How could you ever check?)
- *deep:* When you stop noticing the fan, where does its sound go? (hint: Think about the moment the fan stopped. How did you know it had stopped, if you were not hearing it?)
- *big:* What is it like to be an ant on the kitchen floor? (hint: An ant smells and feels the floor through their feelers and feet. Their world is full of smells you cannot sense.)
- *deep:* In a dream, who is noticing the dream? (hint: Your body is lying still in bed. Yet something is aware of the dream.)
- *deep:* In deep sleep you remember almost nothing. Were you still there? (hint: Your body was there, breathing all night. What about your awareness?)

## Story: Meenakshi and the Power Cut
*A made-up story. It is true that steady sounds fade from notice, that a change makes us notice again, and that house geckos make clicking calls.*

Meenakshi lived on the fourth floor of a building in Madurai. She was seven. Her little brother Arjun was five. Their Paati, their grandmother, lived with them.

One evening, Meenakshi was doing her sums at the dining table. Arjun was building a tower of steel tumblers on the floor. The ceiling fan turned above them. Paati was in the kitchen, shelling peas into a bowl.

Then there was a click, and every light went out. The fan slowed down and stopped. "Power cut!" shouted Arjun.

Meenakshi sat very still in the dark. "Paati," she called, "it has gone so quiet. What stopped?"

Paati came in with a torch. "The fan stopped," she said. "And the fridge. The fan was humming all evening."

"But I didn't hear the fan," said Meenakshi. "Not once."

"Your ears heard it," said Paati. "You stopped noticing it. When a sound goes on and on, your mind lets it fade into the background. When it stops, that is a change. So you notice."

Paati sat down on the mat and switched off the torch. "Let's play a noticing game," she said. "Close your eyes. Hold up one finger for every new sound you hear."

At first Meenakshi heard nothing at all. Then a scooter horn beeped down on the road. One finger. A pressure cooker whistled in the flat next door. Two fingers. A crow cawed from the water tank on the roof. Three.

Arjun was still knocking his tumblers together. "Arjun, you are the loudest sound of all," laughed Meenakshi. Arjun stopped.

In the new quiet, Meenakshi heard more. A dog barked far away. Someone's TV was talking very softly. The kitchen tap went drip, drip. Then a small chik-chik-chik came from the wall. "That's the gecko," whispered Paati. Meenakshi had seven fingers up.

"Were all these sounds here before the power cut?" asked Paati.

Meenakshi thought hard. The road had been there all evening. The neighbour cooked every night. The gecko lived behind the calendar. "I think so," she said slowly. "But I was busy with my sums."

"That's it," said Paati. "Your attention was on your sums, so you noticed your sums. Now your attention is on sounds, so you notice sounds."

"Teach me, Akka!" said Arjun. Akka means big sister in Tamil. So Meenakshi taught him. "Shut your eyes. Keep your hands still. Now listen, and hold up a finger for each new sound."

Arjun found the dripping tap and the gecko. Then he found one that Meenakshi had missed: the soft snap of a pea pod in Paati's hands. Meenakshi was surprised. It had been right next to her.

"What does the gecko notice?" asked Arjun.

"The gecko notices her own world," said Paati. "The moths near the window. The warm wall under her feet. She is aware in her own gecko way, and we are aware in ours."

Meenakshi had another question. "Paati, when you hear the cooker whistle, is it the same as when I hear it?"

Paati smiled in the dark. "We both hear it. But I can never get inside your ears to check. All we can do is tell each other what it is like."

Then, with a hum, the lights came back on. The fan began to turn. Meenakshi listened. There it was, the fan's soft whirr, and the fridge buzzing in the kitchen. "I can hear the fan now!" she said.

Ten minutes later, she was back at her sums. When Appa came home, he said, "Is the fan making that noise again?" Meenakshi looked up. She had forgotten all about the fan.

She laughed and took his hand. "Appa, sit down on the mat and close your eyes," she said. "I am going to teach you a game."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Meenakshi first thinks the quiet came with the power cut. She finds out that the sounds were there all along, and that her attention decided which ones she noticed. Then she forgets the fan again, which shows how easily we get used to a steady sound.

- Ask: Why did the flat suddenly seem so quiet in the power cut?
- Ask: Why did Meenakshi not hear the fan while she did her sums?
- Ask: Which sound did Arjun find that Meenakshi had missed?
- Ask: Paati says she can never get inside Meenakshi's ears to check. What does she mean?
- Ask: What do you think the gecko was noticing?

### g1 · Noticed It Before? A card game (15 min)
- **rules:** Make three piles on the floor: 'I was noticing it', 'I notice it now' and 'Hard to notice, even now'.; Mix the cards and put them face down.; Take turns. Pick a card, read the front out loud, and stop for five seconds to notice it.; Put the card on a pile and say why.; Turn the card over and read the fact on the back.; Other players may say if they would choose a different pile, and why. Both answers can be right.; When all the cards are played, count each pile.
- **Printable cards: 12 cards to cut out, with a fact on the back:** The fan's hum (back: A steady sound fades from notice after a while. It still reaches your ears.); Your tongue in your mouth (back: Your tongue touches your teeth all day. You notice it only when you think about it.); The smell of your home (back: You stop smelling your own home. Visitors notice its smell at once.); Your breathing (back: Breathing goes on by itself. You can notice it, and slow it down, when you choose.); Your heartbeat (back: Most of the time you hardly feel it. Put a hand on your chest after running, and you will.); Your nose (back: Your nose is in front of your eyes all day.   *Parent says:* Stop and notice it. Which pile? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Pick a card. Stop and notice it. Which pile? Why?

- **s3 (3 min)**

  *Child mode:* Count each pile. Which is biggest?
  - Ask: What does the biggest pile tell us? (good: Lots of things are there that we don't notice.; We notice things when we pay attention.)

## Experiments
### e1 · Busy or Still: do you notice more sounds when you pay attention? (25 min)
- **question:** Do you notice more sounds when you sit still and listen than when you are busy with a job?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will you notice more sounds while you are busy, while you sit still, or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** In each room, use the same two minutes and the same rule: tell all the sounds you noticed straight after, and count each sound once. The sound of the beans themselves does not count. Only one thing changes: a busy job or sitting still and listening. In the still round, keep your eyes on the bowl of beans, as you did in the busy round. Do the second room in the other order, so going second is not what makes the difference.
- **record:** Fill in the results table with the number of sounds for each round. Circle the bigger number in each room.
- **results table:** Room | Busy 2 minutes: sounds I noticed | Still 2 minutes: sounds I noticed
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the question. Wait for a guess and a reason. Write the guess at the top of the paper.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to count sounds. Will you notice more sounds while you are busy, or while you sit still? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will you notice more sounds busy or still? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Still, because I can listen properly.; The same, because the sounds are the same.; Busy, because I will be moving and alert.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine if it has a reason. Do not say what usually happens.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix the rajma and chana in the bowl. 2. Put the two katoris beside it. 3. Draw the results table with two rooms.  
  *Parent says:* In the busy round, you sort rajma into one cup and chana into the other, as fast as you can. In the still round, you only listen.  
  *Child mode:* Rajma in one cup. Chana in the other. Fast!
  - Ask: What must stay the same in every round? (good: Two minutes each time.; The same room for both rounds.; Tell all the sounds straight after.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Room 1, busy round. 1. Start the timer. 2. The child sorts beans for two minutes. 3. Stop. 4. The child tells every sound they noticed. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Sort as fast as you can. Keep your eyes on the beans. Stop! Now tell me every sound you noticed.  
  *Child mode:* Sort the beans fast for two minutes. Then tell every sound you heard.
  - Note: Do not hint at any sound. Count each sound once, even if it came many times. Your own voice saying start and stop does not count, and neither does the sound of the beans.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Room 1, still round. 1. Pour the beans back into the bowl and stir them. 2. Start the timer. 3. The child sits still for two minutes, looking at the bowl and listening. 4. The child tells every sound. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Now sit still. Keep your eyes on the bowl and just listen. Stop! Tell me every sound you noticed.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Look at the bowl. Listen for two minutes. Then tell every sound.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Room 2, the other order. 1. Move to a second room, for example the kitchen or a bedroom. 2. Do the still round first. 3. Then stir the beans and do the busy round. 4. Write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* This time we do the still round first, then the busy round. Why do you think we swap the order?  
  *Child mode:* New room. Still round first. Then the busy round.
  - Ask: Why do we swap the order? (good: In case going second helps you.; So it is fair for both kinds of round.)
  - Note: If the flat is very quiet, switch on the fan in both rounds of a room, or open a window. Sounds in a city flat are usually the fan, the fridge, traffic, horns, birds, neighbours, a lift and the water motor.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. In each room, circle the bigger number.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. In which round did you notice more sounds? Was it the same in both rooms? Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your numbers. Which round was bigger? Was your guess right?
  - Ask: What did you find? (good: I noticed 2 sounds busy and 8 sounds still.; Still was bigger in both rooms.)
  - Note: Most children notice two or three times as many sounds when still. If busy came out the same or bigger, ask why. Perhaps they listened while sorting. That is a real finding too. Only after the child has said what they saw, read the card on attention.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask about one steady sound, such as the fan or fridge, that was on in both rounds.

### e2 · Same Lemon, Different Sour: does everyone feel the same sour? (15 min)
- **question:** If everyone tastes the same lemon juice, will everyone show the same amount of sour?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will everyone hold up the same number of fingers, or different numbers? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use one lemon, three drops on a clean spoon for each person, and the same finger scale from 1 for a little sour to 5 for very sour. Everyone shuts their eyes and shows fingers at the same moment, so nobody copies anyone.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then circle anyone whose number was different from yours.
- **results table:** Person | Sour fingers (1 to 5) | One word for how it felt
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the question. Wait for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Everyone will taste the same lemon. Will everyone show the same number of sour fingers? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Same lemon for everyone. Same fingers? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The same, because it is the same lemon.; Different, because some people like sour things.; Different, because people are different.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine if it has a reason. Ask everyone at home who is willing to taste. Skip anyone who has a mouth ulcer or does not want to.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the finger scale: 1 finger is a little sour, 5 fingers is very sour. 2. Squeeze three drops onto each spoon.  
  *Parent says:* One finger means a little sour. Five fingers means very, very sour. Three drops each, on your own spoon.  
  *Child mode:* One finger is a little sour. Five is very sour.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone tastes their drops. 2. Everyone shuts their eyes. 3. Count to three. 4. Everyone shows fingers. 5. Write each number down. Then everyone sips water.  
  *Parent says:* Taste it. Now shut your eyes. One, two, three: show your fingers! Keep them up while I write.  
  *Child mode:* Taste it. Shut your eyes. On three, show your fingers.
  - Note: Eyes shut stops people copying each other. Write the numbers before anyone opens their eyes.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each person says one word for how the sour felt. Write it next to their number.  
  *Parent says:* Say one word for how it felt. Sharp? Tingly? Ouch? Nice?  
  *Child mode:* Say one word for how it felt.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together.  
  *Parent says:* Did everyone show the same number? If two of us both showed four, did we feel the same sour? How could we check?  
  *Child mode:* Did everyone show the same? Can you check someone else's sour?
  - Ask: Can we know if two fours feel the same? (good: No, I can only feel my own sour.; We can only tell each other in words and fingers.)
  - Note: Numbers usually differ a little. If everyone showed the same, the question still stands: is your four the same as mine? There is no way to check from the outside. That is the point of the test. Only after the child has said what they think, read the card on what it is like to be you.
  - Think: Your sour is yours. How do other people find out about it?

## Applications
- Traffic lights, ambulance sirens and road signs are made bright, flashing or changing, so drivers notice them.
- Doctors called anaesthetists use medicines that make a person unaware during an operation, then let their awareness come back.
- Many alarms beep in a changing pattern, because people stop noticing a sound that stays the same.
- Teachers clap a rhythm or ring a bell to catch a class's attention.
- Some schools teach children to notice their breath for a minute, to help them settle and pay attention.

## Spotted around us
- In a Hyderabad flat, everyone notices the sudden quiet when the fan stops in a power cut.
- A flower seller in a busy Chennai market hears her own name through all the noise.
- A crow on a Kochi balcony turns her head the moment a steel plate clinks.

## Open questions
- **How does being aware happen at all?** Scientists can see which parts of the brain are busy when people notice things. Nobody knows how or why that comes with an experience. The philosopher David Chalmers called this the hard problem in 1995.
- **Which beings are aware, and what is it like to be them?** We can watch what a bee or a plant does. We cannot step inside their experience. Scientists still argue about how to test this.
- **Is your red the same as my red?** We both call a tomato red and agree on which things are red. There is no known way to check whether the colour looks the same inside each of us.
- **Why do we dream?** Scientists have many ideas, such as sorting memories or practising for danger. None has been proved.

Who noticed the most sounds? Who noticed the fewest? **Deepa noticed the most, with 9. Chitra noticed the fewest, with 3.**. Put them in order: 3, 6, 7, 9. The biggest is 9 and the smallest is 3. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- Sana, a girl in Hyderabad, did the sound test in four rooms. Busy: 2, 3, 1, 2. Still: 7, 6, 8, 5. In how many rooms did the still round win? What does that tell you that one room alone could not? **The still round won in all 4 rooms. So it was not a fluke of one room.**. Compare each pair: 7 is more than 2, 6 is more than 3, 8 is more than 1, 5 is more than 2. The same pattern four times makes it much more likely to be real. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the lemon test, five people showed 3, 4, 4, 2 and 5 fingers. How many people showed more than 3? Does that mean the lemon tasted more sour to them? **3 people showed more than 3. We can't be sure the lemon tasted more sour to them, because one person's 4 may not feel the same as another's 4.**. 4, 4 and 5 are more than 3. That is 3 people. But the numbers are words for experiences, and only each person has their own experience. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In Five Things Right Now, you notice 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you feel, 2 you smell and 1 you taste. How many things is that in all? **15 things.**. 5 + 4 = 9. 9 + 3 = 12. 12 + 2 = 14. 14 + 1 = 15. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L2`)
