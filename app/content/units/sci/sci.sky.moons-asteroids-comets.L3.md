# Moons, asteroids and comets: the smaller worlds, and why shooting stars glow
`sci.sky.moons-asteroids-comets.L3`

**Summary.** A shooting star is not a star. Most are grains of space dust, about as small as a grain of rava, burning up high in our air. Many of these grains were left behind by comets. A comet is a lump of ice, dust and rock. Near the Sun, some of its ice turns to gas, and the gas and dust stream away as a long tail. Asteroids are lumps of rock and metal, and most of them go round the Sun between Mars and Jupiter. Most planets have moons too. Mars has two small moons, and Jupiter and Saturn have dozens each.

**Intro.** *Have you ever seen a shooting star? It is not a star at all. Most shooting stars are made by a grain of dust no bigger than a grain of rava. So how can something so small make a light you can see from 100 kilometres away?* In this unit you find out. You meet the smaller worlds of the solar system: moons, asteroids and comets. You make a comet from ice and dirt, and watch what happens to it in the sun. And you find out where the dust that makes shooting stars comes from.

## Terms
- **Moon**: A natural world that goes round a planet. A moon is also called a natural satellite. *e.g.* Our Moon goes round the Earth once in about 27 days. Mars has two moons, Phobos and Deimos.
- **Orbit**: The path one thing takes as it goes round another, again and again. *e.g.* A moon orbits its planet. A planet, an asteroid or a comet orbits the Sun.
- **Asteroid**: A lump of rock, or rock and metal, that goes round the Sun. Asteroids are much smaller than planets, and most are not round. *e.g.* Ceres is the biggest asteroid. It is about 940 km wide, about the distance from Chennai to Pune in a straight line.
- **Asteroid belt**: The wide ring of space between Mars and Jupiter where most asteroids go round the Sun. *e.g.* The asteroids in the belt are very far apart. Spacecraft fly through it without hitting any.
- **Comet**: A lump of ice, dust and rock that goes round the Sun. Near the Sun, its ice turns to gas and it grows a glowing head and a long tail. *e.g.* Halley's Comet comes near the Sun about every 76 years. It was last seen in 1986.
- **Nucleus of a comet**: The solid lump at the centre of a comet, made of ice, dust and rock. It is usually a few kilometres across. *e.g.* The nucleus of Halley's Comet is about 15 km long, and darker than coal.
- **Coma**: The glowing cloud of gas and dust that forms round a comet's nucleus when it comes near the Sun. *e.g.* A comet's coma can be wider than the whole Earth, even though the nucleus is only a few kilometres across.
- **Sublimation**: When a solid turns straight into a gas, without first melting into a liquid. *e.g.* In space, a comet's ice does not melt into water. It sublimates, turning straight into gas.
- **Meteoroid**: A small piece of rock or dust travelling through space. Most are smaller than a grain of sand. *e.g.* Many meteoroids are bits of dust left behind by comets.
- **Meteor**: The streak of light we see when a meteoroid rushes into the Earth's air and burns up. It is also called a shooting star. *e.g.* On a dark night you may see a meteor flash across the sky in less than a second.
- **Meteorite**: A piece of space rock that survives the fall through the air and lands on the ground. *e.g.* Most meteorites are found in deserts and on the ice of Antarctica, where dark stones are easy to spot.
- **Meteor shower**: A night when many more meteors than usual are seen. It happens when the Earth passes through a stream of dust left by a comet or an asteroid. *e.g.* The Geminid meteor shower comes every year around 13 and 14 December.
- **Crater**: A round hollow made when something hits the ground very fast. *e.g.* The Moon is covered in craters. Lonar Lake in Maharashtra fills a crater made by a meteorite long ago.

## Explanations
### Most planets have moons · *defined*
**Definition:** A moon is a natural world that goes round a planet. Six of the eight planets have moons. Only Mercury and Venus have none.

The Earth has one moon. Mars has two small ones, Phobos and Deimos. They are lumpy, like potatoes. Phobos is about 22 kilometres across and Deimos about 12. The giant planets have many moons. Jupiter has more than 90 known moons, and Saturn more than 270. Uranus and Neptune have many more than the Earth too. Most of these moons are small rocks, but a few are big worlds. New small moons are still being found, so these numbers keep going up.
- e.g. Ganymede, a moon of Jupiter, is the biggest moon in the solar system. It is wider than the planet Mercury.
- e.g. Titan, a moon of Saturn, has thick air, clouds and lakes. The lakes are not water. They are a liquid called methane, because Titan is far colder than any freezer.
- e.g. Our Moon is the fifth biggest moon in the solar system.
### Jupiter's four big moons · *defined*
**Definition:** Jupiter's four biggest moons are Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. They are called the Galilean moons, after Galileo, who saw them in January 1610.

Galileo pointed his small telescope at Jupiter and saw three, then four, tiny dots in a line beside the planet. Night after night, the dots changed places. Sometimes a dot was on the left, sometimes on the right, sometimes it was missing. Stars do not move like that. Galileo worked out that the dots were moons going round Jupiter. This mattered a lot, because many people then taught that everything in the sky goes round the Earth. Here were four things going round something else. You can see these moons yourself with ordinary binoculars held very still.
- e.g. Io has hundreds of volcanoes. It is the most volcanic place in the solar system.
- e.g. Europa is covered in ice. Under the ice there is very likely a deep, salty ocean.
- e.g. Callisto is covered in so many craters that they overlap.
### Asteroids · *defined*
**Definition:** An asteroid is a lump of rock, or rock and metal, that goes round the Sun. Most asteroids are in the asteroid belt, between Mars and Jupiter.

Asteroids are pieces left over from when the planets formed, about 4.6 billion years ago. More than a million asteroids are known. The biggest, Ceres, is about 940 km wide, but most are much smaller, from the size of a house to the size of a town. Small asteroids are not round. Their pull, called gravity, is too weak to squeeze them into a ball. So they look like lumpy potatoes. Spacecraft have visited asteroids, and two, Hayabusa2 from Japan and OSIRIS-REx from the United States, brought bits of asteroids back to Earth.
- e.g. Ceres was the first asteroid found, on 1 January 1801, by Giuseppe Piazzi in Palermo, Italy.
- e.g. Some asteroids are mostly iron and nickel, like a lump of metal.
- e.g. In 2022, a NASA spacecraft called DART crashed into a small asteroid on purpose and changed its path a little.
### Comets · *defined*
**Definition:** A comet is a lump of ice, dust and rock that goes round the Sun, usually on a long, stretched path. Near the Sun, its ice turns to gas, and it grows a glowing head and tails.

Most of the time, a comet is far out in the cold, beyond the planets. There it is just a dark, frozen lump a few kilometres across. Its path brings it in close to the Sun, then swings it far out again. As it comes near the Sun, sunlight warms it. Its ice sublimates, which means it turns straight into gas, without melting into water first. The gas carries dust out with it. This makes a glowing cloud called the coma, and long tails. The dust that stays behind builds up as a dark crust on the comet. That is why the nucleus of Halley's Comet is darker than coal. In 1950, the American astronomer Fred Whipple called comets 'dirty snowballs'.
- e.g. In Tamil and Malayalam, a comet is called vaal natchathiram or vaal nakshatram, the 'tail star'.
- e.g. Caroline Herschel found eight comets between 1786 and 1797. She was one of the first women paid to do science.
- e.g. Maria Mitchell found a comet from a rooftop in America on 1 October 1847 and became famous for it.
### Why a comet's tail points away from the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** A comet's tails always point away from the Sun, whichever way the comet is moving. Sunlight and a stream of tiny particles from the Sun, called the solar wind, push the gas and dust outwards.

On Earth, when you run, your hair streams out behind you. A comet's tail is different. The Sun is always pushing outwards, a little like a fan that blows in every direction at once. So the tail is pushed away from the Sun. When a comet is heading in towards the Sun, its tail is behind it. When it has passed the Sun and is going away, its tail goes in front of it. Most bright comets have two tails. The gas tail is thin, straight and bluish. The dust tail is wider, curved and yellowish-white, because the dust is pushed less strongly and drifts behind a little.
- e.g. The fan test shows this: with the fan on, the tissue tail points away from the fan, even when you walk away from it.
- e.g. Comet NEOWISE, seen from India in July 2020, showed a curved dust tail and a faint straight gas tail.
- e.g. The Sun's push is very gentle, but in space there is no air to slow the dust down.
### Meteoroid, meteor and meteorite · *defined*
**Definition:** A meteoroid is a small piece of rock or dust in space. When it rushes into the Earth's air and glows, the streak of light is a meteor. If a piece survives and lands on the ground, it is a meteorite.

These three words are about the same piece of space rock at three moments. Out in space, it is a meteoroid. As it burns through the air, we see a meteor, which people also call a shooting star. If any of it reaches the ground, that piece is a meteorite. Almost all meteors come from grains so small that they burn up completely, high in the air. Only bigger pieces, about the size of a pebble or more, leave something behind to land.
- e.g. A grain of dust the size of a grain of rava makes a meteor, but leaves no meteorite.
- e.g. A fireball is a very bright meteor, made by a bigger piece, about the size of a pebble or larger.
- e.g. In 1803, thousands of meteorites fell near the town of L'Aigle in France.
### Why a shooting star glows · *defined*
**Definition:** A meteoroid enters the air very fast, often about 30 kilometres every second. It squashes the air in front of it so hard that the air and the grain become extremely hot. The grain turns into glowing gas.

At 30 kilometres a second, a grain would cross from Chennai to Bengaluru in about 10 seconds. That is far faster than a bullet. The grain cannot push the air out of its way in time, so the air in front of it is squashed. Squashing air makes it hot, and at this speed it becomes hotter than a furnace. The grain heats up and turns into gas, and the hot gas glows. This happens very high up, about 80 to 120 kilometres above the ground. That is why a tiny grain can make a light you see from far away. It is not the grain that is big. It is the speed.
- e.g. When you pump air into a bicycle tyre, the end of the pump gets warm. Squashing air heats it up.
- e.g. Spacecraft coming back to Earth need heat shields for the same reason.
- e.g. Most shooting stars last less than a second.
### Meteor showers come from comet dust · *defined*
**Definition:** A comet leaves a trail of dust along its path round the Sun. If the Earth's path crosses that trail, the Earth runs into the dust on the same dates every year, and we see a meteor shower.

Every time a comet passes the Sun, it loses some ice and dust. The dust spreads out slowly along the comet's path, like a dusty ring round the Sun. The Earth goes round the Sun once a year. If its path crosses one of these dust rings, it reaches the crossing on about the same date each year. Then many grains rush into our air, and we see many shooting stars in one night. In 1866, the Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli showed that the August shower, the Perseids, follows the path of a comet called Swift-Tuttle.
- e.g. The Orionid shower in October comes from dust left by Halley's Comet.
- e.g. The Geminid shower in December comes from Phaethon, a rocky object that sheds dust near the Sun, a bit like a comet.
- e.g. The shooting stars in a shower all seem to come from one small patch of sky.
### How did our Moon form? · *debated*
Scientists agree that the Moon is about 4.5 billion years old and is made of rock much like the Earth's outer layers. They still argue about exactly how it formed. Most think it formed after a giant crash, but the details are not settled.
- e.g. Moon rocks brought back by the Apollo astronauts are very like rocks from the Earth's outer layer.
- e.g. The Moon has only a small iron core, much smaller for its size than the Earth's.
- e.g. Computer models of a giant crash can make a Moon, but they do not all agree on how big the crash was.
- **Most planetary scientists:** A world about the size of Mars crashed into the young Earth. The crash threw out a huge cloud of hot rock, and the Moon formed from it. This explains why Moon rock is so like the Earth's outer rock, and why the Moon has little iron.
- **Some scientists who study the crash in computer models:** The crash may have been even bigger, and melted and mixed the Earth and the Moon together, which would explain why their rocks are almost identical.
- **An older idea, now mostly given up:** The Moon was a passing world caught by the Earth's gravity, the way Mars may have caught its two small moons. Most scientists gave this up because Moon rock is too like Earth rock.
### Could a space rock hit the Earth? · *defined*
**Definition:** Small space rocks hit the Earth's air every day and burn up. Big ones are rare. Scientists watch for big asteroids whose paths come near the Earth.

Most space rocks that reach us are dust and tiny grains. Rocks big enough to do real damage come much more rarely. About 66 million years ago, an asteroid about 10 kilometres wide hit what is now Mexico, and this helped end the time of the big dinosaurs. Today, telescopes around the world search for asteroids that come near the Earth and work out their paths. None of the large asteroids found so far is on a path to hit us in the next hundred years.
- e.g. Lonar Lake in Maharashtra fills a crater made by a meteorite long ago.
- e.g. In 2013, a rock about 20 metres wide burst in the air over Chelyabinsk in Russia. The blast broke many windows.
- e.g. The DART spacecraft showed in 2022 that crashing into a small asteroid can change its path.

## Mostly agreed
- Six of the eight planets have moons. Mercury and Venus have none. *How we know:* Telescopes and spacecraft have searched near every planet. New small moons keep being found round the giant planets, but none round Mercury or Venus.
- Comets are made of ice, dust and rock, and their tails point away from the Sun. *How we know:* Spacecraft such as Giotto, at Halley's Comet in 1986, and Rosetta, at comet 67P from 2014 to 2016, flew close and photographed the icy nucleus and its jets of gas.
- Shooting stars are grains of space rock and dust burning up in our air. *How we know:* Cameras in two places photograph the same meteor and work out its height and path. Special nets and planes high in the air have collected space dust.
- Meteor showers come from dust left along the paths of comets. *How we know:* The dates and directions of showers match where the Earth's path crosses the paths of known comets, as Schiaparelli first showed in 1866.
- Stones really do fall from space. *How we know:* Thousands of meteorites have been found, some seen falling. Their minerals and age are different from the rocks around where they land.

## How we found out
**Ernst Chladni, a German scientist, and Jean-Baptiste Biot, a French scientist, 1794 (Chladni's book) and 1803 (the fall at L'Aigle and Biot's report).** For a long time, most scientists in Europe did not believe that stones could fall from the sky. Farmers told of hot stones landing in their fields after a bright flash and a bang. Learned men said these were mistakes. The stones, they said, had been struck by lightning or thrown out of volcanoes, and fireballs were a kind of weather high in the air. In 1794, the German scientist Ernst Chladni wrote a book saying the opposite. Fireballs, he said, were lumps of rock and iron coming in from space, glowing as they rushed through the air. Many scientists laughed at him. Then, on 26 April 1803, thousands of stones fell around the town of L'Aigle in France. The young scientist Jean-Baptiste Biot was sent to find out what had happened. He visited many villages, talked to the people who had seen and heard the fall, and collected stones. He mapped where they had landed, in an oval area several kilometres long. The stones were unlike any rock found nearby. After his report, scientists accepted that stones really do fall from space. Today we call them meteorites.  
*What people believed before:* Most scientists thought stones could not fall from the sky, and that fireballs were a kind of weather. Biot's careful study of the L'Aigle fall showed that the stones came from space.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Stones cannot fall from the sky. The stones farmers found were struck by lightning or thrown up by volcanoes.** (Many European scientists in the 1700s; *rejected*)  
  For: No one had ever seen space rocks, and the idea of rocks floating in space seemed strange.  
  Against: In 1803, Jean-Baptiste Biot studied the stones that fell at L'Aigle in France and showed they had fallen from the sky. They were unlike any rock nearby.
- **A comet in the sky brings bad luck, war or disaster.** (People in many lands, for thousands of years; *rejected*)  
  For: Comets appear without warning and look strange, so people linked them to sad events that happened at the same time.  
  Against: Comets come back on paths we can work out, like Halley's Comet every 76 years or so. Bad and good things happen whether a comet is in the sky or not.
- **Shooting stars are stars falling from the sky.** (A common belief, and where the name comes from; *rejected*)  
  For: They look like a star that suddenly moves.  
  Against: The stars stay where they are. A shooting star is a tiny grain burning up only about 100 km above us. Real stars are millions of times further away, and far bigger than the Earth.
- **Comets and asteroids brought much of the Earth's water.** (Some planetary scientists; *debated*)  
  For: The young Earth was very hot, so it may have lost its first water. Comets and some asteroids hold a lot of ice.  
  Against: Water in many comets is slightly different from the water in our seas. Some scientists think most of the Earth's water was there from the start, inside its rocks.
- **Mars's two moons are asteroids that Mars caught.** (Some astronomers; *debated*)  
  For: Phobos and Deimos are small, lumpy and dark, like many asteroids.  
  Against: Their paths are almost round and line up with Mars's middle, which is hard to get by catching a passing rock. Others think they formed from rock thrown up when something hit Mars.

## Roots
We have learned about moons, asteroids, comets and shooting stars. Now we ask what a comet's journey tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your sunny ice comet shrank fastest, and its dirt stayed behind as a dark crust and a muddy trail. A real comet near the Sun loses ice and dust in the same way. The Earth runs into the dust that comets leave along their paths. So many shooting stars are tiny pieces of comets, ending their journey in our air.  
*Habit it breaks:* We give a comet, its tail, a shooting star and dust on a roof four different names, as if they were four different things. They are the same ice, rock and dust at different moments of one journey.  
*Evidence:* In the ice comet test, the comet in the sun lost ice fastest and left its dirt behind, on top and in the plate. Real comets get dark crusts and leave dust along their paths. Meteor showers come on the dates the Earth crosses those dust trails.

- *who am i:* Every atom in your body was once in the cloud of gas and dust that made the Sun, the planets and the comets.
- *what is life:* Living things are made mostly of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Comets are full of these too.
- *what is this world:* The Earth is still being added to. Space dust falls on it every day, even on your balcony.

Before the planets, there was one spinning cloud of gas and dust round the young Sun. Out of it came the Sun, the planets, the moons, the asteroids and the comets. They have different names, but they are made from the same cloud. Comets still carry some of that first ice and dust. Near the Sun they lose it, and some of it falls on the Earth as shooting stars. So the pieces of the one cloud are still meeting each other again.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu: by knowing one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay. A pot or a cup is a name given to a new shape. What is real is the clay. (Comet, tail, meteor and space dust are names for one material at different moments, as pot and cup are names for clay in different shapes.)
- **Dhammapada 277:** Everything that is put together changes and does not last. A person who sees this clearly suffers less. (A comet loses some of itself every time it passes the Sun, like your ice comet in the sunny window. In the end, its ice runs out.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 12 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 500 BCE:** On those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow. (Halley's Comet keeps its name and its path, but each time it returns it has lost some ice and dust. It is the same comet and not quite the same.)
- Science (settled): Meteor showers happen when the Earth passes through dust left along the paths of comets. Schiaparelli linked the Perseids to comet Swift-Tuttle in 1866.
- Science (debated): Some of the Earth's water may have come from comets and asteroids. How much is still argued about.
- Other view, The Nyaya and Vaisheshika schools of Indian philosophy: When parts come together, they make a real new whole, not just a name. A pot is a real pot, not only clay. In the same way, a comet is a real comet while it lasts, and a meteor is a real meteor, even if they share the same dust.
- Practice, Watch a drop leave (3 min): Let's put an ice cube on a steel plate and sit very still. Watch one drop form at the bottom of the ice. Breathe in slowly. As the drop runs away, breathe out slowly. Let's do this for five drops. The ice is getting smaller, and the water is going somewhere new. Where will it go next?
- Ask: A comet, its dust and a shooting star are made of the same stuff. Are they one thing or three?
- Ask: Your ice comet lost ice but kept its dirt. Is it still the same comet at the end?
- Ask: Some of the dust on your balcony may have come from space. Where does the sky end and the Earth begin?

## Story: Seven Shooting Stars over Madurai
*Nila, Arjun, Revathi Athai, Paati and this night are made up. The facts in the story are real: the Geminid shower in mid-December, its link to Phaethon, the size and speed of the grains, the height where they glow, and Halley's Comet in 1986 and 2061.*

On the night of 13 December, Revathi Athai carried three straw mats up to the terrace of the flat in Madurai. Nila carried the flask of hot milk. Her little brother Arjun carried nothing, because he was already wrapped in Paati's shawl and could hardly walk.

'Why are we sleeping on the terrace in winter?' Arjun asked.

'We are not sleeping,' said Athai. She taught physics at a college and always brought a notebook. 'Tonight is the Geminid shower. If we are lucky, we will see shooting stars.'

They lay on the mats with their feet towards the water tank. The sky over the city was orange near the edges, from all the street lights. Only a few stars showed overhead.

'I can't see anything,' said Arjun after one minute.

'Your eyes need time,' said Athai. 'Give them twenty minutes in the dark, and don't look at the phone.'

Nila looked at the patch of sky above the neem tree. She thought about the shooting stars in films, big and slow, with long sparkling tails. She waited.

Then a thin white line flashed across the sky and was gone. It took less time than a blink.

'There!' Nila sat up. 'Did you see it? Where did it fall?'

'It didn't fall anywhere near us,' said Athai. 'It burned up about 100 kilometres above our heads. That is higher than any aeroplane, higher than any cloud.'

Arjun was sure it had landed in the next street. 'I'll find it tomorrow,' he said.

Athai took something out of her pocket. It was a small paper packet from the kitchen. She tipped a few grains of rava onto the back of her notebook and shone her phone torch on them for a moment.

'Most shooting stars are made by grains about this big,' she said. 'Some are even smaller, like dust.'

'That's too small,' said Nila. 'You couldn't see something that small from 100 kilometres away.'

'You can't see the grain,' said Athai. 'You see the light it makes. That grain was moving about 35 kilometres every second. At that speed, it could go from here to Thoothukudi in about 4 seconds. It hit the air so hard that it squashed the air in front of it. Squashed air gets hot. This air got so hot that the grain turned into glowing gas.'

Arjun held a grain of rava between his finger and thumb and looked at it very seriously.

A second shooting star flashed, then a third, low over the temple towers in the distance. Nila made a mark in Athai's notebook for each one.

'Why tonight?' she asked. 'Why not every night?'

Athai drew a big circle in the notebook. 'This is the Earth's path round the Sun. It takes one year to go round.' Then she drew a long, stretched oval that crossed the circle. 'This is the path of a rocky thing called Phaethon. Each time Phaethon passes close to the Sun, the heat makes it shed bits of dust. The dust spreads out along Phaethon's path, like a trail of crumbs.'

She tapped the place where the circle and the oval crossed. 'Every year, on these same nights in December, the Earth reaches this crossing. We run into the crumbs. They rush into our air, and we see shooting stars.'

'So it happens on the same date every year,' said Nila slowly, 'because the Earth gets to the same place on the same date.'

'Exactly,' said Athai. 'Most other showers come from comets. Comets are lumps of ice and dust. Near the Sun, their ice turns to gas, and the gas blows dust out into a long tail. That dust stays along the comet's path too.'

Paati had come up the stairs slowly, carrying a plate of murukku. She sat down on the edge of a mat.

'In 1986 I saw a comet,' Paati said. 'From the roof of our old house in Tirunelveli. Everyone called it the vaal natchathiram, the tail star. We expected something grand. It was only a faint smudge, like a thumbprint on a window. But I was so happy to see it.'

'That was Halley's Comet,' said Athai. 'It comes back about every 76 years. It will be near the Sun again in 2061.'

Nila counted on her fingers. 'I'll be 44,' she said. 'I'll show it to my children.'

'And some of the shooting stars you see each October,' said Athai, 'are dust that Halley's Comet left behind long ago. So you have already seen pieces of Paati's comet.'

Paati laughed and patted Nila's head. 'Then the comet came back to see you,' she said.

Just then, a bright yellow streak crossed half the sky, slower than the others. It left a faint glowing line that faded in a second. Arjun shrieked. Nila dropped the pencil. Even Athai said, 'Oh!'

'That was a fireball,' Athai said. 'A bigger piece, perhaps the size of a pea.'

By midnight, Nila had seven marks in the notebook. Athai said that from a dark village far from the city they might have seen more than fifty in that time. But Nila did not mind. Seven was enough.

As they rolled up the mats, Arjun opened his fist. The grain of rava was still there, a bit damp. 'I'm keeping it,' he said. 'It's my shooting star.'

'Then keep it safe,' said Athai. 'Real ones don't last as long as that.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: a shooting star is a grain of dust, often as small as a grain of rava, entering the air so fast that the air in front of it is squashed and becomes extremely hot. The grain turns to glowing gas about 100 km up. A meteor shower comes on the same dates every year because the Earth reaches the same point in its orbit, where it crosses a trail of dust left by a comet, or, for the Geminids, by the rocky object Phaethon. So a comet, the dust it leaves, and the shooting stars we see are the same material at different times.

### g1 · The Comet's Journey: a board game (20 min)
- **rules:** Each player is a comet. ### e1 · The Ice Comet: what happens to its dirt in the sun? (45 min, 2 days)
- **question:** When an icy, dirty ball is warmed by the sun, what happens to its ice and to its dirt?
- **predict:** Guess first. One ice comet sits in the sun and one in the shade. Which will shrink faster? As the ice goes, will the comet get cleaner, stay the same colour, or get darker? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Make both ice comets the same way, from the same amount of crushed ice and the same amount of dirt, in two bowls of the same size. Put them on the same kind of plate. Change only one thing: one sits in a sunny spot and one in the shade. Measure both at the same times.
- **safety:** A grown-up crushes the ice with the rolling pin. Wash hands after touching soil. Don't eat the ice comets.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both ice comets at the start and at 30 minutes, showing where the dirt is.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Sunny comet: height (cm) | Sunny comet: dirt on top (1 a little, 2 some, 3 a dark crust) | Shady comet: height (cm) | Shady comet: dirt on top (1, 2 or 3)
- **schedule:** day 1: Crush the ice, mix in the dirt, press it into two katoris and freeze overnight. (10 min); day 2: Guess. Put one ice comet in the sun and one in the shade. Measure and look every 10 minutes for 30 minutes. Compare. (35 min)
- **s1 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put about 16 ice cubes in a folded towel or a plastic bag. 2. Crush them with a rolling pin into small pieces. 3. In each katori, mix half the crushed ice with 1 spoon of soil and 1 spoon of tea leaves. 4. Press it down hard with a spoon. 5. Put both katoris in the freezer overnight.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to make two small comets. A real comet is a lump of ice, dust and rock. Ours is ice and dirt pressed together.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up crushes the ice. Mix in the dirt. Press it into two katoris. Freeze them overnight.
  - Note: Crushing the ice spreads the dirt all through it. If you freeze muddy water instead, the dirt sinks to the bottom before it freezes. Freeze for at least 3 hours, or overnight.
  - Wait: Leave the katoris in the freezer overnight.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Run tap water over the outside of each katori for a few seconds. 2. Tip each ice comet out onto its own steel plate, flat side down. 3. Measure the height of each one. 4. Look at the dirt on top. Score it 1 for a little, 2 for some, or 3 for a dark crust.  
  *Parent says:* Here are our two comets. Let's measure them. How much dirt can you see on top right now?  
  *Child mode:* Tip out your ice comets. Measure how tall they are. Look at the dirt on top. Score it 1, 2 or 3.
  - Note: They should be about 3 to 5 cm tall, and the same height. At the start, most of the dirt is inside the ice, so the score is usually 1.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess before you move the plates.  
  *Parent says:* One comet will go in the sun, like a comet coming close to the Sun. The other stays in the shade, like a comet far out in the cold. Which one will shrink faster? And as the ice goes, will the comet get cleaner, stay the same, or get darker? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One comet goes in the sun. One stays in the shade. Which shrinks faster? Will it get cleaner or darker? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The sun is warmer, so that one melts faster.; The water will wash the dirt away, so it will get cleaner.; The ice goes but the dirt stays, so it will get darker.)
  - Note: Many children guess the comet will get cleaner, because water washes things. Don't correct the guess. Let the test answer it.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one plate in a sunny spot, such as a windowsill or balcony with direct sun. 2. Put the other in a shady spot in the same room. 3. Start a timer.  
  *Parent says:* Sunny comet here, shady comet there. Now we wait 10 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Put one plate in the sun. Put one in the shade. Start the timer.
  - Note: If no spot gets direct sun today, put one plate on top of a steel vessel of warm tap water, and the other on the counter. The warm water plays the part of the Sun.
- **s5 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every 10 minutes, for 30 minutes, measure the height of each comet. 2. Score the dirt on top of each one: 1, 2 or 3. 3. Look at the water in each plate. Is it clear or muddy? 4. Write everything in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Time to look. How tall is each comet now? What is happening to the dirt on top? Is the water in the plate clear or muddy?  
  *Child mode:* Every 10 minutes, measure both comets. Score the dirt on top. Look at the water in the plate.
  - Note: In direct sun, the sunny comet usually shrinks by 1 to 2 cm in 30 minutes and gets a dark, gritty crust. The shady one changes more slowly. Tea leaves cling to the ice, so the crust is easy to see. If both look the same, leave them another 20 minutes.
  - Check: In the ice comet test, after 30 minutes, what does the top of the sunny comet look like? → **Darker, with a crust of dirt on top**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child how this fits a real comet.  
  *Parent says:* A real comet does just this near the Sun. Its ice goes, and its dust is left behind as a dark crust. That is why comets are darker than coal. In space there is no air, so the ice turns straight into gas instead of melting. The gas blows some dust off into a tail. The rest of the dust spreads along the comet's path, like the muddy water in our plate.  
  *Child mode:* A real comet does this near the Sun. Its ice goes. Its dust stays as a dark crust. Some dust is left along its path.
  - Check: A comet is far out beyond Neptune, in the cold. What is it doing? → **It stays frozen, with no tail**
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think happens to a comet near the Sun? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Ask: What is your claim, evidence and reasoning? (good: The comet near the Sun loses its ice faster. My sunny comet shrank more. The sun warmed it more.; The dirt stays behind. The sunny comet got a dark crust. The ice went away but the dirt could not.)
### e2 · Fan and Tail: does a comet's tail stream out behind it? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a comet's tail always stream out behind it, or does it point away from the Sun?
- **predict:** Guess first. The fan is the Sun. When you walk slowly away from the fan with your paper comet, will its tail be behind your hand or in front of it? Why do you think so?

- **safety:** Keep hands, hair and the tissue tail at least one metre from the fan. A grown-up switches the fan on and off.
- **record:** Draw a small picture for each walk: the fan, your comet, an arrow for the way you walked, and the tail.
- **results table:** Fan on or off | Which way I walked | Which way the tail pointed | Drawing
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push the lemon onto the end of a pencil. 2. Tape 6 tissue strips to one side of the lemon.  
  *Parent says:* This is our comet. The lemon is the head, and the tissue strips are the tail.  
  *Child mode:* Push the lemon onto the pencil. Tape on the tissue strips. That's your comet.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* With the fan off, your child walks slowly across the room holding the comet up.  
  *Parent says:* Fan off. Walk slowly across the room. Where does the tail go?  
  *Child mode:* Fan off. Walk slowly. Where does the tail go?
  - Ask: Where did the tail go? (good: Behind the comet.; It trailed behind, like hair when you run.)
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch the fan on to a low or middle speed. 2. Ask your child to guess before walking.  
  *Parent says:* Now the fan is the Sun. Soon you will walk slowly away from the fan. Will the tail be behind your hand, or in front of it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The fan is the Sun. You will walk away from it. Will the tail be behind or in front? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Behind, because tails always trail behind.; In front, because the fan blows it.)
  - Note: Most children guess the tail stays behind, because that is what happened with the fan off. Let the test show them.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand 2 metres in front of the fan. 2. Walk slowly towards the fan, stopping 1 metre away. 3. Walk slowly sideways, across the fan's air. 4. Walk slowly backwards, away from the fan. 5. After each walk, draw which way the tail pointed.

  *Child mode:* Walk towards the fan. Then sideways. Then away. Draw the tail each time.
  - Note: Walk slowly, about one small step each second. If you walk fast, your own movement can beat the fan's air and the tail flops. That is a fair thing to notice.
  - Check: In the fan and tail test, you walk slowly away from the fan. Where does the tissue tail point? → **Away from the fan, in front of the comet**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child how this fits a real comet.  
  *Parent says:* The Sun pushes on a comet's tail all the time. It pushes with its light, and with a stream of tiny particles called the solar wind. So a comet's tail always points away from the Sun. When the comet is going away from the Sun, its tail goes in front. The real Sun pushes outwards in every direction, not just one way like our fan.  
  *Child mode:* The Sun pushes a comet's tail away. So the tail points away from the Sun. Even when the comet goes away.
  - Ask: How is the real Sun different from our fan? (good: The Sun pushes in every direction.; The fan only blows one way.)

   **Answer:** The streak of light when a bit of space rock burns up in our air. *Why:* The streak of light is the meteor. Picking 'a stone that lands' describes a meteorite, the piece that reaches the ground.
10. (mcq) Why does a tiny grain glow so brightly when it enters our air?  
   Options: It moves so fast that it squashes the air in front of it, and the air gets extremely hot / It is on fire before it arrives / It reflects the light of the Moon / It is a small star  
   **Answer:** It moves so fast that it squashes the air in front of it, and the air gets extremely hot. *Why:* At tens of kilometres a second, the grain squashes the air ahead so hard that it gets hotter than a furnace. The grain turns to glowing gas. Picking 'on fire before it arrives' is a common idea, but space has no air, so nothing can burn there.
11. (mcq) Why does the Geminid meteor shower come around 13 and 14 December every year?  
   Options: The Earth reaches the same dust trail at the same point in its path each year / Stars fall more in winter / The Moon sends dust in December / December nights are longer  
   **Answer:** The Earth reaches the same dust trail at the same point in its path each year. *Why:* The Earth takes one year to go round the Sun, so it crosses the dust trail at the same point, on the same dates, every year. Picking 'December nights are longer' is a sensible thought, because longer nights give more time to watch, but it does not explain the dates.
12. (mcq) In 1803, thousands of stones fell near L'Aigle in France. What did the scientist Jean-Baptiste Biot show?  
   Options: The stones really had fallen from the sky / The stones had been thrown up by a volcano / The stones had been struck by lightning / Nobody had really seen anything  
   **Answer:** The stones really had fallen from the sky. *Why:* Biot talked to many people who saw the fall, mapped where the stones landed, and found they were unlike local rock. This convinced scientists that stones fall from space. 'Lightning' and 'volcano' were the old explanations that his study ruled out.

## Applications
- Space agencies track asteroids that come near the Earth, so any danger is known years ahead.
- In 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft crashed into a small asteroid and changed its path, as a test of how to protect the Earth.
- Spacecraft bring back bits of asteroids and comets, such as Japan's Hayabusa2 from the asteroid Ryugu, to learn what the early solar system was made of.
- Engineers put shields on spacecraft and the International Space Station to protect them from tiny grains of space dust.
- Heat shields on spacecraft coming back to Earth work against the same squashed, hot air that makes shooting stars glow.
- Meteor cameras around the world record fireballs and work out where any meteorites landed, so people can find them.

## Spotted around us
- Shooting stars on the Geminid nights, around 13 and 14 December, from a terrace or a dark beach in Tamil Nadu or Kerala.
- Jupiter as a bright, steady dot in the evening sky, with tiny moons beside it if you use binoculars.
- Lonar Lake in Buldhana district, Maharashtra, a round lake inside a crater made by a meteorite.
- Meteorites on display in museums and science centres, such as the Geological Survey of India's collections.
- The word 'vaal natchathiram', the tail star, in old Tamil stories and in grandparents' memories of Halley's Comet in 1986.

## Open questions
- **Is there life in the ocean under the ice of Jupiter's moon Europa?** Europa very likely has a salty ocean, but nobody has looked inside it yet. NASA's Europa Clipper spacecraft is on its way to study it.
- **Where did the Earth's water come from?** Some was probably there from the start, and some may have come from comets and asteroids. Scientists still argue about how much came from each.
- **How exactly did our Moon form?** Most scientists think a giant crash made it, but no computer model yet matches every fact about Moon rock.
- **Exactly how much space dust reaches the Earth each day?** Different ways of measuring give very different answers, from a few tonnes to many tonnes a day.

## Connected maths
- A grain that makes a shooting star moves about 30 kilometres every second. How far does it go in 10 seconds? **300 kilometres.**. Each second it goes 30 km. In 10 seconds it goes 10 times as far. 30 times 10 is 300. So it goes 300 km, about the distance from Chennai to Bengaluru. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- In a made-up ice comet test, the sunny comet went from 4 cm to 2.5 cm tall in 30 minutes. The shady comet went from 4 cm to 3.5 cm. How much did each one shrink? How many times as much did the sunny one shrink? **The sunny one shrank 1.5 cm and the shady one 0.5 cm. The sunny one shrank 3 times as much.**. 4 take away 2.5 is 1.5. 4 take away 3.5 is 0.5. 1.5 is three lots of 0.5. So the sunny comet shrank 3 times as much. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Halley's Comet came near the Sun in 1986. It comes back about every 75 years. In which year will it come back? If you were born in 2017, how old will you be then? **In about 2061. You will be 44.**. 1986 plus 75 is 2061. 2061 take away 2017 is 44. The real time between visits changes by a year or two, because the planets' gravity tugs on the comet. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- In a made-up Geminid watch, Kavya counted 2 shooting stars in the first 10 minutes, 3 in the next 10 minutes and 5 in the last 10 minutes. Draw a bar chart. How many did she see in all? What pattern do you see? **10 in all. The count went up as the night went on.**. 2 plus 3 plus 5 is 10. The bars get taller, so more shooting stars came later. The Geminids' part of the sky climbs higher as the night goes on, so more meteors can reach your view. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- The Earth has 1 moon, Mars has 2 and Neptune has 16. Jupiter has more than 90, and Saturn has more than 270, though Saturn is smaller than Jupiter. Put the five planets in order of how many moons they have. Does the biggest planet have the most moons? **Earth, Mars, Neptune, Jupiter, Saturn. No. Jupiter is biggest, but Saturn has the most known moons.**. 1 is less than 2, 2 is less than 16, 16 is less than 90, and 90 is less than 270. Jupiter is the biggest planet, but Saturn has more moons. So size alone does not set the number of moons. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- The Earth takes about 365 days to go round the Sun. The Geminids peak around 14 December this year. About when will they peak next year? Why? **Around 14 December next year.**. After 365 days, the Earth is back at the same point on its path, where it crosses Phaethon's dust trail. So the shower comes back on about the same date. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
