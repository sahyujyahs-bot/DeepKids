# Questions we can test: guess first, then try it
`sci.how-we-know.asking-questions.L2`

**Summary.** Can a pencil lift a whole bottle of rice? You cannot know by thinking about it. You have to try it and watch. A question like this is a testable question: doing something shows the answer to anyone who looks. Other questions, like 'Which colour is the best?', have no test, because each person's answer is true for them. Your child sorts questions, makes predictions with reasons, tests them, and hears how two astronomers, Edmond Halley and Nicole-Reine Lepaute, predicted the return of a comet.

**Intro.** *Here is a bottle full of rice and one pencil. Push the pencil into the rice. Can you lift the whole bottle by the pencil? What do you think, and why?* You just made a prediction. A prediction is what you think will happen, and why, said before you try. Some questions can be answered by trying, like this one. Others cannot, like 'Is rice nicer than roti?' In this unit you learn to tell the two kinds apart. You make predictions, test them, and count what happens. A wrong prediction is useful too: it shows you which idea to change.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can tell a question we can test by trying from one we cannot, and say why.
1. By the end I can make a prediction and give a reason for it, before a test.
2. By the end I can do a test, count what happens, and say if my prediction was right.
3. By the end I can say what I learned when my prediction was wrong.
4. By the end I can say that some things, like a dropped spoon falling, can be predicted every time, and others, like one coin toss, cannot.
5. By the end I can tell how Halley and Lepaute predicted the return of a comet, and what it showed.

## Terms
- **Testable question**: A testable question is one you can answer by doing something and watching what happens. Anyone who does the same thing sees the same answer. *e.g.* 'Does a steel spoon sink in water?' is testable. Drop one in a tumbler of water and watch.
- **Prediction**: A prediction is what you think will happen, and why, said before you try. It is a guess with a reason. *e.g.* 'The crumpled paper will land first, because it is a small ball and the air slows it less.'
- **Test**: A test is something you do to find out if your prediction is right. You do it, watch carefully and write down what happened. *e.g.* Pushing a pencil into rice and lifting it is a test of the question 'Can a pencil lift a bottle of rice?'
- **Result**: The result is what really happened in your test. You write it down, even when it is not what you predicted. *e.g.* 'The bottle lifted after 30 pokes' is a result.
- **Opinion**: An opinion is what one person likes, wants or feels is best. Two people can have different opinions, and both can be true for them. *e.g.* 'Idli is tastier than dosa' is an opinion. Your friend may think dosa is tastier.
- **Friction**: Friction is the grip between two things that touch. The harder they press together, the stronger the grip. *e.g.* Your chappal does not slip on the road because of friction.

## Explanations
### Questions we can test, and questions we can't · *defined*
**Definition:** A question is testable when you can do something, watch what happens, and get an answer that anyone else would also see.

'Can a pencil lift a bottle of rice?' is testable. You try it, and everyone watching sees the same thing. 'Which colour is the best?' is an opinion, so no test can answer it. Some questions are not testable yet, like 'Is there life on a planet near another star?' Nobody can travel there today. Some questions are answered by asking, like 'When was Amma born?'
- e.g. Testable: 'Does a steel spoon sink in water?' Drop one in and see.
- e.g. An opinion: 'Is a mango nicer than a banana?' Each person's answer is true for them.
- e.g. Not testable yet: 'What is at the very edge of space?' Nobody can go there to look.
- e.g. Answered by asking: 'What did Ammamma eat when she was small?' Ask her.
### A prediction is a guess with a reason · *defined*
**Definition:** A prediction says what you think will happen in a test, and why you think so, before the test starts.

'I think the bottle will fall' is a guess. 'I think the bottle will fall, because rice is loose and slippery' is a prediction, because it has a reason. If the bottle lifts, the reason shows which idea to change. Say your prediction before the test. After the test, anyone can say 'I knew it!'
- e.g. 'The ice will float, because I have seen ice floating in a glass of water.'
- e.g. 'The crumpled paper will land first, because it is smaller, so the air pushes it less.'
- e.g. 'It will rain today, because the sky is dark grey and the wind is cool.'
### A wrong prediction still teaches you something · *defined*
**Definition:** When a result does not match a prediction, the idea behind the prediction needs to change.

Many people predict that a pencil cannot lift a bottle of rice. Then they see it lift. Their idea, 'rice is too loose to grip', was wrong for packed rice. Scientists are wrong often too. They write down what happened, change their idea, and test again.
- e.g. In 1835, a French thinker, Auguste Comte, wrote that people would never find out what stars are made of. Within about 30 years, scientists began to find out from starlight.
- e.g. In the Rice Bottle Lift, loose rice lets the pencil slide out. That result tells you to try packing the rice.
### Why the rice held the pencil · *defined*
**Definition:** Rice grains pressed tightly together push hard on each other and on the pencil, and friction between them holds the pencil.

Loose rice has air gaps between the grains. The grains can slide past each other, so the pencil slides out. Each poke and tap pushes the grains closer and fills the gaps. Then they press hard on the pencil from every side. Friction is the grip between two things that touch, and it gets stronger when they press harder. Hundreds of grains each grip the pencil a little. Together they hold up the whole bottle.
- e.g. A tightly packed dabba of dal is hard to push a spoon into.
- e.g. A gunny sack of rice stands up by itself when it is full and tight.
### Some things can be predicted every time, and some can't · *defined*
**Definition:** Some events happen the same way every time, so we can predict each one. Others change by chance, so we can only predict what happens over many tries.

A spoon you let go of falls down every single time, because the Earth pulls it. You can predict every drop. A coin shaken in your hands lands heads or tails by chance. Nobody can predict one shake, but over many shakes about half land heads. Weather is in between. Forecasters at the India Meteorological Department use measurements to predict rain. They are right often, but not always.
- e.g. Sure every time: a dropped spoon falls, and the Sun rises in the east.
- e.g. Chance: one coin toss, one roll of a Ludo die.
- e.g. Often right: the weather forecast for tomorrow. Anna Mani, a scientist from Kerala, made and improved weather instruments for the India Meteorological Department, so forecasters had better measurements.
- e.g. Eclipses can be predicted years ahead. In 499 CE, Aryabhata wrote that a solar eclipse happens when the Moon covers the Sun, and a lunar eclipse when the Moon moves into the Earth's shadow.
### Can we test what it is like to be someone else? · *debated*
Ants sense, remember and choose. You can test what an ant does: put sugar out and watch which way she goes. It is much harder to test what it is like, from the inside, to be that ant. Scientists argue about whether a test can ever show this.
- e.g. A test can show that a crow chooses the bigger piece of roti.
- e.g. A test can show that a bean plant turns their leaves towards the window.
- **Many scientists who study other animals:** What a being does tells us a lot about what they feel and think, just as we know a friend's feelings by what they do and say.
- **Some philosophers and scientists:** We can test what others do, but what it feels like inside them can never be checked from outside, not even for another person.

## Mostly agreed
- A testable question is answered by doing something and watching what happens. *How we know:* When anyone does the Rice Bottle Lift in the same way, with the rice packed tight, the bottle lifts. The result does not depend on who is watching.
- A prediction must be said before the test. *How we know:* After a result, it is easy to think 'I knew it'. Scientists write predictions down first, so that the test can really show if they were wrong.
- One test can be a fluke, so we test again. *How we know:* In the Lucky Blow test, the two counts of sixes are often different just by chance. Doing it again often swaps which one is bigger.

## How we found out
**Edmond Halley, an English astronomer, and Nicole-Reine Lepaute, a French astronomer and calculator, with Alexis Clairaut and Jérôme Lalande, 1705 (Halley's prediction) to 1759 (the comet's return).** For thousands of years, people around the world saw comets as surprises. Many thought each comet came once and was a sign of bad times. In England, Edmond Halley studied old records. He saw that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 had moved across the sky along almost the same path, about 76 years apart. In 1705 he wrote that they were one comet going round the Sun, and predicted it would come back around 1758. Halley died in 1742, before he could see if he was right. In France, Nicole-Reine Lepaute worked with Alexis Clairaut and Jérôme Lalande. For months they calculated how the pull of Jupiter and Saturn would slow the comet down. They predicted it would pass closest to the Sun in mid-April 1759. On Christmas night in 1758, a farmer in Germany named Johann Palitzsch spotted it. It passed closest to the Sun on 13 March 1759, about a month from their date. Clairaut later left Lepaute's name out of his book, but Lalande gave her the credit.  
*What people believed before:* Each comet comes once, passes by and never returns, often as a sign of bad times.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Blowing on a die before you roll it brings more sixes.** (A common belief in many games, in India and elsewhere; *rejected*)  
  For: Sometimes a six comes right after a blow, and we remember those times.  
  Against: Count the sixes in many rolls with a blow and many without. The counts come out about the same. We tend to remember the lucky times and forget the others.
- **Some questions can never be tested, like what the stars are made of.** (Auguste Comte, a French thinker, in 1835; *rejected*)  
  For: In 1835 nobody could travel to a star or bring back a piece of one.  
  Against: In 1859 and 1860, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen in Germany found that each kind of material gives out its own colours of light. Starlight carries those colours. In 1925, Cecilia Payne showed from starlight that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
- **A real scientific idea must be one that a test could prove wrong.** (Karl Popper, a philosopher born in Austria, in the 1930s; *debated*)  
  For: An idea that no test could ever show wrong fits everything, so it tells us nothing.  
  Against: Some scientists say ideas about the very start of the universe are still science, though nobody can test them yet. They hope tests will come later, as they did for the stars.

## Roots
We have sorted questions, made predictions and tested them with rice, coins and a die. Now we ask what the Rice Bottle Lift tells us about questions and answers.

**Deep truth.** In the Rice Bottle Lift, you and your grown-up made different predictions. The bottle did the same thing in front of both of you. Anyone who repeats the test sees the same result. A question about which colour is best has no test, because each person's answer is true for them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat every question as the same kind: either you know the answer or you don't. But some questions are answered by a test that everyone sees the same way, and others by what each person likes or feels.  
*Evidence:* In the Rice Bottle Lift, two people made different predictions, and the bottle did the same thing in front of both of them. In the story, the two tumblers showed the same to Ila and Kiran mama, while 'idli or dosa?' had a different right answer for each person.

- *who am i:* You are someone who can wonder, predict and test. Your likes, like your favourite colour, are part of you too.
- *what is life:* Living beings get ready for what is coming. A young sunflower turns to face east during the night, ready for the morning sun.
- *what is this world:* The world answers our tests in the same way for everyone who tries. That is why people everywhere can share what they find.

Before the test, the answer was not in anyone's guess. It was in the rice and the pencil, and how hard the grains pressed together. Everyone who watched saw the same result. Your liking for a colour is real too, but it belongs to the person who has it.

- **Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 63:** A monk named Malunkyaputta asks the Buddha questions such as 'Is the world eternal?' The Buddha does not answer. He tells of a man hit by a poisoned arrow who refuses help until he knows who shot it. The man would die before the questions were answered. The Buddha says to work first on what can be worked on. (Some questions can be answered by a test today, and some cannot. Knowing which kind you have helps you choose what to do next.)
- **Confucius, Analects 2.17:** Confucius tells his student: when you know a thing, say that you know it. When you do not know a thing, say that you do not know it. That is real knowing. (A prediction is said honestly before a test. When the test shows it was wrong, a good finder-out says so, and learns.)
- Science (settled): A test question is answered the same way for everyone who does the test carefully. That is why scientists in different countries can check each other's work.
- Science (debated): Some questions, like what the stars are made of, looked untestable and were tested later. Others, like what the universe was like at its very start, may or may not ever be testable. Scientists argue about this.
- Other view, Some philosophers: They say that even a test result is seen by each person in their own way, so no two people ever see exactly the same thing. Others answer that, even so, everyone who watched can agree that the bottle lifted.
- Practice, Predict your breath (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Before you breathe, predict: will this breath feel warm or cool on your top lip? Now breathe out through your nose and feel. Were you right? Now predict a breath in. Breathe in slowly. Notice what really happened. Do it three times.
- Ask: If a test shows the same thing to everyone, is the answer in the world or in us?
- Ask: Can a question be important even if no test can answer it?
- Ask: Is there anything you were sure about, until a test showed you something else?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If nobody can test which colour is the best, does that make the question silly? (hint: Think about why people still ask it. What does your answer tell someone about you?)
- *deep:* Your prediction was wrong, but you learned something. Was it a good prediction or a bad one? (hint: Think about the reason you gave. Did the wrong result show you which idea to change?)
- *big:* A question nobody could test in 1835 was tested later. Which questions that we can't test today might be tested one day? (hint: Think about the stars, the middle of the Earth, the inside of an ant's life, or the start of everything.)
- *big:* Why do you think people like to predict things, like rain, a cricket score or the end of a story? (hint: Think about what you do when you know what is coming, and how a surprise feels.)

## Story: Two Tumblers of Batter
*Visakhapatnam is a city by the sea in Andhra Pradesh. Ammamma means mother's mother in Telugu, and mama means mother's brother. Ila, Kiran mama, Amma and Ammamma are made up. It is true that dosa batter rises much less in a fridge: the microbes that make it rise, bacteria and yeasts, work slowly in the cold.*

Ila was seven. She lived on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Visakhapatnam, close enough to the sea to smell it when the wind blew. She lived with Amma and Ammamma, her grandmother.

That week, Kiran mama had come from Pune. He was Amma's younger brother. He was tall, he laughed loudly, and he was always sure he was right.

On Saturday evening, Ammamma ground rice and urad dal into a thick, white batter for the next morning's dosas. She poured it into a big steel vessel and set it on the shelf beside the stove, where the kitchen was warmest.

Kiran mama looked at it. 'Why leave it out?' he said. 'Put it in the fridge. The fridge keeps things fresh. It will rise just the same.'

Ammamma said nothing. She only smiled and looked at Ila.

Ila looked at the batter. In the morning it was always higher in the vessel, puffy, with tiny holes on top. She had never wondered why. Now she did.

'Does it rise the same in the fridge?' she said slowly. 'We could find out.'

'Find out how?' said Kiran mama.

'We test it,' said Ila. 'Ammamma, can I have two tumblers?'

Ammamma gave her two steel tumblers that were exactly the same. Ila poured batter into each one, up to the same height. Ammamma helped her slip a rubber band round each tumbler, right at the top of the batter.

'Before we test,' said Ammamma, 'each of you must make a prediction. What will happen, and why?'

Kiran mama folded his arms. 'Both will rise the same. Batter rises by itself. Cold or warm makes no difference.'

Ila thought hard. 'I think the shelf one will rise more,' she said. 'Because Ammamma always keeps it near the stove. She must have a reason.'

Ammamma wrote both predictions on the back of an old calendar page. Then one tumbler went into the fridge and one went on the warm shelf.

At dinner, Kiran mama ate three chapatis and said, 'Now here is a real question for you, Ila. Which is better, idli or dosa?'

'Dosa,' said Amma at once.

'Idli,' said Ammamma.

Ila laughed. 'We can't test that one,' she said. 'Amma likes dosa and Ammamma likes idli. They are both right, for themselves.'

Kiran mama raised his eyebrows. 'So the batter question is a different kind of question?'

'Yes,' said Ila. 'For the batter, we just look in the morning. Everyone will see the same thing.'

In the morning Ila ran to the kitchen before she even brushed her teeth. The tumbler on the shelf was full. The batter had risen far above the rubber band. It was puffed up and bubbly, and it smelled a little sour.

Then she opened the fridge. The batter in that tumbler sat almost exactly at its rubber band. It was smooth and flat, with hardly a bubble.

'Mama! Come and see!'

Kiran mama came in, yawning. He looked at the two tumblers for a long time. 'Well,' he said. 'I was wrong.'

'Your prediction helped, though,' said Ila. 'If you hadn't said it, we would never have tested it.'

Ammamma poured a ladle of the risen batter onto the hot tava. It spread into a thin circle and sizzled. 'Do you know why it rises?' she asked them both.

Neither of them did. So Ammamma explained. Batter is full of tiny living beings, too small to see, called microbes. All night they feed on the rice and dal, and they make gas. The gas makes the bubbles, and the bubbles push the batter up. In the warm kitchen they work fast. In the cold fridge they work very slowly.

'So the fridge does keep it fresh,' said Kiran mama. 'It keeps the microbes slow.'

'Yes,' said Ammamma. 'That is why we put the batter in the fridge after it has risen, not before.'

Ila ate her dosa. It was crisp at the edges and soft in the middle. She looked at the calendar page with the two predictions on it.

'Mama,' she said, 'let's test something else today.'

Kiran mama groaned, but he was smiling. 'Only if I get to predict first.'

**For the parent:** Ila found that some questions, like 'Does batter rise in the fridge?', can be answered by a test that everyone can see. Others, like 'Which is better, idli or dosa?', are opinions, and each person's answer is true for them.

- Ask: What were the two predictions, and what reason did each person give?
- Ask: Why did Ila use two tumblers that were exactly the same, with rubber bands at the same height?
- Ask: Why couldn't anyone test 'Which is better, idli or dosa?'
- Ask: Kiran mama was wrong. How did his prediction still help?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Rice Bottle Lift: can a pencil lift a bottle of rice? (25 min)
- **question:** Can a pencil pushed into rice lift the whole bottle? Does it work better when the rice is loose, or packed tight?
- **predict:** Look at the bottle of rice and the pencil. Will the pencil lift the bottle, or slide out? Why do you think so? If we poke and press the rice many times, how many pokes will it take before it lifts?
- **fair test:** Use the same bottle, the same rice and the same pencil every time. Only one thing changes: how tightly the rice is packed. Lift slowly and the same way each time.
- **safety:** Use a plastic bottle, never glass. Lift it only a hand's width above the tray, so it falls softly if it slips.
- **record:** Draw the bottle hanging from the pencil, or falling off it. Write your prediction, the number of pokes and what happened for each try.
- **results table:** Try | My prediction | How many pokes | Did the bottle lift? | What I saw
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the bottle on the tray. 2. Your child pours rice in through the paper cone, up to the very top. 3. Do not tap or press it yet.  
  *Parent says:* Let's fill this bottle right to the top with rice. Pour gently. Don't press it down yet.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bottle to the top with rice. Pour gently.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a prediction and a reason. Make your own prediction too. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* If you push the pencil in and pull it up, will the bottle come up too? Why do you think so? I'll say my prediction too.  
  *Child mode:* Will the pencil lift the bottle? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: No, because rice is loose and slippery.; No, the bottle is too heavy for a pencil.; Yes, because the rice will squeeze the pencil.)
  - Note: Any prediction is fine, as long as it has a reason. Do not hint at the answer.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child pushes the pencil straight down, deep into the rice. 2. They hold the bottle's neck with one hand and the pencil with the other. 3. They let go of the bottle and lift the pencil slowly.  
  *Parent says:* Push the pencil in, deep. Now let go of the bottle and lift the pencil slowly. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Push the pencil in deep. Let go of the bottle. Lift slowly.
  - Note: With loose rice the pencil nearly always slides out. That is a real result. Write it down.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a new prediction: how many pokes until it lifts? Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Now we'll poke the pencil in and out, and tap the bottle, to press the rice tight. How many pokes do you think it will take before it lifts? Why?  
  *Child mode:* We will poke and tap. How many pokes until it lifts? Guess a number.
  - Ask: Why that number? (good: Ten, because the rice needs to get tight.; A hundred, because it was very loose.)
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child pokes the pencil in and out, counting aloud. 2. Every 10 pokes, tap the bottle on the tray 5 times. 3. Top up the rice to the very top. 4. After every 10 pokes, leave the pencil in and try a slow lift.  
  *Parent says:* Poke in and out, and count. Every ten, tap the bottle and fill it up again. Then push the pencil in and try lifting.  
  *Child mode:* Poke and count. Every ten, tap it and add rice. Then try to lift.
  - Note: The rice gets lower as it packs, so keep adding rice to the very top. Many bottles lift after 20 to 50 pokes, but it varies. A rough wooden chopstick or ice-cream stick grips better than a shiny painted pencil. If yours has not lifted after 60 pokes, use a narrower bottle or fill it fuller.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When the bottle lifts, write the number of pokes. 2. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* It lifted! How many pokes did it take? Was your prediction right? What do you think made the difference?  
  *Child mode:* How many pokes? Was your guess right? What made it work?
  - Ask: What made it work the second time? (good: The rice was squashed tight round the pencil.; There were no gaps, so the rice held on.; Tapping pushed the rice together.)
  - Note: Listen to your child's reason before you explain anything.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pull the pencil out. Ask for one more prediction. Then push it back in and lift.  
  *Parent says:* Now I'll pull the pencil out. If I push it back in, will it lift straight away, or need many pokes again? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Pencil out, then back in. Will it lift at once? Why?
  - Note: It usually lifts at once, because the rice is still packed tight. If not, it needs only a few pokes.
  - Check: The rice was loose and the pencil slid out. Then you packed it tight and it lifted. What changed? → **How tightly the rice was packed**
- **s8 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now explain, using your child's own words first.  
  *Parent says:* Loose rice has air gaps, so the grains slide. Poking and tapping squeezed them together. Then they pressed hard on the pencil. That grip is called friction. Many grains each gripped a little, and together they held the bottle.  
  *Child mode:* Squashed rice presses on the pencil. That grip is called friction. Many grains together held the bottle.
  - Note: Pour the rice back into the tin afterwards. It is clean and can still be cooked.
### e2 · The Lucky Blow: does blowing on a die bring more sixes? (20 min)
- **question:** Does blowing on a die before rolling it give more sixes than not blowing?
- **predict:** Will you get more sixes when you blow on the die, fewer, or about the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same die, the same hand and the same tray every time. Shake the die in your closed hand the same way and roll it onto the tray. Only one thing changes: blowing on it or not.
- **results table:** Rolls | Sixes with a blow | Sixes without a blow
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw two columns on paper: 'Blow' and 'No blow'. 2. Ask for a prediction and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Some people blow on a die to get a six. Does it work? What do you predict, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Does blowing on the die bring a six? Guess, and say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Yes, because people do it in games.; No, because blowing does not change how the die falls.; Maybe, I am not sure.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child blows on the die in their closed hand, shakes it and rolls it onto the tray. 2. Count 30 rolls. 3. Make a tally mark in 'Blow' for each six.  
  *Parent says:* Blow, shake, roll. Thirty times. Each time it shows six, make a mark in the Blow column.  
  *Child mode:* Blow, shake, roll. Thirty times. Mark each six.
  - Note: Count the rolls on your fingers or with pebbles, so you stop at exactly 30.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do 30 more rolls the same way, without blowing. Mark each six in 'No blow'.  
  *Parent says:* Now 30 more, exactly the same, but no blowing. Mark each six.  
  *Child mode:* Thirty more. Same shake. No blowing. Mark each six.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count both columns. Ask what the counts show.  
  *Parent says:* How many sixes with a blow? How many without? Is the difference big or small? Do you think it would be the same if we did it again?  
  *Child mode:* Count the sixes in each column. Which has more? Is it a big difference?
  - Note: Each count is usually somewhere between 2 and 9, and the two counts are rarely exactly the same. A small difference is just chance. If you have time, do both again: the bigger count often swaps sides.
  - Check: Blowing gave 6 sixes and not blowing gave 4. What does that show? → **The difference is small, so it may be chance. We need to test again**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a claim, the evidence and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* So, does blowing help? What is your evidence? Why do you think the die did that?  
  *Child mode:* Does blowing help? What did your marks show?

  - Note: Nearly every child gets all 10 spoon drops right. Coin guesses are usually somewhere between 3 and 7 right. The Earth pulls the spoon down every time. A coin lands by chance.

### a2 · Turn It Into a Test (12 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read out this question: 'Which paper plane is the best?' Ask if it can be tested.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a question: which paper plane is the best? Can we test that? What does 'best' mean?  
  *Child mode:* Which paper plane is best? Can you test that? What does best mean?
  - Ask: What could 'best' mean? (good: The one that flies furthest.; The one that stays up longest.; The prettiest one.)
  - Note: The prettiest is an opinion. The furthest or the longest in the air can be tested.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child change it into a test question. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make it a test question. Which plane flies further from the same line?  
  *Child mode:* Make it a test. Which plane flies further from the same line?
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold two different planes. 2. Ask for a prediction and a reason. 3. Throw each one 3 times from the same line. 4. Mark where each lands with a slipper.  
  *Parent says:* Which plane will go further? Why? Now throw each one three times from the line. Throw the same way each time.  
  *Child mode:* Which plane will go further? Why? Throw each three times.
  - Note: Three throws each, because one throw can be a fluke.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to change one more question into a test.  
  *Parent says:* Now you try. 'Is my ball the best ball?' How could we turn that into a test?  
  *Child mode:* Is my ball the best ball? Turn it into a test question.
  - Ask: What test question could we ask? (good: Which ball bounces highest?; Which ball rolls furthest down the corridor?)
### a3 · Guess the Grains: predict, count, predict again (12 min)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child scoops one level spoonful of chana. Ask for a prediction and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* How many chana are in this spoon? Make a prediction. Why that number?  
  *Child mode:* How many chana in the spoon? Guess. Why that number?
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tip the spoon onto the plate. Your child counts the chana into groups of ten. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count them in groups of ten. How many groups? How many left over?  
  *Child mode:* Count them in tens. How many?
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a new prediction: how many in two level spoonfuls? Ask for the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Now you know how many are in one spoon. How many will be in two spoons? Why?  
  *Child mode:* How many in two spoons? Use what you counted.
  - Ask: Why that number? (good: One spoon had 25, so two spoons will have about 50.; Double, because it is two spoons.)
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Scoop two level spoonfuls, count them, and compare both predictions with the counts.  
  *Parent says:* Count again. Which prediction was closer, your first one or your second one? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Count again. Which guess was closer? Why?
  - Note: The second prediction is usually much closer, because it was built on a count. Two spoons will not be exactly double, because each spoonful is a little different.

## Challenges
### c1 · Fewest Pokes: make the rice grip faster (15 min)

## Open questions
- **Will we ever be able to predict earthquakes?** Scientists can say which places are more likely to have earthquakes. Nobody can yet predict the day or hour of one.
- **Is there life on planets around other stars?** Scientists have found thousands of planets around other stars. They are looking for signs of life in the light from some of them, but nobody knows yet.
- **Can a test ever show what it feels like to be an ant or a crow?** We can test what ants and crows do, and that tells us they sense, remember and choose. Scientists and philosophers still argue about whether any test can show what their life feels like from the inside.

## Connected maths
- In the Lucky Blow test, Shreya got 6 sixes with a blow and 4 sixes without, in 30 rolls each. How many more sixes did blowing give? Does that prove blowing works? **2 more. No, that does not prove it.**. 6 take away 4 is 2. But a die lands by chance, so counts of sixes change every time. A difference of 2 is small. Shreya needs to test again to see if it was a fluke. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- In the Sure or Not Sure activity, Leela predicted 10 coin shakes and got 5 right. Her brother Dev got 7 right. Is Dev better at predicting coins? **No. Both got about half right, which is what chance gives.**. A coin lands by chance, so over 10 shakes anyone gets about half right, sometimes a few more, sometimes a few fewer. 5 and 7 are both near half of 10. If Dev shakes again, he may get 4. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L2`)
- In Guess the Grains, one spoon of chana had 23 chana. Predict how many are in two spoons. Then the real count was 45. How close was the prediction? **Predict 46. It was 1 more than the real count.**. 23 and 23 make 46. The real count was 45, and 46 take away 45 is 1. So the prediction was only 1 away, because it used the first count. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- In the Rice Bottle Lift, Thara first predicted the bottle would lift after 5 pokes. It took 30. Next time she predicted 25, and it took 20. Which prediction was closer? **The second one, 25.**. From 5 to 30 is 25 pokes away. From 20 to 25 is only 5 pokes away. 5 is less than 25, so her second prediction was much closer. It was closer because she used her first result. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
