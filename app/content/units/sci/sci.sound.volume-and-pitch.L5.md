# Volume and pitch: what makes a sound loud or quiet, high or low
`sci.sound.volume-and-pitch.L5`

**Summary.** Pluck a string harder and it sounds louder, but the note stays the same. Hang a heavier weight on it and the note rises, but the loudness hardly changes. In this unit you will find three ways to change a string's pitch, and test what makes a ringing phone quietest.

**Intro.** *A mosquito near your ear is quiet, and her whine is high. A chenda drum across a temple yard is loud, and its beat is low. If you hit the chenda harder, will its note go higher? If the mosquito comes closer, will her whine go lower?* Both answers are no. Hitting harder makes the drum louder, and its note stays where it was. Coming closer makes the whine louder, and it stays just as high. Loud and high are two different things, and different changes move them. In this unit you build one string on a table and test it with a bottle of water. You measure its loudness in decibels and its note in hertz. Then you wrap a ringing phone in a towel and find what the towel changes and what it leaves alone.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can describe any sound with two measures, its volume and its pitch, and tell them apart.
1. I can link volume to amplitude and pitch to frequency, and name their units, the decibel and the hertz.
2. I can read the decibel scale, and say why sounds above 85 decibels can harm hearing.
3. I can make the pitch of a string higher or lower in three ways, and explain each one.
4. I can explain how air columns and skins change pitch, and how the three families of instruments are played.
5. I can make a sound louder or quieter, and say where the energy comes from or goes.
6. I can plan a fair test, name its variables, and repeat my measurements.
7. I can explain why pitch reaches a far listener unchanged, and volume does not.

## Terms
- **Volume**: How loud or quiet a sound is. Volume depends on how much energy the sound brings to your ear each second. *e.g.* A whisper has a low volume. A pressure cooker whistle has a high volume.
- **Pitch**: How high or low a note sounds. Pitch depends on how many vibrations reach your ear each second. *e.g.* A mosquito's whine has a high pitch. A chenda's beat has a low pitch.
- **Amplitude**: How far a vibrating thing moves from its rest position. A bigger amplitude gives a louder sound. *e.g.* In Gentle Pluck, Hard Pluck, you pull a string aside by half a centimetre, then by 2 centimetres. The second pull has four times the amplitude.
- **Frequency**: The number of vibrations in one second. A higher frequency gives a higher pitch. *e.g.* A string that moves back and forth 220 times each second has a frequency of 220 hertz.
- **Hertz**: The unit of frequency. One hertz is one vibration each second. It is written Hz for short. *e.g.* A tuner app shows the frequency of a plucked string in hertz.
- **Decibel**: The unit of volume. It is written dB for short. Every 10 decibels more means ten times as much sound energy. *e.g.* Talking at one metre is about 60 decibels. A mixie one metre away is often about 85 decibels or more, and it varies by machine.
- **Sound level meter**: A tool that measures volume in decibels. A free app turns a phone into a rough one. *e.g.* In the Muffle Test, a phone with a sound meter app sits 50 centimetres from a ringing phone.
- **Tension**: How hard a string or a skin is pulled tight. More tension gives a higher pitch. *e.g.* In the Water-Bottle String, a bottle of water hangs from the string. More water gives more tension.
- **Air column**: The air inside a tube or a bottle. It vibrates when you blow across it or into it. A shorter air column gives a higher pitch. *e.g.* In the Filling Bottle, the air column above the water gets shorter as the bottle fills.
- **Soundboard**: A wide, thin surface that vibrates with a string and pushes a lot of air. It makes the sound louder without adding energy. *e.g.* The hollow wooden body of a veena is its soundboard. A wooden table does the same for the Water-Bottle String.
- **Muffle**: To make a sound quieter by covering its source, or by putting something in its path. *e.g.* A phone ringing under a pillow is muffled.
- **Absorb**: To take in. A soft, thick material absorbs sound: the vibration gets into it and ends up as a tiny amount of warmth. *e.g.* Thick curtains and cushioned seats absorb sound in a cinema hall.
- **Amplifier**: An electric machine that makes a weak signal from a microphone stronger. It adds energy from a battery or the mains. *e.g.* At a kutcheri, the singer's microphone is joined to an amplifier.
- **Loudspeaker**: A machine that turns an electric signal into sound. The signal pushes a stiff cone back and forth, and the cone pushes the air. *e.g.* A phone has a loudspeaker smaller than your fingernail. A festival stage has ones as tall as a door.
- **String instrument**: An instrument in which tight strings vibrate. The player changes the pitch by changing the length or the tension of a string. *e.g.* The veena, the sitar, the violin and the ektara are string instruments.
- **Wind instrument**: An instrument in which an air column vibrates. The player changes the pitch by making the air column longer or shorter. *e.g.* The bansuri, the nadaswaram and the shehnai are wind instruments.
- **Percussion instrument**: An instrument that is struck, shaken or scraped. In a drum, a tight skin vibrates. *e.g.* The mridangam, the tabla, the kanjira and the ghatam are percussion instruments.
- **Noise**: Sound that someone does not want, or sound loud enough to harm. The same sound can be music to one listener and noise to another. *e.g.* A loudspeaker at midnight is noise to a sleeping baby and to the birds roosting nearby.
- **Infrasound and ultrasound**: Infrasound has a frequency below 20 hertz, too low for humans to hear. Ultrasound is above 20,000 hertz, too high for humans to hear. *e.g.* Elephants call to each other with infrasound. Bats find insects with ultrasound.
- **Independent variable**: The one thing you change on purpose in a fair test. *e.g.* In the Water-Bottle String, the independent variable is the amount of water in the bottle.
- **Dependent variable**: The thing you measure in a fair test. It depends on what you changed. *e.g.* In the Water-Bottle String, the dependent variable is the frequency of the note.
- **Control variables**: The things you keep the same in a fair test, so that only one thing changes. *e.g.* In the Water-Bottle String, the string, its length and the place you pluck stay the same.

## Explanations
### Every sound has a volume and a pitch · *defined*
**Definition:** Volume is how loud or quiet a sound is. Pitch is how high or low it is. The two are separate, and each can change while the other stays the same.

Think of two questions you can ask about any sound. How loud is it? How high is it? A mosquito's whine is quiet and high. A chenda's beat is loud and low. A pressure cooker whistle is loud and high, and a cat's purr is quiet and low. So all four mixes exist. In Four Hums, you hum a low note softly and then strongly. You do the same with a high note. Your throat can change the loudness and leave the note alone. It can also change the note and leave the loudness alone. This tells you that two different things are being changed. The next cards say what they are.
- e.g. A lullaby and a shout can be sung on the same note. Only the volume differs.
- e.g. Sa and the higher Pa can be sung equally softly. Only the pitch differs.
- e.g. On a harmonium, each key gives one pitch. Pumping the bellows harder changes only the volume.
### Volume comes from the amplitude of the vibration · *defined*
**Definition:** A bigger vibration pushes the air harder and gives the sound more energy. More energy reaching your ear each second means a louder sound.

The amplitude of a vibration is how far the thing moves from its rest position. In Gentle Pluck, Hard Pluck, you pull a string aside by half a centimetre, 1 centimetre and 2 centimetres. The bigger pull gives a wider blur and a louder sound. The reason is energy. Your finger does more work to pull the string further, so the string starts with more energy. A string that swings wider pushes the air harder, and the air carries more energy to your ear. The frequency does not change. The string makes bigger trips in the same time, so it must move faster on each trip. As the sound dies away, the blur gets narrower and the note stays the same.
- e.g. Hitting a mridangam harder makes the skin move further, and the beat is louder.
- e.g. Blowing more air through a whistle makes the air inside it vibrate more strongly.
- e.g. Each time the amplitude doubles, the meter reads about 6 decibels more.
### The decibel scale · *defined*
**Definition:** Volume is measured in decibels. Every 10 decibels more means ten times as much sound energy, and it sounds about twice as loud.

The quietest sound a young person can hear is called 0 decibels. Leaves rustling are about 20 decibels, and a whisper is about 30. Talking at one metre is about 60. A mixie at one metre is often 85 or more, and it varies by machine. A horn close by can reach 100 or more. The scale grows by multiplying. A sound of 70 decibels has ten times the energy of a sound of 60. A sound of 80 has a hundred times the energy. Yet 70 decibels sounds only about twice as loud as 60 to most people. This is why two singers are not twice as loud as one. Two equal sounds together read only 3 decibels more than one. Sounds above 85 decibels can harm the hair cells in the inner ear if you hear them for hours. The louder the sound, the shorter the safe time. In India, the Noise Pollution Rules of 2000 set a daytime limit of 55 decibels for places where people live.
- e.g. A sound meter app in a quiet bedroom at night reads about 30 to 40 decibels.
- e.g. Ten people talking at once read about 70 decibels. That is 10 decibels more than one person talking.
- e.g. Earplugs or earmuffs lower the sound that reaches the ear by about 15 to 30 decibels.
### Pitch comes from the frequency of the vibration · *defined*
**Definition:** Frequency is the number of vibrations each second, measured in hertz. A higher frequency gives a higher pitch.

Each vibration gives the air one push. A string that vibrates 220 times a second sends 220 pushes a second to your ear. Your ear and brain do not count them. You hear how often they come as a pitch. A tuner app can count them, and it shows the frequency in hertz. A young person can hear from about 20 hertz up to about 20,000 hertz. A man's speaking voice is about 120 hertz, and a woman's is about 210. Other animals hear different ranges. An elephant hears and makes sounds below 20 hertz, called infrasound. A dog hears up to more than 40,000 hertz, and many bats hear above 100,000. Sound above 20,000 hertz is called ultrasound.
- e.g. The note A that orchestras tune to is 440 hertz.
- e.g. A mosquito's wings beat about 500 times a second, so her whine is about 500 hertz.
- e.g. A field cricket's chirp is about 5,000 hertz, which is why it sounds so thin and high.
### Three ways to change the pitch of a string · *defined*
**Definition:** A string gives a higher pitch when it is tighter, shorter or lighter. It gives a lower pitch when it is looser, longer or heavier.

A tighter string is pulled back to its rest position harder, so each trip takes less time. In the Water-Bottle String, more water in the hanging bottle gives a higher note. But twice the pull does not give twice the frequency. It takes four times the pull to double the frequency, and nine times to make it three times as high. A shorter string has less distance to cover and less of itself to move. Half the length gives twice the frequency. A heavier string has more to move, so each trip takes longer. A string with a second strand wound round it gives a lower note than the string alone. The French scientist Marin Mersenne published these three rules in 1636. A veena player uses all three. The thick strings are for low notes. The pegs change the tension. Pressing a string on a fret makes the vibrating part shorter.
- e.g. A guitar's six strings are the same length. The thick ones are tuned low and the thin ones high.
- e.g. A veena player pulls a string sideways across a fret. This tightens the string and slides the note up.
- e.g. When you sing higher, small muscles stretch the vocal folds in your throat tighter and thinner.
### How air columns and skins change pitch · *defined*
**Definition:** A shorter air column gives a higher pitch. A tighter, smaller or lighter skin gives a higher pitch.

In a wind instrument, the air inside the tube vibrates. A short air column vibrates faster than a long one. In the Filling Bottle, the note of the pouring water rises as the air column above the water gets shorter. A bansuri player changes the length of the air column with her fingers. Opening a hole lets the air column end at that hole. The column is shorter, so the note is higher. In a drum, a tight skin vibrates. A tighter skin is pulled back harder and gives a higher pitch. A tabla player taps wooden blocks under the straps with a small hammer to tighten the skin. A bigger skin has more to move and gives a lower pitch. A skin that is damp is looser, so its pitch drops. A kanjira player sprinkles water on the skin to lower the note.
- e.g. A long bansuri plays lower notes than a short one.
- e.g. The two ends of a mridangam are different sizes. The bigger skin gives the lower sound.
- e.g. In hot, dry air a drum skin tightens and its pitch creeps up during a concert.
### How to make a sound louder · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound gets louder in four ways: a bigger vibration, more air pushed, a sound sent in one direction, or energy added by an amplifier.

There are four ways. First, give the vibration more energy: pluck harder, hit harder or blow harder. Second, push more air with the same vibration. A thin string cuts through the air and moves very little of it. Rest the string on a soundboard, such as a wooden box, and the wide board pushes far more air. The board adds no energy. It passes the string's energy to the air faster. Third, send the sound one way. A cone or cupped hands stop the sound spreading out on every side. Fourth, add energy from outside. A microphone turns the sound into a weak electric signal. An amplifier makes the signal stronger, using energy from a battery or the mains. A loudspeaker turns the strong signal back into sound. Only this fourth way puts more energy into the sound than the player gave it.
- e.g. A veena's hollow body is its soundboard. Without it the strings could hardly be heard.
- e.g. A nadaswaram ends in a wide bell that sends its sound out into the open air.
- e.g. A phone lying in an empty steel tumbler sounds louder, because the tumbler sends the sound one way.
### How to make a sound quieter, and what stays the same · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound gets quieter when its energy is absorbed, blocked or spread over more space. Its frequency stays the same all the way.

In the Muffle Test, a phone plays a tune under different covers. A thick towel absorbs sound. The vibration gets in among the threads, rubs against them and ends as a tiny amount of warmth. More layers absorb more. A closed steel dabba blocks sound, because its heavy wall is hard for the air to shake. But a gap one finger wide lets a great deal of sound out. This is why a door left a little open hardly keeps noise out. Distance makes a sound quieter too, because the same energy spreads over more space. Through all of this, the tune stays the same tune. Covers and distance take energy from the sound. They do not change how often the pushes arrive, because every push that leaves the phone still arrives, only weaker. Walls and air do weaken high notes more than low notes. That is why far thunder is a low rumble. The notes that get through still have the frequency they started with. Only a source that is moving towards you or away from you changes the pitch you hear.
- e.g. A song from the next flat is faint, but you can still sing along in tune with it.
- e.g. Thick curtains, carpets and cushioned seats absorb sound in a cinema hall.
- e.g. Earmuffs for airport workers have a hard shell to block sound and soft foam to absorb it.
### How instruments use both measures · *defined*
**Definition:** Every instrument has a way to set the pitch of each note. It also has a way to change the volume.

Instruments fall into three main families. In string instruments, strings vibrate. The player sets the pitch with the length and the tension of a string. The strength of the pluck or the bow sets the volume. In wind instruments, an air column vibrates. The player sets the pitch with the length of the column, and the volume with the breath. In percussion instruments, a skin, a pot or a bar is struck. Its pitch is mostly set before the concert, by its size and its tension. The player changes the volume with the strength of each stroke. Some instruments can slide smoothly from one pitch to the next, and some cannot. A veena or a violin can slide, because a finger can stop the string anywhere. A harmonium cannot, because each key has one metal reed with one fixed pitch.
- e.g. A veena player presses a string against a fret to change the pitch. She plucks the string harder to change the volume.
- e.g. A bansuri player opens or covers the finger holes to change the pitch. She blows more air to change the volume.
- e.g. Before the concert, a mridangam player tightens the straps to set the pitch. During the concert, a harder stroke gives a louder sound.
### Is loudness in the air or in the listener? · *debated*
**Definition:** The strength of a sound can be measured in the air. Loudness is what a listener hears. Scientists describe the link between the two in different ways.

A sound meter measures the strength of a sound in the air. Loudness is what a listener hears. The two do not always agree. In 1933, Harvey Fletcher and Wilden Munson asked listeners to match the loudness of tones of different pitch. A low tone of 100 hertz needed far more energy than a 1,000 hertz tone to sound equally loud. Human ears are most sensitive at about 3,000 hertz. So the same reading on a meter can be loud at one pitch and faint at another. Other animals differ again. A sound of 30,000 hertz can be strong in the air, loud to a dog and silent to you.
- e.g. Many alarms beep near 3,000 hertz, where human ears are most sensitive.
- e.g. A dog whistle can read high on a meter that picks up ultrasound, and a human hears nothing.
- e.g. Sound meters often use a setting called dB(A), which counts low sounds less, as human ears do.
- **A physicist who measures sound:** The strength of a sound is a fact about the air. It can be measured in decibels with no listener in the room. To a physicist, loudness is simply that strength, and a meter gives it as closely as we can measure.
- **A scientist who studies hearing:** Loudness is made by an ear and a brain. It depends on the pitch, on the listener's age and on what kind of animal the listener is. The air only has strength. Loudness begins when someone hears.
- **A person who is deaf:** A strong low sound can be felt through the floor and in the chest with no hearing at all. So the strength of a sound reaches a body in more than one way. Hearing is only one of them.

## Mostly agreed
- Volume depends on the amplitude of the vibration, and pitch depends on its frequency. The two can be changed separately. *How we know:* In Gentle Pluck, Hard Pluck, a bigger pull on the same string gives a higher reading in decibels. The reading in hertz stays the same. Microphones and sound meters show the same for every instrument.
- The frequency of a string doubles when its length is halved. It also doubles when its tension is made four times as great. *How we know:* Vincenzo Galilei, a lute player in Italy, tested strings with hanging weights in about 1589. Marin Mersenne measured and published the full rules in 1636. The Water-Bottle String repeats the test.
- Every 10 decibels more is ten times the sound energy, and it sounds about twice as loud to most listeners. *How we know:* The decibel is defined in this way. In the 1930s the American scientist Stanley Stevens asked many listeners to judge when a sound was twice as loud.
- Long exposure to sound above about 85 decibels harms hearing, and the harm does not heal. *How we know:* Studies of workers in noisy factories, over many years, show hearing loss that grows with the loudness and the hours. Under a microscope, the hair cells of the inner ear are seen to be damaged. In humans they do not grow back.
- Sound keeps its frequency as it travels. The exception is a source or a listener moving towards or away from the other. *How we know:* A tuner reads the same frequency near a steady source, far from it, and through a wall or a towel. In the Muffle Test, the covers lower the reading in decibels, and the tune does not change.

## How we found out
**Harvey Fletcher and Wilden Munson, American scientists at Bell Telephone Laboratories, They published their results in 1933. Other teams measured again in 1956 and in 2003..** By the 1920s, telephone engineers in America could measure the strength of a sound with instruments. It seemed natural that strength and loudness were the same thing. Two sounds of the same strength should then sound equally loud. Harvey Fletcher and Wilden Munson, at Bell Telephone Laboratories in New York, tested this. Listeners wore earphones and heard two tones in turn. One tone was always 1,000 hertz. The other was lower or higher. Its strength was changed until the listener judged both tones equally loud. Then the scientists compared the two strengths. The idea failed. A quiet tone of 100 hertz needed many times the energy of the 1,000 hertz tone. Only then did it seem as loud. Human ears were most sensitive at about 3,000 to 4,000 hertz. Fletcher and Munson published their curves in 1933. Later teams measured again with more listeners, and the curves changed a little. The main finding held. Loudness depends on pitch as well as on strength. Sound meters today count low sounds less, as human ears do.  
*What people believed before:* That two sounds of the same strength always sound equally loud. Fletcher and Munson showed that a low tone needs far more energy than a middle tone to sound as loud. Human ears are less sensitive to low sounds.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Hitting a drum harder makes its note higher.** (A common belief, and many children's first guess.; *rejected*)  
  For: A hard stroke does sound sharper and brighter than a soft one. A very hard pluck also pulls a string a little tighter for a moment, so its note starts slightly high.  
  Against: A tuner shows nearly the same frequency for a soft stroke and a hard one. In Gentle Pluck, Hard Pluck, a pull four times as big gives a louder sound at the same frequency. The brighter sound comes from extra high vibrations mixed in, and the main note stays put.
- **Two singers are twice as loud as one.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Two singers do send out twice the sound energy of one singer.  
  Against: A sound meter reads only 3 decibels more for two equal voices than for one. To most listeners, a sound is twice as loud when it is 10 decibels more. That takes about ten equal voices.
- **'High' and 'low' are the natural words for pitch.** (Speakers of English, and of many Indian and European languages.; *accepted*)  
  For: The words feel natural. Many people raise their chin and eyebrows for a high note. Written music puts high notes higher on the page.  
  Against: Nothing in a high note is higher up. It has a higher frequency. Speakers of Farsi and Turkish call the same notes 'thin' and 'thick'. The ancient Greeks called them 'sharp' and 'heavy'. In 2013, Sarah Dolscheid and her team tested speakers of Farsi and of Dutch. Each group remembered pitches in the way their own words describe them.
- **Bigger animals always have lower voices.** (A rule that works most of the time.; *debated*)  
  For: A bigger animal usually has longer, heavier vocal folds, and these vibrate more slowly. An elephant rumbles far lower than a mouse squeaks.  
  Against: There are exceptions. A male koala bellows about as low as an elephant, though he weighs about as much as a small dog. In 2013, Benjamin Charlton and his team found a second, much larger pair of folds higher in his throat. So the size of the vibrating part matters, and it does not always match the size of the body.
- **A sound meter tells you how loud a sound is.** (Makers of sound meter apps, and most people who use them.; *accepted*)  
  For: A meter gives one number, in decibels, that anyone can check. Laws about noise need a number of this kind.  
  Against: A meter measures the strength of the sound in the air. Loudness also depends on the pitch and on the listener. A low hum and a high beep with the same reading do not sound equally loud. A phone's microphone is also rough, and two phones can differ by several decibels.

## Roots
In this unit you changed the volume and the pitch of a string, and you muffled a ringing phone. Here we ask which part of a sound you can trust when it reaches you from far away.

**Deep truth.** In the Muffle Test, four layers of towel made the phone's tune much quieter. Every note of the tune stayed at its own pitch. Towels, walls and distance take away a sound's energy and leave its frequency alone. So the pitch you hear is the same pitch the source made, however far the sound has travelled. That is how you can tell what made a sound from a long way off.  
*Habit it breaks:* We usually treat loudness and pitch as two equal facts about a sound. In one way they are not equal. Loudness changes on the way to you, because distance and walls make a sound weaker. Pitch is set once, at the source, and it arrives just as it left.  
*Evidence:* In the Muffle Test, the sound meter read lower under every cover. The tune could still be hummed along with. In Gentle Pluck, Hard Pluck, the note stayed the same while the sound faded to nothing. A tuner reads the same number of hertz near a steady string and far from it. The one exception is a source that moves towards the listener or away.

- *who am i:* Your family can tell it is you speaking from another room, even when your voice is faint. The loudness has been lost on the way. But the pitches that make up your voice arrive just as you made them, and that is how they know it is you.
- *what is life:* A female cricket or frog finds a male of her own kind by the frequency of his call. He may be far away and faint among a hundred other calls. Many living beings trust pitch in this way.
- *what is this world:* Light keeps its frequency as it travels too. Astronomers read what a star is made of from frequencies of light that left it thousands of years ago. If the star is moving away, the frequencies arrive a little lower, and that tells them its speed.

The Water-Bottle String was one string. With three amounts of water and three lengths, it gave many notes. Nothing was added to the string. Only the way it was held and pulled had changed. Many sages taught that the many things of the world come from one source in a similar way. That is a teaching, and a string cannot prove it. What the string shows is smaller and certain. One string can give many notes. Each note belongs to the string together with the way it is held.

- **Anguttara Nikaya 6.55, the Sona Sutta (the Buddha's talk with the monk Sona):** “Taṁ kiṁ maññasi, soṇa, yadā te vīṇāya tantiyo accāyatā honti, api nu te vīṇā tasmiṁ samaye saravatī vā hoti kammaññā vā? / Taṁ kiṁ maññasi, soṇa, yadā te vīṇāya tantiyo atisithilā honti, api nu te vīṇā tasmiṁ samaye saravatī vā hoti kammaññā vā? / Yadā pana te, soṇa, vīṇāya tantiyo na accāyatā honti nātisithilā same guṇe patiṭṭhitā, api nu te vīṇā tasmiṁ samaye saravatī vā hoti kammaññā vā? / Evamevaṁ kho, soṇa, accāraddhavīriyaṁ uddhaccāya saṁvattati, atisithilavīriyaṁ kosajjāya saṁvattati.” Sona had been a veena player. The Buddha asked him about his strings. When they were too tight, the veena could not be played well. When they were too loose, it could not be played well either. It played well when the strings were tuned to the right pitch between the two. The Buddha told Sona to tune his own effort in the same way. (The talk rests on the science of this unit: the tension of a string sets its pitch. The Buddha used a fact that every player knew to say something about effort. The fact about strings can be tested. The advice about effort is a teaching.)
- **Bharata, Natya Shastra, chapter 28 (India, perhaps 2,000 years ago; its date is not certain):** The book describes a test with two veenas tuned exactly alike. One is left as it is. On the other, the strings are loosened a little at a time. The two are compared by ear at each step. In this way a listener learns the smallest steps of pitch, which the book calls shrutis. (This is a fair test. One veena is the control, and one thing is changed on the other: the tension. In the Water-Bottle String you did the same. Water took the place of a peg, and a tuner took the place of a trained ear.)
- **Aristotle, Metaphysics, Book 1, chapter 5, on the followers of Pythagoras (Greece, about 2,350 years ago):** “ἔτι δὲ τῶν ἁρμονιῶν ἐν ἀριθμοῖς ὁρῶντες τὰ πάθη καὶ τοὺς λόγους / τὰ τῶν ἀριθμῶν στοιχεῖα τῶν ὄντων στοιχεῖα πάντων ὑπέλαβον εἶναι, καὶ τὸν ὅλον οὐρανὸν ἁρμονίαν εἶναι καὶ ἀριθμόν” Aristotle reports that the followers of Pythagoras found the notes of a scale could be written as numbers. From this they came to think that numbers were the makings of all things. They thought the whole sky was a scale. (Pitch really can be written as a number, the frequency, and you measured it in hertz. The step from 'notes are numbers' to 'everything is number' was a bold guess, and Aristotle himself doubted it. Physics still writes the world in numbers, and nobody is sure why that works so well.)
- Science (settled): When a wave passes from one material into another, its frequency stays the same. Its speed changes, and so does the distance between one push and the next.
- Science (settled): The frequency that arrives changes only if the source or the listener is moving towards or away from the other. This is called the Doppler effect. Light from far galaxies arrives at lower frequencies because space itself has stretched on the way.
- Science (open): Pitch as you hear it is built by your ear and brain. It uses the place and the timing of signals in the inner ear. How it does this is still being worked out.
- Other view, Many scientists who study hearing: Pitch is not the same thing as frequency. Pitch is something a brain makes. An old telephone line carries no frequencies below about 300 hertz. A man's voice at 120 hertz still sounds low on it. The brain rebuilds the low pitch from the higher frequencies that do arrive. So the pitch you hear can be a pitch that never reached your ear. On this view, pitch tells you about your own brain as much as about the source.
- Practice, The far sound (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Listen past the sounds in this room. Find the farthest steady sound you can: a train, a temple bell, a koel, a pressure cooker in another flat. It is very quiet by the time it reaches you. Now hum its note, as softly as you can. The loudness you hear has been lowered by the long trip. The note you are humming is exactly the note that was made over there.
- Ask: A song comes faintly through a wall. Which part of it is still exactly as the singer made it, and which part is not?
- Ask: A sound meter and your ears can disagree about how loud a sound is. Which one would you trust, and for what?
- Ask: One string gave many notes in the Water-Bottle String. Does the note belong to the string, to the bottle, or to neither alone?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the Muffle Test, a towel makes the alarm quieter, and the tune stays the same. Where does the missing loudness go? (hint: Sound is energy, and energy does not vanish. Think about what the threads of the towel are doing as the sound passes.)
- *deep:* A string gives no clear note until it is pulled tight. Is the note already in the string before you pluck it? (hint: In the Water-Bottle String, one string gave many notes. Ask what decides the note: the string, the bottle, the pencils, or all of them together.)
- *deep:* A dog hears a 30,000 hertz whistle clearly, and you hear nothing. Is that whistle loud? (hint: A meter that picks up ultrasound gives it a number in decibels. Decide whether 'loud' needs a listener, and which listener.)
- *big:* One family's festival music is another family's sleepless night. Who should decide how loud is too loud? (hint: Think of everyone who hears it: a baby, a person who is ill, a night-shift worker, the birds in the tree. Then think about what a rule in decibels can and cannot settle.)
- *big:* Light has a frequency too, and we see it as colour. Is pitch the colour of sound? (hint: Red light has a lower frequency than violet light. Think about what is alike and what is different. Can you mix pitches the way you mix colours?)
- *big:* A recording lets you hear a singer who died long ago, at the pitch she sang. What exactly has been kept? (hint: The air she moved is long gone. Think about what the recording stores, and what it has to rebuild each time it plays.)

## Story: The Kanjira That Climbed
*Yazhini, Nila, Kathir and Sumathi Akka are made up, and so is the hall. 'Akka' is a Tamil word for elder sister, and pupils often use it for a young teacher. These things are real. A kanjira is a small frame drum of South India. It is a ring of jackfruit wood about 18 to 23 centimetres wide, with a skin glued across one side. A few small metal discs are set in a slot in the ring. The skin was once the skin of a monitor lizard. Monitor lizards are now protected by law in India, and most makers use goat skin. A kanjira's pitch rises as its skin dries under hot lamps. Players really do wet the inside of the skin with a few drops of water to bring the pitch down.*

The hall in Madurai had a tin roof, and by six in the evening it was as hot as a dosa pan. Four stage lamps hung low over the mat where the musicians sat.

Yazhini was ten. She held her kanjira on her knee and tried not to look at the two hundred people on the folding chairs. A kanjira is a small drum, a ring of jackwood about as wide as a small plate, with a goat skin glued across one side. This was her first concert without her teacher beside her. Sumathi Akka's bus was stuck behind a temple procession, and she had phoned to say, "Start without me. You know everything you need."

Yazhini was not sure that she did.

In front of her sat Nila, who was sixteen and would sing. To her left sat Kathir, who was twelve, with his violin. Beside Yazhini's knee stood the small brass bowl of water that Sumathi Akka put next to every kanjira player. Yazhini had filled it because that was the rule. Nobody had ever told her what it was for. She had always supposed it was for thirsty drummers.

The first song went well. The kanjira's deep dhum sat just under Nila's voice, the way a floor sits under a dancer.

In the second song something went wrong. The dhum was not so deep any more. It had crept up, a little with every line. By the third song it had climbed so far that it sounded like a steel spoon on a tumbler. It went ting where it should have gone dhum, and it quarrelled with Nila's note. Nila glanced back. A man in the front row frowned.

Kathir leaned over. "You are too high," he whispered. "Play softer."

Yazhini played softer. Now the drum was quiet and too high.

"Harder, then," hissed Kathir.

She struck harder. Now the drum was loud and too high. People in the third row could hear exactly how wrong it was.

"It is broken," Kathir whispered. "The skin has gone bad. Just tap the wood until the end."

Yazhini's ears burned. But a thought had caught hold of her, and she held on to it. Soft or hard, the note had not moved at all. Her hand could make the drum louder. Her hand could make it quieter. Her hand could not make it lower. So whatever had pushed the note up, it was not her playing. It was something about the skin.

She pressed the skin with her thumb. That morning it had given a little, like the seat of a cane chair. Now it was as hard as the wooden rim. She looked up at the lamps. They had been shining on the drum for forty minutes. The skin was as dry as an appalam, and it had pulled itself tight.

Tight. She looked at Kathir's violin. Before the concert he had turned a peg, and the string had stretched tighter, and its note had gone up. Tighter, higher. Was it the same for a skin as for a string? If a tight skin sang high, then a looser skin would sing low. And what loosens a dry skin?

She looked down at the brass bowl. It was not for thirsty drummers. It was for thirsty drums.

Between two lines of the song, she dipped two fingers into the bowl. She rubbed the water over the inside of the skin, where the audience could not see. Then she tried one stroke.

Ting. Nothing had changed. Her stomach turned over.

She kept the beat on the wooden rim and counted ten slow breaths. Then she tried again.

Dhum. It was not all the way down, but it was lower. She wet her fingers once more, and only once. A soaked skin goes slack and dull, like wet newspaper. She waited ten more breaths.

Dhum. The note slid back under Nila's voice and stayed there. Nila did not turn round, but her shoulders came down, and the next line of the song had a smile in it.

Kathir stared at the drum. "How did you mend it?"

"It was never broken," Yazhini whispered. "It was dry. The lamps dried the skin and pulled it tight, and a tight skin sings high. I loosened it."

"With water?"

"With water."

When the last song ended, Sumathi Akka was standing at the back of the hall, dusty from the bus. She had heard the last two songs. She pointed at the brass bowl and raised her eyebrows. Yazhini held up two wet fingers.

Sumathi Akka laughed out loud. "I could have told you what the bowl is for," she said afterwards. "But now you will never forget it. A few drops every twenty minutes under those lamps. Never more."

Kathir looked at his violin. Then he looked at the bowl.

"Do not even think about it," said Yazhini. "You have pegs."

**For the parent:** For the parent: the pitch of a drum skin depends on how tight the skin is. The hot lamps dried the skin and tightened it, so the pitch rose. A few drops of water loosened it, and the pitch came down. Before Yazhini could work this out, she had to notice something else. Playing softer and playing harder changed only the volume. That told her the fault was in the pitch, and pitch is not set by the strength of the stroke.

- Ask: Yazhini played softer and then harder. Why did neither bring the note down?
- Ask: The lamps touched nothing on the drum. How did they raise its note?
- Ask: A violin string is tightened with a peg. What does a kanjira player use to loosen a skin, and why does it work?
- Ask: What do you think would have happened if Yazhini had poured the whole bowl of water on the skin?

### e1 · The Water-Bottle String: does twice the pull give twice the frequency? (40 min)
- **question:** How does the pull on a string change its pitch? Does twice the pull give twice the frequency? What do a shorter string and a thicker string do?

  *Parent does:* 1. Open the tuner app in place of the sound meter. 2. Pull the string to the half-centimetre mark, let go and read the frequency. 3. Do the same for 1 cm and 2 cm. 4. After the 2 cm pull, keep watching the number as the sound dies away.  
  *Parent says:* Now the tuner. Same three pulls. Read me the hertz each time. After the biggest pull, keep watching as the sound fades. Does the number fall?  
  *Child mode:* Now use the tuner. Do the same three pulls. Read the hertz. Watch the number as the sound fades.
  - Note: The three readings should be nearly the same, within a few hertz. The biggest pull may read a little high for the first instant, because the pull stretches the string tighter. As the sound fades, the number stays steady until the app loses the sound.
  - Check: In Gentle Pluck, Hard Pluck, the pull on the string goes from half a centimetre to 2 centimetres. What changes? → **Volume goes up. Pitch stays the same.**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they measured, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you measured, and why you think it happened. What does a harder pluck change?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you measured. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: As the sound faded, the string's wiggle got smaller. What happened to the note? (good: It stayed the same.; It got quieter, and it did not get lower.)
  - Note: Your finger does more work to pull the string further. So the string starts with more energy and a bigger amplitude. It pushes the air harder, and the sound is louder. The frequency does not change. The string makes wider trips in the same time, by moving faster. So volume comes from amplitude, and pitch comes from frequency. As the sound dies, the string loses amplitude and keeps its frequency.
  - Think: A singer holds one note and lets it fade away. What is her voice box changing, and what is it keeping the same?
### e3 · The Muffle Test: what makes a ringing phone quietest? (30 min)
- **question:** Which cover makes a ringing phone quietest: one layer of towel, four layers, or a closed steel dabba? What does a small gap do? Does any cover change the pitch of the tune?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put the five covers in order, from the loudest reading to the quietest. Will any cover make the tune sound lower or higher? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only the cover. That is the independent variable. Use the same phone, the same tune from its start, and the same volume setting, at about half. Keep both phones on the same marks, 50 centimetres apart. Measure the highest reading in decibels in 10 seconds. That is the dependent variable. Test each cover twice. Keep the room as quiet as you can for every test.
- **record:** Draw a table with five rows. The first three are for no cover, one layer of towel and four layers of towel. The last two are for the closed dabba, and the dabba with its lid open by one finger-width. Write two readings in decibels for each. In the last column write yes or no: did the tune keep its pitch?
- **results table:** Cover | Test 1 (decibels) | Test 2 (decibels) | Did the tune keep its pitch?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold one towel and lay it on the table. Put the first phone on it. 2. Choose an alarm tune or a song and set the volume to about half. 3. Put the second phone on a book 50 cm away and open the sound meter. 4. Mark both places with tape. 5. Play the tune for 10 seconds with no cover. Write the highest reading. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* This phone will play the same tune every time, from the start. The other phone will measure it. First we measure it with no cover at all.  
  *Child mode:* Put the phone on a folded towel. Play the tune. Read the highest number on the other phone.
  - Note: The folded towel under the phone stops the table acting as a soundboard. Half volume is enough. Nobody needs to put an ear near the phone. Without a sound meter app, walk away from the phone until you cannot hear the tune, and count your steps.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the covers: one towel, the same towel folded into four layers, and the steel dabba. 2. Say that the dabba will be tested closed, and then with its lid open by one finger-width. 3. Ask for an order, from loudest to quietest, and a reason. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Here are the covers. Put them in order, from the loudest reading to the quietest. Will any of them change the tune itself? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the covers. Which will be loudest? Which will be quietest? Will the tune change?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: More layers should soak up more sound.; Steel is hard, so sound might bounce around inside and come out.; The gap will let sound out.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. The order of the towel and the dabba is not obvious, and homes differ.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the second towel over the phone in one layer. Play the tune for 10 seconds and write the highest reading. Do it twice. 2. Fold that towel into four layers and cover the phone again. Measure twice. 3. Each time, listen to the tune. Hum along with it.  
  *Parent says:* One layer first, then four. Watch the meter. Then listen to the tune itself. Can you still hum along? Is it the same tune, at the same pitch?  
  *Child mode:* Cover the phone with one layer. Read the number. Now fold the towel into four. Read again. Hum along.
  - Note: Expect a small drop for one layer and a bigger drop for four. The tune sounds duller, because cloth takes more from high notes than from low notes. Every note that gets through is still at its own pitch, so your child can hum along in tune.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the towel off. Put the phone inside the dabba, on a small folded cloth, and close the lid. Measure twice. 2. Lift one side of the lid by one finger-width and rest it there. Measure twice. 3. Listen to the tune each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the steel dabba, closed. Read the meter. Now I open the lid by the width of one finger. Guess first. Will the reading change a little or a lot?  
  *Child mode:* Put the phone in the dabba. Close the lid. Read the number. Open the lid a little. Read again.
  - Note: A closed dabba usually gives a low reading, because its heavy wall is hard for the air to shake. A narrow gap lets a lot of the sound back out. If your dabba gives a higher reading than the towels, write that down. A thin or rattling lid can pass sound on.
  - Check: In the Muffle Test, the lid of the steel dabba is opened by one finger-width. What happens to the reading? → **It goes up a lot**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the phone out and play the tune with no cover. 2. Your child walks to the next room, or as far away as the home allows, and listens. 3. They hum the tune there, then walk back still humming.  
  *Parent says:* Walk to the next room and listen. Is the tune quieter? Is it still the same tune? Hum it there, and keep humming as you walk back.  
  *Child mode:* Walk to the next room. Is the tune quieter? Hum it. Walk back, still humming. Does your hum still fit?
  - Note: The tune is fainter in the next room, and the hum still fits when your child comes back. Distance and walls lower the volume. They do not change the frequency of any note.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they measured, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you measured, and why you think it happened. Which cover was best? What did no cover change?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you measured. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: What did every cover change, and what did no cover change? (good: Every cover made the tune quieter.; No cover changed the pitch of the tune.)
  - Ask: Where did the missing sound energy go? (good: Into the towel, as a tiny amount of warmth.; Some bounced back inside the dabba.)
  - Note: A thick towel absorbs sound. The vibration gets in among the threads, rubs against them, and ends as a tiny amount of warmth. More layers absorb more. A steel wall blocks sound, because it is heavy and hard to shake, but any gap lets sound through. None of this changes the frequency. Every push that leaves the phone still arrives, only weaker, so the pushes arrive just as often. This is why you know a song through a wall.
  - Think: A friend calls your name from far away. What tells you who it is: the loudness of the call, or something else?

## Activities
### a1 · Four Hums: can you change loudness and pitch separately? (10 min)
- **record:** Fold a sheet of paper into four boxes: quiet and low, loud and low, quiet and high, loud and high. Write two sounds from your home in each box.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rests one hand lightly on the front of their throat. 2. They hum a low note softly, then the same note strongly. 3. They hum a high note softly, then the same note strongly. A strong hum is enough. Nobody needs to shout.  
  *Parent says:* Hum a low note softly. Now the same note, stronger. Now a high note softly, and then stronger. What do you notice in your throat? What do you wonder?  
  *Child mode:* Put a hand on your throat. Hum low and soft. Then low and strong. Then high and soft. Then high and strong.
  - Ask: What changed when the hum got stronger? What changed when it got higher? (good: The buzz under my hand got bigger when it was stronger.; Something in my throat tightened or moved up when it was higher.; I could change one without the other.)
  - Note: Do not explain yet. Write down what your child notices and wonders. Most children feel a bigger buzz for the strong hum and a tightening or a lift for the high hum.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold a sheet of paper into four boxes. 2. Label them: quiet and low, loud and low, quiet and high, loud and high. 3. Together, think of eight sounds in your home and street. 4. Your child says which box each one goes in. Write each name there, or let your child write it.  
  *Parent says:* Here are four boxes. Where does the fridge's hum go? The doorbell? A crow? A spoon dropped on the floor? Find two sounds for every box.  
  *Child mode:* Think of eight sounds at home. Say which box each one fits in.
  - Note: There is no single right box for some sounds, and the talk matters more than the answer. A fridge is quiet and low. A mixie is loud. A sparrow is quiet and high. If a box stays empty, listen at the window for a minute.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the question below. 2. Take any answer, and write it down to look at again later.  
  *Parent says:* You made a hum louder without making it higher. You made it higher without making it louder. What do you think about that?  
  *Child mode:* Think about your four hums. You changed one thing at a time.
  - Ask: Are loud and high the same thing? (good: No. I can change one and keep the other.; Loud is how strong it is. High is something else, maybe how fast it shakes.)
  - Note: Any reasoned answer is good. The experiments that follow measure the two things separately.
### a2 · The Sound Grid: eight home sounds, two numbers (20 min)
- **safety:** Measure every sound from at least 1 metre away, and the mixie from 2 metres. Run the mixie for only 5 seconds. Do not put an ear or the phone close to any loud sound.
- **record:** Make a table with four columns: the sound, the reading in decibels, the pitch (low, middle or high), and a guess of what is vibrating. Then draw the grid and put a dot with a name for each sound.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose eight sounds in your home, some quiet and some loud. 2. Your child guesses which will be loudest and which quietest, in decibels. 3. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* We will measure eight sounds, each from 1 metre away. Which do you think will give the biggest number? Which the smallest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Pick eight sounds at home. Guess which is loudest and which is quietest. Say why.
  - Note: Good quiet choices are a whisper, the fridge, a fan and a running tap. Louder ones are talking, a spoon on a tumbler, the doorbell and the mixie. Leave out any sound that hurts.
- **s2 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure 1 metre from the first sound. 2. Hold the phone there and read the highest number in 5 seconds. 3. Your child says the pitch by ear: low, middle or high. 4. Write both in the table. 5. Do the same for all eight. A grown-up runs the mixie for 5 seconds, and you measure it from 2 metres.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the phone at the 1 metre mark. What is the number? Now listen to the sound itself. Is its pitch low, middle or high?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the phone 1 metre away. Read the number. Is the pitch low, middle or high? Write both.
  - Note: Phone meters are rough, and two phones can differ by several decibels. The order of the sounds is what matters. Some sounds, such as a fan, have no clear pitch. Write 'low' if the sound is mostly a deep hum. Write 'high' if it is mostly a hiss or a ring. Without an app, score each sound from 1 for very quiet to 5 for very loud.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a grid. Write low, middle and high along the bottom. 2. Mark decibels up the side, from 20 to 100 in steps of 10. 3. Draw a red line across at 85. 4. Your child puts a dot and a name on the grid for each sound.  
  *Parent says:* Each sound gets one dot. Its place along the bottom shows its pitch, and its height shows its volume. The red line is 85 decibels, where long, loud sound can hurt ears. Is any dot above it?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the grid, or use a sheet of squared paper. Put a dot for each sound. Pitch goes along, and decibels go up.
  - Note: Sounds above 85 decibels can harm hearing over hours. A mixie is near the line, and it runs for only a minute or two at a time.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the finished grid together. 2. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your grid. What does it tell you about volume and pitch?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your dots. Where are the loud sounds? Where are the quiet ones?
  - Ask: Are the loud sounds all high-pitched? (good: No. The dots are spread over the whole grid.; A sound can be loud and low, or quiet and high.)
  - Ask: One sound reads 10 decibels more than another. How much more energy does it carry? (good: Ten times as much.)
  - Note: The dots spread over the grid because volume and pitch are separate measures. Knowing one tells you nothing about the other.
### a3 · The Filling Bottle: hear an air column get shorter (15 min)
- **record:** Make a table with three rows: nearly empty, half full and nearly full. In each row write the length of the air column in centimetres, and write 'lowest', 'middle' or 'highest' for the note. You can also take a photo of each bottle.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the bottle in the sink or the bucket. 2. Pour water in from the jug in a thin, steady stream, until the bottle is full. 3. Your child listens with eyes open. 4. Empty the bottle.  
  *Parent says:* Listen to the sound the water makes inside the bottle while I pour. What do you notice? What do you wonder?  
  *Child mode:* Listen closely while the water pours in.
  - Ask: What happened to the sound as the bottle filled? (good: It got higher and higher.; It rose fastest near the end.)
  - Note: The sound rises in pitch as the bottle fills. Keep the stream thin and steady, and aim it at the middle, not down the side. Do not explain yet.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shuts their eyes. 2. Pour again in the same steady stream. 3. Your child says 'stop' when they think the bottle is three-quarters full. 4. Measure the height of the water and the height of the bottle with the ruler. 5. Swap places and try again.  
  *Parent says:* Now shut your eyes. Say stop when you think the bottle is three-quarters full. Use only your ears. How did you know?  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Listen to the pouring. Say stop when the bottle is three-quarters full.
  - Note: Most people get close on the second or third try. You do this every time you fill a water bottle at the tap without looking.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the bottle a quarter full. Measure the air column, from the water up to the mouth. 2. Do the same half full and three-quarters full. 3. Ask the question below before you explain. 4. Then read the note to your child.  
  *Parent says:* The water is not what sings. Something else in the bottle is getting shorter as the water rises. What is it? What does a shorter one do to the note?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the air above the water at three levels.
  - Ask: What is getting shorter as the bottle fills? (good: The air above the water.; The air column.)
  - Note: The falling water sets the air column in the bottle vibrating. As the bottle fills, the air column gets shorter. A shorter air column vibrates faster, so the note rises. A bansuri works in the same way: opening a hole makes the air column shorter.
  - Think: A short bansuri and a long bansuri are played with every hole covered. Which gives the lower note?
### a4 · The Rubber Band Puzzle: three changes at once (15 min)
- **safety:** A stretched rubber band can snap. Hold it away from faces, and do not stretch it past 30 centimetres.
- **record:** Write the three lengths, 15 cm, 22 cm and 30 cm. Beside each write whether the note was lower, the same or higher than the one before. Then write what the pinch did.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hook the band over a door handle. 2. Your child pulls the other end with one finger until the band is 15 cm long, and plucks it. 3. Ask for a guess: what will the note do at 22 cm and at 30 cm? 4. Then they stretch it to 22 cm and 30 cm, and pluck each time.  
  *Parent says:* At 30 centimetres this band will be pulled about three times as hard as at 15. What will the note do? Why do you think so? Now try it.  
  *Child mode:* Stretch the band to 15 cm and pluck. Guess what happens at 30 cm. Then try it.
  - Ask: What did you expect, and what happened? (good: I expected a much higher note, because it was much tighter.; The note rose only a little.)
  - Note: Most bands rise a little and then stay nearly the same. A few rise more. Either way, the rise is far smaller than the same tightening gives on a string of fixed length. Do not explain yet.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the band at 22 cm. 2. A partner pinches the band firmly, exactly halfway along. 3. Your child plucks one half. 4. Let go of the pinch and pluck the whole band again.  
  *Parent says:* Now the stretch stays the same. My pinch makes the vibrating part half as long. Guess first. What will the note do?  
  *Child mode:* Keep the band at 22 cm. A partner pinches the middle. Pluck one half.
  - Ask: What did the pinch do to the note? (good: It went up a lot.; Half the length gave a much higher note.)
  - Note: The pinch changes one thing only, the length. The note jumps up clearly. This is a fair test.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the question below before you explain. 2. Let your child look at the band at 15 cm and at 30 cm. 3. Then read them the note.  
  *Parent says:* When you stretched the band, tighter was not the only change. Look at it at 15 centimetres, and again at 30. What else changed?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the band short, then long. Tighter was one change. Find two more.
  - Ask: What three things changed when the band was stretched? (good: It got tighter.; It got longer.; It got thinner.)
  - Ask: Which of those raise the note, and which lowers it? (good: Getting tighter and getting thinner raise it.; Getting longer lowers it.)
  - Note: Stretching changes three things at once. The band gets tighter, which raises the note. It gets thinner, which also raises the note. It gets longer, which lowers the note. The changes nearly cancel. So this test cannot show what tension does. In the Water-Bottle String, only the water changed, and the length stayed at 40 cm. That is why a fair test changes one thing at a time.
  - Think: When you sing a higher note, your vocal folds are stretched longer and tighter. Why does the note go up and not down?
### a5 · Instrument Detective: how does each instrument change its volume and its pitch? (20 min)
- **record:** Make a table with three rows, one for each instrument. Give it three columns: what vibrates, how the player changes the pitch, and how the player changes the volume.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the list together: veena, violin, bansuri, nadaswaram, harmonium, mridangam, kanjira, ghatam. 2. Your child chooses one string, one wind and one percussion instrument. 3. Find the real instrument, a picture of it, or a concert video.  
  *Parent says:* Choose three instruments, one from each family. In a string instrument, strings vibrate. In a wind instrument, air vibrates. A percussion instrument is struck.  
  *Child mode:* Pick three instruments: one with strings, one you blow, one you strike. Find a picture or a video.
  - Note: A harmonium is a wind instrument, because air from its bellows makes its metal reeds vibrate. Search for a concert clip by the instrument's name. Watch it first yourself.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child looks at each instrument or watches it being played for two minutes. 2. For each one they answer three questions and fill in a row of their own table. 3. Do not show the answer table yet.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the player's hands. What is vibrating? What does the player do to get a higher note? What does the player do to get a louder note?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the player's hands. Fill in one row of your table for each instrument.
  - Ask: What does the player do to get a higher note? (good: The player makes the string shorter with a finger.; The player opens a hole, so that the air column is shorter.; A drum's note is set before playing, by tightening the skin.)
  - Note: Use what the Water-Bottle String and the Filling Bottle showed. A shorter or tighter string is higher. A shorter air column is higher. A tighter skin is higher.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the answer table. 2. Your child compares their three rows with it. 3. Talk about any row that differs.  
  *Parent says:* Now compare your table with this one. Where do you agree? Where is yours different? Could both be right?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the answer table. Check your three rows. What did you find that the table missed?
  - Note: A child often notices things the table leaves out, such as a violin player's shaking finger. Give credit for what they saw.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the question below. 2. Let your child test the answer by humming a slide from a low note to a high note.  
  *Parent says:* Some instruments can slide smoothly from one note to the next, and some cannot. Which of your three can slide? What lets them do it?  
  *Child mode:* Hum a slide from a low note to a high note. Can any of your instruments do that?
  - Ask: Which instruments can slide between notes, and why? (good: A veena or a violin can, because a finger can stop the string anywhere.; A harmonium cannot, because each reed has one pitch.; A voice can, because the vocal folds can tighten smoothly.)
  - Note: Sliding needs a pitch control that changes smoothly: the length or the tension of a string, or the tightness of the vocal folds. A bansuri player slides by uncovering a hole a little at a time.

### p1 · Two Instruments, One Tune: match a string to three bottles (50 min, 2 days)
- **record:** For each of the three notes, write two lengths in centimetres: the air column in the bottle, and the string. Write the frequency too, if you have a tuner. Then write the tune you played as a row of numbers, such as 1 2 3 2 1.
- **schedule:** day 1: Tune three bottles to three rising notes by adding water and blowing across them. Mark the water levels. (25 min); day 2: Find the three string lengths that match the bottles. Mark them, measure them, and play a tune on both instruments. (25 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the three bottles in a row. 2. Your child blows gently across the mouth of an empty one until it hoots. 3. Pour water into the first bottle until it is about a quarter full. This is note 1. 4. Add water to the second until its note is one step higher. This is note 2. 5. Do the same for the third. This is note 3.  
  *Parent says:* Blow across the top, as if you were cooling a hot drink. Sing Sa, Re, Ga. Now add water to each bottle until the three bottles sing those three notes.  
  *Child mode:* Blow across each bottle. Add water to change the note. Make three notes that step up.
  - Note: To make a bottle hoot, rest the lower lip on the rim and blow a thin stream across the opening. More water gives a shorter air column and a higher note. Any three notes that step up will do. With a tuner app, write down each frequency.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark each water level with tape. 2. Measure each air column, from the water to the mouth, and write it down. 3. Your child plays a short tune on the bottles, such as 1 2 3 2 1. 4. Keep the bottles for the next day.  
  *Parent says:* Measure the air column in each bottle. Which bottle has the shortest one? Which gives the highest note? Now play me a tune on the three.  
  *Child mode:* Mark the water levels. Measure the air in each bottle. Play a tune: 1 2 3 2 1.
  - Note: If water evaporates overnight, top each bottle up to its tape mark the next day.
  - Wait: Carry on the next day
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check that the Water-Bottle String has its bottle half full. 2. Your child blows bottle 1, then plucks the string. 3. They slide one pencil until the string's note matches the bottle. 4. Tape a paper mark on the table at the pencil. 5. Do the same for bottles 2 and 3. 6. Measure the three string lengths.  
  *Parent says:* Blow the bottle, then pluck the string. Is the string higher or lower than the bottle? Which way must the pencil move to bring them together?  
  *Child mode:* Blow bottle 1. Pluck the string. Slide the pencil until the two notes match. Mark the place.
  - Note: Matching by ear takes patience. Hum the bottle's note first, then find it on the string. A tuner app helps: move the pencil until the string shows the same number as the bottle. If the string is too low even at 15 cm, hang the full bottle on it.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child plays the same tune on the bottles and then on the string. 2. Someone listens with eyes shut and says which instrument is playing. 3. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Play the tune on the bottles, then on the string. I will shut my eyes. The notes are the same. So how can I still tell which instrument it is?  
  *Child mode:* Play your tune on the bottles. Then play it on the string. Can a listener tell them apart?
  - Ask: How did you raise the note of the bottle? How did you raise the note of the string? (good: I made the air column shorter with more water.; I made the string shorter with the pencil.)
  - Ask: The two instruments played the same notes. Did they sound the same? (good: No. The bottle hoots and the string twangs.; The pitch was the same, and the kind of sound was different.)
  - Note: Two instruments at the same pitch have the same main frequency. They still sound different, because each adds its own mix of weaker, higher vibrations. That mix is called timbre, and it is a third measure of a sound.

## Challenges
### c1 · One String, Five Notes: an instrument heard across the room (30 min)
- **problem:** Build an instrument with one string that can play five different notes, going up in order. A listener four big steps away must be able to hear each note clearly. You have 30 minutes and things from around your home.
- **your turn:** Now design your own instrument. Draw it first, or tell your grown-up your plan, and say how you will get five notes. Build it and test it with a listener four big steps away. Write which idea changed the pitch, which idea changed the volume, and what you would change next time.
- **constraints:** Only one string, or one rubber band, may vibrate.; The five notes must go up in order. You must be able to play them again in the same order.; No phone, speaker or amplifier may be used to make it louder.; A listener four big steps away must hear every note.
- **general:** Stretch the string over a hollow box, with a pencil under each end as a bridge. The box is the soundboard.; Mark five places along the string. Press the string down at each mark, or slide a pencil to it, to make the vibrating part shorter.; Tie one end of the string to a pencil. Turn the pencil like a peg to tighten the string for each note.
- **creative:** Turn a plastic bucket upside down. Thread the string through a small hole in its base and knot it inside. Tie the other end to the top of a stick that stands on the bucket. Lean the stick back to tighten the string.; Slide a steel tumbler along the string as you pluck. A chitravina player slides a smooth block along the strings in this way.; Thread the string through the bottom of a paper cup, so that the cup pushes the air.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem and the four rules aloud. 2. Your child plucks the bare string, held tight between two hands by a helper. 3. The listener stands four big steps away and says whether they can hear it. 4. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the problem. One string, five notes, heard from four steps away. A bare string is very quiet. Why?  
  *Child mode:* Read the problem. Pluck the bare string. Can the listener hear it?
  - Ask: What will you change to get five different notes? (good: The length of the string.; How tight the string is.)
  - Ask: What will you change to make it louder? (good: Put it on a hollow box.; Pluck it harder.)
  - Note: A thin string pushes very little air, so it is quiet without a soundboard. Pitch and volume need two separate fixes.

### Changing the pitch and changing the volume (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (count) A string gives 110 hertz. The pull on it is made four times as great. What is the new frequency?

   Options: It doubles / It halves / It stays the same / It becomes four times as high  
   **Answer:** It doubles. *Why:* Half the length gives twice the frequency. If you chose 'halves', you matched a shorter string to a smaller number. A shorter string vibrates faster.
3. (mcq) Why does a thick string give a lower note than a thin one of the same length and tension?

### Testing ideas about volume and pitch (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A tuner reads 220 hertz beside a steady humming string. What does it read from across the room?

2. (mcq) Two equal loudspeakers play together. How does the meter reading compare with one alone?  
   Options: About 3 decibels more / Twice as many decibels / 10 decibels more / The same  
   **Answer:** About 3 decibels more. *Why:* Two equal sources give twice the energy, and twice the energy is only about 3 decibels more. It takes ten times the energy to add 10 decibels.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a sound of 80 decibels has twice the energy of a sound of 40 decibels.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each 10 decibels is ten times the energy. From 40 to 80 is four steps: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10, which is 10,000 times the energy.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Ultrasound above 20,000 hertz and infrasound below 20 hertz can carry plenty of energy. Human ears do not respond to them, though a dog or an elephant may hear them well.

## Applications
- Makers of veenas, sitars and violins choose the thickness of each string and the tension it will carry. In this way every string gives its own note at the same length.
- India's Noise Pollution Rules of 2000 set limits in decibels. In places where people live, the limit is 55 decibels by day and 45 by night.
- Workers in factories, airports and building sites wear earmuffs. A hard shell blocks sound, and soft foam inside absorbs it.
- Cinema halls and recording studios line their walls with soft panels and thick curtains. These absorb sound, so that it does not bounce around.
- A hearing aid has a tiny microphone, an amplifier and a loudspeaker. It is set to add most volume at the pitches its wearer hears least well.
- Doctors use ultrasound, far above 20,000 hertz, to make pictures of a baby before birth. The sound is too high for anyone to hear.
- Elephants call in infrasound, because low frequencies are weakened least by air and forest. A herd can keep in touch over a long distance.

## Spotted around us
- A veena or guitar player turning the pegs before a concert, plucking and listening after each small turn.
- A tabla player tapping the wooden blocks under the straps with a small hammer. She then strikes the skin to hear the new pitch.
- A bansuri seller at a fair or on a beach, carrying flutes of many lengths on one pole. The long ones play the low notes.

- A steel pot filling at the kitchen tap. The note of the water rises as the pot fills, and you know when to turn the tap off.

- A male koel in summer. His call climbs higher with each 'ku-oo', and gets louder too.
- The volume bar on a phone. Each press of the button changes the amplitude of the loudspeaker's movement. The pitch of the song stays the same.

## Open questions
- **Why do a few people have perfect pitch, and name any note they hear?** It runs in some families, and it is more common in people who began music very young. It is also more common among speakers of languages that use pitch to tell words apart. Nobody knows how much is inborn and how much is learned.
- **Can the hair cells of a human inner ear be made to grow back?** Birds and fish regrow these cells after loud sound damages them. Humans and other mammals do not. Scientists are trying to find out what switches the regrowth on, and no treatment works yet.
- **How much does the noise of ships harm whales and other sea animals?** The sea has become much louder at low frequencies, where large whales call. Some whales call louder or leave noisy places. How much this harms their feeding and their young is still being measured.
- **Do plants respond to the pitch of the sounds around them?** In a 2019 study, evening primrose flowers made sweeter nectar within minutes of hearing a bee's low buzz. High-pitched sounds had no such effect. Few other teams have repeated this yet.
- **Are babies born linking pitch to height, or do they learn it from words?** In 2014, a study tested babies of four months. They matched a rising pitch to a rising ball, and also to a thinning tube. How language later strengthens one link and weakens the other is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the Water-Bottle String, 250 grams of water gave 110 hertz, and 1,000 grams gave 220 hertz. How much water would give 440 hertz? **4,000 grams, which is 4 kilograms or 4 litres of water.**. From 250 to 1,000 grams the pull became 4 times as great, and the frequency doubled. To double it again, from 220 to 440, the pull must become 4 times as great again. 1,000 × 4 = 4,000 grams. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A string gives 155 hertz when 40 cm of it vibrates. What will it give at 20 cm, and at 10 cm? What fraction of 40 cm is each length? **310 hertz at 20 cm, which is one half of 40 cm. 620 hertz at 10 cm, which is one quarter of 40 cm.**. Half the length gives twice the frequency: 155 × 2 = 310. A quarter of the length gives four times the frequency: 155 × 4 = 620. The fraction of the length and the multiple of the frequency are a matching pair: one half and 2, one quarter and 4. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- A whisper is 30 decibels and a mixie is 90 decibels. How many times the sound energy of the whisper does the mixie have? **1,000,000 times, which is one million.**. Every 10 decibels more is 10 times the energy. From 30 to 90 is 60 decibels, which is 6 steps of 10. So multiply by 10 six times: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000,000. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In the Muffle Test, the readings in decibels were 70 with no cover and 66 with one layer of towel. They were 60 with four layers, 54 in the closed dabba and 64 with the lid a little open. Draw a bar chart. How many decibels did each cover take away? **One layer took away 4 decibels and four layers took away 10. The closed dabba took away 16, and the dabba with its lid a little open took away 6.**. Subtract each reading from 70. 70 − 66 = 4. 70 − 60 = 10. 70 − 54 = 16. 70 − 64 = 6. Four layers took away 10 decibels, so only a tenth of the sound energy got through. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L5`)
- In Gentle Pluck, Hard Pluck, pulls of half a centimetre, 1 cm and 2 cm were made. They gave 48, 54 and 60 decibels. What is the pattern? Predict the reading for a pull of 4 cm. Why might the real reading be different? **Each time the pull doubles, the reading goes up by 6 decibels. The pattern predicts 66 decibels for 4 cm. The real reading may differ, because a string pulled that far can hit the table or slip on the pencils.**. 54 − 48 = 6 and 60 − 54 = 6. The pull doubled each time: half, 1, 2. Doubling again gives 4 cm, and 60 + 6 = 66. A pattern holds only as far as the thing keeps behaving in the same way. (queues `math.algebra.input-output.L5`)
- A bottle is 24 cm tall. Water fills it to 6 cm, then 12 cm, then 18 cm. How long is the air column each time? What fraction of the bottle is air? **18 cm, which is three quarters. 12 cm, which is one half. 6 cm, which is one quarter.**. Subtract the height of the water from 24 cm. 24 − 6 = 18, 24 − 12 = 12, and 24 − 18 = 6. Out of 24, 18 is three quarters, 12 is one half and 6 is one quarter. The shortest air column, 6 cm, gives the highest note. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L5`)
- Three readings of one note on a tuner were 152, 155 and 157 hertz. A fourth reading was 310 hertz. Which reading should be checked again, and why? **The reading of 310 hertz. It is exactly twice 155.**. Three readings lie within 5 hertz of each other. The fourth is double. A tuner app sometimes catches a higher part of the sound, at twice the main frequency. Pluck again, gently, at the middle of the string. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What does the volume of a sound tell you? → How loud or quiet it is.
- What does the pitch of a sound tell you? → How high or low it is.
- Which part of a vibration sets the volume? → Its amplitude, which is how far the thing moves.
- Which part of a vibration sets the pitch? → Its frequency, which is the number of vibrations each second.
- What unit is volume measured in? → The decibel.
- Name three ways to make a string's note higher. → Make it tighter, shorter or lighter.
- What happens to the note when an air column gets shorter? → It gets higher.
- A towel covers a ringing phone. What changes, and what stays the same? → The volume drops. The pitch stays the same.
