# Luck or cause: when two things happen together, what does it show?
`sci.how-we-know.questioning-evidence.L4`

**Summary.** On a hot beach in Kerala, the more ice golas a cart sold in a day, the more people went home sunburnt. Do golas burn skin? Your child will flip a coin 200 times to see how often luck alone makes a pattern, and measure okra pods to see what a pattern can and cannot tell us. Then they learn four ways to test a pattern fairly: look for a hidden third thing, compare with a group that did not get the thing, let a coin choose the groups, and hide who got what. They also meet John Snow, the doctor who used a map and a comparison to find out what was really making people ill in London in 1854.

**Intro.** *On a Kerala beach, a doctor counted the people who came to her tent with sunburn each day. A gola seller counted the golas he sold. On the days he sold more golas, she saw more sunburn. A man in the crowd said, "Ban the golas!" Was he right?* The two counts really did rise and fall together. That was true. But the idea that golas burn skin may still be wrong. You may already know three questions to ask about any claim: says who, how do they know, who is missing. This time you ask a harder question. When two things go together, what is really going on? You will use a coin, okra pods and some sweet cups to find out.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain why two things happening together does not show that one causes the other.
1. I can look for a hidden third thing behind a pattern, and check it by comparing like with like.
2. I can say why a few tries can look special by chance, and why more tries give better evidence.
3. I can explain why a fair comparison needs a control group, and why a coin should choose the groups.
4. I can explain what a blind test and a placebo are, and why they are used.
5. I can spot sneaky tricks in evidence: only the hits, too few tries, asking only fans, and a seller testing their own product.
6. I can tell how John Snow used a map and a comparison in 1854 to show what was making people ill.

## Terms
- **Correlation**: Two things that rise and fall together. When one goes up, the other tends to go up too. *e.g.* On a beach, more golas were sold on the days when more people got sunburnt.
- **Cause**: Something that makes another thing happen. If you take the cause away, the result goes away too. *e.g.* Pressing the switch makes the fan turn. Cut the power and the fan stops.
- **Hidden third thing**: Something that is not in your table but makes two things in the table rise and fall together. Scientists call it a confounder. *e.g.* Hot sunshine makes people buy golas and also makes them sunburnt. The sun is the hidden third thing.
- **Chance**: What happens when nothing pushes the result one way or the other, like a coin landing heads or tails. Chance can still make patterns. *e.g.* A coin can land heads four times in a row with no help from anybody.
- **Streak**: The same result coming up several times in a row. *e.g.* Heads, heads, heads, heads is a streak of four.
- **Trial**: One go at a test. One coin flip is one trial. Ten flips are ten trials. *e.g.* A child who flips a coin 200 times has done 200 trials.
- **Spread**: How much the results jump around from one set of tries to the next. With few trials the spread is big. With many trials the results settle. *e.g.* Ten flips can give 3 heads or 8 heads. Two hundred flips will give close to 100 heads.
- **Control group**: The group that is treated the same as the others, except that it does not get the thing being tested. You compare the test group with it. *e.g.* One banana is wrapped at the stalk. Its twin from the same bunch is left bare. The bare banana is the control.
- **Random**: Decided by chance, with nobody choosing. A coin flip is a fair way to be random. *e.g.* A coin flip decides which of two cups gets the fancy label.
- **Blind test**: A test where the people being tested do not know which group they are in. In a double-blind test the people measuring do not know either. *e.g.* Tasting two cups without knowing which one has the fancy label.
- **Placebo**: A dummy treatment with nothing active in it, such as a sugar pill. It lets a test separate the effect of the real treatment from the effect of expecting to feel better. *e.g.* In a medicine test, one group gets the medicine and another gets a plain sugar pill that looks the same.
- **Cherry-picking**: Showing only the results that fit your story and hiding the rest, like picking only the best cherries from a basket. *e.g.* A shop shows three happy customers and does not mention the thirty who sent the product back.
- **Anecdote**: One person's story about what happened to them. It can be true and still not show what caused it. *e.g.* "My cousin drank this tea and his cold went away" is an anecdote.
- **Conflict of interest**: When the person doing a test would gain if the answer comes out one way, for example because they sell the product. *e.g.* A biscuit company says its own tests show its biscuits make children cleverer.
- **Natural experiment**: A real-life situation where luck has already made two groups that differ in only one way, so we can compare them without setting up a test. *e.g.* Two water companies supplying the same streets, so neighbours drank different water.
- **Cholera**: A serious illness of the gut, caused by a bacterium called Vibrio cholerae. Cholera spreads when people drink water or eat food that carries the bacterium. *e.g.* Clean boiled drinking water helps stop cholera from spreading.

## Explanations
### Two things that go together: what a pattern can and cannot show · *defined*
**Definition:** A correlation means two things rise and fall together. It is a real pattern, but it does not show that one thing causes the other.

On a beach in May, the more golas the cart sold, the more people got sunburnt. The numbers really did go together. That is called a correlation. A correlation is worth noticing, because it tells you where to look. But it does not tell you what is making what happen. To know that, you have to do more work. You can look for a third thing, check which came first, and compare with a situation where one of the two is missing.
- e.g. More golas sold and more sunburn on the same days is a correlation.
- e.g. Taller children read better books. Both go with being older.
- e.g. On the days more umbrellas are open in Chennai, the roads are wetter. Neither makes the other happen.
### The hidden third thing · *defined*
**Definition:** A hidden third thing is something outside your table that pushes two things in the table up and down together. Scientists call it a confounder.

Ask: what else is different on the days when both numbers are high? On the beach the answer is the hot sun. Sun makes people buy golas, and sun burns skin. The golas and the sunburn never touched each other. To check, compare like with like. Look only at sunny days, so the sun is the same in every row. If golas still go with sunburn when the sun is the same, golas might matter. If they do not, the sun was doing it all.
- e.g. Fire engines and damage: the bigger the fire, the more engines come and the more damage there is. The size of the fire is the hidden third thing.
- e.g. Children with big shoes spell better. Age is behind both.
- e.g. Towns with more tea stalls have more phone shops. The size of the town is behind both.
### Which way round? Sometimes the arrow points the other way · *defined*
**Definition:** Sometimes two things go together because the second one causes the first. The arrow points the opposite way from what you guessed.

Children who visit the dentist often have more fillings. Does the dentist cause fillings? No. Having bad teeth sends children to the dentist. The cause comes first, and the visit follows. Whenever two things go together, ask which one came first, and ask whether the arrow might point the other way. A diary, a photo with a date, or a timestamped record can show which came first.
- e.g. People who carry inhalers have more breathing trouble. The trouble is why they carry them.
- e.g. Wet umbrellas outside the school door do not cause rain. Rain causes wet umbrellas.
- e.g. Where there are more doctors, more people seem ill. Illness brings doctors to a place.
### Chance can make patterns · *defined*
**Definition:** Chance means no cause is pushing the result. Even so, chance makes streaks and lumps that look like patterns, most often when you have only a few tries.

A fair coin lands heads about half the time. In ten flips, though, you will often see 7 heads, because ten flips is a small number. About one set of ten flips in six shows 7 or more heads with no wishing at all. In a long run of flips, a streak of four heads in a row turns up many times. People who see a streak often feel a reason must be behind it. Usually the reason is just luck. The way to tell is to ask how often chance alone would do that.
- e.g. Your cricket team wins three matches in a row when you wear a red shirt. Three is a small number.
- e.g. Two buses to the same stop come together. Chance bunches things up.
- e.g. A fair coin gives five heads in a row once in a while. It is not broken.
### More tries, steadier results · *defined*
**Definition:** The more times you try something, the closer the results get to what usually happens. With few tries, the results jump around.

In ten flips, the number of heads can swing from 3 to 8. In 100 flips, the number of heads is between 40 and 60 about 96 times out of 100. In 200 flips, it stays even closer to half. So 7 heads out of 10 is not strong evidence of anything. But 70 heads out of 100 would be, because chance almost never does that. This does not mean bigger is always better. If a test is unfair, asking more of the same people only gives you more of the wrong answer.
- e.g. A bowler who takes 3 wickets in one match may be lucky. One who takes 3 wickets a match for 40 matches is good.
- e.g. One spoonful from a jar is a small try. Ten spoonfuls from different places are steadier.
- e.g. In 1936 a US magazine got more than two million replies to its poll and still guessed the wrong winner. It had asked the wrong people.
### A fair comparison needs a control group · *defined*
**Definition:** A control group is treated exactly like the test group, except for the one thing being tested. The only way to see what a thing does is to compare it with a case where it is missing.

Suppose someone says wrapping the stalk of a banana slows its ripening. If you wrap one banana and watch it, you learn nothing, because bananas ripen anyway. You need a second banana, from the same bunch, kept in the same place, with no wrapping. That is the control. If the wrapped banana ripens slower, the wrapping might be the reason. Then you check by trying again. It is like checking an old saying about crows and guests: you need the days when the crow was missing. A control group is that idea, made on purpose.
- e.g. A medicine test gives the medicine to one group and a dummy pill to another.
- e.g. A new fertiliser is tried on half the pots, while the other half get plain water.
- e.g. A school tries a new reading method in one class and keeps another class on the old way.
### Let a coin choose the groups · *defined*
**Definition:** Random means chance decides who goes into which group. This stops the person running the test from choosing, even by accident, the ones who would do better.

Suppose you test a plant food on six plants. If you pick which plants get it, you may give it to the strongest ones without noticing. Then the strong plants grow more, and the food gets the credit. A coin flip has no favourites. Heads goes in group A. Tails goes in group B. Over many items, the two groups come out alike in every way except the thing you are testing. You may not know all the hidden third things, and a coin takes care of them anyway.
- e.g. Flip a coin to decide which of two cups gets the fancy label.
- e.g. Draw names from a hat to decide who tries the new game first.
- e.g. Flip a coin for each banana from the bunch to decide which one is wrapped.
### Blind tests and the placebo effect · *defined*
**Definition:** In a blind test, people do not know which group they are in. A placebo is a dummy treatment. Both are used because expecting something can change how people feel.

In many medicine tests, people who were given a plain sugar pill said their pain was less, and some said they felt better. This is called the placebo effect. The feelings are real. The sugar pill did not kill germs or heal bones, though. If a test does not have a placebo group, the effect of expecting to feel better gets mixed up with the effect of the medicine. So testers keep people blind. In a double-blind test, the doctors giving out the pills do not know who got which either, so no sign, look or word can give it away.
- e.g. Two cups of the same water, one labelled expensive. Many people say the labelled one tastes better.
- e.g. A new tablet is tested against a look-alike sugar tablet, and nobody knows who got which until the end.
- e.g. A teacher marks maths papers with the names covered, so the child's name cannot change the mark.
### Sneaky tricks in evidence · *defined*
**Definition:** Evidence can mislead even when every number in it is true. Cherry-picking, tiny samples, asking only fans, and tests run by sellers are the common tricks.

Here are five ways true numbers can mislead. One: only the hits are shown, like three happy customers out of three hundred. Two: too few tries, like a toothpaste tested on five people. Three: only the fans were asked, like a survey handed out at the biscuit stall. Four: the seller ran the test, so it might be tilted. Five: a chart that starts its bars at 90 instead of 0, so a tiny gap looks huge. None of these proves a claim is wrong. They tell you to ask for more before you believe it.
- e.g. "Nine out of ten users agree!" Nine out of ten of which ten people?
- e.g. A bar chart where one bar looks twice as tall because the scale starts at 90.
- e.g. A cereal box says a study found children who eat the cereal do better. The cereal company paid for the study.
### When you cannot run the test: a natural experiment · *defined*
**Definition:** A natural experiment is a real-world case where luck has already sorted people into two groups that differ in just one way. Scientists compare them as if they had set up the test.

In 1854 many people in one part of London were dying of cholera. Most doctors thought it spread through bad air. A doctor named John Snow had another idea: it spread through water. He could not give half the people clean water and half dirty water, which would have been wrong. But he found streets where neighbours were supplied by two different water companies, one drawing water from a dirtier place on the river. The streets, the poverty and the air were the same. The only difference was the water. The households with the dirtier water had many times more deaths. This was strong evidence, and he had not run a test himself.
- e.g. Two villages get electricity in different years, and scientists compare their school results.
- e.g. A new road splits a town, and doctors compare the health of the two sides.
- e.g. Twins who grow up in different cities can be compared on one thing at a time.
### Stronger and weaker evidence · *idea*
Not all evidence is equal. Here is one way to rank it, from weakest to strongest. A single story is weakest. A table of every case is better. A comparison with a control group is better again. A comparison where a coin chose the groups and nobody knew who got what is stronger still. The strongest evidence is many such tests, done by different people in different places, that agree. People still argue about how to rank evidence, and some questions can never be tested with an experiment, such as what happened in history or what is inside a star. For those, scientists use the best comparisons they can find.
- e.g. One person says the tea cured their cold. That is a single story.
- e.g. A coin-chosen, blind test of the tea on 400 people is much stronger.
- e.g. Ten such tests in ten countries that all agree is stronger again.
### How strong must the evidence be? · *debated*
How much evidence is enough? Thinkers disagree. One idea is that the stranger the claim, the stronger the evidence must be. Another is that the real question is how likely the claim was before you looked. Both agree that a story is not enough.
- e.g. A claim that the Moon is made of cheese needs much more than a claim that it will rain tomorrow.
- e.g. A claim that a new vitamin helps people sleep is believable, but still needs a fair comparison.
- **Carl Sagan, an American astronomer (1934 to 1996):** Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence. The odder the claim, the more you should ask for.
- **Thinkers who use probability, sometimes called Bayesians:** Start with how likely the claim was before. Then let each piece of evidence move you up or down. Odd claims start low, so they need more pushes, but nothing is ruled out.
- **Many working scientists:** Use the same careful standard for every claim. Oddness is a reason to check twice, but the test is the same.

## Mostly agreed
- Two things going together does not show that one causes the other. *How we know:* Again and again, a third thing has been found behind a pattern, such as the sun behind golas and sunburn. In Snow's London, the cause of cholera was found by looking behind the first pattern.
- Chance alone can make results that look special, especially with few tries. *How we know:* With a fair coin, about one set of ten flips in six gives 7 or more heads. This follows from counting the ways a coin can fall, and your own 200 flips will show it.
- To see what a thing does, compare with a group that did not get it. *How we know:* Medicine tests, farm trials and school studies all use a control group, because without one the effect of the treatment cannot be told from things that would have happened anyway.
- Expecting to feel better can change how people feel, so good tests use a placebo and keep people blind. *How we know:* In many tests, people given dummy pills reported less pain than people given nothing. A large review of such tests found this effect, mostly for pain and nausea, which are reported by the person.
- Many tries give steadier results than few tries, but more tries cannot fix a test that asked the wrong people. *How we know:* The counts from coin flips settle towards half as the flips grow. The 1936 poll of more than two million replies still got the winner wrong because of who was asked.
- People who would gain from a result are not the best ones to test it. *How we know:* Reviews of many studies found that studies paid for by the maker of a product more often favoured that product than studies paid for by others.

## How we found out
**John Snow, an English doctor in London, 1854.** In 1854, cholera struck the Soho part of London. Many doctors believed it spread through bad air, which they called miasma. A doctor named John Snow thought it spread through water. He marked each death on a map of the streets, and the marks gathered around one water pump on Broad Street. Then he looked for the exceptions. A workhouse near the pump, which had its own well, had few deaths. A brewery close by, where workers drank beer, had few or none. Some people far away who had water brought from the Broad Street pump fell ill. Snow also compared houses on the same streets that were supplied by two different water companies. The houses with the dirtier water had many times more deaths. The pump handle was taken off. A pattern was not enough for Snow. He looked for what stood behind it, and he checked by comparing like with like.  
*What people believed before:* Many doctors and officials believed that cholera was spread by bad air, called miasma. Snow's map, his search for the exceptions and his comparison of houses on the same streets showed that the water was carrying the illness.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If two things go together, one of them must cause the other.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Sometimes it is true. Smoke and fire go together, and fire does cause smoke.  
  Against: Golas and sunburn went together, and the sun was behind both. A pattern is a reason to look closer, not an answer.
- **What happened to me is the best proof.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: Your own experience is real, and you saw it with your own eyes.  
  Against: One person's story cannot say what would have happened without the treatment. Colds go away, and luck happens. Your story is a good reason to test, not the test itself.
- **If a poll or test has many people, it must be right.** (Some people who like big numbers; *rejected*)  
  For: More tries do give steadier results when the test is fair.  
  Against: In 1936, a US magazine asked millions of people and still guessed wrong, because the people it asked were not like all voters. A big biased sample is still biased.
- **A placebo effect means the person was only imagining it.** (A common view; *rejected*)  
  For: A placebo has no active ingredient.  
  Against: People in these tests report real changes, especially in pain, and some body measures change too. The feeling is real. But a sugar pill cannot kill germs or heal a broken bone.
- **We can only know a cause by running an experiment.** (Some scientists; *debated*)  
  For: An experiment is the strongest way, because you set the groups yourself.  
  Against: We cannot run experiments on stars, on history, or on things that would harm people. Snow found the cause of cholera by comparing houses that luck had already sorted. Many scientists say careful comparisons are enough when experiments are impossible.

## Roots
We found that golas and sunburn went together because the sun stood behind both. Now we ask what this tells us about how things in the world are joined.

**Deep truth.** Golas and sunburn rose and fell together, yet neither made the other happen. The hot sun stood behind both and was not in the table. Many things that move together are two results of one thing behind them. Finding that thing is where understanding starts.  
*Habit it breaks:* We see two things together and draw an arrow between them, from the one we noticed first to the one we care about. The arrow feels like seeing, but it is a guess, and it leaves out whatever stands behind both.  
*Evidence:* In the beach table, the golas no longer went with the sunburn once only sunny days were compared, and a day with no golas still had burns. Your coin sets and okra pods showed the other lesson: a pattern in numbers can be real, wobbly, and silent about its cause.

- *who am i:* You feel hot, thirsty and grumpy, all at the same time. You may blame one feeling for another. Often one thing, the heat, is behind all three.
- *what is life:* Many flowers open when the sun is high, and bees come to them at that hour. The flowers and the bees both answer the same sun.
- *what is this world:* Rain clouds, wet roads, open umbrellas and calling frogs all arrive together in the monsoon. They look like separate events, but many of them share one source.

Every pair of things that go together has a story behind it. Sometimes one makes the other. Sometimes the second makes the first, sometimes luck bunches them, and sometimes a third thing, like the sun, stands behind both. Telling these apart takes patience, a fair comparison and a willingness to look behind what everyone has already noticed.

- **Samyutta Nikaya 12 (dependent origination), a collection of the Buddha's teachings:** The Buddha taught: when this exists, that comes to be. When this does not exist, that does not come to be. Nothing arises alone. It arises from conditions. (On the day the cart was closed, the golas ended but the sunburn did not end. By this test, the sunburn did not depend on the golas. It depended on the sun.)
- **Tirukkural 423, by Thiruvalluvar, an ancient Tamil book of short poems:** Whatever you hear, and from whomever you hear it, wisdom is to find the true meaning in it. (The man at the beach sounded sure when he said golas burn the skin. A careful person looks for the true thing behind what is said, and that thing was the sun.)
- Science (settled): Chance alone makes streaks and clusters, and scientists work out how often chance would give a result before they call it a surprise.
- Science (settled): A hidden third thing can be ruled out by comparing like with like, or by letting a coin choose the groups.
- Science (debated): Expecting to feel better can change reported pain and other feelings. How this works in the body is still being studied.
- Other view, David Hume, a Scottish philosopher of the 1700s: We never actually see one thing make another happen. We see one thing follow another again and again, and our minds form a habit of expecting it. So 'cause' is the name of that habit, not something we can see.
- Practice, Look behind the two (3 min): Sit by a window. Feel the warmth on your arm. Look at the bright patch of light on the floor. Breathe in slowly three times. Now ask what is behind both the warmth and the light. Notice that it is one thing, far away, reaching both places at once.
- Ask: Think of two things you have seen go together. What could be behind both?
- Ask: If you could never run a test, how would you tell a cause from a pattern?
- Ask: Is a cause something in the world, or a habit of our minds?
- Ask: What do you believe because two things went together, and have you ever looked behind it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Golas and sunburn went together, but the sun was behind both. How many other things we think are connected might just be standing in the same sun? (hint: Think of two things you believe go together, and ask what else is different on those days.)
- *deep:* If chance makes streaks and patterns all the time, how can anyone tell a real pattern from a lucky one? (hint: Think about how many times you would have to flip the coin to be sure.)
- *deep:* A sugar pill can make people feel less pain. What does that tell us about the link between what we expect and what our bodies do? (hint: Think of how you feel when a grown-up kisses a bump.)
- *big:* We cannot run an experiment on the Sun or on history. How can we ever know what caused what in such cases? (hint: Think of Snow, who compared two kinds of houses that luck had already made.)
- *big:* Who should test a new product: the company that makes it, or someone else? Who pays for the someone else? (hint: Think of a child marking their own homework.)
- *deep:* The philosopher David Hume said we never see a cause, only one thing following another many times. Do you agree? (hint: Think about pushing a door. Do you see the push make it open, or do you just see push then open?)
- *big:* A village farmer says her rain dance works because rain came the next day. Who should test it, and how would you do it kindly? (hint: Think about the days she danced and no rain came, and the days it rained without a dance.)
- *big:* A pattern can be true, and useful, and still not be a cause. When is it fine to act on a pattern anyway? (hint: Think of carrying an umbrella when you see dark clouds.)

## Story: The Ban on the Gola Cart
*A made-up story. The science in it is real: two counts can rise and fall together because a third thing, here the sun, is behind both, and comparing only days when the third thing is the same shows this. Ice golas are shaved ice with syrup, sold on beaches across India.*

Meenu was ten, and every evening in May she helped her uncle Basheer at his ice gola cart on Kozhikode beach. Her job was to turn the handle of the shaver. The ice went chhk, chhk, chhk, and fell into a heap as white as snow. Uncle poured red syrup on it, then orange. People paid five rupees and walked away with a cold, sweet, dripping cone.

That week a green tent went up next to the cart. A banner said BEACH HEALTH CAMP. Inside sat Dr. Leela, who had a stethoscope round her neck and a big register in front of her. All day, people with red, burning shoulders came in. She wrote each one down.

On Sunday evening, the town councillor, Mrs. Fathima Beevi, came to the tent. A young man from the camp, Sreejith, pinned a sheet of paper to the board. On it was a table of seven days. One column said how many golas Uncle Basheer had sold, because Uncle always counted in his notebook. The next column said how many sunburn cases Dr. Leela had seen.

"Look," said Sreejith. "Big gola days are big sunburn days. Small gola days are small sunburn days." He tapped the board. "The golas are doing this."

Mrs. Fathima frowned. "Then golas must go. No carts on the beach from next Monday."

Uncle Basheer went very quiet. The cart had been his father's. Meenu looked at the paper. The numbers were true. She had seen them in Uncle's notebook herself. But something bothered her, and she could not say what.

At school, Mrs. Sulochana, her teacher, had a saying. "When two things go together, look for who else is standing there." Meenu copied the table into her own notebook.

She went through Uncle's pages with her friend Aysha. She added a new column on the right. Aysha had a good memory for the weather, so Meenu wrote it in: sunny, sunny, cloudy, sunny, cloudy, sunny, sunny.

"Look," whispered Aysha. "The two cloudy days are the two smallest days. Both columns."

Meenu put her finger on the Thursday row. The cart axle had broken on Thursday. Uncle had shut the cart all day. The golas column said zero. But the camp's column for Thursday said fourteen. Fourteen people had come in with sunburn on a day with no golas at all.

"That's odd," said Aysha. "If golas burn people, how did fourteen get burnt?"

Meenu thought hard. "Maybe the golas are not the thing. Maybe the sun is. Sun makes people want golas. Sun burns people. The golas and the burns never touch each other."

"But Sreejith will say you are only saying that to save your uncle," said Aysha.

Meenu nodded. "So I have to show it a way nobody can argue with. I can't just say the sun is behind it."

She thought for a long time, until the sky went dark. Then she took a clean page and wrote only the sunny days. Forty golas and thirteen burns. Thirty-eight golas and fifteen burns. Zero golas and fourteen burns. Ninety golas and fourteen burns. A hundred and twenty golas and fourteen burns.

"Now the sun is the same in every row," she told Aysha. "If golas made people burn, then on sunny days the big gola days would have more burns. Look. They don't. Forty or a hundred and twenty, it's about fourteen."

On Monday, Mrs. Fathima came back with Sreejith and Dr. Leela. Meenu stood in front of the tent with her chart paper. Her hands shook. She showed them the first table and the second.

"How do I know you are not just saying this for your uncle?" asked the councillor.

"You don't have to believe me," said Meenu. "Just look at the sunny days only. Then look at Thursday, when there were no golas at all."

Sreejith crossed his arms. "Five sunny days is not many. It could be luck."

Dr. Leela smiled. "He is right about that, and so is Meenu. Five days is few. Let's count for two more weeks. But Thursday is a good test. If golas caused the burns, a day without golas should have been quiet, and it was not. It could have failed, and it didn't."

Mrs. Fathima read the chart again, slowly. "So what does burn the skin?"

"The sun," said Meenu. "And standing in it for hours."

The councillor did not ban the carts. Instead she asked the camp to put up a shade tent, and to hand out caps. Dr. Leela asked Meenu to help her count for the next two weeks.

That evening Uncle Basheer filled a cone with red syrup and held it out to her. "The first one is yours," he said. "Cold, and sweet, and not guilty."

**For the parent:** Golas and sunburn rose and fell together because the hot sun was behind both. Meenu checked by looking only at the sunny days, so the sun was the same in every row, and the golas and the burns no longer went together. A day with no golas and plenty of burns also showed that golas were not the cause. A pattern tells you where to look. It does not tell you what is behind it.

- Ask: Why did Meenu add a column for the weather?

### e1 · The Lucky Coin: how often does chance alone look special? (35 min)
- **question:** A friend says she can make heads come up by wishing, and she got 7 heads in 10 flips. How often does an ordinary coin, with no wishing, give 7 or more heads in 10 flips?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Out of 20 sets of 10 flips, how many sets will show 7 or more heads? Will your coin ever show 3 or fewer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same coin and toss it from the same height onto the same plate every time. No wishing, no tricks. Each set is exactly 10 flips, and heads is always the side with the picture on it. We write down every set, even the boring ones, and we do not throw away a set because it looks odd. Only chance decides the result.
- **safety:** Keep coins away from children under five, who might put them in their mouths.
- **record:** Fill in 20 rows, one for each set of 10 flips. Then count how many sets had 7 or more heads, and how many had 3 or fewer. Add up all the heads in the 200 flips. Last, draw a bar chart: one bar for each number of heads, from 0 to 10, showing how many sets had that number.
- **results table:** Set number | Heads out of 10 | Longest streak of one side | 7 or more heads, or 3 or fewer?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell the story of the friend who got 7 heads in 10 flips. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both at the top of the page.  
  *Parent says:* A friend flipped a coin ten times and got seven heads. She says she wished it. If an ordinary coin is flipped ten times, how often do you think it gives seven or more heads? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A friend got 7 heads in 10 flips. Is that special? What do you guess? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think an ordinary coin will, or will not, give 7 heads sometimes? (good: It should give 5 heads each time, so 7 is odd.; It is a coin, so any number can come.; It is random, so sometimes 7 will come.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children think 5 heads should come every time. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a table with 20 rows and the four columns. Put the plate on a cloth. Practise two flips from about 20 centimetres above the plate.  
  *Parent says:* We flip the same way every time. Hold the coin about this high above the plate and let it drop. Heads is the side with the picture.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the table. Flip from the same height onto the plate. Heads is the picture side.
  - Check: Why must every flip be done in the same way, from the same height onto the same plate? → **So the coin only lands the way chance decides**
- **s3 (16 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child flips 10 times for the first set. The helper tallies heads in a notebook. Do the next set and the next, up to 20 sets. Swap the flipper after every 5 sets.  
  *Parent says:* Flip ten times. I will count the heads. Do not wish for anything. Now the next set.  
  *Child mode:* Flip 10 times. Your helper counts heads. Then do the next set. No wishing.
  - Note: Stay calm about odd sets. If your child gets 8 heads or 2 heads in a set, say nothing and write it down. Also write the longest streak in each set, for example HHHH is a streak of four. If the coin rolls off the plate, repeat that flip.
  - Wait: The 20 sets take about 15 minutes. Take a short break after set 10.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the sets with 7 or more heads. Count the sets with 3 or fewer. Find the longest streak in all 20 sets. Write these three numbers under the table.  
  *Parent says:* How many sets had seven or more heads? How many had three or fewer? What is the longest streak you found?  
  *Child mode:* Count the sets with 7 or more heads. Count the sets with 3 or fewer. Find the longest streak.
  - Ask: Did the coin change when it gave 8 heads? What do you think made it happen? (good: No. It is the same coin. It was just luck.; I wished nothing, and it still happened.; Maybe ten flips is too few.)
  - Note: Most families find that 2 to 5 sets of 20 show 7 or more heads. The exact number varies. If you got none, or ten, write it down and talk about it. That is the spread.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the heads from all 20 sets. Compare the total with 100, which is half of 200. Then compare it with the best set.  
  *Parent says:* Add all the heads. Is it close to one hundred? Your best set was far from five. Is the total of all 200 flips closer to half?  
  *Child mode:* Add all your heads. Is the total close to 100? Is it closer to half than your best set?
  - Check: One set of 10 flips gave 8 heads. All 200 flips together gave 103 heads. Why is the total closer to half? → **Because more flips smooth out the luck of each set**
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what the friend's 7 heads in 10 does and does not show. Ask for a claim, the evidence from the table, and the reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Now think about your friend. What is your claim about her seven heads? What in your table is the evidence? What is your reason?  
  *Child mode:* Does 7 heads in 10 show your friend can wish? What is your evidence?

  - Note: Good answer: 7 heads in 10 happens by chance about one set in six, so it shows nothing yet. To be convincing she would need a very large number of heads, in many more flips, compared with a plain coin. Her result is a reason to try more flips, not a proof.

   **Answer:** A strong answer names a real claim, names the right trick, such as too few, only the hits or asked only fans, and gives a question that would find the missing piece.. *Why:* This uses the ideas on something from the child's own life.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does a correlation tell you about two things?  
   Options: That one causes the other / That they rise and fall together, but not why / That they are the same thing / That one is bigger than the other  
   **Answer:** That they rise and fall together, but not why. *Why:* A correlation is a real pattern. It is a clue about where to look. It does not say what causes what.
2. (mcq) In Chennai, on days when more people carry umbrellas, auto fares are higher. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Umbrellas make fares go up / Rain makes people carry umbrellas and also makes autos harder to find / Auto drivers dislike umbrellas / Fares make it rain  
   **Answer:** Rain makes people carry umbrellas and also makes autos harder to find. *Why:* Rain is the hidden third thing behind both. People open umbrellas in rain, and autos are in demand in rain.
3. (mcq) A girl suspects that the hot sun is behind a pattern between golas and sunburn. She compares only sunny days. Why does this help?  
   Options: On sunny days the sun is the same in every row, so she can see if golas still matter / Sunny days are prettier / Cloudy days have no golas / The doctor works only on sunny days  
   **Answer:** On sunny days the sun is the same in every row, so she can see if golas still matter. *Why:* Comparing like with like holds the hidden third thing the same. If golas and sunburn no longer go together among sunny days, the sun explains the pattern.
4. (mcq) A fair coin is flipped 10 times and gives 7 heads. Which is the best conclusion?  
   Options: The coin is not fair / This happens by chance about 1 set in 6, so it shows very little / Heads is luckier / The flipper wished for heads  
   **Answer:** This happens by chance about 1 set in 6, so it shows very little. *Why:* Seven or more heads in 10 flips comes up about 17 times in 100 sets from chance alone. It is too common to be a surprise.
5. (mcq) Why does a total of 200 flips give a steadier picture than one set of 10 flips?  
   Options: Because the coin warms up / Because over many flips the luck of single sets cancels out / Because 200 is a round number / Because later flips count more  
   **Answer:** Because over many flips the luck of single sets cancels out. *Why:* In a small set, chance can swing far from half. In a large number of flips, the highs and lows balance out and the total settles near half.
6. (mcq) Which is the best plan for testing whether wrapping a banana stalk slows ripening?  
   Options: Wrap one banana and watch it / Ask a neighbour what they think / Wrap three bananas and leave three twins bare, using a coin to choose, and score all six each day / Wrap all the bananas in the shop  
   **Answer:** Wrap three bananas and leave three twins bare, using a coin to choose, and score all six each day. *Why:* You need twins from the same bunch, a control group left bare, and a coin to choose. That makes the two groups alike apart from the wrapping.
7. (mcq) In a taste test, a coin decides which cup gets the fancy label. Why use a coin and not choose yourself?  
   Options: A coin is faster / Your own choice could tilt the test without you noticing / Coins are lucky / The label looks nicer on one side  
   **Answer:** Your own choice could tilt the test without you noticing. *Why:* If you choose, you may put the label on the cup you expect people to like. A coin has no preference, so the label goes on either side by chance.
8. (mcq) A medicine test gives half the people a sugar pill that looks identical to the real one. Why?  
   Options: To save money / So the effect of expecting to feel better can be told apart from the effect of the medicine / To trick the doctors / Because sugar is a medicine  
   **Answer:** So the effect of expecting to feel better can be told apart from the effect of the medicine. *Why:* Both groups expect to feel better. If the medicine group improves more than the sugar pill group, the extra is the effect of the medicine.
9. (mcq) In a double-blind test, who does not know which people got the real treatment?  
   Options: Only the people being tested / The people being tested and the people measuring / Only the scientist who designed it / Nobody keeps it secret  
   **Answer:** The people being tested and the people measuring. *Why:* If the testers know, a look or a word can give it away, and their hopes can change what they measure. Keeping both blind removes this.
10. (mcq) What is the most important thing to check on a bar chart before you trust the size of the gap?  
   Options: The colours of the bars / Whether the side scale starts at zero / How many bars there are / Whether the bars are touching  
   **Answer:** Whether the side scale starts at zero. *Why:* If the scale starts above zero, the bottoms of the bars are cut off and a small gap can look large. The numbers on the side tell the truth.
11. (mcq) A company says: Our own test shows our oil is the healthiest. What is the best next question?  
   Options: What colour is the bottle? / Has anyone else tested it, and who paid for the test? / How much does it cost? / Is it advertised on TV?  
   **Answer:** Has anyone else tested it, and who paid for the test?. *Why:* A company gains if the answer is yes. Tests by others who gain nothing are stronger evidence.
12. (mcq) In 1854, John Snow compared houses on the same streets that got water from two different companies. Why was this such strong evidence?  
   Options: The houses were alike in most ways except the water / The companies were large / The streets were famous / He drew a good map  
   **Answer:** The houses were alike in most ways except the water. *Why:* The streets, the air and the poverty were the same. The main difference left was the water, so the difference in illness pointed to the water.

## Applications
- Doctors compare a new medicine with a dummy pill in tests where neither the patients nor the doctors know who got which.
- Health workers map where an illness is spreading and compare the people who fall ill with those who do not.
- Farmers and agriculture colleges try a new seed on half the field and keep the other half as it was.
- Weather scientists check forecasts against what happened over many years before they trust a method.
- Engineers test a new bridge design against an old one under the same loads.
- Teachers and schools compare two ways of teaching with a coin flip deciding who gets which.
- Journalists ask who paid for a study and who was left out before they report it.
- Judges and police look for other evidence because one witness can be sure and wrong.
- Shoppers can spot an advert that shows only the happy customers.
- Anyone can ask of a pattern in the news: what else changed on those days?

## Spotted around us
- A forwarded message that says: 9 out of 10 people agree. Which ten?
- A bar chart in a newspaper where one bar looks twice as tall as another, but the side scale starts above zero.
- The weather forecast on TV that says a 70 percent chance of rain, which is a pattern from past days with the same sky.
- The coin toss at the start of a cricket match, which gives each captain a fair chance by luck.
- A lucky charm hanging on an auto-rickshaw mirror, kept because nothing bad has happened so far.
- A cough syrup advert that shows a smiling child and says it works fast, but does not say who tested it.
- Two buses arriving at the same stop together after a long wait, which is chance bunching things up.
- A fruit seller who shows only the best mangoes on top of the basket and keeps the spoiled ones underneath.

## Open questions
- **Why do people see patterns in things that are random, such as faces in clouds and streaks in coins?** Scientists have ideas about how the brain is built to look for patterns, but nobody knows exactly why the habit is so strong or when it helps and when it fools us.
- **How does expecting to feel better change what the body does?** The placebo effect is real in many tests, especially for pain, but how it works is still being studied, and scientists disagree on how much it does.
- **Can we ever be certain that one thing causes another?** Philosophers from David Hume onwards have argued that we only ever see one thing follow another. Scientists use careful tests to get as close to certain as they can, but the argument is not over.
- **How should a town act when the evidence is weak but the danger is serious?** In 1854 the pump handle was removed on a strong hint, before the proof was complete. People still argue about how much evidence is enough before acting.
- **Does a bigger test always mean a better test?** Bigger tests are steadier, but they cost more, and a big test of the wrong people can still mislead. Scientists keep arguing about how to balance size and care.
- **Which stories do we keep telling because they are true, and which because we like them?** Historians and scientists are still sorting some well-known stories into what happened and what was added later. Nobody has checked every story.

## Connected maths
- In Nandini's coin test, 4 of her 20 sets had 7 or more heads. What fraction of her sets is that, and what is it as a percent? Chance alone gives about 17 in 100 sets. Is she close? **4 out of 20 is one fifth, which is 20 percent. She is close to 17 percent.**. 4 out of 20 is 4 divided by 20, which is 0.2, or 20 out of 100. That is 20 percent. Chance gives about 17 percent, so 20 percent is close. With only 20 sets, a few sets either way is normal. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A friend got 7 heads in 10 flips. Nandini got 103 heads in 200 flips. Write each as a percent of heads. Whose result is closer to half? **The friend: 70 percent. Nandini: 51.5 percent. Nandini is much closer to half.**. 7 out of 10 is 70 out of 100, so 70 percent. 103 out of 200 is 51.5 out of 100, so 51.5 percent. Half is 50 percent. 70 is 20 away from 50, and 51.5 is only 1.5 away. More flips pull the result close to half. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A run of four heads in a row has a chance of 1 in 16 from any starting flip, because one half times one half times one half times one half is one sixteenth. In 200 flips there are about 197 places a run could start. About how many runs of four heads should a fair coin make? **About 12 runs.**. 197 divided by 16 is about 12. So a fair coin is expected to make a dozen or so runs of four heads in 200 flips, with no wishing at all. Seeing one or two streaks is not a surprise. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- In Aditi's okra test, a pod 8 centimetres long had 36 seeds, and a pod 12 centimetres long had 60 seeds. How many seeds is that for each centimetre in each pod? About how many seeds would you guess for a pod 10 centimetres long? **4.5 seeds for each centimetre in the first pod, and 5 in the second. About 45 to 50 seeds for a pod of 10 centimetres.**. 36 divided by 8 is 4.5. 60 divided by 12 is 5. A 10 centimetre pod lies between the two, so 10 times 4.5 is 45 and 10 times 5 is 50. We can guess between 45 and 50. A real pod might still be an odd one. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- On the beach, the sunburn cases on the five sunny days were 13, 15, 14, 14 and 14. On the two cloudy days they were 1 and 2. What is the average on sunny days and on cloudy days? About how many times as many cases are there on a sunny day? **Sunny days: 14. Cloudy days: 1.5. About 9 times as many.**. 13 + 15 + 14 + 14 + 14 is 70, and 70 divided by 5 is 14. 1 + 2 is 3, and 3 divided by 2 is 1.5. 14 divided by 1.5 is about 9. So sunny days had about 9 times as many cases. The sun is the thing that changes the number of burns. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- Among the sunny days on the beach, one day had 120 golas and 14 sunburn cases. Another sunny day had no golas and 14 cases. How many more golas were sold on the first day? How many more sunburn cases? What does that say about golas? **120 more golas and 0 more cases. Golas do not change the sunburn on sunny days.**. 120 minus 0 is 120 more golas. 14 minus 14 is 0 more cases. So 120 extra golas brought no extra burns. If golas caused burns, the day with the most golas should have had more. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- On a street in a city, 20 houses used pump A and 20 houses used pump B. 6 houses using pump A and 1 house using pump B had an outbreak of illness. How many times as many houses with illness used pump A? Is that enough to be sure that pump A is the cause? **6 times as many. It is a strong hint but 20 houses each is small, so check with more houses.**. 6 divided by 1 is 6, so six times as many houses. With only 20 houses in each group, luck could play a part. A strong hint is a reason to test further, and also a reason to act with care while testing. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- On a chart that starts at 0, with 1 centimetre for each 10 points, bars for scores of 92 and 95 are 9.2 and 9.5 centimetres tall. On a chart that starts at 90, with 1 centimetre for each point, how tall are the two bars? How many times taller is the second bar? **2 centimetres and 5 centimetres. The second bar is 2.5 times taller.**. On the second chart, 92 minus 90 is 2, so the first bar is 2 centimetres. 95 minus 90 is 5, so the second bar is 5 centimetres. 5 divided by 2 is 2.5. A gap of only 3 points looks like a bar two and a half times as tall. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
