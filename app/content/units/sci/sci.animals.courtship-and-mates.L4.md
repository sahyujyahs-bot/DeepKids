# How animals choose partners: displays, songs, dances and nests
`sci.animals.courtship-and-mates.L4`

**Summary.** A peacock's long tail makes flying harder, so why does he have it? In this lesson you will watch pigeons at the window, test whether one change to a display changes what is chosen, test how a long tail slows flight, and play a call-and-answer game.

**Intro.** *A peacock's train is about one and a half metres long. A tiger can see the train from far away, and the train is heavy to carry. He sheds the train every year and grows a new one. Why does he grow something so costly?* The peacock grows his train because peahens choose. A peahen walks past several peacocks, watches them, and picks one to be the father of her chicks. The things animals do to find and win a partner are called courtship. A magpie robin sings. Two sarus cranes dance. A weaver bird builds a nest and waits for a female to inspect it. In this lesson you run a choosing test with paper trains. You also find out what a long tail costs a flyer.

## Terms
- **Courtship**: Everything an animal does to find and win a partner for having young. *e.g.* A male pigeon puffs up his neck, bows and coos in front of a female. That is courtship.
- **Mate**: An animal's partner for having young. To mate means to join with a partner so that young can begin. *e.g.* A peahen chooses one peacock as her mate. Later she lays eggs.
- **Species**: One kind of living being. Animals of the same species can have young together. *e.g.* A house crow and a jungle crow look alike, but they are two species.
- **Display**: A show of colour, shape or movement that one animal gives for another to see. *e.g.* A peacock raising and shaking his train is giving a display.
- **Train**: The very long feathers that grow from a peacock's back and cover his tail. They are not his tail feathers. *e.g.* A peacock's train has about 150 eyespots. His real tail is short and brown, and it holds the train up.
- **Ornament**: A body part that is used for showing, more than for eating, moving or keeping warm. *e.g.* A peacock's train, a rooster's comb and a male bullfrog's blue throat sacs are ornaments.
- **Song**: A long or repeated pattern of sounds that an animal gives, most often in the breeding season. *e.g.* A male magpie robin sings from the top of a pole at sunrise.
- **Duet**: A song or call that two partners give together, each one fitting their part to the other's. *e.g.* A pair of sarus cranes point their beaks at the sky and call together.
- **Courtship gift**: Food or another useful thing that one animal gives to a partner during courtship. *e.g.* A male kingfisher brings a fish and offers this gift to a female, head first.
- **Breeding season**: The part of the year when a species courts, mates and raises young. *e.g.* For many Indian frogs, the breeding season starts with the first heavy monsoon rain.
- **Lek**: A place where many males gather and display close together, and females come to choose among them. *e.g.* Blackbuck males gather on a lek in the grassland of Velavadar in Gujarat.
- **Pair bond**: The lasting tie between two animals who stay together to raise young. *e.g.* A pair of great hornbills may stay together for many years.

## Explanations
### Courtship: finding and winning a partner · *defined*
**Definition:** Courtship is everything an animal does to find and win a partner for having young.

Most animals have two parents. A new animal begins when two tiny cells join. One cell, the egg, comes from the mother. The other comes from the father. So before any young can begin, two animals must find each other and accept each other. Courtship does three jobs. It helps an animal find a partner of their own species. It shows that the animal is ready to breed now. It gives the other animal time to judge and to choose. Courtship can be a display of colour, a song, a dance, a nest or a gift. Many animals use several of these together. Humans are animals too. People also sing, dress up, dance and give gifts to someone they hope to marry.
- e.g. A male pigeon bows and coos in front of a female on a balcony.
- e.g. A male magpie robin sings from a rooftop every morning in the breeding season.
- e.g. A pair of sarus cranes bow and leap together in a wet field.
### Finding your own species · *defined*
**Definition:** Each species has their own courtship code: a pattern of calls, flashes, colours or moves that others of that species know.

A pond in the monsoon may hold five species of frogs, all calling at once. A female must find a male of her own species, because only then can her eggs grow into young. So each species of frog has a different call. One gives slow, deep calls. Another gives fast clicks. The female goes towards the pattern of her own species and passes the others by. Crickets do the same with their chirps, and fireflies do the same with flashes of light. Each firefly species flashes in a rhythm of their own. A code like this saves time and energy for both partners. It is also the first question in every courtship: are you my kind?
- e.g. Indian bullfrogs and small cricket frogs call in the same pond, with different calls.
- e.g. Two species of firefly in one field flash in different rhythms.
- e.g. A female cricket walks towards the chirp pattern of her own species.
### Displays of colour and shape · *defined*
**Definition:** A display is a show of colour, shape or movement that one animal gives for another to see.

A peacock lifts his train into a fan and turns to face a peahen. Then he shakes the train about 25 times each second, so that it rustles and the eyespots seem to shimmer. A male Indian bullfrog turns bright yellow after the first heavy monsoon rain. His two throat sacs turn deep blue. The colour lasts only a day or two. A male fan-throated lizard in Maharashtra flicks out a blue, black and orange throat fan. Many of these colours are shown only in the breeding season, or only for a moment. A peacock's blue and green are a special case. His feathers hold only a brown colouring. The tiniest parts of each feather are stacked in neat rows, far too small to see. These rows reflect blue and green light. This is called structural colour.
- e.g. A peacock raises his train and shakes it so that it rustles.
- e.g. A male Indian bullfrog turns yellow after the first heavy rain.
- e.g. A fan-throated lizard flicks out a bright throat fan on a stone.
### Songs and calls · *defined*
**Definition:** A song is a long or repeated pattern of sounds. In courtship, a song tells others who is singing, where they are and that they are ready to breed.

Sound travels round leaves and walls, and it works in the dark. So a song can reach a partner who cannot see the singer. A male magpie robin sings from a high perch at sunrise. His song tells females that he is there. It also tells other males that this place is taken. The male koel calls 'ku-oo, ku-oo' through the hot months. Male frogs and crickets call at night. Singing takes energy, and a bird who sings for an hour is not feeding for that hour. People once thought that only male birds sing. In 2014 a team led by Karan Odom checked songbirds across the world. They found that females sing in about seven out of ten of the species with good records. In some species the two partners sing a duet.
- e.g. A male magpie robin sings from the top of a coconut tree at dawn.
- e.g. A male koel calls 'ku-oo' from a mango tree in April.
- e.g. A pair of sarus cranes call together in a duet.
### Dances · *defined*
**Definition:** A courtship dance is a set of moves that an animal makes for a partner. Two partners may also dance together.

The sarus crane is the tallest flying bird in the world, about as tall as a grown woman. A pair of sarus cranes bow to each other, spread their wings and leap into the air. They toss grass and sticks. They also call together in a duet. Sarus pairs stay together for many years, and they go on dancing long after they first pair up. So a dance is not only for winning a partner. It also keeps a pair bond strong. Great crested grebes, who are water birds, face each other and shake their heads in turn. Each partner copies the other. Julian Huxley described this dance in 1914. He saw that the male and the female both display, and both choose.
- e.g. Two sarus cranes bow and leap together in a paddy field in Uttar Pradesh.
- e.g. Flamingos in the Rann of Kutch march side by side in a big group, turning their heads together.
- e.g. A pair of great crested grebes shake their heads at each other on a lake.
### Nests and gifts · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals court with something they have made or found: a nest, a decorated place or a gift of food.

A male baya weaver bird weaves a hanging nest from strips of grass or paddy leaf. When the nest is half built, he hangs under it, flaps his wings and calls. A female comes and inspects the nest and the place where it hangs. If she accepts, he finishes the nest and she lays her eggs inside. A male satin bowerbird in Australia builds two walls of sticks on the ground, called a bower. The bower is not a nest. He decorates the ground around it with blue things, such as feathers, flowers and bottle caps. A male white-spotted pufferfish, about 12 centimetres long, shapes a circle of ridges in the sand of the sea floor. The circle is about 2 metres wide and takes him about a week. A male kingfisher brings a fish to a female. A gift of food shows that the giver can find food.
- e.g. A male baya weaver hangs under his half-built nest and flaps his wings.
- e.g. A satin bowerbird lays blue bottle caps in front of his bower.
- e.g. A male kingfisher offers a small fish to a female.
### Who chooses, and what the chooser learns · *defined*
**Definition:** In courtship, at least one partner chooses. A chooser watches or listens, compares, and accepts or refuses.

In many species the female chooses. A peahen visits several peacocks before she accepts one. She will put a great deal into her eggs and chicks, so a poor choice costs her a lot. A display can tell a chooser several things. It shows that the other animal is of her species. A bright, full display is hard to grow or to keep up when an animal is ill or hungry. So it may also show good health. A well-made nest shows skill, and a gift shows that the giver can find food. In other species both partners choose, as sarus cranes and grebes do. In a few species the usual roles are swapped. A female greater painted-snipe is brighter than the male, and she courts him. He then sits on the eggs and looks after the chicks.
- e.g. A peahen walks away from two peacocks and stays near a third.
- e.g. A female baya weaver inspects several half-built nests before she accepts one.
- e.g. A bright female painted-snipe displays to a duller male.
### A display has a cost · *defined*
**Definition:** A cost is anything an animal gives up or risks. Displays cost energy, time and safety.

Growing a long train or a long tail takes food. Singing for hours takes energy, and the singer is not feeding in that time. A bright colour or a loud call can also be noticed by a hunter. Male tungara frogs in Central America add a 'chuck' sound to their call. Females prefer calls with chucks. Bats who eat frogs also find those calls more easily. So every display is a balance between being chosen and staying alive. Many animals lower the cost. A peacock folds his train when he is not displaying, and he sheds it after the breeding season. A fan-throated lizard keeps his throat fan folded away. A male baya weaver has a yellow head only in the breeding season.
- e.g. A male long-tailed widowbird in Kenya flies slowly, with a tail half a metre long.
- e.g. A calling frog is easier for a bat to find.
- e.g. A peacock sheds his whole train after the breeding season.
### Courtship follows the seasons · *defined*
**Definition:** The breeding season is the part of the year when a species courts, mates and raises young.

Young animals need a lot of food. So most animals court at a time that puts their young into the world when food is plentiful. Earth is tilted, and this gives us seasons with longer and shorter days. Many birds can sense the length of the day. When the days grow longer, their bodies make chemical messengers called hormones. The hormones start the singing and the nest building. In much of India the monsoon sets the time. Peacocks display most as the rains come. Frogs call after the first heavy rain, because their tadpoles need pools. Baya weavers build when the paddy and the grass are long and green. So the timing of a peacock's dance depends on the monsoon rains, and on the tilt of the Earth that gives us the seasons.
- e.g. Peacocks call and display most at the start of the monsoon.
- e.g. Bullfrogs gather to call after the first heavy rain.
- e.g. Baya weavers start to weave when the grass grows long.
### Many ways to pair and raise young · *defined*
**Definition:** Species differ in how many partners an animal has, how long partners stay together, and who looks after the young.

There is no single way that animals live together. Sarus cranes and great hornbills live as pairs for many years. A hornbill mother seals herself inside a tree hollow with her eggs. The father brings fruit to her and the chick for about four months. A peacock does no nest work at all. He displays, several peahens choose him, and each peahen raises her chicks alone. A female bronze-winged jacana keeps a patch of lake and may have several male partners there. Each male sits on a set of her eggs and cares for the chicks. On the island of Oahu in Hawaii, about one third of the Laysan albatross pairs are two females. The two females raise a chick together, year after year. Each of these ways works for the species that lives that way.
- e.g. A pair of great hornbills raise one chick together.
- e.g. A peahen raises her chicks without the peacock.
- e.g. A male bronze-winged jacana leads the chicks across lotus leaves.
### Why do choosers like what they like? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that animals choose partners, and that choosing over many generations has shaped trains, songs and dances. They disagree about why a chooser prefers one display to another.

A peahen prefers some peacocks to others. What is her preference about? There are three main answers, and working scientists hold each of them. More than one may be true at once.
- e.g. Peahens stay near some peacocks and walk away from others.
- e.g. Female widowbirds nest most with the males who have the longest tails.
- **Scientists who say a display is a report on health, such as Amotz Zahavi, William Hamilton and Marlene Zuk:** A big, bright display is costly. Only a healthy, well-fed animal can grow one and stay alive. So a chooser who picks the best display gets a healthy partner, and her young may be healthy too.
- **Scientists who say beauty is enough, such as Charles Darwin, Ronald Fisher and Richard Prum:** Animals feel pleasure in certain colours, sounds and moves. If most females like long tails, then males with long tails are chosen more often. Their sons inherit the long tails, and their daughters inherit the liking for them. So a liking can spread and grow, even if the tail says nothing about health.
- **Scientists who say the senses came first, such as Michael Ryan:** A chooser's eyes, ears and brain were already tuned to notice some things, such as the colour of ripe fruit. A display that matches what the senses already notice gets more attention.

## Mostly agreed
- Animals choose their partners, and in many species the female does most of the choosing. *How we know:* In 1982 Malte Andersson made the tails of some male widowbirds longer and others shorter. Females nested most with the males whose tails he had made longer.
- Each species has a courtship code of their own, which helps partners of the same species find each other. *How we know:* Scientists play recorded calls from a speaker. Female frogs and crickets walk towards the call of their own species and ignore the others.
- Choosing by partners, over many generations, has shaped ornaments such as long tails, bright colours and long songs. *How we know:* Chosen animals have more young, and the young inherit their parents' features. Charles Darwin set this idea out in 1871, and many tests since then have supported it.
- Displays have costs in energy, time and safety. *How we know:* Bats who eat frogs find calling frogs more easily. Michael Ryan and Merlin Tuttle showed this with tungara frogs in Panama in the 1980s.
- Females sing in many species of songbird. *How we know:* A study led by Karan Odom in 2014 checked the songbird species with good records. Females sang in about seven out of ten of them.
- Animals pair and raise young in many different ways, and no one way is the rule. *How we know:* Field studies have followed marked animals for years. They show lasting pairs in cranes and hornbills. They show peahens raising chicks alone, and jacana fathers raising chicks alone.

## How we found out
**Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in England, Malte Andersson of Sweden, working in Kenya, and Marion Petrie in England, Darwin's book The Descent of Man came out in 1871. Andersson published his widowbird test in 1982, and Petrie published her peafowl study in 1991..** In 1871 Charles Darwin wrote that female animals choose their partners. He said that long tails and bright colours grew over many generations because females preferred them. Most scientists of his time did not accept this. Even his friend Alfred Russel Wallace doubted that a bird could have such a taste. They said that bright colours only helped animals to know their own species. For about a hundred years almost nobody tested the idea. Then, in 1982, Malte Andersson went to the grasslands of Kenya. Male long-tailed widowbirds there have tails about half a metre long. He cut the tails of some males short. He glued the cut pieces on to other males, which made their tails longer. He left some males as they were. Then he counted the new nests on each male's patch of grass. The males with the longest tails had the most new nests. In 1991 Marion Petrie showed that peahens also choose among peacocks. Darwin had been right that females choose.  
*What people believed before:* That female animals have no preferences, and that bright colours only help animals to know their own species. Andersson changed the tails of widowbirds and showed that females nested most with the longest-tailed males.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A peahen chooses the peacock with the most eyespots.** (Marion Petrie and her team in England, in 1991 and 1994.; *debated*)  
  For: In their study, males with more eyespots won more mates. When they cut 20 eyespots from some trains, those males won fewer mates than the year before.  
  Against: Mariko Takahashi's team watched peafowl in Japan for seven years and found no such preference. Peacocks differ very little in eyespot number, so peahens may judge something else, such as the shaking or the call.
- **Only males display, and only females choose.** (Many older books about animals.; *rejected*)  
  For: In peafowl and many other species, the male has the ornament and the female chooses.  
  Against: Both partners display in cranes and grebes. Females sing in most songbird species. In painted-snipes and jacanas the female is the brighter one and she courts the male.
- **A peacock's long train makes flying much harder for him.** (A common belief, and some scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: The train is long and adds weight. Growing it each year takes food.  
  Against: In 2014 Graham Askew filmed peacocks taking off with and without their trains. They rose just as fast with the train. The train still has other costs, such as the food needed to grow it.
- **A costly display proves that an animal is healthy.** (Amotz Zahavi, who called this the handicap principle in 1975.; *debated*)  
  For: Sick or hungry animals often have duller colours and shorter songs. Alan Grafen showed in 1990 that the idea can work in mathematics.  
  Against: In 2020 Dustin Penn and Szabolcs Szamado argued that the idea is mistaken. They say that a signal can be honest without being wasteful.
- **Birds who pair for life never change partners.** (A common saying about swans, cranes and doves.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many pairs of cranes, albatrosses and hornbills do stay together for many years.  
  Against: Scientists who follow marked birds find that some pairs part, most often after a year when no chick was raised. A bird whose partner dies usually finds a new one.
- **A female weaver bird chooses the male who weaves best.** (A common belief about baya weavers.; *debated*)  
  For: Females inspect the half-built nests closely and pull at them.  
  Against: Suhel Quader studied baya weavers in India and published the work in 2006. He found that the place mattered more than the weaving. Females preferred nests hung high, on thorny trees and on thin branches.

## Roots
In this lesson you counted what choosers pick, and you found that a long tail shortens a paper bird's flight. Here we ask what choosing has made, and whether beauty is in the thing or in the one who looks.

**Deep truth.** In the Long Tail Test, the paper bird with the longest tail flew the shortest distance. A male long-tailed widowbird still grows a tail half a metre long. Female widowbirds choose long-tailed males, so those males have more young. So the choices of females have shaped the bodies of males, even where flying became harder.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think every part of a body is there to help its owner stay alive: legs to run, a beak to feed. A long tail does not help a widowbird stay alive. It is there because others of his kind preferred it.  
*Evidence:* In the Long Tail Test the 60 centimetre tail cut the flight of the paper bird. In 1982 Malte Andersson lengthened the tails of some male widowbirds in Kenya. Those males had the most new nests on their patches of grass.

- *who am i:* You have two parents, four grandparents and eight great-grandparents. Every one of your ancestors found a partner. If even one had not, you would not be here.
- *what is life:* Most animals cannot make young alone. Their kind of life goes on only where two beings find each other and accept each other.
- *what is this world:* Much of the colour and song around you was shaped by choosers. A peacock's train and a magpie robin's song exist because, over many generations, partners watched and listened and chose.

Two animals begin as separate beings, and often as strangers. In courtship each one pays close attention to the other. Then one cell from each joins, and a new being begins from the two. Many wise teachers taught that many beings come from one source. This lesson shows something that goes the other way. Two separate beings come together, and from the two a new life begins, again and again, in every generation.

- **Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda, sarga 2:** The poet Valmiki sees a pair of krauncha birds together by a river. A hunter shoots the male, and the female cries out. Valmiki's grief comes out as a verse, which tradition calls the first shloka. (Many scholars think the krauncha is the sarus crane. The poem takes the bond between two birds seriously. Field studies agree that sarus pairs stay together for many years. Whether a crane grieves as a person does cannot yet be measured.)
- **Kurunthokai 40, by Sembulappeyaneerar (Tamil Sangam poetry):** The poet asks: what is my mother to yours, and how did you and I ever meet? Yet, like red earth and pouring rain, our hearts have mixed. (The poem is about two people. It says plainly what happens in every pair: two beings who were strangers come to act together. Two sarus cranes calling in a duet show the same joining.)
- **Charles Darwin, The Descent of Man (1871), Part 2, the chapters on birds:** Darwin wrote that birds have a taste for the beautiful. He said that female birds, by choosing over a long time, had added to the beauty of the males. (This is the idea that the Eyespot Test and the widowbird test are about. Darwin gave other animals a sense of beauty at a time when most scientists gave it only to humans.)
- Science (settled): Almost every animal with two parents begins when two cells join, one from each parent. So each of these animals carries instructions from two beings.
- Science (debated): The scientist Richard Prum argues that birds feel pleasure in what they choose. He says this pleasure has shaped feathers and songs. Others say a display is only a report on health.
- Other view, Alfred Russel Wallace, and many biologists today: A female bird need not judge beauty at all. Her eyes and brain may simply respond more strongly to a bigger or brighter signal. She then goes to the strongest one. On this view, words such as taste and beauty should be kept for humans until there is evidence for more.
- Practice, Liking comes first (3 min): Put three leaves, three flowers or three pieces of cloth in front of you. Look at them quietly for one minute. Notice which one your eyes keep going back to. Do not ask why yet. Now point to that one. Try to say what drew you to it. Was the liking there before the reason?
- Ask: In the Eyespot Test, each chooser picked a train within a count of five. Did they know their reason before they pointed?
- Ask: Female widowbirds chose long tails, and now male widowbirds have them. Who made the long tail: the males or the females?
- Ask: A peahen, a tiger and you all look at the same peacock. Do you all see the same train?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Is a peacock's train beautiful by itself, or only when someone is looking at it? (hint: Think about who decides. A peahen, a tiger and a person each look at the same train.)
- *deep:* A peahen cannot tell us why she chose. How could we ever find out? (hint: In the Eyespot Test you changed one thing and counted choices. What else could a scientist change?)
- *deep:* If a display puts an animal in danger, why has the display not disappeared? (hint: An animal who lives long but is never chosen leaves no young.)
- *big:* Sarus cranes go on dancing together for years. What might the dance do for them? (hint: People who live together also keep doing small things for each other.)
- *big:* Animals pair and raise young in many ways. Is any one way the natural one? (hint: Compare hornbills, peafowl, jacanas and albatrosses. Each way works for that species.)
- *big:* Humans are animals who also sing, dress up and give gifts. How much of that is courtship? (hint: People also sing and dress up for friends, for festivals and for themselves.)

## Story: The Half-Built Nest
*Yazhini, her grandmother and the birds at their well are made up. These facts are real. Male baya weavers grow yellow heads in the breeding season. They weave hanging nests from strips of paddy leaf or grass, often over water. When a nest is half built, the male hangs under it, flaps his wings and calls. A female goes inside, pulls at it and looks around before she accepts or leaves. After she accepts, he adds the long entrance tube. What the female in the story is thinking is imagined.*

Paati pulled once, and Yazhini's box came apart.

It had taken Yazhini three evenings. Her grandmother sold palm-leaf boxes at the Friday market in Tirunelveli, and this year Yazhini had asked to learn. She had split the dry palmyra leaf into strips. She had woven the strips over and under until her fingers were sore. Now the bottom of her first box hung open like a mouth.

"It looked fine," Yazhini said. Her voice shook.

"It looked fine," Paati agreed. "But somebody will carry murukku home in it for her children, on a bus, on a bad road. Looking fine is where a box starts." She put the strips back in Yazhini's lap and went in to stir the kuzhambu.

Yazhini did not want to look at the strips. She walked to the old well at the edge of the paddy field, where nobody would ask her about boxes.

A palmyra tree leaned over the well. From its lowest leaves hung six green shapes that had not been there last week.

They were nests, but they were not finished. Each one looked like a small helmet with a strap across the bottom. They were woven from thin strips of paddy leaf, still fresh and green.

On the nearest one sat a bird the size of a sparrow, with a head as yellow as turmeric. He held a strip in his beak that was longer than his whole body. He pushed it under one strand and pulled it over the next. Under, over. Under, over.

He is doing what I do, Yazhini thought. And he has no fingers.

Then a small flock dropped out of the sky and landed along the leaves. These birds were brown and streaked, with no yellow at all. They were the females.

The six yellow-headed males seemed to go mad. Each one swung under his own half-built nest, beat his wings as fast as he could, and called, a wheezy chee-ee-ee. All six called at once. Yazhini laughed out loud.

One brown female flew to the nearest nest. The male beat his wings even harder. She did not look at him. She went inside.

The nest shook. Yazhini could see the female's beak through the weave. She pulled at one strand, then at another. She pushed her head against the wall. She leaned out and looked down at the dark water far below. She looked up at the thin leaf-tip that held the whole nest. She did not hurry. The male had stopped calling. He only hung there and waited.

Then she flew to the next nest.

"Oh," said Yazhini. She felt it in her stomach. She knew exactly how three evenings of weaving felt.

The male sat on his nest for a while. Then he dropped down to the paddy, bit the edge of a leaf and flew up, tearing a long strip free. He went back to his weaving. Under, over.

The female was inside the third nest now. She pulled. She pushed. She looked down, and she looked up.

Yazhini watched her and stopped feeling sorry for the first male. Where would this bird's eggs lie? In that small woven room, over a deep well, through weeks of wind and rain. Then the chicks would lie there, getting heavier every day. If one strand gave way, there would be nothing under them but water.

She is not asking whether the nest looks fine, Yazhini thought. She is asking whether it will hold.

The female stayed in the third nest. She came to the opening and sat there. The male of that nest stopped beating his wings. He sang a long, soft song, quite different from the wheeze. Then he flew down for another strip. By tomorrow he would be weaving the long tube that makes the doorway.

Yazhini ran all the way home. Paati was on the thinnai with a heap of leaf beside her.

"Paati! The weaver birds at the well. The female goes inside and pulls at the nest before she takes it. She pulls just like you!"

Paati laughed until she had to put down her knife. "Who do you think I learned it from? My own paati sent me to that well when I was your age. Those birds were weaving before anybody's grandmother was born."

Yazhini sat down and picked up her strips. They were only a little bent.

"Under, over," she said. "Tighter this time. And when it is done, Paati, you must pull."

"I will pull," said Paati.

"Pull hard," said Yazhini.

**For the parent:** Yazhini was hurt when her grandmother pulled her box apart. Then she saw a female weaver bird pull at a nest and leave. The female was checking whether the nest would keep her eggs and chicks safe over the water. A chooser tests the things that will matter to her young.

- Ask: Why did the female weaver look down at the water and up at the leaf-tip?
- Ask: The first male was refused. What did he do next, and why might that help him?
- Ask: What is the same about choosing a nest and testing a box before market day?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Eyespot Test: does one change alter what choosers pick? (30 min)
- **question:** Three paper trains are the same in every way except the number of spots: 4, 8 or 16. Do choosers pick one of them more often than the others? Or do they pick by where a train lies on the table?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which paper train will most choosers pick: the one with 4 spots, with 8 spots or with 16 spots? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three sheets of the same paper. Use dots of one size and one colour, and spread them evenly on each sheet. Only the number of dots differs. Bring the choosers in one at a time, so that nobody copies anyone else. Ask every chooser the same question in the same words. Give each one a slow count of five to look. Change the left-to-right order of the trains for every chooser. Do not tell the choosers what differs until the end.
- **record:** Make a table with one row for each chooser. Write the order of the trains, the train chosen and the place chosen. At the end, count the picks for each train and for each place.
- **results table:** Chooser | Order of the trains from left to right | Train chosen: 4, 8 or 16 spots | Place chosen: left, middle or right
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child the plan. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will make three paper trains with 4, 8 and 16 spots. Then we will ask people to choose one. Which train will most people pick?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which train most people will pick. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think choosers will pick that train? (good: More spots look richer.; Too many spots look messy.; People may not care about spots at all.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Nobody knows what your choosers will do, and that is why it is worth testing.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the three sheets the same way round. Stick 4 dots on the first, 8 on the second and 16 on the third. Spread the dots evenly over each sheet. Write a small 4, 8 or 16 on the back.  
  *Parent says:* These are our three trains. They must be the same in every way except one. What is the one thing that differs?  
  *Child mode:* Stick 4 dots on one sheet. Stick 8 on the next. Stick 16 on the last.
  - Note: If you draw the dots, use one pen and draw round the same coin each time. A dot of another size or colour would be a second change.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the trains in a row. Bring in one chooser. Say the question below, and count slowly to five. Record the order, the train chosen and the place. Change the order. Repeat with at least six choosers.  
  *Parent says:* Please look at these three paper trains for a count of five. Which one looks best to you? Just point to it.  
  *Child mode:* Bring in one chooser. Ask which train they pick. Write it down. Change the order.
  - Note: For relatives on the phone, take three photos with the trains in three different orders. Send a different photo to each person. Ask them to reply with left, middle or right.
  - Check: Why do you change the order of the trains for each chooser in the Eyespot Test? → **So the place does not sway the result**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the picks for each train. Then count the picks for each place: left, middle and right. Write both counts down.  
  *Parent says:* Count how many choosers picked each train. Now count how many picked the left, the middle and the right. Which count is more uneven?  
  *Child mode:* Count the picks for each train. Then count the picks for each place.
  - Note: With 6 choosers, luck alone would give about 2 picks to each train. A count of 4 or more for one train is worth noticing. A count of 3 is not.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for the three things below. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Did the number of spots change the choices? How sure are you, with this many choosers?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: How could you become more sure of your result? (good: Ask more choosers.; Run the test again on another day.; Check that the place on the table did not decide.)
  - Ask: You tested people. What would you need to do to test peahens? (good: Change real trains and watch what peahens do.; Count which peacocks the peahens stay with.)
  - Note: There is no right result here. In many homes the 16-spot train gets the most picks, and in some the 8-spot train does. Write down what you found. The explanation: you changed one thing and counted choices. Scientists must work in the same way, because a peahen cannot say what she prefers. In 1994 Marion Petrie and Tim Halliday cut 20 eyespots from the trains of some peacocks. Those males won fewer mates than they had the year before. A later study in Japan did not find that peahens prefer more eyespots, so the question is still open.
  - Think: If you took 8 dots off the 16-spot train, would the same choosers still pick it?
### e2 · The Long Tail Test: does a long tail make flying harder? (30 min)
- **question:** A paper bird is thrown with no tail, with a 30 centimetre tail and with a 60 centimetre tail. Does the length of the tail change how far the paper bird flies?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will fly farthest: the paper bird with no tail, with the short tail or with the long tail? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use one paper bird for the whole test, so that the folds are the same. The same person throws every time, from the same line, with the same gentle push. Throw five times for each tail, because one throw can be a fluke. Use strips of the same newspaper and the same width. Only the length of the tail changes. At the end, throw three more times with no tail. This checks that the paper bird has not become worn out.
- **record:** Make a table with three rows: no tail, 30 centimetre tail and 60 centimetre tail. Write the five distances in each row. Then write the middle distance for each row.
- **results table:** Tail | Throw 1 | Throw 2 | Throw 3 | Throw 4 | Throw 5 | Middle distance
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the paper and the newspaper strips. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will throw a paper bird with no tail, with a short tail and with a long tail. Which will fly farthest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which paper bird will fly farthest: no tail, short tail or long tail? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will fly farthest? (good: A tail keeps a kite steady, so it may help.; A tail adds weight.; The tail will drag in the air.)
  - Note: Many children expect the tail to help, because a kite flies better with a tail. Do not correct this. The test will answer it.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fold the sheet in half the long way and open it. Fold the two top corners in to the middle line. Fold the two slanted edges in to the middle line. Fold the whole shape in half. Fold each wing down. Cut three newspaper strips. Throw three practice flights.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the paper bird under the middle, level with your shoulder. Push the paper bird forward gently, straight ahead. Try to throw the same way every time.  
  *Child mode:* Fold the paper bird. Cut three strips of newspaper. Practise three gentle throws.
  - Note: A gentle push is easier to repeat than a hard throw. If the paper bird hits the far wall, throw more softly or step further back.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the start line. Your child throws the paper bird five times with no tail. Measure from the line to the place where the nose comes to rest. Record each distance.  
  *Parent says:* Stand behind the line. Throw five times with no tail. After each throw, we measure and write the distance.  
  *Child mode:* Throw five times with no tail. Measure each flight. Write it down.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape one strip flat under the back end of the paper bird, so that 30 centimetres trail behind. Throw five times from the same line. Record each distance.  
  *Parent says:* Now the paper bird has a tail 30 centimetres long. Throw the same way as before. Watch how the bird moves in the air.  
  *Child mode:* Tape on one strip as a tail. Throw five times. Measure each flight.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the other two strips end to end. Replace the short tail with this 60 centimetre tail. Throw five times and record. Then take the tail off and throw three more times with no tail.  
  *Parent says:* Now the tail is 60 centimetres long. Throw five times. Then we take the tail off and throw three more times, as a check.  
  *Child mode:* Make the tail twice as long. Throw five times. Then throw three times with no tail.
  - Check: In the Long Tail Test, why do you throw again with no tail at the end? → **To check the paper bird still flies well**
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each row, put the five distances in order and circle the middle one. Then ask your child for the three things below. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note.  
  *Parent says:* Find the middle distance for each tail. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Find the middle distance in each row. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What did the long tail do to the flight? (good: The paper bird slowed down sooner.; The tail fluttered and dragged.; The paper bird dropped earlier.)
  - Ask: A male widowbird's tail is half a metre long. What might the tail cost him? (good: He flies more slowly.; He uses more energy to fly.; A hunter may catch him more easily.)
  - Note: In most homes the paper bird with no tail flies farthest. The 60 centimetre tail cuts the flight to about half or less. Write down what you really found. The explanation: a paper bird has to push air aside while flying. A long strip has a large surface that rubs against the air and flutters. This rubbing is called drag, and it slows the paper bird down. A slower paper bird drops sooner. A real bird with a very long tail must work harder to fly. If the tail made little difference in your Long Tail Test, cut wider strips, about 5 centimetres, and try again.
  - Think: A long tail makes flying harder. So why do male widowbirds still grow one?

## Challenges
### c1 · Open and Shut: build a display that you can hide (20 min)
- **problem:** A fan-throated lizard must be noticed by a partner, and must not be noticed by a hawk. He keeps a bright throat fan folded away and flicks it out for a moment. Your problem is like his. Build a paper display that a watcher five steps away sees clearly when it is open. When it is shut, the watcher must not find it against a plain bedsheet.
- **your turn:** Now design your own. Plan it first, by drawing it or telling your grown-up. Test it six times: three open and three shut, in a mixed-up order. Write how many times the watcher saw it when it was open, and how many times when it was shut.
- **constraints:** Use only paper, colours and tape.; It must open in two seconds and shut in two seconds, with one hand.; When shut, it must lie flat against a plain bedsheet or a plain wall.; The watcher stands five big steps away.; The watcher gets a count of three to look each time.

  *Child mode:* Name one easy-to-see thing. Name one hard-to-see thing.
  - Ask: What makes a thing easy to see from far away? (good: A colour that differs from the background.; A big size.; Movement.)
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child plans a design, by drawing it or by telling you, and then builds it. Check that it opens and shuts with one hand.  
  *Parent says:* Plan your idea first, by drawing it or telling me. Then build it. Show me that it opens in two seconds and shuts in two seconds.  
  *Child mode:* Plan it. Build it. Open it and shut it with one hand.

## Worksheets
### Courtship: who displays and who chooses (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is courtship?  
   Options: Building a home for the winter / Fighting over food / Teaching chicks to fly / Finding and winning a partner for having young  
   **Answer:** Finding and winning a partner for having young. *Why:* Courtship is everything an animal does to find and win a partner for having young. It can be a display, a song, a dance, a nest or a gift.
2. (mcq) Why does each species of cricket have a chirp pattern of their own?  
   Options: So crickets stay awake / So hunters can hear them / So the chirps sound louder / So partners of the same species meet  
   **Answer:** So partners of the same species meet. *Why:* A female cricket walks towards the pattern of her own species. A pattern shared with another species would lead her to the wrong partner.
3. (fill) A show of colour, shape or movement that one animal gives for another to see is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['display']. *Why:* A display is made for the eyes. A peacock's raised train and a lizard's throat fan are displays.
4. (truefalse) The long feathers that a peacock raises are his tail feathers.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The long feathers are called the train, and they grow from his back. His real tail is short and brown, and it props the train up.
5. (match) Match each animal to their courtship.  
   **Answer:** Peacock → raises and shakes a train; Magpie robin → sings from a high perch; Sarus cranes → bow and leap together; Baya weaver → weaves a hanging nest; Kingfisher → gives a fish. *Why:* These are five kinds of courtship: a display, a song, a dance, a nest and a gift. Many animals use more than one kind.
6. (sort) Sort these courtship signals. Is each one for the eyes or for the ears?  
   **Answer:** a peacock's train → For the eyes; a koel's call → For the ears; a bullfrog's yellow skin → For the eyes; a cricket's chirp → For the ears; a lizard's throat fan → For the eyes; a magpie robin's song → For the ears. *Why:* Colours and movements are seen, so they need light and a clear view. Calls and songs are heard, so they also work at night and among leaves.
7. (mcq) A peahen walks past three displaying peacocks and stays with one. Who chose?  
   Options: The peahen / The three peacocks / The oldest peacock / Nobody chose  
   **Answer:** The peahen. *Why:* The peacocks all displayed. The peahen compared them and stayed with one. In peafowl the female does most of the choosing.
8. (truefalse) In every species, only the male displays.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both partners dance in sarus cranes and grebes. In the greater painted-snipe the female is the brighter one, and she courts the male.
9. (order) Put these steps of a baya weaver's courtship in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The male weaves half a nest', 'He hangs under the nest and flaps his wings', 'A female goes inside and inspects the nest', 'She accepts the nest', 'He weaves the entrance tube', 'She lays her eggs inside']. *Why:* The male builds first and displays at the half-built nest. The female inspects before she accepts. The nest is finished only after she has chosen it.
10. (fill) The part of the year when a species courts, mates and raises young is called the ____ season.  
   **Answer:** ['breeding']. *Why:* Most animals have a breeding season. It puts their young into the world when food is plentiful.
11. (mcq) In the Long Tail Test, which paper bird usually flies the shortest distance?  
   Options: The one with no tail / The one with the longest tail / The one with the short tail / All fly the same distance  
   **Answer:** The one with the longest tail. *Why:* A long strip drags against the air and slows the paper bird, so the bird drops sooner. If you chose 'no tail', look at your results table again.
12. (write) Give two things that a chooser can learn from a courtship display.  
   **Answer:** One: the other animal is of her own species. Two: the other animal is healthy, or skilful, or good at finding food.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two different things. Species, readiness to breed, health, skill and the ability to find food are all correct.
13. (write) In the Eyespot Test, why must the three paper trains differ in only one way?  
   **Answer:** If the trains differed in two ways, I could not tell which difference changed the choices. With one difference, any change in the choices comes from the spots.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a second difference would be a second possible reason for the result.
14. (draw) Draw a peacock displaying to a peahen. Label the train, one eyespot and the chooser.  
   **Answer:** A peacock with his train raised in a fan, facing a peahen. Labels: 'train' on the fan, 'eyespot' on one round mark, and 'chooser' on the peahen.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the peahen as the chooser. The train should rise from the back, not from the tail tip.
15. (mcq) Which of these animals stay together as a pair for many years?  
   Options: Sarus cranes / A peacock and a peahen / Fireflies / Bullfrogs  
   **Answer:** Sarus cranes. *Why:* A pair of sarus cranes may stay together for many years and raise chicks together. A peacock and a peahen part after mating, and she raises the chicks alone.
### Costs, seasons and many ways to pair (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A male bullfrog is bright yellow for only a day or two. Why not all year?  
   Options: The yellow washes off in rain / Females dislike yellow / Yellow skin feels too warm / Bright skin is easy for hunters to see  
   **Answer:** Bright skin is easy for hunters to see. *Why:* A bright colour is noticed by partners and by hunters. Showing the colour only at breeding time keeps the cost low.
2. (mcq) Four species of frogs call in one pond. How does a female find her own species?  
   Options: By the loudest call / By the nearest frog / By the pattern of the call / By luck  
   **Answer:** By the pattern of the call. *Why:* Each species has a call pattern of their own. She goes towards the pattern of her species, even if another call is louder or nearer.
3. (mcq) A peacock sheds his train after the breeding season. What does he gain?  
   Options: Less to carry, and harder to see / A warmer body / A louder call / Sharper claws  
   **Answer:** Less to carry, and harder to see. *Why:* The train is needed only for courtship. Without the train he has less to carry and is harder for a hunter to notice.
4. (mcq) Why do many Indian frogs start courtship after the first heavy rain?  
   Options: Rain makes calls louder / Frogs dislike sunshine / Rain washes hunters away / Their tadpoles need pools of water  
   **Answer:** Their tadpoles need pools of water. *Why:* Frog eggs and tadpoles need water. Courtship that starts with the rain puts the tadpoles into full pools.
5. (truefalse) Female birds never sing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A study in 2014 found that females sing in about seven out of ten songbird species with good records. In some species partners sing duets.
6. (truefalse) A satin bowerbird's bower is the nest where the eggs are laid.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The bower is a place for courtship only. After choosing a male, the female builds her own nest somewhere else.
7. (sort) Sort these animals by who looks after the young.  
   **Answer:** great hornbill → Both parents; sarus crane → Both parents; peafowl → Mother alone; bronze-winged jacana → Father alone; greater painted-snipe → Father alone. *Why:* Animals raise young in many ways. In hornbills and cranes the pair shares the work. A peahen works alone. In jacanas and painted-snipes the father does the caring.
8. (odd) Three of these are made or brought for courtship. Which one grows on the body?  
   Options: A kingfisher's fish / A weaver's nest / A bowerbird's blue collection / A peacock's train  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The fish, the nest and the collection are found or made. The train is an ornament, a part of the peacock's own body.
9. (count) A peacock shakes his train 25 times each second. How many times does he shake it in 4 seconds?  
   **Answer:** 100. *Why:* 25 shakes each second for 4 seconds is 25 times 4, which is 100 shakes.
10. (order) Put these steps in order, to test whether female birds prefer long tails.  
   **Answer:** ['Catch some males of one species', 'Make some tails longer and some shorter', 'Leave some tails unchanged', 'Let the males go on their own patches', 'Count the new nests on each patch', 'Compare the counts']. *Why:* The tails are changed first. The counting comes after the females have had time to choose. Malte Andersson tested widowbirds in this way in Kenya.
11. (write) A frog who calls loudly is found by more females and by more bats. Why does he still call?  
   **Answer:** A frog who stays silent is safe from bats, but females cannot find him, so he has no young. A frog who calls takes a risk and may have young.. *Why:* A strong answer weighs the gain against the cost. Being chosen matters as much as staying alive, because only chosen animals leave young.
12. (write) Six choosers picked: 16 spots three times, 8 spots twice, 4 spots once. Is this strong evidence? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No, it is weak evidence. Luck alone would give about 2 picks to each train, and 3 is only one more. I would need many more choosers.. *Why:* A strong answer compares the counts with what luck would give, and asks for more choosers.
13. (write) Sarus cranes dance together even after years as a pair. Give one reason why this may help them.  
   **Answer:** Dancing together may keep the pair bond strong. The two birds must share the work of guarding the nest and feeding the chick.. *Why:* A strong answer links the dance to working together as a pair. Any sensible reason is accepted.
14. (perspective) A peahen stays with the peacock who has the fullest train. Scientists explain her choice in different ways.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that a costly display is hard for a sick animal to keep up. One test is to check whether the chicks of chosen males grow better.. *Why:* Working scientists hold each of these views, and more than one may be true. A strong answer gives a reason and suggests a test.
15. (label) Label three parts of this half-built baya weaver nest.  
   **Answer:** ['The thin branch: a snake struggles to climb along a thin branch', 'The woven room: it must hold the eggs and the chicks', 'The opening: the female goes in here to inspect the nest']. *Why:* A female weaver inspects the nest and the place where it hangs. Each part matters for the safety of her eggs.
### Testing what choosers prefer (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A scientist changes the tails of some widowbirds and leaves others unchanged. Why leave some unchanged?  
   Options: The glue ran out / To compare them with the changed birds / Those birds were ill / To save time  
   **Answer:** To compare them with the changed birds. *Why:* The unchanged birds show what happens with no change at all. Without them, nobody could tell whether the changed tails made a difference.
2. (mcq) A scientist cuts a tail and glues the same piece back on. What does this check?  
   Options: Whether the glue is strong / Whether the bird can fly / Whether cutting and gluing alone changes the result / The colour of the tail  
   **Answer:** Whether cutting and gluing alone changes the result. *Why:* Catching, cutting and gluing might upset a bird. A bird with the same tail length after cutting shows whether the handling itself matters.
3. (mcq) Suppose hunters became very common. What would probably happen to displays over many generations?  
   Options: They would become bigger / They would stay the same / They would become louder / They would become smaller or duller  
   **Answer:** They would become smaller or duller. *Why:* With more hunters, the showiest animals are caught more often and leave fewer young. John Endler found duller male guppies in streams with more fish-eating fishes.
4. (mcq) In jacanas the father raises the chicks. Which partner is larger and guards the patch of lake?  
   Options: The male / The female / Neither of them / The oldest chick  
   **Answer:** The female. *Why:* In jacanas the usual roles are swapped. The female is larger, guards the patch and may have several male partners. Each male cares for a set of eggs.
5. (truefalse) If most choosers pick the same paper train, they must all have the same reason.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A count shows what was chosen. It does not show why. One chooser may like many spots, and another may like a train that looks full.
6. (truefalse) A display that is hard to keep up when ill can tell a chooser about health.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. If only a healthy animal can sing for hours or grow bright feathers, then the display carries news about health.
7. (match) Match each courtship to what it may show a chooser.  
   **Answer:** A gift of fish → the giver can find food; A nest in a safe place → the builder chooses places well; A long song at dawn → the singer has energy to spare; The right call pattern → the caller is of her species; A big collection of rare things → the collector searches and guards well. *Why:* Each kind of courtship gives a chooser a different kind of news. Scientists still argue about how much choosers use this news.
8. (count) Twelve choosers each pick one of three paper trains. By luck alone, about how many picks would each train get?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 12 picks shared equally among 3 trains is 12 divided by 3, which is 4. A train with many more than 4 picks is worth noticing.
9. (fill) A place where many males display close together, and females come to choose, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lek']. *Why:* Peacocks and blackbuck display on leks. A female can compare many males in one visit.
10. (write) The Eyespot Test used human choosers. Why can its result not tell us what peahens prefer?  
   **Answer:** People and peahens are different species with different eyes and brains. To learn what peahens prefer, a scientist must change real trains and count what peahens do.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a result about one kind of chooser cannot be passed to another kind. The method can be shared, but the result cannot.
11. (write) In the Long Tail Test, the last no-tail throws were much shorter than the first ones. What should you do?  
   **Answer:** The paper bird has probably become bent or loose. I should fold a fresh paper bird and run the whole test again.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that the paper bird changed during the test. Then the tail was not the only thing that changed.
12. (write) On one island, many albatross pairs are two females who raise a chick together. What does this show?  
   **Answer:** It shows that animals pair in more than one way. Two birds who share the work can raise a chick, whoever they are.. *Why:* A strong answer says that there is no single pattern of pairing in nature. Raising a chick depends on shared work.
13. (write) A bird lives in thick, dark forest. Which would work better for courtship there: colour or song? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Song would work better. Leaves block the view and there is little light, so colours are hard to see. Sound travels round leaves and works in dim light.. *Why:* A strong answer links the kind of courtship to the place. A child may also suggest a colour shown in a patch of sunlight, with a reason.
14. (perspective) A pair of sarus cranes nests in a farmer's wet paddy field every year.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that a pair has one breeding season each year, so a lost nest is a lost year. Another is that the farmer's loss is real and should be shared.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses the fact that a pair breeds once a year, and takes the farmer's loss seriously.
15. (draw) Invent a courtship signal for an animal in muddy water. Draw the signal and name the sense used.  
   **Answer:** Any signal that does not need a clear view. A call, a drumming on the river bed, a smell, a touch or an electric pulse would all work. The drawing names the sense.. *Why:* A strong drawing chooses a sense that works in muddy water. A colour display is not a good answer, because colours are hard to see in mud.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is courtship for?  
   Options: Keeping warm at night / Finding and winning a partner for having young / Finding water in summer / Scaring away every other animal  
   **Answer:** Finding and winning a partner for having young. *Why:* Courtship brings two animals of one species together and gives each a chance to choose. A song may also warn rivals, but its main work in courtship is to win a partner.
2. (mcq) Each firefly species flashes in a rhythm of their own. What does the rhythm tell another firefly?  
   Options: What time it is / Where the food is / That rain is coming / Which species is flashing  
   **Answer:** Which species is flashing. *Why:* The rhythm is the code of the species. A firefly answers only the rhythm of their own species.
3. (mcq) Which of these is a courtship dance?  
   Options: A crow carrying a twig / Two sarus cranes bowing and leaping / A dog chasing a ball / A cat washing her face  
   **Answer:** Two sarus cranes bowing and leaping. *Why:* Sarus cranes bow, leap and spread their wings together. The dance helps to form the pair and to keep the pair bond strong.
4. (mcq) A male kingfisher brings a fish to a female. What can the gift show her?  
   Options: He is not hungry / He dislikes fish / He can find food / The river is deep  
   **Answer:** He can find food. *Why:* A partner who can catch fish now can probably catch fish for the chicks later. A gift is news about a skill.
5. (mcq) In peafowl, who usually does the choosing?  
   Options: The peacock / The chicks / The oldest bird / The peahen  
   **Answer:** The peahen. *Why:* Peacocks display, and each peahen compares several of them before she accepts one.
6. (mcq) You want to know whether spots change what choosers pick. What must you do?  
   Options: Change only the number of spots / Change the spots and the paper colour / Ask only one chooser / Tell choosers which to pick  
   **Answer:** Change only the number of spots. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing. If two things differ, you cannot tell which one changed the choices. One chooser is too few to show a pattern.
7. (mcq) Why does a long newspaper tail shorten a paper bird's flight?  
   Options: The tail makes the paper bird lighter / The tail pushes the paper bird forward / The tail drags against the air / The tail hides the paper bird  
   **Answer:** The tail drags against the air. *Why:* A long strip has a large surface that rubs against the air and flutters. This drag slows the paper bird, and a slower paper bird drops sooner.
8. (mcq) Which of these is a cost of a loud courtship call?  
   Options: The caller grows taller / The pond dries up / A hunter can find the caller / Partners hear the call better  
   **Answer:** A hunter can find the caller. *Why:* A loud call reaches partners and hunters alike. Bats who eat frogs find calling frogs more easily. Being heard by partners is the gain, not the cost.
9. (mcq) Which of these animals raise their young as a lasting pair?  
   Options: Great hornbills / Peafowl / Bullfrogs / Fireflies  
   **Answer:** Great hornbills. *Why:* A hornbill father feeds the mother and the chick for months, and the pair may stay together for years. A peahen raises her chicks alone.
10. (mcq) A scientist wants to know whether female birds prefer long tails. What is the best test?  
   Options: Lengthen some tails, shorten others, count nests / Watch one bird for an hour / Measure the longest tail / Ask a bird keeper  
   **Answer:** Lengthen some tails, shorten others, count nests. *Why:* Changing the tails and counting what females do is a fair test. Watching one bird, or measuring one tail, cannot show a preference.
11. (mcq) Which sentence about choosing is correct?  
   Options: Only males ever choose / In some species both partners choose / Only humans choose partners / Choosing happens only in birds  
   **Answer:** In some species both partners choose. *Why:* Females choose in many species, and in cranes and grebes both partners choose. Frogs, fishes, insects and mammals choose too. Humans are one choosing animal among many.
12. (mcq) Many birds begin courtship when the days grow longer. Why does the timing matter?  
   Options: Long days are quieter / Chicks hatch when food is plentiful / Feathers grow only in summer / Hunters sleep longer  
   **Answer:** Chicks hatch when food is plentiful. *Why:* Chicks need a great deal of food. Courtship at the right time of year puts the chicks into the world when insects, seeds or fruit are plentiful.

## Applications
- People who protect rare animals let the animals choose their partners where they can. In a 2015 study, giant pandas paired with a partner they preferred had more cubs.
- Bird counters listen for songs. Each species sings a pattern of their own. So a trained listener can count the species in a forest without seeing one bird.
- Each species has a song pattern of their own. So phone apps can now name a bird from a recording of the song.
- Bright lights at night hide the flashes of fireflies, and partners cannot find each other. Some forest camps switch off their lights in the firefly season.
- Some farmers in Uttar Pradesh leave a sarus crane nest alone in their paddy field. This helps the same pair to raise a chick year after year.
- A peacock feather makes blue and green with tiny stacked parts and only a brown colouring. Scientists copy this to make colours that never fade.

## Open questions
- **What exactly does a peahen judge when she chooses a peacock?** One team found that eyespot number matters, and another team found that it does not. She may judge the shaking, the call, the colours or all of them together.
- **Do other animals find some things beautiful?** Charles Darwin thought that birds have a taste for the beautiful. We can count what an animal chooses, but nobody has yet found a way to measure what the animal feels.
- **Why do birds sing most at dawn?** Scientists have several explanations: still air, poor light for feeding, and showing strength after the night. They do not yet agree on one.
- **What does a female pufferfish judge in a sand circle?** The circle was linked to the pufferfish only in 2011. Scientists do not yet know which part of the circle makes a female accept the male.
- **Do animals who pair for years feel attached to each other, as people do?** In prairie voles, who live in pairs, the pair bond needs the same body chemicals that help people to bond. What the voles feel cannot yet be measured.

## Connected maths
- In the Long Tail Test, five throws with no tail went 9, 7, 8, 3 and 8 floor tiles. What is the middle distance? Why is the middle a fairer number to use than the shortest throw? **The middle distance is 8 tiles. The throw of 3 tiles was a bad throw, and the middle value is not pulled down by it.**. Put the distances in order: 3, 7, 8, 8, 9. The third of the five numbers is the middle one, which is 8. One bad throw does not change the middle. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- With no tail, the middle flight was 8 floor tiles. With the 60 centimetre tail, it was 4 tiles. What fraction of the flight was left? Each tile is 60 centimetres wide. How many metres shorter was the flight? **One half of the flight was left. The flight was 2 metres 40 centimetres shorter.**. 4 out of 8 is one half. The flight was 4 tiles shorter, and 4 times 60 centimetres is 240 centimetres. That is 2 metres 40 centimetres. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the Eyespot Test, 12 choosers each picked one of three trains. The 16-spot train got 7 picks, the 8-spot train got 3 and the 4-spot train got 2. If choosers picked by luck alone, about how many picks would each train get? Is 7 far from that? **About 4 picks each. 7 is nearly twice as many as 4, so the spots probably did change the choices.**. 12 divided by 3 is 4. Luck would give counts near 4, such as 3, 4 and 5. A count of 7 beside counts of 3 and 2 is a big gap. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- Hawk Overhead has 24 cards, and 6 are hawks. What is the chance that the first card is a hawk? You turn over three display cards. Now 21 cards are left, with 6 hawks. Has the risk of a hawk on the next card gone up or down? **The chance is 6 out of 24, which is one quarter. After three display cards the chance is 6 out of 21, so the risk has gone up.**. 6 out of 24 is the same as 1 out of 4. With 21 cards left, the 6 hawks make up a bigger share of the pile. 6 out of 21 is more than 6 out of 24. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- In Pigeon Watch, a watcher counted 20 bows. The other pigeon stayed 2 times. What fraction of the bows were accepted? If the male bows 50 times in a morning, about how many times would the other pigeon stay? **One tenth of the bows were accepted. Out of 50 bows, about 5.**. 2 out of 20 is the same as 1 out of 10. One tenth of 50 is 5. The real number will vary from morning to morning. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A male white-spotted pufferfish is 12 centimetres long. His sand circle is 200 centimetres wide. About how many times his own length is the circle? A child is 130 centimetres tall. How wide would a circle be that is the same number of times her height? **The circle is about 17 times his length. For the child, the circle would be about 22 metres wide.**. 200 divided by 12 is 16 with 8 left over, so it is nearly 17. 130 times 17 is 2,210 centimetres, which is about 22 metres. That is as wide as two buses parked end to end. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is courtship? → Everything an animal does to find and win a partner for having young.
- Name four kinds of courtship. → Displays, songs, dances, and nests or gifts.
- In peafowl, who does most of the choosing? → The peahen.
- Why does each species of frog have a call of their own? → So that partners of the same species find each other.
- Name one cost of a display. → A hunter may notice it. It also takes energy and time.
- What is a lek? → A place where many males display together and females come to choose.
- Name a bird pair who dance together and stay together for years. → Sarus cranes.
- How can a scientist find out what a female bird prefers? → By changing one thing, such as tail length, and counting what the females do.
