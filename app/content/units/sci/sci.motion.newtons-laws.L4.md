# Newton's second law: why the same push moves a light cart fast and a heavy cart slowly
`sci.motion.newtons-laws.L4`

**Summary.** Pull a toy cart with a small bag of water, and it creeps. Pull it with a bag four times as heavy, and it shoots across the table. Now put a full bottle on the cart, and the same pull moves it much more slowly. A bigger force changes motion more. A bigger mass changes it less. Isaac Newton wrote this as his second law of motion in 1687. It explains why a loaded lorry needs a longer road to stop than a cycle, why a hammer and a feather fall together where there is no air, why you pull your hands back to catch a ball, and why a rocket speeds up faster as it burns its fuel. Scientists still test the law in far-off galaxies, and they still argue about what a force really is.

**Intro.** *Give a toy cart one small tug, and it rolls a little way. Give it a tug four times as big, and it zooms. Now strap a full water bottle on the cart and tug as before. It hardly moves. The cart, the floor and your hand are the same. What made the difference, and can you work out how much?* The size of the tug changed how quickly the cart's motion changed. The load changed it too, in the opposite way. In this unit you measure both with a cart pulled by a hanging bag of water. Then you find the rule Isaac Newton wrote in 1687, called his second law of motion. You use it to see why a heavy lorry needs a long road to stop, why a feather and a hammer fall together on the Moon, why soft hands catch a ball gently, and why a rocket speeds up faster as it gets lighter.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. A force can start a thing moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down or turn it. *e.g.* Your hand pulling a toy cart along a table is a force.
- **Mass**: How much matter a thing has. More mass means the thing is harder to start, stop or turn. *e.g.* A full water bottle has more mass than an empty one, so it is harder to get moving.
- **Weight**: The pull of the Earth on a thing. Weight is a force. Mass is not a force. *e.g.* On the Moon, a bottle would weigh about 6 times less than on Earth, but it would still have the same mass.
- **Kilogram**: The unit scientists use for mass. A litre of water has a mass of about 1 kilogram. *e.g.* A 1 litre bottle of water has a mass of about 1 kilogram.
- **Newton**: The unit scientists use for force, written N. The Earth pulls on a small apple, about 100 grams, with a force of about 1 newton. *e.g.* Holding a small apple in your hand, you push up on it with about 1 newton.
- **Speed**: How far a thing moves in a certain time, such as metres every second. *e.g.* A cycle that covers 4 metres every second has a speed of 4 metres per second.
- **Acceleration**: How quickly a thing's speed or direction changes. A cart that goes from 0 to 2 metres per second in 2 seconds gains 1 metre per second every second. *e.g.* An auto pulling away from a signal has acceleration. So has a bus that is braking.
- **Change in motion**: Any change in a thing's speed or direction. Starting, stopping, speeding up, slowing down and turning are all changes in motion. *e.g.* A bus that turns a corner changes its motion even if its speed stays the same.
- **Net force**: All the forces on a thing added together, where a push one way takes away from a push the other way. Only the net force changes motion. *e.g.* You pull a cart with 5 newtons and friction pulls back with 2 newtons. The net force is 3 newtons forward.
- **Balanced forces**: Forces on a thing that cancel, so the net force is zero. The thing then keeps the same motion. *e.g.* A cyclist at a steady speed: the push from the legs matches friction and the push of the air.
- **Newton's second law of motion**: Isaac Newton's rule from 1687. How much a thing's motion changes depends on the net force and on the mass. A bigger force changes it more. A bigger mass changes it less. *e.g.* The same kick sends a football flying, but moves a heavy stone only a little.
- **Friction**: The grip between two surfaces that touch. It works against sliding and rolling. *e.g.* A cart rolling over a doormat slows down faster than one rolling on a tiled floor.
- **Air resistance**: The push of the air on a thing that moves through it. It works against the motion. *e.g.* A flat sheet of paper falls slowly because air resistance pushes up on it.
- **Load**: The extra weight that something carries. *e.g.* Sacks of rice on a lorry are its load.
- **Stopping distance**: How far a moving thing travels after the brakes start working, until it stops. *e.g.* A loaded lorry has a longer stopping distance than a cycle moving at the same speed.
- **Gravity**: The pull between things because they have mass. The Earth's gravity pulls everything towards the ground. *e.g.* When you drop a tumbler, Earth's gravity pulls it down.
- **Thrust**: The push that an engine gives to a rocket or a plane. *e.g.* A rocket's engines push hot gas down, and the gas pushes the rocket up with thrust.

## Explanations
### Newton's second law: the net force and the mass decide how much motion changes · *defined*
**Definition:** How much a thing's motion changes depends on two things. A bigger net force changes it more. A bigger mass changes it less.

Think of the cart pulled by a hanging bag. Put a little water in the bag, and the cart speeds up slowly. Put four times as much, and it speeds up much faster. Now put a heavy bottle on the cart, and the same bag speeds it up less. Isaac Newton gathered this into his second law in his book Principia in 1687. Before him, many people thought that a bigger push meant a faster steady speed. Newton saw that a push changes the speed. A bigger push changes it by more every second, and a bigger mass changes it by less.
- e.g. A bigger water bag pulls the cart faster than a smaller water bag.
- e.g. A loaded cart speeds up less than an empty cart when pulled by the same bag.
- e.g. A cricket ball flies off the bat, but a heavy stone hit with the same bat would hardly move.
### Acceleration: how quickly the speed changes · *defined*
**Definition:** Acceleration is how much a thing's speed changes every second. Scientists also use the word when a thing slows down or turns.

Speed tells you how far you go each second. Acceleration tells you how much your speed grows or shrinks each second. A cycle that goes from 0 to 4 metres per second in 4 seconds gains 1 metre per second every second. Its acceleration is 1 metre per second, every second. When the rider brakes, the speed shrinks each second, and that is acceleration too. When a bus goes round a bend at a steady speed, its direction changes every moment, and that is acceleration as well. The second law is about all of these changes, not only about speeding up.
- e.g. An auto pulling away from a signal gains speed every second.
- e.g. A bus braking for a stop loses speed every second.
- e.g. A bus going round a bend at a steady speed changes its direction every second.
### A bigger force changes motion more · *defined*
**Definition:** When the mass stays the same, a net force that is twice as big gives twice the acceleration.

Imagine a cart that gains 1 metre per second every second when the bag pulls with 2 newtons. With a pull of 4 newtons, the same cart gains 2 metres per second every second. Because the cart speeds up faster, it covers a set distance in less time. But the time does not fall to half. Four times the force gives four times the acceleration, and that makes the trip take about half the time. The cart keeps speeding up all the way, so the early part of the trip is slow and the last part is quick. Friction takes some of every pull, so the smallest bag does worst of all.
- e.g. A bag with 400 millilitres of water pulls the cart faster than one with 100 millilitres.
- e.g. Two children pulling a cart together make it speed up more than one child.
- e.g. A strong kick sends a football off faster than a gentle tap.
### A bigger mass changes motion less · *defined*
**Definition:** When the net force stays the same, a mass that is twice as big gives half the acceleration.

A cart with a mass of 1 kilogram gains 2 metres per second every second when pulled with 2 newtons. A cart with a mass of 2 kilograms, pulled with the same 2 newtons, gains only 1 metre per second every second. Twice the mass means half the acceleration. This is why a full gas cylinder is harder to get moving than an empty one, and why it is harder to stop. Remember that the mass includes everything that moves: the cart, the load, and even the bag of water that hangs from the string.
- e.g. A full gas cylinder is harder to start than an empty one.
- e.g. A school bag full of books is harder to swing round than an empty bag.
- e.g. A loaded cart takes longer to cross the table than an empty cart when pulled by the same bag.
### Putting a number on a force: the newton · *defined*
**Definition:** Force equals mass times acceleration. One newton is the force that gives a mass of 1 kilogram an acceleration of 1 metre per second, every second.

Scientists write the second law as a short sum: force = mass x acceleration. If a 3 kilogram cart gains 2 metres per second every second, the net force on it is 3 x 2 = 6 newtons. The unit of force is named newton after Isaac Newton. A small apple weighs about 1 newton, because the Earth pulls on 100 grams with a force of about 1 newton. A 1 kilogram bottle of water weighs about 10 newtons. This short sum lets engineers work out how strong a rope, a brake or a rocket engine must be before they build it.
- e.g. A 3 kilogram cart gaining 2 metres per second every second needs a net force of 6 newtons.
- e.g. A 1 kilogram bottle weighs about 10 newtons, so a rope holding it needs to pull up with about 10 newtons.
- e.g. A rocket engine with a thrust of millions of newtons can lift a rocket with a mass of hundreds of thousands of kilograms.
### Net force: pushes and pulls that add and cancel · *defined*
**Definition:** Only the net force, all the forces added together, changes motion. If the forces cancel, the motion does not change.

A cart pulled with 5 newtons against 5 newtons of friction has a net force of zero. It does not speed up. It either stays still or keeps rolling at the same speed. A book on a table has its weight pulling down and the table pushing up with the same force, so the net force is zero and the book stays at rest. This is why a cyclist who pedals at a steady speed is not 'using no force'. Their legs push forward, friction and air push back, and the two are equal. To speed up, they must push harder than the air and friction push back. The net force then points forward.
- e.g. A book on a table has its weight pulling down and the table pushing up, so it does not move.
- e.g. A cyclist at a steady speed pushes forward as hard as friction and the air push back.
- e.g. A cart pulled with 5 newtons against 3 newtons of friction has a net force of 2 newtons forward.
### Why a heavy thing and a light thing fall together · *defined*
**Definition:** Where air does not get in the way, every falling thing gains speed at the same rate, whatever its mass. The Earth pulls harder on a heavier thing, but the heavier thing also needs a bigger force to speed up in the same way.

The Earth pulls a 1 kilogram bottle with about 10 newtons and a 2 kilogram bottle with about 20 newtons. The second bottle has twice the mass, so it needs twice the force to gain speed at the same rate. And it gets exactly twice the force. So both bottles gain about 10 metres per second every second. A feather falls slowly on Earth only because air resistance pushes up on it. In 1971, the astronaut David Scott of Apollo 15 dropped a hammer and a falcon feather together on the Moon, where there is no air. They landed at the same time. On a ramp, a loaded cart arrives with an empty cart for the same reason.
- e.g. A book with a sheet of paper lying on top falls together, because the book pushes the air away from the paper.
- e.g. A hammer and a feather dropped on the Moon in 1971 landed together.
- e.g. A loaded cart and an empty cart reach the bottom of a smooth ramp at about the same time.
### Why heavy vehicles need longer to stop · *defined*
**Definition:** When the braking force is the same, a bigger mass slows down less every second, so it needs more road to stop. A faster vehicle also needs more road: doubling the speed makes the stopping distance about 4 times as long.

A cycle with two riders is harder to stop than the same cycle with one rider, when the brake pads squeeze with the same force. The extra mass slows down less every second. Real lorries do carry bigger brakes, but a lorry loaded with tonnes of goods usually still needs a longer road to stop than a car at the same speed. Speed matters even more. A vehicle at twice the speed takes about 4 times the distance to stop, with the same braking force. That is why speed limits near schools are low, and why drivers leave a big gap in front of them on a highway.
- e.g. A cycle with two riders needs a longer road to stop than the same cycle with one rider.
- e.g. A loaded lorry needs a longer road to stop than a car at the same speed.
- e.g. A car at 40 kilometres an hour needs about 4 times the stopping distance of the same car at 20 kilometres an hour.
### Stopping gently: spreading a stop over more time · *defined*
**Definition:** To stop a moving thing, the force you need depends on how quickly you stop it. If you take longer to stop it, the force is smaller.

A fielder catching a fast cricket ball pulls their hands back as the ball arrives. The ball still goes from a high speed to zero. But it takes a bit longer to stop, so the force on the hands is smaller and the catch stings less. If you take 5 times as long to stop the ball, the average force is about 5 times smaller. The same idea is used in a cycle helmet with foam that squashes, in a seatbelt that stretches a little, in the crumple zone at the front of a car, and in bending your knees when you land from a jump.
- e.g. A fielder pulls the hands back while catching a fast ball.
- e.g. Bending the knees when you land from a jump makes the stop longer and the force smaller.
- e.g. A helmet's foam squashes slowly, which stops the head more gently than a hard shell alone.
### A rocket speeds up faster as it gets lighter · *defined*
**Definition:** A rocket's engines give a push that stays about the same while the rocket burns fuel. As the fuel burns, the mass falls, so the same push gives more acceleration.

Most of the mass of a rocket on the launch pad is fuel. When ISRO's rockets lift off from Sriharikota, the engines push up harder than the Earth pulls down, so the net force is upwards. As the fuel burns, the rocket gets lighter, so the same push changes its speed more and more every second. Parts that are not needed any more, called stages, are dropped, and that makes the rocket lighter still. This is the second law working in the opposite direction from the loaded cart: less mass, more change.
- e.g. A rocket speeds up faster and faster as its fuel burns.
- e.g. A rocket drops empty stages so that the rest speeds up more.
- e.g. A cart that sheds a heavy bottle speeds up more with the same pull.
### What is a force, and does the law always hold? · *debated*
Newton's second law passes every everyday test. Spacecraft are steered with it, bridges are built with it and cricket balls fly the way it says. But scientists still argue about what the words in the law mean and where it stops working. Nobody has ever seen a force. We see only the changes that forces make, and the stretch of a spring. Some of the biggest questions are not about the cart but about what mass is, how the law behaves near the speed of light, and whether it changes in the faintest pulls between faraway stars.
- e.g. Engineers use the law every day to design bridges, cars and rockets.
- e.g. Particle accelerators show that pushes near the speed of light change motion less and less.
- **Isaac Newton:** Newton wrote that mass is the quantity of matter in a thing, and that a force is what changes a thing's motion. The law is then a discovery about how the two connect.
- **Ernst Mach, an Austrian scientist, in 1883:** Mach pointed out that the law can also be read as a way of defining mass and force. To measure either one, you seem to need the other. He asked how we could measure a mass without using force, and the question is still discussed.
- **Albert Einstein, in 1905:** Einstein showed that near the speed of light, the same push changes a thing's speed less and less. Newton's sum is an excellent rule for everyday speeds, and a part of a larger theory.
- **Mordehai Milgrom, an Israeli physicist, in 1983, and a minority of physicists since:** Milgrom suggested that the law may change for extremely faint pulls, such as at the edges of galaxies. Most astronomers instead think unseen dark matter explains what they see there. The question is not settled.

## Mostly agreed
- For one mass, a net force that is twice as big gives twice the acceleration. *How we know:* Carts pulled by bags of different weights, and countless laboratory tests since Newton's time, show this pattern. Engineers use it to plan the path of every spacecraft.
- For one net force, a mass that is twice as big gives half the acceleration. *How we know:* Loading a cart or a rocket with more mass makes the same push change its speed less. Lorries, trains and ships with big loads take longer to speed up and to stop.
- Mass and weight are different. Mass is how much matter a thing has. Weight is the pull of gravity on it. *How we know:* The Apollo astronauts weighed about 6 times less on the Moon, yet their suits and their bodies were just as hard to push and just as hard to stop.
- Where air does not get in the way, a heavy thing and a light thing fall together. *How we know:* In 1971, on the Moon, David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather together and they landed together. Inside a pumped-out glass tube on Earth, a feather and a coin fall together too.
- Forces that cancel do not change motion. Only the net force does. *How we know:* A book on a table and a cyclist at a steady speed both have forces on them, but the forces are equal and opposite, so their motion does not change.
- Newton's second law is an excellent rule for everyday speeds, and it needs correcting near the speed of light. *How we know:* Einstein's 1905 theory and the results of particle accelerators show the correction. For cars, cricket balls and spacecraft, the corrections are far too small to matter.

## How we found out
**Emilie du Chatelet, a French mathematician and scientist (1706 to 1749), and Willem 's Gravesande, a Dutch scientist, The clay experiments in the 1720s, her book in 1740, and her translation of Newton in 1749.** Around 1700, thinkers argued about how to measure the 'force' of a moving thing. Descartes had taught that it grows in step with the speed: twice the speed, twice the force. Others, following Gottfried Leibniz, said it grows faster. In the 1720s, a Dutch scientist named Willem 's Gravesande dropped brass balls into soft clay. A ball that hit the clay at twice the speed made a dent about four times as deep, he reported. Emilie du Chatelet, a French mathematician and scientist, studied his results. In her book of 1740, she argued that the clay showed that Descartes was wrong, and that a faster ball needs much more than twice the stopping. This is why a car at twice the speed needs about four times the road to stop. In her last years she translated Newton's Principia into French, with her own notes. She finished it in 1749. It was printed after her death, in part in 1756 and in full in 1759.  
*What people believed before:* Descartes and his followers taught that the force of a moving thing grows only in step with its speed. The clay showed that a ball at twice the speed makes a dent about four times as deep, so the stopping needed grows much faster than the speed.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A bigger force makes a thing go at a bigger steady speed.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,300 years ago, and the everyday feeling of most people; *rejected*)  
  For: Push a heavy almirah harder and it moves faster. Pedal harder and the cycle goes quicker.  
  Against: A steady force on a smooth floor keeps changing the speed, so the thing goes faster and faster. A steady speed on a rough floor happens when the push matches friction. The force changes the speed. It does not set the speed.
- **Heavy things fall faster than light things.** (Aristotle, and many people since, who see a stone fall faster than a feather; *rejected*)  
  For: A stone hits the ground before a feather, and a coin before a sheet of paper.  
  Against: The feather is slowed by air resistance. In 1971, David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon, where there is no air, and they landed together. Heavier things get pulled harder, but they also need more force to speed up.
- **A thrown ball carries a force inside it that keeps it flying.** (A common idea among children, and a version of the 'impetus' idea taught by Jean Buridan, a teacher in Paris, about 1350; *rejected*)  
  For: A ball keeps moving after it leaves your hand, so it feels as if you gave it something to keep it going.  
  Against: After the ball leaves your hand, no force pushes it forward. It keeps its motion, and gravity and air change that motion. A force is a push or a pull from outside, and it is not stored in the ball.
- **In space, heavy things are weightless, so they are easy to push.** (A common guess, from pictures of floating astronauts and floating equipment; *rejected*)  
  For: Astronauts and their tools float, and a floating bag of tools feels as if it weighs nothing.  
  Against: Weightless things still have mass. A large piece of equipment on the space station is hard to start moving and hard to stop, as hard as it would be on the ground if there were no friction. Mass resists changes in motion everywhere.
- **The law 'force = mass x acceleration' holds for everything, at every speed and every size.** (Many school books, which teach the law without its limits; *rejected*)  
  For: It predicts the motion of balls, cars, planets and spacecraft with astonishing accuracy.  
  Against: Near the speed of light, the same push changes a thing's speed less and less, and Einstein's theory is needed. For atoms and tiny particles, quantum physics is needed. The law is an excellent rule within a large range.
- **Newton's second law may change for extremely faint pulls, such as at the edges of galaxies.** (Mordehai Milgrom, an Israeli physicist, in 1983, and a minority of physicists since; *minority*)  
  For: The outer stars of many galaxies move faster than the visible matter should allow. Changing the law for faint pulls explains many of these galaxies with no extra matter.  
  Against: Most astronomers think unseen dark matter is a better explanation, because it also explains other things, such as how light bends round galaxy clusters. Nobody has settled the question.

## Roots
We have measured how a pull and a load change the motion of a cart. Now we ask what that says about every push, and about us.

**Deep truth.** When the bag pulled the cart, the cart pulled the bag back just as hard. So the cart sped up, and the bag fell much more slowly than a free bag. Both changed. Mass only decides how the change is shared.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the pull moved the cart', as if one thing does the acting and the other is only moved. A force always acts between two things, so both change. We forget the heavy one because it changes so little.  
*Evidence:* In the hanging bag test, the bag took a second or more to reach the floor, but a bag let go on its own lands in less than half a second. The cart was holding the bag back. If your numbers differ, write them down as they are.

- *who am i:* When you jump, you push the Earth down and the Earth pushes you up. You and the Earth both change your motion. You change a lot. The Earth changes by an amount too tiny to see.
- *what is life:* A crow pushes air down to rise. A fish pushes water back to move forward. A snake pushes the ground behind her to slide ahead. Every living being moves by pushing on something else, and the something else moves too.
- *what is this world:* A cart, a boat, a rocket and the Moon going round the Earth all follow one rule. How much the motion changes depends on the net force and the mass. The same rule works for a toy and for a planet.

Look at the rule again. A cart, a lorry, a rocket, a falling bottle and the Moon going round the Earth all follow it. When forces cancel, nothing changes. When they do not cancel, the difference decides how much the motion changes. Everything that moves, from a toy to a planet, shares out its changes in the same way, according to its mass.

- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika, chapter 2:** Nagarjuna, an Indian Buddhist teacher of about the 2nd century CE, looked closely at motion. He argued that 'the mover', 'the moving' and 'the path' cannot be found apart from each other. Each depends on the others. (A cart does not speed up alone. It speeds up because of a pull, and the pull needs something to pull against. The change belongs to both of them together.)
- **Isaac Newton, letter to Robert Hooke, February 1675 by the old calendar:** Newton wrote, 'If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants.' He meant that his laws were built on the work of earlier people. (The second law stands on the work of Galileo, Descartes, Huygens and others. In France, Emilie du Chatelet then translated and explained Newton's book, so that many more people could use it.)
- Science (settled): Newton's second law describes the motion of cars, planets and spacecraft with very high accuracy. Near the speed of light, Einstein's theory corrects it.
- Science (debated): Whether the second law changes for extremely faint pulls, as in the outer parts of galaxies, is still argued about. Most astronomers favour dark matter instead.
- Other view, Ernst Mach and many physicists after him: A force is not a hidden thing in the world. It is a handy name for 'mass times the change in motion'. On this view, there is no need to ask who is doing the acting. You only need to say how each mass changes.
- Practice, Soft jumps (3 min): Stand with your feet apart on the floor. Bend your knees a little and jump a tiny jump, then land softly. Feel the floor push you up and push you back as you land. Breathe out slowly each time you land. Do five tiny jumps. Then stand still. Notice that the floor is pushing up on you, even now.
- Ask: When the bag pulls the cart, which one is 'doing' and which one is 'receiving'? Can you really tell?
- Ask: When you jump, the Earth moves a tiny bit too. Where does the effect of your jump stop?
- Ask: Is a force something in the world, or a name we give to the reason for a change?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Doubling the mass doubles the Earth's pull on a thing, and it also doubles how hard the thing is to speed up. So everything falls together. Is it luck that these two match, or is there a reason? (hint: Einstein asked this question and it led him to a new idea about gravity. Why would 'how hard the Earth pulls' and 'how hard it is to push' be connected?)
- *deep:* When the bag pulls the cart, the cart pulls the bag back just as hard. Which one is 'doing' the pulling, and which one is 'being pulled'? (hint: Both of them change their motion. Think about which changes more, and why.)
- *deep:* When you jump, you push the Earth down and the Earth pushes you up. The Earth moves too. Why can nobody see it? (hint: Think about the Earth's mass compared with yours. What does the second law say about a very big mass?)
- *deep:* A cyclist at a steady speed has the same push forward as backward, so the net force is zero. Is nothing happening? (hint: Think about the legs, the chain, the air and the road. Is 'no change' the same as 'no activity'?)
- *deep:* Nobody has ever seen a force. We see a cart speed up, or a spring stretch. Is a force a real thing in the world, or a name we give to the reason for a change? (hint: Think of a spring balance. It shows a number for a force. What did the spring actually do?)
- *big:* A huge ship takes many kilometres to stop. What other big things in the world take a very long time to change direction, and what makes them so slow to change? (hint: Think of a huge ship, a river, a big crowd or a planet. What do they have a lot of?)
- *big:* A rocket gets lighter and lighter, so the same push speeds it up more and more. Is there anywhere else where losing something makes change easier? (hint: Think of a bag you carry, or a cart that sheds its load.)
- *big:* Newton's second law is almost right for everyday things, and needs fixing for very fast things. Is a rule that is almost right still true? (hint: Spacecraft reach the planets using Newton's law. Is it more like a map that is accurate enough, or a statement that is exactly true?)

## Story: The Boat That Would Not Move
*Amudha, Murugan Uncle, Valli Paati and this morning are made up. Kovalam, also called Covelong, is a real fishing village near Chennai, and fishing families really do haul boats up the beach when high seas are coming. Rolling heavy loads on logs is a very old way of moving them.*

Before the sun was up, the loudspeaker on the pole near the Kovalam fish market crackled. 'High waves by noon,' said a tired voice. 'Pull every boat above the weed line.' The weed line was the row of dry seaweed that showed where the highest tide had reached.

Amudha sat up on her mat. She was ten years old, and she knew what the message meant. Every boat on the beach had to be dragged up the sand before the sea came for it. Her grandmother, Valli Paati, was already outside, tying up her sari and looking at the sky.

On the beach, twenty people stood round Murugan Uncle's boat. It was big and blue. The engine was still on its back, and the nets and the ice box were still inside. 'Ready?' shouted Murugan Uncle. 'Eyyaa... lessa!' Everyone pushed. The boat moved a hand's width and stopped.

'Again!' Eyyaa, lessa! Another hand's width. Behind them, each wave slid a little further up the sand.

Amudha had her science stopwatch in her pocket. Her teacher had asked the class to time something that moves, and she had been meaning to time the boats. She pressed the button without thinking. Nine seconds for the boat to creep one step. Then nine again. Then nine.

She thought of the cart at school. She had pulled it with a bag of water, and she remembered what she had written in her book. Bigger pull, the cart speeds up more. More load, it speeds up less. 'Murugan Uncle,' she said, 'there are only two things we can change. The push, or the weight.'

'I have no more people,' said Murugan Uncle. 'And the weight is the weight. It is a boat, child.'

'Not all of it is boat,' said Amudha. 'The engine is not the boat. The nets are not the boat. The ice box is not the boat. Take them out, and the same people push less weight.'

Murugan Uncle frowned. The engine was his pride. But Valli Paati folded her arms. 'The girl is right,' she said. 'An engine can walk up the beach on four men's shoulders.' So four men lifted the engine off. Others carried the nets and the ice box up the sand.

'Now push,' called Amudha. 'Eyyaa, lessa!' The same twenty people pushed. This time the boat jumped forward a whole stride. Amudha pressed her watch. Five seconds. She held it up. 'Nine before. Five now. Nobody pushed harder. The boat just had less to carry.'

'Better, but still too slow,' said Murugan Uncle. A wave ran up to his ankles. 'The sand grips the boat like glue.' 'Then give it something that rolls,' said Valli Paati. 'My grandfather used coconut trunks. One under the boat, then the next, then the next.'

The young men ran to the old palm trunks stacked behind the net shed. They laid them across the sand and heaved the front of the boat onto them. 'Eyyaa, lessa!' Now the boat did not creep. It rolled.

Faster and faster it went up the slope, with the people running beside it and laying new trunks in front. Amudha ran too. She did not need to time it any more. The boat was moving as if it wanted to go.

Then she saw the wall of the net shed ahead. 'Stop pushing!' she shouted. 'Stop now!' Everyone let go. But the boat did not stop. All that weight rolled on, slow and heavy, towards the wall. Four men threw a trunk across its path and leaned on the rope. At last the boat stopped, a metre from the wall.

'It is as hard to stop as it was to start,' said Amudha, breathing hard. 'A heavy thing takes a long push to get going, and a long time to stop.'

By noon, every boat stood above the weed line in a long blue and yellow row. The waves came in, white and loud, and they ate the sand where Murugan Uncle's boat had stood an hour before. He watched them for a long time. Then he turned to Amudha. 'Tomorrow,' he said, 'you will show me how to use your stopwatch. And you will tell me about that cart of yours.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: how quickly the boat's motion changed depended on two things, the net push and the mass. Twenty people pushed with the same push each time, so taking the engine, nets and ice box out made the same push change the motion more. The rollers took away most of the friction, so more of the push was left over as net force. When the people let go, the boat's mass kept it rolling, because stopping a big mass needs a long push too. This is Newton's second law: acceleration equals the net force divided by the mass.

- Ask: Why did the same twenty people move the boat faster after the engine and nets were taken out?
- Ask: The people did not push harder when the boat was on logs. Why did the boat move faster?
- Ask: Amudha timed the boat before she told anyone her idea. Why might the times have helped her?
- Ask: Why did the boat not stop when everyone let go?
- Ask: How would you test Amudha's idea with a cart, a bag of water and a stopwatch?

### g1 · Bigger, Smaller or the Same? (20 min)
- **rules:** One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and reads the front aloud. The reader does not show the back.; Every card is about the change in speed each second. The question is always how it compares with the other situation on the card.; Every other player picks one token: Bigger, Smaller, The same, or None. None is for a moment when the speed does not change at all.; Everyone puts the token face down. Then everyone turns the tokens over together.; The reader turns the card over and reads the answer. Each right token scores 1 point. A player who also gives the right reason scores 1 more.; Take turns as the reader. A game is 12 cards. If there is time, shuffle and play 12 more.; The player with the most points wins, and ties share the win. Playing alone? Read the front, pick a token, then check the back. Try to beat your last score out of 12.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a moment on the front, and the answer with the reason on the back:** A cart is pulled by a bag of water. Now the net force on the cart is doubled. The cart is the same. (back: Bigger. Twice the net force gives twice the change in speed each second.); A cart is pulled by a bag of water. Now the cart carries a load, so its total mass is doubled. The net force is the same. (back: Smaller. Twice the mass gives half the change in speed each second.); The net force on a cart is tripled, and the cart's mass is tripled too. (back: The same. Three times the force and three times the mass cancel out.); A full gas cylinder and an empty one get the same push. Compare the empty one with the full one. (back: Bigger. The empty cylinder has less mass, so the same push changes its speed more.); A cyclist rides at a steady speed on a flat road. The push from the legs equals the push of friction and the air. (back: None. The forces are balanced, so the net force is zero and the speed does not change.); A bag of water pulls a cart. The water in the bag is doubled. Compare with before. (back: Bigger. A bigger pull is a bigger net force on the same cart.); A big steel ball and a small steel ball fall together where there is no air. Compare the big ball with the small one. (back: The same. The Earth pulls the big ball harder, but the big ball also has more mass. Both gain about 10 metres per second every second.); A hammer and a feather are dropped together on the Moon. Compare the feather with the hammer. (back: The same. There is no air on the Moon, so nothing slows the feather. David Scott showed this in 1971.); A coin and a sheet of paper are dropped together on Earth. Compare the paper with the coin. (back: Smaller. Air pushes up on the paper with a big force compared with its tiny weight.); A rocket burns fuel while its engines push with the same force. Compare the rocket later with the rocket at the start. (back: Bigger. The same push now acts on a smaller mass.); A helicopter hovers in one place. The air pushes it up exactly as hard as the Earth pulls it down. (back: None. The forces are balanced, so its motion does not change.); A football and a heavy stone get the same kick. Compare the stone with the football. (back: Smaller. The stone has far more mass, so the same kick changes its speed less.); A cart is pulled with the same pull on a smooth floor instead of a doormat. Compare the smooth floor with the doormat. (back: Bigger. The smooth floor gives less friction, so more of the pull is left as net force.); A cycle's brakes squeeze with the same force. A second rider climbs on. Compare the slowing each second with one rider. (back: Smaller. More mass is slowed less by the same brake force, so the cycle needs a longer road to stop.); A lorry and a car are braked with the same braking force. Compare the lorry's slowing each second with the car's. (back: Smaller. The lorry has much more mass.); A car brakes with the same force from 40 kilometres an hour as it did from 20 kilometres an hour. Compare the slowing each second. (back: The same. The braking force and the mass are the same. But the stopping distance is about 4 times as long.); A ball tossed straight up reaches the top of its flight. Compare the change in speed each second now with a moment before. Ignore the air. (back: The same. Gravity pulls the ball all the time, so its speed changes by the same amount every second, even at the top.); A skater glides on very smooth ice. Nobody pushes, and friction is almost zero. (back: None, or almost none. With almost no net force, the speed hardly changes.); A trolley is pushed by 10 newtons against 10 newtons of friction. The pusher adds 5 newtons more. Compare with before. (back: Bigger. Before, there was no net force. Now there are 5 newtons of net force.); Two children pull a cart, each as hard as one child pulled before. Compare with one child pulling. (back: Bigger. Two pulls add up to twice the force.); A child jumps off the ground. Compare the change in the Earth's speed with the change in the child's speed. (back: Smaller. The Earth has so much mass that its change is far too small to notice.); A pebble and a big rock roll down the same smooth slope. Compare the rock with the pebble. (back: The same. The slope pulls the rock harder, but the rock also has more mass.); A rickshaw puller pulls a rickshaw with two passengers, then pulls it empty, pulling just as hard. Compare the empty rickshaw with the full one. (back: Bigger. The same pull acts on a smaller mass.); A boat is pushed by 10 people, then by 20 people who each push as hard as before. Compare the two. (back: Bigger. Twice as many pushers give twice the force.)
- **Printable tokens: 8 tokens: two sets of four, which say Bigger, Smaller, The same and None:** Bigger; Smaller; The same; None; Bigger; Smaller; The same; None
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player name; Right tokens; Bonus points: a reason given; Total
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the 8 tokens. Shuffle the cards and put them face down. Give each player one set of four tokens. Two sets serve three players. A fourth player writes the four words on scraps of paper.  
  *Parent says:* Each card describes a moment. You pick a token to show how the change in speed each second compares. Each player takes four tokens.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take your four tokens.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play one practice card together. A bag of water pulls a cart, and then the water in the bag is doubled. Everyone picks a token and turns it over together.  
  *Parent says:* Practice card. A bag of water pulls a cart. Now the water in the bag is doubled. Pick a token, then turn it over.  
  *Child mode:* Practice card. The water in the bag is doubled. Pick a token. Turn it over.
  - Check: A bag of water pulls a cart. The water in the bag is doubled. How does the change in speed each second compare? → **Bigger, because the net force is bigger**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns as the reader. Play the first 6 cards. After each card, ask who can say why.  
  *Parent says:* Reader, read the front. Everyone picks a token. Turn them over together. Now read the back. Who can say why?  
  *Child mode:* Read a card. Pick a token. Turn it over. Check the back.
  - Ask: What do the 'Bigger' cards have in common? (good: The net force went up.; The mass went down.; The friction went down, so more of the pull was left.)
  - Note: Give 1 bonus point for a reason that uses force, mass, balanced forces or air. A guess that sounds like 'because it is heavier' needs a follow-up: heavier than what, and does the pull get bigger too?
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the next 6 cards. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. Read, pick, turn over and check. Which cards made you change your mind?  
  *Child mode:* Play six more cards. Which card made you change your mind?
  - Ask: Look at the cards where the answer was 'The same'. What do they have in common? (good: The force and the mass both changed by the same amount.; A heavier thing is pulled harder, but is also harder to speed up.; The pulling force did not change, even if something else did.)
  - Note: The 'The same' cards are the hardest. They are about falling and sliding, where a bigger mass means a bigger pull and also a bigger mass to move. The two cancel.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the points. Ask which card was the hardest, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Add up your points. Which card was the hardest? What would you check next time?  
  *Child mode:* Add up your points. Which card was the hardest? Why?
  - Check: A cart's net force triples and its mass doubles. Compared with before, is the change in speed each second bigger, smaller or the same? → **Bigger**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Hanging Bag Cart: what changes how quickly the cart speeds up? (40 min)
- **question:** A bag of water pulls a cart across a table. How does the time for the trip change when the bag has more water? How does it change when the cart carries more load?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will a bag with more water make the trip quicker, slower or the same? Will more load on the cart make it quicker, slower or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same cart, the same table, the same thread and the same start line every time. In Part A, only the water in the bag changes: 100, 200, then 400 millilitres. The cart carries two bottles. In Part B, the bag stays at 100 millilitres and only the load changes: one bottle, two bottles, three bottles. Time every setting 3 times, because one try can be a fluke. Use the middle time of the three.
- **safety:** Water on the floor is slippery, so keep a towel under the bag and wipe up spills straight away. Keep feet out of the way of the hanging bag and the bottles. Keep the book stopper in place so the loaded cart cannot run off the table edge onto toes.
- **record:** Fill in the table, one row for each of the six settings. Then draw two bar charts of the middle times, one for the three bags and one for the three loads. Write one sentence under each chart saying what the bars show.
- **results table:** Part and setting | Time 1 (seconds) | Time 2 | Time 3 | Middle time
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the cart, the bottles and the bag. Ask for a guess and a reason for each question. Write the guesses at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* A bag of water will pull this cart across the table. What happens if the bag has more water? What happens if the cart has more load? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A bag of water pulls a cart. What happens with more water in the bag? What happens with more load on the cart? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that will happen? (good: A heavier bag pulls harder.; A heavier cart is harder to move.; A bigger pull should make it quicker.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write down what they say, even if you think it is wrong.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the pen across the edge of the table. 2. Tie the thread to the front of the cart. 3. Lay the thread over the pen and tie the bag to the other end. 4. Strap two bottles onto the cart with the rubber band. 5. Mark a start line 60 centimetres from the pen. 6. Put the book where the cart will stop.  
  *Parent says:* We are building a pulling machine. The thread goes over the pen, and the bag hangs down. When we let go, the bag falls and pulls the cart.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the pen to the table edge. Tie the thread to the cart. Hang the bag. Mark the start line.
  - Note: The thread rubbing on the pen takes a little of the pull, but it is the same every run. The thread must run level from the cart to the pen. The bag must be able to fall at least 60 centimetres before it touches the floor. Put the book where the cart stops, so it does not hit the pen. If your cart is flimsy, use one bottle in Part A and loads of zero, one and two bottles in Part B.
  - Check: Why must the start line be in the same place for every run? → **So the cart travels the same distance every time**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a towel on the floor. Put 100 millilitres of water in the bag and tie it shut. Hold the bag at the height of the table, away from the cart, and let it drop onto the towel. Notice how quickly it lands.  
  *Parent says:* First, a test with no cart. Let the bag drop onto the towel. It lands in a blink. Will the bag fall as fast when it is pulling the cart?  
  *Child mode:* Let the bag drop onto the towel. It lands in a blink. Will it fall as fast with the cart?
  - Ask: Do you think the bag will fall as fast when it pulls the cart? Why? (good: No, the cart holds it back.; It will be slower because it has to pull the cart as well.)
  - Note: A 60 centimetre fall takes about a third of a second, a blink. Remember this: it is the free fall time for the bag. We will come back to it at the end.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Part A. Put 100 millilitres in the bag, and two bottles on the cart. Hold the cart at the start line. Say 'go', let it go, and time it until it hits the book. Do this 3 times. Then repeat with 200 and with 400 millilitres.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the cart at the line. Ready? Go! Time the trip. Do each bag three times, and write down every time.  
  *Child mode:* Put two bottles on the cart. Put 100 millilitres in the bag. Let go. Time the trip. Do it three times.
  - Note: Use the middle of the three times. Each bigger bag should give a shorter time. Four times the water should not take a quarter of the time. It typically takes a bit more than half the time. A phone stopwatch can be 0.2 seconds out, so a gap smaller than that means nothing: if you can, film the run and step through the video. With 2 to 4 children: one builds and releases, one times, one records, one watches the bag. Swap after each setting, and each child makes their own guess and chart. If the cart does not move with 100 millilitres, write 'did not move' and use 200, 300 and 400 instead.
  - Check: The bag with 400 millilitres of water pulled the cart across in less time than the bag with 100 millilitres. What does that tell you? → **A bigger pull changes the cart's speed by more every second**
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Part B. Keep 100 millilitres in the bag. Put one bottle on the cart and time 3 runs. Then use three bottles and time 3 runs. You already did two bottles in Part A, so reuse those times.  
  *Parent says:* Now the pull stays the same and the load changes. What do you expect to see this time? Write down every time.  
  *Child mode:* Keep 100 millilitres in the bag. Try one bottle, then three bottles. Time each one three times.
  - Note: Expect each extra bottle to make the trip longer. If the cart's wheels bend, stop at two bottles. Whatever you see, write it down as it is.
  - Check: With the same bag, the cart with three bottles took longer than the cart with one bottle. Why? → **The same pull changed the speed of the bigger mass by less every second**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: their claim, their evidence and their reason. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say what you saw. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: What happened to the time when the bag got heavier? What happened when the cart got heavier? (good: The time got shorter with a heavier bag.; The time got longer with a heavier cart.; More pull changes the speed more, and more mass changes it less.)
  - Note: After your child has said their ideas, tell them: this is Newton's second law. A bigger force changes motion more, and a bigger mass changes it less. The cart also pulls the bag back, which is why the bag falls more slowly than a free bag. If the results were messy, say that scientists see that too, and that is why we do each setting three times.
  - Think: The bag fell much more slowly than when you dropped it alone. What was the cart doing to the bag?
### e2 · The Ramp Surprise: does a heavy cart take longer to roll down? (30 min)
- **question:** A cart rolls down a ramp. Does the trip take less time when the ramp is steeper? Does it take longer when the cart carries a load?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will a steeper ramp make the trip quicker, slower or the same? Will a loaded cart take longer, less time or the same time? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same ramp, the same cart, the same start line and the same finish line every time. In Part A, only the height of the ramp changes: 6, 12 and 24 centimetres. In Part B, the ramp stays at 12 centimetres and only the load changes: none, one bottle, three bottles. Release the cart by letting go, never by pushing. Time every setting 3 times and use the middle time.
- **safety:** Keep fingers and toes away from the bottom of the ramp, where the cart stops hard. Make sure the stack of books cannot fall over.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart of the middle times for the three ramp heights, and another for the three loads. Write one sentence for each chart. Circle the result that surprised you.
- **results table:** Part and setting | Time 1 (seconds) | Time 2 | Time 3 | Middle time
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the ramp, the cart and the bottles. Ask for a guess and a reason for each question. Write the guesses at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* A cart will roll down this ramp. What happens if the ramp is steeper? What happens if the cart carries a heavy load? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A cart rolls down a ramp. What happens if it is steeper? What happens with a load? Why?
  - Ask: Do you think the loaded cart will take longer? Why? (good: Yes, a heavy cart is harder to get going.; No, the heavy cart is pulled down harder.; I am not sure, because it is heavier but also pulled more.)
  - Note: Many children, and many adults, say the loaded cart will be slower. Write down what your child says and why. Do not correct it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift one end of the board with books so that it is 12 centimetres higher than the other. Check with the ruler. 2. Mark a start line near the top. 3. Mark a finish line 80 centimetres below it. 4. Put a book at the bottom as a stopper.  
  *Parent says:* We will lift one end of the board to 12 centimetres first. Check it with the ruler. The cart starts at the top line and finishes at the bottom line.  
  *Child mode:* Lift one end of the board 12 centimetres. Mark the start line and the finish line.
  - Note: Measure the height straight up, from the table to the bottom of the raised end. The board must not wobble. If the cart wanders sideways, put two strips of tape as lanes.
  - Check: Why do we release the cart by letting go and never by pushing? → **A push would add a force that changes from run to run**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Part A. With no load, time the cart 3 times at 12 centimetres. Then change the height to 6 centimetres and time 3 runs. Then change the height to 24 centimetres and time 3 runs.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the cart at the line. Ready? Go! Time it to the finish line. Do each height three times and write down every time.  
  *Child mode:* Let the cart go. Time it to the finish line. Do three heights, three times each.
  - Note: A steeper ramp should be quicker. Doubling the height does not halve the time. The new time is typically about two thirds of the earlier time, or a little less. With 2 to 4 children: one releases, one times, one records, one guards the bottom of the ramp. Swap after each height, and each child draws their own chart. If the cart does not move at 6 centimetres, the wheels have a lot of friction: use 9, 18 and 36 instead.
  - Check: The cart took less time on the ramp that was 24 centimetres high than on the one 6 centimetres high. Why? → **The steeper ramp gave a bigger pull along the slope**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Part B. Set the ramp at 12 centimetres. Strap one bottle onto the cart and time 3 runs. Then strap on three bottles and time 3 runs. Compare with the no load runs at 12 centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* Now the ramp stays the same and the load changes. Which will arrive first, the loaded cart or the empty cart? Write down every time.  
  *Child mode:* Keep the ramp at 12 centimetres. Add one bottle, then three. Time each load three times.
  - Note: The surprise is that the times should be almost the same. A loaded cart may be a little slower, because its axles rub more. But three bottles do not make it three times as slow. A phone stopwatch can be 0.2 seconds out, so only a gap bigger than that counts: if you can, film the runs. If your loaded cart is much slower, check that the bottles are not touching the wheels.
  - Check: The loaded cart took almost the same time as the empty cart on the ramp. What is the best explanation? → **The heavier cart is pulled harder along the slope, and it also has more mass to move**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what surprised them. Then ask the question below. Write the answer under the chart.  
  *Parent says:* What surprised you? On the table, a heavier cart was slower. On the ramp, it was not. Why do you think that was?  
  *Child mode:* What surprised you? Heavy was slower on the table. On the ramp it was not. Why?
  - Ask: On the table, the heavier cart was slower, but on the ramp it was not. What was different? (good: On the table, the bag's pull stayed the same.; On the ramp, the pull came from the Earth, and a heavier cart is pulled harder.; On the ramp, the pull grew with the load.)
  - Note: After they have said their ideas: on the table, the pull was the same bag of water, so more load meant more mass and the same pull. On the ramp, the pull is part of the weight of the cart itself, so a heavier cart is pulled harder along the slope. Force and mass grow together, and the speed changes in the same way. That is why a heavy ball and a light ball fall together.
  - Think: A heavy ball and a light ball fall together. How is that like the loaded cart on the ramp?

### a1 · Empty or Full: which is harder to get going? (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up lifts the heavy load. Keep toes and fingers away from the wheels.
- **record:** Measure how far each rolls after you let go (count floor tiles or steps, or use a tape) and do it 3 times for each. Draw two pictures, one for the empty thing and one for the full thing, each with an arrow for the push. Write 'harder' or 'easier' beside the words 'to start' and 'to stop'.

  *Child mode:* Push this gently. Then load it and push again. What will be different? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will be different about the full one? (good: It will be harder to get going.; It will be harder to stop.; It will not move as far.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand behind the empty thing. Give it one gentle push while you count one, two. Then let go. Stop it with one hand.

  *Child mode:* Push it gently. Count one, two. Let go. Stop it with your hand.
  - Note: Count the same way each time, so the two pushes feel alike. Mark where it stops with a bit of tape and count the floor tiles (or measure) from the place you let go.   *Child mode:* A grown-up loads it. Push the same way. Which is harder to start? Which is harder to stop?

### a2 · Book and Paper: do heavy and light things fall together? (15 min)
- **safety:** Drop things onto a cushion, away from feet. Keep faces out of the way, and make sure the bottle caps are tight.
- **record:** Do the bottle drop 5 times and tally which landed first: full, empty or together. Draw a picture for each drop and write 'together' or 'not together' under it. Write one sentence on why the full and empty bottles landed as they did.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the book, the paper and the two bottles. Ask your child to guess what will happen in each drop. Ask why.  
  *Parent says:* We will drop a book with a paper on top. Then we will drop a full bottle and an empty bottle together. What do you think will happen? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what happens in each drop. Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Which do you think will land first, the full bottle or the empty bottle? Why? (good: The full one, because it is heavier.; Both at the same time.; I am not sure.)
  - Note: Many people say the heavier bottle will land first. Do not correct it. Write down what your child says.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the book flat at shoulder height over the cushion, with the paper lying on top. 2. Let go. Watch the paper. 3. Drop the paper alone. 4. Crumple the paper into a ball and drop it beside the book.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the paper when I let go. Does it stay on the book? Now the paper alone. Now the crumpled paper beside the book.  
  *Child mode:* Drop the book with the paper on top. Drop the paper alone. Crumple it and drop it again.
  - Note: The flat paper stays on top of the book as they fall, because the book pushes the air out of the way. The paper alone flutters down slowly. The crumpled ball falls almost as fast as the book. If the paper slides off, hold the book flatter and drop it more gently.
  - Check: The same paper fell slowly on its own, but fell quickly when lying on the book. What is the best reason? → **The air pushes up on the flat paper, and the book kept the air away from it**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the bottles on their sides, side by side, at chest height above the cushion. Let go of both together. Listen for one thud or two. Do it five times and tally the result: full first, empty first, or together.

   **Answer:** Force → a push or a pull; Mass → how much matter a thing has; Acceleration → how quickly speed or direction changes; Net force → all the forces on a thing added together; Balanced forces → forces that cancel, so the motion does not change. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit.    **Answer:** Either answer is fine if it is backed by evidence. A strong answer agrees with Meenakshi by saying that we feel pushes and pulls with our bodies, and the cart speeds up when the bag pulls. It credits Aarti by saying that what we measure is the cart's change in motion, or a spring's stretch, and a force is how we explain them.. *Why:* Scientists still discuss this. Ernst Mach pointed out in 1883 that the second law can be read as a way of defining force. Both friends have noticed something true.
13. (match) Match each safety idea to the reason it works.  
   **Answer:** Foam inside a cycle helmet → it squashes slowly, so the head takes longer to stop; A seatbelt → it holds you to the seat, so you stop with the car; A crumple zone at the front of a car → it squashes, so the car takes longer to stop; A low speed limit near a school → a slower car needs much less road to stop; A big gap behind a lorry on a highway → a heavy fast vehicle needs a long road to stop. *Why:* All five ideas come from the second law. A longer stop needs a smaller force, a lower speed needs a much shorter stop, and a bigger mass needs more road.
14. (count) In the made-up example, the 100 millilitre bag took 1.8 seconds and the 400 millilitre bag took 0.8 seconds. How many seconds shorter was the trip with the bigger bag?  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* 1.8 take away 0.8 is 1.0. The trip took 1 second less.
15. (mcq) Four times the pull should take about half the time. If a trip takes 1.8 seconds with the 100 millilitre bag, about how long would you expect with the 400 millilitre bag, if friction did not matter?  
   Options: 0.45 seconds / 0.9 seconds / 1.4 seconds  
   **Answer:** 0.9 seconds. *Why:* Four times the acceleration makes the trip take half the time, because the time depends on the square root of the acceleration. Half of 1.8 is 0.9. In a real test, friction changes the numbers a little.
16. (label) Here is a picture of a cart on a table that is being pulled by a thread. Label the four forces on the cart.  
   **Answer:** ['pull of the thread: forward', 'friction: backward', 'weight of the cart: downward', 'push of the table: upward, equal to the weight']. *Why:* The weight and the table's push are equal, so they cancel. The pull and friction act along the table. When the pull is bigger than friction, the net force is forward, and the cart speeds up.
17. (odd) Three of these are changes in motion. Which one is not?  
   Options: A bus slows down for a stop / A bus turns a corner at a steady speed / A bus travels along a straight road at a steady speed / A bus starts from a stop  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A change in motion means a change in speed or direction. Slowing down, turning and starting are all changes. A steady speed in a straight line is not.
### Newton's Law Puzzles (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A bag of 200 millilitres of water pulls a cart across a table in 1.2 seconds. Which change would make the trip take the least time?  
   Options: Take one bottle off the cart / Use a longer thread / Paint the cart a bright colour  
   **Answer:** Take one bottle off the cart. *Why:* Taking a bottle off lowers the mass, so the same pull changes the speed by more every second. A longer thread does nothing to the pull or the mass, and paint does not matter.
2. (count) A cart has a mass of 1 kilogram. The thread pulls it forward with 7 newtons and friction pulls back with 2 newtons. By how much does its speed grow every second, in metres per second?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* The net force is 7 take away 2, which is 5 newtons. 5 divided by 1 kilogram is 5. The speed grows by 5 metres per second every second.
3. (count) A cart has a mass of 2 kilograms. A thread pulls it with 8 newtons and friction pulls back with 2 newtons. By how much does its speed grow every second, in metres per second?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The net force is 8 take away 2, which is 6 newtons. 6 divided by 2 kilograms is 3. The speed grows by 3 metres per second every second.
4. (mcq) A bathroom scale in a lift reads more than usual just after the lift starts going up. Why?  
   Options: The lift is speeding up, so the scale has to push up on you with more than your weight / Your mass increases when you go up / Gravity gets stronger in a lift  
   **Answer:** The lift is speeding up, so the scale has to push up on you with more than your weight. *Why:* To speed you up, the scale must push up with more than the Earth pulls down. The scale shows how hard it pushes. Your mass and the Earth's pull do not change.
5. (truefalse) In a lift moving up at a steady speed, a bathroom scale reads more than it does when the lift is at rest. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. At a steady speed, the forces on you are balanced, so the scale pushes up with exactly your weight. It reads more only while the lift is speeding up.
6. (write) On the table, the loaded cart was slower. On the ramp, the loaded cart was not slower. Explain the difference using the words 'the pull' and 'the mass'.  
   **Answer:** On the table, the pull came from a bag of water that did not change, so a loaded cart had more mass and the same pull, and it sped up less. On the ramp, the pull is part of the cart's own weight, so a loaded cart is pulled harder. The bigger pull and the bigger mass cancel, so both carts speed up equally.. *Why:* A strong answer says that on the table the pull stayed the same while the mass grew, and that on the ramp the pull grew with the mass.
7. (perspective) Two friends in Hyderabad talk about the hammer and feather dropped on the Moon in 1971.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with Kabir. The Moon has gravity, about one sixth of the Earth's, which is why things fall there. They fell together because nothing pushed up on the feather. It credits Anika for noticing that the Moon is different from the Earth, and that without air things fall differently from what we see at home.. *Why:* Anika has mixed up gravity and air. The Moon pulls things down, and the astronauts' hammer fell. Only the lack of air let the feather keep up.
8. (mcq) Suppose a car needs 4 metres to stop from 20 kilometres an hour, with a steady braking force. About how far would it need from 60 kilometres an hour?  
   Options: 12 metres / 36 metres / 24 metres  
   **Answer:** 36 metres. *Why:* Three times the speed needs 3 times 3, which is 9 times the stopping distance. 4 times 9 is 36 metres. Picking 12 metres only multiplies by 3.
9. (order) The same net force of 12 newtons acts on three carts. Put the carts in order, from the one that gains the least speed every second to the one that gains the most.  
   **Answer:** ['the 6 kilogram cart', 'the 3 kilogram cart', 'the 2 kilogram cart']. *Why:* Acceleration is force divided by mass. 12 divided by 6 is 2. 12 divided by 3 is 4. 12 divided by 2 is 6. The lightest cart gains the most.
10. (draw) A fielder catches a fast ball. Draw two catches side by side, one with hands that do not move back and one with hands that move back. Mark which catch has the longer stop.  
   **Answer:** Two pictures of a fielder. In the second, the hands are drawn moving back with an arrow, and the second catch is marked as the longer stop.. *Why:* Moving the hands back makes the ball take longer to stop. The same change in motion over more time needs a smaller force, so the catch is gentler.
11. (sort) Sort each safety item: does it make the stop take longer, or does it hold you to the vehicle?  
   **Answer:** Foam inside a cycle helmet → Makes the stop take longer; A car seatbelt → Holds you to the vehicle; Bending your knees when you land → Makes the stop take longer; A crumple zone at the front of a car → Makes the stop take longer; A strap on a child seat → Holds you to the vehicle; Pulling your hands back in a catch → Makes the stop take longer. *Why:* Foam, bent knees, crumple zones and soft hands all spread a stop over more time, so the force is smaller. A seatbelt and a child seat strap keep you with the vehicle, so you stop with it and not by hitting something.
12. (mcq) At the edge of some galaxies, stars move faster than the matter we can see should allow. What is one idea scientists have for this?  
   Options: There may be unseen dark matter pulling on them / Stars at the edge are heavier / Newton's laws do not exist there  
   **Answer:** There may be unseen dark matter pulling on them. *Why:* Most astronomers think unseen dark matter is pulling on the stars. A few physicists think the second law itself may change for extremely faint pulls. Nobody has settled the question.
13. (fill) A force of 1 ____ gives a mass of 1 kilogram a speed gain of 1 metre per second every second.  
   **Answer:** ['newton']. *Why:* This is how the newton is defined. One newton on a 1 kilogram mass gives it an acceleration of 1 metre per second every second.
14. (mcq) A 1 kilogram bottle of water weighs about how many newtons?  
   Options: 10 newtons / 1 newton / 100 newtons  
   **Answer:** 10 newtons. *Why:* A small apple of about 100 grams weighs about 1 newton. A bottle of 1 kilogram has 10 times as much mass, so it weighs about 10 newtons.
15. (write) When you jump, the floor pushes you up and you push the floor down. Why can nobody see the Earth move?  
   **Answer:** The Earth does move, but it has so much mass that the same push changes its motion by an amount far too small to see. A person has very little mass, so the same push changes their motion a lot.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the second law. The same size of force changes a very big mass by a tiny amount.
16. (label) Here is a picture of a rocket on its way up. Label what each arrow shows.  
   **Answer:** ['arrow pointing up from the engine: thrust', 'arrow pointing down from the middle: weight', "the fuel tanks: most of the rocket's mass at the start", 'the part that gets smaller as fuel burns: the mass of the rocket']. *Why:* The thrust is bigger than the weight, so the net force is upwards. As the fuel burns, the mass shrinks, and the same thrust speeds the rocket up more.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A cart is pulled with a net force. The net force is doubled and the cart is the same. What happens to the change in speed each second?  
   Options: It doubles / It halves / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It doubles. *Why:* Twice the net force gives twice the acceleration. Picking 'halves' mixes up force and mass.
2. (mcq) The same pull acts on a cart. A load is added so the cart's mass is three times as big. What happens to the change in speed each second?  
   Options: It becomes one third / It triples / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It becomes one third. *Why:* Three times the mass gives one third of the acceleration for the same net force. Picking 'triples' reverses the rule.
3. (mcq) In the Hanging Bag Cart test, which change makes the cart's trip take less time?  
   Options: More water in the bag / More bottles on the cart / Starting the cart further back  
   **Answer:** More water in the bag. *Why:* More water is a bigger pull, so the cart's speed changes by more every second. More bottles slow it down, and starting further back makes the trip longer.
4. (mcq) Which of these is a change in motion?  
   Options: A bus going round a bend at a steady speed / A bus parked at a stand / A bus going straight at a steady speed  
   **Answer:** A bus going round a bend at a steady speed. *Why:* A change in motion is a change in speed or in direction. Going round a bend changes the direction every moment, even if the speed stays the same.
5. (mcq) A heavy ball and a light ball are dropped together in a place with no air. What happens?  
   Options: They land together / The heavy one lands first / The light one lands first  
   **Answer:** They land together. *Why:* The Earth pulls the heavy ball harder, but it also has more mass, so the two cancel. Both gain speed at the same rate.
6. (mcq) A cyclist rides at a steady speed on a flat road. What is true of the forces along the road?  
   Options: The forward push equals the backward push / There is only a forward push / There are no forces at all  
   **Answer:** The forward push equals the backward push. *Why:* At a steady speed the net force is zero. The forces are balanced, not missing.
7. (truefalse) Two cycles have the same brakes, squeezing with the same force. The cycle with two riders needs a longer road to stop than the cycle with one rider. True or false?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The same braking force slows a bigger mass by less every second, so the cycle with two riders takes more road to stop.
8. (mcq) A rocket's engines push with the same force while it burns fuel. What happens to its acceleration?  
   Options: It gets bigger, because the mass gets smaller / It gets smaller, because the fuel is gone / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It gets bigger, because the mass gets smaller. *Why:* The same push acts on a smaller mass, so the speed changes by more every second.
9. (count) A net force of 15 newtons acts on a cart with a mass of 3 kilograms. By how much does its speed grow every second, in metres per second?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* Acceleration is force divided by mass. 15 divided by 3 is 5. The speed grows by 5 metres per second every second.
10. (mcq) Which question about the second law do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Does the law change for very faint pulls far out in galaxies? / Does a bigger force change motion more? / Do heavy things fall faster than light things?  
   **Answer:** Does the law change for very faint pulls far out in galaxies?. *Why:* The first two are settled by countless tests. Whether the law changes for extremely faint pulls is an open question, linked to the puzzle of dark matter.
11. (order) A bag of water pulls three carts. Put the carts in order, from the one that speeds up most to the one that speeds up least.  
   **Answer:** ['an empty cart', 'a cart with one bottle', 'a cart with three bottles']. *Why:* The pull is the same, so the cart with the least mass speeds up most. More bottles mean more mass, so the cart speeds up less.
12. (mcq) A book rests on a table. Which statement is right?  
   Options: The Earth pulls it down and the table pushes it up equally / Only the Earth pulls on it / The table pushes up harder than the Earth pulls down  
   **Answer:** The Earth pulls it down and the table pushes it up equally. *Why:* The book is not moving, so the net force is zero. The table's push upwards must equal the Earth's pull downwards.
13. (mcq) On a smooth ramp, a cart with three bottles and an empty cart are let go together. What happens?  
   Options: They take about the same time / The loaded cart takes much longer / The empty cart takes much longer  
   **Answer:** They take about the same time. *Why:* The ramp pulls the loaded cart harder, and the loaded cart also has more mass. The two cancel, so both carts speed up at about the same rate.

## Applications
- Engineers use force equals mass times acceleration to choose the engines for cars, trains, ships and rockets.
- Lorry and bus drivers keep a big gap on highways, because heavy fast vehicles need a long road to stop.
- Speed limits near schools are low, because a car at half the speed needs about a quarter of the road to stop.
- Heavy goods trains on the railways are pulled by more than one engine, so that the pull is big enough for the mass.
- Lift makers start and stop lifts gently, because a sudden start would push up on your feet far harder than your own weight.
- Seatbelts, airbags, crumple zones and helmet foam make a stop take longer, so the force on the body is smaller.
- Cricket fielders pull their hands back when they catch, and high jumpers land on thick mats.
- ISRO plans the path of its rockets and of Mangalyaan with Newton's laws of motion.
- Cranes, ropes and chains are chosen to be strong enough for the weight of the load plus the extra pull needed to start it moving.
- Packers load heavy things low and tied down, because a heavy load that starts or stops suddenly needs a big force to hold it.

## Open questions
- **Why do things have mass, and why does mass resist a change in motion?** Scientists can measure mass extremely well, and they know that much of the mass of ordinary things comes from the energy inside atoms. But a full answer to why mass resists changes in motion is still being worked on.
- **Why does the mass that resists a push match the mass that the Earth pulls on, so exactly?** Tests have shown the two masses to be equal to an extremely tiny fraction. Einstein's general theory of relativity builds on this match, but a deeper reason may need a theory that joins gravity with quantum physics, and nobody has one yet.
- **Does the second law change for extremely faint pulls, such as those at the edge of a galaxy?** Most astronomers think unseen dark matter explains why stars at the edge of galaxies move so fast. A minority think the law itself changes there. Neither idea has been proved, and new observations keep testing both.
- **Is a force a real thing in the world, or only a useful name for why a motion changes?** Physicists and philosophers still disagree. Some say the forces are real, and some say the laws of motion define the word force. The cart tests cannot settle this, because they only show changes in motion.
- **Are the laws of motion the same everywhere in the universe, and were they the same long ago?** Observations of far-away galaxies and of light that left them billions of years ago show no change so far. Scientists keep checking, because even a tiny difference would be a major discovery.
- **Do all the forces we know, such as pushes, pulls, friction and gravity, come from a smaller set of basic forces?** Physicists have joined several forces into one, such as electricity and magnetism. Whether gravity can join them is still an open question.

Doubling the force from 3 to 6 doubled the gain from 3 to 6. Tripling it to 9 tripled the gain. For a 1 kilogram cart, force in newtons and acceleration are the same number. The gain goes up in step with the force. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
