# Our senses: many more than five
`sci.senses.five-senses.L3`

**Summary.** Pinch your nose, and a spoon of elaichi sugar tastes only sweet. Let go, and the elaichi comes back, because most of what we call taste is really smell. A sense is a way your body detects something and tells the brain. People often say we have five senses, but that list comes from the Greek thinker Aristotle, more than 2,300 years ago. Scientists count many more: balance, knowing where your body parts are, warmth and cold, pain, thirst, and sensors inside you that check your blood. In every sense, special cells called receptors turn what they detect into tiny electric signals, and nerves carry the signals to the brain. Other animals detect things we cannot, like ultraviolet light or echoes in the dark. Nobody yet knows how electric signals in a brain become the taste of a mango.

**Intro.** *Shut your eyes and pinch your nose. Now someone puts a pinch of sugar on your tongue. It has a secret spice mixed in. Can you tell which spice it is? Keep pinching. Now let go of your nose.* When you let go, the spice suddenly appears. Your tongue did not change. Your nose started working again. Next, you follow each sense from the outside of your body down to tiny cells and electric signals. You also find out that you have many more than five senses. And you find out what a bat, a fly and a snake can sense that you cannot.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a sense is: a way the body detects something from the world and sends a message to the brain.
1. I can explain how hearing works, from shaking air to the eardrum, three tiny bones, hair cells in the cochlea and the brain. I can show why two ears help find where a sound comes from.
2. I can explain that skin has different receptors for different kinds of touch, and show with a fair test where touch is sharpest.
3. I can name the five basic tastes, explain that the tongue map is wrong, and say why chilli is not a taste.
4. I can explain that flavour is mostly smell, and show it with a test where I pinch my nose.
5. I can explain that eyes detect light with rods and cones, and that every sense turns the world into the same kind of electric signal.
6. I can give examples of animals that sense things humans cannot, and say what scientists still do not know about how senses become experience.
7. I can explain that we have many more than five senses, such as balance, body position, warmth and cold, and thirst, and test some of them at home.

## Terms
- **Sense**: A way your body finds out about the world. It detects something, like light or sound, and sends a message to your brain. To detect something means to notice that it is there. *e.g.* Hearing is a sense. It tells you a pressure cooker is whistling, even when you are in another room.
- **Sense organ**: A part of the body built to detect one kind of thing. Ears, eyes, nose, tongue and skin are the best-known sense organs. The balance organs deep inside your ears are sense organs too. *e.g.* Your nose is the sense organ for smell.
- **Balance sense**: The sense that tells you which way is down and when your head tilts or turns. Its receptors are hair cells in the inner ear, next to the cochlea. Scientists call it the vestibular sense. *e.g.* Spin round and stop. The room seems to keep spinning, because the liquid in your balance organs is still moving.
- **Proprioception**: The sense of where the parts of your body are and how they are moving. Stretch sensors in your muscles and joints send the signals. Say it pro-pree-oh-SEP-shun. *e.g.* With your eyes shut, you can still touch your nose with one finger.
- **Interoception**: Sensing what is happening inside your own body. Sensors inside you notice a full bladder, a stretched stomach, thirst and the carbon dioxide in your blood. *e.g.* Thirst is interoception. Cells in your brain notice when your blood gets too salty, and you want a drink.
- **Receptor**: A special cell that notices one kind of thing, like light, a push or a chemical. When it notices it, it makes a tiny electric signal. *e.g.* Your eyes have receptors that notice light. Your tongue has receptors that notice sugar.
- **Cell**: The tiny living building block that bodies are made of. Your body has many millions of millions of cells, of many different kinds. *e.g.* Skin cells, blood cells and nerve cells are all cells.
- **Nerve**: A long thread made of nerve cells that carries messages between your body and your brain. *e.g.* When you touch a hot tawa, nerves carry the message from your finger to your brain in a tiny fraction of a second.
- **Nerve signal**: A tiny pulse of electricity that travels along a nerve. Every sense sends its messages to the brain as nerve signals. *e.g.* The fastest nerve signals travel about 100 metres in one second. That is the length of a football field.
- **Brain**: The part inside your head that receives the nerve signals from all your senses and puts them together into one world. *e.g.* Your brain puts the sight, the sound and the smell of a frying bajji together, so you know what it is at once.
- **Vibration**: Fast shaking, backwards and forwards. Sound is a vibration that travels through air, water or solid things. *e.g.* Touch your throat while you hum. You can feel it vibrate.
- **Eardrum**: A thin, tight skin at the inner end of your ear canal. It shakes when sound reaches it, like the skin of a tabla. *e.g.* When someone claps, the shaking air makes your eardrum shake too.
- **Cochlea**: A small tube coiled like a snail shell, deep inside your ear. It is full of liquid and lined with hair cells. *e.g.* The cochlea is about the size of a pea.
- **Hair cell**: A receptor in the cochlea with a tiny tuft on top. When sound shakes the liquid, the tuft bends and the cell makes a nerve signal. *e.g.* Each ear has about 15,000 hair cells. Very loud sounds can break them, and they do not grow back.
- **Taste bud**: A tiny bunch of taste receptor cells, shaped like a flower bud. Most are in the little bumps on your tongue. *e.g.* You have a few thousand taste buds. Each one has dozens of taste cells.
- **Umami**: The savoury, meaty basic taste. It is one of the five basic tastes, with sweet, sour, salty and bitter. *e.g.* Ripe tomatoes, mushrooms, cheese and a good sambar have lots of umami.
- **Molecule**: The smallest piece of a substance that is still that substance. It is far too small to see, and it is made of atoms joined together. Smells and tastes come from molecules. *e.g.* When you open a jar of elaichi, some of its molecules float out into the air and up your nose.
- **Flavour**: What you notice when you eat. It is taste from the tongue mixed with smell from the nose, plus touch and temperature. *e.g.* With a blocked nose, food tastes flat. You still taste sweet and salty, but the flavour has gone.
- **Retina**: A thin layer at the back of the eye that detects light. It is covered in two kinds of receptor cells, rods and cones. *e.g.* The picture you see is made on your retina first, upside down.
- **Rods and cones**: The two kinds of light receptor in the retina. Rods work in dim light but see no colour. Cones need more light and see colour. *e.g.* At night in a dark room, you see shapes but hardly any colour. Only your rods are working.
- **Afterimage**: A ghost picture you see after staring at something bright. It happens because some of the colour cells in your eye, called cones, get tired. Scientists call this tiredness adaptation. *e.g.* Stare at a red square for 30 seconds. Then look at a white wall. You see a green-blue square.
- **Echolocation**: Finding things by making a sound and listening for its echo, the sound bouncing back. *e.g.* Bats find moths in the dark by echolocation. Some blind people do it too, with tongue clicks.
- **Umwelt**: A German word scientists use for the world as one kind of animal senses it. Every animal has its own Umwelt. *e.g.* A dog's Umwelt is full of smells. A bat's Umwelt is full of echoes.
- **Perception**: What your brain makes out of the signals from your senses. It is the world as you experience it. *e.g.* Your eyes send signals. Your perception is 'a yellow mango on the table'.

## Explanations
### What a sense is · *defined*
**Definition:** A sense is a way the body detects something from the world, or from inside itself, and turns it into a message for the brain. Each sense has its own receptors.

Every sense works in three steps. First, something from the world reaches the body. It might be light, shaking air, a push on the skin or a chemical. Second, receptors detect it. A receptor is a special cell that detects one kind of thing. When it detects it, it makes a tiny electric signal. Third, nerves carry the signal to the brain. Only then do you see, hear, feel, taste or smell. People often say we have five senses: sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell. That list comes from the Greek thinker Aristotle, more than 2,300 years ago. It is a tradition, not a scientific count. Scientists count many more. This unit starts with the five well-known senses, then meets the others.
- e.g. Hearing: shaking air reaches your ear, hair cells detect it, and a nerve carries the signal to your brain.
- e.g. Smell: molecules of hot payasam float up your nose, smell receptors detect them, and your brain says 'payasam'.
- e.g. Touch: a mosquito lands on your arm, touch receptors in your skin detect the tiny push, and you swat it.
### Many more than five senses · *defined*
**Definition:** The body has far more than five senses. Besides sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell, it senses balance, the position of its parts, warmth, cold, pain, and many things inside itself.

The list of five senses comes from Aristotle, who picked one sense for each organ you can see. Many senses have no organ you can point to. Your balance sense is in your inner ear, next to the cochlea. Some of its hair cells sit under a layer of jelly topped with tiny crystals. Gravity tugs on the crystals when you tilt your head. Three curved tubes full of liquid tell you when your head turns. Proprioception comes from stretch sensors in your muscles and joints. It tells you where your arms and legs are, even in the dark. Your skin has separate nerve endings for warmth, for cold and for pain. Inside each tooth, under the hard enamel, nerves feel cold and pressure. There are sensors deep inside you too. Stretch sensors tell you when your stomach is full or your bladder needs emptying. Cells in your brain notice when your blood is too salty, and you feel thirsty. When you hold your breath, the strong need to breathe comes mostly from sensors that notice carbon dioxide building up in your blood. Scientists do not agree on the exact number of senses. Depending on how they count, it is somewhere between about a dozen and over 30.
- e.g. Stand on one foot and close your eyes. It is much harder, because your brain balances with your eyes, your inner ears and your muscle sensors together.
- e.g. Put one hand in cold water and one in warm water for a minute. Then put both in the same lukewarm water. One hand feels warm and the other feels cold.
### Every sense speaks the same language: electric signals · *defined*
**Definition:** Every sense turns what it detects into the same kind of message: tiny pulses of electricity called nerve signals. The brain never receives light, sound or smell itself. It only receives signals.

Light never reaches your brain. Sound never reaches it either. What reaches it are tiny electric pulses, travelling along nerves. A signal from your eye looks almost exactly like a signal from your ear. So how does the brain know which is which? It goes by where the signal arrives. Signals from the eyes go to the seeing parts of the brain. Signals from the ears go to the hearing parts. In the 1830s, a German scientist called Johannes Müller pointed out a clue. If the eye or its nerve is pressed, a person sees flashes of light, even in the dark. The nerve from the eye can only send one message: 'light'. That is why rubbing your closed eyes can make you see sparkles. Do not press on your eyes to try it.
- e.g. When people bump the back of their head hard, they may 'see stars'. The seeing part of the brain is at the back of the head. The bump sets off signals there, so they see light that is not there.
- e.g. A signal from your tongue saying 'sweet' and a signal from your skin saying 'cold' are both little electric pulses. Only their path is different.
### How hearing works · *defined*
**Definition:** Sound is a vibration of the air. The ear passes the vibration inwards to hair cells in the cochlea, and the hair cells turn it into nerve signals.

When a bell rings, it shakes the air around it. The shaking travels through the air to you. Your ear flap collects it and funnels it down the ear canal. At the end is the eardrum, a thin tight skin like the top of a tabla. The eardrum shakes. Behind it are the three smallest bones in your body, called the hammer, the anvil and the stirrup. The stirrup is about as big as a grain of rice. The three bones pass the shaking on to the cochlea, a liquid-filled tube coiled like a snail shell. The liquid ripples. Inside are about 15,000 hair cells, each with a tiny tuft on top. When the tufts bend, tiny gates in the cell open, and the cell makes a nerve signal. High sounds bend hair cells near the start of the coil. Low sounds bend hair cells near the far end. So the cochlea is laid out a little like the keys of a harmonium. A Hungarian scientist, Georg von Békésy, worked this out, and won a Nobel Prize for it in 1961.
- e.g. Hum with your fingers on your throat. You feel the vibration that will travel through the air to someone's ear.
- e.g. Children can hear very high squeaks that most grown-ups cannot, because hair cells for the highest sounds wear out first as we get older.
### Two ears find where a sound comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain finds where a sound comes from by comparing the two ears. A sound from one side reaches the nearer ear a tiny bit sooner and a little louder.

Think of a sound on your left. It reaches your left ear first. It has to travel around your head to reach your right ear. The difference is less than one thousandth of a second. Your brain notices even that. The right ear also hears the sound a little more quietly, because your head is in the way. Your brain compares both clues and points you towards the sound. A sound straight in front of you or straight behind you reaches both ears at the same moment. So those are the hardest to place. The folds of your ear flap help with that. They change the sound slightly depending on where it comes from, so your brain can tell front from back.
- e.g. A barn owl's ears are at different heights on its head. So it can tell up from down as well as left from right, and catch a mouse in total darkness.
- e.g. When you are not sure where a sound is, you turn your head. That gives your brain new clues to compare.
### Hair cells that never grow back · *defined*
**Definition:** In people, hair cells in the cochlea do not grow back once they are broken. Very loud sounds, or loud sounds for a long time, can break them.

A hair cell's tuft is very delicate. A firecracker going off close by, or loud music in earphones every day, can bend the tufts so hard that they break. Your body cannot make new hair cells to replace them. So hearing lost this way stays lost. Ringing in the ears after a loud noise is a warning sign. Here is a surprise. Birds can grow new hair cells. Scientists found this in the 1980s, and they are still trying to work out how humans might do it too.
- e.g. A simple rule for earphones: if someone sitting next to you can hear your music, it is too loud.
- e.g. Cover your ears or move away when firecrackers go off nearby.
### How touch works · *defined*
**Definition:** Skin has several kinds of touch receptor. Some detect light touch, some detect steady pressure, some detect vibration and some detect stretching. Other nerve endings detect heat, cold and pain.

Your skin is your biggest sense organ. It is packed with nerve endings. Some of them are wrapped in tiny capsules. Some detect the lightest stroke, like a feather. Some detect steady pressure, like a heavy bag on your lap. Some detect vibration, the fast shaking of a phone on silent mode. Some detect your skin stretching when you bend a finger. When the skin is pushed, the receptor gets squashed a tiny bit. That opens tiny gates in it, and it makes a nerve signal. The gate for touch is made of a protein called Piezo2. An American scientist, Ardem Patapoutian, found it, and won a Nobel Prize in 2021. Fingertips, lips and the tongue have touch receptors packed very close together. Your back has them spread far apart. So fingertips feel much more detail. In one test, people could feel the difference between surfaces whose bumps were only about 13 millionths of a millimetre high.
- e.g. People who read Braille feel patterns of raised dots with their fingertips, often very fast.
- e.g. A star-nosed mole has 22 fleshy fingers around its nose, covered in thousands of touch sensors. It finds food in dark tunnels by touch alone.
### Chilli is not a taste · *defined*
**Definition:** The 'hot' of chilli is not a taste. A chemical in chilli, called capsaicin, switches on the nerve endings that warn you of heat. Mint does the opposite and switches on the cold nerve endings.

Your mouth has nerve endings that warn you when something is hot enough to burn. They have a gate that opens at about 43 degrees Celsius. That is a little hotter than a hot bath. Capsaicin from chilli fits that gate and opens it too, even when the chilli is cold. So your brain gets the message 'burning!' That is why chilli makes you sweat and your face go red. In 1997 an American scientist, David Julius, found this gate by using chilli. He shared the 2021 Nobel Prize with Ardem Patapoutian. Menthol from mint opens the gate of the cold nerve endings. So mint feels cool, even in a warm mouth. Birds have a slightly different heat gate that capsaicin cannot open. So birds eat chillies without feeling any burn, and spread the seeds.
- e.g. Chilli on your fingers can make them burn too, because skin has the same heat nerve endings. That is why you must wash your hands before touching your eyes.
- e.g. A cold glass of water does not stop the chilli burn for long. The capsaicin is still holding the gate open.
### How taste works · *defined*
**Definition:** Taste buds hold receptor cells that notice five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Every part of the tongue that has taste buds can notice all five.

Look at your tongue in a mirror. It is covered in tiny bumps. Inside many of them are taste buds, too small to see. Each taste bud is a bunch of about 50 to 100 taste cells. Food has to dissolve in spit first. Then its molecules reach the taste cells. Each kind of taste cell has receptors shaped to detect certain molecules, a bit like a lock that only a certain key fits. Sugar fits the sweet receptors. Salt and acid, like lemon, work on salty and sour cells. Bitter receptors detect many different molecules, and many poisons are bitter. So bitterness is partly a warning. Umami receptors detect glutamate, a molecule in tomatoes, mushrooms and cheese. A Japanese chemist, Kikunae Ikeda, named umami in 1908. Taste cells wear out and are replaced about every two weeks. That is why a burnt tongue heals.
- e.g. Sweet: gur, honey and ripe mango. Sour: imli, lemon and curd. Salty: salt and papad. Bitter: karela, neem and black coffee. Umami: tomato, mushroom and cheese.
- e.g. Cats cannot taste sweet at all. In 2005 scientists found that the gene for their sweet receptor is broken.
### The tongue map that was never true · *defined*
**Definition:** The famous 'tongue map', with sweet only at the tip and bitter only at the back, is wrong. Every part of the tongue that has taste buds can taste every basic taste.

In 1901 a German scientist, David Hänig, tested people's tongues. He found that some parts were very slightly more sensitive to some tastes. The differences were tiny. In 1942 an American psychologist, Edwin Boring, redrew Hänig's results in a book. The drawing made the tiny differences look like separate zones. Other books copied it as a map, and it was printed in school books around the world. In 1974 another American scientist, Virginia Collings, tested it again carefully. She found every taste could be tasted all over the tongue. Later, scientists found taste receptors for every taste spread across the whole tongue. You can test it yourself with a cotton bud.
- e.g. Dab sugar water on the very back of your tongue. You can taste sweet there.
- e.g. Dab a drop of karela juice on the tip. You can taste bitter there.
### How umami was found · *defined*
**Definition:** Umami was named in 1908 by a Japanese chemist, Kikunae Ikeda. For almost 100 years many scientists doubted it, until umami receptors were found in taste cells in 2000 and 2002.

In about 1907, a Japanese chemist called Kikunae Ikeda was eating tofu in a broth made from kombu, a kind of seaweed. He noticed a taste that was not sweet, sour, salty or bitter. He wanted to know what made it. He boiled down a large amount of kombu and found crystals of a substance called glutamate. In 1908 he named its taste umami, a Japanese word for a savoury, delicious taste. For most of the 1900s, many scientists outside Japan did not accept this. Their books said there were only four basic tastes. They said umami was just a mix of salty and the others. Ikeda's idea could only be settled by finding a receptor for it. In 2000, a team led by Nirupa Chaudhari and Stephen Roper found a receptor in taste cells that detects glutamate. In 2002, Charles Zuker's team found another. Umami had its own receptors, so it was a basic taste, just as Ikeda had said almost 100 years before.
- e.g. Ikeda found umami in kombu, a seaweed used to make broth in Japan. In Indian food, ripe tomatoes give a lot of umami.
- e.g. For a long time, many books said there were four basic tastes. Now most scientists count five.
### Is there a sixth taste? · *debated*
Scientists agree on five basic tastes. Some think there may be more. To count as a basic taste, scientists want a receptor on the tongue that detects it, and a taste that feels different from all the others. Different people and traditions count differently.
- e.g. Some people say very fatty foods, like ghee, have their own taste, separate from their smell and feel.
- e.g. Ayurveda counts six tastes, called rasas: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent.
- **Most scientists today:** There are five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Each has its own receptors on the tongue.
- **Richard Mattes and Cordelia Running, scientists in the USA, in 2015:** Fat may be a sixth basic taste. They called it 'oleogustus'. In their tests, people could pick out fat in food even when smell and feel were hidden. Other scientists are not yet convinced.
- **The Ayurveda tradition of India, in books such as the Charaka Samhita:** Food has six rasas: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent. Pungent is like chilli or ginger. Astringent is the dry, puckering feeling of raw banana or strong tea. Scientists would say these two are touch feelings in the mouth, not taste-bud tastes. But they are real parts of eating.
### How smell works · *defined*
**Definition:** Smells are molecules floating in the air. Smell receptor cells high up inside the nose detect them and send signals to the brain.

When you open a box of laddoos, molecules float off them into the air. You breathe some in. High up inside your nose is a patch of skin about the size of a postage stamp. It holds millions of smell receptor cells. Each cell has one kind of smell receptor, shaped to detect certain molecules. People have about 400 different kinds. Most smells are made of many molecules, so one smell switches on a pattern of receptors. Your brain reads the pattern, like reading a word from its letters. That is how 400 kinds of receptor can tell apart a huge number of smells. Two American scientists, Linda Buck and Richard Axel, found the smell receptors in 1991. They won the Nobel Prize in 2004. Smell signals go almost straight to the parts of the brain for feelings and memory. That may be why a smell can suddenly bring back a memory from long ago. Smell receptor cells are nerve cells, and they are replaced every month or two. Most nerve cells are never replaced.
- e.g. The smell of rain on dry earth has a name: petrichor. Part of it comes from a molecule called geosmin, made by bacteria in the soil. Bacteria are living things made of just one cell.
- e.g. The smell of your grandmother's house, or a school classroom, can bring back a whole day in a flash.
### Flavour is mostly smell · *defined*
**Definition:** Flavour is taste plus smell, with touch and temperature too. Most of what we call the 'taste' of food is really smell.

When you chew, smell molecules from the food float up from the back of your mouth into your nose. You do not notice them going in. Your brain mixes them with the signals from your tongue. Then it makes the whole flavour seem to be in your mouth, even though much of it came from your nose. Your tongue only tells you sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Your nose tells you mango or guava, cardamom or cinnamon, fresh or burnt. So when your nose is blocked by a cold, food tastes flat. Pinch your nose and the spice in a spoon of sugar vanishes. Let go, and it comes back. Many people who caught COVID-19 lost their sense of smell for a while. They often said food had lost its taste.
- e.g. With a blocked nose, a spoon of sugar with elaichi tastes only sweet. Let go of your nose, and the elaichi appears.
- e.g. Hot food usually smells stronger than cold food, because heat sends more molecules into the air.
### How good is the human nose? · *debated*
People often say humans have a poor sense of smell. Scientists now argue about whether that is true. Dogs are far better at many smells. But the human nose may be much better than people think.
- e.g. A dog's nose has many millions more smell cells than yours. Trained dogs can follow a person's smell trail that is many hours old.
- e.g. In one experiment, blindfolded people crawled across grass and followed a trail of chocolate smell with their noses.
- **Paul Broca, a French brain scientist, in the 1800s, and many books since:** The smell part of the human brain is small compared with the rest of our brain. So our sense of smell must be weak.
- **John McGann, an American brain scientist, in 2017:** That was a guess, not a test. The human smell part of the brain has about as many nerve cells as that of many other mammals. People can smell some molecules at tiny amounts, better than some animals.
- **Andreas Keller and others, in 2014, and the scientists who checked their maths:** Keller's team calculated that people can tell apart at least a trillion smells. Other scientists showed that the number depends on how the maths is done. It might be much smaller or much bigger. Nobody knows the real number.
- **Asifa Majid and Niclas Burenhult, language scientists, in 2014:** English speakers find smells hard to name. But the Jahai people of the Malaysian rainforest name smells as easily as colours. So naming smells may depend on practice and language, not on the nose.
### How seeing works, in one card · *defined*
**Definition:** We see when light enters the eye and is detected by rods and cones in the retina. Each cone or rod has a molecule that changes shape when light hits it, and that starts a nerve signal.

Light bounces off things and into your eye through the black hole in the middle, the pupil. The clear front of the eye and the lens bend the light, so it makes a small, upside-down picture on the retina, the back wall of the eye. The retina has about 120 million rods and about 6 million cones. Rods work in dim light. Cones work in bright light and see colour. There are three kinds of cone. One kind detects mostly red light, one mostly green and one mostly blue. Your brain mixes their signals into every colour you see. Inside each rod and cone is a molecule that straightens out when one tiny particle of light hits it. That change starts the nerve signal. A rod can notice a single particle of light. About one million nerve threads carry the signals from each eye to the brain. The picture on the retina is upside down, but the brain makes sense of it, so you see the world the right way up. The unit 'How we see' goes much deeper into light and the eye.
- e.g. Stare at a bright red shape and then at a white wall. Your red cones are tired, so you see a green-blue ghost: an afterimage.
- e.g. Dogs have only two kinds of cone. They see blues and yellows well, but red and green look alike to them.
### The senses work together · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain mixes the signals from all the senses into one world. What one sense tells it can change what another sense seems to say.

Your brain does not keep the senses in separate boxes. It mixes them. Flavour is one example: taste and smell mixed together. Here are more. In 1976 two scientists in England, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald, made a film of a face saying 'ga' while the sound said 'ba'. People heard 'da'. Their eyes changed what their ears heard. A ventriloquist's voice seems to come from the puppet's moving mouth, because your eyes pull the sound towards it. In 2004, two scientists in England, Massimiliano Zampini and Charles Spence, played people the sound of their own chip-crunching through headphones. When the crunch was made louder, the chips seemed fresher and crisper. Even the colour of a drink can change how sweet it seems. So the brain does not take each sense on its own. It makes its best guess about the world from all of them together.
- e.g. In a noisy place, you understand someone better when you can see their lips move.
- e.g. Give people lemon sharbat coloured with orange food colour. Some of them say it tastes of orange.
### When one sense is missing, the brain finds another way · *defined*
**Definition:** When a person cannot use one sense, the brain often puts the other senses to work in new ways. It can even use brain parts that would normally be for the missing sense.

Brains can change with use. Think of people who have been blind from birth. In their brains, the parts normally used for seeing get used for touch and hearing instead. In 1996 a team in the USA, led by a Japanese scientist, Norihiro Sadato, scanned blind people reading Braille. The 'seeing' part of their brains was busy while their fingers read. Some blind people find their way by making tongue clicks and listening to the echoes, like bats. Daniel Kish, who lost both eyes as a baby, does this so well that he rides a bicycle. In 2011, Lore Thaler and her team in Canada studied expert blind clickers. The seeing part of their brains was busy while they listened to echoes. Their ears are not better than other people's. Their brains have learned to use the sounds more.
- e.g. Louis Braille lost his sight as a small boy in France. At about 15 years old, he invented the raised-dot writing that blind people around the world read with their fingertips.
- e.g. Helen Keller could neither see nor hear. In 1887 her teacher, Anne Sullivan, spelled 'w-a-t-e-r' into one of her hands while water ran over the other. Helen understood that things have names.
### Every animal lives in its own sense-world · *defined*
**Definition:** Each kind of animal senses only some parts of the world. The world as one animal senses it is called its Umwelt. No animal senses everything.

A German scientist, Jakob von Uexküll, gave us the word Umwelt more than 100 years ago. A honeybee sees ultraviolet light, which we cannot see. Many flowers have ultraviolet stripes that lead the bee to their nectar. A bat fills the dark with squeaks too high for us, and hears the echoes. A pit viper has two small pits on its face that feel the warmth of a mouse in total darkness. An elephant hears rumbles far too low for us, and may feel them through its feet. A housefly and a butterfly taste with their feet. A catfish has taste buds all over its skin, so its whole body tastes the water. An octopus tastes with its suckers. A silk moth can smell a female moth from far away. Our own Umwelt is just one of millions. An Indian scientist, Obaid Siddiqi, studied how fruit flies taste and smell. He showed that the genes of a tiny fly can teach us how senses and nerves work.
- e.g. Crickets have their ears on their front legs.
- e.g. Mosquitoes find you partly by smelling the breath and sweat you give off.
### People's senses really differ · *defined*
**Definition:** Different people's senses are not exactly the same. Some people taste bitterness more strongly, some smell certain molecules differently, and some see colours differently.

About 8 in every 100 boys and men, and about 1 in every 200 girls and women, see some colours differently from most people. Usually red and green look alike to them. An American scientist, Linda Bartoshuk, found that some people are 'supertasters'. They have more taste buds and find bitter foods, like karela, much more bitter. For some people, fresh dhania leaves taste like soap. This is partly because of their genes. So when two people taste the same chutney, they may not taste quite the same thing. Neither one is wrong.
- e.g. You might love karela while your brother can hardly eat it. Your tongues may really be different.
- e.g. A colour-blind child may find it hard to tell a ripe red tomato from a green one by colour alone.
### How do signals become experience? · *debated*
Scientists can follow a sense all the way from a ripe mango to electric signals in the brain. But here is the mystery. Why does it feel like anything at all? Why are some signals the warm yellow of a mango, and others its sweet smell? Scientists do not yet know. Some call this the hardest question about the mind.
- e.g. You cannot know for sure that the red you see is the same as the red your friend sees. You both call it 'red'.
- e.g. A bat's echoes must feel like something to a bat. Can we ever imagine what?
- **Most brain scientists:** Experience is something the brain does. As we learn more about how brain cells work together, we will understand how signals become seeing and tasting.
- **David Chalmers, an Australian philosopher, in 1995:** Explaining what brain cells do is one problem. Explaining why it feels like something is a different, much harder problem. He called it 'the hard problem'. It is still open.
- **Thomas Nagel, an American philosopher, in 1974:** He asked, 'What is it like to be a bat?' Even if we knew everything about a bat's brain, we might still not know what its echoes feel like to it.
- **Some philosophers, such as Philip Goff:** Maybe a tiny bit of awareness is part of everything, all the way down. This idea is called panpsychism. Most scientists do not accept it, but nobody has disproved it.

## Mostly agreed
- Every sense works by receptor cells detecting something from the world and turning it into nerve signals for the brain. *How we know:* Scientists can record the tiny electric signals in single receptor cells and nerves, for example in a rod cell when light hits it.
- There are five basic tastes, and every part of the tongue with taste buds can taste all of them. *How we know:* Careful taste tests since 1974, and the discovery of the taste receptors themselves, spread across the whole tongue.
- Most of the flavour of food comes from smell. *How we know:* Pinch your nose and you can no longer tell cinnamon sugar from plain sugar. People who lose their sense of smell say food has lost its taste.
- In people, the hair cells of the inner ear do not grow back, so loud noise can cause hearing loss that lasts. *How we know:* Doctors find damaged hair cells in the ears of people who worked for years in loud places, like factories.
- Many animals sense things humans cannot, such as ultraviolet light, echoes, very low rumbles and the warmth of prey in the dark. *How we know:* Careful tests: bees trained to visit ultraviolet patterns, bats catching moths in the dark, snakes striking at warm targets with their eyes covered.

## How we found out
**Aristotle in ancient Greece, Charles Bell and Alexander Crum Brown in Scotland, Pierre Flourens in France, Ernst Mach and Josef Breuer in Austria, and Charles Sherrington in England, About 350 BCE to 1906.** About 2,350 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle wrote a book called On the Soul. In it he listed five senses: sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch. Each one had a body part you could point to: eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin. Aristotle also argued that there could be no sixth sense. He was so respected that books copied his list for about 2,000 years. It was also easy to teach, because every sense had an organ to show. But some senses have no organ you can see. In the 1820s, a Scottish surgeon called Charles Bell described a 'muscle sense' that tells you where your arms and legs are. In 1906, Charles Sherrington named it proprioception. Also in the 1820s, a French scientist, Pierre Flourens, damaged the tiny curved tubes deep inside pigeons' ears, and the birds lost their balance. In 1873 and 1874, three scientists, Ernst Mach, Josef Breuer and Alexander Crum Brown, each worked out that these tubes sense the head turning. Today scientists count many senses, from about a dozen to over 30, and they do not agree on one number. Textbooks still say five because it is an old habit, not because it is true.  
*What people believed before:* People believed there were exactly five senses, one for each organ you can see. Senses with no organ to point at, like the muscle sense and the balance sense of the inner ear, showed that the list was far too short.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Humans have exactly five senses.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker, in his book On the Soul, about 2,350 years ago. Many school books still say it.; *rejected*)  
  For: Five sense organs are easy to see: eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin. Many traditions, in India too, counted five.  
  Against: Scientists count many more. Balance, knowing where your hands are with your eyes shut, feeling hot and cold, pain, hunger and thirst all have their own receptors. Depending on how you count, there are somewhere between about a dozen and over 30.
- **Different parts of the tongue taste different tastes: sweet at the tip, bitter at the back.** (The 'tongue map', printed in school books for much of the 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: A study in 1901 did find tiny differences in how sensitive parts of the tongue were.  
  Against: The differences were tiny, and a 1942 drawing made them look like zones. Tests in 1974 and the discovery of taste receptors showed every part of the tongue with taste buds tastes everything.
- **Humans have a poor sense of smell.** (Many books, going back to the French brain scientist Paul Broca in the 1800s; *debated*)  
  For: Dogs, rats and elephants have many more kinds of smell receptor than we do, and dogs follow trails we cannot.  
  Against: In 2017 the scientist John McGann argued that this was based on a guess, not tests. People can smell some molecules at tiny amounts, and can follow a smell trail across grass.
- **There are six tastes: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent.** (The Ayurveda tradition of India, for example in the Charaka Samhita; *debated*)  
  For: These six really are six different feelings when we eat, and they are useful for cooking and describing food.  
  Against: Scientists count five tastes that the taste buds detect. Pungent, like chilli, and astringent, like raw banana, are detected by touch and pain nerve endings in the mouth, not by taste buds. And umami is missing from the six.
- **Umami is not a real taste. It is just a mix of the other tastes.** (Many scientists outside Japan, for most of the 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: Umami foods are often also salty, and it is hard to taste umami on its own.  
  Against: Around the year 2000, scientists found receptors on the tongue that detect glutamate, the umami molecule. So Kikunae Ikeda, who named umami in 1908, had been right all along.
- **Blind people have better ears than sighted people.** (A common belief; *debated*)  
  For: Many blind people notice sounds that sighted people miss, and some can find their way with echoes.  
  Against: Their ears are usually just the same. What changes is the brain. With practice it learns to get more out of the same sounds, and even uses the parts normally used for seeing.
- **Eating lots of carrots gives you night vision.** (A story spread in Britain during the Second World War; *rejected*)  
  For: Carrots have a substance the body turns into vitamin A. Without enough vitamin A, people really do get night blindness.  
  Against: Extra carrots do not give a healthy person better night vision than normal. The story was spread partly to hide the fact that British pilots were using radar.

## Roots
We have been learning how each sense detects the world and turns it into signals for the brain. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** With your nose pinched, elaichi sugar tastes only sweet. Let go, and the elaichi comes back. Your nose sensed it, yet the flavour seemed to be in your mouth. So most of a food's flavour comes through your nose, though you feel it on your tongue.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a feeling happens where we feel it, so flavour must be on the tongue. But the brain places every feeling, using all the signals it gets. Most of flavour comes from the nose, and the brain puts it in the mouth.  
*Evidence:* In the Hold Your Nose test, the spiced sugars tasted just sweet with the nose pinched. With the nose open, the spice came back, and it still seemed to be on the tongue. Scientists have found the same: smells from food in the mouth are felt as tastes.

- *who am i:* The elaichi seemed to be on your tongue, but your nose found it. You do not sense with one part at a time. Your whole body and brain sense together.
- *what is life:* Every living thing senses something. A bacterium senses sugar, a bee senses ultraviolet, and you sense a mango. Being alive means sensing the world and acting on what you sense.
- *what is this world:* The world holds far more than any animal can sense. Each creature detects only a small part of it, and builds its own world from that.

Your senses detect very different things: light, vibrations, pushes, warmth, gravity, the stretch of your muscles and many kinds of molecules. Yet every one of them becomes the same kind of electric signal. The brain joins all these signals into one world, so the mango you see, smell and taste is one mango. The same awareness is present in every creature that senses, from a bacterium to a bee to you. Each one senses its own part of the same world.

- **Samyutta Nikaya 35.23, the Sabba Sutta (the Buddha's teaching on 'the All'):** The Buddha was asked, 'What is the All?' He named six pairs. The eye and what it sees. The ear and sounds. The nose and smells. The tongue and tastes. The body and touches. The mind and thoughts. That, he said, is the All. (This unit found the same pairs: a receptor and what it detects, like a cone and light, or a smell cell and a molecule. The Buddha also counted the mind as a sense. In this unit, the brain joined the signals from the nose and tongue into one flavour.)
- **Katha Upanishad 1.3.3 to 1.3.4:** The body is like a chariot. The senses are the horses, and the things they sense are the roads. The mind is the reins, and understanding is the driver. The Self, the one who experiences, rides in the chariot. (Senses carry signals in from the world, like horses running along roads. The Upanishad asks who is riding. Science can follow the signals into the brain, but it cannot yet explain who or what experiences them.)
- **Tao Te Ching 12:** The five colours can blind the eye. The five notes can deafen the ear. The five flavours can dull the tongue. (The Tao Te Ching warns that chasing strong colours, sounds and flavours dulls the senses. Science has found something related. In the Fading Coin activity, your skin stopped feeling a coin that did not move. A sense given the same thing for a long time stops reporting it.)
- Science (settled): Every sense works by receptor cells detecting something from the world and turning it into electric signals for the brain.
- Science (open): Nobody knows how electric signals in the brain become experiences, like the taste of mango. This is sometimes called the hard problem of consciousness.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India, and many brain scientists today: The Charvaka thinkers taught that what the senses perceive is the only sure way of knowing, and that awareness ends when the body ends. Many brain scientists today agree in their own way: awareness is something the brain does, and there is no One behind it. These are honest views, held by thoughtful people.
- Practice, Circles of listening (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. First, listen for the furthest sound you can hear. Maybe a horn, or a bird far away. Now listen for something nearer, inside the house. Now nearer still: the sound of your own breath. Now just stay still for a few breaths. All those sounds came to you, without you doing anything. Who is it that is hearing them?
- Ask: When you hear a bird, is the sound out there in the tree, or in here inside you? Or both?
- Ask: A bee, a bat and you all sense the same garden. Whose garden is the real one?
- Ask: Your brain gets only electric signals. So who, or what, is tasting the mango?
- Ask: When you taste a mango, where is the taste: in the mango, on your tongue, in your nose, or in your brain?

## Story: Tara Listens to the Walls
*Tara, Arjun, Kannan and the house are made up. Tongue-click echolocation is real: some blind people, such as Daniel Kish in the USA, use it to walk, hike and even ride bicycles, and scientists have studied it since the 1940s.*

Arjun came to his cousin's house in Madurai for the summer holidays. It was an old house with too many rooms. The rooms were joined by long, dim passages, and an open courtyard sat in the middle.

His cousin Tara was ten, a year older than him. She had been blind since she was born. On the train, Arjun had worried about what to say to her. He had imagined her world as an endless dark room, with nothing in it at all.

But Tara walked through the passages faster than he did. As she walked, she made a small sound with her tongue against the roof of her mouth. Tock. Tock. Like a drop of water falling into a steel bucket.

'Why do you keep doing that?' Arjun asked on the first day.

'I'm listening to the walls,' said Tara.

Arjun laughed, because he thought it was a joke. Tara did not laugh. She just went on clicking, and turned left exactly where the passage turned left.

Over the next few days, Arjun watched her. She knew the kitchen by the nutty smell of sesame oil, and the terrace by the jasmine flowers in their pots. In the courtyard she took off her chappals, because the stone floor was cool and told her feet where she was. She found the brass knob of her grandfather's door without reaching for it twice. Arjun began to feel that Tara lived in a full house, and he lived in an empty one.

Then came the evening of the storm.

It started with a game of hide and seek. Kannan, Arjun's little brother, was four, and he was very good at hiding. He wore silver anklets with tiny bells, so the grown-ups could always hear where he was. That evening, nobody could find him.

At first it was funny. Then the sky turned the colour of a bruise, and the rain came down on the roof like a thousand drums. With a click and a sigh, the lights went out. The inverter had been broken for a week. The whole house fell into a darkness so thick that Arjun could not see his own hand.

Everyone began to shout at once. 'Kannan! Kannan!' Mama switched on the torch of his phone. It made one small bright circle on the wall, and everything outside the circle became even blacker. Mami's phone had no charge. Paati was calling from upstairs. The rain roared. Arjun ran down the passage towards where he thought the storeroom was. He hit a doorframe with his shoulder and sat down hard on the floor, his eyes wide open and useless.

Then a voice cut through the noise. It was not loud. It was very calm.

'Everyone,' said Tara. 'Please stop talking.'

Something in her voice made them do it. One by one, the shouting stopped. Mama switched off his torch. Now there was only the rain, and the dark, and everyone breathing.

Tara stood very still in the middle of the passage. Arjun could just make out her shape. She turned her head slowly to the right, and then slowly to the left, the way a bird does.

'There,' she whispered. 'He moved in his sleep. Left, and low down.'

Arjun had heard nothing. He strained his ears until they hurt. Nothing but rain.

Tara found his hand in the dark. 'Close your eyes,' she said.

'It's dark anyway. What's the point?'

'Your eyes are still trying,' said Tara. 'They're shouting at you. Close them, so you can hear.'

Arjun closed his eyes. At first nothing changed. Then, under the drumming of the rain, he heard it. Chhm. The tiniest sound, like a grain of rice dropped on a plate. He turned his head the way Tara had. When his left ear faced the sound, it was a little clearer. When he turned away, it faded.

'Left,' he breathed.

'Left,' agreed Tara.

She led him by the hand, and she clicked. Tock. Tock. Arjun began to hear what she heard. When they walked close to a wall, the click came back sharp and quick, as if the wall were clicking back. In the open courtyard, the click floated away and was gone. At a doorway, it changed again. It became hollow, as if the sound were falling into a well.

'The storeroom,' said Tara. Arjun smelled it before she said it: old rice sacks, dust and camphor. His fingers touched something cold and smooth. It was one of the big steel trunks. Tara knelt. Behind the last trunk, curled up on a folded quilt, Kannan was fast asleep with his thumb in his mouth. As Tara touched his foot, his anklet chimed once more.

At that moment the lights came back on. Everyone appeared at the storeroom door at once, blinking and squinting, their hands over their eyes. Mami scooped Kannan up and he woke, very cross, and asked why everyone was looking at him. Tara simply stood there. For her, nothing had changed at all.

That night, Arjun lay in bed with his eyes shut. The rain had stopped. The house was quiet. He made a small sound with his tongue. Tock. From the wall beside his head, faintly, something answered, like a hand cupped close to his ear.

He lay very still and listened. The room was not empty after all. It never had been.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the world Arjun experiences is not in his eyes. It is built by his brain from whatever signals arrive. When his eyes gave him nothing, they were still 'shouting', and Tara teaches him to let them rest so his ears can work. Then he hears what she hears. Two ears comparing a tiny sound tell him its direction. Echoes from her clicks tell him how near the walls are. Smell and touch tell him where he is. Tara does not have better ears than him. She has trained her brain to use them, and to mix all her senses into one full world.

- Ask: Why could Tara hear Kannan's anklet when everyone else could not?
- Ask: Why did Tara tell Arjun to close his eyes, even though it was already dark?
- Ask: How did turning his head help Arjun find which way the sound came from?
- Ask: What did Tara's clicks sound like near a wall, in the open courtyard and at a doorway?
- Ask: At the start, Arjun thought Tara lived in an empty dark room. At the end, what did he think?

  - Note: Grind whole spices with a little sugar in a mortar if you have no ground spice. Mix really well, so there are no lumps of spice to feel.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the blindfold on the taster. 2. Ask them to make their guesses before tasting anything.  
  *Parent says:* Before we start, guess. With your nose pinched shut, will you be able to name the spice? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put on the blindfold. Now guess. With your nose pinched, will you name the spice?
  - Ask: Where do you think taste comes from? (good: From my tongue.; From my tongue and my nose.)
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The taster pinches their nose firmly shut. 2. Put about a quarter teaspoon from one bowl on their tongue. Choose the bowls in a mixed-up order. 3. They keep the nose pinched and say what they taste. 4. Write their guess. 5. They sip water. 6. Do all four bowls.  
  *Parent says:* Keep your nose pinched tight, the whole time. What can you taste? Is there a spice? Which one?  
  *Child mode:* Pinch your nose tight. Keep it pinched. Taste the sugar. Is there a spice? Which one?
  - Note: Most children say 'just sweet' for every bowl. If they name a spice, check that the nose is fully closed. Grainy bits of spice can also give a clue, so mix more finely next time.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give a spoonful from the first bowl again, with the nose pinched. 2. Halfway through, say 'Let go!' 3. The taster lets go of their nose while the sugar is still in their mouth. 4. Repeat for every bowl, and write the new guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Pinch again. Taste. Now, let go of your nose! What happened?  
  *Child mode:* Pinch your nose and taste. Now let go! What happened? Does the spice appear?
  - Ask: What changed when you let go of your nose? (good: The spice suddenly appeared.; I could tell it was elaichi.)
  - Check: With your nose pinched, the elaichi sugar tasted only sweet. When you let go, you could taste elaichi. Why? → **The smell of elaichi could float up to your nose again**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now about where the spice flavour comes from? What did you notice that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I noticed. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the spice flavour comes from my nose, not my tongue. What I noticed: with my nose pinched, all four bowls tasted just sweet. When I let go, I could name the spice. Why: the smell of the spice floats up into my nose when I chew.
### e2 · The Two-Point Test: where is touch sharpest? (25 min)
- **question:** Which parts of your skin can feel two points close together, and which feel them as just one?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put these in order, from the place you think feels most detail to the place that feels least: fingertip, palm, forearm, back. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only the place on the skin. Use the same testers and press just as gently every time, so both points touch at the same moment. Mix in some touches with only one point, so the person cannot just say 'two' every time. Do 3 two-point touches at each gap on each place, with one single-point touch mixed in.
- **safety:** Touch gently. Use toothpicks with blunt ends, or snip the sharp tips off first. Never touch near the eyes or the face.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw an outline of a body and colour the places that felt the most detail.
- **results table:** Place on the skin | Gap 0.5 cm: felt two? | Gap 1 cm: felt two? | Gap 2 cm: felt two? | Gap 4 cm: felt two? | Smallest gap felt as two
- **s1 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On each cardboard strip, mark two dots with the ruler. The gaps are 0.5 cm on the first strip and 1 cm on the second. They are 2 cm on the third and 4 cm on the fourth. 2. Poke a toothpick through each dot, blunt end sticking out about 1 cm on the same side. 3. Tape them firmly in place. 4. Keep one extra toothpick as the 'one point' tester.  
  *Parent says:* We're making four touch testers. Each has two toothpick points, with a different gap between them. We'll find out where your skin can tell two points from one.  
  *Child mode:* Make four testers. Each has two toothpick points. The gaps are 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 centimetres. Check with a ruler.
  - Note: A grown-up pokes the holes. Many toothpicks are pointed at both ends. If yours are, a grown-up snips the very tip off each point with scissors, so it presses without pricking. Both points must stick out the same amount, or one will touch first and give the answer away. Test each tester on your own palm first: both points should press together.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The person being tested shuts their eyes. 2. Start with the fingertip and the 0.5 cm tester. Touch it gently 3 times with the tester and once with the single toothpick, in a mixed-up order. 3. Ask 'one or two?' each time. 4. If they said two for at least 2 of the 3 two-point touches, write yes for that gap. 5. Do the same with the 1 cm, 2 cm and 4 cm testers. 6. Then test the palm, the forearm and the upper back in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes shut. I'm going to touch your skin. Sometimes with one point, sometimes with two. Each time, tell me: one or two?  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Someone touches your skin with the points. Each time, say: one or two? Be honest. Guessing is fine.
  - Note: What to expect: most fingertips feel two points even at 0.5 cm. The palm usually needs about 1 cm. The forearm often needs 4 cm or more. On the back, even 4 cm can feel like one point. If the results are mixed, do 2 more touches.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Put the places in order, from the one that felt most detail to the one that felt least.  
  *Parent says:* Which place could feel two points closest together? Why would that part of your body need to feel so much detail?  
  *Child mode:* Which place felt two points closest together? Why might your fingertips need that?
  - Check: Your fingertip felt two points 0.5 cm apart. Your back felt two points 4 cm apart as just one. Why? → **Your fingertip has touch receptors packed much closer together**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now about touch? What did you find that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I found. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: fingertips feel the most detail. What I found: my fingertip felt two points at 0.5 cm, but my back felt two points 4 cm apart as one. Why: fingertips have touch receptors packed closer together.
### e3 · Point at the Sound: are two ears better than one? (20 min)
- **question:** Can you point to where a sound comes from with your eyes shut? Do you need both ears to do it?
- **predict:** Guess first. Out of 8 sounds, how many will you point to correctly with two ears? How many with one ear blocked? Which direction will be hardest, and why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only the number of ears. Use the same sound, at the same loudness, from the same 8 places, at the same distance of about 2 big steps. Do the places in a mixed-up order each time.
- **safety:** Clear the floor so the blindfolded person cannot trip. Never make the sound right next to anyone's ear. Do not push the cotton wool deep into the ear: just hold it at the opening.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Count the right answers for two ears and for one ear. Draw a bar for each.
- **results table:** Where the sound came from | Two ears: right or wrong? | One ear blocked: right or wrong?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the stool in the middle of a clear room. 2. The listener sits on it and puts on the blindfold. 3. Choose 8 places around them, about 2 big steps away: front, behind, left, right, front-left, front-right, back-left and back-right.  
  *Parent says:* You're going to be a sound detective. I'll click two spoons from somewhere in the room. Point to where you think the sound came from.  
  *Child mode:* Sit on the stool. Put on the blindfold. Listen for a click. Point to where it came from.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Walk quietly to one of the 8 places. 2. Click the spoons once, softly. 3. The listener points. If they point within about an arm's width of you, it is right. 4. Write right or wrong. 5. Do all 8 places in a mixed-up order.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Listen... Point! Now wait for the next one.  
  *Child mode:* Listen. Point to the click. Wait for the next one. Keep your eyes covered.
  - Note: Walk quietly in socks, so your footsteps do not give the answer away. Make each click the same, soft but clear.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The listener blocks one ear. They hold a small ball of cotton wool at the opening and press their flat palm firmly over that ear. 2. Do all 8 places again, in a new mixed-up order. 3. Write right or wrong for each.  
  *Parent says:* Now block one ear, tightly. Let's see if you can still find the sounds.  
  *Child mode:* Block one ear. Press your palm over it, tight. Now point to the clicks again.
  - Note: Blocking an ear by hand never stops all the sound, so the difference may be smaller than you expect. If the scores are the same, make the clicks much softer, or rub two fingers together instead. Sounds straight in front or behind are often mixed up, even with two ears.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the right answers in each round and compare them.  
  *Parent says:* How many did you get with two ears? With one? Which places were hardest?  
  *Child mode:* Count your right answers. Two ears or one: which was better? Which places were hardest?
  - Check: In the sound test, most people find sounds better with two ears than with one. Why? → **The brain compares when and how loudly the sound reaches each ear**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now about two ears? What did you find that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I found. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: two ears are better for finding sounds. What I found: I got 7 out of 8 right with two ears, but only 4 out of 8 with one ear blocked. Why: my brain compares my two ears to work out where the sound is.

### a1 · One Quiet Minute: what are your senses telling you? (8 min)
- **safety:** Do this sitting down, somewhere safe.
- **record:** Draw six columns: ears, skin, nose, tongue, eyes, and inside my body. Write everything you noticed in the right column.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit down together, somewhere ordinary, like the kitchen or the balcony. 2. Set a timer for one minute. 3. Both of you close your eyes.  
  *Parent says:* For one minute, let's be completely still and quiet, with our eyes shut. Notice everything your body is telling you. Every sound, every smell, every touch, and anything you feel inside you. Don't say anything until the timer rings.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Close your eyes. For one minute, just notice. Every sound. Every smell. Every touch.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When the timer rings, each of you makes a six-column list: ears, skin, nose, tongue, eyes, and inside my body.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's write everything we noticed. Put each thing under the sense that noticed it. Which column is longest?  
  *Child mode:* Open your eyes. Make six columns: ears, skin, nose, tongue, eyes, and inside my body. Write everything you noticed.
  - Note: Good finds: a fan humming, a bird outside, the floor pressing on your bottom, a cooking smell, the taste left from breakfast, warmth from the sun. For 'inside my body': feeling hungry or thirsty, a heartbeat, tired legs, needing the toilet. Children are often surprised how much they notice with their eyes shut.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare your two lists, and talk about the questions.  
  *Parent says:* Did you notice anything you never usually notice? Was the fan there before we started, or did we just not hear it?  
  *Child mode:* Compare lists. Did you notice something new? Was it there before, or did you just not notice it?
  - Think: What do you wonder about your senses? Write one question. You'll find many answers in this unit.
### a2 · Feel the Sound: three ways to feel and see a vibration (15 min)
- **safety:** Never bang the plate near anyone's ears. Keep the phone volume moderate, not at its loudest.
- **record:** Draw the path of a sound: from the plate, through the air, to the cling film. Then draw your ear doing the same.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play music with a strong beat on the phone. 2. Hold the balloon between both hands, touching the phone's speaker. 3. Feel it. 4. Move it away slowly.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the balloon against the speaker. What do your hands feel? Sound is a vibration of the air. The balloon picks up the vibration, just like your eardrum does.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the balloon against the speaker. Can you feel it shake? That shaking is sound.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stretch cling film tightly over the bowl, like a drum skin, and hold it with the rubber band. 2. Sprinkle a few sugar grains on top. 3. Hold the steel plate about one hand's length above the bowl, a little to one side. 4. Bang it hard with the ladle. 5. Watch the grains.  
  *Parent says:* This cling film is like your eardrum. Watch the sugar when I bang the plate.  
  *Child mode:* Stretch cling film over a bowl. Sprinkle sugar on top. Bang a plate near it. Watch the sugar jump!
  - Note: The cling film must be tight, like a drum. Hold the plate close, about one hand's length away. Sugar or salt grains jump best because they are light. If they hardly move, bang harder or hold the plate closer. A phone playing a deep bass note held just above the film also works.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child puts one ear flat on the table. 2. Tap the far end of the table gently with a pencil. 3. Then lift their head and tap again the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Put your ear on the table. I'll tap the other end. Now lift your head and listen again. Which was louder?  
  *Child mode:* Put your ear on the table. Listen to the tap. Now lift your head. Which was louder?
  - Note: Most children hear the tap much louder through the table. Sound travels well through solid things, because their particles are packed close.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about how all three were the same thing.  
  *Parent says:* The balloon, the cling film and the table all shook. Your eardrum shakes in the same way. Behind it, three tiny bones pass the shaking to your cochlea.  
  *Child mode:* The balloon, the film and the table all shook. Your eardrum shakes just like that.
  - Check: The sugar jumped on the cling film when the plate was banged. What reached the cling film? → **Shaking air, a vibration**
### a3 · The Mystery Bag: how much can fingertips tell? (12 min)
- **safety:** Nothing sharp in the bag. Small things are a choking risk for babies, so keep them away from little brothers and sisters.
- **record:** Write two numbers: how many things you named with bare fingers, and how many with socks on.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the 8 things in the bag without your child seeing them.  
  *Parent says:* There are 8 things hidden in this bag. You can only use your fingers. Before you guess, describe each one: is it rough or smooth, hard or soft, cold or warm, heavy or light?  
  *Child mode:* There are 8 things in the bag. Feel them without looking. Describe each one before you guess.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child reaches in with bare hands. 2. For each thing, they describe it first, then guess. 3. Count the right guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me how it feels first. Then guess. Which part of your hand are you using most?  
  *Child mode:* Feel one thing. Say how it feels. Then guess what it is.
  - Ask: How did you tell the coin from the button? (good: The coin was cold and heavy.; The coin had a ridged edge. The button had holes.)

  *Child mode:* Put socks on your hands. Try again. Is it harder? Why?
  - Note: With socks on, most children can still tell big shapes, but lose small details like the ridges on a coin or the seam of an elaichi pod. The sock stops the tiny pushes from reaching the touch receptors in the fingertips.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the star-nosed mole and Braille.  
  *Parent says:* A star-nosed mole finds food in dark tunnels with 22 fleshy fingers on its nose. Blind people read Braille with their fingertips. Why fingertips, and not elbows?  
  *Child mode:* A star-nosed mole touches with its nose. Blind people read with their fingertips. Why not elbows?
  - Think: Cold steel feels colder than a cotton ball, even in the same room. Are they really different temperatures?
### a4 · The Fading Coin: senses notice changes (10 min)
- **safety:** Waft the smell towards your nose. Do not put your nose right into the jar.
- **record:** Write how many seconds until you stopped feeling the coin. Then write your smell scores, from 5 down.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lies or sits still with their forearm resting on a table, palm up, eyes shut. 2. Put the coin gently on the forearm and start the clock. 3. They raise a finger of the other hand when they can no longer feel the coin. 4. Write the time. 5. Then nudge the coin slightly. Ask what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Keep very still. Raise a finger when you stop feeling the coin. Now... I'll move it a tiny bit.  
  *Child mode:* Eyes shut. Keep still. Raise a finger when you stop feeling the coin.
  - Note: Many children stop noticing the coin within about half a minute if they keep perfectly still. When you nudge it, they feel it again at once. Touch receptors mostly report changes. If they still feel it after a minute, that's fine: everyone is different.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child wafts the smell from the jar towards their nose and gives it a score from 5 (very strong) to 0 (nothing). 2. Keep wafting gently every 15 seconds for 2 minutes, and score each time. 3. Then go into another room for 2 minutes, come back and smell again.  
  *Parent says:* Score the smell from 5 to 0. Keep going every 15 seconds. Does it stay the same?  
  *Child mode:* Waft the smell. Score it: 5 is very strong, 0 is nothing. Again every 15 seconds.
  - Note: The score usually drops a little, perhaps from 5 to 3. After a break, it often jumps back up. This is called adaptation. It is why you cannot smell your own home, but visitors can.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about why senses work like this.  
  *Parent says:* Your senses mostly tell you what is changing. Why might that be useful?  
  *Child mode:* Your senses mostly notice changes. Why might that be useful?
  - Check: You stopped feeling the coin, but felt it again when it moved. What does that show? → **Touch receptors mostly report changes, not things that stay the same**
### a5 · The Blue Mango: make your cones tired (15 min)
- **safety:** Never stare at the Sun or a bright bulb. Only stare at the paper drawing.
- **record:** Draw what you saw on the white paper, in the colours you saw.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On one sheet, draw a big mango shape, as big as your hand. 2. Colour it in solid, bright blue. 3. Draw a leaf on top and colour it pink. 4. Put a small black dot in the middle of the mango.  
  *Parent says:* We're drawing a strange mango: a blue mango with a pink leaf. Colour it in thickly, with no white gaps.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a big mango. Colour it bright blue. Give it a pink leaf. Put a black dot in the middle.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the drawing in good light. 2. Your child stares at the black dot for 30 seconds without moving their eyes. 3. Then they look straight at the blank white sheet. 4. Wait a few seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Stare at the black dot. Don't move your eyes. Count 30 slow seconds with me. Now look at the white paper. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Stare at the dot for 30 seconds. Don't move your eyes! Now look at the white paper. What do you see?
  - Note: Most children see a faint yellow-orange mango with a green leaf floating on the white paper. It lasts a few seconds. If nothing appears, colour more thickly, use brighter light, and stare a full 30 seconds without blinking much.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain what happened, using the cones.  
  *Parent says:* Your eye has cones for three colours. Staring at blue tired out your blue cones. When you looked at white, the tired blue cones sent weaker signals than the others. So your brain saw the colour opposite to blue, which is yellow. Pink light works your red and blue cones but hardly your green ones. So after the pink leaf, your green cones sent the strongest signals, and you saw a green leaf.  
  *Child mode:* Staring tired out some of your colour cells. Then the rested ones sent stronger signals. So you saw the opposite colours!
  - Check: After staring at the blue mango, you saw a yellow mango on white paper. Why? → **Your blue cones were tired, so the others sent more signals**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about where the yellow mango was.  
  *Parent says:* The paper was blank. So where was the yellow mango? On the paper, in your eye, or in your brain?  
  *Child mode:* The paper was blank. So where was the yellow mango?
  - Think: If your eyes and brain can make a colour that is not there, how do you know the colours you usually see are there?
### a6 · Two Eyes, One Picture: the pencil-tip test (10 min)
- **safety:** Use capped pens or blunt pencils. Keep them at arm's length from your face.
- **record:** Write how many touches out of 5 you managed with two eyes, and with one eye.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold a pencil upright at arm's length. 2. Your child holds another pencil and tries to touch its tip to the tip of yours, coming from the side. 3. Do it 5 times with both eyes open. 4. Count the hits.  
  *Parent says:* Touch your pencil tip to mine. Come in from the side. Let's try 5 times.  
  *Child mode:* Touch your pencil tip to the other pencil tip. Try 5 times. Count your hits.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Move your pencil to a new spot. 2. Your child closes one eye. 3. They try 5 times again. 4. Count the hits.  
  *Parent says:* Now close one eye, and try again. Is it easier or harder?  
  *Child mode:* Close one eye. Try 5 more times. Easier or harder?
  - Note: Most children miss much more often with one eye shut. They overshoot or stop short. Each eye sees from a slightly different place, and the brain compares the two pictures to judge how far away things are.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the 'jumping thumb' to see the two pictures.  
  *Parent says:* Hold your thumb up in front of you. Close one eye, then the other, again and again. Your thumb jumps! Each eye sees a slightly different picture. Your brain joins them and works out how far away things are.  
  *Child mode:* Hold up your thumb. Close one eye, then the other. Your thumb jumps! Each eye sees it from a different place.
  - Think: An owl has both eyes on the front of its face. A pigeon has eyes on the sides. Which one sees further around? Which one judges distance better?
### a7 · Senses You Forgot You Had: balance, body position, warm and cold (15 min)
- **safety:** Do the balance test next to a wall on a clear floor, with a grown-up standing close. The warm water must be only comfortably warm, like a bath. A grown-up tests it with an elbow first.
- **record:** Write how many seconds you stood on one foot with eyes open and with eyes shut. Write how many times out of 6 you touched your nose. Write what each hand felt in the middle bowl.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands next to a wall, on one foot, with eyes open. 2. Count the seconds until the other foot touches the floor. Stop at 30. 3. Now they try again with eyes shut. 4. Write both times.  
  *Parent says:* Stand on one foot and look straight ahead. I'll count. Now do it again with your eyes shut. Before you try, guess: will it be easier or harder? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Stand on one foot near a wall. Count how long you can stay up. Now close your eyes and try again. Is it harder?
  - Note: Most children stand for the full 30 seconds with eyes open, but only a few seconds with eyes shut. The brain balances with three senses together: the eyes, the balance organs in the inner ears and the stretch sensors in the muscles. Shutting the eyes takes one away.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shuts their eyes and stretches both arms out to the sides. 2. They touch the tip of their nose with one pointing finger, then put the arm back out. 3. Do it 3 times with each hand. 4. Count the touches out of 6.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes shut. Arms out wide. Now touch the tip of your nose with one finger. How did your finger know where your nose was?  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Stretch your arms out. Touch your nose with one finger. Now the other hand. How did you know where your nose was?
  - Ask: How did your finger find your nose without your eyes? (good: My body knows where my arm is.; Something in my arm told my brain where it was.)
  - Note: Most children touch their nose almost every time. Stretch sensors in the muscles and joints tell the brain where each part of the body is. This sense is called proprioception.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill three bowls: cold water on the left, water straight from the tap in the middle, warm water on the right. 2. A grown-up tests the warm bowl with an elbow. 3. Your child puts the left hand in the cold bowl and the right hand in the warm bowl. 4. They hold them there for 1 minute. 5. Then both hands go into the middle bowl at the same time.  
  *Parent says:* Before you put both hands in the middle bowl, guess: will they feel the same? Now try it. What does each hand tell you?  
  *Child mode:* Left hand in the cold bowl. Right hand in the warm bowl. Wait one minute. Now put both hands in the middle bowl. What does each hand feel?
  - Note: The hand that was in cold water feels the middle bowl as warm. The hand that was in warm water feels it as cold. The warm and cold nerve endings report the change from what they are used to, not the exact temperature. The English thinker John Locke wrote about this test in 1690.
  - Check: Both your hands were in the same middle bowl. The hand from the cold bowl felt warm, and the hand from the warm bowl felt cold. Why? → **Your warm and cold nerve endings report the change from what they were used to**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Dry hands with the towel. Then talk about the senses inside the body.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes for a moment. Are you hungry? Thirsty? Do you need the toilet? Which sense told you? None of your eyes, ears, nose, tongue or skin did.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Are you hungry or thirsty? Which part of you told you?
  - Note: This is interoception, sensing the inside of the body. Stretch sensors notice a full stomach or bladder. Cells in the brain notice when the blood is too salty, and that makes you thirsty.
  - Think: Aristotle counted five senses. How many have you used today?

  *Child mode:* Put each smelly thing in a jar. Number the jars underneath. Write the list and hide it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Blindfold your child. 2. Give the jars in a mixed-up order. 3. They waft each one and name it. 4. Write their first score out of 6. 5. Then take off the blindfold and go through them once, saying each name.  
  *Parent says:* Wave the air towards your nose. What is it? It's fine not to know. Lots of people know a smell but can't find its name.  
  *Child mode:* Blindfold on. Waft each jar towards your nose. Name it. It's fine if you can't.
  - Note: A first score of 2 or 3 out of 6 is very normal. People often say 'I know it, but I can't think of the word'. Scientists call this the 'tip of the nose' feeling.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2 and day 3, go through all 6 jars with eyes open. Smell each one, say its name and choose one describing word for it, like 'warm', 'sharp' or 'sweet'.  
  *Parent says:* Smell it, say its name, and give it a describing word. Words help your brain hold on to a smell.  
  *Child mode:* Smell each jar. Say its name. Give it one describing word, like warm or sharp.
  - Note: The Jahai people of the Malaysian rainforest have many words just for smells. A scientist called Asifa Majid found they name smells as easily as English speakers name colours.
  - Wait: Practise once a day for 2 days.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 3, test a family member who has not practised. 2. On day 4, blindfold your child and test them again, in a new order. 3. Compare the scores.  
  *Parent says:* Let's see if your nose has learned. Did your score change from day 1?  
  *Child mode:* Test a grown-up who hasn't practised. Then test yourself again. Did your score go up?
  - Check: Your nose-test score went from 3 on day 1 to 6 on day 4. What is the best explanation? → **Your brain got better at matching smells to their names**
- **s5 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the bar chart. Then ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. Can noses learn? What did you find that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two bars: day 1 and day 4. Then say what you think, what you found, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: practice makes me better at naming smells. What I found: I got 3 out of 6 on day 1 and 6 out of 6 on day 4. Appa, who did not practise, got 3. Why: my brain learned to link each smell to its name.

### c2 · A Kitchen Shelf for Someone Who Cannot See (35 min)
- **problem:** Imagine a friend who is blind is coming to cook with you. Six jars look exactly the same: sugar, salt, turmeric, chilli powder, jeera and rice. Make labels they can read without seeing, so they can find the sugar first time.

Make the dots for the first letter of each jar, for example T for turmeric and J for jeera.; Use shape as well as texture: a triangle of card for chilli, because chilli is sharp and hot.; Ask a blind person, or an organisation that works with blind people, what labels they really use.

### Sense Detective (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) What does a receptor do?  
   Options: It stores food for the body / It notices one kind of thing, like light or a push, and makes a nerve signal / It pumps blood / It makes sounds  
   **Answer:** It notices one kind of thing, like light or a push, and makes a nerve signal. *Why:* A receptor is a special cell that notices one kind of thing and turns it into a tiny electric signal. Picking 'makes sounds' usually mixes up catching a sound with making one.
2. (fill) Nerves carry tiny electric ____ from the senses to the brain.  
   **Answer:** ['signals']. *Why:* Every sense sends its messages to the brain as tiny pulses of electricity called nerve signals.
3. (match) Match each sense organ to what it detects.  
   **Answer:** Ear → shaking air; Eye → light; Nose → molecules in the air; Tongue → molecules in food; Skin → pressure, warmth, cold and pain. *Why:* Each sense organ detects one kind of thing from the world and has receptors built for it.
4. (order) Put the path of a sound in order, from outside to the brain: eardrum, cochlea, ear flap, three tiny bones, ear canal, nerve.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* The ear flap collects the sound. It goes down the ear canal and shakes the eardrum. Three tiny bones pass the shaking to the cochlea. Hair cells there make signals, and the nerve carries them to the brain.
5. (truefalse) The smallest bone in your body is inside your ear.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The stirrup, one of the three tiny bones behind the eardrum, is about the size of a grain of rice.
6. (mcq) In the Point at the Sound test, which sounds are hardest to point to?  
   Options: Sounds on your left / Sounds on your right / Sounds straight in front or straight behind / Loud sounds  
   **Answer:** Sounds straight in front or straight behind. *Why:* A sound straight in front or behind reaches both ears at the same moment and loudness. So the brain has no difference to compare. Picking 'left' or 'right' usually means forgetting that the brain uses the difference between the two ears.
7. (mcq) In the Two-Point Test, which place felt two points closest together?  
   Options: The back / The forearm / The fingertip / They were all the same  
   **Answer:** The fingertip. *Why:* Fingertips have touch receptors packed very close together, so they feel the most detail. Picking 'all the same' usually means thinking all skin is alike. The test shows it is not.
8. (sort) Sort these foods by their main basic taste.  
   **Answer:** gur → Sweet; imli → Sour; papad → Salty; karela → Bitter; ripe tomato → Umami; lemon → Sour; honey → Sweet; neem leaf → Bitter. *Why:* Sweet foods have sugars. Sour foods have acids. Salty foods have salt. Bitter foods, like karela and neem, have bitter molecules. Ripe tomatoes have glutamate, which gives umami.
9. (truefalse) The tip of your tongue can only taste sweet things.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. That is the old tongue map, and it is wrong. Every part of the tongue with taste buds can taste all five basic tastes.
10. (mcq) Why does food taste flat when you have a blocked nose?  
   Options: Your tongue stops working / Most flavour is really smell, and the smell cannot reach your nose / The food goes cold / A cold makes food less salty  
   **Answer:** Most flavour is really smell, and the smell cannot reach your nose. *Why:* Your tongue still tastes sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami. But the smells from food cannot float up into a blocked nose, and most flavour is smell. Picking 'tongue stops working' usually means thinking all taste comes from the tongue.
11. (fill) Taste plus smell together make ____.  
   **Answer:** ['flavour', 'flavor']. *Why:* Flavour is taste from the tongue mixed with smell from the nose, plus touch and temperature.
12. (mcq) Which cells in your eye see colour?  
   Options: Rods / Cones / Hair cells / Taste cells  
   **Answer:** Cones. *Why:* Cones see colour, in bright light. Rods work in dim light but see no colour. Hair cells are in the ear. Picking rods is a common mix-up, because the two words go together.
13. (mcq) Which animal tastes with its feet?  
   Options: A housefly / A cat / An elephant / A bat  
   **Answer:** A housefly. *Why:* Houseflies and butterflies have taste hairs on their feet, so they taste what they land on. Picking 'cat' may come from remembering that cats are unusual tasters. Cats cannot taste sweet.
14. (write) Your friend says, 'We have exactly five senses.' What would you tell them? Give one example.  
   **Answer:** We have more than five. For example, balance tells us if we are tipping over, and we can feel where our hands are with our eyes shut.. *Why:* The five senses are an old list. Scientists count many more, each with its own receptors.
15. (mcq) In Senses You Forgot You Had, standing on one foot was much harder with your eyes shut. Why?  
   Options: Your legs get weaker when your eyes are shut / Your brain balances using your eyes, your inner ears and your muscle sensors together, and you took one away / Your ears stop working when your eyes are shut / Balance only uses the eyes  
   **Answer:** Your brain balances using your eyes, your inner ears and your muscle sensors together, and you took one away. *Why:* Balance uses three senses at once: your eyes, the balance organs in your inner ears and the stretch sensors in your muscles. Shutting your eyes takes one away, so the other two have to do all the work. Picking 'balance only uses the eyes' is wrong, because you could still balance for a few seconds with your eyes shut.
16. (draw) Draw the path of a smell: from a bowl of hot payasam, up your nose, to your brain. Label the smell molecules, the smell receptors and the nerve.  
   **Answer:** Molecules float from the payasam into the nose. They reach receptor cells high inside the nose. A nerve carries the signal to the brain.. *Why:* Smells are molecules. Receptors high in the nose detect them, and nerves carry the signals to the brain.
17. (write) Look at the chart from the Point at the Sound test. The child found 7 sounds with two ears and 4 with one ear blocked. What does this show?  
   **Answer:** Two ears are better for finding where a sound comes from. The child found 3 more sounds with two ears. The brain compares the two ears.. *Why:* With one ear blocked, the brain has much less to compare, so pointing gets worse.
### How Senses Work, Inside (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A signal from your eye and a signal from your ear both reach your brain. How are they different?  
   Options: One is light and one is sound / They are the same kind of electric signal, but they arrive at different parts of the brain / The eye signal is much faster / The ear signal is bigger  
   **Answer:** They are the same kind of electric signal, but they arrive at different parts of the brain. *Why:* No light or sound reaches the brain. Every sense sends the same kind of electric signal. The brain knows which is which by where it arrives. Picking 'one is light' is the most common idea, and it is the surprise of this unit.
2. (mcq) What happens to a hair cell in your cochlea when sound reaches it?  
   Options: It glows / Its tiny tuft bends, gates open, and it makes a nerve signal / It grows longer / It makes a sound back  
   **Answer:** Its tiny tuft bends, gates open, and it makes a nerve signal. *Why:* Sound ripples the liquid in the cochlea. The tufts on the hair cells bend. That opens tiny gates, and the cell makes a signal. Picking 'it makes a sound back' mixes up hearing with echoes.
3. (truefalse) If loud noise breaks the hair cells in a person's ear, new ones grow back in a few weeks.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In people, broken hair cells do not grow back. That is why hearing lost to loud noise stays lost. Birds can grow new ones, and scientists are studying how.
4. (mcq) Chilli feels 'hot' in your mouth. What is really happening?  
   Options: The chilli is really very hot / A chemical in chilli opens the gates of the nerve endings that warn of burning / Chilli is a sixth basic taste / Chilli makes your tongue bigger  
   **Answer:** A chemical in chilli opens the gates of the nerve endings that warn of burning. *Why:* Capsaicin, the chemical in chilli, opens the same gates that open at burning heat. So your brain gets the message 'hot', even from cold chilli. Picking 'sixth taste' is understandable, but chilli is caught by heat nerve endings, not taste buds.
5. (mcq) Birds eat chillies and do not feel the burn. Why?  
   Options: Birds have no mouths / Birds' heat gate is a slightly different shape, so capsaicin cannot open it / Birds only eat cold chillies / Birds cannot taste anything  
   **Answer:** Birds' heat gate is a slightly different shape, so capsaicin cannot open it. *Why:* Capsaicin fits the heat gate in mammals, like us, but not the bird version. So birds eat chillies happily and spread their seeds. Picking 'cannot taste anything' is wrong, because birds do have taste buds.
6. (fill) The savoury, meaty basic taste of tomatoes and mushrooms is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['umami']. *Why:* Kikunae Ikeda named umami in 1908. It is the taste of glutamate.
7. (mcq) People have about 400 kinds of smell receptor. How can they tell apart so many more than 400 smells?  
   Options: Each smell switches on a different pattern of receptors, like letters making words / The nose guesses / Each receptor smells 1,000 things on its own / We can only smell 400 smells  
   **Answer:** Each smell switches on a different pattern of receptors, like letters making words. *Why:* Most smells are mixes of molecules that switch on several kinds of receptor at once. The brain reads the pattern, the way you read a word from its letters. Picking '400 smells only' means thinking one receptor gives one smell.
8. (truefalse) A single rod cell in your eye can notice one tiny particle of light.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Inside each rod is a molecule that changes shape when one particle of light hits it. That starts a nerve signal.
9. (mcq) In the Blue Mango activity, you stared at a blue mango and then saw a yellow one on white paper. What made the yellow?  
   Options: Yellow ink on the paper / Tired blue cones, so the other cones sent more signals / Sunlight / A reflection  
   **Answer:** Tired blue cones, so the other cones sent more signals. *Why:* Staring tired out your blue cones. On white paper, the other cones sent more signals, so your brain saw yellow. Picking 'yellow ink' shows how real afterimages feel, but the paper was blank.
10. (mcq) In the Fading Coin activity, you stopped feeling a still coin, but felt it again when it moved. What does this show?  
   Options: Senses mostly notice changes / Coins lose weight / Skin only works when you look at it / Touch receptors stop working for ever  
   **Answer:** Senses mostly notice changes. *Why:* Many receptors fire when something changes and go quiet when nothing does. That is why you stop noticing your clothes. Picking 'stop working for ever' is wrong, because you felt the nudge at once.
11. (match) Match each animal to its super-sense.  
   **Answer:** Honeybee → sees ultraviolet; Pit viper → feels the warmth of a mouse in the dark; Bat → hears echoes of its squeaks; Catfish → tastes with its whole skin; Elephant → hears very low rumbles. *Why:* Each animal lives in its own sense-world, called its Umwelt. These animals detect things humans cannot.
12. (mcq) In the McGurk experiment, people watch a face saying 'ga' while the sound says 'ba'. Many hear 'da'. What does this show?  
   Options: Their ears are broken / The brain mixes what the eyes see with what the ears hear / Sound travels slower than light / Everyone hears differently  
   **Answer:** The brain mixes what the eyes see with what the ears hear. *Why:* The brain makes its best guess using all the senses together, so the moving lips change what you hear. Picking 'ears are broken' is wrong. Close your eyes, and you hear 'ba' correctly.
13. (write) Some blind people find their way with tongue clicks. Explain how this works, using the word echo.  
   **Answer:** They click and listen for the echo, the sound bouncing back. A quick sharp echo means a wall is close. Their brain learns to build a picture from the echoes.. *Why:* This is echolocation, the same idea bats use. Expert clickers even use the seeing parts of their brains for it.
14. (mcq) In the Hold Your Nose test, one child named a spice even with her nose pinched. What is the best thing to check?  
   Options: That her nose was fully shut, and the spice was well mixed / That she has a special tongue / That the sugar was fresh / Nothing, the test failed  
   **Answer:** That her nose was fully shut, and the spice was well mixed. *Why:* A nose not fully pinched lets some smell through. Lumps of spice can be felt by the tongue too. A good scientist checks the test was fair before deciding. Picking 'the test failed' gives up too soon.
15. (write) Your brother loves karela, but you find it far too bitter. Is one of you wrong? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Neither of us is wrong. People's tongues really differ. Some people, called supertasters, have more taste buds and find bitter things much more bitter.. *Why:* People's senses really differ. Linda Bartoshuk found supertasters, who taste bitterness more strongly.
### Senses, Animals and Big Mysteries (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Johannes Müller noticed that pressing on the eye's nerve makes people see flashes of light. What does this tell us?  
   Options: The eye makes its own light / A nerve from the eye can only say 'light', whatever starts its signal / Pressing the eye is good for it / Light is inside the nerve  
   **Answer:** A nerve from the eye can only say 'light', whatever starts its signal. *Why:* A signal on the seeing pathway is read as light, whatever caused it. So the kind of sense depends on where the signal goes. Picking 'the eye makes its own light' is a natural guess, but there is no light. It is just a signal.
2. (truefalse) Light reaches your brain through your eyes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Light stops at the retina. Only electric signals travel along the nerve to the brain. Inside the skull it is dark.
3. (mcq) A barn owl's two ears are at different heights on its head. How does this help it?  
   Options: It can hear only high sounds / It can tell whether a sound is above or below, not just left or right / It looks more frightening / It hears through its feathers  
   **Answer:** It can tell whether a sound is above or below, not just left or right. *Why:* With ears at different heights, a sound from below reaches one ear slightly differently from the other. So the owl's brain can work out up and down too, and catch a mouse in the dark.
4. (mcq) The tongue map stayed in school books for many years. Which person showed in 1974 that it was wrong?  
   Options: Virginia Collings / Edwin Boring / Aristotle / Kikunae Ikeda  
   **Answer:** Virginia Collings. *Why:* Virginia Collings repeated the tests carefully in 1974 and found every taste all over the tongue. Edwin Boring drew the graph in 1942 that started the mistake. Ikeda named umami.
5. (write) Why do you think a wrong idea, like the tongue map, can stay in books for a long time? How could you check an idea for yourself?  
   **Answer:** Books copy from other books, and people trust what is printed. I could test it myself, with a fair test, for example with cotton buds and sugar water on different parts of my tongue.. *Why:* Science means checking ideas against what really happens, not just trusting a famous book.
6. (mcq) Ayurveda counts six tastes, including pungent, like ginger, and astringent, like raw banana. How would a scientist explain those two?  
   Options: They are detected by taste buds like the others / They are touch and pain feelings in the mouth, not taste-bud tastes / They do not exist / They are smells  
   **Answer:** They are touch and pain feelings in the mouth, not taste-bud tastes. *Why:* Pungent and astringent are real feelings when we eat, but they come from nerve endings for heat, pain and touch, not from taste buds. Picking 'they do not exist' is unfair. People feel them clearly.
7. (mcq) Scientists argue about how many smells people can tell apart. Why is it so hard to count?  
   Options: Nobody has tried / The answer depends on how the maths is done, and nobody can test every smell / Smells change colour / Noses are too small to study  
   **Answer:** The answer depends on how the maths is done, and nobody can test every smell. *Why:* There are far too many possible mixtures to test one by one. So scientists estimate, and different methods give very different numbers. Picking 'nobody has tried' is wrong. Many teams have tried.
8. (mcq) Asifa Majid found that the Jahai people of Malaysia name smells as easily as colours. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Jahai people have bigger noses / Naming smells may depend on language and practice / Only Jahai people can smell / English has no smell words at all  
   **Answer:** Naming smells may depend on language and practice. *Why:* Their noses are like everyone's. Their language has many words just for smells, and they practise using them. Picking 'bigger noses' means looking for the answer in the body, when it may be in practice and words.
9. (mcq) In the Train Your Nose project, why test a family member who did not practise?  
   Options: To have someone to beat / To compare, so you know if practice made the difference and not luck / To make the jars smell stronger / Because it is polite  
   **Answer:** To compare, so you know if practice made the difference and not luck. *Why:* Comparing with someone who did not practise helps show whether the practice really made the difference. Scientists call this a comparison or a control.
10. (write) Blind people's brains use their 'seeing' parts for touch and hearing. What does this tell you about brains?  
   **Answer:** Brains can change. If one sense is missing, the brain can give its parts a new job for other senses.. *Why:* Norihiro Sadato's team found the seeing part of the brain busy in blind people reading Braille. Brains change with use.
11. (truefalse) Two people tasting the same chutney always taste exactly the same thing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. People's tongues and noses differ. For example, some people find dhania tastes like soap, partly because of their genes.
12. (write) A bee sees ultraviolet stripes on a flower that you cannot see. Which is the 'real' flower: the one you see or the one the bee sees? Explain what you think.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer is good. For example: both are real. Each animal senses a different part of what is there. Nobody sees the whole flower.. *Why:* This is an open question. Every animal has its own Umwelt, or sense-world. No animal senses everything.
13. (mcq) What is 'the hard problem' that David Chalmers wrote about in 1995?

   **Answer:** Why signals in the brain feel like anything at all, like the taste of mango. *Why:* Scientists can follow signals into the brain. But nobody yet knows why some signals feel like the colour yellow or the smell of rain. That is the hard problem, and it is still open.

   **Answer:** Tiny electric signals along nerves. *Why:* Light and sound never reach the brain. Only electric signals do. Picking 'real light or sound' is the most common idea, and it is not right.
3. (mcq) Which part of the ear shakes first when sound reaches it?  
   Options: The cochlea / The eardrum / The nerve / The brain  
   **Answer:** The eardrum. *Why:* Sound goes down the ear canal and shakes the eardrum first. Then three tiny bones pass the shaking to the cochlea. Picking the cochlea skips two steps.
4. (mcq) Why do two ears help you find where a sound comes from?  
   Options: Two ears hear twice as much / The brain compares which ear the sound reaches first and loudest / One ear is for loud sounds and one for soft / They don't help  
   **Answer:** The brain compares which ear the sound reaches first and loudest. *Why:* A sound from one side reaches the nearer ear sooner and louder. The brain compares them. Picking 'twice as much' means thinking loudness is the only clue.
5. (mcq) Why should you keep earphones at a low volume?  
   Options: Loud sound can break hair cells, and they do not grow back / Loud sound makes ears bigger / Earphones run out of battery / Loud sound is only bad for grown-ups  
   **Answer:** Loud sound can break hair cells, and they do not grow back. *Why:* In people, broken hair cells do not grow back, so the hearing loss lasts. Picking 'only bad for grown-ups' is wrong. Children's hair cells can break too.
6. (mcq) Where on your body is touch sharpest?  
   Options: Your back / Your fingertips / Your elbow / It is the same everywhere  
   **Answer:** Your fingertips. *Why:* Fingertips have touch receptors packed very close together. Picking 'the same everywhere' usually means not yet having tried the two-point test.
7. (mcq) Which of these is NOT one of the five basic tastes?  
   Options: Sweet / Umami / Chilli hot / Bitter  
   **Answer:** Chilli hot. *Why:* Chilli 'heat' is detected by nerve endings that warn of burning, not by taste buds. Picking umami is common, because it is the least known of the five tastes.
8. (mcq) Can the back of your tongue taste sweet?  
   Options: No, only the tip tastes sweet / Yes, every part with taste buds tastes every taste / Only at night / Only if you are hungry  
   **Answer:** Yes, every part with taste buds tastes every taste. *Why:* The tongue map is wrong. Taste receptors for every taste are spread across the tongue. Picking 'only the tip' is the old tongue map.
9. (mcq) With your nose pinched, elaichi sugar tastes only sweet. Why?  
   Options: Elaichi's flavour is a smell, and pinching stops it reaching your nose / Pinching makes the tongue numb / Elaichi has no flavour / Sugar hides all spices  
   **Answer:** Elaichi's flavour is a smell, and pinching stops it reaching your nose. *Why:* Most flavour is smell. The elaichi molecules float from your mouth into your nose, unless your nose is pinched. Picking 'tongue numb' is wrong, because you still tasted the sweetness.
10. (mcq) Where are your smell receptors?  
   Options: On your tongue / High up inside your nose / In your ears / On your lips  
   **Answer:** High up inside your nose. *Why:* Smell receptors are in a small patch high inside your nose. Picking 'tongue' mixes up smell and taste, which is easy, because they work together.
11. (mcq) Which cells in your eye work in dim light but see no colour?  
   Options: Cones / Rods / Hair cells / Taste cells  
   **Answer:** Rods. *Why:* Rods work in dim light. That is why at night you see shapes but little colour. Cones see colour in brighter light.
12. (mcq) Which animal can see ultraviolet stripes on flowers that we cannot see?  
   Options: A honeybee / A dog / A cat / A catfish  
   **Answer:** A honeybee. *Why:* Honeybees see ultraviolet. Many flowers have ultraviolet patterns that guide bees to the nectar. Dogs and cats cannot see ultraviolet the way bees do.
13. (mcq) How do bats find moths in the dark?  
   Options: By smell only / By squeaking and listening for echoes / By seeing ultraviolet / By tasting the air  
   **Answer:** By squeaking and listening for echoes. *Why:* Bats use echolocation. They squeak and hear the echoes bounce back from moths. Some blind people use tongue clicks in the same way.
14. (mcq) Which question do scientists still NOT know the answer to?  
   Options: Do we have taste buds? / Why do signals in the brain feel like the taste of mango or the colour red? / Do ears have eardrums? / Do bees see ultraviolet?  
   **Answer:** Why do signals in the brain feel like the taste of mango or the colour red?. *Why:* Scientists know a lot about how senses send signals. But how signals become experience is still a mystery. Some call it the hard problem.
15. (mcq) How many senses do people have?  
   Options: Exactly five / Many more than five, such as balance, body position, warmth and cold, and thirst / Only one / Three  
   **Answer:** Many more than five, such as balance, body position, warmth and cold, and thirst. *Why:* Five is an old count, from Aristotle more than 2,300 years ago. Scientists count many more, each with its own receptors, though they do not agree on the exact number. Picking 'exactly five' is the answer many school books still give.
16. (mcq) You stand on one foot with your eyes shut. Which sense helps you most to stay up?  
   Options: Smell / Taste / The balance sense in your inner ears / Hearing  
   **Answer:** The balance sense in your inner ears. *Why:* Your inner ears hold balance organs that sense gravity and the turning of your head. Picking 'hearing' is close, because the balance organs sit right next to the cochlea. But they do a different job.

## Applications
- Hearing aids and cochlear implants. A cochlear implant sends electric signals straight to the nerve of the cochlea, so some deaf people can hear.
- Ear protection for people who work with loud machines, and safe-volume settings on phones.
- Braille books, Braille signs in lifts and on medicine boxes, and raised dots on Indian currency notes for people who cannot see well.
- Tactile paving: the yellow ridged tiles on Metro platforms and footpaths that blind people can feel with their feet or a cane.
- Eye tests and spectacles. The eye doctor checks how sharply each eye sees.
- Cataract surgery, which gives sight back to millions of people. Aravind Eye Hospital in Madurai has done millions of these operations.
- Gas companies add a strong-smelling chemical to cooking gas, which has no smell of its own, so people notice a leak.
- Sniffer dogs at airports and in rescue work, finding people buried after earthquakes.
- Food scientists design flavour, crunch and colour together, because the senses mix.
- Phones that buzz in your hand, using the touch receptors that feel vibration.
- Night-vision cameras and heat cameras let people sense infrared, which our eyes cannot.
- Smell training is used by doctors to help people whose sense of smell has gone weak after an illness.

## Spotted around us
- The yellow ridged tiles on Metro platforms and at railway stations, for blind people to feel underfoot.
- Raised marks on Indian currency notes, so people who cannot see well can tell them apart.
- The smell of cooking gas, which is added on purpose so a leak can be noticed.
- A temple bell, a pressure cooker whistle and a vegetable seller's call. Each one is shaking air reaching your eardrum.
- Spice markets, like Mattancherry in Kochi or Khari Baoli in Old Delhi, where your nose can tell you where you are with your eyes shut.
- Bats leaving a banyan tree or an old fort at dusk, finding moths by echoes.
- Bees visiting flowers on a balcony or in a park, guided by ultraviolet patterns you cannot see.
- People talking in Indian Sign Language, using their eyes and hands instead of ears and voice.

## Open questions
- **How do electric signals in the brain become experiences, like the colour of a mango or the smell of rain?** Scientists can follow the signals into the brain, but nobody knows why they feel like anything at all. This is sometimes called the hard problem.
- **Is the red you see the same as the red your friend sees?** You both call it red and point at the same things. But there is no way, so far, to compare the experiences themselves.
- **What is it like to be a bat, or a bee, or a fish?** We can measure what their senses detect, but not what it feels like to them.
- **How many smells can people tell apart?** One team estimated at least a trillion. Others showed that the answer depends on the maths used. Nobody can test every mixture.
- **Why can birds grow new hair cells in their ears, but people cannot?** Scientists are studying the genes that switch this on in birds, hoping one day to help people with hearing loss.
- **Is there a sixth basic taste, such as fat?** Some tests suggest people can taste fat on its own. Scientists are still arguing about whether it counts as a basic taste.
- **How exactly does the brain join all the senses into one world, at the same moment?** Each sense is handled in different parts of the brain, at slightly different speeds. How they are bound together into one experience is still not understood.
- **Do insects and other small animals feel what they sense?** Flies and bees learn, remember and avoid harm. Whether they have any inner experience is still argued about.

## Connected maths
- In the Two-Point Test, a child felt two points on her fingertip when they were 0.5 cm apart. On her forearm, the points had to be 4 cm apart. How many times bigger was the forearm gap? **8 times bigger.**. The forearm gap was 4 cm. The fingertip gap was 0.5 cm, which is half a centimetre. Two half centimetres make 1 cm, so 4 cm holds 4 × 2 = 8 half centimetres. So the forearm gap was 8 times bigger. The fingertip feels detail 8 times finer. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In Point at the Sound, a child found 7 out of 8 sounds with two ears, and 4 out of 8 with one ear blocked. Draw a bar for each. How many more did she find with two ears? **3 more sounds. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw two bars, one 7 tall and one 4 tall. The difference between them is 7 − 4 = 3. So she found 3 more sounds with two ears. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the Hold Your Nose test, a family of 4 people each tasted 3 spiced sugars with their noses pinched. Make a tally of how many spices were named correctly: Amma 0, Appa 1, Akka 0, me 0. Then, with noses open: Amma 3, Appa 3, Akka 2, me 3. How many were named correctly in all, with noses pinched and with noses open? **With noses pinched: 1 out of 12. With noses open: 11 out of 12.**. With noses pinched, add 0 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 1. With noses open, add 3 + 3 + 2 + 3 = 11. Each person tasted 3 spices, and there were 4 people, so there were 4 × 3 = 12 tastes in each round. So 1 out of 12 pinched, and 11 out of 12 open. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- In the Fading Coin activity, a child stopped feeling the coin after 25 seconds the first time, 20 seconds the second time and 30 seconds the third time. Put the times in order. What is the middle value? **In order: 20, 25, 30 seconds. The middle value is 25 seconds.**. Put the three times in order from smallest to biggest: 20, 25, 30. The middle one is 25. So the coin usually faded after about 25 seconds. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- In the Blue Mango activity, you stare at the dot for 30 seconds. The yellow afterimage lasted 8 seconds. Then you tried staring for 60 seconds, and it lasted 15 seconds. About how many times longer did you stare the second time? Did the afterimage last longer too? **You stared 2 times as long, and the afterimage lasted almost 2 times as long.**. 60 seconds is 2 lots of 30 seconds, so you stared 2 times as long. The afterimage lasted 15 seconds instead of 8. 8 + 8 = 16, and 15 is nearly 16. So it lasted almost twice as long. The longer the cones work, the more tired they get. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
