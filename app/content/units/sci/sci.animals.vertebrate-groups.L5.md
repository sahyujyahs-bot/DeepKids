# In-between animals: mammals that lay eggs, fish that breathe air, and what the five groups tell us about evolution
`sci.animals.vertebrate-groups.L5`

**Summary.** A platypus has fur and feeds her babies milk, yet she lays eggs. A murrel is a fish, yet she comes up to breathe air. In this unit you will test how much air cold and warm water hold, put animal features in order of arrival, and find old leftover parts in your body.

**Intro.** *Which came first in the history of animals: lungs or legs? And which came first: milk, or babies born without an egg? Say what you think, and why.* Many people guess that lungs and legs arrived together, when animals first moved onto land. Many also guess that birth came before milk. In the first activity, the Feature Ladder, you tick the features of seven animals and read the order from your own table. Two animals settle the question: a fish with lungs and a mammal who lays eggs. Then you test why a fish would ever need to breathe air.

## Terms
- **Vertebrate**: A vertebrate is an animal with a backbone. Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are all vertebrates. *e.g.* A sardine, a frog, a house gecko, a crow and you are all vertebrates.
- **Evolution**: Evolution is the slow change of living beings over many generations. Young are a little different from their parents, and over millions of years the small differences add up. *e.g.* Over about ten million years, the fins of some fish became limbs with fingers.
- **Ancestor**: An ancestor is a relative who lived before you and from whom you descend. Your grandmother is an ancestor. So is an animal who lived millions of years ago, if her line of young leads to you. *e.g.* A crow and a crocodile share an ancestor who lived about 240 to 250 million years ago.
- **Family tree of life**: The family tree of life is a drawing that shows how living beings are related. Each fork is an ancestor whose young went two different ways. *e.g.* On the family tree, the branch with frogs splits off before the branch with lizards and the branch with mammals.
- **Branch**: A branch of the family tree is one ancestor together with all of her descendants. Scientists also call a branch a clade. *e.g.* All mammals form one branch. All birds form one branch, and that branch sits inside the dinosaur branch.
- **Feature**: A feature is a part of a body, or a way a body works, that can be compared between animals. Scientists also call a feature a trait. *e.g.* Lungs, feathers, fur, milk and a shelled egg are all features.
- **Dissolved oxygen**: Dissolved oxygen is oxygen gas that is mixed into water, too finely to see. Fish take this oxygen out of the water with their gills. *e.g.* A litre of cool river water holds about 9 milligrams of dissolved oxygen. A litre of air holds about 30 times as much.
- **Air-breathing fish**: An air-breathing fish is a fish who can take oxygen from air as well as from water. She swims up and gulps air at the surface. *e.g.* The murrel and the climbing perch of Indian ponds are air-breathing fish.
- **Lobe-finned fish**: A lobe-finned fish has thick, fleshy fins with bones and muscles inside. Lungfish and coelacanths are the lobe-finned fish alive today. *e.g.* The bones inside a lungfish's fin match the bones of your upper arm.
- **Tetrapod**: A tetrapod is a vertebrate with four limbs, or one whose ancestors had four limbs. Amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are tetrapods. *e.g.* A snake is a tetrapod, because her ancestors were lizards with four legs.
- **Fossil**: A fossil is what is left of a living being from long ago, kept in rock. Bones, shells, eggs and footprints can all become fossils. *e.g.* Fossil dinosaur eggs lie in the rocks near Balasinor in Gujarat.
- **Transitional fossil**: A transitional fossil is a fossil with some features of an older group and some of a newer group. It shows what the animals in between looked like. *e.g.* Tiktaalik had scales and gills like a fish, and a neck and wrists like a four-limbed animal.
- **Amnion**: The amnion is a thin bag of water that surrounds a growing baby reptile, bird or mammal. It lets the baby grow away from ponds and streams, inside a shelled egg or inside the mother. *e.g.* A chick inside an egg and a calf inside a cow both float in an amnion.
- **Monotreme**: A monotreme is a mammal who lays eggs. There are five kinds alive: the platypus and four kinds of echidna. *e.g.* A platypus mother lays about two soft-shelled eggs and then feeds the hatched babies on milk.
- **Placenta**: The placenta is a part that grows inside a pregnant mammal mother. It passes food and oxygen from her blood to the baby until birth. *e.g.* You were fed through a placenta before you were born. Your navel marks where the cord from the placenta joined you.
- **Convergent evolution**: Convergent evolution happens when the same feature arises separately in animals who are not close relatives. The likeness comes from a similar way of living, and not from a shared ancestor. *e.g.* Bats and birds both have wings, but their shared ancestor had none. Wings arose twice.
- **Vestigial part**: A vestigial part is a small leftover of a part that was bigger and did more in an animal's ancestors. *e.g.* Your tailbone is a vestigial part. It is what is left of a tail.
- **DNA**: DNA is the long molecule in every cell that carries the instructions for building a body. Parents pass copies of their DNA to their young. *e.g.* The more alike the DNA of two animals is, the more recently they shared an ancestor.

## Explanations
### The five groups are branches of one family tree · *defined*
**Definition:** All vertebrates descend from one group of small, fish-like animals who lived about 500 million years ago. The five groups we name today are branches that split off from that one family at different times.

In Class 3 you learnt how the five groups breathe, keep warm and have young. In Class 4 you learnt how they sense the world. Now ask a different question. Why are there groups at all? The answer is that animals are related, as cousins are. Long ago one population of animals split into two, and each half went on changing in its own way. This happened again and again. Animals whose shared ancestor lived recently are still much alike, so we put them in one group. Animals whose shared ancestor lived very long ago have had more time to grow different. So a group is a set of close relatives. This also explains the animals who do not fit neatly. Between any two groups there were once animals in between. Most of them died out, and that is why the groups look so separate today. When one of the in-between animals is still alive, like the platypus, or turns up as a fossil, the gap between two groups closes a little.
- e.g. A crow and a sparrow share an ancestor who lived some tens of millions of years ago, so they are much alike.
- e.g. A crow and a carp share an ancestor who lived more than 400 million years ago, so they are very different.
- e.g. The platypus has fur and milk like other mammals and lays eggs like a reptile or a bird. Her branch split from ours very early.
### The features of a group arrived one at a time · *defined*
**Definition:** No group got all of its features at once. Each feature first appeared in one ancestor, and only the animals descended from that ancestor carry it.

In the Feature Ladder you ticked the features of seven animals. The ticks made steps. Every animal had a backbone. All but the carp had lungs. Five had four limbs. Four grew inside an amnion. Two had fur and milk. Only the cow was born alive after being fed through a placenta. The steps show the order in which the features arrived. The backbone came first. Lungs came before limbs, because the lungfish has lungs and no limbs. The amnion came after limbs. Milk came before birth without an egg, because the platypus has milk and still lays eggs. So the list we learn for each group, such as 'a mammal has fur, gives milk and is born alive', was never one set. It was built up one feature at a time over millions of years. Be careful with the ladder in one way. It shows the order of branching. It does not show that the animals on the higher steps are better. Every animal on the ladder is alive today, and every one of them has been evolving for exactly the same length of time.
- e.g. Backbone: about 500 million years old. Fingers and toes: about 365 million years old. Milk: more than 200 million years old.
- e.g. A frog has lungs and four limbs but no amnion. Her eggs must stay wet, so most frogs lay them in water.
- e.g. Feathers are a feature of a side branch. In the Feature Ladder only the pigeon had them.
### Why some fish breathe air · *defined*
**Definition:** Water holds very little oxygen, and warm, still water holds least. Fish who live in warm, still water often have a second way to get oxygen: they swim up and gulp air.

A fish breathes with gills. Gills are thin, feathery sheets full of blood, and they take dissolved oxygen out of the water that flows over them. There is not much to take. A litre of water at 20 °C holds about 9 milligrams of oxygen. A litre of air holds about 30 times as much. In the Air in Water test you saw what warmth does. The cold water gave up bubbles of air as it warmed, because warm water cannot hold as much dissolved air as cold water. Now think of a village tank or a paddy field in May. The water is shallow, still and warm. Rotting leaves use up oxygen too, and so do the water plants at night. By dawn there may be almost no oxygen left, and fish with gills alone crowd at the surface and gasp. Some fish have another way. The murrel has a chamber above her gills, lined with thin skin full of blood. She swims up, gulps a mouthful of air into the chamber, and takes the oxygen from it. The climbing perch has folded plates of thin bone in the same place. The mudskipper of our mangroves takes in oxygen through her wet skin and through the lining of her mouth and throat. The murrel and the climbing perch depend on air so much that they must reach the surface to live. Kept under water with no way up, they can drown.
- e.g. Murrels and climbing perches are sold alive in wet baskets in markets in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal and Assam, because they can breathe air for hours.
- e.g. A fighting fish in a small bowl swims up to gulp air every few minutes. She is a relative of the climbing perch.
- e.g. Mudskippers hop over the wet mud at Point Calimere in Tamil Nadu and at Coringa in Andhra Pradesh.
### Lungs are older than legs · *defined*
**Definition:** Lungs first appeared in fish, more than 400 million years ago, long before any animal had legs. Land animals did not invent lungs. They inherited them.

A lung is a bag that opens off the throat and has thin walls full of blood. A fish who gulps air into such a bag gets oxygen from the air. Lungfish have lungs that work as ours do. Six kinds of lungfish are alive today, in Africa, South America and Australia. The South American lungfish must breathe air to live. Lungfish in Africa and South America can also lie in the mud right through the dry season and breathe air until the rain returns. Another fish with lungs is the bichir of Africa. The bichir's branch split off very early from the branch of most other fish. Because both lungfish and bichirs have lungs, scientists conclude that the shared ancestor of all bony fish had lungs too. So where are the lungs of a carp or a sardine? They are still there, changed into something else. Most fish today have a swim bladder, a bag of gas that keeps them from sinking. The swim bladder grows from the same place as a lung. Charles Darwin guessed that lungs came from swim bladders. The evidence now points the other way. The swim bladder is a changed lung.
- e.g. Your lungs and a lungfish's lungs come from the same ancestor's lungs.
- e.g. The swim bladder of a rohu is the silver balloon you may have seen when a fish is cleaned at the market.
- e.g. When Tiktaalik's relatives moved into shallow water about 375 million years ago, they already had lungs.
### From fin to limb: Tiktaalik and Acanthostega · *defined*
**Definition:** Limbs with fingers and toes evolved from the fleshy fins of lobe-finned fish, between about 375 and 365 million years ago. Fossils show the stages in between.

Feel your arm. There is one bone in the upper arm, two bones in the lower arm, many small bones in the wrist, and then fingers. A frog's front leg, a crow's wing and a whale's flipper all have this same pattern. A lobe-finned fish has the beginning of the pattern inside each fleshy fin: one bone, then two. In 2004, Edward Daeschler, Neil Shubin and Farish Jenkins found a fossil in rocks about 375 million years old on Ellesmere Island, in the far north of Canada. They named her Tiktaalik. She had scales and gills like a fish. She also had a flat head, a neck that let her turn her head, strong ribs, and fins with a shoulder, an elbow and a wrist. She could prop herself up in shallow water. She had no fingers yet. Acanthostega lived about ten million years later, in what is now Greenland. She had four limbs with fingers, eight on each hand. Yet she still had gills, and her limbs and ribs were too weak to hold her up on land. So the first limbs with fingers were used in water. Walking on land came later.
- e.g. One bone, two bones, many small bones, fingers: the same pattern is inside your arm, a bat's wing and a dolphin's flipper.
- e.g. Tiktaalik had a neck. A fish has none, so she must turn her whole body to look sideways.
- e.g. Five fingers is not the oldest number. Acanthostega had eight.
### Why did animals with limbs first leave the water? · *debated*
Limbs and lungs were both there before any vertebrate lived on land. So something else must explain why some of these animals began to spend time out of the water. Nobody watched it happen, and the fossils do not say why. Scientists have several ideas, and more than one may be true.
- e.g. Insects, millipedes and plants were living on land long before the first vertebrate walked there.
- e.g. Fossils of the early limbed animals are found in rocks from wet, wooded river plains, not from deserts.
- e.g. Mudskippers today leave the water to feed on the mud and to escape fish who hunt them.
- **Escaping drying ponds:** The American scientist Alfred Romer argued in the 1900s that ponds dried up, and fish who could crawl to the next pond survived. Few scientists accept this now. The first limbs were too weak for crawling, and the fossils come from wet places.
- **Food and safety at the edge:** The shallow edges of rivers were full of small animals to eat and had no large fish to fear. An animal who could push into the shallows and onto the bank found food that no one else was using. Many scientists favour this idea.
- **Better breathing in warm shallows:** Warm, shallow water holds little oxygen. A flat head and a neck let an animal lift the nose into the air without swimming up. On this idea the body changed for breathing first, and walking was a later use for it.
### Mammals who lay eggs · *defined*
**Definition:** The platypus and the four kinds of echidna are mammals who lay eggs. They are called monotremes, and they live in Australia and New Guinea.

A platypus has fur. She makes milk. Her body makes its own heat, and stays at about 32 °C, a little cooler than ours. All of this makes her a mammal. Yet she lays about two small eggs, each about 17 millimetres long, with soft, leathery shells. She curls round them in a burrow for about ten days until they hatch. An echidna mother lays one egg and carries it in a pouch on her belly. The babies hatch tiny and unfinished, and then they drink milk for months. A monotreme mother has no teats. Her milk comes out through small openings in a patch of skin, and the babies take it from her fur. How can one animal have milk and eggs? The answer is in the order of the features. The early ancestors of all mammals laid eggs. Milk began in those egg-laying ancestors. The branch that leads to the platypus split off at that point, between about 160 and 220 million years ago. On the other branch, much later, the eggs came to be kept inside the mother, and the placenta evolved. So the platypus is not a mixture of a duck and a beaver. She is a mammal whose branch kept an old way of having young.
- e.g. A platypus baby drinks milk for about four months.
- e.g. The four echidnas are the short-beaked echidna of Australia and New Guinea and three long-beaked echidnas of New Guinea.
- e.g. Kangaroos and other pouched mammals are on our side of the split. Their babies are born alive, very small, and finish growing in the pouch.
### Milk is older than birth without an egg, and your DNA shows it · *defined*
**Definition:** Milk evolved while the ancestors of mammals still laid eggs. As milk took over the feeding of the young, egg yolk was needed less and less, and in most mammals the instructions for making yolk broke down.

A hen's egg must hold all the food a chick needs until hatching. That food is the yolk. The body makes yolk by following instructions in the DNA, called yolk genes. In 2008, David Brawand, Walter Wahli and Henrik Kaessmann looked for these genes in mammals. Hens have three working yolk genes. The platypus has one that still works, and her small eggs do hold some yolk. Humans and dogs have none that work. But the broken pieces of the old yolk genes are still there in our DNA, in the place where a hen has hers. The same study found that the genes for the main proteins of milk were already present in the shared ancestor of all mammals. So the order was this. First, egg-laying ancestors began to make milk. Then, over a very long time, yolk was needed less, and the yolk genes stopped working one by one. You carry in every cell the worn-out instructions for making an egg yolk. One thing is still unknown. Nobody is sure what the very first milk was for. One idea is that it kept the soft-shelled eggs moist and free of germs, and that the hatched young began to lick it later.
- e.g. A platypus egg is small and has little yolk, because milk does most of the feeding after hatching.
- e.g. A hen's egg is large for her body, because the yolk must do all the feeding.
- e.g. Broken yolk genes in humans sit beside the same neighbouring genes as the working yolk genes of a hen.
### In-between does not mean unfinished · *defined*
**Definition:** An animal who has some old features is not an old or unfinished animal. Every living animal has been evolving for the same length of time, and each has new features of her own.

People often call the platypus, the lungfish or the coelacanth a 'living fossil', as if they had stopped changing. This is a mistake. The platypus kept egg-laying, but her branch also evolved things that no ancestor had. When she dives, she shuts her eyes, ears and nose. She finds prawns and insect young in the mud with her bill, which holds about 40,000 sensors for the tiny electric signals that moving muscles give off. A male platypus has a spur on each back ankle that injects venom. In the same way, the murrel's air chamber is a new feature of her branch, and not a half-made lung. So there are no higher and lower animals on the family tree. There are only branches, and each branch has kept some old features and gained some new ones. This is true of us as well. Humans kept five fingers, which is an old feature. Horses changed theirs to one.
- e.g. The coelacanth was known only from fossils and thought to have died out 65 million years ago. In 1938 Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, who ran a small museum in East London, South Africa, spotted one in a fishing boat's catch.
- e.g. The murrel's air chamber is found in no early fish. Her branch evolved it.
- e.g. A snake lost her legs. Losing a feature is evolution too.
### Birds are dinosaurs, so what is a reptile? · *debated*
Fossils and DNA agree that birds are a branch of the dinosaurs. They also agree that crocodiles are the closest living relatives of birds. The line that leads to crocodiles and the line that leads to dinosaurs and birds split about 240 to 250 million years ago. Lizards and snakes split off earlier still. So a mugger crocodile is a closer relative of a crow than of a monitor lizard, even though she looks far more like the lizard. There are signs of this in how they live. A crocodile mother builds a nest, guards it, and answers her young when they call from inside the eggs, much as a bird does. This raises a question about names. The old group 'reptiles' holds lizards, snakes, turtles and crocodiles, and leaves birds out. But birds grew out of that same branch. Scientists agree on the family tree. They do not all agree on how to use the old word.
- e.g. A crow and a mugger crocodile share an ancestor who is not an ancestor of any lizard.
- e.g. Crocodiles and birds both build nests and guard their eggs.
- e.g. Scaly legs and feet on a hen are the same kind of scales that a crocodile has.
- **Name only whole branches:** Most scientists who draw family trees say a group should be an ancestor and all of her descendants. On this view, either birds are reptiles, or 'reptile' is not the name of a real branch. Many now say that birds are reptiles.
- **Keep the everyday group:** Others say 'reptile' is still a useful word for scaly animals who take their warmth from outside and lay shelled eggs on land. On this view a group can describe a way of living, as long as we remember that it is not a whole branch.
### The same feature can arise many times · *defined*
**Definition:** When the same feature arises separately in animals who are not close relatives, it is called convergent evolution. Such a feature tells us about a way of living, and not about who is related to whom.

Some features are good clues to the family tree, because they arose only once. Milk arose once. Feathers arose once. Other features have arisen again and again. Giving birth to live young is the best example. The scientist Daniel Blackburn counted how many separate times it arose among vertebrates. He found more than 150 times, and at least 115 of these were in lizards and snakes. The Russell's viper, the saw-scaled viper and the common sand boa of India all bear live young, while the cobra and the rat snake lay eggs. Some sharks, the guppy and some frogs bear live young too. Among mammals it arose only once. So 'born alive' sounds like a mammal feature, but it is a poor clue to family. A warm body that makes its own heat arose separately in birds and in mammals. Wings arose separately in birds, in bats and in the extinct flying reptiles called pterosaurs. A long body with no legs arose in snakes, in some lizards and in the legless amphibians called caecilians. Why does the same answer keep turning up? Because bodies meet the same problems. A baby who grows inside the mother is safe from egg thieves and from cold. Any branch in which mothers began to keep their eggs inside for longer could go that way.
- e.g. A Russell's viper may give birth to 20 to 40 live young at a time.
- e.g. In 2012 a new family of caecilians was named from the soil of Northeast India, after about 250 digs over five years by Rachunliu Kamei, S. D. Biju and their team. The mothers stay wrapped round their eggs for two to three months.
- e.g. A dolphin and a shark have the same smooth shape for fast swimming, but one is a mammal and the other a fish.
### Old parts in new bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** Bodies keep leftovers of parts that did more in their ancestors. These leftovers are called vestigial parts, and they are evidence of where an animal came from.

Evolution does not start again from nothing. It changes what is already there, and often a small leftover stays. You carry several. At the bottom of your backbone is the tailbone, made of three to five small bones joined together. It is what is left of a tail. Before you were born, when you were about five to eight weeks old inside your mother, you had a small tail, and then your body took it back in. When you are cold, a tiny muscle at the root of each hair pulls the hair upright, and you get goosebumps. On a furry mammal this fluffs the coat and holds in more warm air. On your thin body hair it does very little. Whales have leftovers too. A whale has no back legs, but deep inside her body lie small hip bones. They fit with a find from India. In rocks about 48 million years old in Kashmir, the geologist A. Ranga Rao collected the bones of a small hoofed animal, about the size of a fox, now called Indohyus. In 2007, Hans Thewissen and his team showed that she had a thick ear bone found only in whales, and heavy bones for wading. She was a close relative of the first whales. The closest living relatives of whales today are hippos.
- e.g. Some people can wiggle their ears a little. The small muscles round the ear turn the ears of a dog or a cow towards a sound.
- e.g. A long tendon in the wrist, called the palmaris longus, is missing in roughly one person in seven worldwide. The share differs a great deal from one part of the world to another.
- e.g. Some pythons have two tiny claws near the tail, the leftovers of back legs.
### How scientists check the family tree with DNA · *defined*
**Definition:** DNA is copied from parent to young with rare small mistakes. The longer two animals have been on separate branches, the more differences have built up between their DNA. Counting the differences shows who is related to whom.

For two hundred years scientists built the family tree from bones and bodies alone. Then a second kind of evidence arrived. Every cell carries DNA, a long molecule made of four kinds of unit that we write as the letters A, T, G and C. When DNA is copied for the next generation, a letter is now and then copied wrongly, and the change is passed on. So two branches that have been apart for a long time differ by many letters. The same is true of proteins, the molecules that DNA gives the instructions for. In 1965 the American scientist Margaret Dayhoff collected every protein sequence then known, 65 of them, into one book. In 1966 she and Richard Eck used a computer to build family trees from such sequences. It was the first time anyone had done this. The trees built from molecules matched the trees built from bones in almost every place. Where they did not match, scientists looked again. That is how we learnt that whales are close relatives of hippos, and that crocodiles are close relatives of birds. Two separate kinds of evidence giving the same tree is strong evidence that the tree is real.
- e.g. Human DNA and chimpanzee DNA differ in far fewer places than human DNA and hen DNA.
- e.g. The platypus's DNA was read in full in 2008. It showed both the yolk gene of an egg-layer and the milk genes of a mammal.
- e.g. DNA can also tell which fish a piece of fish in a market came from.
### Moving continents carried the branches apart · *defined*
**Definition:** The continents move very slowly over millions of years. When a piece of land splits, the animals on each piece go on evolving separately. So where an old branch lives today can show how the land once fitted together.

The family tree of vertebrates grew on a moving Earth. About 150 million years ago India was joined to Africa, Madagascar, Australia and Antarctica in one great southern land. India broke away, drifted north as an island for tens of millions of years, and met Asia about 50 million years ago. Some animals rode on India the whole way. The purple frog of the Western Ghats lives underground and comes up for a few days in the monsoon. S. D. Biju and Franky Bossuyt described her in 2003. Her closest living relatives live on the Seychelles islands, far out in the Indian Ocean. The Seychelles were once joined to India. The frogs did not cross the sea. The land split under their ancestors. The egg-laying mammals tell a similar story. They survive only in Australia and New Guinea, which have been cut off from the other continents for a very long time.
- e.g. The purple frog of Kerala and Tamil Nadu has her nearest relatives on islands about 3,000 kilometres away.
- e.g. Fossil dinosaur eggs in Gujarat were laid when India was still an island on its way north.
- e.g. In rocks about 68 million years old at Dholi Dungri in Gujarat, a fossil snake 3.5 metres long was found coiled round a dinosaur egg, beside a hatchling.

## Mostly agreed
- All vertebrates share one ancestor, and the five groups are branches of one family tree. *How we know:* Family trees built from bones and trees built from DNA were made separately, and they match in almost every place. Fossils of the right age fill the gaps between the groups.
- Four-limbed animals evolved from lobe-finned fish. *How we know:* The bones inside a lungfish's fin match the bones of our limbs. Fossils such as Tiktaalik, about 375 million years old, and Acanthostega, about 365 million years old, have mixes of fish and four-limbed features.
- Lungs evolved in fish, before limbs. *How we know:* Lungfish and bichirs, on two very different branches of bony fish, both have lungs. Tiktaalik and Acanthostega had gills and signs of air breathing together.
- Monotremes are mammals, and milk evolved before birth without an egg. *How we know:* The platypus and the echidnas have fur and milk and lay eggs. The platypus's DNA, read in 2008, has milk genes shared with all mammals and one working yolk gene. Other mammals have only broken yolk genes.
- Birds are dinosaurs, and crocodiles are the closest living relatives of birds. *How we know:* Fossil dinosaurs with feathers and wishbones have been found since 1996, and the DNA of crocodiles is closer to that of birds than to that of lizards.
- Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen than cold water. *How we know:* It has been measured many times. A litre of fresh water holds about 11.3 milligrams of oxygen at 10 °C, about 9.1 at 20 °C and about 7.6 at 30 °C.

## How we found out
**Jenny Clack and Michael Coates, with the team who collected the fossils in East Greenland. Alfred Romer was the best-known supporter of the earlier idea., 1987 (the fossils collected), 1990 (eight fingers reported) and 1991 (gills reported).** For a long time scientists thought they knew why fish grew legs. In the last century the American scientist Alfred Romer pictured fish stranded in drying ponds. Those with stronger fins could crawl to the next pond, and over many generations their fins became legs. So legs were for walking on land. In 1987 Jenny Clack, a British scientist, led a small team to East Greenland. They brought back blocks of rock holding an animal called Acanthostega, about 365 million years old. In Cambridge the bones were slowly freed from the rock, which took years. In 1990 Clack and Michael Coates reported that the hand had eight fingers. In 1991 they reported that she had gills like a fish. Her elbows could not bend forward, and her ribs were too short to hold up her chest out of water. Here was an animal with legs and fingers who lived in water. So legs came first, and walking on land came later.  
*What people believed before:* That legs evolved for walking on land, in fish who had to crawl from one drying pond to the next. Acanthostega had legs with fingers, and also gills and a body too weak for land, so the first legs were used in water.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Fish grew legs so that they could walk on land.** (A common way of telling the story, and older textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: Legs are what land animals walk on, so it seems they must have evolved for walking.  
  Against: Acanthostega had four limbs with fingers, but she had gills, and her limbs and ribs were too weak to hold her up on land. The first limbs were used in water. Also, no animal grows a part because she will need it later. A part spreads only if it helps at the time.
- **The platypus is a primitive animal, left over from the past.** (Many books and films, which call her a 'living fossil'; *rejected*)  
  For: She lays eggs, as the early ancestors of mammals did.  
  Against: Her branch has been evolving for exactly as long as ours. She has features no early mammal had, such as a bill that senses electric signals, and venom spurs on the males.
- **Mammals evolved from reptiles.** (Many older textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: The early relatives of mammals had scaly-looking bodies and sprawling legs, and were once called 'mammal-like reptiles'.  
  Against: The line leading to mammals and the line leading to lizards, crocodiles and birds split about 320 million years ago, from early animals with an amnion. Those shared ancestors were not lizards, snakes, turtles or crocodiles. Neither line came from the other.
- **Birds are reptiles.** (Most scientists who name groups by whole branches; *accepted*)  
  For: The branch that holds lizards, turtles and crocodiles also holds the dinosaurs, and birds are dinosaurs. A crocodile is a closer relative of a crow than of a lizard.  
  Against: In everyday speech 'reptile' means a scaly animal who takes warmth from outside. Birds have feathers and make their own heat, so the everyday word does not fit them.
- **Evolution climbs a ladder from fish up to humans.** (A very old picture, still common in cartoons; *rejected*)  
  For: Fish came first, and humans are recent.  
  Against: The family tree is a branching tree with no top. Most vertebrates alive today are fish. Fish did not stop evolving when one branch moved onto land.
- **Lungs evolved from the swim bladders of fish.** (Charles Darwin suggested it in 1859; *rejected*)  
  For: A swim bladder and a lung are both bags of gas, and they grow from the same part of the gut.  
  Against: Bichirs and lungfish, on two early branches, both have lungs. So the shared ancestor of bony fish probably had lungs, and the swim bladder is the later change.

## Roots
We have been learning about animals who seem to sit between two groups, and about the order in which features arrived. Now we ask what that order tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Feature Ladder, the lungfish had lungs and no legs. The platypus had milk and still laid eggs. So lungs are older than legs, and milk is older than birth without an egg. The features of each group arrived one at a time, over millions of years. An in-between animal is no mistake, because she shows us that order.  
*Habit it breaks:* We learn each group as a fixed list that comes as one set: a mammal has fur, gives milk and is born alive. Then an animal with only part of the list looks like a rule-breaker. But the list was never one set. Each feature on it began at a different time, in a different ancestor.  
*Evidence:* In the Feature Ladder, no animal had a later feature without all the earlier ones, and the ticks made a staircase. The lungfish and the platypus each stood one step short of a group. The platypus's DNA has milk genes shared with every mammal and one working yolk gene. Human DNA still holds the broken remains of yolk genes.

- *who am i:* Your body is made of features of very different ages. Your backbone is about 500 million years old. Your lungs are more than 400 million, your fingers about 365 million, and your milk-drinking more than 200 million.
- *what is life:* Life does not make new kinds of animals from nothing. It changes what is already there. A lung became a swim bladder, a fin became an arm, and a tail became a tailbone.
- *what is this world:* Every animal with a backbone is your relative. The names of the five groups mark the places where one family branched.

All vertebrates descend from one population of small, fish-like animals who lived about 500 million years ago. That one family became the many: sardines, frogs, crocodiles, crows, platypuses and us. The groups look separate only because most of the relatives in between have died out. When one of them turns up, alive like the platypus or in rock like Tiktaalik, we can see the join. Everything is conscious, so each branch is also one more way of being aware of the same world.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** “यथा सोम्यैकेन मृत्पिण्डेन सर्वं मृन्मयं विज्ञातं स्यात् / वाचारम्भणं विकारो नामधेयं मृत्तिकेत्येव सत्यम्” By knowing one lump of clay, everything made of clay is known. The different shapes are names that come from speech. What is real is the clay. (A pot and a plate differ in shape and are one in their clay. A crow and a crocodile differ in shape and are one in their family. 'Bird' and 'reptile' are names we gave to shapes.)
- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), chapter 13 (paraphrased):** Closeness of descent is the hidden bond that our ways of sorting living beings have partly shown us, without our knowing it. (People sorted animals into groups long before they knew about evolution. Darwin saw why the sorting worked: the groups were families all along.)
- **Tao Te Ching 1 (paraphrased):** “道可道，非常道，名可名，非常名。” The name that can be spoken is not the lasting name. Naming is the mother of the ten thousand things. (We named five groups, and then the platypus did not fit. The animals were never divided as sharply as our names for them.)
- Science (settled): All vertebrates share a common ancestor. Fossils and DNA, studied separately, give the same family tree.
- Science (settled): The features that define each group arose at different times. Lungs came before limbs, and milk before birth without an egg.
- Science (debated): Whether groups such as 'reptiles' or 'fish' are real things in nature, or useful lines that people draw, is still argued by biologists and philosophers.
- Other view, Georges Cuvier, the French scientist who founded the study of fossil animals: Cuvier held that every part of an animal fits every other part so closely that one part cannot change alone. A meat-eater's teeth need a meat-eater's claws and gut. So he thought a body could not be rebuilt piece by piece, and that each kind of animal had stayed the same from the beginning. He knew more about animal bodies than anyone of his time, and he was right that the parts must work together. The in-between fossils that answer him were found long after he died in 1832.

## Story: The Fish in the Brass Pot
*A made-up story. Yazhini, Kathir, Thangam Akka, the village and the events are imagined. The fish is real. The murrel, called viral in Tamil, lives in tanks and paddy fields across India, burrows into the mud when the water dries, and must come to the surface to breathe air. Sharing out the fish of a drying tank is a real custom in many Tamil villages.*

By the middle of May the big tank at the edge of the village was a small one. The water had drawn back from the stone steps, then from the reeds, and at last from everything but a brown pool in the middle, no deeper than Yazhini's knees.

That was the day the whole village came to fish. It happened every year when the tank ran low. People walked in with baskets and old saris and their bare hands, and whatever they caught was shared out by the steps.

Yazhini was ten, and she did not like the fishing day. She liked the fish. She stood at the edge with her small brother Kathir and watched the pool.

The carp were in trouble before anyone touched them. They hung just under the surface in a crowd, all facing up, their mouths opening and closing at the top of the water. Some had already turned on their sides.

'They are thirsty,' said Kathir.

'They are in water,' said Yazhini. 'How can they be thirsty?' But she had no better word for it.

Away from the pool the mud had dried into plates with curled edges. Yazhini walked out over it, because nobody else was looking there. Near a clump of dead reeds one of the plates moved.

She knelt down. Under the crust the mud was still soft, and in the soft mud lay a fish as long as her arm, dark and round as a rolling pin, with a flat head like a snake's. There was not a cupful of water round her. Her gill covers lifted and fell. She looked at Yazhini with a small bright eye.

'Viral,' said Thangam Akka, who sold fish in the market and had come up behind her with a basket. 'A big one. She has been sitting in there a week, I would say. Shall I take her?'

'No,' said Yazhini. 'I found her. I am going to keep her until it rains.'

Thangam Akka laughed, but she did not argue. She wrapped the fish in a wet cloth for her and went back to the pool.

At home Yazhini took the tall brass pot from behind the kitchen door, the one with the narrow neck that was used only at Pongal. She carried it to the well and filled it. Then she thought of how dry the fish had been in that mud, and she went on pouring until the water stood right at the lip.

She slid the fish in. The fish went down into the dark and turned once.

'The cat,' said Kathir.

The cat was already on the wall, looking interested. Yazhini fetched a steel plate and pressed it down on the mouth of the pot. It fitted well. A little water ran over the sides. She put a stone on top.

'There,' she said. 'All the water she wants, and nobody can get at her.'

They sat down in the shade to guard the pot.

After a while there was a knock from inside. It was soft, like a knuckle on a door. Then another.

'She wants to come out,' said Kathir.

'She does not know she is safe yet,' said Yazhini.

The knocking came again, four or five times, harder. The stone shifted on the plate. Then there was a long quiet. Yazhini lifted the edge of the plate a finger's width and looked in. The fish was lying just under the brass, on her side, with her mouth pressed against the plate.

Yazhini's stomach turned over. She had seen fish lie like that an hour ago. The carp in the pool had hung at the top with their mouths at the surface, in all that water. And this one had lain a week in mud with no water at all, and her eye had been bright.

She thought about the tank in January, when it was full. She used to sit on the steps and count the rings. A ring would open on the still water, far out, with a small sound like a drop falling. Then nothing for a few minutes. Then another ring in nearly the same place. Her grandmother had told her it was the viral coming up. Yazhini had never asked what the viral came up for.

'Take the stone off,' she said.

She pulled the plate away, put both arms round the pot, and tipped it until water poured over her feet. She tipped out a good three tumblers. Now there was a hand's width of air in the neck of the pot.

For a moment the fish did not move. Then she righted herself, rose, and put her mouth through the surface. There was a small sound like a drop falling. A bubble slid out from under her gill cover. She sank back down into the dark.

Kathir looked at his sister.

'She was not trying to get out,' said Yazhini. 'She was trying to get up. She breathes it. The same as us.'

'A fish?'

'This fish.'

They watched. In a little while the fish came up again, took her mouthful, and went down.

The cat was still on the wall. Yazhini went into the kitchen and came back with the net bag the onions came in. She stretched it over the mouth of the pot and tied it round the neck with string. The cat could look. The air could go in.

That evening Thangam Akka came by with their share of the carp. She looked through the net at the dark shape turning in the pot and raised her eyebrows.

'Still alive? You have not drowned her, then. People do.'

'I nearly did,' said Yazhini.

The rain came eleven days later, in the night. In the morning the pool in the tank had spread back as far as the reeds. Yazhini and Kathir carried the pot down between them and tipped it at the edge.

The fish slid out and was gone. They waited. Far out on the brown water, a ring opened.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the carp and the murrel are both fish, but they get their oxygen in different ways. The carp have gills only. In a warm, shrinking pool the water holds very little oxygen, so they gasp at the surface even though they are surrounded by water. The murrel has gills and an air chamber above them, and she depends on gulping air. That is why she could live for a week in damp mud. It is also why a pot filled to the brim and shut with a plate nearly killed her. She could not reach the air. Yazhini works this out from what she saw: which fish was dying in water, which was alive without it, and the rings on the tank.

- Ask: The carp were in water and in trouble. The murrel was in mud and well. What does that tell you about how each one gets oxygen?
- Ask: Why did filling the pot to the brim and covering it put the murrel in danger?
- Ask: What had Yazhini seen on the full tank in January that helped her work it out?
- Ask: Why was the net bag a better cover than the steel plate?
- Ask: If you kept a fish, what would you want to find out about her first?

## Experiments
### e1 · Air in Water: why would a fish ever need to breathe air? (30 min)
- **question:** Three tumblers of water look exactly the same. One was kept in the fridge, one stood on the kitchen counter, and one was boiled and cooled. When all three stand in a warm place for an hour, in which one will the most bubbles of air appear on the glass?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which tumbler will show the most bubbles after an hour in a warm place: the cold water, the room-warm water or the boiled water? Which will show the fewest? Write your guess and your reason for it.
- **fair test:** Change only what happened to the water before the test. Use the same drinking water for all three. Use three tumblers of the same size and shape, filled to the same height. Stand them side by side in the same warm place for the same time. Count bubbles on the inside of the glass only, and count each tumbler at the same three times.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water and handles the hot pot. Let the boiled water cool completely before your child touches it.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three tumblers. If there are more than 30 bubbles, write 'more than 30'. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Tumbler | Water before the test | Bubbles at 20 minutes | Bubbles at 40 minutes | Bubbles at 60 minutes
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Half-fill two bottles with drinking water and cap them. 2. Shake each bottle hard for 30 seconds. 3. Put one bottle in the fridge and leave the other on the counter. 4. A grown-up boils two tumblers of water in the pot for 5 minutes. 5. Put the lid on the pot and leave it to cool. 6. Do not stir or shake the boiled water.  
  *Parent says:* We are making three kinds of water that look the same. One will be cold, one room-warm, and one boiled. Shaking mixes air into the water.  
  *Child mode:* Half-fill two bottles and shake them hard. Put one in the fridge. A grown-up boils the third water.
  - Note: Shaking a half-full bottle lets the water take in as much air as it can hold at that temperature. Boiling drives nearly all the dissolved air out, and the lid slows its return.
  - Wait: Leave all three for at least 3 hours, until the boiled water is as cool as the room.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shake the cold bottle again for 30 seconds and put it back in the fridge for 2 minutes. 2. Meanwhile, read the question aloud. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. 4. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* All three waters will warm up at the window. Which glass will show the most bubbles, and which the fewest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which tumbler will show the most bubbles? Which the fewest? Say why. Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The boiled one, because boiling makes bubbles.; The cold one, because something changes when it warms up.; All the same, because it is the same water.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children pick the boiled water, because they have seen bubbles in a boiling pot. The two minutes of rest lets the tiny shaken bubbles rise out of the cold water.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the three paper slips on the window sill. 2. Pour cold water gently into the first tumbler, to two finger-widths below the rim. 3.   *Parent says:* Pour gently, so that we do not mix in new air. Look closely. Is there any bubble on any glass now?

  - Note: If a few bubbles cling to a glass right after pouring, tap the glass to free them, and start the clock after that.
  - Wait: Leave the tumblers for 20 minutes. Do not move them.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 20 minutes, look at the inside wall of each glass without moving it. 2. Count the bubbles on each and write the numbers. 3. Touch the outside of each glass and say which feels coolest. 4. Count again at 40 minutes and at 60 minutes.

### e2 · Lap or Suck: how much does a teat help a baby drink? (27 min)

   **Answer:** ['monotreme']. *Why:* The five monotremes are the platypus and four kinds of echidna.

   **Answer:** Ancestor → a relative from long ago from whom you descend; Tetrapod → a vertebrate with four limbs, or with four-limbed ancestors; Fossil → what is left of a living being from long ago, kept in rock; Amnion → the bag of water a baby reptile, bird or mammal grows in; Vestigial part → a small leftover of a part that did more in ancestors. *Why:* These words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
5. (sort) Sort these fish: breathes with gills only, or also breathes air.  
   **Answer:** rohu → Gills only; murrel → Also breathes air; sardine → Gills only; climbing perch → Also breathes air; lungfish → Also breathes air; mudskipper → Also breathes air. *Why:* The murrel and the climbing perch gulp air into a chamber above the gills. The lungfish has lungs. The mudskipper takes in oxygen through her wet skin and mouth lining.
6. (order) Put these features in the order in which they first appeared.  
   **Answer:** ['Backbone', 'Lungs', 'Four limbs', 'Amnion', 'Fur and milk']. *Why:* This is the order of the steps in the Feature Ladder. No animal has a later feature without the earlier ones.
7. (mcq) Bubbles appeared in cold water as it warmed up. What does this show?  
   Options: Warm water holds less dissolved air / Warm water makes new air / Glass lets air through / Cold water has no air in it  
   **Answer:** Warm water holds less dissolved air. *Why:* The cold water held more dissolved air than it could keep once it warmed, so the extra came out as bubbles. Choosing 'makes new air' misses that nothing was added to the water.
8. (write) A murrel lives for a week in damp mud. Carp die in a warm, shrinking pool. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The murrel gulps air into a chamber above her gills, so she needs dampness and air, not deep water. The carp have gills only. Warm, shallow water holds very little dissolved oxygen, so they cannot get enough.. *Why:* A strong answer says how each fish gets oxygen, and says that warm water holds little of it.
9. (count) Acanthostega had 8 fingers on each hand. How many fingers did she have on two hands?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* 8 + 8 = 16. Five fingers on a hand came later.
10. (draw) Draw the bones inside your arm, from shoulder to fingertips. Show one bone, two bones, many small bones, then fingers.  
   **Answer:** One long bone in the upper arm, two side by side in the forearm, a cluster of small bones at the wrist, then the bones of five fingers.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the pattern in order. The same pattern is in a frog's leg, a crow's wing and a whale's flipper, because all inherited it from one ancestor.
11. (odd) Three of these are mammals. Which one is not?  
   Options: Platypus / Echidna / Fruit bat / Penguin  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A penguin is a bird, with feathers and no milk. The platypus and the echidna lay eggs, but they have fur and milk, so they are mammals.
12. (truefalse) True or false: a crocodile is a closer relative of a crow than of a lizard.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Crocodiles and birds share an ancestor who is not an ancestor of lizards. Crocodiles look like lizards because both kept old features, such as scales.
13. (mcq) Which feature has arisen many separate times among vertebrates?  
   Options: Giving birth to live young / Milk / Feathers / A backbone  
   **Answer:** Giving birth to live young. *Why:* Live birth arose more than 150 separate times, mostly in lizards and snakes. Milk, feathers and the backbone each arose once.
14. (label) Draw a platypus. Label three features that make her a mammal, and one that most mammals lack.  
   **Answer:** ['Fur', 'Milk for her young', 'A body that makes its own heat', 'She lays eggs']. *Why:* A strong answer names fur and milk. Egg-laying is the feature most mammals lack. The bill and webbed feet are her own special features. Her group is decided by fur and milk.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about the platypus.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the Feature Ladder: the platypus has fur and milk, and her row differs from the cow's by one feature only. It chooses a view, gives that evidence, and says what another view has right.. *Why:* The child is right that she breaks a common rule. The teacher is right that the rule was made from too few animals. The biologist explains why: groups are branches, and rules are our short descriptions of them.
### In-between animals: evidence and reasons (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What did the fossil Acanthostega show about the first limbs with fingers?  
   Options: They were used in water / They were for running on land / They were for digging burrows / They always had five fingers  
   **Answer:** They were used in water. *Why:* She had limbs with eight fingers, and also gills, weak ribs and elbows that could not bend forward. She lived in water. Choosing 'running on land' is the old idea her fossil overturned.
2. (write) Explain how the platypus shows that milk is older than birth without an egg.  
   **Answer:** The platypus makes milk and still lays eggs. Her branch split from ours after milk evolved. If birth without an egg had come first, an egg-laying mammal with milk would be hard to explain. All mammals share the same milk genes, so milk was there in their shared ancestor.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the order of branching: a feature shared by both branches was present before the split.
3. (mcq) Why is 'born alive' a poor clue to which animals are close relatives?  
   Options: It arose many separate times / Only mammals do it / It is hard to see / Eggs are older than babies  
   **Answer:** It arose many separate times. *Why:* Vipers, some sharks, guppies and cows all bear live young, but they are not close relatives. The feature arose separately in each branch.
4. (fill) When the same feature arises separately in animals who are not close relatives, it is called ____ evolution.  
   **Answer:** ['convergent']. *Why:* Convergent means 'coming together'. Wings in bats and birds are an example.
5. (truefalse) True or false: the platypus stopped evolving long ago.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Her branch has evolved for as long as ours, and gained new features such as a bill that senses electric signals. She kept one old feature, egg-laying.
6. (sort) Sort each likeness: inherited from a shared ancestor, or arose separately.  
   **Answer:** arm bones of a human and a bat → From a shared ancestor; wings of a bat and a crow → Arose separately; milk of a cow and a platypus → From a shared ancestor; live birth in a viper and a cow → Arose separately; smooth shape of a dolphin and a shark → Arose separately; feathers of a crow and a penguin → From a shared ancestor. *Why:* A likeness from a shared ancestor is a clue to the family tree. A likeness that arose separately is a clue to a shared way of living.
7. (count) A litre of air holds 270 milligrams of oxygen. A litre of water holds 9. How many times more is that?  
   **Answer:** 30. *Why:* 270 ÷ 9 = 30. Air holds about 30 times as much oxygen as the same amount of water, which is why gulping air helps a fish so much.
8. (match) Match each animal to the surprise she holds.  
   **Answer:** Murrel → a fish who can drown; Echidna → a mammal who carries her egg in a pouch; Russell's viper → a snake who bears live young; Indohyus → a four-legged relative of the first whales; Tiktaalik → a fish with a neck and wrists. *Why:* Each of these animals has a feature we do not expect from her group. Each one makes sense once the groups are seen as branches of one tree.
9. (write) A friend says a cow is 'more evolved' than a carp. What would you tell them?  
   **Answer:** Both have been evolving for exactly the same length of time since their shared ancestor. The cow's branch gained limbs, an amnion and milk. The carp's branch gained its own features, such as the swim bladder and fan fins. The family tree has branches, not a top.. *Why:* A strong answer says that every living animal is equally modern, and names a feature that the carp's branch gained.
10. (mcq) Which of these is evidence that your ancestors had thick fur?  
   Options: Goosebumps / Fingernails / Your navel / Milk teeth  
   **Answer:** Goosebumps. *Why:* A tiny muscle raises each hair when you are cold. On a furry animal this fluffs the coat. On us it does almost nothing, so it is a leftover.
11. (draw) Draw a small family tree for a lizard, a crocodile and a crow. Show which two are closest.  
   **Answer:** The crocodile and the crow join at one fork. That fork then joins the lizard's line further down.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the crocodile with the crow. Putting the crocodile with the lizard goes by scales and shape, which are old features.
12. (order) Put these animals in order, from your most distant relative to your closest.  
   **Answer:** ['Carp', 'Lungfish', 'Frog', 'Hen', 'Platypus']. *Why:* Each one shares one more step of the family tree with you than the one before. The platypus is a mammal, so she is the closest of these.
13. (odd) Three of these have four limbs, or had ancestors with four limbs. Which one does not?  
   Options: Snake / Whale / Caecilian / Seahorse  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A seahorse is a fish with thin fan fins, and her ancestors never had four limbs. Snakes, whales and caecilians lost limbs that their ancestors had.
14. (mcq) Scientists compare the DNA of two animals and find few differences. What does that suggest?  
   Options: They share a recent ancestor / They are the same size / They live in the same place / They eat the same food  
   **Answer:** They share a recent ancestor. *Why:* Copying mistakes build up over time. Few differences means little time on separate branches.
15. (perspective) Two scientists talk about the word 'reptile'.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer chooses one view and gives a reason from the family tree or from everyday use. It also says what the other view gets right.. *Why:* Both scientists accept the same family tree. They disagree about what names are for: showing who is related, or describing a way of living.
### In-between animals: argued ideas and deep time (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Lungs and limbs came before life on land. Give two ideas for why limbed animals first left the water.  
   **Answer:** One idea: the banks had food, such as insects and millipedes, and no big hunters. Another: warm shallows held little oxygen, and a raised head could reach the air. An older idea, that they crawled between drying ponds, is now doubted.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two ideas and treats them as ideas. Nobody knows for certain.
2. (mcq) Humans carry broken genes for making egg yolk. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Our distant ancestors laid yolky eggs / Humans will lay eggs one day / We get them from eating eggs / They came from hens  
   **Answer:** Our distant ancestors laid yolky eggs. *Why:* The early ancestors of mammals laid eggs with yolk. After milk took over the feeding of young, the yolk genes were no longer needed and stopped working. Genes do not come from food.
3. (count) One centimetre is 1 million years. Tiktaalik is at 375 and Acanthostega at 365. How many centimetres apart?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* 375 − 365 = 10 centimetres, which stands for 10 million years.
4. (write) Bones and DNA were studied separately, and they give the same family tree. Why is that strong evidence?  
   **Answer:** The two kinds of evidence do not depend on each other. If the tree were wrong, there would be no reason for them to agree. Since they agree in almost every place, the tree is very likely real.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the two methods are separate, so their agreement is not a coincidence.
5. (truefalse) True or false: lungs evolved from the swim bladders of fish.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False, as far as the evidence shows. Lungfish and bichirs, on two early branches, both have lungs. So the first bony fish probably had lungs, and the swim bladder is a changed lung.
6. (mcq) The purple frog's closest relatives live on the Seychelles islands. What explains this best?  
   Options: The land they lived on split apart / Frogs swam across the ocean / People carried them there / Birds dropped their eggs  
   **Answer:** The land they lived on split apart. *Why:* India and the Seychelles were once joined. The land split and drifted apart with the frogs' ancestors on both pieces. Frogs cannot survive in sea water for long.
7. (sort) Sort these: scientists agree, or scientists still argue.  
   **Answer:** Birds are dinosaurs → Agree; Four-limbed animals came from lobe-finned fish → Agree; Why limbed animals first left the water → Still argue; What the first milk was for → Still argue; Warm water holds less oxygen than cold → Agree; Whether to call birds reptiles → Still argue. *Why:* Scientists agree on the family tree itself. They still argue about the reasons for some changes, and about how to name the groups.
8. (draw) Draw an animal halfway between a fish and a frog. Label three fish features and three newer features.  
   **Answer:** Fish features could be scales, gills and fin rays. Newer features could be a neck, a flat head with eyes on top, and sturdy front fins with a wrist.. *Why:* A strong drawing mixes old and new features in one body, as Tiktaalik did. Any reasoned mixture is acceptable.
9. (fill) A small leftover of a part that was bigger in an animal's ancestors is called a ____ part.  
   **Answer:** ['vestigial']. *Why:* The human tailbone and the small hip bones of a whale are vestigial parts.
10. (order) Put these in order, from the oldest to the most recent.  
   **Answer:** ['First vertebrates', 'Tiktaalik', 'First mammals', 'Archaeopteryx, an early bird', 'The asteroid strike']. *Why:* About 500, 375, 225, 150 and 66 million years ago. Mammals are older than birds, which surprises many people.
11. (write) If every in-between animal were still alive, could we sort vertebrates into five groups? Explain.  
   **Answer:** It would be very hard. Each animal would be only a little different from her nearest relatives, so there would be no gaps to draw lines in. The groups look separate today because most of the in-between animals died out.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the gaps between groups come from extinction. Any answer that reasons from this is good, whichever way it concludes.
12. (mcq) Water holds 11.3 milligrams of oxygen per litre at 10 °C and 7.6 at 30 °C. What fraction is lost?  
   Options: About a third / About a half / About a tenth / None  
   **Answer:** About a third. *Why:* 11.3 − 7.6 = 3.7. And 3.7 out of 11.3 is close to one third. So a pond at 30 °C holds about a third less oxygen than one at 10 °C.
13. (match) Match each piece of evidence to what it suggests.  
   **Answer:** A fossil with both gills and fingers → the first limbs were used in water; Lungs in both lungfish and bichirs → the first bony fish had lungs; Broken yolk genes in humans → our ancestors laid eggs with yolk; Small hip bones inside a whale → her ancestors had back legs; Nest-guarding in crocodiles and birds → their shared ancestor may have guarded nests. *Why:* Each line goes from something that can be seen to a conclusion about the past. A strong answer could say how sure each conclusion is.
14. (label) Draw a murrel's head from the side. Label the gills, the air chamber, and her mouth at the surface.  
   **Answer:** ['Gills: behind the head, under the gill cover', 'Air chamber: above the gills', 'Mouth: just through the water surface']. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the air chamber above the gills, and shows the fish reaching the surface. She uses the gills and the chamber together.
15. (perspective) Three ideas about why limbed animals first left the water.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one idea and uses evidence from the unit: the first limbs were too weak for crawling, the fossils come from wet wooded places, insects were on land already, and warm water holds little oxygen.. *Why:* Most scientists now doubt the drying ponds idea. The other two are both taken seriously, and both may be partly right.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A murrel is kept in a pot filled to the brim, under a tight lid. What is the danger?  
   Options: She cannot reach air and may drown / The water is too clean / The water is too cold / She has no room to turn  
   **Answer:** She cannot reach air and may drown. *Why:* A murrel must gulp air at the surface. With no air gap she cannot breathe properly, even though she is in water.
2. (mcq) Which holds more oxygen: a litre of air or a litre of pond water?  
   Options: Air, about 30 times as much / Water, about 30 times as much / They hold the same / It depends on the fish  
   **Answer:** Air, about 30 times as much. *Why:* Air is about one-fifth oxygen. Water holds only a little dissolved oxygen, about 9 milligrams in a litre at 20 °C.
3. (mcq) Which came first in the history of animals?  
   Options: Lungs, then legs / Legs, then lungs / Both at the same time / Nobody has any evidence  
   **Answer:** Lungs, then legs. *Why:* Lungfish have lungs and no legs, and every animal with four limbs has lungs. Fossils agree: fish had lungs long before any had limbs.
4. (mcq) Which animal shows that milk evolved before mammals stopped laying eggs?  
   Options: Platypus / Kangaroo / Fruit bat / Penguin  
   **Answer:** Platypus. *Why:* The platypus lays eggs and feeds her hatched young on milk. A kangaroo is born alive, and a penguin is a bird with no milk.
5. (mcq) Tiktaalik had gills and scales, and also a neck and wrists. What is such a fossil called?  
   Options: A transitional fossil / A living fossil / A vestigial part / A convergent fossil  
   **Answer:** A transitional fossil. *Why:* A transitional fossil has features of an older group and of a newer group. It shows what the animals in between were like.
6. (mcq) Which of these is the closest living relative of a crow?  
   Options: Mugger crocodile / Monitor lizard / Fruit bat / King cobra  
   **Answer:** Mugger crocodile. *Why:* Crocodiles and birds share an ancestor that lizards and snakes do not. A bat flies, but she is a mammal.
7. (mcq) Bats and birds both have wings. Why is this not a sign that they are close relatives?  
   Options: Wings arose separately in each / Bats are a kind of bird / Wings are a very old feature / Both lay eggs  
   **Answer:** Wings arose separately in each. *Why:* Their shared ancestor had no wings. Each branch evolved wings separately, which is called convergent evolution.
8. (truefalse) True or false: the steps of the Feature Ladder show which animals are better.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The steps show the order in which features first appeared. Every animal on the ladder is alive today and has evolved for the same length of time.
9. (mcq) Which part of your body is the leftover of a tail?  
   Options: The tailbone / The navel / The kneecap / The heel  
   **Answer:** The tailbone. *Why:* The tailbone is three to five small joined bones at the end of the backbone. The navel is where the cord from the placenta joined you.
10. (mcq) Why do fish in a warm, shallow pond gasp at the surface at dawn?  
   Options: The water has very little dissolved oxygen / They are hungry / The water is too cold / They are looking for light  
   **Answer:** The water has very little dissolved oxygen. *Why:* Warm water holds little oxygen, and plants and rotting leaves use it up overnight. The thin top layer of water, next to the air, has the most.
11. (mcq) A fossil has feathers, and also teeth and a long bony tail. What does it most likely show?  
   Options: An animal between older dinosaurs and birds / A bird that grew teeth by mistake / A lizard that could fly / Two animals mixed up  
   **Answer:** An animal between older dinosaurs and birds. *Why:* Feathers are a bird feature. Teeth and a long bony tail are older dinosaur features. Archaeopteryx, about 150 million years old, is such an animal.
12. (mcq) In the Air in Water test, what had to stay the same for all three tumblers?  
   Options: The tumbler, the amount and the place / Only the colour of the tumbler / How cold the water was at the start / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** The tumbler, the amount and the place. *Why:* Only the water's history was changed: cold, room-warm or boiled. Everything else was kept the same, so any difference in bubbles came from that one change.

## Applications
- Fish farmers pump air into ponds on hot nights, because warm water holds little oxygen. Farmers of murrel and climbing perch can keep them in shallow, still ponds, because these fish breathe air.
- Live murrels and climbing perches are carried to market in wet baskets with very little water, so they reach the buyer fresh.
- Anyone who keeps a fighting fish or a gourami must leave an air gap under the lid, because these fish gulp air.
- Doctors and scientists learn about human bodies from mice, zebrafish and frogs. This works because we share ancestors with them, and so we share most of our genes and organs.
- Fossil hunters, like those who found Tiktaalik, choose where to dig by working out how old the rock must be and what kind of place it formed in.
- Wildlife officers use DNA to find out which animal a piece of meat, skin or fish came from.
- People who protect nature give special care to animals on old, lonely branches of the family tree, such as the purple frog and the caecilians of India.

## Spotted around us
- Live murrels or climbing perches in a tub at a fish market, coming up to the surface for air while other fish lie on ice.
- A fighting fish in a small bowl at a pet shop, swimming up every few minutes to gulp air.
- Mudskippers hopping on the wet mud at low tide in a mangrove creek, such as at Point Calimere in Tamil Nadu or Coringa in Andhra Pradesh.
- Fish crowding and gasping at the surface of a temple tank or village pond early on a hot summer morning.
- The scaly legs and feet of a crow, a hen or a pigeon. They are the same kind of scales a crocodile has.
- Goosebumps on your own arm when you step out of a bath on a cool morning.
- A silver balloon inside a fish being cleaned at the market. It is the swim bladder, a changed lung.
- Frogs calling from puddles on the first nights of the monsoon. They must lay their eggs in water, because the eggs have no amnion or shell.

## Open questions
- **Why did animals with limbs first come out of the water?** Limbs and lungs were there already. Fossils show bodies and the places they lived, but they do not show reasons. Food on the banks, safety from hunters and easier breathing are all possible.
- **What was the very first milk for?** Milk does not fossilise. One idea is that it kept soft-shelled eggs moist and free of germs. Another is that it fed the hatched young from the start. The genes give hints and no clear answer.
- **How many separate times did fish evolve a way to breathe air?** Air breathing is found in many branches of fish, with different organs in each. Scientists count dozens of separate origins, and the count changes as more fish are studied.
- **What is it like for a platypus to sense the electric signals of a prawn?** We can measure the sensors in her bill and how her brain uses them. Nobody can know from outside what the sense feels like to her.
- **Why did one branch of mammals keep laying eggs for 160 million years or more?** Egg-laying clearly works well for the platypus and the echidnas. Whether something about their way of living favoured it, or whether live birth simply never arose in their branch, is not known.
- **Is a group of animals something real in nature, or a line that people draw?** Branches of the family tree are real. But where one group ends and the next begins depends on which in-between animals have died out, and on what we choose to name.

## Connected maths
- A litre of air holds about 270 milligrams of oxygen. A litre of pond water at 20 °C holds about 9 milligrams. How many litres of water must pass over a fish's gills to bring as much oxygen as one litre of air? **About 30 litres of water.**. 270 ÷ 9 = 30. One litre of air holds as much oxygen as 30 litres of water. So one gulp of air is worth many mouthfuls of water. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A litre of fresh water holds about 11.3 milligrams of oxygen at 10 °C, 9.1 at 20 °C and 7.6 at 30 °C. Does the amount fall by the same number for each 10 degrees? Predict the amount at 40 °C. **No. The fall gets smaller each time. A good prediction for 40 °C is between 6 and 7 milligrams. The measured value is about 6.4.**. From 10 to 20 °C the fall is 11.3 − 9.1 = 2.2. From 20 to 30 °C it is 9.1 − 7.6 = 1.5. The second fall is smaller. If the next fall is smaller again, about 1.0 to 1.2, the amount at 40 °C is about 6.4 to 6.6. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
- In your Feature Ladder table, 6 animals have lungs and 5 have four limbs. Is every animal with four limbs also in the group with lungs? Is every animal with lungs also in the group with four limbs? What does this tell you? **Every animal with four limbs has lungs. Not every animal with lungs has four limbs, because the lungfish has lungs and no limbs. So lungs came first.**. Draw a big circle for the 6 animals with lungs. The 5 animals with four limbs all fit inside it, in a smaller circle. One animal, the lungfish, is in the big circle and outside the small one. A feature that arrived later is always found inside the group that has the earlier feature. (queues `math.logic.sets-and-venn.L4`)
- On the string timeline, the first mammals are at 225 centimetres and the asteroid strike at 66. For what fraction of their history, to the nearest tenth, did mammals live beside the big dinosaurs? **About seven tenths.**. Mammals and big dinosaurs shared the Earth for 225 − 66 = 159 million years. The whole history of mammals is 225 million years. 159 ÷ 225 is about 0.7, or seven tenths. Mammals have lived without the big dinosaurs for only the last three tenths. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In a made-up Lap or Suck test, a child lapping from a plate drank 1 tablespoon in 20 seconds. Through a straw she drank 4 tablespoons in 8 seconds. How many times faster was the straw? **10 times faster.**. Lapping: 1 tablespoon in 20 seconds. Straw: 4 tablespoons in 8 seconds, which is 1 tablespoon in 2 seconds. 20 ÷ 2 = 10. The straw delivered water 10 times as fast. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Giving birth to live young arose about 150 separate times among vertebrates. About 115 of these were in lizards and snakes, and 1 was in mammals. Roughly what fraction arose in lizards and snakes? **About three quarters.**. 115 out of 150 is 23 out of 30, which is a little more than 0.75. So about three quarters of all the separate beginnings of live birth were in lizards and snakes. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- In Count the Differences, the cow string differs from the human string in 2 places and the hen string in 4. None of the places are the same. Predict how many places differ between the cow and the hen. Then count. **6 places.**. The cow has 2 changes that the human lacks, and the hen has 4 different ones. Going from the hen to the cow, you pass all 4 and then all 2. 4 + 2 = 6. Counting the two strings square by square also gives 6. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Name a mammal who lays eggs. → The platypus, or an echidna.
- Name an Indian fish who breathes air. → The murrel, the climbing perch or the mudskipper.
- Which came first, lungs or legs? → Lungs. Fish had them long before any animal had legs.
- Which holds more dissolved oxygen, cold water or warm water? → Cold water.
- Which living animals are the closest relatives of birds? → Crocodiles.
- What is your tailbone the leftover of? → A tail.
- How many fingers did Acanthostega have on each hand? → Eight.
- What do few differences between the DNA of two animals show? → That they shared an ancestor not very long ago.
