# One cell, then two: how you grew and how your body stays new
`sci.what-is-life.cells-divide.L4`

**Summary.** You started as one cell, smaller than the dot of a pencil. Today you are made of about 30 to 40 trillion. No one added them from outside. Each cell copied the instructions inside her, and then split in two. The same copying goes on today. The lining of your gut is replaced in days, and your skin in weeks. In this unit you cut the tip off a young root to find where plants really grow, and you work out how a body can be rebuilt all the time and still be yours.

**Intro.** *Look at a young root of a moong sprout, about one finger-joint long. Tomorrow it will be much longer. Where does the new length come from? Does the whole root stretch like a rubber band, or does the root grow in one small place?* Think about your own skin as well. Every day, some of it rubs off on your clothes and in the bath. Yet your skin never wears thin. Something is making new skin as fast as the old skin leaves. In this unit you find out where new cells come from, how one cell becomes two, and how that made you.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that a cell makes a new cell by copying her instructions and splitting into two, and that this is how living things grow and mend.
1. I can describe the order of cell division: copy the instructions, line up the copies, pull them apart, and pinch (or wall) the cell in two.
2. I can use a fair test to show that a root grows at its tip, where cells divide, and explain what the result means.
3. I can explain how one fertilised egg became a whole body, and why a skin cell and a muscle cell can come from the same first cell.
4. I can give examples of cells that are replaced in days, weeks or months, and cells that last for most of a life.
5. I can work out how many cells there are after a number of doublings, and say why doubling grows so fast.
6. I can explain why a body is not the same stuff for ever, and give two views on what stays the same.

## Terms
- **Cell division**: The way one cell becomes two. The cell copies her instructions, shares the copies out, and splits. *e.g.* A root grows longer because cells at the tip keep dividing.
- **Daughter cells**: The two new cells made when one cell divides. The word daughter is used for both, whatever the sex of the body. *e.g.* One skin cell divides and makes two daughter skin cells.
- **DNA**: The long instruction book inside a cell. It is written in four letters, A, T, C and G. It is copied each time the cell divides. *e.g.* A human cell holds about 6 billion DNA letters. Written out, they would fill thousands of thick books.
- **Chromosome**: One long piece of DNA wound up tightly, like a thread on a reel. Chromosomes become easy to see when a cell is about to divide. *e.g.* A human cell has 46 chromosomes, which is 23 pairs.
- **Gene**: One short stretch of DNA with the instructions for making one useful thing, such as a part of the body's machinery. *e.g.* One gene carries the instructions for the red substance that carries oxygen in blood.
- **Mitosis**: The careful way a cell with a nucleus shares one full set of chromosomes into each daughter cell. The name comes from a Greek word for thread. *e.g.* Mitosis happens when a skin cell, a root-tip cell or a gut-lining cell divides.
- **Cell cycle**: The repeating life of a dividing cell: grow, copy the instructions, check the copies, divide, and start again. *e.g.* A cell in a root tip may go once round the cycle in about a day.
- **Fertilised egg**: The first cell of a new person or other animal. It forms when a sperm cell joins an egg cell. It is the cell that divides to make the whole body. *e.g.* Every person began as a fertilised egg about the size of a full stop.
- **Embryo**: A very young body made of only a few cells, or a few thousand, that has just started to divide. *e.g.* After three rounds of division, an embryo has 8 cells.
- **Specialised cell**: A cell that has taken on one job, such as carrying oxygen or making a muscle pull. She uses only the pages of the instruction book that job needs. *e.g.* A muscle cell and a skin cell hold the same instructions but use different pages.
- **Stem cell**: A cell that can keep dividing, and can make daughter cells that become specialised for different jobs. *e.g.* Stem cells in your bone marrow make new blood cells every second.
- **Meristem**: The growing region of a plant, such as the tip of a root or a shoot, where cells divide again and again. *e.g.* The pale tip of a moong root is a meristem.
- **Binary fission**: The way a bacterium divides. She copies her one loop of DNA, grows longer, and pinches in the middle into two. *e.g.* A bacterium in warm milk can divide every 20 to 30 minutes.
- **Budding**: The way a yeast cell often divides. A small bump grows on the side of the cell, takes a copy of the instructions, and breaks off. *e.g.* Yeast cells in dough make buds.
- **Replacement**: Making new cells to take the place of old ones that have worn out or died. Scientists also call this cell turnover. *e.g.* The lining of your gut is replaced every few days.
- **Wound healing**: How a body repairs a cut. Cells near the edges divide and move in, until the gap is closed. *e.g.* A scrape on a knee is usually closed in a week or two.

## Explanations
### A cell makes a new cell by splitting in two · *defined*
**Definition:** Cell division is when one cell copies her instructions and then splits into two daughter cells, each with a full set of the instructions.

Every cell comes from a cell that was there before. A cell cannot be made from nothing, and she cannot be sewn together from parts. She has to divide. One cell becomes two, two become four, and four become eight. That is how a seed grows into a plant and an egg grows into an animal, including a human. It is also how a cut heals. A cell that divides does not die and leave a body behind. She becomes the two daughters.
- e.g. A bacterium in warm milk divides into two, then four, then eight.
- e.g. A yeast cell grows a bud and the bud breaks off as a new cell.
- e.g. A fertilised egg divides into 2 cells, then 4, then 8.
### Copying the instructions first: DNA · *defined*
**Definition:** DNA is the long molecule inside a cell that holds her instructions. Before a cell divides, she copies all of it.

DNA looks like a twisted ladder. Each rung joins two letters. A joins only with T, and C joins only with G. So each side of the ladder spells out the other. To copy it, the cell unzips the ladder down the middle. Then she builds a new matching side on each half. The result is two complete ladders, each with one old side and one new side. A human cell copies about 6 billion letters in a few hours, and only a handful of mistakes get through.
- e.g. Unzip a zip fastener, and each half can be matched with a new half.
- e.g. A line of letters ATTC pairs with TAAG on the other side.
- e.g. Each new DNA ladder has one old side and one new side.
### Chromosomes: the instruction book in 46 volumes · *defined*
**Definition:** A chromosome is one long piece of DNA wound tightly around proteins. Humans have 46 chromosomes in most cells, which is 23 pairs.

DNA is so long that it would not fit in the cell if it were loose. So it is wound up, like thread on a reel. Just before a cell divides, each chromosome has been copied, and the two copies stay joined in the middle so they look like an X. You get 23 chromosomes from your mother's egg and 23 from your father's sperm. Other living things have other numbers. A rice plant has 24, a dog has 78, and the Indian muntjac deer has just 6 in a female and 7 in a male.
- e.g. A fruit fly has 8 chromosomes, a chimpanzee has 48, and a human has 46.
- e.g. The Indian muntjac, a small deer of Indian forests, has the fewest known of any mammal.
- e.g. More chromosomes does not mean a cleverer or bigger living being.
### Mitosis: sharing the copies equally · *defined*
**Definition:** Mitosis is how a cell with a nucleus shares her copied chromosomes so that each daughter cell gets exactly one full set.

After the copying, the cell goes through four steps. First, the chromosomes wind up tight and the nucleus wall breaks down. Second, the chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell. Third, thin threads called spindle fibres pull each pair apart, so one copy goes to each end. Fourth, the cell pinches in the middle, and two new nuclei form. The pulling is the important part. If one daughter got two copies and the other got none, the daughters would not work properly.
- e.g. Imagine tugging a rope from both ends until the middle parts.
- e.g. A dividing cell in an onion root tip shows the chromosomes lined up like a row of beads.
- e.g. Mistakes in sharing are the cause of some illnesses that a person is born with.
### How plant cells and animal cells finish dividing · *defined*
**Definition:** An animal cell finishes by pinching in the middle. A plant cell finishes by building a new wall across the middle.

An animal cell has a soft skin and no wall, so she can squeeze in around the middle, like a balloon pinched by a thread. A plant cell has a stiff wall outside the skin, so pinching is not possible. Instead, she builds a new wall from the middle outwards. The new wall is called a cell plate. This is part of why plants are stiff and why plant cells in a leaf look like boxes stacked neatly side by side.
- e.g. An animal cell pinches like a water balloon squeezed in the middle.
- e.g. A plant cell builds a dividing wall, like putting up a partition in a room.
- e.g. Bacteria also pinch in two, but they do not use mitosis.
### Why a root grows only at the tip · *defined*
**Definition:** A root grows longer at its tip. Cells divide in a small zone at the very end, and the new cells stretch a little just behind it.

The part of a root a few millimetres behind the tip has stopped dividing. Its cells are finished, and they only soak up water. So if you mark dots along a growing root, only the dots close to the tip move apart. The tip also has a thin cap that rubs through the soil and wears away, and it is replaced all the time. If you cut off the tip, the root usually stops lengthening. A root pushes down through soil as a kind of explorer. Only the very end is exploring.
- e.g. A moong sprout's root grows about a centimetre in a day, almost all of it at the tip.
- e.g. The stems of tulsi and chilli grow at shoot tips. Pinch the tip and the plant grows side branches instead.
- e.g. A banyan's hanging roots grow downward until their tips reach the soil.
### From one cell to you: a fertilised egg divides · *defined*
**Definition:** A new person starts as a fertilised egg, one cell. She divides many times, and the daughter cells take on different jobs.

The fertilised egg divides for the first time after about a day. Then 2 become 4, 4 become 8, and 8 become 16. After a few days the group is a hollow ball of cells. Over the weeks that follow the cells move, fold and take on different jobs. A baby is born after about nine months. A grown person has about 30 to 40 trillion cells. To reach that number from one cell takes at least 45 rounds of doubling, since 2 multiplied by itself 45 times is about 35 trillion. In real bodies it takes many more divisions than that, because cells also die and are replaced along the way.
- e.g. Identical twins form when the early group of cells splits into two groups, and each group makes a baby.
- e.g. Doubling 10 times gives 1,024 cells. Doubling 20 times gives over a million.
- e.g. A baby has fewer cells than a grown person, and most of the difference is more cells, not bigger cells.
### Same instructions, different jobs: specialised cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Specialised cells use different parts of the same instructions. Nearly every cell in your body holds the full set.

A skin cell and a muscle cell are very different shapes. Yet each holds the same 46 chromosomes. They look different because they use different pages of the instruction book. A muscle cell uses pages that build the stretchy threads for pulling. A skin cell uses pages that build a tough, waterproof layer. Which pages to use is decided by signals from the cells around her. In 1962 John Gurdon put the nucleus of a frog's gut cell into an egg whose own nucleus had been removed. The egg grew into a tadpole. So the gut cell had kept the whole book.
- e.g. A red blood cell loses her nucleus when she is made, and so can never divide.
- e.g. A neuron is long and thin and sends signals, though she has the same instructions as a bone cell.
- e.g. A cut piece of a tulsi stem can grow roots and become a whole new plant, since the cells still hold the whole book.
### Replacement: cells wear out and are rebuilt · *defined*
**Definition:** Most cells wear out. New cells from dividing stem cells take the place of the old ones.

Cells have different lifetimes. The lining of your gut is replaced in about 3 to 5 days, because food scrapes it all the time. The outer layer of skin is replaced in about a month. Red blood cells last about 4 months. A strand of hair stays for years. A few kinds are almost never replaced in a grown person. Most nerve cells in the thinking part of the brain and the cells in the lens of the eye are as old as you are, and heart muscle cells are replaced very slowly. The parts of the body that meet the world, like skin and the gut lining, are often replaced fastest. Scientists can tell how old cells are, because carbon-14 from the bomb tests of the 1950s and 1960s reached the air, then food, and then the DNA built when each cell was made.
- e.g. Dust and flakes in a sunbeam include tiny pieces of dead skin.
- e.g. Your red blood cells are made at a rate of about two million every second, in the soft middle of your bones, called marrow.
- e.g. A scar remains after a deep cut because the repair is done with tough fibres, not new skin.
### What happens when cells divide too much · *defined*
**Definition:** Cancer is a disease in which some cells in a body ignore the stop signals and divide when they should not.

Cells do not divide all the time. They divide only when signals say so, and they stop when a gap is filled or when the copy has an error. Cancer begins when a cell gets damage in the instructions that control these signals. Then she divides again and again, and her daughters do too. The cluster can crowd out the cells next to it. Doctors can often treat cancer, especially when it is found early. A healthy body gets rid of most damaged cells before they cause trouble. Understanding how division is controlled is one of the biggest areas of research in medicine.
- e.g. A body's cells stop dividing when a cut has closed.
- e.g. Smoke and strong sunlight can damage the instructions in cells they touch.
- e.g. Finding a lump early gives doctors more ways to help.
### How many times can a cell divide? · *debated*
**Definition:** Most normal human cells, grown in a dish, divide about 40 to 60 times and then stop. This is called the Hayflick limit.

In 1961 Leonard Hayflick found that human cells in a dish do not divide for ever. The ends of chromosomes, called telomeres, get a little shorter each time the DNA is copied. When they are short enough, the cell stops dividing. Some cells, such as stem cells and cancer cells, switch on a repair machine called telomerase that rebuilds the ends. Whether the shortening is a main cause of ageing in a whole body, or only one part of it, is still argued about.
- e.g. HeLa cells, taken from a cancer in 1951, keep dividing in dishes in labs all over the world.
- e.g. Hydra, a tiny freshwater animal, seems to renew her cells without any sign of ageing.
- e.g. A cell in a dish is in a very different place from a cell inside a body.
- **Many cell biologists:** Short telomeres make cells stop dividing, and this limit protects the body from cells that divide too much. It is one cause of ageing.
- **Other researchers:** Telomeres matter, but a body ages for many reasons together, such as damage to proteins and to DNA. Telomere length alone does not predict how long a person lives.
- **The honest answer:** Nobody yet knows how much of ageing comes from the limit on division. Studies in animals and in people keep giving mixed results.

## Mostly agreed
- Every cell comes from another cell that divided. *How we know:* Cells have been watched dividing under microscopes since the 1800s. No one has ever seen a cell form from non-living material in a lab or a body.
- Before a cell divides, she copies all her DNA, and each daughter cell gets one full copy. *How we know:* In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl used heavy nitrogen to label DNA. After one round of copying, each new DNA held one old side and one new side.
- Humans have 46 chromosomes in most body cells. *How we know:* In 1956 Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan counted 46 in cells from the lungs of a human embryo. Counts of cells from many other people have given 46 since.
- Nearly every cell in a body holds the same DNA, and specialised cells use different parts of it. *How we know:* In 1962 John Gurdon grew tadpoles from eggs given the nucleus of a gut cell. In 1996 a sheep, Dolly, was cloned from a cell taken from an adult sheep's udder.
- Many kinds of cell in the body are replaced throughout life, but some are not. *How we know:* From 2005 on, teams led by Jonas Frisén, including Kirsten Spalding, Olaf Bergmann and Ratan Bhardwaj, used the carbon-14 left in the air by bomb tests to date human cells. Fat cells renewed about a tenth each year, heart muscle cells very slowly, and nerve cells in the thinking part of the brain were as old as the person.
- Cell division in bacteria, yeast, plants and animals follows the same basic pattern: copy the instructions, share the copies, and split. *How we know:* The same kinds of molecules do the copying in all of them. Genes for the copying machinery are so alike that a human gene can sometimes work in a yeast cell.

## How we found out
**Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan in Sweden, using a method found by T. C. Hsu, 1952 to 1956.** For about thirty years, textbooks said that a human cell has 48 chromosomes. An American scientist, Theophilus Painter, counted 48 in the 1920s, and everyone trusted him. Counting is hard, because the chromosomes of a dividing cell lie piled on top of one another like a tangle of thread. In 1952, in Texas, a scientist named T. C. Hsu found that dividing cells soaked in weak salt water swell up, and their chromosomes spread out. The story goes that this began as a mistake in the lab. In 1955, in Sweden, Joe Hin Tjio, who was born in Java, tried this with Albert Levan on cells from human embryos. The chromosomes lay apart and could be counted clearly. There were 46, not 48. Other labs soon counted 46 as well.  
*What people believed before:* For about thirty years scientists believed that humans have 48 chromosomes. Better preparation of the cells, so that the chromosomes spread apart, showed that the real number is 46.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Every cell in your body is replaced every seven years.** (Many popular articles and books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some cells really are replaced in days or weeks, so people guess that the whole body is renewed in a fixed time.  
  Against: Different cells have very different lifetimes. Cells in the gut last days, but most cells in the thinking part of the brain and in the eye lens stay for life. There is no single number of years for the whole body.
- **Once you are grown, your body stops making new cells.** (Many people, and some old textbooks.; *rejected*)  
  For: A grown person does not get taller, so it seems that growing has stopped.  
  Against: A grown body makes millions of new cells every second, in the marrow, skin and gut. What stops is growing bigger, and not the making of cells.
- **Adult humans can grow new brain cells in the memory area of the brain.** (Some neuroscientists, and others who disagree.; *debated*)  
  For: In 2018 Maura Boldrini and colleagues found signs of young neurons in the memory area (the hippocampus) of people up to the age of 79.  
  Against: Also in 2018, Shawn Sorrells and colleagues found almost none in people past childhood. The two groups used different ways of preserving and testing brains.
- **Cells in a body are all copies, so there are no differences between them.** (A common misunderstanding.; *rejected*)  
  For: All the cells start from one fertilised egg and share the same DNA.  
  Against: The DNA is nearly the same, but cells use different parts of it, and so they look and work very differently. A few mistakes also creep in during copying, so older bodies carry small differences between their cells.
- **A root stretches along its whole length as it grows.** (Many children and adults, before they test it.; *rejected*)  
  For: A root clearly gets longer, and it seems natural that every part grows a little.  
  Against: Dots marked along a growing root show that only the part within a few millimetres of the tip lengthens. The rest stays the same length.

## Roots
We have watched cells divide, and cut a root to see where growing happens. Now we ask what it means that a body is rebuilt all the time, and what stays when the cells change.

**Deep truth.** Often, the parts of you that meet the world are rebuilt fastest. Your gut lining is replaced in days, your skin in weeks, but safe cells deep inside can last for years. A living body is not a finished thing that gets mended now and then. It is made new, all the time, wherever it touches the world.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a body as a finished object that stays the same, and that is only mended when it is hurt. Cells show that rebuilding is the normal state, and that the busiest rebuilding happens at the edges.  
*Evidence:* In the root test, the tip stopped growing when it was cut off, while the finished part behind it did not lengthen. Skin flakes, gut lining and the root cap are all replaced often, while the cells in the eye lens stay for life.

- *who am i:* You are not a thing that stays still. Parts of you are new every day, and parts of you are older than any of your birthdays.
- *what is life:* A living being keeps copying her cells so that she can keep going. Life is a process of making and remaking, and not a block of stuff.
- *what is this world:* The atoms in a new skin cell came from food, water and air, and a few weeks later they go back to the world as flakes. The world flows through every body.

A fertilised egg has no skin, no gut, no blood and no bones. All of them come from one cell that copied her instructions and divided. The atoms in each new cell come from the food you ate, and the food came from plants, soil, rain and sunlight. When old cells wear out, their atoms go back to the world, and may be in the next plant, the next bird, the next child. A body is a place where the world is for a while, shaped by instructions that are copied from cell to cell. That is the one becoming the many, again and again.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.11:** Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu about a great tree. If the life leaves one branch, that branch dries up. If it leaves the whole tree, the whole tree dries up. The same life is in all of it. (A root grows only at the tip, but the tip is fed by the whole tree. The living part and the finished part are one tree.)
- **Plutarch, Life of Theseus 23:** Plutarch tells how the Athenians kept the ship of Theseus for hundreds of years. As planks rotted, they replaced them with new wood. Thinkers argued about whether it was still the same ship. (Your skin and gut lining are replaced like planks. The old puzzle becomes a question about your own body.)
- **Tao Te Ching 76:** A person is born soft and flexible, and at death is hard and stiff. Green plants are soft and full of sap, and when dead they are dry and brittle. The soft and yielding belongs to life. (The root tip is the softest, palest, wettest part of the plant. That is also the part that grows.)
- Science (settled): Rudolf Schoenheimer used heavy forms of hydrogen and nitrogen in the 1930s to follow molecules in the body. He showed that fats and proteins in a body are broken down and rebuilt all the time, even in a body that stays the same weight.
- Science (debated): Whether adult humans make new nerve cells in the memory area of the brain is still argued about. Two groups looked in 2018 and found different answers.
- Other view, Daniel Dennett, a philosopher (Consciousness Explained, 1991): He argued that the sense of being one fixed self is a story the brain tells, and not a thing hidden inside. On this view, there is nothing deeper to ask about than the changing pattern of the body and its story.
- Practice, Looking at your own hand (3 min): Hold one hand in front of you and look at it slowly. Breathe in and out three times. Remember that the top skin you see is made of cells that have finished their work. Underneath, new cells are pushing up. In about a month, this skin will be new. Ask yourself quietly, which part of me is this hand?
- Ask: If every plank of a ship is replaced one at a time, is it the same ship? Is your body the same body?
- Ask: What would you want to stay the same about you, if every cell in your skin and gut were replaced?
- Ask: Is the pattern of a body, or the instructions copied into the cells, what makes you you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A cell that divides turns into two cells. Did the first cell die, or did she become two? (hint: Think about what is left behind after a bacterium divides, compared with a person who dies.)
- *deep:* Your skin cells are replaced every few weeks. In what way is it still your skin? (hint: Think about a school uniform that gets patched, or a river whose water changes.)
- *deep:* Two cells with exactly the same instructions can grow into a muscle and a skin cell. What decides which one each becomes? (hint: Think about a book where each reader only turns to some pages.)
- *big:* Your body is a community of about 30 to 40 trillion cells that mostly obey the stop signals. Who is in charge? (hint: Think about a city, and about whether any single person runs it.)
- *big:* Cancer cells in a dish can divide for ever, but normal cells stop. Is a cell that never stops dividing a winner or a problem? (hint: Think about what the rest of the body needs from each cell.)
- *big:* Some cells in a body are as old as you are, and some are a few days old. Which are the real you? (hint: Think about whether the answer could be the pattern, and not the pieces.)
- *big:* Henrietta Lacks's cells were taken in 1951 without anyone asking her. They have helped medicine for 70 years. Who should decide what happens to cells from a body? (hint: Think about what she would have chosen, and what her family would say now.)
- *big:* A bacterium divides every 20 minutes and a nerve cell may never divide. Is one way of living better? (hint: Think about what each kind of cell does for the living being she belongs to.)

## Story: The Root That Would Not Be Pulled
*A made-up story with real science and a real tradition. In Meghalaya, the Khasi and Jaintia peoples guide the roots of the rubber fig (Ficus elastica) across streams to make living bridges. A bridge takes many years to become strong enough to walk on. The characters and their names are imagined.*

Daiaphi lived in a village above a deep green valley in Meghalaya, where it rains for most of the year. When she was six, her grandmother had taken her to a stream at the bottom of the valley. A bridge crossed it, made of living roots, as thick as a man's arm. They twisted across the water like a long green rope, and children and goats walked over it every day.

'Who built it?' Daiaphi had asked.

'My grandfather's grandfather started it,' said her grandmother. 'He never walked on it. His grandchildren did. We keep it growing now.'

Now Daiaphi was ten, and in Class 5. Her family was growing a new bridge, further down the same stream. It was her job to look after a young root from a rubber fig tree on the bank. For six years she had guided it. The root crept out of the tree, passed through a hollow trunk of an areca palm, and was now halfway across the stream. It was thin, and a little pale at the end, and it grew toward the far bank only a hand's width each month.

One morning a man came from the town, with a clipboard and a coil of strong rope. His name was Mr Rynjah. He was an engineer, and he was kind, but he was in a hurry. A road was being built, and the officials wanted to know whether the new bridge could be ready in two years.

'Two years?' said Daiaphi's father. 'It will be fifteen.'

'We can speed it up,' said Mr Rynjah. 'Tie a rope to the root's end and pull it across. A little each day. Roots stretch, don't they? The whole root will lengthen, and the gap will close.'

Daiaphi's father looked at the root, and he looked at the rope, and he looked uncertain. Maybe Mr Rynjah was right. He studied in the town. Daiaphi's grandmother only shook her head slowly and said nothing.

Daiaphi had a school notebook with her. In Class 4, her teacher, Madam Lyngdoh, had shown the class how to put tiny dots of ink on a sprout's root, and how to measure them again after a day. Daiaphi had done it with a bean. She still remembered what the dots had shown. She took a deep breath.

'Please, may I try something first?' she said. 'It will take only two days.'

Mr Rynjah looked at his watch. But her father nodded.

Daiaphi found a thin black thread. With her grandmother's help, she tied small knots of thread around the young root, every two fingers' width, from the pale tip back toward the tree, six knots in all. She drew them in her notebook. Then she wrote the distance between each knot.

Two days later, the whole village came to look, because by now everybody was curious. Daiaphi measured the knots again. The gaps between the old knots, near the tree, were exactly as wide as before. They had not grown by even a hair. Only the last gap, between the knot closest to the tip and the tip itself, had grown longer. It had grown by almost a finger's width.

'Look,' said Daiaphi. 'The root is not stretching along its length. It only grows at the end. The long part behind is finished. It has become strong and thick. The new part is only at the very tip, where the cells keep splitting in two.'

'Cells?' said Mr Rynjah slowly.

'Tiny rooms that everything is made of,' she said. 'Our teacher told us. At the tip they copy themselves and split, again and again, and then the new ones stretch a little. Behind the tip they stop. So if you pull the root, you cannot make the thick part longer. You will only tear the tip. And if the tip is gone, the root cannot grow at all.'

Nobody spoke. A drop fell from a leaf.

Then Mr Rynjah crouched down beside the stream and looked for a long time at the thin pale end of the root. He took a pencil and wrote something on his clipboard. After a while he said, 'So the only way to make it faster is to protect that small tip and keep it wet and well fed.'

'And to wait,' said Daiaphi's grandmother, and she smiled for the first time that morning.

'Fifteen years,' said Mr Rynjah. He laughed. 'The officials will not like it. But they will like a bridge that falls into the stream even less.'

He did not take the rope. Instead he asked the village to show him how they guided the tip with a hollow areca trunk, and he sent a note to the officials that said the new bridge should be opened when it was ready, and not before.

That evening Daiaphi sat by the stream with her grandmother. The old bridge hummed in the wind. 'Its oldest roots,' said her grandmother, 'were made before my own grandmother was born. And at the very ends, there are still tiny new tips, growing today.'

'So the bridge is old and new at the same time,' said Daiaphi.

'Like a person,' said her grandmother. 'Like you, with your scraped knees mending.' She touched Daiaphi's hand. 'Every part of us is being made again, at the edges, all the time. And still we say, this is my granddaughter.'

Daiaphi looked down at the thin pale tip of her own young root, hanging over the water. It was so small. But in it, every hour, cells were splitting in two, and the bridge for her own grandchildren was a little closer.

**For the parent:** A root does not stretch along its length. It grows only at the tip, where cells copy themselves and split in two.

- Ask: Why would pulling the root with a rope have torn it instead of lengthening it?
- Ask: If the thread knots stayed the same distance apart, what does that say about the part of the root behind the tip?
- Ask: Daiaphi's grandmother said that we are made again at the edges, all the time. Where on your body might that be true?

## Experiments
### e1 · Cut the Tip: where does a root grow? (50 min, 4 days)
- **question:** If you snip the tip off a young moong root, will the root still grow longer than a root with its tip left on?
- **predict:** Before you cut anything, guess. Will the roots with the tip cut off grow longer, the same, or less than the roots left alone? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every root is from the same moong seeds, on the same kind of plate and tissue, in the same warm place. All roots are touched by the scissors in the same way. On plate A the scissors only touch the tip and cut nothing. On plate B the scissors cut off 3 millimetres of the tip. So the only difference is the missing tip, and any difference in growth comes from that. Plate A is the control.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting, because the scissors are sharp and the root is tiny. Wash hands after handling the sprouts. Do not eat sprouts that smell bad or have fuzzy patches.
- **record:** Write the length of every root at the start, after one day and after two days. Work out how much each root grew. Find the average for plate A and for plate B. Draw a bar for each plate.
- **results table:** Plate | Root | Start (mm) | After 1 day (mm) | After 2 days (mm) | Growth in 2 days (mm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Soak the moong seeds for about 8 hours, then drain them and wrap them in a damp cloth. (5 min); day 2: Guess, set up two plates, measure every root and cut the tips on plate B. (25 min); day 3: Measure every root after one day. (10 min); day 4: Measure every root after two days, find the averages and explain what you saw. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Soak 12 moong seeds in water for about 8 hours or overnight. Drain them. Wrap them in a damp cloth in a bowl. Keep them somewhere warm.  
  *Parent says:* We will grow some moong sprouts first. Soak the seeds overnight. Tomorrow we wrap them in a damp cloth.  
  *Child mode:* Soak the moong in water overnight. Drain them. Wrap them in a damp cloth.
  - Note: You need roots between 5 and 15 millimetres. In a warm kitchen this takes about one day after soaking. In cool weather it can take two. Sprinkle the cloth with water twice a day. It should be damp, not dripping.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the sprouts with your child. Find the root of each one. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Look at these roots. Tomorrow I will cut the tip off some of them. Do you think they will still grow longer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the roots. We will cut the tips off some. Will they still grow? Why?
  - Ask: Which part of the root do you think grows longer? (good: All of it, like a rubber band.; Only the end.; The part nearest the seed.)
  - Ask: What do you think will happen to a root with no tip? (good: It will keep growing.; It will stop.; It will grow slowly.)
  - Note: Do not correct a guess. Most children think the whole root stretches, and that is a good place to start. Write the guesses on the page with the table.
  - Wait: Wait until the roots are 5 to 15 millimetres long.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pick eight sprouts with straight roots. Label two plates A and B. Lay damp tissue on each. Put four sprouts on each plate, roots pointing the same way. Lay a thread along each root, then measure the thread. Write each length.  
  *Parent says:* Put four sprouts on plate A and four on plate B. Lay the thread along each root. Then measure the thread on the ruler. Write down every length.  
  *Child mode:* Put four sprouts on each plate. Measure each root. Write it down.
  - Note: Roots curve a little. A thread laid gently along the root and then measured on the ruler gives the real length. Do not pull the roots straight, because that can hurt them. Choose roots that are about the same length on both plates.
  - Check: Why do we put sprouts from the same bunch on both plates? → **So the only difference is the cut tip**
- **s4 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Wash your hands. A grown-up snips 3 millimetres off the tip of each root on plate B. On plate A, the grown-up only touches each tip with the closed scissors. Cover both plates with damp tissue and a lid. Put them side by side in a warm, dark place.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up snips the very tip off the roots on plate B. On plate A, the scissors only touch. Why do we touch the roots on plate A?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts the tips on plate B. On plate A, the scissors only touch.
  - Ask: Why do we touch the roots on plate A with the scissors? (good: So both plates are handled the same way.; So the only difference is the missing tip.)
  - Note: 3 millimetres is about the width of a pencil's lead. Cut straight across. Both plates must stay together in the same place, so they have the same warmth and light. Check that the tissue stays damp.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After one day, take the lids off. Measure every root with the thread and the ruler. Write the numbers. Look closely at the cut roots. Drip a little water on the tissue and cover the plates again.  
  *Parent says:* It is one day later. Measure every root. Which plate has longer roots? What do you notice about the cut roots?  
  *Child mode:* Measure every root. Write the lengths. Look at the cut roots closely.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the cut roots on plate B? (good: They look shorter.; They have not grown much.; A few have little bumps on the side.)
  - Note: Roots on plate A usually grow 10 to 25 millimetres in a day when the weather is warm. Roots on plate B usually grow 0 to 5 millimetres. The part just behind the cut can still stretch a little on the first day. If all roots on both plates grew the same, check that you cut across the tip and not just the seed side. If you see white fuzz, rinse the tissue and use fresh tissue.
  - Check: After one day, the roots on plate A grew a lot and the roots on plate B grew very little. What does this suggest? → **The tip is where the root gets longer**
  - Wait: Wait one day after cutting.
- **s6 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After two days, measure every root again. Work out how much each root grew. Add the growth of the four roots on each plate and divide by four. Draw one bar for each plate. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Measure again. Find the average for each plate. Draw two bars. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that roots with the tip cut off grew much less, that the tip is where cells divide, and that the part behind the tip is finished. Do not give this answer first. Let your child say what they saw, then add the words cell division. If your results are not clear, write them as they are, and try again with a new bunch.
  - Think: A farmer's goat nibbles the very tips of a row of young roots. What would you expect to happen to the roots?
  - Wait: Wait one more day.
### e2 · Dots on a Root: how far does the growing zone reach? (35 min, 2 days)
- **question:** If you mark dots along a growing root, which gaps between the dots grow wider after a day?
- **predict:** Before you mark the roots, guess. Will all the gaps grow wider, or only some? Which ones? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each root gets the same row of dots, 2 millimetres apart, starting at the tip. All roots stay on the same plate in the same place. The gaps along one root are compared with each other, so the root is its own control. You also leave one root without dots, to check that the dots do not stop it growing.
- **safety:** Use a fine pen with waterproof ink, and keep it away from your mouth. Wash hands after handling the sprouts. Do not eat sprouts that have been marked.
- **record:** Write the length of each gap at the start and after one day. Work out the change. Draw a bar for each gap, from the tip backward.
- **results table:** Gap | Where it is on the root | Start (mm) | After 1 day (mm) | Change (mm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess, mark the dots on the roots and lay the roots on damp tissue. (25 min); day 2: Measure the gaps between the dots and find where the growing happened. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at a root with your child. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will put six dots along a root, two millimetres apart. Tomorrow the root will be longer. Will all the gaps grow wider? Or only some? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will put six dots on a root. Will all the gaps grow wider? Why?
  - Ask: Which gap do you think will grow the most? (good: The one at the tip.; The one nearest the seed.; All the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. If your child did Cut the Tip, they may guess that the gap nearest the tip grows most. Ask why they think so.
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Dry one root gently with tissue. Lay it next to the marked card with the tip at zero. Touch a fine pen to the root at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11 millimetres. Make small dots. Do the same for two roots. Leave one root without dots.  
  *Parent says:* Dry the root with tissue first. Lay the tip at zero on the card. Touch the pen very lightly at each mark. Make small dots, not lines.  
  *Child mode:* Dry the root. Lay it on the card. Make six small dots with the pen.
  - Note: This is the fussy step. A wet root does not hold ink. Dab dry, mark in a second, then put it straight back on damp tissue. If the dots smear or fade, use a toothpick dipped in kajal and touch it to the root. If a root breaks, set it aside and use another.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the roots on damp tissue on a plate. Cover with a lid. Keep the plate in a warm, dark place for one day.  
  *Parent says:* Lay the roots back on damp tissue. Cover them. Leave them for one day.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the roots on damp tissue. Cover them. Wait one day.
  - Wait: Leave the plate for one day.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After one day, measure each gap between the dots. Start at the tip and go backward. Write the gaps in the table. Work out how much each gap changed. Draw a bar for each gap. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Measure each gap, starting at the tip. Which gaps grew? Which stayed the same? Why do you think the growing happened where it did?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each gap from the tip back. Which gaps grew? Which stayed the same?
  - Note: Typical results: the first one or two gaps next to the tip grow the most. The next gaps grow a little. Gaps past about 1 centimetre hardly change. Dots may fade, so measure the same day. If the dots washed off, say so, and try again with kajal. A fuzzy result is still a result.
  - Check: The gaps nearest the tip grew wider, and the gaps far from the tip stayed the same. What does this show? → **Only a short zone near the tip lengthens**

  - Check: About how many doublings of one cell would it take to reach 35 trillion cells? → **About 45**

### a2 · Copy Machine: can you copy a long code without a mistake? (25 min)

### One cell becomes two (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) How does a cell make a new cell?  
   Options: By copying her instructions and splitting into two / By growing out of non-living material / By taking pieces from other cells and joining them  
   **Answer:** By copying her instructions and splitting into two. *Why:* Every cell comes from a cell that divided. She copies her instructions, shares the copies, and splits into two daughter cells. No one has ever seen a cell form from non-living material.
2. (truefalse) When a cell divides, the old cell is left behind as a dead body.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The cell does not die. She becomes the two daughter cells, and nothing is left behind.
3. (order) Put the steps of cell division in the right order.  
   **Answer:** ['The cell copies all her chromosomes', 'The copies line up across the middle of the cell', 'Threads pull the copies apart to the two ends', 'The cell pinches in two, or builds a wall across the middle']. *Why:* Copying must come first, so each daughter can get a full set. The pulling comes before the split. Splitting comes last.
4. (fill) Before a cell divides, she must make a copy of her ____.  
   **Answer:** ['DNA', 'chromosomes', 'instructions']. *Why:* If a cell split before copying, each daughter would get only half of the instructions.
5. (mcq) Why do the copied chromosomes line up in the middle of the cell before they are pulled apart?  
   Options: So that one copy of each can be pulled to each end / So that the cell can store them / So that the cell can become bigger

6. (mcq) A root with its tip cut off hardly grows longer, but a root left alone does. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Cells dividing at the tip make most of the new length / The tip is the only part that drinks water / Cutting makes roots weaker for ever  
   **Answer:** Cells dividing at the tip make most of the new length. *Why:* The two roots differed only in the tip. So the tip is where most of the lengthening comes from. Cells divide there and then stretch a little.
7. (truefalse) A root grows longer evenly along its whole length, like a rubber band stretching.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dots marked along a growing root show that only the part within a few millimetres of the tip lengthens. The rest stays the same length.

   **Answer:** Bacterium → pinches in two, without mitosis; Yeast cell → grows a bud that breaks off; Plant cell → builds a new wall across the middle; Animal cell → pinches in the middle. *Why:* Different cells share the copying and the splitting, but finish in different ways. A plant cell has a stiff wall, so she builds a new wall. An animal cell has none, so she pinches.
9. (mcq) What is a fertilised egg?

11. (mcq) Which of these is replaced fastest in a grown person?

12. (fill) Cells that meet the outside world, such as skin and the gut lining, are often replaced ____ than cells deep inside.  
   **Answer:** ['faster', 'more quickly', 'sooner']. *Why:* The outside world scrapes and wears these cells, and so they are replaced fast. Cells deep inside are better protected and can last for years.
13. (count) A bacterium divides into two every 30 minutes. Start with 1 bacterium. How many are there after 2 hours?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* 2 hours is four periods of 30 minutes. 1 doubles four times: 2, 4, 8, 16. So there are 16 bacteria.

   **Answer:** At the tip, cells divide again and again and the new cells stretch a little. Behind the tip the cells have finished. They do not divide, so that part stays the same length.. *Why:* A strong answer says that cells divide at the tip, that the new cells make the new length, and that the older part behind the tip does not lengthen.

### Chromosomes, jobs and replacement (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)

2. (mcq) A human cell has 46 chromosomes. How many does each daughter cell get after normal mitosis?

   **Answer:** 46. *Why:* The cell copies her chromosomes to 92 and then shares them equally. Each daughter gets 46, the same as the cell she came from.

## Applications
- Healing a cut or scrape. Cells at the edges divide and move in until the gap is closed.
- Bone marrow transplants for some blood illnesses. Healthy stem cells settle in the marrow and make new blood cells.
- Pinching the tip of a tulsi, mint or chilli so that side branches grow and the plant becomes bushy.
- Growing banana plants from tiny pieces of plant in a laboratory, called tissue culture. Many banana farms in Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra plant them.
- Growing a new plant from a cutting of a money plant or a rose, because stem cells can make roots.
- Treating cancer with medicines and radiation that harm cells that divide very fast, together with care to protect healthy cells.
- Counting chromosomes under a microscope to find out whether a baby has extra or missing ones.
- Growing viruses for some vaccines in cells in laboratory dishes, which began with cell lines such as those from Henrietta Lacks.
- Skin grafts for burns, where skin is grown from a small piece of healthy skin.
- Growing vegetables from tissue-cultured seedlings of ginger, potato and cardamom.

## Open questions
- **How does a cell know when a gap has been filled, and when to stop dividing?** Cells use many signals from their neighbours, and scientists understand some but not all. Cancer is partly a failure of these signals.
- **Can adults make new nerve cells in the memory area of the brain?** Two groups studied human brains in 2018 and found different answers. The brains were prepared in different ways, and the question is not settled.
- **Why can a salamander regrow a leg, or a hydra regrow most of her body, when a human cannot?** Humans have many of the same genes. Scientists do not yet know why the regrowing is switched off, or whether it could be switched on.
- **Does the shortening of telomeres cause ageing, or is it only one sign of it?** Cells with short telomeres stop dividing, but many things change as a body ages. Studies in people and animals have given mixed results.
- **How does a fertilised egg 'decide' which of her daughters will become skin, muscle or nerve?** Signals between cells are known in some animals, but the whole pattern in a human embryo is not yet understood.
- **Why do some animals, such as some tortoises and a few sharks, seem to age so slowly?** Their cells divide, repair and die like ours, but scientists do not yet know what protects them.

## Connected maths
- Aarav folds a sheet of paper in half again and again, and counts the boxes after each fold. He gets 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64. How many boxes will he have after 8 folds, if he could fold that far? **256 boxes.**. The number doubles with every fold. After 6 folds there are 64. After 7 folds there are 128, and after 8 folds there are 256. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A fertilised egg divides, and each daughter divides, again and again. After 12 doublings, how many cells are there? Then say how many there would be if the egg had started with 3 cells. **4,096 cells, and 12,288 if you start with 3.**. 2 doubled 12 times is 4,096. With 3 cells at the start, the number is 3 times as big. 3 times 4,096 is 12,288. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the Cut the Tip test, the four roots on plate A grew 12, 18, 15 and 15 millimetres in two days. The four roots on plate B grew 2, 4, 3 and 3 millimetres. Find the average growth on each plate, and say how many times bigger the average on plate A is. **Plate A averaged 15 millimetres and plate B averaged 3 millimetres. A is 5 times as much.**. A: 12 + 18 + 15 + 15 = 60, and 60 divided by 4 is 15. B: 2 + 4 + 3 + 3 = 12, and 12 divided by 4 is 3. 15 divided by 3 is 5. So plate A grew 5 times as much. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In Dots on a Root, five gaps start at 2 millimetres each. After a day, the gaps are 5, 3, 2, 2 and 2 millimetres, counting from the tip. What fraction of the total growth came from the gap nearest the tip? **3 out of 4, which is three quarters.**. The gaps grew by 3, 1, 0, 0 and 0 millimetres, which is 4 millimetres in all. The first gap grew 3 of those 4 millimetres. So three quarters of the growth was at the first gap. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Marrow makes about 2 million red blood cells every second. About how many is that in one minute, and in one hour? **120 million in a minute, and 7,200 million (7.2 billion) in an hour.**. 2 million times 60 seconds is 120 million. 120 million times 60 minutes is 7,200 million, which is 7.2 billion. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- The lining of the gut is replaced about every 4 days. About how many times does it get replaced in a year, and about how many times in your life so far if you are 9? **About 90 times a year, and about 800 times by age 9.**. 365 divided by 4 is about 91, so about 90 times in a year. 9 times 90 is 810, so about 800 times. These are rough, because the time varies. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- In Pinch the Tulsi, stem A (pinched) grew from 12 centimetres to 14 centimetres and has 4 side shoots. Stem B (not pinched) grew from 12 centimetres to 20 centimetres and has none. How much taller did stem B grow than stem A, and how many side shoots more does stem A have? **Stem B grew 6 centimetres more, and stem A has 4 more side shoots.**. Stem A grew 14 minus 12, which is 2 centimetres. Stem B grew 20 minus 12, which is 8 centimetres. 8 minus 2 is 6. Stem A has 4 side shoots and stem B has none, so stem A has 4 more. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
- A dog has 78 chromosomes, a human has 46, a rice plant has 24 and a fruit fly has 8. Put them in order from fewest to most. How many more chromosomes does the dog have than the rice plant? Does a bigger number mean a cleverer animal? **Fruit fly 8, rice 24, human 46, dog 78. The dog has 54 more than rice. No, the number does not show how clever or how big a living being is.**. Ordering the numbers: 8, 24, 46, 78. 78 minus 24 is 54. The number of chromosomes is just a count of how the DNA is packaged, and it does not say anything about how clever or big a living being is. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
