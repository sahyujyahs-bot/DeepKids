# Chemistry detectives: how ink and flames show what things are made of
`sci.chemistry-detectives.finding-out-whats-inside.L3`

**Summary.** A black sketch pen seems to hold one black ink. Put a dot of it on a paper strip and let water climb the paper. The black spreads out into other colours, such as blue, purple or yellow. Most black ink is a mixture: several dyes mixed together. The water carries each dye a different distance, so the dyes come apart. This way of separating a mixture is called chromatography. A flame can also show what is inside a thing. Salt turns a blue gas flame yellow-orange, because salt has sodium in it. Each element gives a flame its own colour. Chemists use tests like these to find out what things are made of, even the Sun.

**Intro.** *Try this. Make a fat dot with a black sketch pen near the bottom of a strip of kitchen towel. Dip the very end of the strip in water, below the dot. Watch for five minutes. The water climbs, and the black dot starts to turn into other colours. Why would black ink turn into other colours?* Chemists are scientists who find out what things are made of, and how they change. Often they cannot see the answer with their eyes alone. So they use tests that make a thing show what is in it. In this unit you use two of their oldest tests. Wet paper pulls a mixture apart. A flame shows a colour for each element. Then you use them like a detective, to find out which pen wrote a secret note.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that a mixture is two or more substances mixed together, and that most black sketch pen ink is a mixture of dyes.
1. I can separate the dyes in a sketch pen's ink with paper and water, and explain why they come apart.
2. I can run a fair test to find out which pen wrote a note, and give my evidence.
3. I can say that each element gives a flame its own colour, and that salt turns a flame yellow-orange because it has sodium in it.
4. I can tell how Bunsen and Kirchhoff used the colours of flames to find new elements and to show what the Sun is made of.
5. I can name ways chemists' tests help people today, such as checking food, medicines and documents.

## Terms
- **Chemist**: A chemist is a scientist who studies what things are made of and how they change. *e.g.* A chemist in a food lab may test chilli powder to check that no extra colour was added to it.
- **Substance**: A substance is one kind of material, the same all the way through. Water, salt and sugar are each a substance. *e.g.* Salt is one substance. Salt water is two substances, salt and water, mixed together.
- **Mixture**: A mixture is two or more substances mixed together, but not joined into a new substance. A mixture can often be separated again. *e.g.* Salt water is a mixture. When the water dries up in the sun, the salt is left behind.
- **Dye**: A dye is a coloured substance that dissolves in a liquid and colours what it touches. *e.g.* Sketch pen inks, food colours and the colours in a cotton saree are made with dyes.
- **Dissolve**: When a substance dissolves, it breaks up into pieces too small to see and spreads all through a liquid. *e.g.* Sugar dissolves in tea. The dyes in a sketch pen dissolve in water.
- **Chromatography**: Chromatography is a way of separating a mixture. A liquid moves through paper or powder and carries each substance a different distance. The name comes from Greek words meaning 'colour writing'. *e.g.* A dot of black sketch pen on a wet paper strip spreads into a blue band and a purple band.
- **Chromatogram**: A chromatogram is the pattern of coloured bands left on the paper after chromatography. *e.g.* Your dried strip, with its bands of colour, is a chromatogram. Each ink makes its own.
- **Capillary action**: Capillary action is how water creeps up through tiny gaps, such as the gaps between the fibres in paper or cloth. *e.g.* Dip one corner of a towel in a bucket of water. The wet patch slowly climbs up the towel.
- **Element**: An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. Scientists know of 118 elements. *e.g.* Sodium, copper, oxygen and carbon are elements. Salt is not an element, because it is sodium and chlorine joined together.
- **Sodium**: Sodium is an element. On its own it is a soft, silvery metal, but in nature it is always joined to other elements. Table salt is sodium joined to chlorine. *e.g.* When salty rasam boils over onto a gas flame, the flame turns yellow-orange. That colour comes from sodium.
- **Flame test**: A flame test finds out which elements are in a substance. You put a little of it in a flame and look at the colour. *e.g.* A flame that turns bright yellow-orange shows that there is sodium.
- **Evidence**: Evidence is what you saw or measured that shows whether an idea is true. *e.g.* The secret note's strip had the same blue and pink bands as pen B. That is evidence that pen B wrote the note.
- **Fair test**: A fair test changes only one thing and keeps everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* Use the same paper, the same size of dot and the same water for every pen. Only the pen changes.
- **Forensic scientist**: A forensic scientist uses science to find out what happened, often to help the police or a court. *e.g.* A forensic scientist can test the ink on a cheque to see whether someone added a word with a different pen.

## Explanations
### Mixtures and pure substances · *defined*
**Definition:** A pure substance is one kind of material all the way through. A mixture is two or more substances mixed together but not joined.

Most things around us are mixtures. Air is a mixture of gases. Seawater is water with salt and other substances dissolved in it. Rasam is water, tamarind, tomato, salt and spices. In a mixture, each substance keeps its own properties. That is why a mixture can be separated again. You only need a method that treats each substance differently. A sieve separates by size. A magnet picks out iron. Paper and water separate dyes.
- e.g. Muddy water: let it stand, and the mud sinks to the bottom.
- e.g. Rice with small stones: people spread it on a plate and pick the stones out by hand.
- e.g. Salt water: let the water dry up, and the salt is left.
### Why black ink splits into colours · *defined*
**Definition:** Most black sketch pen ink is a mixture of several coloured dyes. Together they look black. Chromatography pulls them apart.

Mix enough colours together and your eye sees black or a very dark brown. Pen makers do this on purpose. They mix dyes such as blue, purple, red and yellow to make black. On a wet paper strip, the water climbs and dissolves the dyes in the dot. Each dye moves with the water at its own speed. So the dyes spread apart into separate bands. Different pen makers use different mixtures, so two black pens can give two different patterns.
- e.g. Colour one spot with every sketch pen in the box, one on top of another. You get a dark, muddy blot.
- e.g. Many green sketch pens split into a yellow band and a blue band.
- e.g. Some black inks are made of one black substance, such as the carbon in Indian ink. These do not split.
### Why some dyes travel further · *defined*
**Definition:** A dye that dissolves easily in water and holds on to the paper only weakly travels furthest. A dye that holds on to the paper more strongly stays nearer the dot.

Two things act on each dye at once. The moving water carries it up the strip. The paper fibres hold it back. Each dye is a different substance, so the balance is different for each one. Some dyes travel almost as far as the water. Others hardly leave the dot. That is what makes the pattern. With the same paper and the same liquid, the same dye always travels about the same share of the way.
- e.g. On kitchen towel, the blue in many black sketch pens travels further than the purple.
- e.g. If the water climbs 10 cm and a dye goes 5 cm, that dye went half the way. On a longer strip, it would still go about half the way.
### Some inks do not move in water · *defined*
**Definition:** Chromatography works only when the liquid can dissolve the substances. Ballpoint ink and permanent marker ink do not dissolve in water, so in water they stay on the dot.

Ballpoint ink is a thick paste made with oily liquids. Permanent markers use dyes that do not dissolve in water. That is why they do not wash off easily. Chemists choose a liquid that dissolves the mixture they want to test. For these inks they would use a liquid such as alcohol. A dot that does not move at all in water is still a clue. It tells you the ink is a different kind.
- e.g. A dot of ballpoint pen on a wet kitchen-towel strip stays a dot.
- e.g. A permanent marker dot also stays where it is, while a sketch pen dot next to it spreads.
### Chromatography in labs today · *defined*
**Definition:** Chemists use chromatography to separate and find the substances in almost any mixture, such as food, medicines, blood, water and air.

In a lab, the mixture is often pushed through a long, thin tube packed with powder. Each substance comes out of the far end at its own time. A machine notes when each one comes out and how much of it there is. Food labs check whether spices have added colours. Hospital labs test blood. Sports labs test for banned medicines. Forensic scientists compare the inks on documents. All of them use the same idea as your paper strip.
- e.g. Food safety labs in India test chilli and turmeric powder for colours that are not allowed.
- e.g. A hospital lab can test a small sample of blood for many medicines at once.
### Every element has its own flame colour · *defined*
**Definition:** When some elements are heated in a flame, they give out light of their own colour. Chemists use the colour to tell which element is there.

A gas stove flame is blue and almost see-through. Add a little salt, and it flashes yellow-orange. The yellow-orange comes from sodium, which is part of salt. Other elements give other colours. Copper gives green or blue. Strontium gives red. Lithium gives crimson. Potassium gives a pale lilac. Firework makers use these elements to colour their sparks. Sodium's colour is very strong. Even a tiny trace of sodium can hide the colours of the others.
- e.g. When rasam or dal boils over onto the stove, the blue flame flares yellow-orange for a moment.
- e.g. Old orange street lights glow with the light of hot sodium.
- e.g. Green fireworks usually have barium in them, and red ones strontium.
### Why heat makes coloured light · *defined*
**Definition:** The heat of a flame gives energy to the electrons in each atom. When the electrons give that energy out again, it comes out as light of exact colours.

Electrons are the tiny particles on the outside of every atom. Each kind of atom has its own set of energies that its electrons can have. So each element gives out its own exact set of colours, and no other element gives the same set. That is why a flame colour works like a fingerprint for an element. You do not need to see the atom. You only need to see its light.
- e.g. Sodium always gives the same yellow-orange, whether it comes from table salt, rasam or seawater.
- e.g. A prism splits sodium's light into two very thin yellow lines, close together.
### Reading what the Sun is made of · *defined*
**Definition:** Sunlight split into its colours has thin dark lines in it. Each element makes its own set of lines, so the lines show which elements are in the Sun.

Sodium gives out two thin yellow lines. Sunlight has two thin dark lines in exactly the same place. In 1859, Gustav Kirchhoff showed why. Sodium in the Sun's outer layers takes in that yellow light, so it goes missing from sunlight. Since then, astronomers have read the light of stars to find what they are made of. Most stars, like the Sun, are mostly hydrogen and helium. Helium was found in the Sun's light 27 years before anyone found it on Earth.
- e.g. Iron, calcium and sodium have all been found in the Sun from its light.
- e.g. The Sun is about 15 crore kilometres away, and nobody has ever taken a sample of it.
### Is anything perfectly pure? · *defined*
**Definition:** Chemists say almost nothing is perfectly pure. Nearly every substance has tiny traces of other substances in it.

Tap water has small amounts of dissolved salts. That is why a drop of it can make a few tiny orange flashes in a flame. Even the purest substances made in labs have a few atoms of something else. So chemists ask 'How pure is it?' rather than 'Is it pure?'. Better tests keep finding smaller and smaller traces.
- e.g. Bottled water lists the salts dissolved in it on the label.
- e.g. A medicine packet says how pure the medicine must be.

## Mostly agreed
- Most coloured inks and food colours are mixtures of dyes, and chromatography can separate them. *How we know:* Anyone can do it at home with paper and water. Labs separate the same dyes and name each one.
- Each element gives out its own exact set of colours of light when it is heated. *How we know:* Bunsen and Kirchhoff tested many elements in 1859 and 1860 and found each had its own pattern. Every lab since has found the same.
- Two substances that look the same can be told apart by tests. *How we know:* Salt and sugar both look like white grains. Salt turns a flame yellow-orange, and sugar melts and turns brown when heated.
- The Sun and stars are made of the same elements as the Earth. Most stars are mostly hydrogen and helium. *How we know:* The lines in starlight match the lines of elements tested in labs on Earth.

## How we found out
**Robert Bunsen, a German chemist, and Gustav Kirchhoff, a German physicist, proving Auguste Comte, a French philosopher, wrong, 1835 (Comte's claim), 1859 (sodium in the Sun) and 1860 (caesium).** In 1835, the French philosopher Auguste Comte wrote about the stars. We can measure their shapes, distances and movements, he said, but we will never know what they are made of. It seemed obvious. Nobody can travel to a star. In Heidelberg, in Germany, the chemist Robert Bunsen was studying the colours that substances give to a flame. His new gas burner made a hot flame that was almost colourless, so each colour showed clearly. But some colours looked alike. His friend Gustav Kirchhoff, a physicist, suggested splitting the light with a prism. In 1859 they found that each element gave its own set of thin coloured lines. Sodium always gave two yellow lines, close together. Sunlight split by a prism has thin dark lines in it, and two of them sat exactly where sodium's yellow lines were. Kirchhoff shone sunlight through a flame with sodium in it, and those dark lines grew darker. Sodium takes in the same colour it gives out. So there is sodium in the Sun. In 1860, the two men saw new blue lines in spring water. They had found a new element, and named it caesium, from a Latin word for sky blue. Comte had died two years before he was shown to be wrong.  
*What people believed before:* We can never know what the stars are made of, because we cannot go there. Bunsen and Kirchhoff showed that each element's light has its own pattern, and found sodium's pattern in sunlight.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Black is one colour, so black ink is one substance.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Black ink looks the same all over the page.  
  Against: On wet paper, most black sketch pen inks split into several colours. Some black inks really are one substance, such as the carbon in Indian ink, and these do not split.
- **We will never know what the stars are made of, because nobody can go there.** (Auguste Comte, a French philosopher, in 1835; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody can travel to a star or bring back a piece of one.  
  Against: In 1859, Kirchhoff and Bunsen showed that light carries a pattern for each element that gave it out. Starlight shows what stars are made of.
- **A flame's colour shows only how hot it is.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: Heat does change a flame's colour. A gas flame with plenty of air burns blue and very hot, and a candle flame is yellow and cooler.  
  Against: What is in the flame matters too. The same blue gas flame turns yellow-orange with salt and green with copper.
- **Paper chromatography tells you the name of each dye.** (A common hope; *rejected*)  
  For: Each dye makes its own band, at its own height.  
  Against: A strip shows how many dyes there are and how far each one goes. To name a dye, chemists run a known dye beside it on the same paper, or use more tests.

## Roots
We have pulled ink apart with water and seen salt colour a flame. Now we ask what these tests tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Two black pens looked exactly alike. On wet paper, each black ink spread into its own set of colours. Each black was a mix of dyes, and water carried each dye its own distance. So things that look the same can be made of different things, and a test can show which.  
*Habit it breaks:* We decide what a thing is by how it looks: black ink is black ink, and a white powder is a white powder. Looks only show the surface. What a thing is made of shows up in how it behaves when we test it.  
*Evidence:* In the Which Pen test, black pens that looked the same on paper gave different bands on the wet strips, and the secret letter matched only one of them. In the flame test, salt water and sugar water looked the same, but only the salt water turned the flame yellow-orange.

- *who am i:* Your body is a mixture of thousands of substances. Doctors find out which ones, and how much, by testing a little of your blood.
- *what is life:* Plants make many coloured substances of their own: green ones to catch sunlight, and yellow, orange and red ones too. Chromatography was first used to separate them.
- *what is this world:* The Sun, the stars and the Earth are made of the same elements. We know because their light shows the same colours as the elements in our labs.

Black ink looks like one thing, yet water shows it is several dyes. Each dye is made of atoms of a few elements. Every element is built from the same few kinds of tiny particle. Going inwards, many things turn out to be made of fewer and fewer kinds. Going outwards, the same 118 elements make a pen, a flame, your body and the Sun.

- **Nyaya Sutra 1.1.3 and 1.1.6, by Gautama (also called Akshapada), an Indian book on how we know:** There are four ways of knowing: seeing for yourself, working it out from signs, comparing with something already known, and the word of a trustworthy person. Comparing means knowing a new thing by its likeness to a thing you know well. (In the Which Pen test you used two of these. You worked out from signs, the coloured bands, which pen was used. You compared the secret strip with strips whose pens you already knew.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 123 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 500 BCE:** Nature loves to hide. (The blue and purple in black ink, and the sodium in clear salt water, could not be seen. A test made them show themselves.)
- Science (settled): Every element gives out its own exact set of colours of light. The same elements are found on Earth, in the Sun and in far-away stars.
- Science (open): Nobody yet has a complete list of every substance in a single living cell. Some are there in amounts too small for today's tests.
- Other view, Many chemists: Tests tell us what a thing is made of, and that is all they tell us. Knowing the dyes in an ink is useful, but it carries no lesson about how to see the world. Reading more into it is our choice, not the ink's.
- Practice, Test with your senses (3 min): Here are two cups of water. One has a pinch of salt in it. Let's look at them for three slow breaths. Can your eyes tell them apart? Now smell each one, with three slow breaths. Can your nose tell? Now dip a clean finger in each and touch it to your tongue. Which is which? Your eyes and nose missed it. Your tongue was the test that found it.
- Ask: Black ink was blue and pink together. Is the black still real, or only the blue and pink?
- Ask: If two things pass every test you can think of, are they the same thing?
- Ask: We know the Sun has sodium in it without going there. What else might we know without going there?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A black dot spread into blue and purple. Were the blue and purple there before, or did the water make them? (hint: Put a blue dot and a purple dot on top of each other, and run that strip. What do you expect, and what happens?)
- *deep:* Why does each dye stop at its own height on the strip? (hint: Think of the two things acting on each dye: the water carrying it up, and the paper holding it back.)
- *deep:* How can the light of a flame tell you what is in it, when you cannot see a single atom? (hint: Sodium always gives the same yellow-orange. What would you need to know about other elements to use this?)
- *big:* If two things look exactly alike, how could you ever be sure they are the same? (hint: Think of two black pens, two white powders and two people who look alike. What tests could tell each pair apart?)
- *big:* Your body is a mixture of thousands of substances. If a test found every one of them, would it have found you? (hint: Is a person the substances they are made of, or something more? There is no agreed answer.)
- *big:* We know there is sodium in the Sun without ever going there. What else could light be telling us that we have not noticed yet? (hint: Telescopes now read the air of planets around other stars from their light.)

## Story: The Extra Line on Amma's List
*Kitchen-towel chromatography of sketch pens really works, and permanent marker ink really does not move in water. The family and the list are made up.*

Every Saturday morning, Amma wrote the shopping list on the back of an old calendar page. She stuck it on the fridge with a magnet shaped like a mango. Anyone in the flat could add to it. That was the rule.

This Saturday, the list said: rice, toor dal, tomatoes, curry leaves, one coconut, soap. Then, at the very bottom, in thick black letters, someone had added: TWO BIG PACKETS OF MURUKKU.

"Who wrote this?" asked Amma. She was smiling, but only a little.

Arun, who was six, shook his head so hard that his ears went red. Revathi, who was nine, said, "Not me." Appa was reading the newspaper. He held up both hands. Ammamma, their grandmother, was out on the balcony watering her tulsi plant. She did not turn round.

Revathi looked closely at the letters. They were thick and black, like a sketch pen. "There are three black pens in this flat that could write like that," she said. "My sketch pen is in my pencil box. Arun's sketch pen is in his school bag. And there's Appa's fat black marker in the pen stand, the one he uses for parcels."

"They all look the same," said Arun. "Black is black."

"Maybe not," said Revathi. "Our science teacher showed us something. Black ink is often many colours mixed together. Amma, can I have a steel tumbler of water and the kitchen towel?"

She cut three long strips of kitchen towel. Near the bottom of each one, she drew a pencil line. On each line she made a fat black dot, one dot from each pen. At the top she wrote R, A and P in pencil, for Revathi, Arun and Appa. Amma, who was the judge, cut a thin strip off the bottom of the list. It went right through the tail of the letter S in PACKETS.

Revathi laid a pencil across the top of the tumbler. She hung all four strips over it, so that their ends just touched the water. The dots stayed above the water. Arun lay on the floor with his chin in his hands and watched.

The water crept up the towel. When it reached the dots, the black began to run. On strip R, the black spread into a blue band, with a purple band above it. On strip A, it became blue at the bottom and pink higher up. On strip P, the black did not move at all. It stayed a dark, sharp dot.

"Appa's marker is a different kind of ink," said Revathi. "Water can't dissolve it, so water can't carry it."

The strip from the list was slower, because calendar paper is thicker than kitchen towel. They waited. Appa put down his newspaper and came to watch too. At last a blue smudge crept out of the letter. Above it, faint but clear, came a pink one.

"Blue and pink," said Revathi. "The same as strip A." Everyone looked at Arun.

Arun went red again. "But I didn't! I can't even write murukku!" That was true. He could write his own name, and CAT, and that was all.

Revathi frowned at the strips. Then she said slowly, "The strip tells us which pen. It doesn't tell us whose hand. Arun, who else used your sketch pen this week?"

Arun thought hard. "Ammamma borrowed it. On Thursday. To write the label on her mango pickle jar."

Everyone turned to the balcony. Ammamma put down her watering can. She came inside slowly, with a very straight face. "A list is for the things a house needs," she said. "This house needs murukku."

Amma laughed so much she had to sit down. Appa said Ammamma was clearly a very clever criminal. Ammamma said she was seventy-two, far too old to be a criminal, and she would like her murukku now, please.

Later, Revathi stuck the four dried strips into her notebook in a row. Under them she wrote: All three pens looked black. Inside, they were different. The strip tells you which pen, not who held it. So you have to ask more questions.

That evening, Arun took his sketch pen and made a careful dot on a strip of towel, all by himself. He watched the blue and the pink creep apart. "So the blue and the pink were in there all the time," he said. "Yes," said Revathi. "Black is how they look when they are together."

**For the parent:** For the parent: two black inks can look the same on paper but be made of different dyes. Water carries each dye its own distance, so each ink leaves its own pattern, its chromatogram. The strip from the list matched pen A, so pen A wrote it. Appa's marker did not move, because permanent marker ink does not dissolve in water. Revathi also sees the limit of her evidence. The test answers 'which pen?', not 'who?'. Real forensic scientists say the same: a test answers one question, and the rest needs more questions.

- Ask: Why did Revathi make a separate strip for each pen?
- Ask: Why did the black from Appa's parcel marker stay as a dot?
- Ask: The list matched Arun's pen. Why did Revathi not decide that Arun wrote it?
- Ask: What could Revathi do if two pens gave exactly the same pattern?

## Experiments
### e1 · Which Pen Wrote the Note? (40 min)
- **question:** Three dark pens look almost the same. Can water and paper show which one wrote a secret letter?
- **predict:** Look at the three pens' marks on paper. Do you think their inks are the same? What do you think each ink will do when the water reaches it? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same kind of strip for every pen. Draw every dot on a pencil line 2 cm from the bottom, the same size, gone over 3 times. Dip every strip 1 cm into the same water, for the same time. Only the pen changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps with the scissors. Wipe up spilled water so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Stick the dried strips into your notebook in a row, and write under them which pen wrote the secret letter and how you know.
- **results table:** Strip | My guess: what will the ink do? | Colours I saw, from bottom to top | Height of the highest colour (cm) | Does it match the secret letter?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the three pens on the table. Call them A, B and C. 2. Scribble a little with each on scrap paper. 3. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* These three inks all look dark. Do you think they are the same ink? What will each one do when water reaches it? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the three scribbles. Are the inks the same? Guess what water will do. Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen to the black dot? (good: It will stay black.; It might spread out.; It might turn into other colours, like the intro strip.)
  - Note: Write down your child's guess and their reason in the table before testing. Any guess is fine. The test decides.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The child turns away. 2. The grown-up picks one pen secretly. 3. On a fourth strip, they draw a pencil line 2 cm from the bottom. 4. They write one thick letter, such as P, sitting on the line. 5. They mark a star at the top and hide the pen.  
  *Parent says:* No peeking. I'm writing a secret letter with one of the three pens. Your job is to find out which.  
  *Child mode:* Turn away. A grown-up is writing a secret letter. Which pen? You will find out!
  - Note: Go over the letter twice, so there is plenty of ink. Keep it small, about 1 cm tall, and on the line. Put the pens back in a row so the child can't tell which one you used.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a pencil line 2 cm from the bottom of three strips. 2. Write A, B and C at the top in pencil. 3. On each line, make a dot as wide as a pencil with its pen. 4. Let it dry for a few seconds, then go over it twice more.  
  *Parent says:* We use pencil for the line and the labels, because pencil does not dissolve in water. A small, strong dot gives the clearest bands.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a pencil line on each strip. Write A, B, C on top. Make a small, dark dot on each line.
  - Note: A dot about 4 to 5 mm across works best. A big blot smears the bands together.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour water 1 cm deep into each tumbler. 2. Clip two strips to a pencil, and lay it across one tumbler. 3. Do the same with the other two strips. 4. Check that each strip's end is in the water and each dot is above it.  
  *Parent says:* The dot must stay above the water. If it goes under, the ink just washes into the tumbler. Why do you think the strips must not touch?  
  *Child mode:* Clip the strips to a pencil. Lay it on the tumbler. The dot stays above the water!
  - Note: Strips must not touch each other or the sides of the tumbler, or the colours will run across. If the water is too deep, pour a little out.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Watch the water climb. 2. When the water reaches about 1 cm from the top, lift the strips out. 3. Mark with pencil how high the water reached on each strip.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the dots. What is happening to the ink? Which strip is changing first?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the water climb. What happens when it reaches the dots?
  - Ask: What do you see on each strip? (good: The black is spreading into blue and purple.; One strip has pink in it and the others don't.; One dot is not moving at all.)
  - Note: On kitchen towel, the water climbs about 8 to 10 cm in 5 to 10 minutes. Blotting paper is slower. A permanent marker dot will not move, which is a useful result. If every black dot stays black, try a different black sketch pen, or a dark brown or purple one.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the strips flat on a plate to dry. 2. For each strip, write the colours from bottom to top in the table. 3. Measure from the pencil line to the top of the highest colour.  
  *Parent says:* Let's record what each ink became. Which colours, in which order, and how high?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the strips on a plate. Write each strip's colours, bottom to top. Measure the top colour.
  - Note: Colours fade a little as they dry, so write them down while the strips are still damp.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the star strip next to strips A, B and C. 2. Find the one with the same colours in the same order. 3. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which strip matches the secret letter? What is your evidence? Tell me what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Put the star strip next to A, B and C. Which one matches? Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: pen B wrote it. What I saw: the star strip and strip B both had blue at the bottom and pink above it. Why: each ink is its own mix of dyes, so it makes its own pattern.
  - Check: The secret letter's strip shows a blue band low down and a pink band higher up. Strip A shows blue and purple. Strip B shows blue low down and pink higher up. Strip C did not move. Which pen wrote the letter? → **Pen B**
- **s8 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The grown-up shows which pen they used.  
  *Parent says:* Let's check. Were you right?  
  *Child mode:* Now the grown-up shows the pen. Were you right?
  - Think: If two of the pens had given exactly the same pattern, how could you tell them apart?

### e3 · The Flame Test: which cup has salt in it? (25 min)
- **question:** Three cups of water look the same. Can a flame show which one has salt dissolved in it?
- **predict:** One cup is plain water, one is sugar water and one is salt water. They all look clear. What do you think the flame will do with each one? Why do you think so?

Tie back long hair, and keep dupattas and loose sleeves away from the stove. The spoon gets hot: cool it in the sink. Homes with induction cooktops have no flame. Instead, watch the coloured flames video in this unit.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the flame for each cup, with the colour you saw.
- **results table:** Cup | My guess: what is in it? | Colour of the flame | How long the colour lasted | What I now think is in it
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The child turns away. 2. The grown-up stirs half a teaspoon of salt into one cup of water, and half a teaspoon of sugar into another. 3. The third cup is plain water. 4. Write 1, 2 and 3 on paper under the cups, and note secretly which is which.  
  *Parent says:* These three cups look the same. One has salt in it, one has sugar, and one is plain water. Can a flame tell them apart? What do you think, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Three cups look the same. One has salt. Guess: can a flame find it? Why?
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The child stands at least one metre from the stove. 2. The grown-up lights one burner on low. 3. Look at the flame's colour before testing anything.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the flame first. What colour is it? We need to know that before we test anything.  
  *Child mode:* Stand back one big step. Look at the flame. What colour is it?
  - Note: A gas flame is mostly blue. Remember this colour, because it is the 'before' for every test.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dip the bowl of spoon 1 into cup 1. 2. Hold the wet spoon in the edge of the flame for 5 seconds. 3. Watch the colour. 4. Cool the spoon in the sink. 5. Do the same for cups 2 and 3, with a clean spoon each time.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the edge of the flame around the spoon. What colour do you see? Does it last?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the flame by the spoon. What colour? Does it last?
  - Note: The salt water gives bright yellow-orange flashes that keep coming as the water boils off. Plain tap water may give a few tiny orange sparks, because tap water has a little salt in it too. Sugar water gives little or no colour. Hold the spoon by its end with a cloth. Never hold your face over the flame.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill in the table. 2. Ask your child to say which cup has salt, and why. 3. Uncover the secret labels.  
  *Parent says:* Which cup has salt in it? What did you see that tells you? Now let's check the labels.  
  *Child mode:* Which cup had salt? What is your evidence? Check the labels.
  - Check: Cup 2 made the flame flash bright yellow-orange. Cups 1 and 3 hardly changed the flame. What is in cup 2? → **Salt water**
- **s5 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Turn off the stove. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Salt water and sugar water look exactly the same. So how did the flame tell them apart?  
  *Child mode:* The cups looked the same. How did the flame tell them apart?
  - Think: When rasam boils over onto the stove, the flame turns yellow-orange. What is in rasam that does that?

## Worksheets
### Ink Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (sort) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3 in Kochi, looks at things in her kitchen. Sort each one: is it a mixture or a pure substance?  
   **Answer:** salt water → Mixture; rasam → Mixture; a spoon of sugar → Pure substance; air → Mixture; black sketch pen ink → Mixture; a spoon of salt → Pure substance; muddy water → Mixture; distilled water, the kind sold for batteries → Pure substance. *Why:* A mixture has two or more substances mixed together. Rasam, air, ink, muddy water and salt water are mixtures. Sugar, salt and distilled water are each one substance, almost pure. A strong answer can say what is mixed in each mixture.
2. (truefalse) A pure substance, such as sugar, can be separated into different colours with paper and water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Paper and water separate a mixture into the substances in it. A pure substance has only one substance to start with, so there is nothing to separate.
3. (fill) Separating a mixture of dyes by letting a liquid climb paper is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['chromatography']. *Why:* The word comes from Greek words meaning 'colour writing', because the first separations were of coloured substances.
4. (mcq) When you dip the end of a kitchen-towel strip in water, the water climbs up it. What makes it climb?  
   Options: The heat of the room / Capillary action: water creeping through the tiny gaps between the paper's fibres / The ink pulls the water up  
   **Answer:** Capillary action: water creeping through the tiny gaps between the paper's fibres. *Why:* Paper is made of tiny fibres with tiny gaps between them. Water creeps up through the gaps. This is capillary action. A plain strip with no ink climbs just as well, so the ink is not pulling it.
5. (order) Put the steps of the Which Pen test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Draw a pencil line 2 cm from the bottom of each strip', 'Make a dot of ink on each pencil line', 'Hang the strips so their ends dip in the water', 'Wait until the water is near the top, then take the strips out', "Compare the dried strips with the secret letter's strip"]. *Why:* First mark where the dot goes, then put the ink on, then let the water carry the dyes, and only then compare the patterns.
6. (label) Here is a drawing of one strip hanging in a tumbler during the Which Pen test. Label the parts.  
   **Answer:** ['The clip is at the top, holding the strip to the pencil.', 'The paper strip hangs down into the tumbler.', 'The pencil line is 2 cm from the bottom of the strip.', 'The ink dot sits on the pencil line.', 'The water is at the bottom, below the dot.']. *Why:* A strong answer puts the ink dot above the water line. If the dot is under the water, the ink washes into the tumbler.
7. (draw) Most green sketch pens are a mixture of two dyes. Draw what a green dot might look like after the water has climbed the strip. Colour and label each band.  
   **Answer:** A strip with a pencil line at the bottom, a band of one colour, such as blue, and a band of another colour, such as yellow, above or below it.. *Why:* Many green sketch pens split into a yellow dye and a blue dye. Which one goes higher depends on the pen. A strong drawing shows two separate bands, both above the pencil line.
8. (write) Your black sketch pen dot turned into blue and purple bands. Explain why, in two or three sentences.  
   **Answer:** The black ink was a mixture of a blue dye and a purple dye. The water dissolved them and carried them up the paper. Each dye travelled a different distance, so they came apart.. *Why:* A strong answer says the colours were in the ink all along, and that each dye travels its own distance.
9. (mcq) Arjun puts four dots on one strip: a black sketch pen, a green sketch pen, a ballpoint pen and a brown sketch pen. Which dot will most likely stay where it is?  
   Options: The black sketch pen / The green sketch pen / The ballpoint pen / The brown sketch pen  
   **Answer:** The ballpoint pen. *Why:* Ballpoint ink does not dissolve in water, so the water cannot carry it. Sketch pen inks dissolve in water and move up the strip.
10. (truefalse) If two black inks look exactly the same, they must be the same mixture of dyes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Different pen makers mix different dyes to make black. Two inks can look the same and still split into different colours on a wet strip.
11. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Mixture → two or more substances mixed but not joined; Dye → a coloured substance that dissolves in a liquid; Dissolve → to break into pieces too small to see and spread through a liquid; Chromatogram → the pattern of bands left on the paper; Evidence → what you saw or measured that shows whether an idea is true. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit. A strong answer could also give an example of each.
12. (perspective) Two friends in Mysuru look at a black sketch pen dot that has split into blue and purple.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both have part of it. The ink is a mixture of blue and purple dyes, as the strip shows. Lakshmi is right that the mixture looks black. Farhan is right that blue and purple are in it. The black is not a trick: it is how the dyes look together.. *Why:* This question asks the child to use evidence and to see that 'looks black' and 'is made of blue and purple dyes' can both be true.
13. (mcq) Rasam boils over onto the gas flame, and the blue flame turns yellow-orange for a moment. What in the rasam makes that colour?

14. (write) On your strip, one pen's dot did not move at all, even when the water went past it. What can you say about that ink?  
   **Answer:** The ink does not dissolve in water, so the water cannot carry it up the strip. It might be a ballpoint or a permanent marker. To separate it, you would need a different liquid.. *Why:* A strong answer explains the result, instead of saying the test failed. A dot that does not move is a clue too.

1. (mcq) In the Which Pen test, the water climbed 10 cm and a blue dye went 5 cm, half the way. Divya runs the same pen on a strip where the water climbs 20 cm. About how far will the blue dye go?  
   Options: 5 cm / 10 cm / 20 cm  
   **Answer:** 10 cm. *Why:* With the same paper and water, a dye travels about the same share of the way. Half of 20 cm is 10 cm. Picking 5 cm forgets that the water went further. Picking 20 cm means thinking the dye keeps up with the water.
2. (write) In the Which Pen test, why must every dot be the same size and on the same pencil line?  
   **Answer:** So the test is fair. If one dot is bigger or starts higher, its bands could look different because of that, not because of the ink. Then we could not tell which pen matched.. *Why:* A strong answer says that only the pen should change, so any difference comes from the ink.
3. (perspective) In the story, the secret note's strip matched Arun's pen. Two cousins argue about what this proves.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the strip shows which ink wrote the note: Arun's pen. It does not show who held the pen. Divya is right that the evidence points at Arun's pen. Arjun is right that a pen can be used by anyone, so more questions are needed. But the test does tell us something, so it does not prove nothing.. *Why:* Real forensic scientists are careful in the same way: evidence answers one question, not every question.
4. (truefalse) Sodium's yellow-orange colour is so strong that it can hide the colours of other elements in a flame.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Even a tiny trace of sodium gives a bright yellow-orange. Chemists who test for other elements have to keep everything very clean, or the sodium colour hides the rest.
5. (match) Match each element to the colour it gives a flame.  
   **Answer:** Sodium → yellow-orange; Copper → green or blue; Strontium → red; Potassium → pale lilac; Lithium → crimson, a deep red. *Why:* Each element gives its own colours. Firework makers use strontium for red and copper for blue, for example.
6. (order) Put these events in the order they happened.  
   **Answer:** ['Auguste Comte writes that we will never know what stars are made of', "Bunsen's new burner gives an almost colourless flame", 'Bunsen and Kirchhoff find that each element gives its own set of coloured lines', 'Bunsen and Kirchhoff find a new sky-blue element, caesium']. *Why:* Comte wrote in 1835. Bunsen's burner came in 1855. The coloured lines were found in 1859, and caesium in 1860. A strong answer sees that the burner had to come first, so the colours could be seen clearly.
7. (draw) Draw the flame in the flame test for each of the three cups: plain water, sugar water and salt water. Colour each flame and label it.  
   **Answer:** Three flames: a blue flame for plain water, perhaps with a tiny orange spark, a blue flame for sugar water, and a bright yellow-orange flame for salt water.. *Why:* Only the salt water has much sodium, so only it turns the flame bright yellow-orange. A strong drawing labels which flame is which.
8. (mcq) In the flame test, the grown-up uses a clean spoon for each cup. Why?  
   Options: A clean spoon gets hotter / So no salt from one cup is carried into the next test / So the spoons don't get scratched  
   **Answer:** So no salt from one cup is carried into the next test. *Why:* Even a trace of salt left on a spoon can make a flame flash yellow-orange. A clean spoon for each cup makes the test fair.
9. (fill) An element is a substance made of only one kind of ____.  
   **Answer:** ['atom']. *Why:* Sodium is made only of sodium atoms. Salt is not an element, because it is made of sodium atoms and chlorine atoms joined together.
10. (write) Salt water and sugar water look exactly the same. Explain how a flame can tell them apart.  
   **Answer:** Salt has sodium in it. When salt water is heated in a flame, the sodium gives out yellow-orange light. Sugar has no sodium, so sugar water does not make that colour.. *Why:* A strong answer names sodium and says that each element gives its own colour.
11. (sort) Sort these inks and colours: will a dot of each one move up a wet paper strip?  
   **Answer:** a sketch pen → Moves in water; a ballpoint pen → Stays on the dot; a permanent marker → Stays on the dot; a highlighter → Moves in water; a drop of food colour → Moves in water; Indian ink, made of carbon → Stays on the dot. *Why:* Inks made with dyes that dissolve in water move up the strip. Ballpoint ink, permanent marker ink and the carbon in Indian ink do not dissolve in water, so they stay put.
12. (mcq) In the flame test, even the plain tap water gave two or three tiny orange sparks. Why?  
   Options: The flame was too hot / Tap water has a little salt dissolved in it / The spoon was made of gold  
   **Answer:** Tap water has a little salt dissolved in it. *Why:* Tap water has small amounts of dissolved salts, including a little sodium. So it gives a few sparks, but far fewer than salt water. This is why chemists ask 'how pure?'.
13. (label) Here is a drawing of a dried strip from the Which Pen test. Label the parts.

14. (odd) Three of these are elements. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Sodium / Copper / Salt / Oxygen  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Salt is not an element. It is sodium and chlorine joined together. Sodium, copper and oxygen are each made of one kind of atom.
15. (write) Your friend says, 'My strip tells me the names of the dyes in my pen.' What does the strip really show? What would a chemist do to name a dye?  
   **Answer:** The strip shows how many dyes there are, their colours, and how far each one went. It does not give their names. A chemist would run a dye whose name is known beside it on the same paper, and see if they match, or do more tests.. *Why:* A strong answer separates what the evidence shows from what it does not, and suggests comparing with a known dye.
### Thinking Like a Chemist (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Lakshmi has a bottle of green food colour. Design a test to find out whether it is one dye or a mixture. Say what you would use, what you would do, and what each result would mean.  
   **Answer:** Use a strip of kitchen towel, a pencil line 2 cm from the bottom, one small drop of food colour on the line, and a tumbler with 1 cm of water. Hang the strip so the end dips in. If the green stays as one band, it is probably one dye. If it splits into yellow and blue bands, it is a mixture.. *Why:* A strong answer names the materials, the steps and what each result would show. An even stronger one runs a yellow and a blue food colour beside it to compare.
2. (perspective) Two real people disagreed about the stars. Auguste Comte wrote in 1835. Gustav Kirchhoff did his tests in 1859.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that Comte's view was sensible with what people knew in 1835: nobody could go to a star or test a piece of one. What changed was new evidence. Kirchhoff and Bunsen found that each element's light has its own pattern, and matched sodium's pattern in sunlight. So you do not have to go somewhere to test what it is made of.. *Why:* This question shows that a wise person can be wrong when new evidence has not been found yet.
3. (mcq) Two black pens give exactly the same bands on kitchen towel in water. What should a chemist do next?  
   Options: Decide they are the same ink / Try another test, such as a different paper or a different liquid / Give up  
   **Answer:** Try another test, such as a different paper or a different liquid. *Why:* One matching test is good evidence, but two different inks could still behave alike on one paper. A different paper or liquid may separate them. Chemists use more than one test before they are sure.
4. (truefalse) A test that shows which pen wrote a note also shows who wrote it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The strip shows which ink was used. Anyone could have held the pen. In the story, the strip matched Arun's pen, but Ammamma had borrowed it.
5. (draw) Predict: you put a yellow dot and a blue dot on top of each other on one strip, and run it. Draw what you expect. Then draw what a green sketch pen strip looked like, if you tested one. Are they alike?  
   **Answer:** Two strips. The yellow and blue mix splits into a yellow band and a blue band. The green strip may show the same two colours.. *Why:* If a green ink gives the same bands as yellow plus blue, that is strong evidence that the green is a mixture of a yellow dye and a blue dye.
6. (mcq) In 1868, astronomers saw a yellow line in the Sun's light that no known element gave. Why did they decide it was a new element?  
   Options: The line was very bright / Every known element had been tested, and none gave a line in that exact place / The Sun is very hot  
   **Answer:** Every known element had been tested, and none gave a line in that exact place. *Why:* Each element gives lines in its own exact places. A line that matched no known element had to come from an unknown one. It was named helium, and found on Earth in 1895.
7. (fill) The Greek words inside 'chromatography' mean ____ writing.  
   **Answer:** ['colour']. *Why:* 'Chroma' means colour and 'graphein' means to write. Mikhail Tsvet chose the name because his first separations were of coloured substances in leaves.
8. (sort) Sort these tests. Does each one separate a mixture, or show which element is in something?  
   **Answer:** a paper strip in water → Separates a mixture; a flame test → Shows which element is there; a sieve → Separates a mixture; splitting starlight with a prism → Shows which element is there; a magnet over iron filings and sand → Separates a mixture; a chalk column → Separates a mixture. *Why:* Paper strips, chalk columns, sieves and magnets pull the parts of a mixture apart. Flame colours and split starlight show which elements are present, from their light.
9. (order) Put the steps of a fair flame test in order.  
   **Answer:** ["Look at the flame's colour before testing anything", 'Dip a clean spoon in the first cup', 'Hold the wet spoon in the edge of the flame for 5 seconds', 'Write down the colour', 'Cool the spoon and use a clean one for the next cup']. *Why:* You need the flame's normal colour first, to compare with. A clean spoon each time stops one cup's salt spoiling the next test.
10. (write) Mikhail Tsvet poured leaf colour through a glass tube packed with chalk powder. Explain how his chalk tube and your paper strip work in the same way.  
   **Answer:** In both, a liquid moves through something with tiny gaps, chalk powder or paper fibres. The liquid carries the substances in the mixture, and the chalk or paper holds them back. Each substance moves at its own speed, so they separate into bands.. *Why:* A strong answer names both parts: the moving liquid and the material that holds the substances back.
11. (mcq) Robert Bunsen made a gas burner whose flame is almost colourless. Why did that help him study flame colours?  
   Options: It was cheaper / The colours of the substances showed clearly, without the flame's own colour in the way / It made more smoke  
   **Answer:** The colours of the substances showed clearly, without the flame's own colour in the way. *Why:* A candle flame is already bright yellow, so a yellow from sodium would be hard to see. In an almost colourless flame, each substance's colour stands out.
12. (label) Here is a drawing of the flame test. Label the parts.  
   **Answer:** ['The blue gas flame is on the burner.', 'The wet steel spoon is held in the edge of the flame.', 'The yellow-orange flash is around the spoon.', "The cloth is in the grown-up's hand, around the spoon's end.", 'The child stands one metre away.']. *Why:* A strong answer shows the safety parts too: the cloth and the child standing back.
13. (match) Match each job to the chemist's test it uses.  
   **Answer:** A food lab checks chilli powder for colours that are not allowed → chromatography; A firework maker wants a red firework → adding an element that gives a red flame; An astronomer finds out what a star is made of → reading the lines in its light; A forensic scientist checks whether a word was added to a cheque → chromatography of the ink; A chemist checks a white powder for sodium → a flame test. *Why:* Chromatography separates mixtures, and light shows which elements are present. A strong answer could say why each test fits its job.
14. (write) A spoon of plain tap water gave tiny orange sparks in the flame. Use this to explain why chemists say almost nothing is perfectly pure.  
   **Answer:** Tap water looks pure, but the orange sparks show it has a little sodium in it, from dissolved salts. Most things have small traces of other substances in them. Better tests find smaller and smaller traces, so chemists ask how pure something is, not whether it is pure.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the sparks as evidence and links them to traces of other substances.
15. (write) Black ink looked like one thing, but water showed it was several dyes. Can you think of something else that looks like one thing but is really a mixture? How could you test it?  
   **Answer:** For example, air looks like nothing, but it is a mixture of gases. Seawater looks like water, but it has salt in it: let some dry in a plate in the sun and salt is left. Rasam looks like one liquid, but it is many things.. *Why:* A strong answer gives an example and a real way to test it, such as drying, sieving or a flame test.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Salt water is made of salt and water mixed together. What kind of material is it?  
   Options: A pure substance / A mixture / An element  
   **Answer:** A mixture. *Why:* Salt water is two substances mixed but not joined, so it is a mixture. When the water dries up, the salt is left behind. Picking 'element' means mixing up an element, one kind of atom, with any material.
2. (mcq) A black sketch pen dot on a wet paper strip spreads into blue and purple bands. Where did the blue and purple come from?  
   Options: They were in the black ink all along / The water made them / The paper had them in it  
   **Answer:** They were in the black ink all along. *Why:* The black ink is a mixture of blue and purple dyes. The water only carried them different distances, so they came apart. A plain wet strip with no dot stays white, so the paper and water did not make them.
3. (mcq) Why must the ink dot stay above the water when you start?  
   Options: So the dot dries faster / So the ink does not just wash off into the tumbler / So the paper does not tear  
   **Answer:** So the ink does not just wash off into the tumbler. *Why:* If the dot is under the water, the dyes dissolve into the water in the tumbler instead of climbing the strip. Above the water, the climbing water carries them up the paper.
4. (mcq) On a strip, the blue dye went 8 cm and the purple dye went 4 cm. Why did the blue go further?  
   Options: Blue is a lighter colour / The blue dye holds on to the paper less strongly than the purple / There was more blue in the dot  
   **Answer:** The blue dye holds on to the paper less strongly than the purple. *Why:* The water carries each dye up, and the paper holds each dye back. A dye that holds the paper less strongly travels further. Picking 'more blue' is a natural guess, but a bigger amount gives a darker band, not a longer trip.
5. (mcq) Why do we draw the start line on the strip in pencil, not in pen?  
   Options: Pencil is easier to rub out / Pencil does not dissolve in water, so it will not run / Pencil is darker  
   **Answer:** Pencil does not dissolve in water, so it will not run. *Why:* Pencil marks are made of graphite, which does not dissolve in water. A pen line could dissolve and run up the strip, mixing with the colours you are testing.
6. (mcq) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, tests three pens. She uses kitchen towel for pen A, blotting paper for pen B and newspaper for pen C. Why is this not a fair test?  
   Options: She changed the paper as well as the pen / She used too many pens / She should have used ink, not pens  
   **Answer:** She changed the paper as well as the pen. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. Kavya changed the pen and the paper, so she cannot tell whether a difference came from the ink or from the paper.
7. (mcq) The secret note's strip has the same colours, in the same order, as pen B's strip. What does that show?  
   Options: Pen B's ink is the same mixture as the note's ink / Pen B's owner wrote the note / All the pens are the same  
   **Answer:** Pen B's ink is the same mixture as the note's ink. *Why:* The strip shows which ink, so it points to pen B. It does not show whose hand held pen B. That needs more questions, as Revathi found in the story.
8. (mcq) A grown-up holds a spoon of salt water in a gas flame. What colour does the flame turn?  
   Options: Green / Yellow-orange / Red  
   **Answer:** Yellow-orange. *Why:* Salt has sodium in it, and sodium makes a flame yellow-orange. Green would mean copper or barium, and red would mean strontium or lithium.
9. (mcq) Firework makers want a red firework. Which element should they add?  
   Options: Sodium / Strontium / Copper  
   **Answer:** Strontium. *Why:* Strontium gives a bright red. Sodium gives yellow-orange and copper gives green or blue. Each element gives its own colours, and that is how fireworks get theirs.
10. (mcq) In 1835, Auguste Comte said we would never know what stars are made of. How were Bunsen and Kirchhoff able to show he was wrong?  
   Options: They flew to the Sun / They found that each element's light has its own pattern, and matched sodium's pattern in sunlight / They dug up a fallen star  
   **Answer:** They found that each element's light has its own pattern, and matched sodium's pattern in sunlight. *Why:* Kirchhoff and Bunsen found that each element gives out its own set of colours. Sodium's yellow matched two dark lines in sunlight, which showed sodium is in the Sun. Nobody had to go there.
11. (mcq) Bunsen and Kirchhoff found a new element in spring water because of a colour no known element gave. What colour was it?  
   Options: Sky blue, so they named it caesium / Bright green, so they named it chlorine / Yellow, so they named it sodium  
   **Answer:** Sky blue, so they named it caesium. *Why:* In 1860 they saw blue lines that no known element made. They named the new element caesium, from a Latin word for sky blue. A year later, red lines led them to rubidium.
12. (mcq) A food safety lab wants to check whether some chilli powder has a colour added that is not allowed. Which method would help most?  
   Options: Weigh the powder / Separate the colours in it, like chromatography / Smell it  
   **Answer:** Separate the colours in it, like chromatography. *Why:* Chromatography separates the coloured substances in the powder, so the lab can see whether there is a dye that should not be there. Weighing or smelling cannot show which colours are mixed in.

## Applications
- Food safety labs use chromatography to check spices, sweets and oils for colours and substances that are not allowed.
- Hospital labs separate the substances in blood and urine to check a person's health and the medicines in their body.
- Sports labs test athletes for banned medicines with chromatography.
- Forensic scientists compare the inks on letters, cheques and wills to find out whether words were added later.
- Water labs test drinking water for harmful substances, such as pesticides, with chromatography.
- Medicine factories check that every batch of tablets is pure enough.
- Firework makers, such as those in Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu, colour their sparks with elements: strontium for red, barium for green, copper for blue and sodium for yellow.
- Old orange street lights glow with sodium's yellow-orange light.
- Astronomers read the light of stars and planets to find out what they are made of.
- Chemists use a method called Raman spectroscopy, named after C.V. Raman, to identify substances from the light they scatter. Raman found the effect in Kolkata in 1928.

## Open questions
- **How many different substances are there in one living cell?** Labs have found thousands, but some are present in amounts too small for today's tests. Nobody has a complete list for any cell.
- **Can we find signs of life on a planet around another star, from the light that passes through its air?** Telescopes can now read the air of a few far planets. Scientists look for gases that living things make. No clear sign of life has been found yet, and some claims are still argued about.
- **What is dark matter made of?** Most of the matter in the universe gives out no light at all, so chemists' light tests cannot read it. Nobody knows what it is.
- **Could a machine ever find substances as well as a sniffer dog's nose?** Sniffer dogs can find tiny traces of explosives and drugs. Engineers are building 'electronic noses', but none yet matches a trained dog everywhere.
- **Are there more elements still to be found?** Scientists have made elements up to number 118 in labs. Heavier ones may exist for a tiny moment, and teams are trying to make them.

## Connected maths
- On one strip, the water climbed 10 cm. The blue dye went 8 cm and the purple dye went 3 cm. How much further did the blue dye travel than the purple? **5 cm further.**. The blue went 8 cm and the purple went 3 cm. 8 take away 3 is 5. So the blue travelled 5 cm further. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- On a strip where the water climbed 10 cm, a yellow dye went 5 cm, half of the way. On a longer strip, the water climbs 16 cm. Where would you expect the yellow dye to stop? **At about 8 cm.**. The yellow dye went half the way on the first strip. With the same paper and water, it goes about half the way again. Half of 16 cm is 8 cm. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- In the Does Every Colour Split test, the black pen had 3 dyes, brown had 4, green had 2, purple had 2, orange had 2 and yellow had 1. How many of the six pens were mixtures? Which colour was most likely just one dye? **5 pens were mixtures. Yellow was most likely one dye.**. A mixture has more than one dye. Black, brown, green, purple and orange each had 2 or more, so that is 5 pens. Yellow showed only 1 band, so it is most likely one dye. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Auguste Comte wrote in 1835 that we would never know what stars are made of. Kirchhoff showed there was sodium in the Sun in 1859. How many years later was that? **24 years later.**. Take the earlier year from the later year. 1859 take away 1835 is 24. Check: 1835 + 24 = 1859. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Each set of strips takes about 8 minutes for the water to climb. You have three tumblers and want to run 12 strips, 2 in each tumbler at a time. How many rounds do you need, and how long will it take? **2 rounds, about 16 minutes.**. Three tumblers with 2 strips each hold 6 strips at a time. 12 strips need 2 rounds of 6. 2 rounds of 8 minutes is 16 minutes. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
