# Animal minds: how animals think, feel and remember
`sci.animals.animal-minds.L3`

**Summary.** Animals think, feel, remember and decide. A crow remembers a face for years, and a dog follows a pointing finger to a hidden treat. Puppies, rats and even bumblebees play, just for fun. You will test what raises water in a glass with the fewest pieces, as rooks did, and learn how scientists test animal minds fairly. Some scientists say we cannot yet know what an animal feels inside, and nobody knows what it is like to be a crow.

**Intro.** *You probably know the story of the thirsty crow. It drops pebbles into a pot until the water rises. Do you think a real crow could do that? Or is it just a story?* In 2009, two scientists in England tested it. They gave rooks, a kind of crow, a tall tube of water. A worm floated on the water, too low for a beak to reach. Stones lay next to the tube. The rooks picked up stones and dropped them in, one by one. The water rose, and they ate the worm. They even chose the big stones first. So the story was right: a real bird can solve this problem. In this unit you will find out what else animals learn, remember and work out.

## Terms
- **Mind**: The part of you that thinks, feels, learns and remembers. Nobody can see a mind directly. We can only see what it does. *e.g.* You decide to pick the mango, not the banana. Your mind made that choice.
- **Learning**: Changing what you do because of what happened before. *e.g.* A dog runs to the gate when it hears the gate click. It learned that the click means someone is coming home.
- **Memory**: Keeping something you learned, so you can use it later. *e.g.* You remember where you hid your sweets last week.
- **Instinct**: Something an animal does without being taught. It is born ready to do it. *e.g.* A baby turtle walks towards the sea the moment it hatches. Nobody shows it the way.
- **Play**: Doing something just for fun, again and again, when you are safe and relaxed. It does not get you food or shelter right away. *e.g.* Two puppies chase each other and roll about, but nobody gets hurt.
- **Tool**: An object you use to help you do a job. It is not part of your body. *e.g.* A spoon is a tool for eating. A twig is a tool for a crow fishing out a grub.
- **Problem solving**: Finding a new way to get what you want, when the usual way does not work. *e.g.* The ball is under the bed and your arm is too short. You use a broom to reach it.
- **Brain**: The body part inside the head that thinks, feels and remembers. It takes in messages from the senses and sends out messages that move the body. *e.g.* When you see a ball coming, your eyes send a message to your brain. Your brain tells your hands to catch it.
- **Neuron**: A tiny messenger cell in the brain and body. Neurons pass tiny electric signals to each other. *e.g.* Your brain has about 86 billion neurons. A honeybee's brain has about one million.
- **Emotion**: A feeling, like happy, scared or angry. It changes your face and body. *e.g.* When you are scared, your heart beats fast and you want to run.
- **Body language**: What a body shows about feelings, without words. *e.g.* A dog with its tail tucked under and ears flat is scared.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing, and keep everything else the same. *e.g.* To test big stones against small ones, use the same glass and the same water each time.
- **Blind test**: A test where the person giving it does not know the answer. Then they cannot give away the answer by mistake. *e.g.* One friend hides a coin under a cup while you look away. Now your brother tries to find it. You cannot give him clues, because you do not know where it is either.
- **Evidence**: What you saw or measured that shows an idea is true or false. *e.g.* The rook dropped stones and ate the worm. That is evidence that it solved the problem.
- **Scientist who studies animal behaviour**: A person who watches animals very carefully, and tests what they can do. This science is called ethology. *e.g.* Jane Goodall watched chimpanzees in Tanzania for many years.
- **Mirror test**: A test to see if an animal knows the animal in a mirror is itself. A mark is put on its body where it can only see it in the mirror. *e.g.* A child with a sticker on their forehead looks in the mirror and touches their own forehead.

## Explanations
### What a mind is · *debated*
Animals think, feel, remember and decide. We know it from what they do: a crow chooses a stone, and a dog greets you with its whole body. Nobody can see a thought directly, in an animal or in a person. With a friend too, you see what they do and say, and you know their mind from that. Some scientists say this is not enough for animals, and that every claim needs a careful test.
- e.g. A cat waits by the kitchen door at the same time every evening. It remembers when dinner comes, and it chooses to wait there.
- e.g. A crow drops a hard nut onto a road, waits for a car to crack it, then flies down to eat.
- **Rene Descartes, a French thinker, in 1637:** Animals are like very clever machines, like a clock. They move and make sounds, but they do not think or feel anything inside.
- **Charles Darwin, an English scientist, in 1871 and 1872:** Humans are animals too, and we come from the same family tree. So other animals have the same kinds of feelings and thinking as us, but in different amounts.
- **Many scientists today:** Many animals clearly learn, remember and solve problems. We can test those things. What an animal feels inside is much harder to test. So they say: test carefully, and choose the simplest explanation that fits.
### Animals learn · *defined*
**Definition:** Learning is changing what you do because of what happened before.

Almost every animal learns, from a worm to an elephant. The simplest learning is noticing that one thing comes before another. Around 1900, Ivan Pavlov, a Russian scientist, found that dogs learned to drool at a sound that came before food. Another kind is learning by trying. Edward Thorndike, an American scientist, put cats in puzzle boxes in 1898. Each time, a cat escaped a little faster. Animals also learn by watching others, just as you learn by watching a cousin.
- e.g. Your dog runs to the kitchen when it hears the fridge open.
- e.g. Pigeons in Japan learned to tell paintings by Monet from paintings by Picasso. Shigeru Watanabe and his team showed this in 1995.
- e.g. Young crows watch older crows, and learn which people are dangerous.
### Animals remember · *defined*
**Definition:** Memory is keeping what you learned, so you can use it later.

Some birds hide food and come back for it months later. A Clark's nutcracker, a bird of the American mountains, hides many thousands of pine seeds in autumn. It finds a lot of them in winter, under snow. In 1998, Nicola Clayton and Anthony Dickinson showed that scrub jays remember what they hid, where they hid it, and when. Crows also remember faces. Elephants remember the calls of their family and friends.
- e.g. In the United States, John Marzluff's team caught a few crows while wearing a mask. For years after, crows scolded anyone wearing that mask, even crows that had never been caught.
- e.g. In Kenya, Karen McComb and her team played elephant calls to elephant families in 2001. Families with an older grandmother elephant as leader were better at telling friends' calls from strangers' calls.
### Animals play · *defined*
**Definition:** Play is doing something again and again just for fun, when you are safe and relaxed. It does not get food or shelter right away.

Gordon Burghardt, an American scientist, wrote down clear signs that tell play apart from other things animals do. Using those signs, scientists have found play in many animals. Puppies, kittens and goats play. Rats play chase, and they make a happy chirp when tickled. The chirp is too high for us to hear without a special microphone. Jaak Panksepp, an American scientist, studied this from the late 1990s. Octopuses have pushed floating bottles around their tank again and again. In 2022, scientists in London found that bumblebees rolled little wooden balls, even with no food as a reward.
- e.g. A puppy bows with its front legs down and its tail up. This 'play bow' means 'let's play, this is not a real fight'.
- e.g. Young goats jump and twist in the air, just for fun.
- e.g. Bumblebees rolled wooden balls many times, for nothing but the rolling. The young bees rolled them the most.
### Why animals play · *debated*
Play uses energy and can be risky. So why do animals do it? Scientists have several ideas, and they still argue. Most think play does more than one job.
- e.g. Lion cubs pounce on each other's tails.
- e.g. Children make up rules for a game, then change them.
- **The practice idea:** Play is practice for real life. A kitten pouncing on a ball is practising hunting. A puppy wrestling is practising fighting, without getting hurt.
- **The friendship idea:** Play builds friendships. Animals that play together learn to trust each other, and to take turns.
- **The surprise idea:** Play trains the body and brain to cope with surprises. When you fall over in a game, you learn to get up quickly.
- **The fun idea:** Some scientists say play may simply feel good, and that is reason enough. The bumblebees rolled balls with no prize at all.
### Animals use tools · *defined*
**Definition:** A tool is an object an animal uses to help it do a job. It is not part of its own body.

People once said only humans use tools. In 1960, Jane Goodall watched a chimpanzee in Tanzania. She named him David Greybeard. He pulled a grass stem, stripped off its leaves and poked it into a termite mound. Termites grabbed the stem, and he ate them. Since then, scientists have found many tool users. New Caledonian crows, from islands in the Pacific Ocean, make hooks from forked twigs and cut tools from stiff leaves. They use them to pull grubs out of holes. In 2002, a crow called Betty bent a straight wire into a hook to lift a little bucket of food out of a tube.
- e.g. In Africa, Egyptian vultures throw stones at ostrich eggs to crack them open. Jane Goodall and Hugo van Lawick reported this in 1966. These vultures live in India too.
- e.g. Sea otters float on their backs and crack shellfish on a stone.
- e.g. Some scientists think mugger crocodiles in India balance sticks on their snouts in the nesting season, to trick birds looking for twigs. Others are not sure yet.
### Animals solve problems · *defined*
**Definition:** Problem solving is finding a new way to get what you want, when the usual way does not work.

About a hundred years ago, Wolfgang Kohler, a German scientist, put bananas out of reach of chimpanzees. One chimpanzee, Sultan, fitted two short sticks together to make one long one, and pulled the fruit in. In 2009, Christopher Bird and Nathan Emery gave rooks a tube of water with a worm floating too low to reach. The rooks dropped in stones until they could reach it. They picked big stones first. Later, New Caledonian crows passed similar tests, and chose sinking stones over floating things.
- e.g. A crow drops a hard nut onto a road, so a car cracks it.
- e.g. A cat learns to pull down a door handle with its paw.
### Dogs read our faces · *defined*
**Definition:** Dogs notice where people look and point, and they can tell some human feelings from faces.

If you point at one of two bowls, most pet dogs go to the bowl you pointed at. Brian Hare and his team showed this in 2002. Dogs even do better at this than chimpanzees. In 2015, scientists in Vienna showed dogs photos of human faces. The dogs learned to tell happy faces from angry ones, even for people they had never met. In India, Anindita Bhadra and her team at IISER Kolkata tested street dogs that nobody had trained. Many of them followed a pointing finger to a bowl.
- e.g. Your dog looks at your face, then at the ball, then back at you.
- e.g. A street dog moves away from someone shouting, and towards someone kneeling and speaking softly.
### Why dogs are so good with people · *debated*
Dogs came from wolves that began living near people many thousands of years ago. Scientists argue about why dogs read us so well.
- e.g. Wolf pups raised by people follow pointing less well than dog puppies do, in many tests.
- e.g. Dogs who live in houses get lots of practice watching people.
- **Brian Hare and others:** Over thousands of years, the dogs that were best at reading people did best living with people. So reading people became part of being a dog, from birth.
- **Monique Udell, Clive Wynne and others:** Dogs learn it while growing up with people. Wolves raised closely with people can learn to follow pointing too.
- **Many scientists today:** Probably both. Dogs are born ready to watch people, and living with people makes them better at it.
### Clever Hans: the horse who read faces · *defined*
**Definition:** The Clever Hans effect is when an animal or person picks up tiny clues from someone who knows the answer, without anyone meaning to give them.

Around 1904, a horse in Berlin called Hans seemed to do sums. He tapped his hoof to give the answer. Then a scientist called Oskar Pfungst tested him carefully. He wrote a book about it in 1907. Hans was right only when the person asking knew the answer, and when Hans could see them. As Hans tapped, the asker tensed up. When he reached the right number, the asker relaxed a tiny bit. Hans saw it and stopped. So Hans could not do sums. But he could read tiny changes in people's faces and bodies, which is clever in its own way.
- e.g. Scientists who test animals now often make the test 'blind'. The person in the room does not know the answer.
- e.g. A dog may 'find' a hidden toy because its owner glances at the hiding place.
### Brains, neurons and tiny sparks · *defined*
**Definition:** A brain is made of tiny messenger cells called neurons. Neurons pass signals by moving charged atoms, called ions, in and out.

In a brain, every thought, memory and feeling comes with neurons sending tiny electric signals. The signal is made by charged atoms of sodium and potassium, the same atoms that are in salt and bananas. Your brain has about 86 billion neurons. Suzana Herculano-Houzel, a Brazilian scientist, helped count them. A honeybee has about one million neurons in a brain smaller than a sesame seed. In 2016, scientists found that bird brains pack neurons very tightly. A crow's small brain has about as many neurons in its thinking part as some monkeys have. So brain size alone does not tell you how clever an animal is.
- e.g. Your brain uses about a fifth of all the energy your body uses, even while you sleep.
- e.g. An elephant's brain is more than three times as heavy as yours.
- e.g. A crow's brain is about the size of a walnut, but crows make tools.
### A mind with eight arms: the octopus · *idea*
An octopus has about 500 million neurons. About two thirds of them are in its arms, not its head. Each arm can feel, taste and grab on its own. Our last shared ancestor with the octopus was a small worm-like animal, more than 500 million years ago. So clever minds came about at least twice, separately, on two very different branches of life. Peter Godfrey-Smith, a philosopher who dives with octopuses, wrote about this in his book Other Minds, in 2016. What it feels like to be an octopus, nobody knows.
- e.g. Octopuses in aquariums have unscrewed jar lids to get crabs inside.
- e.g. Some octopuses carry coconut shell halves and hide inside them.
### Animals feel · *debated*
Animals feel. A dog shows joy with its whole body, and a hurt fish avoids the place where it was hurt. Many scientists agree, at least for mammals and birds. Others say we cannot yet test what an animal feels inside, so we should not be sure.
- e.g. A dog greets you with its whole body wagging.
- e.g. Elephants touch and smell the bones of dead elephants for a long time.
- **Charles Darwin, in his book The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals, 1872:** Animals show fear, anger and joy with their faces and bodies, much as we do. So they probably feel them.
- **Conwy Lloyd Morgan, an English scientist, in 1894:** Do not say an animal is thinking or feeling something complicated, if a simpler explanation fits. Be careful, and test first.
- **Many scientists, in the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, 2024:** There is strong evidence that mammals and birds have feelings. There is a real chance that fish, octopuses, crabs and insects do too. It would be wrong to ignore this.
- **Some other scientists:** Behaviour can look like feeling without any feeling inside. A robot can cry out when hit. We need better tests before we say an insect feels.

## Mostly agreed
- Many animals learn from what happens to them. *How we know:* Dogs, cats, rats, pigeons, bees and even worms change what they do after rewards or shocks. Scientists have tested this thousands of times.
- Many animals remember for a long time. *How we know:* Jays find hidden food months later. Crows scolded a 'dangerous' mask for years in John Marzluff's study.
- Humans are not the only tool users. *How we know:* Jane Goodall saw chimpanzees fish for termites in 1960. Crows, vultures, otters and many others use tools too.
- Many animals play. *How we know:* Play has been seen in mammals and birds, and there is evidence for it in octopuses and bumblebees.
- Dogs follow human pointing and notice human faces. *How we know:* Pet dogs, and many untrained street dogs in India, go to the bowl a person points at in careful tests.

## How we found out
**Ludwig Edinger first, then Erich Jarvis, Anton Reiner and many others, and later Suzana Herculano-Houzel's team, who counted the neurons, Around 1900, then 2004 to 2016.** For about a hundred years, scientists thought birds were poor thinkers. Around 1900, a German brain scientist called Ludwig Edinger studied bird brains. He decided that most of a bird's forebrain matched a part of our brain thought to run instincts. The part we think with, the wrinkled outer layer called the cortex, seemed to be almost missing in birds. So for a long time, books said birds act mostly on instinct, and 'bird brain' became a word for a silly person. Then birds kept surprising scientists. In 2002, a crow called Betty bent a wire into a hook. Crows remembered faces, and jays remembered where and when they hid food. In the early 2000s, a group of scientists, including Erich Jarvis and Anton Reiner, looked at bird brains again. They found that most of a bird's forebrain does the same job as our cortex, only arranged in clumps instead of layers. So they gave its parts new names in 2004. In 2016, scientists counted the neurons. A crow's small forebrain held about as many as some monkeys have.  
*What people believed before:* Birds have almost no thinking part in their brains, so they act mostly on instinct. A closer look showed that most of a bird's forebrain does the job of our cortex, and in 2016 neuron counts showed that crows pack in as many as some monkeys.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only humans make and use tools.** (Many people before 1960. Some books still say it.; *rejected*)  
  For: No other animal builds cars, phones or houses like ours.  
  Against: Jane Goodall saw chimpanzees make tools from grass stems in 1960. Her teacher Louis Leakey wrote that we must now redefine 'tool', redefine 'man', or accept chimpanzees as humans. Crows bend hooks too.
- **Animals are machines with no feelings inside.** (Rene Descartes, a French thinker, in 1637, and many people after him.; *minority*)  
  For: We cannot see feelings, only behaviour. A machine could act as if it were in pain.  
  Against: Animals have the same brain chemicals for fear and pleasure as we do. They choose to avoid pain and seek comfort. Most scientists now think at least mammals and birds feel.
- **A bigger brain always means a cleverer animal.** (Many of us think this without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: Humans have big brains for their size, and we do many clever things.  
  Against: Elephants and whales have bigger brains than us. Crows have tiny brains but make tools, because their neurons are packed tightly. Bees learn a lot with a brain smaller than a sesame seed.
- **A dog that looks 'guilty' knows it did something wrong.** (Many dog owners.; *debated*)  
  For: The dog lowers its head and tail, and won't meet your eyes, after it chews a chappal.  
  Against: In 2009, Alexandra Horowitz tested this. Dogs looked most 'guilty' when the owner scolded them, even if the dog had done nothing wrong. The look may mean 'please don't be angry', not 'I feel guilty'.
- **The mirror test shows which animals know themselves.** (Gordon Gallup, an American scientist, who invented the test in 1970.; *debated*)  
  For: Chimpanzees, an Asian elephant called Happy, and magpies touched a mark they could only see in a mirror.  
  Against: Dogs mostly fail the mirror test, but they know their own smell. In 2017, Alexandra Horowitz showed dogs sniff a changed smell of their own longer. Animals that do not use eyes much may need a different test. A small fish, the cleaner wrasse, seemed to pass in 2019, and scientists are still arguing about what that means.
- **Fish and insects cannot feel pain.** (Many people, and some scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: Their brains are very different from ours, and much smaller.  
  Against: Fish rub a sore lip and avoid a place where they were hurt. Bees and crabs also act in pain-like ways in some tests. Many scientists now say we should be careful and kind.

## Roots
We have been learning that animals play, learn, remember and read faces. Now we ask what that tells us about minds, about ourselves and about the world.

**Deep truth.** In your glass, big stones raised the water with the fewest pieces, and you could say why. Rooks given a tube of water also chose big stones first, though they cannot tell us why. Nobody can see inside another mind, so we judge even a friend's mind by what they do. Judged the same way, the rook was thinking, just as you were.  
*Habit it breaks:* We accept that a friend thinks because of what they do and say. For animals we often ask for much more proof. That uses one rule for people and a stricter one for every other animal.  
*Evidence:* In 2009, Christopher Bird and Nathan Emery gave rooks a tube with a worm floating out of reach. The rooks dropped in stones until they could eat it, and they picked big stones first. In the thirsty crow test, you found the same thing: big sinking stones raise the water with the fewest pieces.

- *who am i:* You are one of many minds on Earth. A crow, a dog and you all learn, remember and decide.
- *what is life:* Living things notice, learn, remember and choose. Crows, dogs, octopuses and you all do this, with very different bodies.
- *what is this world:* A crow, a dog and a bee notice different things in the same street. Each of them lives in its own version of the world.

A crow, a dog and you look very different from outside. Inside, your thinking and theirs runs on neurons passing signals with the same sodium and potassium atoms. Clever minds came about separately in crows, octopuses and apes, on branches of life that split long ago. Awareness runs through all of life, so it keeps showing up wherever life goes, in a bird, a dog or an eight-armed octopus.

- **Bhagavad Gita 5.18:** The wise see with an equal eye a learned person, a cow, an elephant, a dog, and a person whom society looks down on. (This unit found minds at work in elephants and dogs: elephants remember the calls of their friends, and dogs read our pointing fingers. The Gita says a wise person looks at all of them with the same eye.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 17 (Autumn Floods), the fish of the Hao river:** Zhuangzi and his friend Huizi stood on a bridge. Zhuangzi said, 'See how the fish swim about. That is their joy.' Huizi said, 'You are not a fish. How do you know what fish enjoy?' Zhuangzi said, 'You are not me. How do you know I don't know?' (Huizi could not see inside Zhuangzi's mind either, yet he argued with him as a mind. We know every other mind, a friend's or a fish's, from what it does.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 6:** One who sees all beings in their own self, and their own self in all beings, does not hate anyone. (When we see that a crow learns and a dog feels, we start to see ourselves in them, and them in us.)
- Science (settled): Many animals learn, remember, play and solve problems. This has been tested many times.
- Science (debated): Many scientists agree that mammals and birds very likely have feelings. For fish, octopuses, crabs and insects, many see a real chance, and the tests go on.
- Other view, Many careful scientists, following Conwy Lloyd Morgan, and the philosopher Rene Descartes before them: We should not read human feelings into animals. A crow bending a hook may be following simple learned rules, with nothing it is like inside. Descartes went further and said animals feel nothing at all. Most scientists today disagree with Descartes, but many still warn against seeing a mind like ours in every animal. That is an honest and careful view.

Just watch it for one minute without talking. Now imagine looking out through its eyes. What does it see? What might it want? Then come back to your own eyes, and breathe once more.
- Ask: When you watched the animal, did it feel like someone was in there looking back?
- Ask: Is a crow's way of knowing the world less real than yours?

- Ask: Where do you think your own mind is?

- *deep:* Why do animals play, when play does not get them food? (hint: What do you learn when you play a game with friends?)
- *deep:* Is a thought made of anything? What is a memory made of? (hint: Neurons pass tiny electric signals made by moving atoms. Is a memory just atoms, or something more?)
- *deep:* A horse called Hans fooled clever people for years. How can we make sure we are not fooling ourselves when we test animals? (hint: What if the person giving the test does not know the answer?)

## Story: The Horse Who Watched Faces
*Clever Hans was a real horse in Berlin, owned by a retired teacher called Wilhelm von Osten. In 1904 crowds came to see him, and Oskar Pfungst tested him and showed he was reading tiny movements of the people asking. Lotte, her father and the scenes in the stable are made up.*

In Berlin, more than a hundred years ago, there was a courtyard with tall grey walls. In the courtyard lived a horse who could count. Or so everybody said.

His name was Hans. He was a tall black stallion with a white star on his face. Every afternoon, his owner, an old teacher called Herr von Osten, led him out into the yard. People crowded at the gate in their best hats. Herr von Osten would ask, 'Hans, what is three and two?' And Hans would lift his right front hoof and tap the cobbles. One, two, three, four, five. Then he stopped. The crowd clapped and gasped. Newspapers called him Clever Hans.

Lotte was nine, and she knew Hans better than anyone. Her father was the groom who brushed him and fed him. Lotte carried the oats in a bucket that banged against her knees. She knew which spot behind Hans's ear he liked scratched. She knew he snorted when she was late. And she knew something else, which nobody had asked her about. Hans always knew when she was sad.

On the day her best friend moved away to Hamburg, Lotte had come into the stable with her face stiff. She had not cried. She had not said a word. But Hans had turned from his hay and pushed his big soft nose into her shoulder, and kept it there. 'How did you know?' she had whispered. Hans had only blinked.

Then the scientists came. There were thirteen of them at first, with notebooks and pocket watches. They watched Hans for days. They looked for tricks, for signals, for strings. They found none. Herr von Osten was not cheating, they said. He truly believed his horse could think like a schoolboy.

But one young man stayed on. His name was Oskar Pfungst. He had round glasses and a careful, quiet voice. He did not clap when Hans got a sum right. He wrote everything down.

'May I ask Hans a question,' he said one morning, 'that I do not know the answer to myself?'

Herr von Osten frowned. 'What a strange thing to ask.'

Pfungst wrote a number on a card. He did not look at it. He held it up in front of Hans, face out, so only the horse could see it. 'Tap the number, Hans.'

Hans tapped. And tapped. And tapped. He tapped past five, past ten, past twenty. He did not stop. The courtyard went very quiet. Lotte felt her cheeks go hot, as if she were the one getting it wrong.

Pfungst turned the card round. It said seven. He wrote something in his notebook.

For days he tested. When the person asking knew the answer, Hans was almost always right. When the person asking did not know, Hans was almost always wrong. When Hans wore big blinkers and could not see the person's face, he was lost.

'So he is a fraud,' said a man at the gate, and spat. 'A horse that cannot do sums.'

Herr von Osten went grey. He stopped coming down to the yard. Lotte's father brushed Hans without humming. The crowds thinned to nobody. Lotte sat on an upturned bucket and stared at the horse, and the horse stared back.

Then Pfungst did one more thing, and Lotte was there to see it. He stood in front of Hans and asked a sum. As Hans began to tap, Pfungst did not speak or move his hands. But when Hans reached the right number, Pfungst let his head lift, the tiniest bit. It was less than the width of a fingernail. Hans stopped.

Pfungst did it again with a wrong number. He lifted his head at four, when the answer was six. Hans stopped at four.

'There,' Pfungst said softly, and he looked almost happy. 'Every person who asks him leans forward a little while he taps. When he reaches the answer, they relax. They do not even know they do it. But he sees it. Every time.'

Lotte stood up off her bucket.

She thought of the crowd at the gate, leaning, holding their breath, then letting it out in a rush. She thought of Herr von Osten's eyebrows rising. She thought of her own stiff face on the day her friend went to Hamburg, and the warm nose pushing into her shoulder.

Hans could not do sums. That was true. But he had been doing something else all along, something none of them had thought to test. He had been reading them. He had been watching hundreds of strangers' faces, every afternoon, and catching changes too small for the people themselves to notice. He knew when they were waiting. He knew when they were pleased. He knew when a girl was sad, and said nothing.

'They are calling him stupid,' Lotte said to Pfungst. She was surprised to hear her own voice. 'But none of them could do what he does.'

Pfungst looked at her for a long moment over his round glasses. Then he wrote something in his notebook, and he did not show her what.

That evening Lotte brought the oats. She did not hurry. She stood in front of Hans and let herself feel what she felt, which was a mix of sorry and proud and glad. She did not say anything.

Hans lowered his head and blew warm air into her hands. Then he waited, the way he always did, watching her face to see what she would do next.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Hans could not do sums, but he could read tiny changes in human faces and bodies, smaller than people knew they were making. The crowd saw only the trick they expected and missed the real cleverness. Animal minds are often clever in ways we do not think to test. The story also hides the idea of a blind test: Pfungst found the truth by asking a question he did not know the answer to himself.

- Ask: In the story, why did Hans tap and tap when Oskar Pfungst held up a number he had not looked at?
- Ask: What tiny thing did the people asking Hans do when he reached the right number?
- Ask: The man at the gate said Hans was a fraud. Lotte said none of them could do what Hans did. Who do you agree with, and why?
- Ask: How did Hans know Lotte was sad, when she had not said a word?
- Ask: Has an animal ever seemed to know how you were feeling? What did it do?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Thirsty Crow Test: what raises the water best? (25 min)
- **question:** Which raises the water to the line with the fewest pieces: big stones, small stones or pieces of thermocol?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will need the fewest pieces to raise the water to the line? Which will need the most? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only what you drop in. Use the same glass, fill it to the same starting line, and use the same top line each time. Drop things in one at a time.
- **safety:** Wash the stones first. Small children must not put small stones or marbles in their mouths. Wipe up spills so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each thing you dropped in.
- **results table:** What we dropped in | Guess: how many to reach the line? | How many it really took | Did it ever reach the line?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the glass with water until it is about 4 cm below the top. 2. Put a strip of tape at the water level. This is the starting line. 3. Put a second strip 2 cm higher, about two finger-widths. This is the top line. 4. Float the thermocol 'worm' on the water.  
  *Parent says:* Remember the thirsty crow story? The crow dropped pebbles in a pot until it could drink. Let's be the crow. The thermocol is the worm. Our beak can only reach the top line.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the glass. Mark where the water is. Mark a line two fingers higher. Float the worm. Can you bring it up to the line?
  - Note: Use a glass about 6 to 7 cm across. In a wider glass the water rises too slowly, and you will need many more stones. A steel tumbler works too, but a clear glass lets you see the water rise.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess how many of each thing they will need. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which do you think will need the fewest: big stones, small stones or thermocol? Why do you think so? Let's write our guesses down first.  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. How many big stones? How many small stones? How many bits of thermocol? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will need the fewest? (good: Big stones take up more room.; Heavy things sink and push the water up.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drop in big stones one at a time, counting, until the worm reaches the top line. Write the number. Empty the glass, refill to the starting line, and do the same with small stones. Then do it with the thermocol pieces. Stop the thermocol at 20.  
  *Parent says:* One stone at a time. Count out loud. Stop when the worm reaches the line. Now let's empty it and start again, so the test is fair.  
  *Child mode:* Drop in one big stone at a time. Count. Stop at the line. Empty the glass. Fill it to the start line. Now try small stones. Then thermocol.
  - Note: In a glass 6 to 7 cm across, the water must rise 2 cm, so you need to push about 60 to 75 millilitres of water out of the way. A walnut-sized stone takes up about 10 millilitres, so it takes about 5 to 8 big stones. A marble-sized stone takes up only about 2 millilitres, so it takes about 30 to 40 small ones. Thermocol floats and weighs almost nothing, so 20 pieces hardly raise the water at all. This surprises most children.
  - Check: In the thirsty crow test, why did the thermocol hardly raise the water? → **It floats, so it does not push much water out of the way**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the numbers, with one bar for each thing.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn our numbers into a picture. A short bar means it only took a few. The thermocol never got there, so we stop its bar at 20 and write 'never reached'. Which bar is shortest?  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each thing. A short bar means it took only a few. Which bar is shortest?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child what real rooks and crows did in this test.  
  *Parent says:* In 2009, Christopher Bird and Nathan Emery gave rooks a tube like this. The rooks dropped in stones to reach a floating worm. They chose the big stones first. Later, New Caledonian crows passed similar tests. They chose sinking things over floating ones, just as you found out.  
  *Child mode:* Real rooks did this test! They chose big stones first. Crows picked sinkers, not floaters. Did you?
  - Think: The rooks could not measure or talk it over. How do you think they worked out which stones to use?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: big stones raise the water with the fewest pieces. What I saw: it took 6 big stones but 32 small stones, and the thermocol never got there. Why: big sinking stones take up more room under the water, so the water has to go up.
### e2 · The Clever Hans Test: can you read a face? (20 min)
- **question:** Can you find a hidden coin by watching the face of someone who knows where it is?
- **predict:** Guess how many times out of 10 you will find the coin when your helper knows where it is. Then guess how many when your helper does not know. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep everything the same except one thing: whether your helper knows where the coin is. Use the same 3 cups, the same coin and 10 tries each round. Your helper must not speak or point.
- **safety:** No safety worries. Keep the coin away from very small children.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw two bars: one for 'helper knows' and one for 'helper does not know'.
- **results table:** Round | Does the helper know where the coin is? | Right guesses out of 10
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 3 cups upside down in a row. 2. The guesser shuts their eyes. 3. The hider puts the coin under one cup, and the helper watches. 4. The helper sits facing the guesser, hands in lap, and says nothing.  
  *Parent says:* A horse called Hans seemed to do sums. Really, he watched people's faces. Let's see if you can do what Hans did. Your helper knows where the coin is, but must not speak or point.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. The coin goes under one cup. Your helper knows where. Open your eyes. Watch their face, not the cups!
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The guesser moves a finger slowly along the cups, watching the helper's face, then picks one. Do this 10 times, hiding the coin in a new place each time. Count the right guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Move your finger slowly over each cup. Keep your eyes on my face. Pick the cup when you feel sure.  
  *Child mode:* Move your finger slowly over the cups. Watch the face. Pick one. Do it 10 times. Count how many you get right.
  - Note: Helpers should try to keep a still face, but most people still give tiny clues without meaning to. That is exactly what Hans saw.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now the helper also shuts their eyes or turns away while the hider hides the coin. The hider then leaves the room or turns their back. Play 10 more times and count.  
  *Parent says:* Now I won't know where the coin is either. This is called a blind test. Let's see what happens to your score.  
  *Child mode:* Now your helper doesn't know either. This is a blind test. Play 10 more times. Count your right guesses.
  - Check: In the Clever Hans test, what usually happens when the helper does not know where the coin is? → **The guesser gets fewer right, about 3 out of 10, like lucky guessing**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw two bars, one for each round. Compare them with your guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw our two scores as bars. Was there a difference? Was it big?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two bars. One for 'helper knows'. One for 'blind'. Which is taller?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why did it happen? And why do scientists use blind tests when they test animals?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why. Then say why scientists use blind tests.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: faces give away clues. What I saw: I got 8 right when you knew, and only 3 when you didn't. Why: your face changed a tiny bit at the right cup. Scientists use blind tests so an animal cannot get the answer from them by mistake. If both scores were about the same, that is a real result too. It means your helper kept a very still face.
### e3 · The Pointing Test: does a dog follow your finger? (25 min)
- **question:** Does a dog find a hidden treat more often when a person points at it?
- **predict:** Guess how many times out of 10 the dog will pick the right bowl with pointing. Then guess without pointing. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only one thing: pointing or no pointing. Use the same two bowls, the same spot and the same treat. Rub the treat on both bowls so the dog cannot just smell which one has it.
- **safety:** Only use a pet dog, or a dog whose owner is there and says it is calm. Never test an unknown street dog. A grown-up holds the dog's collar between tries. Wash hands afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw two bars: one for 'pointing' and one for 'no pointing'.
- **results table:** Round | Pointing or no pointing? | Right choices out of 10
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two bowls upside down on the floor, about two big steps apart. 2. Rub a treat on both bowls. 3. The owner holds the dog's collar, a few steps back, and turns it away while you hide a treat under one bowl.  
  *Parent says:* Scientists test dogs like this all over the world. Let's see if our dog reads our pointing. First, we make sure the dog can't just smell the treat.  
  *Child mode:* Put out two bowls. Rub the treat on both. Hide it under one while the dog looks away.
  - Note: Choose a calm, friendly dog that knows you. Stop if the dog gets too excited or worried.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Kneel between the bowls. Say the dog's name to get its attention. Point clearly at the bowl with the treat and look at it. The owner lets the dog go. Tick right or wrong. Do this 10 times, changing the bowl in a mixed-up order.  
  *Parent says:* Watch, I'll say its name, point and look at the bowl. Then let go. Which bowl did it go to?  
  *Child mode:* Say the dog's name. Point at the right bowl and look at it. Let the dog go. Tick right or wrong. Do it 10 times.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Now do 10 more tries without pointing. Just kneel between the bowls and look straight ahead.  
  *Parent says:* Now I won't point. If the dog does just as well, what would that tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Now don't point. Look straight ahead. Let the dog go. Tick right or wrong. 10 times.
  - Check: In the pointing test, why did we rub the treat on both bowls? → **So the dog cannot find the treat just by smell**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw two bars. Then talk about what scientists found.  
  *Parent says:* Brian Hare and his team found that most pet dogs follow pointing. In India, Anindita Bhadra's team at IISER Kolkata found that many street dogs follow pointing too, even though nobody trained them. What did our dog do?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two bars: pointing and no pointing. Most dogs follow pointing. Did yours? Say what you think, what you saw and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: our dog reads pointing. What I saw: 8 right with pointing, 5 without. Why: dogs watch where people look and point, because they live so closely with us. If your dog did not follow pointing, that is a real result too. Some dogs do and some don't.

### Animal Minds Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is learning?  
   Options: Being born knowing how to do something / Changing what you do because of what happened before / Growing bigger / Sleeping a lot  
   **Answer:** Changing what you do because of what happened before. *Why:* Learning means you change what you do after something happens, like a dog running to the gate when it clicks. Picking 'born knowing' mixes learning up with instinct, which needs no teaching.
2. (mcq) Which of these is play?  
   Options: A dog eating its dinner / Two puppies chasing each other with wagging tails / A cat running from a big dog / A crow building a nest  
   **Answer:** Two puppies chasing each other with wagging tails. *Why:* Play is done for fun, when safe and relaxed. Eating, running from danger and nest building all have a job to do right now. Picking 'running from a big dog' means a chase was seen as play, but that cat is scared, not relaxed.
3. (fill) An object an animal uses to help it do a job is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['tool']. *Why:* A crow's twig hook and a chimpanzee's grass stem are both tools.
4. (truefalse) Only humans use tools.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* Chimpanzees, crows, Egyptian vultures and sea otters use tools. Jane Goodall saw chimpanzees fishing for termites in 1960.
5. (match) Match each animal to what scientists saw it do.  
   **Answer:** New Caledonian crow → bent a wire into a hook; Chimpanzee → fished for termites with a grass stem; Rook → dropped stones to raise water; Dog → followed a pointing finger. *Why:* Each of these was found in careful tests or long watching by scientists.
6. (sort) Sort these. Is it LEARNED or INSTINCT (born knowing)?  
   **Answer:** a dog sitting when told 'sit' → Learned; a baby turtle walking to the sea → Instinct; a crow avoiding a person who once scared it → Learned; a spider spinning its first web → Instinct; a cow coming home at the same time each evening → Learned. *Why:* Learned things come from experience. Instinct is there from birth, with no teaching. A spider's first web is not taught.
7. (mcq) A dog has its tail tucked under and its ears flat back. How is it probably feeling?  
   Options: Playful / Scared / Proud / Sleepy  
   **Answer:** Scared. *Why:* A tucked tail and flat ears usually mean fear. Give a scared dog space. Picking 'playful' often comes from expecting dogs always to be happy.
8. (fill) A test where the person giving it does not know the answer is called a ____ test.  
   **Answer:** ['blind']. *Why:* In a blind test, nobody nearby can give away the answer by mistake, like the people who asked Clever Hans did.
9. (mcq) Clever Hans the horse seemed to do sums. What was he really doing?  
   Options: Counting in his head / Reading tiny changes in the asker's face and body / Listening to someone whispering / Guessing at random  
   **Answer:** Reading tiny changes in the asker's face and body. *Why:* Hans stopped tapping when the asker relaxed a tiny bit at the right number. He was right only when the asker knew the answer. Picking 'counting' means trusting the trick without a blind test.
10. (mcq) Which bird hides thousands of seeds and finds many of them months later?  
   Options: The peacock / The Clark's nutcracker / The hen / The ostrich  
   **Answer:** The Clark's nutcracker. *Why:* The Clark's nutcracker hides thousands of pine seeds in autumn and finds a lot of them in winter, using landmarks it remembers.
11. (truefalse) Crows can remember a human face for years.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* In John Marzluff's study, crows scolded a 'dangerous' mask for years, and young crows learned it from older ones.
12. (fill) The thirsty crow test: after 6 big stones the water reached the line. After 32 small stones it reached the line too. The big stones took ____ fewer objects.  
   **Answer:** ['26', 'twenty-six']. *Why:* 32 take away 6 is 26. So the big stones took 26 fewer objects. You can check: 6 + 26 = 32.
13. (draw) Draw a crow using a tool. Label the tool and what the crow wants to get.  
   **Answer:** A crow holding a twig or wire hook in its beak, reaching into a hole or tube, with a grub or food labelled.. *Why:* A tool is something outside the body used to help do a job.
14. (mcq) Why did we rub the treat on both bowls in the dog pointing test?  
   Options: So both bowls smell the same, and the dog must use our pointing / To clean the bowls / So the dog gets two treats  
   **Answer:** So both bowls smell the same, and the dog must use our pointing. *Why:* Dogs have powerful noses. If only one bowl smelled of food, the dog could follow its nose instead of the pointing. Picking 'two treats' misses the point of a fair test.
15. (write) Tell about one animal that plays. What does it do, and why do you think it plays?  
   **Answer:** Any true example, for example: puppies chase and wrestle. I think they are practising for real life and making friends.. *Why:* Scientists have several ideas about why animals play: practice, friendship, coping with surprises, and fun.
16. (order) Put these in order, smallest brain to biggest: crow, honeybee, human, dog.

5. (mcq) A person points at one of two bowls. What do most pet dogs do?  
   Options: Go to the bowl the person pointed at / Look at the finger and do nothing / Always go to the left bowl  
   **Answer:** Go to the bowl the person pointed at. *Why:* Most dogs follow pointing. Many untrained street dogs in India do too.
6. (mcq) Scientists showed dogs photos of human faces. What could the dogs learn to tell apart?  
   Options: Happy faces and angry faces / Old photos and new photos / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** Happy faces and angry faces. *Why:* In 2015, dogs in Vienna learned to tell happy from angry faces, even of strangers.
7. (mcq) In the thirsty crow test, why must we fill the glass to the same starting line each time?  
   Options: To make it a fair test / To save water / Because crows like full glasses  
   **Answer:** To make it a fair test. *Why:* If the water starts at different levels, we cannot compare the counts. Change only one thing at a time.
8. (mcq) Clever Hans got sums wrong when the asker did not know the answer. Why?  
   Options: He was tired / He could no longer read the answer from the asker's face / The sums were harder  
   **Answer:** He could no longer read the answer from the asker's face. *Why:* Hans read tiny relaxations in the asker's face. With no answer to give away, he had no clue. This is why scientists use blind tests.
9. (mcq) What is a blind test?  
   Options: A test with your eyes shut / A test where the person giving it does not know the answer / A test done in the dark  
   **Answer:** A test where the person giving it does not know the answer. *Why:* In a blind test, nobody nearby can give clues by mistake. Picking 'eyes shut' takes the word 'blind' too literally.
10. (mcq) Which statement do most scientists agree on?  
   Options: Many animals learn, remember and solve problems / Only humans think / Insects definitely feel exactly what we feel  
   **Answer:** Many animals learn, remember and solve problems. *Why:* Learning and memory are tested and agreed. What insects feel is still argued about.
11. (mcq) What is still argued about?  
   Options: Whether crows use tools / Whether fish and insects feel pain / Whether dogs learn  
   **Answer:** Whether fish and insects feel pain. *Why:* Crow tools and dog learning are well tested. Feelings in fish and insects are still debated.
12. (mcq) A crow's brain is about the size of a walnut. How can crows be so clever?  
   Options: Their brains are packed tightly with neurons / They have two brains / They borrow ideas from people  
   **Answer:** Their brains are packed tightly with neurons. *Why:* Bird brains pack neurons tightly, so a small brain holds a lot. Brain size alone does not tell you how clever an animal is.
13. (mcq) What are neurons?  
   Options: Tiny messenger cells that pass electric signals / Bones in the head / Drops of blood  
   **Answer:** Tiny messenger cells that pass electric signals. *Why:* Neurons pass tiny signals made by charged atoms moving. In a brain, every thought and memory comes with neurons signalling.

## Applications
- Dog trainers and guide-dog schools use reward training, the same learning seen in the Training Game.
- Police and army dogs sniff out bombs and lost people, using learning and memory.
- Zoos give animals puzzle feeders so their minds stay busy and they stay healthy.
- Farmers who understand animal feelings keep calmer, healthier cows and hens.
- Scientists use blind tests, from the Clever Hans lesson, when testing medicines on people too.
- Robot makers study octopus arms to build soft robot arms that can grip things gently.
- Laws in India, like the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, protect animals because they can suffer.
- Doctors study animal memory to understand memory loss in people.
- Elephants follow old routes they remember, so wildlife workers protect those routes as safe corridors between forests.
- Bird counts, like those run by Bird Count India, depend on people who watch birds carefully and know their habits.

## Open questions
- **What does it feel like to be a crow, a bee or an octopus?** We can test what animals do, but we cannot get inside their experience. The philosopher Thomas Nagel asked 'What is it like to be a bat?' in 1974, and it is still unanswered.
- **Do fish, crabs and insects feel pain?** They act in pain-like ways in some tests, but their brains are very different from ours. Scientists still argue.
- **Why do animals play?** There are several good ideas: practice, friendship, coping with surprises, and fun. Nobody knows how much each one matters.
- **How do tiny neurons passing signals become a feeling or a memory?** We can see neurons fire, but nobody knows how that becomes the feeling of being happy. This is called the hard problem of consciousness.
- **Which animals know themselves?** The mirror test suits animals that use eyes. Scientists are still looking for fair tests for animals that live by smell or sound.
- **Did dogs become good at reading people because of their genes, or by growing up with us?** Tests of wolves, pet dogs and street dogs give different answers. Probably both matter.
- **How did intelligence grow up separately in crows, octopuses and apes?** Their brains are built very differently, yet all solve problems. Scientists are still working out what they share.

## Connected maths
- In the thirsty crow test, it took 6 big stones to raise the water to the line. It took 32 small stones. How many more small stones than big stones were needed? **26 more small stones.**. 32 take away 6 is 26. So 26 more small stones were needed. You can check: 6 + 26 = 32. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In the Clever Hans test, Asha got 8 right out of 10 when her helper knew, and 3 right out of 10 in the blind round. What fraction did she get right in each round? **8 out of 10, and 3 out of 10.**. In the first round, 8 out of 10 tries were right. That is 8 tenths. In the blind round, 3 out of 10 were right. That is 3 tenths. 3 tenths is about what lucky guessing gives with 3 cups. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- In the Crow Friends project, the first crow came after 22 minutes on day 1, and after 3 minutes on day 7. How much sooner did it come? **19 minutes sooner.**. 22 take away 3 is 19. So the crow came 19 minutes sooner on day 7. You can check: 3 + 19 = 22. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In Be a Jay, Rohan hid 8 groundnuts. The next day he found 6. What fraction did he find? How many did he forget? **He found 6 out of 8, which is three quarters. He forgot 2.**. 8 take away 6 is 2, so he forgot 2. 6 out of 8 is the same as 3 out of 4, because 6 and 8 can both be halved. So he found three quarters. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- In the pointing test, a dog chose the right bowl 8 times out of 10 with pointing, and 5 times out of 10 with no pointing. Draw a tally for each round. How many more right choices did pointing give? **3 more right choices.**. With pointing, the tally is 8 marks. With no pointing, it is 5 marks. 8 take away 5 is 3. With 2 bowls, a lucky guess is right about 5 times in 10, so the no-pointing score is about luck. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
