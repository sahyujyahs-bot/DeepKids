# Caring for people who need help: ramps, aids, and how to offer help with respect
`sci.community.caring-for-others.L2`

**Summary.** A girl in a wheelchair reaches a doorway with one step, and she cannot get in. Is the trouble only in her legs, or is some of it in the step? In this lesson you test how a ramp makes a climb easier, and you meet the tools that help people see, hear and move. You find out how to offer help so that the other person stays in charge. You also ask why humans and other animals look after their old, their sick and their hurt at all.

**Intro.** *Think of the step at a shop door. Four people arrive at it. One is a child with a heavy bag. One is a grandmother with a stick. One is a parent with a pram. One uses a wheelchair. Who gets in easily, and who has to stop? Who was the step built for?* A step is a small thing. Whoever drew the plan for the door was probably thinking of a person who walks in with free hands. But look at who really comes to a door: people of every age, carrying and pushing all kinds of things. Some people live every day with a body or a mind that works differently from most. And every one of us will need help from other people at some time. Today you find out what really helps, and what only looks like help.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that everyone needs help at some time in life, and give three examples.
1. I can explain how a ramp lets a wheelchair go where a step stops it, and why a longer ramp is easier.
2. I can name four aids, such as Braille, a white cane, a hearing aid and the Jaipur Foot, and say what each does.
3. I can show how to offer help: ask first, listen, and do what the person says.
4. I can say what dignity means and give one way to respect it.
5. I can give two ways to care for an old person and two ways to care for a sick person.
6. I can give three ways to care for animals, and say why they need care.
7. I can tell one way that a ramp or other aid made for some people turns out to help everybody.

## Terms
- **help**: To help is to do something so that another person can do what they need to do. *e.g.* You hold a door open for someone who is carrying two bags.
- **care**: To care is to notice what someone needs and to do what you can. *e.g.* You bring water to a friend who has a fever and sit with her.
- **concern**: Concern is the feeling that makes you want to find out if someone is all right. *e.g.* You see a child crying in the playground and ask, 'Are you all right?'
- **need**: A need is something a person must have. Water, rest and a way in through a door are needs. *e.g.* A baby needs milk. A wheelchair user needs a ramp at the door.
- **disability**: A disability is a lasting difference in how a body, the senses or a mind work. In a world built for other bodies, it makes some everyday jobs hard. *e.g.* A person who cannot see needs a way to read signs. A person who cannot walk far needs a lift.
- **disabled person**: A disabled person is a person who lives with a disability. Many people use this plain name for themselves. *e.g.* Avani Lekhara is a champion shooter. She is also a person who uses a wheelchair.
- **person with a disability**: A person with a disability is a person first, who also lives with a disability. India's law and the United Nations agreement use these words. *e.g.* India's law of 2016 is called the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act.
- **differently abled**: Differently abled is a word used in many school books for people with disabilities. Some people like it and some people do not. *e.g.* If you meet someone, ask which word they like, or just use their name.
- **wheelchair**: A wheelchair is a chair on wheels. A person turns the wheels by hand, or uses a motor, or gets a push from someone else. *e.g.* A wheelchair rolls easily across a flat floor and stops at a high step.
- **wheelchair user**: A wheelchair user is a person who gets about in a wheelchair. The chair is how the person travels, not who the person is. *e.g.* A wheelchair user can be a teacher, a doctor, a cricketer or a child in your class.
- **step**: A step is a small rise in height, such as the edge of a doorway. Wheels cannot roll up a step. *e.g.* A shop door with one step that is about 15 centimetres high.
- **ramp**: A ramp is a sloping surface that joins a lower place to a higher place, so that wheels can roll up. *e.g.* A ramp at a school gate lets a wheelchair, a pram and a trolley go in.
- **slope**: A slope tells how fast the ground goes up. A slope of 1 in 12 goes up 1 centimetre for every 12 centimetres along. *e.g.* A ramp that goes up 10 centimetres over 120 centimetres has a slope of 1 in 12.
- **steep**: A steep slope goes up very quickly over a short way, so it is hard to climb. *e.g.* A short plank leaned on a high step is steep, and a loaded cart is hard to pull up it.
- **gentle**: A gentle slope goes up slowly over a long way, so it is easy to climb. *e.g.* A long plank on a low step is a gentle slope, and a loaded cart rolls up with a small pull.
- **push**: A push moves a thing away from you. How hard you push is how much force you use. *e.g.* You push a trolley up a ramp. The longer the ramp, the smaller the push you need.
- **friction**: Friction is the rubbing grip between two surfaces that makes sliding harder. *e.g.* Rough doormats have more friction than wet tiles, so feet slip less.
- **handrail**: A handrail is a bar along a ramp or a stair that you can hold for a steady grip. *e.g.* An old person holds the handrail beside the temple steps.
- **barrier**: A barrier is anything that stops a person from getting in or taking part. A barrier can be a step, a sign nobody can read, or a rule. *e.g.* A shop with only steps at the door is a barrier for a wheelchair and for a pram.
- **universal design**: Universal design means making a building or a thing so that as many people as possible can use it from the start. *e.g.* A lever door handle can be opened by a hand, an elbow or a child.
- **aid**: An aid is a tool that helps a person do a job, such as spectacles, a hearing aid, a cane or a ramp. *e.g.* Spectacles are an aid that many people wear every day.
- **blind**: A blind person cannot see, or sees only a little. A blind person knows the world with touch, hearing, smell and taste. *e.g.* A blind person reads a book with her fingers and finds her way with a cane.
- **Braille**: Braille is a way of writing with raised dots that fingers read. Each letter is a small pattern of up to six dots. *e.g.* Some lift buttons and medicine boxes carry Braille.
- **white cane**: A white cane is a long light stick that a blind person sweeps along the ground to find steps, poles and holes ahead. *e.g.* The cane taps the edge of the pavement before the foot reaches it.
- **tactile tiles**: Tactile tiles are bumpy tiles on the ground that feet and canes can feel. Lines mean 'go on' and dots mean 'stop, the edge is ahead'. *e.g.* Yellow bumpy tiles run along the edge of a railway platform.
- **deaf**: A deaf person cannot hear, or hears only a little. Many deaf people talk with sign language. *e.g.* A deaf girl at school talks with her friends by signing.
- **sign language**: A sign language is a full language made with the hands, the face and the body. It has its own words and its own grammar. *e.g.* Indian Sign Language is different from American Sign Language, as Tamil is different from English.
- **hearing aid**: A hearing aid is a small machine worn at the ear that makes sounds louder. *e.g.* Your grandfather's hearing aid helps him hear you across the room.
- **cochlear implant**: A cochlear implant is a device placed inside the ear by an operation. It sends sound signals straight to the nerve for hearing. *e.g.* A child with an implant needs years of practice to make sense of the sounds it gives.
- **artificial limb**: An artificial limb is a made leg or arm that takes the place of a limb that was lost. *e.g.* A woman walks to the market wearing an artificial leg.
- **Jaipur Foot**: The Jaipur Foot is an artificial foot and lower leg made in Jaipur in 1968. It bends so that the wearer can squat, sit cross-legged and walk barefoot on rough ground. *e.g.* A farmer wearing a Jaipur Foot walks across a wet field.
- **echo**: An echo is a sound that bounces off a wall or a hill and comes back to you. *e.g.* You shout in an empty hall and hear your own voice come back.
- **elder**: An elder is an older person. Elders carry what a family and a village know. *e.g.* Your neighbour, who remembers how the festival was kept long ago, is an elder.
- **old age**: Old age is the part of life when the body changes slowly. Eyes, ears, bones and balance change, and a person usually has the most to tell. *e.g.* In old age a person may need a stick, a light at night and a little more time on the stairs.
- **balance**: Balance is the body's way of staying upright without falling. *e.g.* On a wet floor your balance is tested, and an old person's balance is tested more.
- **sick**: A sick person is a person whose body is fighting a germ or mending a hurt, so the person has less strength than usual. *e.g.* A child with a fever is sick and needs rest and water.
- **germ**: A germ is a living thing too small to see that can make a person ill. Soap and water wash many germs away. *e.g.* Washing your hands with soap after helping a sick friend stops germs passing on.
- **rest**: Rest is quiet time for the body to get well, such as lying down or sleeping. *e.g.* A sick child rests in a quiet, airy room.
- **company**: Company is another person being with you. To keep someone company is to sit with them. *e.g.* You sit beside a sick friend and read aloud, which gives her company.
- **ask first**: To ask first is to say 'Would you like some help?' before you help, and to wait for the answer. *e.g.* You ask a neighbour, 'May I carry this bag?' and wait.
- **consent**: Consent is a person's own yes or no. Help without consent is not good help. *e.g.* A person says 'No, thank you', and you say 'All right' and stop.
- **dignity**: Dignity is the worth that every person has just by being a person. *e.g.* Talking to a person, and not over her head to the friend who came with her, respects her dignity.
- **respect**: To respect someone is to treat that person as an equal who makes their own choices. *e.g.* You wait for a grandmother to finish her sentence before you speak.
- **pity**: Pity is feeling sorry for someone from above, as if they were smaller than you. It is different from respect. *e.g.* 'Poor thing' is pity. 'What are you reading?' is respect.
- **right**: A right is something every person is owed, so nobody needs to say thank you for it. *e.g.* Every child has the right to go to school, and a ramp is how some children get in.
- **charity**: Charity is giving help or money to people because we are kind. Charity is good, but it is not the same as a right. *e.g.* A person gives a coin to someone out of kindness. That is charity. A ramp at every school is a right.
- **skeleton**: A skeleton is all the bones of a body fitted together. *e.g.* Old skeletons show doctors what hurt a person long ago and how long she lived afterwards.
- **Neanderthal**: A Neanderthal was a kind of human who lived long ago and no longer exists. They lived in Europe and Asia. *e.g.* A Neanderthal man lived at Shanidar, in Iraq, about 45,000 years ago.
- **rescue**: To rescue is to bring a hurt or trapped being to safety. *e.g.* A rat in a lab frees another rat from a closed cage.
- **pain**: Pain is an unpleasant feeling that usually comes when the body is hurt. It makes us pull away and protect the hurt part. *e.g.* A dog with a thorn in her paw limps and licks it.
- **cruelty**: Cruelty is hurting a being on purpose, or not caring that you hurt them. *e.g.* Throwing stones at a street dog is cruelty.
- **compassion**: Compassion is feeling someone's trouble as if it were partly yours, and wanting to help. *e.g.* You feel for a thirsty squirrel and put out a bowl of water.
- **care work**: Care work is the daily work of feeding, washing, lifting, watching over and comforting people who cannot do these things alone. It takes skill and patience. *e.g.* Cooking soft food for a grandfather and reminding him about his medicine is care work.
- **carer**: A carer is a person who looks after another person for a long time. Carers get tired and need care too. *e.g.* A nurse, an ayah or a daughter looking after her mother is a carer.
- **vaccine**: A vaccine is a medicine, often a small injection or drops, that helps the body get ready to fight germs before they arrive. *e.g.* Polio drops given to children helped India stop polio. The last case in India was found in 2011.
- **evolution**: Evolution is the slow change of living things over many, many generations, as those who do well in their surroundings leave more young. *e.g.* Over millions of years, the ancestors of whales, who once walked on land, became animals of the sea.

## Explanations
### Every person needs help, as a baby, when hurt or ill, and often in old age · *defined*
**Definition:** To need help is to be unable to do something alone that one needs to do. Every person is in this position as a baby, when ill or hurt, and usually in old age.

When you were a baby, you could not feed yourself, walk or keep yourself clean. Someone did all of it for you. Last time you had a fever, someone brought you water. If you break an arm, someone will button your shirt. And if you live to be old, you may need a hand on the stairs. So needing help is not a rare thing that happens to a few unlucky people. It is part of every human life. Some people need a certain kind of help for a short time, and some for all their lives.
- e.g. A person carrying a sleeping child and two bags needs a door held open.
- e.g. A visitor from another state who cannot read the bus board needs someone to read it.
- e.g. A newborn calf is licked dry and nudged to stand by the mother.
- **Eva Feder Kittay, a philosopher and the mother of a disabled daughter, in her book of 1999:** Everyone is 'some mother's child'. Every person alive was once kept alive by someone else's work. She argues that needing others is the normal human state, and standing alone is the short exception.
- **The usual picture in stories and advertisements:** The ideal person is strong and needs nobody, and people who need help are a separate group. This picture is common, and it leaves out everyone's babyhood, illnesses and old age.
### Bodies and minds come in many kinds, and about one person in six lives with a disability · *defined*
**Definition:** A disability is a lasting difference in how a person's body, senses or mind work. It makes some everyday activities hard in the world as it is built.

Some people cannot see, or see only a little. Some cannot hear. Some cannot walk, or have lost a hand or a leg. Some learn, speak or understand people in a different way. Some were born so, and others became so after an illness or an accident. In 2022 the World Health Organization reported that roughly 130 crore people live with a disability that matters in daily life. That is one in every six people on Earth. India's 2011 census counted 2.68 crore. Many people who study this think the true number in India is far higher.
- e.g. Wearing spectacles is so common that nobody calls it a disability, though without them many people could not read a board.
- e.g. Avani Lekhara of Jaipur, who uses a wheelchair, won gold medals in shooting at the Paralympic Games of 2021 and 2024.
- e.g. Not every disability can be seen. A person may look like everyone else and still find loud noise or a crowd very hard to bear.
### Which word is right: disabled, differently abled or divyang? People disagree · *debated*
**Definition:** Several words are used in India for people with disabilities. The people described do not all prefer the same one.

Words matter, because a word shows how you see a person. Old words such as 'handicapped' or 'lame' are hurtful, so we avoid them. Your school book may say 'differently abled'. The Government of India has used 'divyang', meaning 'one with a divine body part', since 2016. India's law says 'persons with disabilities'. Many people say plainly 'disabled person'. Others say 'person with a disability', which puts the person first. Which is right? The people themselves do not agree. So the best rule is the simplest one. Use the person's name. If you need a word, use the word that person uses.
- e.g. Many people who use sign language write Deaf with a capital D, to show they belong to a community with its own language.
- e.g. A person who uses a wheelchair is a 'wheelchair user'. The chair is how that person gets about.
- **Those who prefer 'differently abled' or 'divyang':** These words look at what a person can do. They were chosen to be kind and to replace words that were used as insults.
- **Many disabled people's groups in India, including the National Platform for the Rights of the Disabled, in 2016:** They objected to 'divyang'. They said a sweet word changes nothing about the missing ramps and the missing jobs. It also suggests that disabled people are special beings, when they want to be treated as ordinary citizens.
- **Mike Oliver and other writers of the social model in Britain, and many disabled activists:** They say 'disabled' is a plain fact and nothing to hide. Many add that they are disabled by buildings and attitudes, and the word reminds everyone of that.
- **The United Nations agreement of 2006, India's law of 2016, and People First groups started by people with learning disabilities:** They say 'person with a disability' puts the person first and the disability second. A person is never only a disability. Many families and many self-advocacy groups prefer these words, and some people are happy with both.
### Is the problem in the person's body, or in the way the world is built? · *debated*
**Definition:** The medical view says a disability is a fault in a body that medicine should mend. The social view says people are disabled by steps, attitudes and rules that were made without them in mind.

Picture a woman who uses a wheelchair, at a shop with one step. She cannot enter. Why not? One answer: because her legs do not work. Another answer: because the shop has a step. Put a ramp there and she goes in like anyone else. Her legs are the same as before. In the 1970s disabled people in Britain said this clearly for the first time. They said, 'Our bodies are different. What shuts us out is how things are built.' Both views hold some truth, and people still argue about how much.
- e.g. In a village where almost everyone knows some sign language, a deaf person can talk with anyone.
- e.g. A left-handed child with right-handed scissors struggles. With left-handed scissors the trouble is gone.
- e.g. If every door handle were two metres high, most adults would be 'disabled' and tall basketball players would not.
- **Doctors, in the medical view:** Many conditions bring pain or illness that no ramp can remove. Medicine has given people eye operations, hearing aids, vaccines against polio and artificial limbs. Treating the body is real help.
- **The Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation, Britain, 1976, and Mike Oliver, a wheelchair user who named the 'social model' in 1983:** A body's difference is one thing. Being shut out is another, and it is done by society. Change the buildings, the buses and the attitudes, and most of the disability goes.
- **Tom Shakespeare, a disabled scholar, in 2006:** Both are true together. Some difficulties come from the body and some from the world. A good society removes the barriers and also offers the medicine, and lets the person choose.
### A ramp turns one hard lift into a long, gentle push · *defined*
**Definition:** A ramp is a sloping surface that joins a lower level to a higher one. The longer and gentler the slope, the smaller the push needed to go up it.

To get a wheelchair up a step, you must lift the whole weight of the chair and the person straight up. That may be 80 kilograms. On a ramp you never lift it all at once. You push it along a slope, and the ramp carries most of the weight. You travel further, but each moment is easy. Make the ramp longer for the same height, and the push gets smaller still. A ramp gives you an easy push in exchange for a longer path.
- e.g. Workers roll heavy drums up a plank into a lorry. They could not lift the drums straight up.
- e.g. A road up to Ooty or Darjeeling winds back and forth. That makes the road longer and the climb gentle enough for a bus.
- e.g. The builders of the great temple at Thanjavur, about 1,000 years ago, are thought to have hauled an 80-tonne capstone up a very long earth ramp.
### A wheelchair ramp must rise no more than 1 for every 12 along · *defined*
**Definition:** India's building guidelines say a wheelchair ramp should have a slope of 1 in 12 or gentler. That means at least 12 centimetres of length for every 1 centimetre of height.

How gentle is gentle enough? People tested it. A person turning the wheels with their own arms tires fast on a steep slope, and the chair can tip backwards. So India's guidelines give a number: 1 in 12. If a doorway is 30 centimetres above the road, the ramp must be at least 360 centimetres long. That is about the length of a small car. A steep ramp squeezed into a small space looks like help. For someone alone in a wheelchair, it can be as useless as stairs. A good ramp also has a handrail, a rough surface and a flat place to rest.
- e.g. Two ordinary steps are about 30 centimetres high. Measure 360 centimetres on your floor to see how much room a proper ramp needs.
- e.g. Many ramps outside shops are much steeper than 1 in 12. Try pushing a loaded trolley up one.
### The wheelchair was improved again and again by the people who used it · *defined*
**Definition:** A wheelchair is a chair on wheels that a person moves by turning large hand rims, by a motor, or with a push from someone else.

A wheel rolls, and rolling takes far less push than dragging. Put a chair on wheels and a person who cannot walk can travel. A stone carving from China, made about 1,500 years ago, shows a wheeled chair. In 1655 CE Stephan Farffler, a watchmaker in Germany who could not use his legs, built himself a chair that he drove with hand cranks. In 1933 Herbert Everest, an American engineer who had broken his back, built a light steel chair with his friend Harry Jennings. It folded to fit in a car. The people who needed the tool kept making it better.
- e.g. The big wheel of a wheelchair has a second, smaller ring fixed to it. The user pushes this ring, so the hands stay clean.
- e.g. Sports wheelchairs for basketball and tennis have wheels that lean inward, which lets them turn sharply without tipping.
### A ramp made for wheelchairs helps almost everyone · *defined*
**Definition:** Universal design means making buildings and things so that as many people as possible can use them from the start, without special arrangements.

In the early 1970s students who used wheelchairs in Berkeley, a city in the USA, asked the city for slopes at the edges of its footpaths. The city cut them. Their leader was Ed Roberts. Then something happened that nobody had planned. Parents with prams used the slopes. So did people pulling suitcases, workers with handcarts, cyclists and old people with sticks. A change made for a few turned out to help most. Ronald Mace, an architect who used a wheelchair, gave this approach a name in the 1980s: universal design. Design for the person with the most difficulty, and it works for everyone.
- e.g. At a railway station, count who uses the ramp or the lift in ten minutes. Most will not be in wheelchairs.
- e.g. Words printed at the bottom of a video were made for deaf viewers. Now people use them in noisy buses and to learn languages.
- e.g. A lever door handle, made for hands that cannot grip a round knob, can be opened with an elbow when your hands are full.
- **Ronald Mace and designers who follow him:** Planning for everyone at the start costs little. Adding a ramp afterwards costs much more and often gives a poor ramp at the back door.
- **Some disabled writers, such as Aimi Hamraie in 2017:** Saying 'it helps everyone' can push disabled people out of the picture again. A ramp is owed to wheelchair users as a right, even if no one else ever used it.
### Braille lets fingers read, and it was invented by a blind boy of fifteen · *defined*
**Definition:** Braille is a way of writing with raised dots that are read by touch. Each letter is a pattern of up to six dots in a cell small enough to fit under one fingertip.

Run a finger over a strip of tablets from the chemist. You may feel small bumps. That is Braille, a way of writing for people who cannot see. Each letter is a pattern of raised dots in a small block, two dots wide and three dots high. Six dots can make 63 different patterns. That is enough for letters, numbers and music. Louis Braille of France lost his sight as a small child. In 1824, when he was fifteen, he worked out this system. Bharati Braille, agreed in 1951, uses the same six dots for the languages of India.
- e.g. Lift buttons, medicine boxes and some ATM keys in India carry Braille.
- e.g. Indian bank notes have raised marks near the edge, a different shape for each value, so that they can be told apart by touch.
- e.g. A white cane swept ahead along the ground tells a blind walker about steps, poles and holes.
- e.g. The yellow bumpy tiles on station platforms were invented by Seiichi Miyake in Japan in 1965. Feet and canes read them: lines mean 'go on' and dots mean 'stop, edge ahead'.
### A sign language is a full language made with the hands, face and body · *defined*
**Definition:** A sign language uses shapes and movements of the hands, together with the face and body, in place of spoken sounds. It has its own words and its own grammar.

Many deaf people talk with their hands. For a long time, hearing people thought signing was only pointing and acting, a poor copy of speech. In 1960 William Stokoe studied American Sign Language closely. He showed that signs are built from parts, the way spoken words are built from sounds. A sign language can tell jokes, make poems and argue about anything. It is not the same everywhere. Indian Sign Language is different from American. Some deaf people also use a hearing aid, a small machine worn at the ear that makes sounds louder.
- e.g. In Nicaragua in the 1980s, deaf children brought together in new schools made a new sign language among themselves within a few years. No adult taught it to them.
- e.g. India set up a national centre for Indian Sign Language in New Delhi in 2015, and it has published a dictionary of thousands of signs.
- e.g. Babies who see sign language from birth 'babble' with their hands.
- **William Stokoe, and scientists of language after him:** Sign languages are complete human languages. The brain handles them in the same areas that handle speech.
- **Teachers at a meeting in Milan, Italy, in 1880:** They voted that deaf children should be taught only to speak and read lips, and signing was banned in many schools for about a hundred years. Almost all the voters could hear. Deaf adults today call this a great wrong that kept language from children.
### Is deafness something to be fixed? Deaf people and doctors do not all agree · *debated*
**Definition:** A cochlear implant is a device placed inside the ear by surgery that sends sound signals straight to the hearing nerve. Whether deaf children should receive one is debated within the Deaf community.

Doctors can now put a small device inside the ear of a deaf child. With training, many such children learn to hear speech and to speak. To most hearing people this looks like a plain good. Many Deaf adults see it differently. They say, 'We are not broken. We have a language and a community. Why must our children be changed to fit yours?' Others in the same community welcome the device. Many families now choose both: the implant, and sign language as well.
- e.g. A child with an implant hears something, but not what a hearing child hears, and needs years of practice.
- e.g. Some Deaf parents are glad when their baby is born deaf, because the child will share their language.
- **Ear doctors and many parents:** Nine in ten deaf children are born to hearing parents who do not know sign language. An implant given early lets the child share the family's speech and attend any school.
- **The National Association of the Deaf in the USA, in 1991:** It called implants in children an experiment done without the child's consent, and a threat to Deaf culture. In 2000 the Association softened this. It accepted families' choice and urged that sign language be taught too.
- **Scientists who study how children learn language:** What harms a child is having no full language at all in the first years. A child who gets rich language early, signed or spoken or both, does well.
### The Jaipur Foot: a leg designed for the way people in India really live · *defined*
**Definition:** The Jaipur Foot is an artificial foot and lower leg made of rubber, wood and plastic. It was developed in Jaipur in 1968. The wearer can squat, sit cross-legged and walk barefoot on rough ground.

An artificial leg replaces a leg lost to an accident or illness. The ones made abroad in the 1960s were costly and stiff. They were built to be worn with shoes on flat floors. But people in India sit on the floor, squat, walk barefoot and work in wet fields. In Jaipur a master craftsman named Ram Chandra Sharma, who had only a few years of schooling, had an idea. He thought of making a foot from rubber, as a cycle tyre is mended. He and Dr P. K. Sethi built it in 1968. It bends like a real foot and costs very little. Lakhs of people now walk on it.
- e.g. The dancer Sudha Chandran lost a leg after an accident in 1981 and returned to the Bharatanatyam stage wearing a Jaipur Foot.
- e.g. A charity started in Jaipur in 1975 fits the limb free of charge, and it has been sent to people in many countries hurt by wars.
- e.g. Arunima Sinha climbed Mount Everest in 2013 on an artificial leg.
### When one sense is missing, the brain puts that area to other work · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain can change how it uses its parts. In people blind from early life, the area that usually handles sight becomes active during touch and hearing.

At the back of your head is a part of the brain that handles what your eyes see. What does that part do in a person who was born blind? It does not sit idle. In 1996 Norihiro Sadato and his team took pictures of the working brain of blind people as they read Braille. The 'seeing' part was busy, working on touch. Some blind people make sharp clicks with the tongue and listen to the echoes. In this way they can tell where a wall, a tree or a parked car is. So a blind person's world is built differently. It is a full world all the same.
- e.g. Bats and dolphins find their way by echoes all the time.
- e.g. Some studies find that many deaf people notice movement at the edge of their sight more quickly than hearing people do.
- e.g. Close your eyes while someone taps a steel tumbler in different corners of the room. You can already point to the sound.
- **Lore Thaler and colleagues in Canada, in 2011:** They recorded the clicks and echoes used by two blind men and played them back while taking pictures of the brain. The echoes switched on the area normally used for sight. The men were, in a real sense, seeing with sound.
- **Blind writers such as Georgina Kleege:** Stories of blind people with amazing powers of hearing are a trap. Most blind people simply pay careful attention with ordinary ears, as anyone could learn to do.
### Growing old changes the eyes, ears, bones and balance, and old people hold what a family knows · *defined*
**Definition:** Ageing brings slow changes: the eye's lens stiffens, high sounds are lost, bones become lighter and break more easily, and balance and walking slow down.

After about forty, the clear lens inside the eye gets stiff. Close things blur, and people hold the newspaper at arm's length. Later the ears lose high, soft sounds, so speech in a noisy room is hard to follow. Bones get lighter and break more easily. Balance is less sure, so a wet floor is a real danger. None of this changes who the person is. India has more than 10 crore people over sixty. They carry recipes, songs, cures, family stories and the memory of how things were done.
- e.g. Speak a little slower and face an old person when you talk. Shouting does not help, because it bends the sounds.
- e.g. A handrail, a light left on at night and a mat that does not slip prevent many falls.
- e.g. Ask an old person how a festival was kept when they were small. You will hear things written in no book.
- **Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11:** A teacher's parting words to students include: treat your mother as divine, your father as divine, your teacher as divine and your guest as divine. Care for elders is given as a duty that comes with learning.
- **Thiruvalluvar, Thirukkural chapter 45, 'Seeking the help of the great', written about 1,500 to 2,000 years ago:** The poet treats the wise and experienced as a treasure that a sensible person seeks out and keeps close. Elders here are a source of strength for the young, more than a burden to be carried.
- **Doctors who study ageing:** Old people who stay useful and connected stay healthier. Being cut off from others harms an old body about as much as a serious illness does.
### A sick person needs rest, water, food and company, and care must not spread the illness · *defined*
**Definition:** Caring for a sick person means meeting the needs the person cannot meet alone for a while, and stopping germs from passing to others.

A sick body is busy. It is fighting germs or mending an injury, and has little strength left for other things. So others bring water, light food and medicine at the right time. They keep the room quiet and aired. Company matters as well. Sitting with someone and holding a hand has been shown to make pain and fear smaller. Some illnesses pass from person to person. So wash your hands with soap after helping. The sick person can cover a cough and use a separate towel. Caring and keeping safe go together.
- e.g. A child can fetch water, read a story aloud, and keep the noise down.
- e.g. A drawing or a phone call reaches a sick person in hospital when a visit is not allowed.
- e.g. A person with a long illness is still the same person. They want to hear the news and be asked their opinion.
- **The Buddha, in the Vinaya, Mahavagga 8.26:** The Buddha found a monk lying ill and dirty, left alone by the others. He washed the monk with his own hands, with Ananda's help. Then he told the community: whoever would look after me should look after the sick.
- **James Coan and colleagues in the USA, in 2006:** They took pictures of the brain of women who expected a small electric shock. When a woman held her husband's hand, the parts of her brain that react to danger were much quieter. A stranger's hand helped a little.
- **Nurses, following Florence Nightingale's 'Notes on Nursing' of 1859:** Fresh air, clean water, light, quiet and cleanliness do much of the healing. Nightingale also warned visitors against chatter that tires the patient. A good nurse watches what the sick person needs.
### To offer help well: ask first, listen, and do what the person says · *defined*
**Definition:** Help is good help when the person helped stays in charge. That means asking before acting, waiting for the answer, and accepting a 'no'.

Imagine someone grabbing your school bag and pulling you along because they thought you were slow. That is how it feels to be 'helped' without being asked. So the first rule is simple. Ask, 'Would you like some help?' Then wait. If the answer is yes, ask, 'How shall I do it?' The person knows their own body best. If the answer is no, say 'all right' and mean it. Talk to the person, and never over their head to whoever is with them.
- e.g. Do not push a wheelchair or lean on it unless asked. To the user, the chair is part of their own space.
- e.g. To guide a blind person, offer your elbow and let them hold it. Never pull them by the hand or the cane.
- e.g. With a deaf person, stand where your face is in the light and speak normally, or write it down.
- e.g. A guide dog in harness is working. Do not pet or feed them without asking the person.
- **Disabled people's organisations worldwide:** Their slogan since the 1990s is 'Nothing about us without us'. It was spread by activists in South Africa and by James Charlton's book of 1998. Decisions about a person's life must be made with that person.
- **Some families and carers:** Sometimes there is no time to ask, as when someone is about to fall. And some people cannot say what they want in words. Then the carer must act, and watch closely for what the person shows by face and body.
### Dignity: help is a right that people are owed, and pity gets in the way · *defined*
**Definition:** Dignity is the worth every person has simply by being a person. To respect it is to treat someone as an equal who decides for themselves.

There are two ways of thinking about help. In the first, a kind person gives and a poor thing receives. The giver feels good, and the other must be grateful. In the second, every person has the same right to enter a school, catch a bus and earn a living. If a step is in the way, the step must go, and nobody needs to say thank you for it. India's law took the second way in 2016. It says schools, offices, buses and websites must be usable by disabled people.
- e.g. 'Poor thing' said about someone in their hearing is not kindness. Asking 'What are you reading?' is.
- e.g. A ramp at the front door says 'you belong here'. A ramp at the back beside the dustbins says something else.
- e.g. Javed Abidi, a wheelchair user, led the campaign in the 1990s that won India's first disability law and had disabled people counted in the census.
- **The tradition of seva and daan, found in every religion in India:** Serving those in need is among the highest acts, and millions are fed, housed and treated because of it. At its best the one who serves is taught to see the other as their equal or their better.
- **The disability rights movement, and the United Nations agreement of 2006:** Charity depends on someone's mood and can be taken away. A right cannot. Disabled people are citizens who are owed access, and should not have to wait for gifts.
- **An honest look at old law books, such as the Manusmriti, 9.201:** This text bars people born blind or deaf, and others, from a share of family property, though it says they must be fed and clothed. Some old texts also explain disability as the fruit of deeds in a past life. Disabled people today reject that idea as blame laid on the person.
### Old bones show that people cared for the sick and hurt tens of thousands of years ago · *defined*
**Definition:** Skeletons of ancient people sometimes show serious injuries or conditions that had healed or lasted for years, which suggests that others fed and protected the person.

How long have people looked after each other? Bones can tell us. In a cave at Shanidar in Iraq lay the skeleton of a Neanderthal man. He lived about 45,000 years ago. Neanderthals were a kind of human who no longer exist. One arm was withered and useless. He had been hit hard on the head, was probably blind in one eye and partly deaf, and walked with a limp. Yet the bones show that he lived for many years after these injuries. He could hardly have hunted. Most likely someone shared food with him for years.
- e.g. At Man Bac in Vietnam, Lorna Tilley studied a young man buried about 4,000 years ago. His bones show he could not move his arms or legs from boyhood, and he lived about ten years more. Others must have fed, washed and turned him every day.
- e.g. In 2011 Tilley and her colleague Marc Oxenham set out a careful way to read such bones. It is now called the bioarchaeology of care.
- **Erik Trinkaus, who studied the Shanidar bones, and Lorna Tilley:** A person so badly hurt could not have lived alone. The healed bones are direct evidence of help given over years, and so of care.
- **Katherine Dettwyler, an anthropologist, in 1991:** Bones show that a person survived. They cannot show kindness. The person may have done useful work in other ways, or been treated badly. We should not read our own feelings into a skeleton.
### Other animals care for their hurt and sick too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals have been seen to rescue, feed, clean or stay beside an injured or trapped member of their group.

Humans are not the only animals who help. In Africa, Matabele ants raid termite nests, and some ants lose legs in the fight. Their nestmates carry them home. There the others lick the wounds, which stops infection. Most of the carried ants live to raid again. Elephants stand around a fallen herd member and try to lift them with their tusks. In a laboratory, a rat will work to open a cage and free another rat who is trapped inside. The rat keeps working even when chocolate is on offer in a second cage. Caring runs wide through the animal world.
- e.g. Vampire bats who have fed share blood with a hungry roost-mate, who would otherwise starve within about three days.
- e.g. Dolphins have been seen holding a sick dolphin up at the surface so that they can breathe.
- e.g. Crows gather noisily around a dead or injured crow.
- **Erik Frank, who studied Matabele ants in 2017 and 2023:** Injured ants call for help with a chemical signal. Badly hurt ants do not call and refuse rescue. The helpers treat wounds with a substance from their own bodies that kills germs.
- **Inbal Ben-Ami Bartal, Jean Decety and Peggy Mason, in 2011:** Rats freed trapped companions and often shared the chocolate afterwards. The team concluded that rats feel another's distress and act to end it.
- **Alan Silberberg and colleagues, in 2014:** They argued that the rats opened the cage because they wanted company. Some scientists also say ant rescue is a built-in habit that makes the colony stronger, with no feeling needed. This doubt has been raised about humans too.
### Animals need our care, and the same rule holds: notice what they need, and do not force · *defined*
**Definition:** Caring for animals means giving them water, food, shelter and safety where we can, causing them no needless pain or fear, and leaving wild animals free.

The animals around you feel thirst, hunger, pain and fear. In the hot months a street dog, a cow, a squirrel and a sparrow may all be short of water. A bowl kept filled in the shade is real help. So is slowing down for animals on the road, and keeping loud crackers away from them. Do not tease, chase or throw things. And think of the rule for helping people. Ask first. An animal answers with the body. A dog who turns away, tucks the tail or shows teeth is saying no.
- e.g. Put out a shallow clay dish of water on a balcony or wall. Put a stone in it so that bees and small birds can drink safely.
- e.g. A baby bird on the ground is usually being watched by the parents. Leave the bird alone unless a cat is near.
- e.g. Kite strings coated with glass cut thousands of birds every Sankranti. Plain cotton string does far less harm.
- e.g. Plastic bags with food smells are eaten by cows and block their stomachs. Give peels without the bag.
- **Emperor Ashoka, Rock Edict 2, cut in stone in about 257 BCE:** The edict says that he arranged medical treatment for humans and medical treatment for animals across his lands. He had healing plants grown, wells dug and trees planted along the roads for people and other animals to use.
- **Thiruvalluvar, Thirukkural 315:** What use is knowledge to a person who does not treat another being's pain as their own? The verse makes fellow-feeling the test of whether someone has really learned anything.
- **India's Constitution, Article 51A(g), and the law of 1960 against cruelty to animals:** Every citizen has a duty to have compassion for living creatures. In the 1950s the dancer Rukmini Devi Arundale pressed Parliament for the law against cruelty. She then led the board that the law created.
- **Scientists who study wildlife:** Feeding wild animals such as monkeys can harm them. They lose their fear, fight over food and fall ill. For wild beings, care usually means protecting their homes and keeping our distance.
### Do other animals feel pain as we do? Most scientists now say many do, and argue about which · *debated*
**Definition:** Pain is an unpleasant inner feeling that usually comes with harm to the body. A feeling cannot be seen from outside. So scientists use behaviour and the body's wiring as evidence for pain in other animals.

You cannot step inside another person's pain. You believe in it because they cry out and pull away, and because their body is built like yours. The same test can be used for other animals. Dogs, cows, birds and fish all have nerves that carry signals of harm. They guard a hurt part. They learn to avoid what hurt them, and they act normally again when given a painkiller. Animals feel. In 2012, and again in 2024, large groups of scientists signed statements saying the evidence for this is strong. The argument now is about fish, insects and crabs.
- e.g. A dog with a thorn in the paw limps, licks the place and whimpers when it is touched.
- e.g. Hermit crabs given a small shock inside their shell often leave it, but they are slower to leave a shell they like better. That looks like weighing things up, and less like a jerk.
- **Lynne Sneddon, who studied trout from 2003:** Trout have pain nerves in the face. When something that stings was put on their lips, they rubbed their lips on the gravel and stopped feeding. A painkiller brought them back to normal. Fish feel pain.
- **Brian Key, a brain scientist, in 2016:** Fish lack the folded outer layer of the brain that produces the feeling of pain in humans. Their reactions may happen without any inner hurt.
- **The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, signed by hundreds of scientists in 2024:** There is strong support for inner experience in mammals and birds, and a real possibility of it in fish, insects, crabs and octopuses. When there is a real possibility, it is wrong to ignore it.
- **Jain teaching, in the Acharanga Sutra, book 1:** All beings wish to live and none wishes to suffer. So no being should be harmed. Jains count beings with one sense, such as plants, among those who feel. Science has no test that settles where feeling stops.
### Caring is real work, most of it is done by women without pay, and carers need care too · *defined*
**Definition:** Care work is the daily labour of feeding, washing, lifting, watching over and comforting those who cannot do these things alone.

Someone gets up at night for the baby. Someone cooks for the grandfather, washes his clothes and remembers his medicine. This is work. It needs skill, strength and patience. India's government measured it in 2019. On an average day women spent about five hours on unpaid work at home. Men spent about an hour and a half. Nothing in the body makes it so. A boy can spoon kanji as well as a girl. A person who cares for others day after day gets tired too. Sharing the work is care for the carer.
- e.g. Find out who in your home gives the medicines, and who takes an old or sick relative to the doctor.
- e.g. A child can take over one small job for a week, such as filling the water bottles, and see how much time it takes.
- e.g. Nurses, ayahs and hospital attendants do this work for a living, and it is among the lower-paid work in India.
- **Economists such as Nancy Folbre:** Everything else in a country rests on care work, because no one can do any job without first having been raised and fed. It is left out when a country adds up its earnings. That hides the largest job of all.
- **A common belief in many homes:** Women are 'naturally' better at caring. Studies of fathers who do the main caring show their bodies and brains change in the same way mothers' do. Caring is learned by doing it.

## Mostly agreed
- Most disability comes after birth, through illness, accident and old age. *How we know:* Health surveys in India and across the world find that most disabled people were not born so. The share of people with a disability rises steeply with age, and passes one in three among the very old.
- A vaccine ended polio in India, an illness that once left lakhs of children unable to walk. *How we know:* India's last case of wild polio was a two-year-old girl in Howrah, West Bengal, in January 2011. Health workers had given drops to children in every village. In 2014 the World Health Organization declared India free of polio.
- Children who learn beside disabled classmates do as well in their studies as other children. *How we know:* In 2016 Thomas Hehir's team read many school studies together. The marks of children without disabilities did not fall in mixed classrooms. Their attitudes towards people who are different improved.
- Falls are the most common cause of serious injury in old people, and simple changes at home prevent many of them. *How we know:* In trials, some homes were given handrails, better light and non-slip mats. Fewer falls were recorded among the old people in those homes than in homes left as they were.

## How we found out
**Valentin Haüy, Charles Barbier, Louis Braille, 1784 to 1854.** Before the 1800s most people believed that blind children could not be taught to read, or should read the same letters as everyone else. In 1784 Valentin Haüy opened the first school for blind children in Paris. He printed books with ordinary letters pressed up from the page. It seemed sensible, because letters were the only writing that sighted teachers knew. But raised letters are slow to tell apart by touch, the books were enormous, and a child could not write them. In 1821 a soldier named Charles Barbier brought the school a code of raised dots that he had made for passing messages at night. It used up to twelve dots, too many to feel with one touch. A pupil at the school, Louis Braille, had been blind since an accident at the age of three. He worked on the idea for three years. In 1824, aged fifteen, he had a cell of six dots that fits under one fingertip, with patterns for letters, numbers and music. The pupils took to it at once. The sighted teachers did not. One director banned it, and children were punished for using it. They used it in secret. France accepted Braille officially in 1854, two years after Louis Braille had died.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that blind readers should use raised ordinary letters. It was believable because sighted teachers judged by eye, and raised letters looked right to them, though they were poor for fingers.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Humans and other animals help each other only because it helped their own kind of genes to spread.** (William Hamilton in 1964, Robert Trivers in 1971, and Richard Dawkins in 'The Selfish Gene', 1976; *debated*)  
  For: Animals help close relatives most, as the sums predict, and they help unrelated animals who help them back. Vampire bats share blood mostly with bats who shared with them before.  
  Against: People and other animals also help strangers and other species, with no return. Frans de Waal argued that the feeling of care is real in the animal, whatever the distant origin of the feeling. Why a feeling arose does not tell us what the feeling is.
- **Disabled children learn best in separate special schools.** (Many educators through the 1900s, and some parents and Deaf community leaders today; *debated*)  
  For: A special school has trained teachers, Braille or sign language all day, and classmates who share the child's experience. Deaf children in sign-language schools often learn more than those left alone in a hearing classroom.  
  Against: Separate schools kept disabled people out of sight and out of ordinary life. India's 2016 law and the United Nations agreement call for every child to learn in the neighbourhood school, with support brought to them.
- **Empathy, feeling what another feels, is a poor guide to helping well.** (Paul Bloom, a psychologist, in his book 'Against Empathy', 2016; *minority*)  
  For: We feel most for those who are near, look like us or are cute. So empathy sends help to one child in a news story and ignores thousands who are not shown. Calm, fair thinking about what people need does better.  
  Against: Most scientists who study care, such as Frans de Waal, hold that without the feeling nobody would start helping at all. Feeling and thinking are both needed.
- **A disability is the result of a person's own bad deeds in an earlier life.** (A belief found in some old religious and legal texts in India and elsewhere, and still heard today; *rejected*)  
  For: It offered an explanation when no other was known, and in some tellings it urged people to do good.  
  Against: Medicine can trace the causes: a virus, an injury at birth, a change in a gene, an accident. The belief puts blame on the person and has been used to deny them schooling and respect. Disabled people's groups reject it.

## Roots
You have tested a ramp and learned how to offer help. Now we ask what needing and giving help tell us about who we are.

**Deep truth.** A step stopped the loaded toy lorry. A long board let it climb with a small pull. The lorry did not change. So 'cannot' was never inside the lorry alone. It sat between the lorry and the step. People are like this too. What a person can do depends partly on the world that meets them. That world has steps, signs, rules and ways of treating people.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'she can't walk in' or 'he is helpless' as if being unable were a thing inside a person, like the colour of their eyes. That label makes helper and helped look like two kinds of people.  
*Evidence:* In the ramp test the same load needed a big stretch of the rubber band when lifted straight up. It needed a small stretch on the long board. The same lorry was 'unable' and 'able' within a minute, and only the way up had changed.

- *who am i:* You have been carried, fed and taught by others every day of your life. What you can do was built by many hands. So you are not a separate self who sometimes helps. You are someone made by help, who passes it on.
- *what is life:* No living being manages alone. A baby needs a parent, an old elephant needs the herd, a wounded ant needs a nestmate. A life is held up by other lives at its start, at its end, and at many points between.
- *what is this world:* A step or a ramp, a notice in one script or in two: the world we build decides who can take part in it. The world is not finished. Every doorway is a choice about who belongs, and you will help to make those choices.

The lesson's science is small and exact: a longer slope needs a smaller push. It carries a large idea. Ability is something that happens between a body and its surroundings. If that is so, then the line between 'the helpless' and 'the able' is drawn by how we build and behave. It also means that helping is less a gift from one separate person to another than a repair to something that joins them. Many old teachers said that the one who helps and the one helped are not truly two. Some difficulties also begin in the body, and medicine helps with those. Scientists who study evolution, the slow change of living things over many generations, give a different account of helping, and it is set out below.

- **Isha Upanishad, verse 6:** One who sees all beings in their own self, and their own self in all beings, does not shrink away from anyone. (Shrinking away is what people often do on meeting someone whose body or mind is different. The verse says the cure is seeing clearly. The lesson gives one piece of that seeing: the person in the wheelchair and the person on foot differ less than the step makes it seem.)
- **Thiruvalluvar, Thirukkural 315:** What is the use of all one's knowledge, if one does not guard another's pain as one's own? (The scientists who showed that trout and rats react to hurt were doing what the verse asks, with instruments. The verse adds what no instrument gives: that knowing this must change how we act.)
- **Vinaya, Mahavagga 8.26:** After washing a sick monk whom the others had neglected, the Buddha said: you have no mother or father here to look after you. If you do not look after each other, who will? Whoever would look after me should look after the sick. (This is care given as a plain duty among equals, with no pity in it. It is close to what disabled people's groups ask for now: help that is owed.)
- Science (settled): Skeletons from tens of thousands of years ago show people surviving for years with injuries they could not have survived alone.
- Science (debated): Ants, rats and elephants rescue and tend the wounded. Whether this comes with a feeling like human concern is argued over, because feeling cannot be measured from outside.
- Other view, Biologists following William Hamilton (1964) and Richard Dawkins (1976): Helping exists because, over a very long time, animals who helped their relatives and their helpful neighbours left more young than those who did not. On this view each being is separate, and care is a clever way for separate beings to do well. It needs no oneness to explain it. The warm feeling is how the habit works from inside.
- Practice, The other side of the door (4 min): Stand at the main door of your home. Look at it as if you were checking it for visitors. Think of a visitor who uses a wheelchair. Is there a step? Is the door wide enough? Think of a visitor who cannot see. Could they find the bell? Think of a very old visitor who is tired. Is there somewhere to sit? Close your eyes and take three slow breaths. Think of one person you know who needs help, and of one thing they might like to be asked. Tell me what you would ask.
- Ask: When you help someone, where does the help come from: from you, or from everyone who ever helped you?
- Ask: If a ramp can make a 'cannot' disappear, how many other 'cannots' might be in the building and not in the person?
- Ask: Are the one who helps and the one who is helped two different kinds of people, or one kind at two different moments?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A girl in a wheelchair cannot get into a building with steps. Is she disabled, or is the building? (hint: Imagine the same girl at the same door after a ramp is built. Ask what changed and what did not.)
- *deep:* If everybody needs help at some time, why do we talk about 'people who need help' as if they were a different group? (hint: Think back over your own week. Who helped you, and with what?)
- *deep:* When you help someone who did not ask, whose need are you meeting: theirs or your own? (hint: Think of a time someone 'helped' you when you wanted to do it yourself. How did it feel?)
- *deep:* A blind person's brain uses the 'seeing' part for touch and sound. Is that person missing a world, or living in a different one? (hint: A bat knows a room by echoes. Is the bat's room less real than yours?)
- *big:* An ant carries a wounded ant home. A rat frees a trapped rat. Are they being kind, or is 'kind' a word only for humans? (hint: Ask how you decide that a person is being kind. Then ask if the same test can be used on a rat.)
- *big:* Long ago someone fed a man with a useless arm for many years. He could not hunt. Why might they have done it? (hint: List every reason you can think of, not only the nice ones. What could he have given the group?)
- *big:* If a doctor could change a deaf child so that the child hears, should the parents always say yes? (hint: Listen to both sides in your head: a hearing parent, and a Deaf grown-up who loves their language.)
- *big:* Is it better to help because you feel sorry for someone, or because it is fair? Does the reason matter if the help is the same? (hint: Think how each would feel to the person being helped, and which help would still be there next year.)
- *deep:* You cannot step inside a fish's pain, or even your best friend's. So how do you know that anyone other than you feels anything? (hint: Think about what you see and hear when a friend is hurt, and why that is enough for you.)
- *big:* Caring for a baby, a sick person or an old person is hard work, and most of it is unpaid. Who should do it, and how should it be shared? (hint: Find out how it is shared in your home now. Would everyone say that is fair?)

## Story: The Step and the Signboard
*A made-up story set in Mysuru, Karnataka. The children, the school and the carpenter are imagined. What is real: a ramp for a wheelchair should rise no more than 1 for every 12 along, and a longer plank over the same step needs a smaller push.*

Nandini was eight, and she had used a wheelchair since she was three. Her chair was red, with a bell that her aunt had fixed to the armrest. She could spin it in a full circle faster than anyone could count to two.

A new boy named Jintu joined her class in the middle of the term. His family had come to Mysuru from Guwahati, in Assam. That was three days away by train. He was tall and quiet, and he badly wanted to be useful.

On his first morning he saw Nandini rolling towards the classroom. He ran up behind her, took the handles and pushed.

'Ey!' said Nandini. The chair lurched. Her pencil box slid off her lap and burst open on the floor.

'I was helping,' said Jintu.

'You were driving,' said Nandini. 'Nobody asked for a driver. How would you like it if I picked you up and carried you to your desk?'

Jintu went red and sat at the back.

At the lunch break he stood for a long time in front of the notice board. Nandini rolled up beside him. The notice said, in Kannada, that Class 2 would visit the old library on Friday.

'Are you coming on Friday?' she asked.

Jintu looked at his feet. 'I do not know what it says. I can read Assamese and some English. Those letters all look like curly jalebis to me.'

Nandini began to say 'I will read it for you'. Then she stopped. 'Do you want me to read it to you?' she asked.

'Yes,' said Jintu. 'Please.'

So she read it aloud. Then she showed him the letter for 'ka' and the letter for 'ma'. He found three more of each on the board without any help.

On Friday the class walked to the old library. It had a carved wooden door, and in front of the door there was one stone step. The step was as high as a hand with the fingers spread.

Everyone went in. Nandini stopped at the step.

The librarian came out, a kind man with ink on his thumb. 'I will carry her in,' he said.

'No, thank you, sir,' said Nandini. 'I am not a parcel.'

Jintu had started forward. He stopped himself and put his hands in his pockets. 'What do you want to do?' he asked.

Nandini looked across the lane. A carpenter named Mary akka was planing a door there, and long planks leaned against her wall. 'I want a ramp,' said Nandini.

Mary akka brought a short plank first. It reached from the step to the ground, as steep as a slide. Nandini put her hands on the wheel rims and pushed. The chair went up a little way and rolled back. She tried again and it tipped. Jintu's hands flew out of his pockets, and he put them back.

'Too steep,' said Mary akka. 'A short plank asks for all your strength at once.' She fetched the longest plank she had, longer than two children lying head to toe. It met the step at a gentle slope.

Nandini pushed. The chair climbed slowly and steadily. She stopped once on the way up, and then she rolled in at the door. The bell on her armrest rang.

'It is the same step,' said Jintu. 'It is the same height. Why was it easy this time?'

'She went a longer way,' said Mary akka. 'She lifted herself by the same amount, but a little at a time. A ramp does not make the hill smaller. It lets you pay for it in small coins.'

Inside, the librarian was looking at his own doorway. 'In forty years,' he said, 'I never noticed that step.'

'You never needed to,' said Nandini. 'On Monday Jintu could not read the notice board. Nobody noticed that either, because all of you can read it.'

Jintu thought about that all the way back to school. In Guwahati he had been the best reader in his class. He was the same boy here, with the same eyes and the same head. Only the letters on the wall were different.

'Nandini,' he said, 'on Monday, was I disabled?'

Nandini spun her chair in a circle. 'I do not know,' she said. 'Was it you, or was it the notice board?'

Mary akka left the long plank at the library. By the next Friday it had two side rails and a coat of green paint. The first to use it was not Nandini at all. It was the old man who delivered the newspapers, wheeling his cycle up to the door.

**For the parent:** Jintu could read in Guwahati and could not read in Mysuru, though he had not changed. Nandini could not enter by the step and could enter by the plank, though she had not changed. Being able or unable is something that happens between a person and a place.

- Ask: Jintu asked if he was disabled on Monday. What would you tell him?
- Ask: Jintu pushed the chair on the first day and kept his hands in his pockets at the library. Which was the better help, and why?
- Ask: The librarian never noticed the step in forty years. What is there in your home or school that you have never needed to notice?

## Experiments
### e1 · How steep is too steep? The rubber-band ramp test (29 min)
- **problem:** A step stops wheels. A ramp lets wheels roll up, but only if it is gentle enough. In this test a rubber band measures the pull. The more the band stretches, the harder you are pulling.
- **question:** Which way up a 10-centimetre step needs the smallest pull: straight up, a short ramp or a long ramp?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which way will stretch the rubber band the most, and which the least? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Every try uses the same toy, the same load, the same rubber band and the same 10-centimetre step. Only the way up changes. You pull slowly, taking about 5 seconds to reach the top. You do each way 3 times, because one try can be a fluke, and you use the middle number of the three.
- **record:** Write the band's resting length at the top of the table. Fill in the table for each way up. Then draw the three ways up, side by side, and write the extra length under each.
- **results table:** Way up the step | My guess: most, middle or least stretch | Band length, try 1 (cm) | Band length, try 2 (cm) | Band length, try 3 (cm) | Middle number (cm) | Extra length over the resting band (cm)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stack books until the step is exactly 10 centimetres high. Measure it with the ruler. Tape the load onto the toy. Tie the thread to the front of the toy, and tie the other end of the thread to the rubber band.  
  *Parent says:* Wheels cannot roll up a step. A wheelchair meets steps like this at doorways. Today we find out how much a ramp helps. This rubber band tells us how hard we pull. The more the band stretches, the harder the pull.  
  *Child mode:* Build the step. Load the toy. Tie the thread to the toy and to the band.
  - Note: Measure the band's resting length with the ruler and write it at the top of the table. Keep the thread short, about 20 centimetres, so the band pulls along the ramp. Test the load first. Hang the toy and its load from the band. If the band stretches to more than three times its resting length, use a lighter load. If it stretches by less than 3 centimetres, use a heavier load. Your numbers will differ from the example because every band and every toy is different. The order of the three ways is what matters.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three ways up: straight up, the short ramp and the long ramp. Ask for a guess about which stretches the band most and least, and why. Write the guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which way will make the band stretch the most? Which the least? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which way stretches the band most and which least. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: Which way will stretch the band the most? Why? (good: Straight up, because I lift all of it.; The short ramp, because it is steep.; The long ramp, because I go further.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Write down the reason as well as the guess. The test will answer it, and you do not need to say who was right.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the ruler beside the band. Your child pulls the toy straight up from the floor to the top of the step, slowly, taking about 5 seconds. Read the band's length while the toy moves. Do this 3 times and write each number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Lift the toy straight up, slowly. I will read the band while you lift.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the toy straight up the step with the band. Go slowly. Your grown-up reads the ruler.
  - Note: Read the number when the toy is halfway up, while it is moving steadily. If the toy swings, shorten the thread. Pull in a straight line along the slope and not sideways, so that the band shows only the pull.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rest the short board from the floor to the top of the step. Pull the toy up it by the band, slowly, three times, reading the ruler each time. Then do the same with the long board.  
  *Parent says:* Now the short ramp, then the long ramp. Same toy, same load, same band, same step. Only the way up changes.  
  *Child mode:* Pull the toy up the short ramp three times. Then pull it up the long ramp three times.
  - Note: Hold the lower end of each board so that it does not slide. A carton can sag, so lay a magazine under the middle of it. If the toy does not roll easily, use a different toy. A toy that drags on its wheels hides the result, because friction, the rubbing grip, pulls against you.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the middle number of each way in the table. Take away the resting length to find the extra length. Put the three ways in order from the biggest extra length to the smallest. Compare with the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Which way stretched the band most? Which least? Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Find the middle number for each way. Which way stretched the band most, and which least?
  - Ask: Why did the long ramp need the smallest pull? (good: The toy goes up a little at a time.; The ramp holds up most of the weight.; You go further but each part is easy.)
  - Check: Which way up needed the smallest pull? → **The long ramp**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay a measuring tape or a string 120 centimetres long on the floor, starting at the foot of the step. Stand at the far end. Look back at the step.  
  *Parent says:* A real ramp may rise only 1 centimetre for every 12 along. Our step is 10 centimetres high. So the ramp must be 120 centimetres long. That is more than a whole arm span. Is that more room than you thought?  
  *Child mode:* Look along the 120-centimetre line. That is how long a ramp for this step must be.
  - Note: The test cannot show how tired a person's arms get, how a wet surface grips or how a ramp feels in rain. It also cannot show where a rest place is needed. Wheels, surface and arm strength all matter on a real ramp. The test does show one thing clearly: a longer slope needs a smaller push, and you pay for it with a longer path.
  - Think: What does this test not tell us about a real ramp for a wheelchair? What else would you test?
### e2 · Wet or dry, smooth or rough: which surface stops a chappal slipping? (22 min)
- **problem:** A steep and wet ramp can be dangerous for an old person with a stick, a child or anyone with wet feet. This test shows which surfaces grip best.
- **question:** How high must we lift the tray before the chappal slides: on a smooth dry tray, a smooth wet tray and a rough towel?
- **predict:** Guess first. On which surface will the chappal slide at the lowest tilt? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The tray, the chappal and the way you lift stay the same. You lift the end slowly, about one finger-width every 2 seconds, and the chappal starts at the same place each time. Only the surface changes. You do each surface 3 times, because one try can be a fluke, and you use the middle number.
- **safety:** Water on the floor can make anyone slip. Keep the tray over the old towel and wipe up any spills at once.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Write 'did not slip' if the chappal stays still when the end is lifted 30 centimetres. Draw the three surfaces and put them in order from slides first to slides last.
- **results table:** Surface | My guess: slides first, second or last | Height when it slid, try 1 (cm) | Height, try 2 (cm) | Height, try 3 (cm) | Middle number (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the old towel on the floor. Rest the tray on it with one short end against a wall or a heavy bag so that it cannot slide away. Put the chappal in the middle of the tray, with its sole down.  
  *Parent says:* We will tilt the tray slowly. The chappal will slide when the tilt is steep enough. We will measure how high the free end is at that moment.  
  *Child mode:* Rest the tray with one end against a wall. Put the chappal in the middle.
  - Note: Check the ruler reads from the floor to the underside of the lifted end. Practice once. If the chappal slides while you set up, move it to the middle again.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess about which surface the chappal slides on at the lowest tilt, and why. Write the guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Where will the chappal slide first: on the smooth dry tray, the wet tray or the rough towel? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess where the chappal slides first. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: Where will the chappal slide first? Why? (good: The wet tray, because water makes it slippery.; The dry tray, because plastic is smooth.; The towel, because it is soft.)
  - Note: Write the guess and the reason. Do not tell the answer. The test will show it.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On the smooth dry tray, your child lifts the free end slowly and says 'stop' when the chappal starts to slide. You hold the ruler beside the lifted end and read the height. Do this 3 times. Write each height in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Lift slowly, one finger-width every 2 seconds. Say 'stop' when the chappal starts to slide.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the end slowly. Say 'stop' when the chappal slides. Your grown-up reads the height.
  - Note: Put the chappal back in the middle each time. If the chappal does not slide by 30 centimetres, write 'did not slip' and stop lifting.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour a spoonful of water on the tray and spread it with your hand so the tray is wet all over. Wet the chappal's sole too. Do the same lifting 3 times and write each height.  
  *Parent says:* Now the tray is wet. Will the chappal slide at a lower tilt or a higher tilt?  
  *Child mode:* Wet the tray and the chappal. Lift slowly three times. Say 'stop' when it slides.
  - Note: If the wet and dry heights are within 2 centimetres of each other, wet the sole more. Wipe up spills at once, because a wet floor can make anyone slip. Dry the tray and the chappal before the next step.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the rough towel flat on the dry tray. Put the chappal on it and lift 3 times. Write each height, or 'did not slip'.  
  *Parent says:* Now a rough towel. Will it grip better than the smooth tray, or worse?  
  *Child mode:* Put the rough towel on the tray. Lift the end slowly three times.
  - Note: If the towel bunches, tape its edges flat or hold it down at the corners with books.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the middle number for each surface. Put the surfaces in order from slides first to slides last.  
  *Parent says:* Which surface lets the chappal slide first? Where would you want to walk on a rainy day, or with a stick?  
  *Child mode:* Which surface slid first? Which gripped best?
  - Check: Which surface is best for a ramp on a rainy day? → **A rough surface**
  - Think: Why do bathrooms and ramps need a rough surface? Where have you seen a rough mat on the floor?

## Activities
### a1 · Steps and ramps near us: a survey of a door, a school or a shop (25 min)
- **problem:** A building with only steps shuts out people who use wheelchairs, and also people with prams, trolleys and sticks. A ramp opens the way, but only if it is long enough.
- **safety:** Stay with a grown-up near the road. Look at the ramp and the steps, and do not climb on rails.
- **record:** A table with one row for each place. Write the number of steps, whether there is a ramp, and whether there is a handrail.
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Walk to the main door of your building, your school or a nearby shop. Count the steps. Measure how high the steps rise in all.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the door. Could someone with a pram or a wheelchair get in without help? What is in the way?  
  *Child mode:* Count the steps. Measure how high they go up.
  - Ask: Who could not get in easily? (good: A person in a wheelchair.; A person with a pram.; An old person with a stick.)
  - Note: A usual doorstep rises 10 to 20 centimetres. Measure from the ground to the top of the last step. If the place has no steps, notice that and write 'no steps'.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Find a ramp, at your building or somewhere on the way. Measure how high it rises and how long it is along the ground. Divide the length by the height. If the answer is 12 or more, the ramp is gentle enough.  
  *Parent says:* Length divided by height. A ramp that goes up 15 centimetres over 180 centimetres gives 12. Is your ramp 12 or more? Does it have a handrail and a rough surface?  
  *Child mode:* Measure how high the ramp goes up and how long it is. Is the long number at least 12 times the short number?
  - Note: If you cannot find a ramp, measure a kerb slope on the pavement or a ramp in a mall or a station. Do the sum together: for a rise of 10 centimetres, a gentle ramp is at least 120 centimetres long. Many ramps in India are much steeper than 1 in 12, so it is a common finding.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the door, the steps and the ramp. Write where the ramp starts, the front or the back of the building, and whether a person could use it alone.  
  *Parent says:* Is the ramp at the front door, where everyone goes in, or round the back? What does that say to the person who uses it?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the door, the steps and the ramp. Is the ramp at the front or the back?
  - Note: Notice where the ramp is. A ramp tucked away beside the dustbins tells a person that she was not expected at the front door.
  - Think: Who is the building made for? What would you change first?
### a2 · Write your name in Braille with dal grains (30 min)
- **problem:** A blind person reads with the fingertips. Braille uses six dots in two columns of three. Different patterns of dots stand for different letters.
- **record:** Your name in dots on the card, and a drawing of the first letter's dot pattern on paper.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a six-dot cell for each letter of your name, with the dots 2 centimetres apart. Number them: 1, 2, 3 down the left side, and 4, 5, 6 down the right side. Use the chart in the notes to find which dots each letter needs.  
  *Parent says:* Braille is made from a block of six dots. Each letter uses some of the six. The dot numbers go down the left side, then down the right side. Which dots do you need for the first letter of your name?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the six dots for each letter of your name. Number them 1, 2, 3 down the left. Then number 4, 5, 6 down the right.
  - Note: The dot chart. A is 1. B is 1 and 2. C is 1 and 4. D is 1, 4 and 5. E is 1 and 5. F is 1, 2 and 4. G is 1, 2, 4 and 5. H is 1, 2 and 5. I is 2 and 4. J is 2, 4 and 5. For K to T, take the letters A to J and add dot 3. For U, V, X, Y and Z, take A, B, C, D and E and add dots 3 and 6. W is a special one: 2, 4, 5 and 6. A name of three or four letters is enough. Real Braille cells are much smaller than these, smaller than a fingertip.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a small dot of glue on each place where the chart says a dot is needed. Press one grain of dal into each glue dot. Leave the other dot places empty.  
  *Parent says:* A grain on each dot we need, and an empty place where there is no dot. Press each grain down gently.  
  *Child mode:* Put a dot of glue where a dot is needed. Press a grain into it.
  - Note: Use only a small dot of glue, as a big blob takes a long time to dry. If you do not have glue, use a small piece of sticky tape rolled into a ring.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave the card to dry for 20 minutes. In that time, look for Braille at home, such as on a medicine box, a lift button or an identity card.  
  *Parent says:* While the glue dries, can you find Braille at home? Run a finger over a medicine box.  
  *Child mode:* Look for Braille on a medicine box or a lift button.
  - Note: Many medicine boxes carry the name in Braille. Some lift buttons and some ATM keys do. If you cannot find any, feel the raised marks near the edge of a clean note of 100, 200 or 500 rupees. These are for touch.
  - Wait: Leave it for 20 minutes
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Once the glue is dry, close your eyes so that only the fingers read. Run one fingertip lightly over each cell and say how many dots you feel. Then ask a friend or a grown-up to read your name with the chart.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. Slide one fingertip over a cell. How many dots do you feel, and where? A blind reader does this fast, with practice. It takes months to learn.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Slide a fingertip over each cell. Count the dots.
  - Ask: What was hard? What would help? (good: The dots felt too big or too small.; I forgot which cell was first.; It took practice.)
  - Think: A person who reads Braille reads many words a minute. Why could fingers learn this?
### a3 · Ask, wait, do: practising how to offer help (15 min)
- **problem:** Help can go wrong when the helper does not ask. In this activity you practise asking, waiting for the answer and doing what the person says. Nobody acts out a disability, because that is not how to learn about it. You only practise the words.
- **record:** Tick which scene each answer was: yes, no, or yes but a different way.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child the three steps: ask, wait, do what the person says. Say them together.  
  *Parent says:* When you want to help, there are three steps. Ask: 'Would you like some help?' Wait for the answer. Do what the person says. If the answer is no, say 'all right'.  
  *Child mode:* Say the three steps: ask, wait, do what the person says.
  - Note: Keep the scenes about words only. Nobody pretends to have a disability. You read each scene aloud, then answer in your own voice as the person in it.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the scenes one at a time. Your child says what to say and do first. Then you answer as the person. Scene 1 is a woman at a shop door with two heavy bags. You say yes. Scene 2 is an old neighbour on the stairs, who has a stick. You say no thank you. Scene 3 is a person in a wheelchair at a door. You say, 'Please hold the door.' You go through yourself. Scene 4 is a blind woman at a bus stop. You say, 'Please let me hold your elbow.' Scene 5 is a deaf girl at the next desk. You write, 'No thank you, I am fine.'  
  *Parent says:* Here is the first scene. A woman at a shop door has two heavy bags. What do you say?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to each scene. Say what you say first. Then do what the person says.
  - Ask: What did you say first? (good: Would you like some help?; Can I hold the door?; I waited for the answer.)
  - Note: Praise asking and waiting, more than doing. In scene 2, the child should say 'All right' and stop. In scene 3, the child holds the door and does not touch the chair. In scene 4, offer an elbow, and walk at the person's pace. In scene 5, stand where she can see your face and write or speak normally. Do not talk over the person's head to a friend.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to tell one time when someone helped without asking, and how it felt. Then ask what could be done instead.  
  *Parent says:* Has someone ever 'helped' you without asking? How did it feel? What could they have said?  
  *Child mode:* Think of a time someone helped without asking. How did it feel?
  - Think: What would you like a helper to say to you first?
### a4 · Hands full: three jobs and a better design (15 min)
- **problem:** Sometimes a door, a tap or a bottle is hard to use because the object was made for hands that are free. A better design works for many hands, not only for some.
- **record:** A drawing of one object with your change marked on it.
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the school bag on one arm and hold the tumbler of water in the other hand. Try three jobs: open a door that has a handle, press a switch, and unscrew a bottle cap. Notice which job was easiest and which was hardest.  
  *Parent says:* Your hands are busy. Try three jobs. Which was the easiest, and which was hardest? What would have helped?  
  *Child mode:* Carry a bag and a tumbler. Open a door, press a switch and open a bottle. Which was hardest?
  - Ask: Which job was easiest? Why? (good: The door with a lever handle, because I used my elbow.; The switch, because I pressed it with my knuckle.; The bottle was hardest.)
  - Note: This is about objects and not about disability. It shows how an object can fail anyone whose hands are busy. A person who lives with a disability is the real expert on what works, so ask people and listen. Keep the tumbler only half full so that nothing spills.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the door handle again. Is it a round knob, a pull handle or a long lever? Try it with your elbow. Which type works best when your hands are busy?  
  *Parent says:* A long handle can be pushed with an elbow. A round knob needs a hand that can grip and turn. Which works for more people?  
  *Child mode:* Try the handle with your elbow. Which kind of handle works for the most people?
  - Note: Lever handles were made for hands that cannot grip a round knob. They also help anyone with full arms, a small child, or someone with wet hands.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child draws one object with a change to make it easier for many hands. Label who it helps.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the change you would make. Who would it help?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a change to make one object easier. Write who it helps.
  - Think: If a design works for the hands that find it hardest, will it also work for everyone else?
### a5 · Ask an elder: what they know and what would make their day easier (25 min)
- **problem:** Old age changes eyes, ears, bones and balance, and old people carry stories, songs, recipes and cures. This activity listens to one elder and checks one path at home for safety.
- **record:** Three things the elder told you, and one change that would make their day easier.
- **s1 (12 min)**

### c1 · A ramp for a step when there is not much room (30 min)
- **problem:** A step is 10 centimetres high. The rule for a wheelchair ramp is a slope of 1 in 12, which is 1 centimetre up for every 12 along. That is 120 centimetres. But the space in front of the step is only 70 centimetres long. Build a model ramp so that the loaded toy lorry can climb with a small pull.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the card. Stay clear of the cut edges.
- **your turn:** What is your own way to fit a long gentle ramp into a small space? Draw it and test it.
- **constraints:** The ramp must rise no more than 1 centimetre for every 12 centimetres along.; You have only 70 centimetres of floor in a straight line.; It must have edges so the wheels do not roll off.; It must work with the loaded toy and the rubber band from the ramp test.
- **general:** Use a straight ramp 120 centimetres long, and move the start of it to the side so that it runs along a wall.; Make the ramp longer and gentler, and rest it on a stack of books at the middle for support.; Use a rough surface, such as a strip of cloth taped on the card, so that wheels and feet grip.
- **creative:** Build the ramp in two straight runs of 60 centimetres with a flat landing between them. The path turns round, so the ramp fits in a small space. Real ramps beside shops and railway stations use this.; Make the ramp fold up against the wall, so it is only out when someone needs it.; Build a ramp that runs round the corner of the step, with a gentle bend, so it uses the space beside the door.
- **c1s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build the 10-centimetre step. Mark 70 centimetres of floor in front of it with tape. Read the problem aloud.

- **c1s2 (20 min) · grown-up needed**

## Worksheets
### Ramps, helpers and asking first (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) A heavy load must go up a 10-centimetre step. Which way needs the smallest push?  
   Options: Lift it straight up / A short steep ramp / A long gentle ramp  
   **Answer:** A long gentle ramp. *Why:* A long ramp rises only a little over each centimetre along, so the ramp carries most of the weight. A short steep ramp needs a bigger pull, and a straight lift needs the biggest.
2. (truefalse) Everybody needs help at some time in life.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A baby needs help with everything, a person who is ill needs help, and most people need help in old age.
3. (fill) A band pulls a toy up two ramps. The band stretches ____ on the long ramp than on the short one.  
   **Answer:** less. *Why:* The long ramp is gentler, so the pull is smaller and the band stretches less.
4. (match) Draw a line from each helper to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Braille → Lets fingers read raised dots; White cane → Finds steps and poles ahead; Hearing aid → Makes sounds louder; Ramp → Lets wheels roll up a step. *Why:* Each helper is made for one job. Braille is read by touch, a cane sweeps the ground ahead, a hearing aid makes sound louder and a ramp replaces a step.
5. (order) Put these in the order you do them when you want to help someone.  
   **Answer:** ["Ask, 'Would you like some help?'", 'Wait for the answer', 'Do it the way the person says']. *Why:* Ask first, then wait, then do what the person says. If the answer is no, say 'all right'.
6. (sort) Sort each helper by what it mainly does.  
   **Answer:** Braille → Lets us read or talk; Sign language → Lets us read or talk; Wheelchair → Helps us move about; Jaipur Foot → Helps us move about; White cane → Helps us move about. *Why:* Braille and sign language carry words. A wheelchair, a Jaipur Foot and a white cane help a person move along.
7. (mcq) You see a blind woman at a busy crossing. What do you do first?  
   Options: Ask, 'Would you like some help?' / Hold her cane and lead her / Walk past  
   **Answer:** Ask, 'Would you like some help?'. *Why:* Ask first, and do what she says. Holding the cane, or pulling her, takes charge of her body without her yes.
8. (write) A friend says 'Poor thing, she cannot walk' about a girl in a wheelchair. What could your friend say instead?  
   **Answer:** A good answer says hello, or asks her name, or talks about what she likes. Or it says the step is the trouble, not her legs.. *Why:* Pity looks down at a person. Asking and talking treats her as an equal.
9. (label) Look at the school gate. Label one thing that helps a wheelchair user and one that stops her.  
   **Answer:** Helps: the long ramp with a handrail. Stops: the three steps.. *Why:* A ramp opens the way. Steps close it.
10. (draw) Draw a ramp for a doorstep. Show where it starts and ends. Write 'long' or 'short' beside it.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of a long, gentle slope up to the doorstep, labelled 'long'.. *Why:* A long, gentle ramp needs a small push. A short ramp is steep and hard to climb.
11. (perspective) A school wants a ramp. Two children talk about where to put it.  
   **Answer:** Either side, with a reason. The first child is right that one way in for everyone says 'you belong here'. The second child is right that a ramp needs enough room to be gentle.. *Why:* A strong answer weighs the room a long ramp needs against the message of the front door.
12. (truefalse) To be kind, a helper should always push a wheelchair without asking.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The chair is part of the user's own space. Ask first, and do what the person says.
### Slopes, senses and dignity (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (count) A step is 10 centimetres high. A ramp must be 12 times as long. How many centimetres long is the ramp?  
   **Answer:** 120. *Why:* 10 x 12 = 120. The ramp must be at least 120 centimetres long.
2. (mcq) A doorway is 20 centimetres above the road. What is the shortest ramp that fits the 1 in 12 rule?  
   Options: 120 cm / 240 cm / 40 cm  
   **Answer:** 240 cm. *Why:* 20 x 12 = 240. A ramp of 120 centimetres is right for a 10-centimetre step, and 40 centimetres is far too short.
3. (truefalse) A steep ramp squeezed into a small space always counts as help.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A steep ramp can be as useless as stairs for someone alone in a wheelchair. It must be gentle enough.
4. (mcq) A tray is tilted slowly with a chappal on it. On which surface does the chappal slide first?  
   Options: The rough towel / The smooth dry tray / The smooth wet tray  
   **Answer:** The smooth wet tray. *Why:* Water on a smooth surface makes it slippery, so the chappal slides at the lowest tilt. The rough towel grips best.
5. (match) Match each person to a good first thing to do.  
   **Answer:** A deaf girl → Face her in the light and speak normally, or write; A blind man → Offer your elbow and let him hold it; A woman in a wheelchair → Talk to her, not over her head; A guide dog in harness → Do not pet or feed without asking the person. *Why:* Each fits what the person needs. Faces help lip reading. An elbow lets the person guide. Talking to the person respects her. A guide dog in harness is working.
6. (fill) A ramp made for wheelchairs also helps a parent with a ____.  
   **Answer:** pram. *Why:* A pram, a trolley and a suitcase on wheels all roll up the same ramp. A change for some people helps many.
7. (truefalse) Indian Sign Language and American Sign Language are the same language.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each country has its own sign languages, as it has its own spoken languages.
8. (write) A girl who is blind says she has a full picture of her room. Could she be right? Say why.  
   **Answer:** Yes. She can know her room by touch, sound and echoes. The part of the brain for seeing is used for touch and hearing in people who are blind from early life. Every being builds its world from its own senses.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a world made by touch and sound is full, and gives one reason.
9. (order) Put these in order from the smallest push to the biggest push for a load going up a 10-centimetre step.  
   **Answer:** ['A 120-centimetre ramp', 'A 30-centimetre ramp', 'Lift straight up']. *Why:* The longer the ramp, the smaller the push. Lifting straight up is like a ramp of no length, so it needs the biggest push.
10. (perspective) Two people look at a woman in a wheelchair who cannot get into a shop with one step.  
   **Answer:** Either side, with a reason. Both hold some truth: legs that do not walk are a difference, and a step is a barrier. People still argue about how much weight each gets.. *Why:* A strong answer accepts that a body is different and that the world could be built another way.
11. (draw) Draw a good ramp. Label three things that make a ramp good.  
   **Answer:** A gentle slope, a handrail, a rough surface and a flat place to rest.. *Why:* A good ramp is gentle, has a rail to hold, a surface that grips and a place to rest.
12. (mcq) A rat opens a cage to free another rat, even with chocolate nearby. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Rats open cages by accident / Some animals help others who are trapped / Rats prefer cages  
   **Answer:** Some animals help others who are trapped. *Why:* In the test, many rats freed a trapped rat before eating the chocolate. This is one sign that animals other than humans help each other.
### Faulty ideas, fair tests and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) A friend says, 'A ramp built for a wheelchair only helps wheelchair users.' What is the best reply?  
   Options: Ramps are only for decoration / Prams, trolleys, suitcases and old people use it too / Only the school uses it  
   **Answer:** Prams, trolleys, suitcases and old people use it too. *Why:* A ramp helps anyone with wheels or weak legs. This is why designing for the people with the most difficulty works for almost everyone.
2. (truefalse) A longer ramp for the same height needs a smaller push, but you push along a longer path.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. You pay for the easier push with a longer way. Nothing comes free.
3. (write) What can the rubber-band ramp test not tell us about a real wheelchair ramp?  
   **Answer:** It cannot show how tired a person's arms get or how a wet surface grips. It cannot show where a rest place is needed, or how the ramp feels to use in rain.. *Why:* A test answers one question. A real ramp has more to it: arms, surface, rest and weather.
4. (mcq) A ramp is 300 centimetres long and rises 30 centimetres. Does it fit the 1 in 12 rule?  
   Options: Yes, because 300 is more than 30 / No, it needs at least 360 centimetres / Yes, any ramp fits the rule  
   **Answer:** No, it needs at least 360 centimetres. *Why:* 30 x 12 = 360. A ramp of 300 centimetres is too steep by 60 centimetres. The wrong answer 'yes' only compares 300 with 30.
5. (perspective) Should a deaf child get a cochlear implant? Three people see it differently.  
   **Answer:** Any side, with a reason. There is no single right answer. Good answers ask what the child and her family value.. *Why:* Deaf people and doctors still disagree. No view here is marked right.
6. (truefalse) We cannot step inside another animal's pain, so nobody can tell if any animal feels pain.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. We know another person's pain only from what they do and how their body is built. Scientists use the same evidence for animals.
7. (mcq) Which is the best sign that a dog with a thorn in her paw feels pain?  
   Options: She is a big dog / She stays quiet / She guards the paw, licks it and whimpers when it is touched  
   **Answer:** She guards the paw, licks it and whimpers when it is touched. *Why:* Guarding a hurt part, licking it and pulling away are signs of pain. Dogs also have nerves like ours that carry signals of harm.
8. (write) Someone says, 'Helping disabled people is a favour.' What do you think? Use the word 'right' in your answer.  
   **Answer:** A good answer says that people have a right to go to school and catch a bus, so a ramp is not a favour. Help comes with ask-first and respect.. *Why:* India's law of 2016 says schools, offices, buses and websites must be usable. A right needs no thank you.
9. (sort) Sort each action by whether it respects the person.  
   **Answer:** Asking 'Would you like some help?' → Respects the person; Pushing a wheelchair without asking → Takes charge of the person; Saying 'all right' after a no → Respects the person; Grabbing a bag to carry it → Takes charge of the person. *Why:* Asking and accepting a no keep the person in charge. Pushing, pulling and grabbing take charge without a yes.
10. (fill) A ramp must rise no more than 1 centimetre for every ____ centimetres along.  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* India's building guidelines give 1 in 12 as the steepest slope for a wheelchair ramp.
11. (label) Look at the bird water dish. Label two things that make it safe and one thing that is wrong.  
   **Answer:** Safe: shade and a stone to stand on, fresh water. Wrong: a cat hiding behind the pot, or dirty water.. *Why:* A good dish is shallow, in shade, has a stone, and is out of reach of cats. Old water can breed mosquitoes.
12. (mcq) Why is sharing care work a way to care for the carer?  
   Options: Care work is not real work / The carer gets time to rest / Only women can do it  
   **Answer:** The carer gets time to rest. *Why:* Care work is tiring, so a carer who gets help can rest. The jobs do not belong to one sex.

   **Answer:** Anyone can need help for a while. *Why:* Every person needs help at some time, as a baby, when hurt or ill, and often in old age. Needing help is part of life, not a rare thing.
2. (mcq) A teacher says, 'We help them, and they are not like us.' What is the problem?  
   Options: Everybody needs help from others at some time / Teachers should not help / Helping is never good  
   **Answer:** Everybody needs help from others at some time. *Why:* If everybody gets help at some time, 'them' and 'us' is not a true split. The helper today may need help tomorrow.
3. (mcq) A load goes up a 10-centimetre step by a 120-centimetre ramp or a 30-centimetre ramp. Which needs the smaller push?  
   Options: The 30-centimetre ramp / They need the same push / The 120-centimetre ramp  
   **Answer:** The 120-centimetre ramp. *Why:* The long ramp rises only a little for each centimetre along, so the push is smaller. The price is a longer path.
4. (mcq) A step is 15 centimetres high. How long must a ramp of slope 1 in 12 be at least?  
   Options: 27 centimetres / 90 centimetres / 180 centimetres  
   **Answer:** 180 centimetres. *Why:* 15 x 12 = 180. Adding 12 to 15 gives 27, and mixing up the numbers gives 90. For each 1 centimetre up, the ramp must go at least 12 along.
5. (mcq) A blind person sweeps a white cane along the ground ahead of her. What is the cane for?  
   Options: To tell others to stay away / To find steps, poles and holes before her feet reach them / To help her read  
   **Answer:** To find steps, poles and holes before her feet reach them. *Why:* The cane gives her news of the ground ahead through touch. It is a tool for finding the way and does not warn others off.
6. (mcq) Why was the Jaipur Foot built to bend and made of rubber?  
   Options: So that it looks like a shoe / So the wearer can squat and walk barefoot on rough ground / So it is heavy and strong  
   **Answer:** So the wearer can squat and walk barefoot on rough ground. *Why:* Many people in India sit on the floor, squat, and work barefoot in fields. A foot that bends and grips matches how they really live.
7. (mcq) You offer help and the person says, 'No, thank you.' What do you do?  
   Options: Say 'all right' and let her do it herself / Help anyway, to be kind / Ask a grown-up to make her accept  
   **Answer:** Say 'all right' and let her do it herself. *Why:* The person knows her own needs. Accepting a no keeps her in charge. Helping anyway takes charge of her.
8. (mcq) Which sentence shows respect for a deaf boy?  
   Options: 'Poor thing, he cannot hear.' / 'He is deaf, so ignore him.' / 'I will face him, and speak normally or write.'  
   **Answer:** 'I will face him, and speak normally or write.'. *Why:* Facing him lets him see your face and lips. 'Poor thing' is pity, and ignoring him leaves him out.
9. (mcq) India's law of 2016 says schools, offices and buses must be usable by disabled people. What idea is behind it?  
   Options: Help is a favour from kind people / Help is a right that people are owed / Only charities should help  
   **Answer:** Help is a right that people are owed. *Why:* A right needs no thank you. If a step is in the way, the step must go. Charity is good but it is not the same as a right.
10. (mcq) An old man's balance is less sure now. Which helps him most at home?  
   Options: A light on at night and a mat that does not slip / A smooth shiny floor / Moving his things every day  
   **Answer:** A light on at night and a mat that does not slip. *Why:* Falls are a danger in old age, so light, a dry floor and a rough mat prevent them. A smooth floor and things moved around cause trips.
11. (mcq) A friend is ill with a fever at home. Which is the best way to care?  
   Options: Tell her to get up and run / Bring water, sit with her, and wash your hands with soap after / Shout loudly so that she cannot sleep  
   **Answer:** Bring water, sit with her, and wash your hands with soap after. *Why:* A sick body needs rest, water, light food and company. Soap and water stop the germs passing to you or others.
12. (mcq) A street dog turns away and tucks her tail when you come close. What is she telling you?  
   Options: Please come closer / I do not want you near me, so back away / I am hungry for plastic  
   **Answer:** I do not want you near me, so back away. *Why:* Animals answer with the body. A dog who turns away or tucks her tail is saying no, just as a person might say 'No, thank you'.
13. (mcq) On a hot day, which helps a thirsty sparrow most?  
   Options: Loud crackers to make her fly / A shallow dish of clean water in the shade / A plastic bag of chips  
   **Answer:** A shallow dish of clean water in the shade. *Why:* Animals feel thirst and heat. A shallow dish of water in the shade with a stone to stand on is real help. Crackers frighten, and plastic bags can harm.
14. (mcq) A friend says, 'Only one child uses a wheelchair, so the ramp is a waste.' What is missing?  
   Options: Prams, trolleys, old people and anyone with a hurt leg use it too / Ramps are free / A ramp is only for decoration  
   **Answer:** Prams, trolleys, old people and anyone with a hurt leg use it too. *Why:* A ramp made for wheelchairs helps almost everyone. When we design for the person with the most difficulty, it works for many.

## Applications
- Metro stations in Kochi, Delhi, Chennai and Bengaluru have lifts, ramps and yellow tiles. Wheelchair users, parents with prams and blind passengers can travel without help.
- Many city buses in Chennai, Bengaluru and Delhi are low-floor buses with a wide, low step. Old people, people with prams and people with sticks can board more easily.
- A charity in Jaipur has fitted the Jaipur Foot free of charge since 1975. The wearer can squat, climb and walk barefoot.
- Many bank notes carry a raised mark. A person who cannot see can tell a hundred-rupee note from a five-hundred-rupee note by touch.
- News on television often has a small window with a person signing in sign language, so that a deaf viewer can follow it. India set up a centre for Indian Sign Language in New Delhi in 2015.
- Animal rescue groups in cities such as Chennai, Hyderabad and Jaipur treat hurt street dogs, cows and birds. They ask people to put out water in the hot months.

## Spotted around us
- A ramp beside the steps at a school gate, a railway station or a mall. Look at how long it is compared with the steps, and whether it has a handrail.
- Yellow bumpy tiles along the edge of a railway or metro platform. Feel the lines and the dots with your feet, or look at which way the lines run.
- Braille dots on a medicine box, a lift button or the side of a bank note. Run one finger over the dots and feel the pattern.
- A lever door handle on a shop or an office door, opened with an elbow by someone carrying shopping.
- Words at the bottom of a news video or a film on a phone, read by people on a noisy bus.
- A street dog or a cow looking for water on a hot day. Look for a bowl of water kept out by a shop.
- An older person holding a handrail on the stairs. Look for a light left on in the passage at night.

## Open questions
- **Do ants, rats and fish feel anything when they are hurt or when they help?** Their behaviour and their nerves point towards feeling. But science has no instrument that detects inner experience, even in humans. It can only compare behaviour and brains, and scientists weigh that evidence differently.
- **Why did caring for the weak arise at all, when it costs the carer food and effort?** Help to relatives, help that is returned, and groups that do well together have each been proposed. Each explains some cases. Care for strangers and for other species fits none of them neatly.
- **Did the people who fed the injured man at Shanidar feel what we call compassion?** Bones record survival and healing. They do not record feelings or reasons. Archaeologists disagree about how far it is fair to read care into them.
- **What is the best way to teach a deaf child: sign language, an implant and speech, or both?** Studies show that early, rich language of any kind is what matters most. But children, families and schools differ so much that no single plan has been shown to be best for all.
- **How should a country count and pay for care work done at home?** Economists can measure the hours. They do not agree on how to value them, or on who should pay: families, employers or the government. Countries are trying different answers.

## Connected maths
- A step is 10 centimetres high. A proper ramp rises 1 centimetre for every 12 centimetres along. How long must the ramp be? Your floor has only 100 centimetres of room. How much too short is it, and how could a turn help? **The ramp must be 120 centimetres. The floor is 20 centimetres too short. A turn splits the ramp into two runs of 60 centimetres, which each fit.**. 10 steps of 1 centimetre up means 10 steps of 12 centimetres along. 10 x 12 = 120. 120 - 100 = 20, so the floor is 20 centimetres short. Two runs of 60 centimetres fit in a space of 100, with a flat landing at the turn. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L2`)

## Warm-up
- What does a ramp do? → It joins a lower place to a higher place by a slope, so that wheels can roll up.
- Does a long ramp or a short ramp need the smaller push? → A long ramp. It rises a little at a time, so the push is smaller. You push along a longer path.
- What do we do first when we want to help someone? → Ask, 'Would you like some help?' and wait for the answer.
- What does a white cane do? → A blind person sweeps it ahead to find steps, poles and holes.
- What do old people often need at home? → Light at night, a dry floor, a rough mat and a handrail. They also need time and company.
- What do animals need when it is very hot? → Shade and clean water.
