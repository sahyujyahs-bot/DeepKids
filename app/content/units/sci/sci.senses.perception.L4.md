# Perception: why your brain picks one reading of the world, and what it leaves out
`sci.senses.perception.L4`

**Summary.** A drawing of a cube made of plain lines suddenly flips, so that the corner that pointed towards you now points away. Nothing on the paper changed, and you cannot see both cubes at once. Your child counts the flips in one minute, and then watches a tray change right in front of their eyes without anyone noticing for many seconds. They find out how many letters a sentence really hides, whether a face is easier to read the right way up, and how a tiny paper beetle can vanish into a pattern. The unit follows one idea. The brain has to pick one reading of everything, it uses what you expect and what you pay attention to to pick, and it leaves out the rest.

**Intro.** *Read this and count the letter F. FINISHED FILES ARE THE RESULT OF YEARS OF SCIENTIFIC STUDY COMBINED WITH THE EXPERIENCE OF YEARS. Many people find three. There are six. Where did the others go?* Your eyes took in every letter. But your brain was busy reading for meaning, and it slid over the little word 'of' as if it were not there. You did not choose to miss them. In this unit you meet other cases where the brain makes one reading and drops the rest. A cube drawing flips by itself. A big change in a tray goes unseen. Faces jump out of sockets and clouds. A paper beetle vanishes against a cloth. You test each one on yourself and on your family, and then you ask what it means that every one of us lives inside one reading of the world.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain, using a cube drawing, that a picture can have two readings and that my brain shows me only one at a time.
1. I can explain what attention does, and why I can look straight at a big change and not see it.
2. I can explain how what I expect changes what I read and what I find, using the letter F test and a hidden object.
3. I can explain how the brain groups bits into wholes, and how it can show me shapes that are not drawn.
4. I can explain why a face is harder to read upside down, and why I see faces in clouds, walls and fronts of scooters.
5. I can explain that two people can see the same thing differently, and that animals live in different sensory worlds from mine.
6. I can explain how camouflage works by fooling the way the brain groups and expects.

## Terms
- **Perception**: The way the brain turns the signals from your senses into what you see, hear, feel, taste and smell. It is made by the brain, not just recorded. *e.g.* You perceive a friend's face at once, though the signals from your eyes are only patterns of light.
- **Reading**: One way of making sense of something that could be understood in more than one way. A drawing, a word or a sound can have more than one reading. *e.g.* The same drawing can be read as a vase or as two faces.
- **Ambiguous**: Able to mean two or more things equally well. An ambiguous drawing or sentence has more than one reading. *e.g.* 'I saw her duck' is ambiguous. It could mean a bird or a quick bend.
- **Necker cube**: A drawing of a cube made only with lines, so that it can be read as pointing in two ways. It flips by itself as you look. It is named after the Swiss scientist Louis Albert Necker. *e.g.* Look at the cube for half a minute, and it will flip from one reading to the other.
- **Attention**: The brain's way of choosing a few things to deal with in detail. What you do not attend to gets very little detail. *e.g.* In a noisy market, you hear your own name because you attend to it.
- **Inattentional blindness**: Missing something clear and large because your attention is on something else. People with good eyesight do it. *e.g.* People counting passes of a ball in a video often miss a person in a gorilla suit who walks through.
- **Change blindness**: Not noticing a big change in a scene because something hid the moment of change, such as a blink or a flash. *e.g.* A cloth is moved in front of your face, and a person swaps places with another, and you do not notice.
- **Expectation**: What the brain thinks is likely before it looks. Expectation speeds up seeing, and also makes you miss things that do not fit. *e.g.* You read 'Paris in the the spring' and often miss the doubled word.
- **Search image**: A picture in the mind of what you are looking for. It helps you find that thing quickly, and hides other things. *e.g.* A bird that has been eating green caterpillars goes on looking for green ones, and passes brown ones.
- **Grouping**: The brain's habit of joining nearby, similar or connected bits into one thing. The German scientist Max Wertheimer described it in the 1920s. It is also called a Gestalt rule. *e.g.* Dots in a tight cluster look like one shape, though each dot is separate.
- **Figure and ground**: The brain's choice of what is the thing (the figure) and what is the background behind it (the ground). Which is which can swap. *e.g.* In the vase drawing, the vase is the figure, until the faces become the figure.
- **Illusory contour**: An edge that you see although nothing is drawn there. The brain makes the edge to complete a shape. *e.g.* In the ghost triangle, you see white edges that are not on the paper.
- **Pareidolia**: Seeing a face, or another familiar shape, in something that is not one, such as a cloud, a wall stain or a pair of sockets and a switch. *e.g.* A plug socket often looks like a surprised face.
- **Inverted face effect**: The fact that faces are harder to recognise upside down than other objects are. It shows that the brain has a special way of reading upright faces. *e.g.* You know your friend's photo at once, but not as quickly when it is upside down.
- **Camouflage**: Colours or patterns that make a living being hard to find. It works by matching the background, or by breaking up the outline. *e.g.* The stripes of a tiger break up the outline of the tiger in tall grass.
- **Colour constancy**: The brain's habit of seeing the colour of a thing as steady, though the colour of the light changes. It allows a banana to look yellow indoors and outdoors. *e.g.* A white shirt looks white both in sunshine and under a yellow bulb.
- **Umwelt**: The part of the world that an animal can sense and act on. Different animals have different umwelten. The word is German for surroundings, and the idea comes from the biologist Jakob von Uexküll. *e.g.* A tick waits on a leaf for the smell of sweat and the warmth of a body. That is most of their world.
- **Top-down and bottom-up**: Two directions of seeing. Bottom-up starts with the signals from the eyes. Top-down starts with what the brain already knows and expects. Seeing uses both together. *e.g.* You find a friend in a crowd faster because you expect to see them wearing a blue shirt.

## Explanations
### Perception is a best guess, and the brain shows you only one · *defined*
**Definition:** Perception is the brain's reading of the signals from the senses. The signals alone usually fit more than one reading, so the brain chooses the most likely one and shows you only that.

Light that enters the eye is a pattern of brightness and colour. The same pattern could come from many different scenes. A small thing close by and a big thing far away can make the same size on the eye. A flat drawing of a cube can be read as a cube pointing two ways. The brain has to pick, because a body must act on one reading at a time. You cannot reach for a cube that is both near and far. So the brain uses clues, and what it expects, to pick the likeliest reading, and it shows you that. You never see 'maybe'. About a thousand years ago, the scientist Ibn al-Haytham wrote that seeing is not only the light that enters the eye. He said that recognising what we see also needs memory and judgment, though we do not notice ourselves doing it. In the 1860s, Hermann von Helmholtz called this unconscious inference.
- e.g. A distant bus looks tiny but you read it as a normal bus far away, not as a toy bus close by.
- e.g. A face drawn with a few lines still reads as a face.
- e.g. A dark shape on a night road reads as a rock, a dog or a bag, depending on what you expect.
### Pictures with two readings: the cube, the vase and the duck-rabbit · *defined*
**Definition:** An ambiguous picture is a drawing that fits two readings about equally well. You see one reading at a time, and your brain flips between them without your choosing.

In 1832, the Swiss crystal expert Louis Albert Necker described a drawing of a crystal that seemed to turn inside out as he watched. We now call it the Necker cube. In 1915, the Danish psychologist Edgar Rubin published the vase and faces drawing. In it, the vase and the faces share the same edge, and you see either a vase or two faces. In 1899, the American psychologist Joseph Jastrow made famous a drawing that looks like a duck facing left or a rabbit facing right. In every case the lines never change. The flip happens in the brain. Most people can slow the flips by trying to hold one reading, but few can stop them. The flips seem to come when one reading has been seen for a while and the other reading takes over. You cannot see both readings at the same moment. One reading is always the figure, the thing in front. The other becomes the background behind it. Scientists have found brain activity that changes at each flip, but they do not yet fully know why the brain flips.
- e.g. A cube drawn with all twelve edges visible flips in and out.
- e.g. The vase and faces drawing flips between a vase and two faces looking at each other.
- e.g. The duck-rabbit flips between a duck looking left and a rabbit looking right.
### Attention: you see in detail only what you attend to · *defined*
**Definition:** The eye takes in a wide view, but the brain deals with only a few things in detail. Attention is how it chooses. Things that are not attended to can go unseen, even when they are large and in front of you.

In 1975, Ulric Neisser and Robert Becklen played two videos on top of each other, a hand-slapping game and a ball game. People who followed one game often missed odd things in the other. In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris asked people to count the passes made by players in white shirts in a video. About half of the viewers did not see a person in a gorilla suit who walked right through the middle and beat her chest. In 1998, Simons and Daniel Levin had a person stop strangers to ask for directions. While the stranger was giving directions, workers carrying a door passed between them, and the person asking was swapped for a different person. About half of the strangers did not notice that they were talking to someone new. In 1997, Ronald Rensink and others showed that if a blank flash covers the moment of a big change in a photograph, people take many flashes to find it. Without the flash, the change pops out at once, because the brain notices movement. These results are not signs of poor eyesight or carelessness. They show that detailed seeing needs attention.
- e.g. You do not notice your own nose until you are told to think about it.
- e.g. A driver on the phone can look at a red light and still not see it.
- e.g. You miss your mother's new haircut for a whole day.
### Expectation and search images: you find what you look for · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain uses what it expects to read and to search quickly. This helps when the world is as expected, and causes misses when something does not fit.

When you read, your eyes jump from word to word. They often skip short, common words, because the brain can guess them. That is why many people miss the extra 'the' in 'Paris in the the spring', and the letters F in the word 'of'. A related idea is the search image. In 1960, the Dutch scientist Luuk Tinbergen noticed that small birds in pine woods ate a kind of insect mostly when that insect was common, and he suggested that a bird learns a picture of what to look for. In 1971, Marian Dawkins reported tests on young chicks. Chicks that had found one type of grain over and over were slower to find a second type, even when the second was easy to see. Searching for one kind of thing seems to make the brain pay less attention to others. The same happens to people looking for a lost key, for a friend in a crowd, or for a leaf insect on a bush. If you look for a bug on a leaf, you may pass the bug that is the leaf.
- e.g. You find your own slippers in a pile of chappals quickly, because you know what they look like.
- e.g. A mother in a crowd finds her child in a blue school uniform and walks past a red shirt.
- e.g. A birdwatcher who looks for a big bird misses the small one.
### Grouping and ghost shapes: the brain joins bits into wholes · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain joins nearby, similar and connected bits into whole things. It can even draw edges that are not there, to complete a shape. These edges are called illusory contours.

In the 1920s, Max Wertheimer and other German scientists described how we group what we see. We join things that are close together, things that look alike, things that move together, and things that make a closed shape. In 1955, the Italian scientist Gaetano Kanizsa drew three black circles, each with a wedge cut out, set so that the wedges point to the middle. Most people see a white triangle lying on top, with clear edges. The edges are not drawn. In 1984, Rüdiger von der Heydt and his team found nerve cells in the visual part of a monkey's brain that responded to these edges that do not exist, as if they were real lines. Grouping is useful. In the real world, an animal in leaves is often seen only in pieces. The brain joins the pieces into a whole animal. It does not always have enough information to be right.
- e.g. Dots arranged in rows look like rows, though each is separate.
- e.g. The clouds of a constellation look like a shape such as a hunter, when the stars are far apart.
- e.g. A half-hidden cat behind a fence still looks like one cat.
### Faces: a special skill, and faces everywhere · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans are very good at recognising upright faces. The skill depends on the whole face being seen the right way up, and it fails when the face is turned upside down. The brain also finds faces in things that are not faces.

In 1969, Robert Yin showed that faces are harder to recognise upside down than other objects are. In 1980, the British scientist Peter Thompson turned the eyes and mouth of a photo of a famous face upside down inside the face. Held upright, the face looked horrible. Turned upside down, it looked almost normal. This is called the Thatcher effect, after the photo he used. It shows that the brain reads an upright face as a whole, and that the strangeness of the changed parts becomes clear only in the upright face. In 1997, Nancy Kanwisher and her team found a part of the brain on the underside of the cortex that responds strongly to faces. This skill starts early. Tests reported in the 1970s and 1990s found that newborn babies look longer at face-like patterns than at the same patterns jumbled. The skill is so keen that it fires for pairs of dots and a line. That is why we see faces in sockets, scooters, clouds and a burnt chapati.
- e.g. A plug socket with two holes and a switch looks like a surprised face.
- e.g. The front of a scooter looks like a face with two eyes and a nose.
- e.g. A photo of someone you know is harder to recognise upside down.
### Camouflage works by fooling how the brain reads · *defined*
**Definition:** Camouflage is a pattern or shape that helps an animal avoid being found. It works either by matching the background, or by breaking up the animal's outline so that the brain does not read it as one animal.

In 1940, the British zoologist Hugh Cott described many kinds of camouflage in animals. In background matching, an animal is coloured like the place where they live, like a moth on tree bark. In disruptive colouring, dark and light patches run across the body edge and cut it into pieces that the brain joins to the background instead. A tiger's stripes in tall dry grass do this. In 2005, a team led by Innes Cuthill put paper moths on tree trunks. Birds found the paper moths with patterns that cut across the edge less often than moths whose patterns did not cut across the edge. Some animals go further. The leaf butterfly Kallima, found in India, folds their wings to look like a dead leaf with veins and spots. Leaf insects look like green leaves, with veins and sometimes marks like bites. In all these cases the hunter has a search image, and the camouflage is made to match it. It is a trick played on the brain, and not on the eyes.
- e.g. A nightjar sitting on the ground looks like a dead leaf or a stone, and she is hard to see.
- e.g. A stick insect holds still and looks like a twig.
- e.g. A leaf butterfly with wings folded looks like a dry leaf.
### Every animal lives in a world of their own: the umwelt · *defined*
**Definition:** Each kind of animal senses only part of the world, and acts in that part. That part is called the umwelt. Different animals have different umwelten.

In the 1930s, the Estonian-German biologist Jakob von Uexküll described the umwelt with the example of a tick. A tick can wait on a leaf for a very long time, and drops onto an animal when she smells a chemical in sweat and feels warmth. Her world is very small. Honeybees see ultraviolet light, which we cannot see. Many flowers have patterns in ultraviolet that guide bees to the nectar, and a flower that looks plain to us can look striped to a bee. Dogs have two kinds of colour sensor, and we have three, so a dog sees fewer colours than we do. But a dog has a far larger share of the brain for smell, and a smell-world that we can hardly imagine. Bats find insects in the dark by sound echoes. Pit vipers sense heat. None of these animals has the whole world. We do not either. Each of us gets one reading, from one body.
- e.g. A bee sees ultraviolet stripes on a flower that looks plain to us.
- e.g. A dog follows a smell trail across a road that we cannot smell at all.
- e.g. A bat's world is made of echoes.
### The dress: the same photo, different colours · *debated*
In February 2015, a photo of a dress spread across the world. Some people saw it as blue and black. Others saw it as white and gold. The photo itself had one set of colours, so the different answers had to come from the people. Scientists think that the brain tries to remove the colour of the light to find the true colour of the cloth, and that it is not sure what light the dress is in. If the brain assumes the dress is in bluish shade, it removes blue and sees white and gold. If it assumes the dress is in yellowish lamp light, it removes yellow and sees blue and black. Scientists disagree about why people assume different light. One study found that people who are awake early and spend more time in daylight saw it differently from night owls. Others have not found the same, so the idea is not settled.
- e.g. A white cloth looks white in the shade, though the shade is bluish.
- e.g. A friend says a shirt is green, and you say it is blue.
- e.g. A photo of a shoe looks different in sunlight and under a tube light.
- **The assumed-light idea:** The brain takes away the colour of the light it expects. People who expect different light see different colours in the same photo.
- **The daily-light idea:** A person's own life, such as time spent in daylight or in lamp light, may set what light they expect. Some tests found support for this, and others did not.
### Seeing differently, or seeing different things? · *debated*
Two people look at the same red apple. Are they having the same experience? Nobody has a way to check. Everyone has learned to call that colour red, so everyone says red. In 1689, the English thinker John Locke asked whether one person's blue might be another's yellow, and no one would ever know. Today we can measure colour sensors and compare the nerve signals of different people. Most people have the same three kinds of colour sensor, so the signals are likely to be similar. But what the signals feel like from the inside cannot be measured from the outside. This question is called the problem of other minds, or of qualia, which means the felt qualities of experience.
- e.g. A person with red-green colour blindness sees some colours differently from most people, and they learn this only by testing.
- e.g. A bee and a child look at a flower and see different patterns.
- e.g. Two friends taste the same mango, and one says it is very sweet and one says it is only a little sweet.
- **The similar-wiring view:** People have the same body parts, and the same brain layout. Their experiences are probably very much alike, even if we cannot prove it.
- **The cannot-check view:** An experience is private. We can compare what people say, and what their nerves do, but not what the experience is like. We cannot rule out differences.
### Time in your head: a minute is not always a minute · *idea*
**Definition:** How long a stretch of time feels depends on how much attention you give to time itself. When you attend to the clock, it feels longer. When you are busy, it feels shorter.

A waiting minute can feel very long, and a minute of a good game can feel short. Scientists who study this think that the brain keeps a kind of count of beats from an inner clock, and that attention opens and closes the way beats reach the count. When you are watching the clock, many beats are counted and the time feels long. When your attention is on a task, fewer beats are counted and the time feels short. Tests in which people tell when a minute has passed show this pattern on average, but it is a small effect and varies between people. Scientists still argue about how the brain tells time, and there may be more than one inner clock.
- e.g. The last five minutes before the school bell feel very long.
- e.g. An hour of your favourite game feels very short.
- e.g. A visit to the dentist's waiting room feels longer than the visit.

## Mostly agreed
- A drawing that fits two readings is seen as one reading at a time, and the brain flips between them without the viewer choosing. *How we know:* The Necker cube has been tested since 1832 on people of every age. Brain scans show activity changing at each flip, while the drawing on the page stays exactly the same.
- Detailed seeing needs attention, so people often miss large things that they are not attending to. *How we know:* In the gorilla video, about half of the viewers counting passes did not see the gorilla. In the stranger and the door test, about half did not notice that the person asking directions had been swapped for another.
- A change is hard to see when something hides the moment it happens. Without the hiding flash or blink, the same change is noticed at once. *How we know:* Rensink and others showed pairs of photos that differ in one big way. People needed many flashes to find the change, but saw it at once when the flash was removed.
- What the brain expects changes what it reads and what it finds, and searching for one kind of thing can hide another. *How we know:* Many readers miss the doubled 'the' in a familiar phrase. Chicks that had been finding one type of grain were slower to find a second type.
- The brain groups bits into wholes and can show edges that are not there. *How we know:* Most people see a white triangle in the ghost triangle drawing. Nerve cells in the visual part of a monkey's brain responded to those missing edges as if they were drawn.
- Upright faces are read in a special way, and are harder to recognise upside down. *How we know:* In 1969 Robert Yin found that faces suffer more from being turned upside down than other objects do. The Thatcher effect shows it clearly: a face with its eyes and mouth turned upside down looks horrible upright and almost normal upside down.
- Camouflage works by fooling the way a hunter's brain reads a scene, by matching the background or by breaking up the outline. *How we know:* In field tests with paper moths on tree trunks, birds found moths with outline-breaking patterns less often than moths without them.

## How we found out
**Louis Albert Necker, a Swiss crystal expert (1786 to 1861), and Hermann von Helmholtz, a German scientist (1821 to 1894), 1832 and the 1860s.** For a long time, many people thought the eye worked like a camera, so that anything drawn the same way would always look the same. In 1832, a Swiss crystal expert named Louis Albert Necker was studying drawings of crystals. He noticed something odd. A drawing of a simple crystal sometimes seemed to turn inside out by itself, with the front corner suddenly becoming the back. He wrote to a science magazine to say that the drawing had not changed and he had not moved. Only what he saw had changed. Later in the same century, the German scientist Hermann von Helmholtz suggested that the brain draws conclusions from the light it receives, without our noticing. He called this unconscious inference. Over the next 150 years, tests showed this again and again, from drawings that flip, to people who miss a gorilla walking across a screen. The cube drawing is still called the Necker cube.  
*What people believed before:* The idea that the eye works like a camera, so that a drawing always looks the same. Necker showed that one unchanging drawing can look different from moment to moment.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The eye works like a camera and records the world exactly as it is.** (A common school idea, and many people before the 1800s.; *rejected*)  
  For: The eye has a lens and a screen, the retina, like a camera.  
  Against: A camera records whatever it is pointed at. The brain picks. The same lines can be read two ways, big changes can go unseen, and what you expect changes what you see.
- **If something is right in front of you, you will see it.** (Almost everyone, until they are tested.; *rejected*)  
  For: The light from the thing reaches your eye, so it feels as if it must be seen.  
  Against: About half of the people counting passes missed a gorilla that walked across the screen. Without attention, large things can be missed.
- **People who miss the gorilla, or a change, are careless or have poor eyesight.** (Many people, when they miss something they are told is obvious.; *rejected*)  
  For: Once someone points it out, it looks very obvious.  
  Against: People with good eyesight and a lot of experience miss such things too. Missing them is how the brain normally deals with a limited amount of attention.
- **Everyone sees colours in the same way.** (A common assumption.; *debated*)  
  For: Most people have the same three kinds of colour sensor and agree on names such as red.  
  Against: A photo of a dress was seen by some as blue and black and by others as white and gold. People with colour blindness see some colours differently. Nobody can compare what the colour feels like inside.
- **If you try hard enough, you can see everything in a scene.** (Many people, and some teachers.; *rejected*)  
  For: Trying hard to look can make you notice more.  
  Against: Attention is limited. Looking harder at one thing makes you notice less about other things. You can train what you look for, but not see everything at once.
- **Seeing faces in clouds and walls is a mistake the brain should not make.** (Some people, who think it is silly.; *debated*)  
  For: There is no face in a socket or a cloud.  
  Against: The brain's face detector is tuned to fire for even a rough match, because missing a real face is costly and a false alarm is cheap. The skill that finds faces in clouds is the same one that finds friends in a crowd.

## Roots
We have flipped a cube, missed a change in a tray, read a face upside down and hidden a beetle. Now we ask what these tests tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your brain never shows you 'maybe'. When a drawing could mean two things, you see only one of them at a time. The other one disappears, and you cannot see both at once.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk as if seeing were like a camera, which could record a scene that has two readings and keep both. But a body has to act on one reading at a time, so the brain must pick, and it does not tell you that it picked.  
*Evidence:* In the cube test, the lines on the paper never changed, yet the cube flipped several times a minute, and you saw only one version each time. Trying to hold one version slowed the flips for many people, but usually did not stop them.

- *who am i:* Each of us lives inside one reading of the world, chosen by our brain from many that could fit the same light and sound. The reading depends on what we expect, what we attend to and the body we have.
- *what is life:* Every living being has to pick one reading and act. A tick waits for the smell of sweat, a bee heads for patterns in ultraviolet, and a chick looks for the grain she expects. Each lives in the reading their body can make.
- *what is this world:* The world gives the same light to every eye. What anyone sees is a meeting between that light and a particular body and brain. Nobody has a view from nowhere.

Light, sound and smell come from the same world to every creature. A body turns them into one reading so that it can act: reach, run, hide or hunt. Because a particular body makes the reading, the same cube can flip, the same tray can change unseen, and the same flower can be a bright target for a bee and a plain one for you. The world is not made up by the brain. But the way it appears to anyone is always a meeting between the world and a body that has to choose.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2:** Zhuang Zhou dreamed that he was a butterfly, happily flying about. He did not know that he was Zhou. When he woke, he was Zhou again. He did not know whether he was Zhou who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming that he was Zhou. (Two readings of one life both fit, as the two cubes fit one drawing. Zhuangzi leaves the question open and does not pick a reading, which is a hard thing for a brain to do.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta:** The hymn asks where everything came from, and ends by saying that the one who watches over it from the highest heaven surely knows, or perhaps even he does not know. (The hymn ends with a maybe, which the brain never shows us in what we see. It lets a question stay open instead of closing it with one reading.)
- **Plato, Republic, Book 7, 514a to 517a:** Prisoners in a cave, who have only ever seen shadows on a wall, take the shadows for the real things. One prisoner is freed and sees the brighter world outside. He comes back to tell the others, and they do not believe him. (A reading can feel like the whole truth because it is the only one we have. Tests, such as the tray and the cube, are ways to step out of one reading and see how it was made.)
- Science (settled): A picture that fits two readings is seen as one reading at a time, and the brain switches between them without the viewer choosing. This has been known since Necker, in 1832.
- Science (idea): The brain may settle on a reading by combining what its senses report with what it expects, in the way a scientist weighs a hypothesis. This is a main idea in brain science today, called predictive processing.
- Science (open): Why the brain flips, and what a reading feels like from the inside, are not known.
- Other view, Many brain scientists: The two readings are two states of a network of nerve cells that compete and then tire. Words such as 'choice' or 'reading' only describe the competition. The science needs only the nerve cells and their activity.
- Practice, Hold two readings (3 min): Look at the cube drawing. Notice the reading you see now. Breathe slowly. Wait until it flips. When it does, say now, in a whisper. Then try to see both at once, and notice that you cannot. Say quietly, maybe.
- Ask: When you see a face in a cloud, who is making the face: the cloud or you?
- Ask: What is the first thing you see in a new person or a new place? What other reading might you be missing?
- Ask: If a bee sees a flower differently, which of you sees the true flower?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A cube drawing flips by itself, and you cannot see both readings at once. The paper never changes. So where does the flip happen? (hint: Think about where the lines end and where the seeing begins.)
- *deep:* People looked straight at a tray, and a big object came and went, and they did not see it. If you can look at something and miss it, what does it mean to see? (hint: Think about the difference between light entering the eye and the brain noticing.)
- *big:* You and your friend look at the same dress. You see blue and black, and she sees white and gold. Are you seeing differently, or seeing different things? (hint: Think about the same photo, the same light, and two brains making different guesses about the light.)
- *big:* A bee sees stripes on a flower that look plain to you. Is the flower striped or plain? (hint: Think about what is on the flower, and what is in the eyes of whoever looks at it.)

## Story: The Leaf That Was Not a Leaf
*The story is made up, and the people in it are not real. The science is real. Leaf insects live in forests in India, among them the Western Ghats and the North-East, and eat leaves such as guava. Luuk Tinbergen suggested search images in 1960, and Marian Dawkins reported tests with young chicks in 1971. In real life, a leaf insect would be handled only by trained people and put back where she was found.*

Sowmya's grandfather had been a forest guard for forty years, and he had one boast. "In all my years," Thatha liked to say, "no creature has ever hidden from me."

He said it at breakfast. He said it to the postman. He said it once to a cow. So when Leela Chithi, Sowmya's aunt, came home from the insect research station in the hills and heard the boast, she smiled in a way that Sowmya knew meant trouble.

"Thatha," said Chithi, "tomorrow morning there will be a creature on the guava bush by your veranda. If you can find her in one minute, I will carry your umbrella for a month. If you cannot, you will carry mine."

"Done," said Thatha, and he shook her hand so hard that her bangles rang.

In the morning the rain had stopped, and every leaf on the guava bush was dripping and shining. Thatha stood in front of the bush in his old khaki cap. He took off his glasses, wiped them, and put them on again.

"One minute," said Chithi, pressing a button on her phone.

Thatha was a magnificent looker. He looked low, and then high. He looked for movement. He looked for the glint of an eye. He found a snail. He found two ants, a tiny spider, and a caterpillar the size of a grain of rice. Each time he cried, "Is that her?" and each time Chithi said, "No."

"Time," said Chithi at last.

Thatha's moustache drooped. "There is nothing on that bush," he said. "You are cheating."

Sowmya had been sitting on the step with a plate of idli. She was nine, and her eyes were not as sharp as Thatha's. But she had a habit that he did not have. When she could not find something, she asked herself what she was looking for.

"Thatha," she said, "what exactly were you looking for?"

"A creature," he said. "A bug. Something with legs, sitting on a leaf."

"That is it," said Sowmya. "You were looking for something sitting on a leaf. What if she is not sitting on a leaf?"

"Then where?"

"What if she is the leaf?"

Thatha opened his mouth, and closed it. He looked at the bush again. There were hundreds of leaves, all green, all veined, all shining with rain. "How do I find one leaf among hundreds?"

"Do not look for a creature," said Sowmya. "Look for a leaf that is wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Too flat. Too neat. Or with a bite mark that is not like the other bite marks."

So Thatha looked in a way he had not looked in forty years. He did not look for a bug. He looked at every leaf as if it were a suspect. And at last, near his own shoulder, his eyes stopped on one leaf that was just like the others, except for a small brown patch at one edge, like a bite. A breeze came, and every leaf trembled. This leaf swayed to and fro, softly, a little longer than the rest, as if she were not quite sure that she was a leaf.

"Her?" he whispered.

"Her," said Leela Chithi.

Close up, the leaf had a stalk, and veins, and a ragged brown edge. It also had six thin legs, flattened and folded into the shape of leaf pieces, and a small head tucked under one edge. She was a leaf insect, one of the animals who spend their whole lives pretending to be leaves.

Thatha stared for a long time. "Forty years in the forest," he said at last, "and I looked at her a dozen times."

"You did," said Chithi. "You looked straight at her, again and again. You just did not see her, because you were looking for a bug, and she is not one. Birds have the same trouble. A bird that has eaten ten green caterpillars goes on looking for green caterpillars, and a brown one can walk right under her beak."

"And you, Thatha," said Sowmya, "were looking for what you expected."

"My granddaughter," said Thatha, "has been listening to too many scientists." But he was smiling.

That evening, Thatha carried Leela Chithi's umbrella, though it was not raining. He carried it the next day too, and the next. He carried it to the temple, the market and the post office. And whenever anyone asked him why, he said, "I am proud to carry it. My granddaughter found the creature that I was too clever to find."

As for the leaf insect, Chithi carried her back to the station in her box, and put her on her own guava bush. For the rest of that month, whenever Sowmya looked at a bush, she did not look for a bug. She looked for a leaf that was wrong.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the brain finds things faster when it knows what to look for, and it misses things that do not match. A guard who looks for a bug on a leaf can pass a leaf insect many times, because the insect is shaped like the leaf. A mental picture of what to look for is called a search image. Luuk Tinbergen suggested it in 1960, and Marian Dawkins reported in 1971 that chicks used to one type of grain were slower to find another. Camouflage works because it fits what a hunter expects.

- Ask: Why did a forest guard with sharp eyes fail to find the leaf insect for a whole minute?
- Ask: What is the difference between looking for a bug on a leaf and looking for a leaf that is wrong?
- Ask: What is a search image, and how could you use one to find a lost key faster?
- Ask: Why might a hunter that has been eating one kind of prey walk past a different kind?

### g2 · Two Readings: the sentence that fools your brain (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 12 sentence cards. Stack them with the sentence side up. The back stays hidden.; One player is the reader. The reader reads the top sentence aloud, slowly, and keeps the back hidden.; Everyone says aloud, at the same moment, the first meaning that came into their head.; Now everyone hunts for a second meaning. The first player to say a second meaning that makes sense scores 2 points.; The reader turns the card over and reads the back. A player who found a different good meaning that is not on the back also scores 2 points.; The reader job passes to the next player after every card. After all 12 cards, the most points wins. A tie is a shared win.; Playing alone, say the first meaning aloud, then write it down. Hunt for a second meaning. Then turn the card over and check. Score 1 point for each second meaning you find.
- **Printable cards: 12 sentence cards: the sentence on the front, the readings on the back:** I saw her duck. (back: Reading 1: I saw her pet duck. Reading 2: I saw her bend down quickly.); She hit the boy with the umbrella. (back: Reading 1: She used an umbrella to hit him. Reading 2: She hit the boy who was holding the umbrella.); Meena saw the girl with the binoculars. (back: Reading 1: Meena used binoculars to see the girl. Reading 2: The girl was the one holding the binoculars.); Let's eat Paati! (back: Without a comma, it sounds as if Paati is the food. With a comma, 'Let's eat, Paati!' means let us eat together. One comma changes the reading.); The chicken is ready to eat. (back: Reading 1: The cooked chicken is ready to be eaten. Reading 2: The live chicken is hungry and ready to have her own food.); Visiting relatives can be tiring. (back: Reading 1: Going to visit relatives is tiring. Reading 2: Relatives who come to visit us are tiring.); Flying planes can be dangerous. (back: Reading 1: Flying a plane can be dangerous. Reading 2: Planes that are flying can be dangerous.); The old man the boat. (back: Read 'old' as 'old people' and 'man' as 'to crew'. The old people crew the boat. Most readers first take 'the old man' as one person, and then the sentence breaks.); Time flies like an arrow. Fruit flies like a banana. (back: In the first sentence, 'flies' is something time does. In the second, 'fruit flies' are insects and 'like' is a verb. The same words change their jobs.); Tara told Anitha that she was brilliant. (back: 'She' could be Tara or Anitha. The sentence does not say, so readers guess from what they expect.); We walked to the bank. (back: Reading 1: the edge of a river. Reading 2: a place that keeps money.); I cannot find the glasses. (back: Reading 1: spectacles. Reading 2: drinking glasses.)

  *Parent says:* Each sentence has more than one meaning. First, say the meaning that came to you. Then hunt for another.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Choose who reads first.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first four cards. After each, ask the players which meaning came first, and how long it took to find the second.  
  *Parent says:* Say your first meaning now. Now hunt for another one. How long did it take to find it?  
  *Child mode:* Say your first meaning. Then hunt for another one.
  - Ask: Why did the second meaning take longer than the first? (good: My brain chose the first one and stopped looking.; The first one popped into my head at once.; I could only think of one meaning at a time.)
  - Note: Many people find that the second meaning needs a pause. That pause is the point: the brain picks one reading at once, and the other takes effort to find.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the other eight cards. Keep a tally of points. Notice which sentences were the hardest to read in a second way.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. Which sentence was the hardest to read in a second way? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Play the other cards. Which sentence was the hardest?
  - Note: The old man the boat is the hardest for most people. It is a sentence that breaks in the middle, and the reader has to go back and start again.
- **s4 (4 min)**

## Experiments
### e1 · Count the Flips: one cube drawing, two readings (35 min)
- **question:** How many times does a cube drawing flip in one minute? Can you hold it to one reading by trying, and do different people flip at different rates?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many times will the cube flip in one minute? Will trying to hold it still make it flip less, the same or more? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every person looks at the same drawing, at arm's length, in the same light, for exactly 60 seconds. You give every person the same words. Half of the people start with just watching and half start with trying to hold one reading, so that practice does not help one test more than the other. Nobody taps or counts in their head. Only what the person tries to do changes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each person, add the two just-watching counts and divide by two. Draw a bar for each person's average, and a mark for their trying-to-hold count.
- **results table:** Person | Flips in 60 seconds, just watching (try 1) | Flips in 60 seconds, just watching (try 2) | Flips in 60 seconds, trying to hold one reading | What it felt like, in their own words
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a cube drawing in a book, or draw a quick one. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* I will draw a cube with lines. You will look at it for one minute. Will the cube stay the same? Will it change? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A cube made of lines. Will it stay the same for a minute? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think a drawing that does not change might look different? (good: My eyes might get tired.; It could look like a cube from the front or from the back.; My brain might change its mind.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Many children guess that it will not change.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a square 5 centimetres wide with the ruler. 2. Draw a second square of the same size, starting 2 centimetres to the right and 2 centimetres up. The squares overlap. 3. Join each corner of the first square to the matching corner of the second with a straight line. 4. Go over the lines with the black pen.  
  *Parent says:* Draw one square. Draw a second square of the same size, a little up and to the right. Now join the four matching corners with straight lines.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a square. Draw a second, up and to the right. Join the corners.
  - Note: The twelve lines must all show, with no hidden lines. If you prefer, copy the cube from a book. Place the drawing flat on the table at arm's length from your child.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test your child, just watching. Say: look at the cube for one minute. Every time it seems to flip so that a different corner comes forward, say flip. Start the stopwatch, and make a mark each time. Do it twice, with a short rest between.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the cube. Keep your eyes on it. Every time it flips, say flip. I will count. Ready, go.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the cube for one minute. Say flip each time it changes.
  - Note: If there is no flip after 30 seconds, say: let your eyes drift to another corner. Or blink slowly. Some people take a while to see the first flip. Write down the number even if it is 0.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test your child trying to hold one reading. Say: pick one reading and keep it for the whole minute. If it flips, say flip, and try to bring it back. Start the stopwatch and make a mark for each flip.  
  *Parent says:* Now choose one way to see the cube. Try hard to keep it that way for one minute. If it flips anyway, say flip, and bring it back.  
  *Child mode:* Pick one way to see the cube. Try to keep it for a minute.
  - Note: Most people find that trying slows the flips down, but few can stop them. Write down what your child says it feels like. Do not tell them what you expect.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the three other people in the same way: one minute just watching, and one minute trying to hold one reading. Half of the people do the trying test first. Write every number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now let us test other people. Same drawing, same words, same one minute. Let us see if everyone flips the same number of times.  
  *Child mode:* Test three more people. Same words, same minute. Write their flips.
  - Note: Use the same words every time. Do not show anyone how it flips. Some people will say it never flips, and that is a result. Ask them to try the eye-drift tip once. With two to four children, pair up: one child looks while another tallies and holds the stopwatch, then swap, so every child is tested. If nobody sees a flip in two minutes, that person is a result too: try the eye-drift tip, or make the cube bigger with thicker lines. About 3 to 20 flips a minute is typical, and it varies a lot between people.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the numbers. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Do all people flip the same amount? Does trying change it? What do you think is happening, if the lines never change?  
  *Child mode:* Do people flip the same amount? Does trying change it?
  - Note: Common results are that most people flip several times a minute, that trying slows the flips for many people, and that people differ a lot. Your results may differ. If one reading was seen far more than the other, write it down.
  - Check: Why does a cube drawing flip, though the lines on the paper never change? → **The lines fit two cubes equally well, and the brain switches between them**
### e2 · The Blink Tray: how long does a big change stay unseen? (30 min)
- **question:** If a card hides the moment a thing on a tray changes, how many blinks does it take to notice? Is a big thing in the middle found sooner than a small thing at the edge?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many blinks will it take a watcher to find a big red cup that appears and disappears? Will a small button at the edge take more or fewer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every watcher sees the same tray with the same ten things. The card covers the tray for about one second each time, and the tray is in view for about three seconds. The same person holds the card and the same person changes the thing each time. Only which thing changes is different: the big red cup in the middle, or the small button at the edge. You move the other things between tests so that a watcher will not recall where they were.
- **safety:** Keep small things such as buttons and bangles away from children under five, who might put them in their mouths.
- **record:** For each watcher, write how many blinks it took to find each change, up to 15. Add the numbers for the big change and for the small change. Draw two bars.
- **results table:** Watcher | Blinks to find the big red cup in the middle | Blinks to find the small button at the edge | Did the watcher see it at once when the cup was taken away in plain sight? (yes or no)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the tray and the ten things. Explain that a card will cover the tray for a moment, and a thing will change. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* A card will cover the tray for a second. Under the card, one thing will come or go. Do you think the watcher will see it at once? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A card hides the tray for a second. One thing changes. Will you see it at once?
  - Ask: Why do you think people might miss a big change when a card hides it for a second? (good: I would be looking at something else.; My eyes would not see the change happen.; My brain would not compare the before and after.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many people say that they would see it at once, and that is a good guess to test.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Practise with no watcher. One person holds the card flat over the tray for about one second and lifts it away for about three seconds. The other person puts the red cup on the tray or takes it away while the card is down. Practise until it is smooth and silent.  
  *Parent says:* Cover the tray, count one, and lift the card. While the card is down, I will take the cup away or put it back. Then we count one, two, three, and cover again.  
  *Child mode:* Cover the tray for one second. Lift the card. Count to three. Cover again.
  - Note: The change must happen only while the card is down, and silently. Keep the other things still. A smooth rhythm is the key. If the card moves slowly, the watcher can see the cup move. One person can do both jobs with one hand holding the card over the tray and the other hand reaching underneath, so a child and a parent can work alone. With 2 to 4 children, one holds the card, one changes the tray, one is the watcher and one counts and writes; rotate the jobs.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Bring in one watcher and sit them at the table. First, show the plain-sight case: take the red cup away slowly while the watcher watches the tray with no card. Ask what changed. Write yes or no for noticing at once.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the tray, please. I will take something away now. What changed? That was easy. Now, with the card, we will see if it is still easy.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the tray. Something will change in plain sight. Can you see it?
  - Note: Do this with each watcher before their blink tests. In plain sight, almost everyone sees the change at once, because they see it move. This is the control. It shows that the change is easy to see when the card is not hiding it.
- **s4 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test the watcher with the blink. Test 1: the red cup comes and goes at each blink. Count the blinks until the watcher says what changed. Stop at 15. Move the other things to new places. Test 2: the button at the edge comes and goes at each blink. Count the blinks. Test two more watchers, and swap which test goes first.  
  *Parent says:* Now we will use the card. After each blink, tell me if you see a change and what it is. I will count the blinks. Ready?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the tray. After each blink, tell what changed. Count the blinks.
  - Note: Write down the number of blinks. If the watcher guesses wrongly, say no and carry on. Give each watcher the tests in a different order. People who find the cup quickly may take more blinks for the button, and some people find both at once. If every watcher sees the change on the very first blink, the scene is too easy: add four or five more things to the tray, or shorten the time the tray is in view to about two seconds. If nobody sees it by blink 15, stop and say so; that is a real result and it matches how hard this is. Typical results are a few blinks for the big red cup and more for the small button, but yours may differ.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the blinks for the big change and the small change. Draw two bars. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which change took more blinks? Was the plain-sight change easy? What was different when the card was down?  
  *Child mode:* Add up the blinks. Which change took more? Why?
  - Check: A big change in a tray goes unseen when a card covers the moment of change. What is the best explanation? → **Without the sight of the change happening, the brain does not compare the before and the after unless it is attending to that place**
### e3 · Upside-Down Faces: which looks stranger? (30 min)
- **question:** If the eyes and mouth in a face are turned upside down, does the face look strange? Does it look equally strange when the whole face is upside down?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How strange will a face look with its eyes and mouth turned upside down, when it is upright, and when it is upside down? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every rater sees the same four cards for exactly three seconds each, in the same light, and answers the same question: how strange does the face look, from 0 for normal to 5 for very strange. The two upside-down cards are always shown first, so that no rater has seen the odd face upright before rating the upside-down cards. This order is not perfect, but it keeps the odd face from being given away. Only the card changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting with the scissors. Ask the person in the photo before you use it, and do not post the cards or the photos online.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out the average score of the raters for each of the four cards. Draw four bars.
- **results table:** Rater | Normal face, upside down (0 to 5) | Changed face, upside down (0 to 5) | Normal face, upright (0 to 5) | Changed face, upright (0 to 5)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a photo of a face. Explain that you will turn the eyes and the mouth upside down inside the face. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* We will turn the eyes and the mouth of a face upside down. Will the face look strange? Will it look equally strange upside down? Why?  
  *Child mode:* We will flip a face's eyes and mouth. Will it look strange? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think a face might look strange upright but not upside down? (good: Because I am used to seeing faces upright.; Because upside down I look at the parts, not the whole face.; It will look strange both ways, because the eyes are wrong.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children guess that the changed face will look strange in both positions, and that is a good guess to test.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stick the normal photo on a card. This is the normal face. 2. On the second photo, a grown-up cuts out a strip across both eyes and eyebrows, and a patch across the mouth. Keep the two cut pieces. 3. Stick the second photo, with its two holes, on the second card. 4. Turn both pieces upside down and glue them into their holes on the card.  
  *Parent says:* Stick one photo on a card. This is the normal one. On the other photo, a grown-up cuts out the eyes and the mouth. Turn each piece upside down and stick it back.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts out the eyes and mouth. Turn them upside down. Stick them back.
  - Note: Cut slightly outside the eyebrows and the lips so the pieces fit back well. A clear photo of a face looking straight ahead works best. A drawing gives a weaker effect. Cut slightly outside the eyes and the lips, and keep the pieces large, as small ones are hard to turn and glue. With 2 to 4 children, each child can make a card pair from a different photo, and the raters compare them.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show each rater four cards, one at a time, for three seconds each, timed with the phone. The order is: normal upside down, changed upside down, normal upright, changed upright. After each card, ask how strange the face looks, from 0 to 5. Write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* I will show you a face for three seconds. Then tell me how strange it looks. Zero is normal and five is very strange. Do not look for long.  
  *Child mode:* Show a face for three seconds. Ask how strange it looks, from 0 to 5.

## Worksheets
### One Reading at a Time (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A cube drawn with lines flips by itself, though the paper does not change. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: The lines fit two readings, and the brain switches between them / The paper is moving a little / The eyes are tired of the lines  
   **Answer:** The lines fit two readings, and the brain switches between them. *Why:* The same lines could be a cube pointing either way. The brain can show only one reading at a time, so it switches. The paper stays still, and tired eyes are not the main cause.
2. (truefalse) A person can see both the vase and the two faces in the vase and faces drawing at the very same moment.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The brain shows one reading at a time. When the faces are the figure, the vase becomes the background, and when the vase is the figure, the faces are the background.
3. (fill) Missing something large because your attention is on something else is called ____ blindness.  
   **Answer:** ['inattentional']. *Why:* People who count passes in a video often miss a gorilla that walks across. Their eyes saw the gorilla, but their attention was on the passes.
4. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Figure → the thing the brain picks as being in front; Ground → the background the brain puts behind the figure; Expectation → what the brain thinks is likely before it looks; Camouflage → colours or shapes that make an animal hard to find. *Why:* These are four words for how the brain reads a scene. The first two describe how it splits a scene into a thing and a background.
5. (sort) Sort these moments by what the brain is doing.  
   **Answer:** not seeing the doubled 'the' in a familiar phrase → Using expectation; not noticing a gorilla while counting passes → Missing because of attention; seeing a triangle between three circles with slices missing → Grouping bits into a whole; seeing a constellation as a hunter → Grouping bits into a whole; not finding the second colour of grain after finding the first → Using expectation; not seeing a swapped person while giving directions → Missing because of attention. *Why:* Expectation helps you read and search quickly, and hides what does not fit. Attention picks a few things to see in detail. Grouping joins separate bits into one whole.

## Applications
- Soldiers' uniforms and vehicle paint use patterns of dark and light patches that break up the outline, as a tiger's stripes do.
- Road safety teachers explain that a driver on a phone can look at a red light and not see it, because attention is on the call.
- Magicians use misdirection. They draw your attention to one hand while the other does the trick.
- Airport baggage screeners are trained to look for many kinds of dangerous object, since a search image for one kind makes another easy to miss.
- Doctors who read scans use checklists, and a second doctor checks the scan, because even experts miss things they are not looking for.
- Proofreaders catch spelling mistakes by reading aloud or backwards, because the brain reading for meaning skips over mistakes.
- Designers of signs and logos use figure and ground, for example by hiding an arrow in the space between two letters.
- Courts take care with the memories of witnesses, since people can miss large things and read the same scene in different ways.
- Teachers of reading ask children to check their first reading of a sentence, since the brain takes the likeliest reading first.
- Safety signs are placed where people are already looking, since people do not see warnings that are outside their attention.
- People with face blindness, who find it hard to recognise faces, are helped by understanding that face reading is a special skill that can be weaker in some people.

## Open questions
- **Why does the brain flip between two readings of a drawing, and what decides when it flips?** Scientists see the activity of the brain change at each flip. Some think that the nerve cells for one reading tire, and others think that the brain keeps testing the other reading. Nobody has a full account.
- **How does the brain decide what to attend to, and what to leave out?** Scientists know that attention is limited and can be steered by what is bright, moving or important to you. They do not know how the brain weighs all of these in a moment.
- **Do all people see colours in the same way?** Most people have the same colour sensors, so their signals are likely to be alike. Nobody can compare what the colours feel like from the inside.
- **Why do some people find it very hard to recognise faces, though their eyesight is good?** Face blindness runs in some families and can follow an injury. Scientists are still finding out which parts of the face skill are affected.
- **How does the brain make one steady scene from eyes that jump several times a second?** Our eyes make small jumps all the time, yet the world does not seem to jump. Scientists have clues but not a full account.
- **What is it like to be a bee, a bat or a pit viper?** Scientists can measure what each animal senses. Nobody knows how to find out what the experience is like from the inside.
- **Can we ever know the world as it is, apart from any one reading of it?** Every reading is made by a body and a brain. We can compare readings, and test them with instruments, but we cannot step outside all readings. People disagree about what follows.

What is the average for each, and how much higher is the upright average? **The upright average is 4.6 and the upside-down average is 1.4. The upright face scored 3.2 higher.**. 5 plus 4 plus 5 plus 4 plus 5 is 23, and 23 divided by 5 is 4.6. 1 plus 2 plus 1 plus 1 plus 2 is 7, and 7 divided by 5 is 1.4. 4.6 minus 1.4 is 3.2. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A sentence has 6 F's. Four people count them and find 3, 3, 4 and 6. What fraction of the F's did the first person find? What fraction of the people found all six? **3 out of 6, which is one half. One out of 4 people, which is one quarter.**. The first person found 3 of the 6 F's, which is 3 over 6, or 1 over 2. One of the four people found all of them, which is 1 over 4. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Ten people look at two two-way pictures. In picture A, 6 see a vase first and 4 see faces. In picture B, 9 see a vase first and 1 sees faces. What percentage saw a vase first in each? Which picture is the better two-way picture, and why? **Picture A: 60 out of 100. Picture B: 90 out of 100. Picture A is better, because its readings are nearer to equal.**. 6 out of 10 is 60 out of 100, and 9 out of 10 is 90 out of 100. A good two-way picture splits close to 50 and 50. Picture A is 10 away from 50, and picture B is 40 away. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In Is a Minute a Minute?, a person said stop at 48 seconds while waiting, and at 75 seconds while busy. How many seconds off a true minute was each? What fraction of a minute is each? **12 seconds too early, which is one fifth of a minute. 15 seconds too late, which is one quarter of a minute.**. 60 minus 48 is 12, and 12 out of 60 is 1 out of 5. 75 minus 60 is 15, and 15 out of 60 is 1 out of 4. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- In the Blot Gallery, eight people looked at six blots, so there were 48 answers. 20 were animals, 12 were people, 6 were plants, 8 were things and 2 were nothing. Check that the kinds add to 48. What fraction of the answers were animals? **20 plus 12 plus 6 plus 8 plus 2 is 48. Animals were 20 out of 48, which is 5 out of 12.**. 20 plus 12 is 32, plus 6 is 38, plus 8 is 46, plus 2 is 48. 20 over 48 simplifies to 5 over 12, by dividing both numbers by 4. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- In Hide the Beetle, the first beetle took five watchers 10, 15, 22, 28 and 30 seconds to find. The improved beetle took 25, 38, 40, 45 and 47 seconds. Find the average for each, and say about how many times longer the second took. **The first averaged 21 seconds and the second 39 seconds. The second took a little under twice as long.**. 10 plus 15 plus 22 plus 28 plus 30 is 105, and 105 divided by 5 is 21. 25 plus 38 plus 40 plus 45 plus 47 is 195, and 195 divided by 5 is 39. 39 is a little less than 42, which is twice 21. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What does a Necker cube do, though its lines never change? → It flips between two readings.
- What is the name for missing something large because your attention is elsewhere? → Inattentional blindness.
- What helps you find what you are looking for, and hides other things? → A search image.
- In the vase and faces drawing, what does the vase become when you see the faces? → The background.
- What kind of edge do you see in the ghost triangle? → An illusory contour, an edge that is not drawn.
- What happens to a face when it is turned upside down? → It is harder to recognise, because the brain reads upright faces in a special way.
- What is the name for seeing faces in sockets and clouds? → Pareidolia.
- What two tricks does camouflage use? → Matching the background and breaking up the outline.
- What is the part of the world that an animal can sense called? → Its umwelt.
- How many readings does the brain show at once? → One.
