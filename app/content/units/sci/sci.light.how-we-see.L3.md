# How we see: light comes into our eyes
`sci.light.how-we-see.L3`

**Summary.** We can see only when light comes into our eyes. A lamp, the Sun or a fire makes its own light, but most things, like a mango or your friend's face, make none. We see them because light bounces off them and travels into our eyes. At the back of each eye, a thin layer called the retina catches the light, and the optic nerve carries signals from it to the brain. For more than a thousand years, many thinkers believed that eyes sent out rays to touch things. About a thousand years ago in Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham showed that light travels the other way, from things into the eye.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes. Now open them. Did something go out of your eyes to the room? Or did something come into your eyes from the room?* Try this tonight with a grown-up. Go into the darkest room you have, such as a bathroom, switch off the light and shut the door. Push a towel against the gap under the door. Open your eyes as wide as you can, and hold your hand in front of your face. If no light gets in, you cannot see your hand at all, even though your eyes are healthy and open. Why not? By the end of this unit you will know what your eyes need, and what really happens when you look at something.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that we see things only when light comes into our eyes.
1. I can sort things into light sources, which make their own light, and lit objects, which only bounce light.
2. I can draw the path of light from a source, to a thing, and into an eye, with arrows that point the right way.
3. I can name the main parts of the eye and say what each one does, including the pupil getting bigger and smaller.
4. I can run a test, show what I found in a table or a bar chart, and say what I think, what I saw, and why.
5. I can explain why the old idea that eyes send out rays is wrong, using evidence I have seen myself.
6. I can explain that the brain helps build what we see, and that eyes catch tiny packets of light called photons.
7. I can say what scientists agree on about seeing, and what nobody knows yet.

## Terms
- **Light**: Light is what lets us see. It comes from the Sun, lamps and fires, and travels out from them in every direction. *e.g.* When you switch on a tubelight, its light fills the whole room in a blink.
- **Light source**: A thing that makes its own light. *e.g.* The Sun, a diya flame, a torch and a phone screen are light sources.
- **Lit object**: An object that makes no light of its own. We see it only when light from a source lands on it and bounces off. Scientists call light sources luminous, and lit objects non-luminous. *e.g.* A book, a banana, the Moon and your own hand are lit objects.
- **Dark**: Dark means there is no light, or very little. Darkness is not a thing that fills a room. It is what is left when light is missing. *e.g.* When the power goes off at night and there is no moon, the room is dark.
- **Reflect**: When light hits an object and bounces off it, we say the object reflects the light. Most objects reflect light in every direction at once. *e.g.* A white wall reflects sunlight from the window, and the whole room gets brighter.
- **Eye**: The part of your body that catches light. It is a ball about as big as a large grape. It sits in a bony hollow in your skull, called the eye socket. *e.g.* Your two eyes sit side by side, so each sees the world from a slightly different place.
- **Cornea**: The clear, curved window at the very front of the eye. Light passes through it first, and it starts bending the light to make a sharp picture. *e.g.* The cornea has more nerve endings than almost any other part of the body. That is why a speck of dust on it hurts so much.
- **Pupil**: The black circle in the middle of your eye. It is not a black spot. It is a hole that lets light into the eye. *e.g.* Look in a mirror. The pupil is the round black circle in the middle of your eye.
- **Iris**: The coloured ring around the pupil. Tiny muscles inside it make the pupil bigger or smaller. *e.g.* Brown, black, hazel, green and blue eyes are different coloured irises.
- **Lens**: A clear, curved part just behind the pupil. It bends the light so that a sharp picture forms on the retina. Another unit explains how lenses bend light. *e.g.* When an older person has a cataract, the lens has become cloudy, so everything looks misty.
- **Retina**: A thin layer at the back of the inside of the eye. It catches the light that comes in and turns it into signals. *e.g.* The retina is only about as thick as two or three pages of a notebook, but it holds more than a hundred million light-catching cells.
- **Optic nerve**: A thick bundle of nerve threads, like a cable. It carries signals from the eye to the brain. *e.g.* Each eye has its own optic nerve. They meet behind the eyes on the way to the back of the brain.
- **Blind spot**: One small place at the back of each eye that cannot catch light, because the optic nerve leaves the eye there. Everyone has two. *e.g.* You can find your blind spot with a card, a dot and a cross. The dot vanishes!
- **Rods and cones**: The two kinds of light-catching cells in the retina. Rods work in dim light. Cones need brighter light and let us see colours. *e.g.* On a dark night, the rods do almost all the work. That is why things look grey at night.
- **Photon**: The smallest possible amount of light. Light comes in tiny packets, far too small to see one by one. Each packet is a photon. *e.g.* A torch sends out billions of billions of photons every second.
- **Brain**: The part of you inside your head that thinks, feels and makes sense of the signals from your eyes. *e.g.* Your eyes catch light, but it is your brain that tells you 'that is Amma's face'.
- **Eyeshine**: The glow you see in a cat's or a dog's eyes at night when a light shines on them. It is light bouncing back off a shiny layer behind the retina. The eyes are not making light. *e.g.* In a car's headlights, a street dog's eyes can flash green or gold.
- **Scientist**: A person who finds out how the world works by looking carefully, testing ideas and checking them again. *e.g.* Ibn al-Haytham was a scientist. He tested ideas about seeing with lamps and a dark room.

## Explanations
### We need light to see · *defined*
**Definition:** We can see a thing only when light from it comes into our eyes. With no light at all, we see nothing, even with our eyes wide open.

Your eyes do not see by themselves. They catch light, and when there is no light, there is nothing for them to catch. That is why you cannot see your hand in a room with no light at all. As soon as a little light comes in, shapes appear. White and shiny objects show first, because they reflect the most light. Colours come last, because the cells that see colour need more light.
- e.g. In a power cut on a moonless night, you bump into the furniture you see every day.
- e.g. Close a shoebox with a red ball and a white paper inside, and look through a small hole. You see nothing. Open the lid a crack, and the white paper appears first.
- e.g. A cave far underground is completely dark. People who explore caves always carry lamps.
### Light sources and lit objects · *defined*
**Definition:** A light source makes its own light. A lit object makes no light. We see a lit object only when light from a source lands on it and bounces off into our eyes.

Look around the room. Almost everything you can see is a lit object. The table, the curtains, your little brother and the cat make no light at all. The only light sources may be a bulb, a window with the Sun outside, or a screen. Everything else is borrowing their light. The Moon looks bright at night, but it is a lit object too. It is a grey rock with sunlight bouncing off it.
- e.g. A diya flame is a light source. The brass plate under it is a lit object that shines with the flame's light.
- e.g. A phone screen is a light source. Turn it off, and you see the black glass only because the room's light bounces off it.
- e.g. The Sun is a light source. The Moon is a lit object.
### Light bounces off things and into our eyes · *defined*
**Definition:** We see a lit object when light travels from a source to the object, reflects off it, and then travels into our eyes.

Seeing a lit object always needs three parts. First, a light source. Second, the object. Third, an eye. The light goes from the source to the object, then from the object to the eye. If you break any part of the path, you cannot see the object. Switch off the lamp, and the path has no start. Hold a card in front of your eyes, and the path has no end. The object is still there, and still lit, but no light from it reaches you.
- e.g. Sunlight lands on a mango. Some of it bounces off the mango into your eyes, and you see the mango.
- e.g. A torch shines on a toy. Hold a book between the toy and your eyes. The toy is still lit, but you cannot see it.
- e.g. At night, a car's headlights land on a cow on the road. The light bounces off the cow into the driver's eyes, just in time.
### Light bounces off in every direction · *defined*
**Definition:** Most things bounce light off in every direction at once. So light from them reaches every eye in the room.

Think of a class of children all looking at one blackboard. Each child sees the board. That means light from the board reaches every child's eyes, in every seat. This happens because rough or matt surfaces, like paper, cloth and skin, reflect light in all directions. A mirror is different. It reflects light in one neat direction, and the reflection unit tells that story. A torch beam gives another clue. In clean air the beam is almost invisible from the side. Shake a dusty cloth beside it, and the beam appears. Each speck of dust reflects a little of the light sideways, into your eyes.
- e.g. Everyone at a wedding can see the bride at once, from every side of the hall.
- e.g. Sunbeams coming through a window show up only where there is dust or smoke in the air.
- e.g. In a cinema, the light from the projector bounces off the white screen to every seat.
### Do our eyes send out rays? The 2,000-year argument · *defined*
**Definition:** Light travels from things into our eyes. Our eyes do not send anything out to see.

For more than a thousand years, many clever people thought seeing worked the other way. They thought rays went out of the eyes, like invisible fingers, and touched what we look at. It sounds strange, but it matches how looking feels. In English we 'cast a glance'. In Hindi we say 'nazar daalna', to throw a look. Many children today still draw arrows coming out of the eyes. Here is how the argument went.
- e.g. If eyes sent out rays, you would see in a dark room. You can't.
- e.g. Looking at a very bright light hurts, and leaves a dark blob in your sight afterwards. Something came in and acted on your eye.
- e.g. Close your eyes and open them at night. The faraway stars appear at once. Could a ray from your eye really reach a star that quickly?
- **Empedocles, a Greek thinker, about 2,450 years ago, and later Plato:** They thought a kind of fire shines out of the eye, like light from a lantern. When it meets light from the day, we see. Plato knew we need daylight, but he still thought something left the eye.
- **Euclid, a Greek mathematician in Alexandria, about 2,300 years ago, and Ptolemy, about 1,900 years ago:** They thought straight 'visual rays' go out from the eye in a cone. Whatever the rays touch, we see. They did very good geometry with these rays, even though the rays went the wrong way.
- **Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,350 years ago:** He disagreed with the eye-rays idea. He thought the thing we see changes the air around it, and that change passes through the air to the eye. He did not have the details right, but he had the direction right: from the thing to the eye.
- **Ibn al-Haytham, a scientist in Cairo, about 1,000 years ago:** He put the pieces together and tested them. Light from a lamp or the Sun lands on things. From every point of a thing, light spreads out in straight lines, and some of it enters the eye. He wrote this in his great Book of Optics, the Kitab al-Manazir. He also said: do not just trust famous books, test them.
- **Scientists today:** Ibn al-Haytham was right about the direction. Light comes into the eye, lands on the retina at the back, and the retina sends signals to the brain. Nothing that helps us see comes out of the eye.
### The parts of the eye · *defined*
**Definition:** The eye is a ball that lets light in through the pupil, makes a sharp picture with the lens, and catches the light on the retina. The optic nerve carries signals to the brain.

Follow the light into your eye. First it passes through the cornea, a clear window on the front of the eye. Then it goes through the pupil, the black hole in the middle. The pupil looks black because it is a hole into a dark room. Light goes in and hardly any comes back out. Around the pupil is the coloured iris, which makes the hole bigger or smaller. Behind the pupil, the clear lens helps make the picture sharp. The light then crosses the inside of the eye, which is filled with clear jelly. At the back, it lands on the retina. The retina's cells turn light into tiny electric signals. The optic nerve carries those signals to the brain. Eyelids and eyelashes guard the front, and tears keep it clean and wet.
- e.g. When a doctor shines a light into your eye, she is looking through your pupil at your retina.
- e.g. In a photo taken with a flash, eyes sometimes look red. The flash went in through the pupil and reflected off the back of the eye, which is full of tiny blood vessels.
- e.g. A cataract is a lens that has gone cloudy, so light cannot pass through clearly. Doctors can replace it with a clear plastic lens.
### The pupil gets bigger and smaller by itself · *defined*
**Definition:** In dim light the iris opens the pupil wide to let in more light. In bright light it shrinks the pupil to protect the eye.

Your pupil is like the door of a dark room. In the evening it opens wide, to let in every bit of light it can. In bright sunshine it shrinks to a tiny dot, so that too much light does not dazzle or hurt the retina. You do not have to think about it. It happens in less than a second when a light comes on. Opening up again in the dark is slower, and it takes a few seconds. Doctors check this when someone has hit their head. A pupil that does not change can be a sign that something is wrong.
- e.g. Come inside from a sunny playground and the room looks dark for a moment, while your pupils open up.
- e.g. A cat's pupil is a slit. It can close to a thin line in sunshine and open to a big round circle at night.
- e.g. A goat's pupil is a sideways rectangle. It helps the goat see all around the hillside.
### Seeing in the dark: rods, cones and waiting · *defined*
**Definition:** After about 20 to 30 minutes in the dark, the rod cells in the retina become thousands of times more sensitive. But every eye needs at least a little light.

Walk from a bright room onto a dark balcony or terrace at night. At first you see almost nothing. Wait. After a few minutes, shapes appear. After about twenty minutes, you can see much more, and many more stars. This is called getting used to the dark. Your pupils open wide in a few seconds, but the bigger change is slower. The rods in your retina slowly build up a light-catching chemical. Rods are very good in dim light, but they do not see colours. So at night the world looks grey. Bright light uses up the chemical again in a moment. That is why one flash of a phone torch spoils your night eyes.
- e.g. Astronomers looking at stars use dim red torches, so they do not spoil their night eyes.
- e.g. Owls have huge eyes packed with rods, so they can hunt by starlight.
- e.g. A cat sees in much less light than we do. But in a room with no light at all, a cat cannot see either. It uses its whiskers, ears and nose.
### The brain helps make what we see · *idea*
Your eyes catch light, but they do not send a ready-made picture to your brain. They send millions of tiny signals. Your brain puts these together, fills in gaps and decides what you are looking at. Here is some evidence. Each eye has a blind spot, a place with no light-catching cells at all. You never notice a hole in your sight, because your brain fills it in. More evidence comes from India. Some children are born with cloudy lenses and grow up nearly blind. After an operation, their eyes work. But at first, many cannot match a shape they have felt with their hands to the same shape they see. Their brains have to learn to see, and many of them learn within days. Exactly how a brain turns signals into the picture you see is one of the biggest unanswered questions in science.
- e.g. Find your blind spot with a cross and a dot on a card. The dot vanishes, but you do not see a hole. You see plain card.
- e.g. In India, Project Prakash, led by the scientist Pawan Sinha, has helped many children who were born blind to see after surgery. It studies how their brains learn to see.
- e.g. When you look at clouds, your brain sometimes sees faces and animals that are not really there.
### Light comes in tiny packets, and your eye can catch a few · *debated*
Light comes in tiny packets called photons. A torch sends out billions of billions of them every second. When a photon lands on a rod cell in your retina, it hits one molecule. A molecule is a tiny group of atoms, far too small to see. The photon makes that molecule change its shape, from bent to straight. That tiny change starts the signal that goes to your brain. So every time you see anything, it starts with photons changing the shape of molecules. Scientists have asked: how few photons can a person notice? Here are the answers so far.
- e.g. A single rod cell can respond to a single photon. Denis Baylor, Trevor Lamb and King-Wai Yau measured this in 1979.
- e.g. On a dark night, the faint star you can just see may be sending only about a thousand photons into your eye each second.
- **Selig Hecht, Simon Shlaer and Maurice Pirenne, in New York, 1942:** They sat people in total darkness and flashed very dim lights at them. A person could notice a flash when only about 5 to 14 photons were caught by rod cells. So the eye is close to as sensitive as anything can be.
- **Alipasha Vaziri and his team, at Rockefeller University in New York, 2016:** They used a special light source that could send exactly one photon at a time. Their volunteers guessed a little better than chance when a single photon had arrived. So maybe a person can sometimes sense just one photon.
- **Other scientists:** They say the single-photon result was small, and needs to be repeated by other teams before everyone accepts it. Nobody doubts that rod cells can catch one photon. The question is whether the whole person notices.
### Many kinds of eyes, and seeing without eyes · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things have many different kinds of eyes, and some sense light with no eyes at all. All of them work by catching light that comes in.

Our eyes are not the only design. Most spiders have eight eyes. A scallop, a kind of shellfish, can have up to two hundred tiny eyes along the edge of its shell. Each scallop eye uses a curved mirror at the back, made of tiny crystals, instead of a lens. A box jellyfish has twenty-four eyes. Scientists think eyes have appeared many separate times in the history of life. But every kind of eye works the same basic way. Light comes in, and a molecule catches it. Even living things with no eyes sense light. A money plant on a windowsill bends towards the window. Tiny one-celled living things in a pond swim towards light, using a little light-sensing spot.
- e.g. A dragonfly's eyes are made of thousands of tiny eyes packed together.
- e.g. A sunflower bud turns to follow the Sun across the sky. It has no eyes, but it senses light.
- e.g. Earthworms have no eyes, but their skin can sense light. They wriggle away from it.
### Do we see the world as it really is? · *debated*
When you look at a tree, it feels as if you are seeing the tree itself, right there. But we know that light bounced off the tree, came into your eyes, became signals, and your brain built the picture. So what are you really seeing? People give different answers.
- e.g. A bee sees patterns on flowers that our eyes cannot catch. Which flower is the real one, the bee's or ours?
- e.g. Two people can look at the same picture and see different things, like a duck or a rabbit.
- **Many people, most of the time:** We see the world as it is. The tree is green and leafy, and that is what we see.
- **Anil Seth, a British neuroscientist, in his book Being You, 2021:** The brain is always guessing what is out there. It checks its guesses against the signals from the eyes. What we see is the brain's best guess, which is usually very good.
- **James Gibson, an American scientist who studied seeing, 1979:** We do not need a picture inside the head. The light coming into our eyes already carries rich information about the world, and we pick it up directly as we move around.

## Mostly agreed
- We see things only when light from them enters our eyes. *How we know:* In complete darkness nobody sees anything, even with healthy eyes wide open. Block the light between a thing and your eyes, and the thing disappears from view.
- Most things make no light. We see them because they bounce light from a source into our eyes. *How we know:* Switch off the lamp in a closed room, and the table, the books and your hand all vanish together. Switch it on, and they all appear together.
- The retina at the back of the eye turns light into signals, and the optic nerve carries them to the brain. *How we know:* Doctors can look at the retina through the pupil. Scientists have measured the tiny electric signals that retina cells make when light lands on them.
- The pupil gets bigger in dim light and smaller in bright light, by itself. *How we know:* Anyone can watch it happen in a mirror. Doctors check it with a small torch.
- Rod cells can respond to a single photon, and after time in the dark our eyes become much more sensitive. *How we know:* Scientists have measured single rod cells catching single photons. Tests in dark rooms show that what people can see improves for about half an hour.

## How we found out
**Ibn al-Haytham, then Johannes Kepler and Christoph Scheiner, about 1021 (the Book of Optics), 1604 (Kepler) and 1619 (Scheiner).** For more than a thousand years, many thinkers believed that seeing starts in the eye. Euclid, about 2,300 years ago, drew rays going out of the eye in a cone. Ptolemy, and later the scholar al-Kindi in Baghdad, agreed. Then Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo, tested the idea and wrote the Book of Optics, finished around 1021. He set several lamps outside a small hole into a dark room. Each lamp made its own spot of light on the wall inside. When he covered one lamp, only its spot went out. So light travels in straight lines from each lamp to where it lands, and he argued that the same happens when light enters an eye. But nobody yet knew where in the eye the picture forms. In 1604, Johannes Kepler worked out that the eye bends the light into a small, upside-down picture on the retina. In 1619, Christoph Scheiner checked this. He peeled away the back of an animal's eye and saw the tiny upside-down picture himself.  
*What people believed before:* Seeing starts in the eye: rays go out of the eye and touch what we see. The lamp test showed that light travels from things to where it lands, and the upside-down picture found on the retina showed where that light ends up.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Our eyes send out rays that touch what we look at.** (Euclid and Ptolemy, who worked in Alexandria in Egypt, the Greek thinkers Empedocles and Plato, and later al-Kindi in Baghdad. Many children today still draw arrows coming out of the eyes.; *rejected*)  
  For: Looking feels like something we do, reaching out to the world. We turn our eyes to point at things, a bit like pointing a torch.  
  Against: If eyes sent out rays, we would see in the dark. Bright light hurts the eye, which shows something comes in. About 1,000 years ago, Ibn al-Haytham put these clues together, and today we can measure the light landing on the retina.
- **If you wait long enough in a totally dark room, you will start to see.** (Many people believe this, because their eyes do get better in the dark after a while.; *rejected*)  
  For: In a dim room, your eyes really do get much more sensitive after twenty minutes. Things that were invisible appear.  
  Against: That only works when there is a little light, like starlight or a crack under the door. In a room with no light at all, there is nothing for the eye to catch. No amount of waiting helps.
- **Cats can see in complete darkness.** (A very common belief, because cats' eyes glow at night and cats move easily in dim light.; *rejected*)  
  For: Cats really can see in about one-sixth of the light we need. Their pupils open very wide, and a shiny layer behind the retina gives light a second chance to be caught.  
  Against: The glow is light bouncing back, not light made by the eye. With no light at all, a cat sees nothing. It finds its way with its whiskers, ears and nose.
- **Eating lots of carrots lets you see in the dark.** (A story spread in Britain during the Second World War, about pilots who ate carrots.; *rejected*)  
  For: Carrots have something the body turns into vitamin A. The rod cells need vitamin A, and people who badly lack it get night blindness. Eating carrots and green leafy vegetables really does fix that.  
  Against: If your body already has enough vitamin A, extra carrots will not give you better night vision than normal. The wartime story also helped keep secret that the pilots were really using radar.
- **People can feel when someone is staring at the back of their head.** (Many people feel this is true. The biologist Rupert Sheldrake has run tests that he says support it.; *minority*)  
  For: Most of us have turned around and found someone looking at us. Sheldrake reported results a little better than chance.  
  Against: Nothing comes out of the eyes to be felt. We remember the times we turned and someone was looking, and forget the times nobody was. Other scientists who ran careful tests found no effect.
- **The eye works just like a camera, and the brain simply looks at the photo.** (A common way of explaining eyes in books and classrooms.; *debated*)  
  For: The eye and a camera both have a hole that lets light in, something to make the picture sharp, and a layer at the back that catches the light.  
  Against: There is no little person inside the head looking at a photo. The brain builds what we see from signals, fills in the blind spot, and guesses. People who gain sight as children have to learn to see shapes.

## Roots
We have been learning how we see: light bounces off things and comes into our eyes. Now we ask what seeing tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Looking feels like reaching out from our eyes, like a torch beam. Yet in the closed dark box, wide-open eyes saw nothing. The white paper appeared only when light got in and bounced off it into the eye. So looking feels like sending something out, but seeing is receiving.  
*Habit it breaks:* It questions the habit of trusting how something feels as a picture of how it works. Looking feels like something we send out, and that feeling is why people believed in eye-rays for more than a thousand years.  
*Evidence:* In the dark box, open eyes saw nothing until the lid let light in. In the lamps activity, covering one torch put out only its own spot.

- *who am i:* You are a light catcher. Light that touched the mango a moment ago is now inside your eye.
- *what is life:* Many living things, from a money plant on a windowsill to an owl, sense light and live by it.
- *what is this world:* Light is always reflecting from object to object, whether anyone is looking or not.

By day, one Sun lights almost everything we see. The mango, the road and your friend's face are all lit by that same light. Each object reflects it in its own way, and that is why they look different. So when you look around, you are catching one light that has touched everything in front of you. DeepKids believes this is a small, real picture of oneness: many things, joined by the one light that passes between them and into us.

- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.3.2 to 4.3.6:** King Janaka asks the sage Yajnavalkya: 'What light does a person live by?' The Sun, says Yajnavalkya. When the Sun has set? The Moon. When the Moon has set? Fire. When the fire has gone out? Speech, a voice to follow. And when all of these are gone? Then the self is the person's own light. (We have learned that eyes see only by light from outside, from the Sun, the Moon or a fire. The sage then asks about a different kind of light: the awareness that knows the seeing, even in the dark.)
- **Kena Upanishad 1.2:** It is the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind, and the eye of the eye. (The eye catches light, and the brain makes sense of it. But who, or what, is aware of the seeing? The Upanishad points to something behind the eye.)
- **Majjhima Nikaya 18, the Madhupindika Sutta:** The Buddha's teaching, as told by his student Mahakaccana: depending on the eye and on what is seen, eye-consciousness, or seeing, arises. The meeting of the three is called contact. (Seeing is not owned by the eye alone, or by the thing alone. It arises when they meet. Science adds one more thing they need: light. Take the light away, as in the dark box, and seeing does not arise.)
- Science (settled): We see only when light from things enters our eyes and lands on the retina, which sends signals to the brain.
- Science (open): How the brain turns those signals into the experience of seeing is not known. Scientists call this an open problem.
- Other view, Many neuroscientists, and the philosopher Daniel Dennett: Seeing is just the eye and brain processing light. There is no little watcher inside the head, and no need for an 'eye of the eye'. When we understand the brain well enough, there will be nothing left to explain. That is an honest view, and many careful people hold it.
- Practice, Let the light come in (3 min): Let's sit by the window. Close your eyes and take three slow breaths. Now open them very slowly. Don't look at anything in particular. Just let the light come to you. Notice that you don't have to do anything to see. The light from the room comes in by itself. Now close your eyes again. Where did the room go? Open them once more, and let the light back in.
- Ask: Light from the mango is inside your eye. Is the mango you see out there, or in here?
- Ask: If seeing just happens when light comes in, who is the one who sees?
- Ask: Is the world still there when you close your eyes? How do you know?
- Ask: When you and a friend look at each other, what are you sharing?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you look at a mango, is the mango coming into you, or are you going out to the mango? (hint: Think about what travels. Where did the light start, and where did it end up?)
- *deep:* If nobody is in the room and the light is on, is the chair still being seen? (hint: Light is still bouncing off the chair. Is bouncing the same as seeing?)
- *deep:* Why can't you see your own eyes without a mirror or a photo? (hint: For you to see something, its light has to travel into your eyes. Where would the light from your eye have to go?)
- *deep:* Is a colour in the mango, in the light, or in your brain? (hint: In a dark room, does the mango still have a colour? Ask again after the colour unit.)
- *big:* Your brain fills in your blind spot. What else might your brain be filling in without telling you? (hint: Remember the vanishing dot. You saw plain card where there was really a dot.)

## Story: The Lamp-Seller's Daughter
*Ibn al-Haytham was real. He lived in Cairo and wrote the Book of Optics, the Kitab al-Manazir. In it he describes lamps placed outside a small hole into a dark room, and how covering one lamp makes its spot of light on the wall vanish. Later writers tell two stories about him: that he promised the Caliph al-Hakim he could control the floods of the Nile, and that, when he could not, he pretended to be mad and was kept in his house until the Caliph died in 1021. Historians are not sure these stories are true. Zaynab, her father and their conversations are made up.*

In the city of Cairo, a thousand years ago, there was a house that everybody walked past quickly. The shutters were always closed. People said a madman lived inside. They said he had once been a great scholar. He had promised the Caliph he could tame the river Nile. Then he had failed, and his mind had broken.

Zaynab was ten. Every Friday she carried a jar of lamp oil to that house, because her father sold oil and the madman's servant paid well. She always set the jar down by the door and ran.

Her grandmother had warned her. "Never let a madman look at you," she said. "Eyes throw out rays, like a lamp throws out light. A strange man's eyes can reach out and touch you."

Zaynab believed it. Everyone did. Even the old books said so. The great Greek teachers had written that we see by sending invisible rays out of our eyes, like long thin fingers.

One Friday, the servant was ill, and the door was open. A voice called from inside. "Bring the oil in, please. I will not bite."

Zaynab stood still. Then, because she was curious, which was always her trouble, she stepped inside.

The room was dark. Not dim, but truly dark, like the inside of a closed fist. She could not see her own hand. She could not see the jar she was holding. Somewhere in the blackness, a man was breathing.

"Can you see me?" said the voice.

"No," said Zaynab.

"But your eyes are open?"

"Wide open."

"Then where are your rays?" said the voice. It did not sound mad. It sounded amused. "If your eyes threw out rays to touch things, they would touch me now. The dark would not stop them. Yet you see nothing. Why?"

Zaynab had no answer. She waited for her eyes to work. They did not.

"Here," said the voice. There was a scrape, and a thin bar of the shutter slid aside. A hole no bigger than a chickpea opened, high on the wall.

And something happened that Zaynab never forgot.

On the white wall opposite the hole, a picture appeared. It was faint and soft, as if painted in smoke. But it was the street outside. There was the fruit seller's yellow awning. There was a donkey, and a man leading it. They were upside down, and they were moving. The donkey walked slowly across the wall on its head.

Zaynab cried out. "Is it magic?"

"It is light," said the man. Now she could see him a little, by the glow from the wall. He was thin, with ink on his fingers and a gentle face. "The sun lands on the donkey. From every part of the donkey, light bounces off and spreads out in all directions. A little of that light comes through my hole and lands on my wall. The light comes from the donkey to us. Nothing goes from us to the donkey."

"But the old books say..."

"The old books say many things." He smiled. "Euclid was a great man. Ptolemy was a great man. I have read every word they wrote. But a book is not a lamp. A lamp you can test."

He took her oil and filled three small lamps. He set them on a shelf outside the second shutter, side by side. Then he closed the room again, leaving only one small hole. On the wall inside, three little spots of light appeared, one for each lamp.

"Now," he said, "go outside and put your hand in front of the first lamp."

She did. When she came back, he told her. One spot had gone out while her hand was there. Only that one. The other two had glowed on.

"Each lamp sends its own light," he said. "It travels through the hole to the wall. Block the lamp, and its light never arrives. My eyes are like this room, little lamp-seller. They are dark rooms with a small hole in front. The light of the world comes in. I do not reach out for it. I wait for it, and I catch it."

Zaynab thought about this all the way home. She thought about her grandmother, and the rays, and the madman's eyes.

That night there was no moon. She lay on the roof and looked at the stars. Before, she had thought her eyes were reaching all the way out to them, like fingers stretching across the sky. Now she knew it was the other way round. Light from each star had come all that way and dropped into her eyes. Tiny lamps, so far away, were sending her their light.

She closed her eyes. The stars vanished. She opened them, and the stars were back. Her eyes had done nothing but open the door.

Every Friday after that, Zaynab went inside. The man showed her how a bright light hurts the eye and leaves a dark blob floating in your sight. "Something came in and pressed on you," he said. "Eyes are touched by light. They do not touch." He showed her how a dusty sunbeam glows only where the dust is.

One day the servant told her the Caliph had died. A few weeks later, the shutters of the house stood wide open. The man was gone. People said he had never been mad at all. He had only pretended, to stay alive. In the quiet years in the dark room, he had been writing a book.

It was called the Book of Optics. About two hundred years later it was carried across the sea and copied into Latin. Scholars in Europe read it for centuries. They learned from it how light comes into the eye.

Zaynab never saw him again. But she sold lamp oil all her life. And whenever a child came to her stall, she would hold up a lamp and ask a question.

"Tell me," she would say. "When you look at this flame, who is doing the reaching? You, or the light?"

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn of the story is a dark room. With her eyes wide open, Zaynab sees nothing, so eyes cannot be sending out rays. When a small hole lets light in, the street appears on the wall, because light bounced off the donkey and travelled in. Covering one lamp makes only its spot vanish, which shows that each light travels separately from its source to where it lands. Seeing is catching light that comes in, not reaching out. The story also asks whether to trust a famous book or a test, and it quietly turns the 'madman' everyone avoided into the person who knew most.

- Ask: In the dark room, Zaynab's eyes were wide open. Why couldn't she see anything?
- Ask: What made the picture of the street appear on the wall?
- Ask: When Zaynab blocked one lamp, what happened? What does that show about where light goes?
- Ask: Zaynab's grandmother believed that eyes throw out rays. What would you say to her, kindly?
- Ask: People called the man mad and walked past his house quickly. Why do you think they were wrong about him?

### g2 · Photon Relay (15 min)
- **rules:** One player is the SUN, one is the MANGO, one is the EYE and one is the BRAIN. Others can be CLOUDS.; The SUN holds a basket of balls or rolled-up yellow socks. Each one stands for a tiny packet of light, a photon.; The SUN throws light balls gently towards the MANGO. The MANGO catches them and throws them on in any direction. This is bouncing.; The EYE stands with a basket held in front of them. It cannot move its feet. It only catches the balls that come its way.; Each ball that lands in the EYE's basket is passed back to the BRAIN, who stands behind the EYE with closed eyes. The MANGO whispers one clue word with each ball, like 'yellow' or 'round'. The BRAIN guesses what the thing is.; CLOUDS can step between the SUN and the MANGO to block light. Then nothing reaches the EYE.
- **Printable cards: Role cards to wear on a string:** SUN (the light source); MANGO (a lit object); EYE; BRAIN; CLOUD (a blocker)

- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give out the role cards. 2. Put the SUN at one end of the room and the MANGO in the middle. 3. Put the EYE to one side of the MANGO. 4. The BRAIN stands behind the EYE.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to act out how you see a mango. Each sock ball is a tiny packet of light. Scientists call one tiny packet of light a photon.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a role: SUN, MANGO, EYE or BRAIN. The socks are packets of light. Get in your places.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The SUN throws sock balls gently to the MANGO, one at a time. 2. The MANGO throws each one on in any direction, without aiming. 3. The EYE catches what it can and passes it to the BRAIN.

  *Child mode:* SUN, throw the light gently. MANGO, bounce it any way at all. EYE, stand still and catch. Pass what you catch to the BRAIN.

  *Child mode:* BRAIN, listen to the clues that came with your balls. What is the thing?
  - Ask: Why did the EYE not catch every ball? (good: The mango bounced them every way.; The eye only catches what comes towards it.)
  - Check: In Photon Relay, what does the EYE do? → **It catches the balls that come to it**

  *Parent says:* Now the CLOUD comes. What happens to the light reaching the EYE?  
  *Child mode:* CLOUD, step in front of the SUN. What happens now? Does the EYE still get any light?
  - Think: The MANGO is still there when the cloud comes. Why can't the EYE see it?
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit down together and ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Let's sit down and talk about the game. What do you think now about how we see? What happened in the game that shows it? And why does it work that way?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: we see when light comes into our eyes. What I saw: the EYE only knew about the mango when balls landed in its basket. Why: the mango makes no light, so it has to bounce light from the Sun to the eye.

  *Parent says:* The torch is still shining on the toy. Now I put the card in front of your eyes. Can you see it? Now the card goes between the torch and the toy.  
  *Child mode:* The torch still shines on the toy. A card goes in front of your eyes. Can you see the toy now? Now the card blocks the torch. What happens?
  - Check: The torch is shining on the toy, but a card is in front of your eyes. Why can't you see the toy? → **Light bouncing off the toy can't reach your eyes**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn off the torch, with nothing blocking anything.  
  *Parent says:* Last test. No card, but no torch. Can you see the toy?  
  *Child mode:* Last test. The torch goes off. No card. Can you see the toy?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to draw the torch, the toy and an eye, with arrows for the light. Then ask what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the light's path with arrows. Which way do the arrows go? Then tell me what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the torch, the toy and your eye. Draw arrows for the light. Then say: what I think, what I saw, why.
  - Note: Look at the arrows. Many children draw an arrow from the eye to the toy. If your child does, ask: 'In the dark room, did your eye send something out? Then why couldn't you see?' A good answer: What I think: to see the toy, light must go from the torch to the toy and then into my eye. What I saw: when the card blocked either part, the toy vanished. Why: the toy makes no light, so it needs to bounce light from the torch into my eye.

### a3 · Night Eyes: 20 minutes in the dark (30 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up must be with the child on a balcony, terrace or roof at night. Stay well away from the edge.

- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On a clear night, go with your child to the darkest place you can reach, like a balcony or a terrace. 2. Turn off any lights you can. 3. Pick one patch of sky. Look at it through the cardboard tube, and count the stars you see.  
  *Parent says:* Look through the tube at this patch of sky. Count every star you can see. We'll count again in a while and see if anything changes.  
  *Child mode:* With a grown-up, go somewhere dark at night. Look at the sky through the tube. Count the stars you see. Write the number.
  - Note: In a big city you may see only a few stars, or none. Then do it indoors instead. Put 10 small objects, like coins, buttons and a white paper, on the floor of a dark room at night, with only a little light from under the door. Count how many you can see at the start, after 10 minutes and after 20 minutes.
- **s2 (20 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit quietly for 10 minutes. Do not look at a phone or a white light. If you need to see your notebook, use the red torch. 2. Count the stars in the same patch again. 3. Wait 10 more minutes and count once more.

  *Child mode:* Sit still for 10 minutes. No phone, no bright light. Use only the red torch. Then count the stars again. Wait 10 more minutes. Count again.
  - Note: Most people count more stars at 10 minutes and even more at 20. The pupils open wide in a few seconds, but the bigger change is the rod cells building up their light-catching chemical. Rod cells hardly respond to red light, so a dim red torch does not undo their work. While you wait, tell your child about Ibn al-Haytham, or play a quiet guessing game about night sounds.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Back inside, help your child draw a bar chart of the three counts. Then ask what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make a bar for each count. What happened to the number of stars? The sky didn't change, so what did?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each count. Did the number go up? The sky stayed the same. So what changed?
  - Check: You counted more stars after 20 minutes in the dark. What changed? → **Your eyes became more sensitive to dim light**
### a4 · Ibn al-Haytham's Lamps (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes. Never shine the torches into anyone's eyes.
- **record:** Draw the three spots on the screen. Then draw what happened when you covered each torch.
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts a square hole, about 3 cm wide, in one short end of the shoebox, and tapes foil over it. 2. They make one small hole in the foil with a pin or pencil tip. 3. Cut away the other short end and tape tracing paper over it. This is the screen.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to make a little dark room, like Ibn al-Haytham's. Light can only get in through one tiny hole.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes a small hole in foil on the box. Tape tracing paper over the other end. That is your screen.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the room dark. 2. Stand three torches in a row on a table, about one hand apart, all pointing at the pinhole. 3. Put the box about half a metre away. 4. Your child looks at the tracing-paper screen, with the dark cloth over their head and the end of the box.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the screen. How many spots of light can you see?  
  *Child mode:* Turn on three torches in a row. Point them at the hole. Put the cloth over your head. Look at the screen. How many spots?
  - Note: You should see three small spots of light on the screen, one for each torch. Notice that the order is swapped. The left torch makes the right spot. That is because light travels in straight lines through the hole, which another unit explains. Today we only need this: each torch makes its own spot. If the spots are too faint, make the pinhole a little bigger, or move the torches closer.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover one torch with your hand. Your child watches the screen and says which spot vanished. Then try the other torches.  
  *Parent says:* I'm covering one torch now. What happened on the screen?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up covers one torch. Watch the screen. What happened? Try each torch.
  - Check: When one torch was covered, only one spot went out. What does that show? → **Each torch's light travels from the torch into the box**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Like Ibn al-Haytham, tell me: what do you think now about where light goes? What did you see? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: light goes from each torch into the box. What I saw: when one torch was covered, only its spot vanished. Why: each torch sends its own light, and blocking it stops that light arriving. Your eye works the same way: it is a dark room with a small hole, the pupil.
  - Think: Your eye is a small dark room with a hole at the front. What is the 'screen' inside your eye called?

## Worksheets
### How we see: the basics (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What do we need to see a book?  
   Options: Light from the book coming into our eyes / Our eyes sending rays to the book / Only our eyes, nothing else / A loud noise  
   **Answer:** Light from the book coming into our eyes. *Why:* We see a book when light bounces off it into our eyes. Picking 'eyes sending rays' is the very old idea that eyes reach out to see. Picking 'only our eyes' forgets that in a dark room even healthy eyes see nothing.
2. (mcq) Which one is a light source?  
   Options: The Moon / A mirror / A lit candle / A steel plate  
   **Answer:** A lit candle. *Why:* A light source makes its own light. A lit candle does. The Moon, a mirror and a steel plate only bounce light from somewhere else. Picking the Moon is very common, because it looks so bright at night. But it is a rock lit by the Sun.
3. (fill) Things that make their own light are called light ____.  
   **Answer:** ['sources']. *Why:* A light source makes its own light, like the Sun, a bulb or a fire.
4. (sort) Sort these. Does it make its own light, or does it only bounce light?  
   **Answer:** the Sun → Makes its own light; a mango → Only bounces light; a diya flame → Makes its own light; the Moon → Only bounces light; a firefly → Makes its own light; your face → Only bounces light; a tubelight that is on → Makes its own light; a white wall → Only bounces light. *Why:* Light sources make their own light. Everything else we see, including the Moon and the white wall, only bounces light that came from a source.
5. (truefalse) In a room with no light at all, you can see if you open your eyes very wide.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Wide-open eyes still need light to catch. With no light, there is nothing to see. The dark box test showed this.
6. (mcq) You are in the dark box test. The lid is shut. What do you see through the peephole?  
   Options: The white paper only / Nothing at all / Everything, but in grey  
   **Answer:** Nothing at all. *Why:* With the lid sealed, no light gets in, so no light can bounce off the things into your eye. Picking 'the white paper only' is what happens when a crack of light comes in. Picking 'everything in grey' is how things look in dim light, not in no light.
7. (mcq) In the dark box test, which thing do you usually see first when a little light comes in?  
   Options: The red ball / The white paper / They all appear at the same moment  
   **Answer:** The white paper. *Why:* White things bounce back the most light, so they show up first. Picking the red ball usually means thinking bright colours show best in the dark. In dim light, colours are the last thing we see.
8. (order) Put the light's journey in order, so that you see a mango.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Light starts at a source, lands on the thing, bounces off it, enters the eye, and the eye sends signals to the brain.
9. (match) Match each part of the eye to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Pupil → a hole that lets light in; Iris → makes the pupil bigger or smaller; Retina → catches the light at the back of the eye; Optic nerve → carries signals to the brain. *Why:* Light goes in through the pupil. The iris changes its size. The retina catches the light. The optic nerve carries the signals to the brain.
10. (mcq) Why does the pupil look black?  
   Options: It is painted black inside / It is a hole, and very little light comes back out of it / It is the darkest part of the iris  
   **Answer:** It is a hole, and very little light comes back out of it. *Why:* The pupil is a hole into the dark inside of the eye. Light goes in and hardly any comes back out, so it looks black. Picking 'painted black' is a common idea, but there is no paint. Picking 'darkest part of the iris' mixes up the coloured ring with the hole in the middle.
11. (mcq) You come in from a sunny playground into a dim room. What do your pupils do?  
   Options: Get bigger, to let in more light / Get smaller, to keep light out / Stay the same  
   **Answer:** Get bigger, to let in more light. *Why:* In dim light the pupils open wide to catch as much light as they can. Picking 'get smaller' is what happens when you go into bright light. Picking 'stay the same' misses that the pupil changes by itself.
12. (fill) The black circle in the middle of your eye is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pupil']. *Why:* The pupil is the hole in the middle of the iris. Light enters the eye through it.
13. (draw) Draw a lamp, a ball and an eye. Draw arrows to show how the eye sees the ball.  
   **Answer:** One arrow goes from the lamp to the ball. Another arrow goes from the ball into the eye.. *Why:* Light goes from the source to the thing, then bounces into the eye. No arrow comes out of the eye.
14. (truefalse) The Moon makes its own light.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Moon is a rock. We see it because sunlight bounces off it into our eyes.
15. (write) A torch is shining on a toy, but you hold a book in front of your eyes. Why can't you see the toy? Use the word 'light' in your answer.  
   **Answer:** The light that bounces off the toy cannot reach my eyes, because the book is in the way. The toy is still lit, but no light from it comes into my eyes.. *Why:* Seeing needs the whole journey: source, then thing, then eye. Breaking any part stops you seeing.
16. (mcq) Which of these helps you see in the dark?  
   Options: Waiting about 20 minutes in a dim place, away from bright lights / Eating lots of carrots / Staring very hard  
   **Answer:** Waiting about 20 minutes in a dim place, away from bright lights. *Why:* After about 20 minutes in dim light, the rod cells in your eyes get much more sensitive. Picking carrots is a famous myth. Carrots keep eyes healthy, but extra carrots do not give normal eyes night vision. Picking 'staring hard' doesn't help, because the eye can only catch light that comes in.
### How we see: going further (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Everyone in a classroom can see the blackboard at once. Why?  
   Options: The board bounces light off in every direction, so light reaches every child / Each child's eyes send a ray to the board / The board makes its own light  
   **Answer:** The board bounces light off in every direction, so light reaches every child. *Why:* Matt surfaces like a blackboard spray light in all directions. So some light from the board reaches every seat. Picking 'eyes send a ray' is the old eye-rays idea. Picking 'makes its own light' mixes a lit object up with a light source.
2. (mcq) A torch beam crosses a dark room. It is hard to see from the side, until mist is sprayed into it. Why does the mist help?  
   Options: Mist drops bounce some of the light sideways into your eyes / Mist makes light / Mist slows the light down so you can see it  
   **Answer:** Mist drops bounce some of the light sideways into your eyes. *Why:* We only see light that comes into our eyes. Without mist, the beam goes straight to the wall. Mist drops reflect a little light every way, and some reaches you. Picking 'mist makes light' mixes up a lit object with a source. Picking 'slows the light down' is wrong, because mist does not slow light enough to matter. What changes is where the light goes.
3. (truefalse) A cat's eyes glow in the dark because they make their own light.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The glow is light from a torch or headlight bouncing off a shiny layer at the back of the cat's eye. With no light at all, a cat's eyes do not glow.
4. (mcq) In the blind spot activity, the dot vanished. What did you see in its place?  
   Options: A black hole / Plain white card, because the brain filled it in / A bright light  
   **Answer:** Plain white card, because the brain filled it in. *Why:* The blind spot has no light-catching cells. But the brain fills the gap with what is around it, so you see plain card. Picking 'a black hole' is what many people expect. It shows how hard the brain works without telling us.
5. (write) Long ago, many people believed that eyes send out rays to touch what we see. Give two pieces of evidence that this is wrong.  
   **Answer:** First, in a totally dark room we see nothing, even with our eyes open. If eyes sent out rays, we would still see. Second, looking at a very bright light hurts and leaves a dark blob in our sight, which shows something came into the eye.. *Why:* Ibn al-Haytham used clues like these. Another good answer: when one lamp is covered, only its spot of light vanishes.
6. (mcq) Which part of the eye turns light into signals for the brain?  
   Options: The iris / The retina / The eyelid  
   **Answer:** The retina. *Why:* The retina at the back of the eye has millions of light-catching cells, the rods and the cones. They turn light into signals. The iris only changes the size of the pupil, and the eyelid protects the eye.
7. (match) Match each living thing to how it senses light.  
   **Answer:** A scallop → up to 200 small eyes with tiny mirrors; A money plant → no eyes, but it bends towards the window; An owl → huge eyes full of rod cells; A spider → eight eyes. *Why:* Living things sense light in many ways, but all of them catch light that comes in.
8. (fill) The cells in the retina that work best in dim light are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['rods', 'rod cells']. *Why:* Rods work in dim light but do not see colours. Cones need brighter light and see colours.
9. (mcq) Why do astronomers use red torches when looking at stars?  
   Options: Red light disturbs their night eyes less than white light / Red light is brighter / Stars are red  
   **Answer:** Red light disturbs their night eyes less than white light. *Why:* Rod cells, the ones that work in dim light, hardly respond to red light. So a dim red torch lets you read a map without undoing the 20 minutes your rods spent getting used to the dark. A white light would undo it in a moment. Picking 'red light is brighter' is not right: the torch is kept dim on purpose. Picking 'stars are red' is not right either. Most stars look white to us.
10. (truefalse) The smallest packet of light is called a photon.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Light comes in tiny packets called photons. A rod cell in your eye can respond to just one.
11. (order) Put these in order from the front of the eye to the back.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Light passes through the clear cornea, then the pupil, then the lens, then the jelly that fills the eye, and lands on the retina at the back.
12. (mcq) In Ibn al-Haytham's lamps activity, you covered one torch. What happened?  
   Options: All three spots went out / Only that torch's spot went out / Nothing changed  
   **Answer:** Only that torch's spot went out. *Why:* Each torch sends its own light through the hole. Block one, and only its light stops arriving. This shows light travels from each source to where it lands.
13. (write) Your friend says, 'I can see the Moon, so the Moon must make light.' What would you tell your friend?  
   **Answer:** We can see many things that make no light, like a book or a face. We see them because light bounces off them into our eyes. The Moon is like that. Sunlight bounces off it to us.. *Why:* Seeing a thing does not mean it makes light. Most things we see are lit objects.
14. (mcq) In the Eye in a Box project, which pupil made the brightest picture on the retina screen?  
   Options: The biggest hole / The smallest hole / All were the same  
   **Answer:** The biggest hole. *Why:* A bigger hole lets in more light, so the screen glows brighter. That is why your pupil opens wide in the dark.
15. (draw) Draw a cat in the dark with glowing eyes, and a person holding a torch. Draw arrows to show where the glow comes from.  
   **Answer:** An arrow from the torch to the cat's eye, and an arrow bouncing back from the cat's eye to the person.. *Why:* Eyeshine is torchlight bouncing back from a shiny layer inside the cat's eye. No arrow should start inside the cat's eye.
### How we see: thinking like Ibn al-Haytham (challenge, 19 questions)
1. (write) Ibn al-Haytham said do not just trust famous books, test them. Describe a test that would show a friend that eyes do not send out rays.  
   **Answer:** Sit my friend in a totally dark room with eyes open, and ask what they see. Nothing. Then let in a little light, and things appear. If eyes sent out rays, they would see in the dark too.. *Why:* A good test changes one thing, the light, and keeps the eyes the same.
2. (mcq) Selig Hecht and his team found that people can notice a flash when only a few photons are caught. What does this tell us?  
   Options: The eye is extremely sensitive to light / The eye needs huge amounts of light / Photons are big  
   **Answer:** The eye is extremely sensitive to light. *Why:* Catching only about 5 to 14 photons is close to the smallest amount of light there can be. So the eye is one of the most sensitive light detectors we know. Picking 'huge amounts' gets it backwards. Picking 'photons are big' is wrong, because photons are far too small to see one by one.
3. (truefalse) Scientists all agree that a person can notice a single photon.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Rod cells can respond to one photon. One 2016 study suggested a person can sometimes notice a single photon, but other scientists want it repeated before they agree.
4. (write) Children who were born blind and had an operation to clear their cloudy lenses often could not, at first, match a shape they had felt with their hands to the same shape seen with their eyes. What does this tell us about seeing?  
   **Answer:** Seeing is not only done by the eyes. The brain has to learn to make sense of the signals. Their eyes worked after the operation, but their brains still needed time to learn.. *Why:* This was studied in India by Project Prakash, led by Pawan Sinha.
5. (mcq) Why do photos taken with a flash sometimes show red eyes?  
   Options: The flash reflects off the back of the eye, which is full of blood vessels / The eyes make red light / The camera is broken  
   **Answer:** The flash reflects off the back of the eye, which is full of blood vessels. *Why:* The flash goes in through the wide-open pupil, reflects off the back of the eye, which is rich in blood, and comes straight back to the camera. Picking 'the eyes make red light' mixes eyes up with light sources. Picking 'the camera is broken' does not fit, because the same camera takes normal photos in daylight.
6. (mcq) Why can't you see your own eyes without a mirror?  
   Options: Light from your eyes would have to bounce back into them, and nothing is there to bounce it / Your eyes are too close to see / Eyes are invisible  
   **Answer:** Light from your eyes would have to bounce back into them, and nothing is there to bounce it. *Why:* To see something, light from it must reach your eyes. Light from the front of your eye goes away from you, out into the room. A mirror reflects it back to you. Picking 'too close' does not fit, because you can see your nose, which is just as close. Picking 'invisible' is wrong, because other people can see your eyes.
7. (write) Light from a faint star travels a very long way and enters your eye. Explain, using the word 'photon', what happens when it reaches your retina.  
   **Answer:** A photon from the star comes in through my pupil and lands on a rod cell in my retina. It hits a tiny molecule and makes it change shape. That starts a signal that goes along the optic nerve to my brain.. *Why:* Every act of seeing starts with photons changing the shape of molecules in the retina.
8. (mcq) A scallop has eyes with tiny mirrors instead of lenses. A plant has no eyes but bends to the light. What do all these ways of sensing light have in common?  
   Options: They all catch light that comes in / They all send out rays / They all make their own light  
   **Answer:** They all catch light that comes in. *Why:* Every eye and every light-sensing part catches light that arrives. None sends rays out. Some living things also make light, but that is a different unit.
9. (sort) Sort these statements about seeing.  
   **Answer:** We see when light enters our eyes. → Scientists agree; The pupil shrinks in bright light. → Scientists agree; Exactly how the brain turns signals into what we experience. → Still argued about or unknown; Whether a person can notice one single photon. → Still argued about or unknown; Rod cells work best in dim light. → Scientists agree; Whether we see the world as it really is. → Still argued about or unknown. *Why:* The path of light and the parts of the eye are well tested. How brains make experience, and the very edge of how sensitive we are, are still open.
10. (mcq) Rupert Sheldrake says people can feel when they are being stared at. Why do most scientists disagree?  
   Options: Nothing comes out of the eyes to be felt, and careful tests found no effect / Because staring is rude / Because only cats can feel stares  
   **Answer:** Nothing comes out of the eyes to be felt, and careful tests found no effect. *Why:* Eyes catch light. They send nothing out. We tend to remember the times we turned and someone was looking, and forget the times nobody was. Other careful tests have found no effect.
11. (write) Some people say 'the eye is just a camera'. Give one way the eye is like a camera, and one way it is different.  
   **Answer:** Like a camera: both have a hole that lets light in and a layer at the back that catches it. Different: the brain builds the picture from signals, fills in the blind spot and has to learn to see. A camera just records.. *Why:* The camera idea helps with the front of the eye, but not with what the brain does.
12. (truefalse) After about 20 to 30 minutes in the dark, your eyes can become thousands of times more sensitive.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The rod cells slowly build up their light-catching chemical. This is why you see more stars after waiting.
13. (mcq) In the blind spot activity, the dot vanished when the card was about 25 cm from your eye. If you moved the card to 50 cm, what would happen?  
   Options: The dot would come back, because its light no longer lands on the blind spot / The dot would stay hidden at every distance / The cross would vanish too  
   **Answer:** The dot would come back, because its light no longer lands on the blind spot. *Why:* The dot only vanishes when its light lands exactly on the blind spot. At other distances it lands on cells that can catch light, so you see it again.
14. (draw) Draw the room in the Ibn al-Haytham story: the dark room, the small hole, and the donkey outside in the sunlight. Draw arrows to show how the picture of the donkey got onto the wall.  
   **Answer:** An arrow from the Sun to the donkey, then arrows from the donkey through the hole to the wall.. *Why:* Light from the Sun bounced off the donkey. Some of it came through the hole and landed on the wall.
15. (write) If light bounces off everything, why isn't every room full of images of everything, all mixed up?  
   **Answer:** Light from every point of every thing spreads out in all directions and mixes together in the room. That is why a white wall just looks bright, not like a picture. A small hole, or the pupil and lens of an eye, sorts the light so each point lands in one place. That makes a picture.. *Why:* This is the puzzle Ibn al-Haytham worked on. Another unit explains how a small hole makes a picture.
16. (mcq) Why do older people often need more light to read?  
   Options: Their lenses let less light through, and their pupils get smaller with age / They send out weaker rays / Books were printed smaller long ago  
   **Answer:** Their lenses let less light through, and their pupils get smaller with age. *Why:* As we grow old, the lens gets a little cloudy and yellow, and the pupil gets smaller. So less light reaches the retina. Picking 'weaker rays' is the old eye-rays idea.
17. (mcq) Which idea is the deep truth of this unit?  
   Options: Looking feels like sending something out, but seeing is really receiving light / Seeing is reaching out to grab the world / Seeing happens only in the eyes  
   **Answer:** Looking feels like sending something out, but seeing is really receiving light. *Why:* Looking feels like reaching out, but in the dark box, open eyes saw nothing until light came in. The eye catches the light and the brain makes sense of it. Picking 'reaching out' is the old eye-rays idea. Picking 'only in the eyes' forgets the brain's big part.
18. (write) Write two or three sentences for a younger child, explaining how they see their teddy bear.  
   **Answer:** The lamp shines light on your teddy. The light bounces off the teddy and goes into your eyes. Your eyes send a message to your brain, and your brain says 'teddy!'. *Why:* A good answer has a source, a bounce, the eye and the brain, in the right order.
19. (label) Label the parts of the eye on the drawing: cornea, iris, pupil, lens, retina, optic nerve.  
   **Answer:** Cornea at the very front, iris as the coloured ring, pupil as the hole in the middle, lens just behind it, retina lining the back, optic nerve leaving the back.. *Why:* Light passes through these parts in order from front to back, and the optic nerve carries the signal on.

## Quiz

   **Answer:** The Moon. *Why:* The Moon makes no light of its own. It is a rock lit by the Sun. The others all make their own light.

5. (mcq) Which part of the eye is a hole that lets light in?

## Applications
- Eye doctors look through the pupil at the retina with a bright light, to check that eyes are healthy.
- Cataract surgery replaces a cloudy lens with a clear one. Aravind Eye Hospital in Madurai, started in 1976 by Dr G. Venkataswamy with his sister Dr G. Natchiar and others, has done millions of these operations, many of them free.
- An old Indian medical book, the Sushruta Samhita, describes an early operation to push a cloudy lens out of the way.
- Cameras, including phone cameras, work by letting light in through a hole and catching it at the back, like an eye.
- Night drivers rely on headlights bouncing light off the road, animals and signs into their eyes.
- Pale or white paint on walls and ceilings makes rooms brighter, because it bounces more light around.
- Astronomers use dim red torches to protect their night eyes.
- Safety stripes on steps, kerbs and speed breakers are painted white or yellow, so they bounce enough light to be seen at dusk.
- Doctors check the pupils with a small torch after a head injury, because pupils should shrink in light.
- Sunglasses and caps protect the eyes by cutting down the light that comes in.
- Eye tests at school use a chart of letters getting smaller, to find out how sharply each child sees.
- Project Prakash in India helps children born with treatable blindness to see, and studies how the brain learns to see.

- Red eyes in family photos taken with a flash.

## Open questions
- **How does the brain turn signals from the eyes into the experience of seeing?** Scientists know which parts of the brain are busy when we see. But nobody knows how those signals become the feeling of seeing a red mango. Some call this 'the hard problem'.
- **Can a person notice just one single photon?** One study in 2016 said yes, sometimes. The result was small, and other teams need to repeat it.
- **Do other animals experience seeing the way we do?** We can measure what a bee's or an octopus's eyes respond to. We cannot get inside their experience.
- **Exactly how many times did eyes appear separately in the history of life?** Many scientists think eyes appeared dozens of times. But some of the genes for building eyes are shared by very different animals, so the history is still argued about.
- **Why do most people sometimes see faces in clouds and in the fronts of cars?** The brain is quick to find faces. Scientists are still working out exactly how and why it does this so readily.
- **How do children who gain sight late learn to see so quickly?** Project Prakash found many learn faster than expected. Exactly how the brain does this is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the pupil test, a child's pupil was 7 mm in the dim room and 3 mm next to the lamp. How many millimetres smaller did it get? **4 mm smaller.**. The pupil started at 7 mm in dim light. Next to the lamp it was 3 mm. 7 take away 3 is 4. So it shrank by 4 mm. You can check: 3 + 4 = 7. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In the pupil test, a child measured three times in the dim room: 6 mm, 7 mm and 7 mm. What is the middle value? **7 mm.**. Put the three numbers in order: 6, 7, 7. The middle one is the second number, which is 7. So the middle value is 7 mm. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- In the night eyes activity, a child counted 3 stars at the start, 7 after 10 minutes and 11 after 20 minutes. How many more stars could they see after 20 minutes than at the start? **8 more stars.**. At the start they saw 3 stars. After 20 minutes they saw 11. 11 take away 3 is 8. So they could see 8 more stars. The sky did not change. Their eyes did. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In the blind spot activity, the dot vanished when the card was 25 cm from the eye. Read the ruler in the picture. Is the card nearer or further than a 30 cm ruler's length? **Nearer, by 5 cm.**. The card was 25 cm away. A whole ruler is 30 cm. 25 is less than 30, so the card was nearer. 30 take away 25 is 5. So it was 5 cm nearer than a ruler's length. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- In the dark box test, 0 of the 3 things were seen with the lid shut, 2 with a crack open and 3 with the lid off. What fraction of the things could be seen with the lid open a crack? **2 out of 3, or two thirds.**. There were 3 things in the box. With the lid open a crack, 2 of them could be seen. So 2 out of 3 were seen. We write this as two thirds. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)

## Warm-up
- Does the Moon make its own light? → No. Sunlight bounces off it.
- What do we need to see a book: light, an eye, or both? → Both. Light must bounce off the book into an eye.
- What is the black circle in the middle of your eye called? → The pupil. It is a hole that lets light in.
- Bright sunshine: does your pupil get bigger or smaller? → Smaller.
- Do your eyes send out rays to see? → No. Light comes into your eyes.
- Which part at the back of the eye catches light? → The retina.
- Name one light source in this room. → For example, a bulb, a window with daylight, or a screen.
- Why do cats' eyes glow in headlights? → Light bounces back off a shiny layer inside them.
- What is the smallest packet of light called? → A photon.
- Who wrote the Book of Optics in Cairo? → Ibn al-Haytham.
