# Alive, once alive, never alive: the signs of life
`sci.what-is-life.signs-of-life.L1`

**Summary.** A crow, a mint leaf and a steel spoon sit on the same windowsill. Only the crow and the mint are alive. Living things eat, grow, move, breathe and have young. Some things were alive once, like a wooden spoon or a dry leaf. Some things were never alive, like a stone or a steel spoon. In this unit you sort the things in your kitchen, and find out if a picked mint leaf is still alive.

**Intro.** *Hold a fresh mint leaf in one hand and a steel spoon in the other. Both are cool. Both stay still. Which one is alive?* Most children say the mint leaf. But how can we tell? The spoon does not move, and the leaf does not move either. Scientists look for signs of life. A living thing eats, grows, moves, breathes and has young. A spoon does none of these. In this unit we test some of these signs at home, on mint leaves, on our breath and on ourselves.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say if a thing is alive, was once alive, or was never alive, and give one reason.
1. By the end I can name the signs of life: eating, growing, moving, breathing and having young.
2. By the end I can say that plants are alive too, and show one sign of life in a plant.
3. By the end I can match a mother animal to her young.
4. By the end I can make a guess, look carefully, and say if my guess was right.

## Terms
- **Alive**: Alive means living. A living thing eats, grows, moves, breathes and has young. *e.g.* You are alive. So is a crow, an ant and the tulsi plant on the balcony.
- **Never alive**: A thing that was never alive has never eaten, grown or breathed. It is not made from a living thing. *e.g.* A stone, a steel spoon, salt and water were never alive.
- **Once alive**: A thing that was once alive was part of a living thing, but is not living now. *e.g.* A wooden spoon came from a tree. A cotton shirt came from a cotton plant. A dry tej patta was a leaf on a tree.
- **Breathe**: To breathe is to take in air and let air out again. All living things breathe in some way. *e.g.* Breathe out on a cold steel plate. The cloudy patch shows the wet air from inside you.
- **Young**: The young of a living thing are their babies. Young grow up to be like their parents. *e.g.* A chick is the young of a hen. A calf is the young of a cow. A seed grows into a young plant.

## Explanations
### What living things do · *defined*
**Definition:** A living thing eats, grows, moves, breathes and has young. It also senses the world around it.

One sign alone does not tell us. A car moves, but it does not grow or have baby cars. A crow does all of these things. So does a mango tree, only more slowly. Animals eat food. Plants make their own food in their green leaves, using sunlight, air and water. Living things also feel what is around them. A cat hears her name. A plant feels the light.
- e.g. A crow eats, flies, breathes and lays eggs.
- e.g. A baby grows, drinks milk and breathes, and will one day be able to have babies.
- e.g. A toy car moves, but it never eats or grows.
### Once alive and never alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Once alive means the thing was part of a living thing, but is not living now. Never alive means it was never part of a living thing.

Look around the kitchen. A wooden spoon came from a tree, so it was once alive. Paper is made from trees too. A cotton cloth came from a cotton plant. A dry tej patta was a leaf. A steel spoon, salt, water and a stone were never alive. They were never part of a living thing.
- e.g. A wooden chopping board was once alive.
- e.g. A coconut shell was once alive.
- e.g. A steel tumbler was never alive.
### Plants are alive too · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants eat, grow, move, breathe and have young, just like animals, only in their own way.

Plants make their own food in their green leaves. They breathe through tiny holes in their leaves, too small to see. They grow taller and make new leaves. They move slowly. A plant on a windowsill turns their leaves to the light over a few days. Plants also have young. A tomato is full of seeds, and each seed can grow into a new tomato plant.
- e.g. A tulsi plant grows new leaves every week.
- e.g. A money plant on a window turns their leaves to face the light.
- e.g. A cut tomato shows many seeds, which are the young of the tomato plant.
### Is a picked leaf still alive? · *debated*
A leaf picked from a plant does not die at once. In our mint test, droopy picked mint stood up again in water. Picked mint can even grow new roots in a glass of water. But a picked leaf cannot make new seeds alone, and after some days it dries up. So people answer this question in different ways.
- e.g. Droopy picked mint stands up firm again in a cup of water.
- e.g. A mint sprig in water grows white roots in about one or two weeks.
- e.g. A dry tej patta in water stays dry and brown inside.
- **Many plant scientists:** The leaf is still alive for some days, because the tiny parts inside the leaf are still living. That is why the droopy mint could fill up with water again.
- **Some other scientists:** A picked leaf is a living part, not a whole living plant. The leaf cannot live on for long unless it grows roots of its own.
### Do plants feel? · *debated*
Plants feel light, touch, water and warmth, and they act on what they feel. Roots grow towards water. Leaves turn towards light. Some scientists say these actions are automatic, because plants have no brain.
- e.g. A plant on a window turns their leaves to the light.
- e.g. Roots grow down and towards wet soil.
- e.g. A droopy plant stands up again after a drink of water.
- **Many people who study plants closely:** Plants feel, remember and decide. They sense light and water and choose where to grow.
- **Many other scientists:** Plants have no brain, so their actions are automatic, like a door that closes by itself.

## Mostly agreed
- Living things eat, grow, move, breathe and have young. *How we know:* Scientists have watched millions of kinds of living things, from tiny bacteria to elephants and trees. All of them do these things.
- Plants are living things. *How we know:* Plants grow, make their own food, breathe through tiny holes in their leaves and make seeds that grow into new plants.
- Every living thing comes from another living thing. *How we know:* A chick hatches from a hen's egg. A plant grows from a seed. A caterpillar hatches from a butterfly's egg. Nobody has ever seen a living thing start from mud or stone.

## How we found out
**Maria Sibylla Merian, a German painter and scientist, with her daughter Dorothea, 1660 (when she began) to 1705 (her book on the insects of Suriname).** About 350 years ago, many people in Europe believed that some small animals were not born from parents. They thought caterpillars, flies and worms grew out of mud, rotting leaves or old meat. Maria Sibylla Merian was born in Frankfurt, in Germany, in 1647. When she was about 13, she began to keep caterpillars at home. She fed each one the leaves they liked and watched them every day. She saw butterflies lay tiny eggs on leaves. She saw caterpillars hatch from the eggs, eat and grow, and then turn into butterflies. She painted every step, with the plant each caterpillar ate. In 1679 she published a book of her paintings. Later, when she was 52, she sailed to Suriname in South America with her daughter Dorothea, and painted the insects and plants there. Her pictures showed clearly that a caterpillar comes from a butterfly's egg. Every living thing comes from another living thing.  
*What people believed before:* That small animals such as caterpillars, flies and worms grow by themselves out of mud, rotting leaves or old meat, without parents.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Anything that moves by itself is alive.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Most things that move by themselves are animals, like a dog, a crow or an ant.  
  Against: A ceiling fan, a river and the wind move, and they are not alive. In 1827, Robert Brown, a Scottish plant scientist, saw tiny bits jiggling in water under a microscope. At first he wondered if they were alive. Then he saw dust from rock and glass jiggle in the same way. So moving alone does not mean alive.
- **Plants are not really living, because they stay in one place.** (A common idea among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: A plant does not walk or run, and we rarely see a plant move.  
  Against: Plants grow, eat, breathe and make seeds. They move too, only slowly: leaves turn to light, and roots grow down. A plant moved to a window turns their leaves towards the light in a few days.
- **Fire is alive, because fire eats wood, needs air and grows.** (A puzzle many children and philosophers have raised; *rejected*)  
  For: A fire grows bigger, needs air to keep going and spreads to new places.  
  Against: A fire has no body that builds itself from food. A fire only burns the wood up and leaves ash. Scientists say fire is not alive.

## Roots
We have learned how to tell what is alive, once alive and never alive. Now we ask what the droopy mint tells us about being alive.

**Deep truth.** Our mint was picked before we bought it, and the sprigs had gone droopy. In a cup of water, they stood up firm again. The dry tej patta and the paper leaf stayed as they were. So a picked sprig stays alive for days, and living does not stop the moment we pick.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a thing is either alive or dead, and that picking a leaf ends their life at once. The mint shows that going from alive to once alive is slow, and takes days.  
*Evidence:* In the mint test, droopy picked mint stood up firm in water within about an hour, but the dry tej patta did not change. In the mint project, a picked sprig grew new roots in a glass of water.

- *who am i:* You are alive. You eat, grow, move and breathe every day, even while you sleep.
- *what is life:* Life is something a body keeps doing: taking in water and food, growing and making more. The picked mint kept doing it for days.
- *what is this world:* Many things around us were once alive: the wooden spoon, the cotton shirt, the paper in your book. Living things become part of the world around them.

The mint, the snail, the crow and you are all alive, each in their own way. All life on Earth comes from other life, going back a very long time. The wooden spoon and the paper were part of that life once. Everything is conscious, and every living thing feels and acts in their own way. The mint feels the water, and takes it in.

- **Tao Te Ching 76:** When people are born, they are soft and bendy. When they die, they are stiff and hard. Plants are soft and full of sap while they live. When they die, they are dry and brittle. (We felt this in the mint test. The living mint was soft, and stood up full of water. The dry tej patta was stiff and brittle, and water could not bring it back.)
- **Tirukkural 322:** Sharing your food and caring for all living beings is the best of all that the wise have taught. (Every living thing needs food and water. The Tirukkural, from Tamil Nadu, asks us to share them with all living beings, not only people.)
- Science (settled): Picked leaves stay alive for some days. Their tiny living parts can still take in water, which makes a droopy leaf firm again. Dry, dead leaves cannot do this.
- Science (open): Scientists do not agree on one exact moment when a picked leaf stops being alive.
- Other view, Many biologists: A picked sprig is a living part of a plant, not a whole living plant. They say the sprig is alive only in the sense that its tiny parts are still living, until it grows roots of its own.
- Practice, Soft leaf, dry leaf (3 min): Close your eyes. Hold out both hands. I will put a fresh leaf in one hand and a dry leaf in the other. Feel each one gently. Which one is alive? Now put your hand on your tummy. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly. Feel your own body, soft and warm and alive.
- Ask: When we pick a leaf, when does the leaf stop being alive?
- Ask: Is a little bit of the tree still in the wooden spoon?
- Ask: What would you need to stay alive if you were the mint in the glass?

## Story: The Stone in the Tulsi Pot
*The snail facts are true for most land snails: eyes on the tips of the long tentacles, a breathing hole on the side near the shell, a rough tongue called a radula, and small eggs laid in damp soil. Anjali, Kiran and Ajji are made up.*

It was the first week of the monsoon in Mumbai. The rain had stopped, and the air smelled of wet earth. Anjali was six. She went out on the balcony to look at her plants.

Her little cousin Kiran came running after her. He was four, and he was staying with them for the holidays. 'Anju Akka, look!' he shouted. 'A stone in your tulsi pot!'

Anjali bent down. On the wet soil sat a round brown thing, the size of a big marble. It was hard and shiny, and it had a curl on top, like a swirl of jalebi. It did not move at all.

'Is it a stone?' Kiran asked.

Anjali did not know. She called her grandmother, who was drinking tea inside. Ajji came out slowly, with her glasses on her nose. She looked at the brown thing for a long time.

'What do you think?' Ajji asked Anjali. 'How can we find out if this is a stone?'

Anjali thought. 'A stone stays where you put it,' she said. 'Let's leave it, and look again in the morning.'

So they left the brown thing in the tulsi pot. Before bed, Anjali put a little stick next to it, to mark the spot.

In the morning, Anjali ran to the balcony. The brown thing was not next to the stick any more. It was on the edge of the pot. And from the stick to the edge, there was a thin silver line, shining on the soil.

'It moved!' said Anjali. 'Stones do not move by themselves. Something left that silver line.'

Kiran's eyes were big. Then Anjali saw something else. One tulsi leaf had a hole in it, with neat, round edges. It had not been there yesterday.

'And it ate my tulsi!' said Anjali. 'Stones do not eat.'

Ajji smiled and fetched a small steel plate. She put a wet cabbage leaf on it and set the brown thing gently beside it. 'Now, sit very still,' she said. 'Living things are often shy.'

They sat on the balcony floor and waited. For a long time, nothing happened. Kiran started to wriggle. Then, slowly, something soft and grey came out from under the brown curl. Out came a head with two long horns, and on the tip of each horn was a tiny black dot.

'A snail!' whispered Anjali. 'The round thing is a shell. The snail lives inside.'

'Those dots are the snail's eyes,' said Ajji. 'They are at the top of the long horns. And look there, near the shell, on the snail's side.'

Anjali looked. A little hole opened, then closed, then opened again.

'That is how a snail breathes,' said Ajji. 'Air goes in and out of that hole.'

The snail moved onto the cabbage and began to eat. They could hear a tiny scratching sound. 'Snails have a rough tongue, like a file,' said Ajji. 'They scrape the leaf with it.'

Kiran clapped his hands. 'Snail, you eat, you move and you breathe,' he said. 'You are alive!'

Later that day, Anjali found another round shell behind the pot. This one was white and dry, and very light. She looked inside. It was empty.

'Is this one alive?' Kiran asked.

Anjali turned the shell over in her hand. 'No,' she said. 'This shell was once part of a living snail. But no snail lives in it now. It is once alive, like a dry leaf.'

'And a stone?' asked Kiran.

'A stone was never alive,' said Anjali. 'A stone never ate, and never moved, and never had babies.'

Ajji nodded. 'Snails lay tiny eggs in wet soil,' she said. 'If you are lucky, there will be baby snails in the pots next month, no bigger than a grain of rice.'

That evening, Anjali put the snail back in the tulsi pot, with the cabbage leaf. 'You can stay,' she told the snail. 'But please eat the cabbage and leave my tulsi.'

The snail did not say anything. But the next morning, the cabbage was full of holes, and the tulsi was not.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Anjali tests the brown thing for signs of life, one by one. It moves, eats and breathes, so it is alive. The empty shell was once alive. A stone was never alive.

- Ask: How did Anjali find out that the brown thing was not a stone?
- Ask: Why was the empty white shell 'once alive', and not 'never alive'?
- Ask: What other living thing might live on your balcony or window?

## Experiments
### e1 · Droopy Mint: is a picked leaf still alive? (25 min)
- **question:** Is mint that was picked from the plant still alive? What happens to droopy mint, a dry leaf and a paper leaf in water?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which ones will stand up in water: the droopy mint, the dry tej patta, or the paper leaf? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each thing gets the same cup and the same water, for the same time. One droopy mint sprig stays on a dry plate, so we can see what water does.
- **safety:** A grown-up uses the scissors.
- **record:** Draw the three cups and the plate, before and after. Circle the ones that changed.
- **results table:** Thing | At the start | After one hour
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Two or three hours before, leave two mint sprigs on a plate in the room until they go floppy. 2. Now lay out the two droopy sprigs, the tej patta and the paper leaf. 3. Let your child touch each one.  
  *Parent says:* Touch each leaf gently. How does each one feel?  
  *Child mode:* Touch each leaf. Is it soft? Is it crisp? Is it floppy?
  - Ask: Which of these was once part of a plant? (good: The mint and the tej patta.; The mint and the tej patta came from plants. The paper is made from trees too.)
  - Note: The mint should be floppy but still green, not dry and crisp. Coriander or palak leaves work too if you have no mint. Paper is made from wood, so it was once alive too. That is fine: it is now a flat sheet, not a leaf.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, and to say why. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will put each one in water. Which ones will stand up again? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which will stand up in water? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The mint will stand up, because it is still a bit alive.; The paper will go soggy, because paper soaks up water.)
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Snip a little off the bottom of one mint stalk. 2. Stand that sprig in a tumbler of water. 3. Stand the tej patta in the second tumbler and the paper leaf in the third. 4. Leave the second mint sprig on the dry plate.  
  *Parent says:* Each one gets its own cup of water. This mint sprig stays on the dry plate, so we can see what the water does.  
  *Child mode:* Put each leaf in a cup of water. One mint stays on the plate.
  - Note: Cutting the end opens the stalk so water can go in. Put the cups in the shade, not in hot sun.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave the cups for one hour. Play Wiggle, Sit or Statue, or do the steel plate breath test, while you wait.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait one hour. Living things need time.  
  *Child mode:* Now we wait one hour.
  - Wait: Leave the cups for one hour.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at each cup together. 2. Let your child touch each leaf again. 3. Compare the mint in water with the mint on the plate.  
  *Parent says:* Look and touch. What happened to each one?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each cup. Touch each leaf. What changed?
  - Ask: What happened to the mint in water? (good: The mint stood up straight.; The mint feels firm and fresh again.)
  - Ask: What happened to the tej patta and the paper? (good: The tej patta stayed dry and brown.; The paper went soggy and flopped over.)
  - Note: The mint in water usually stands up firm within an hour. If it is still droopy, wait one more hour. The mint on the plate stays droopy or gets worse. The tej patta stays brown and stiff. The paper goes soft and floppy.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why. Then draw the cups together.  
  *Parent says:* So, is the picked mint still alive? What did you see that tells you?  
  *Child mode:* Is the mint still alive? Say what you saw. Then draw it.
  - Ask: Why did only the mint stand up? (good: Because the mint is still alive, so it can drink the water.; Because the tej patta is dry and dead, and paper was never a leaf like that.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses what your child saw: the mint stood up in water, and the dry leaf did not. The living parts inside the mint fill up with water and push the leaf firm. Dry, dead leaves cannot do this.
  - Check: The droopy mint stood up in water, but the dry tej patta did not. What does this tell us? → **The picked mint was still alive**

### p1 · Mint Grows Roots (20 min, 14 days)
- **record:** Draw the stalk under the water on day 1, day 4, day 8 and day 14.
- **schedule:** day 1: Pull the lowest leaves off two mint stalks. Stand them in a clear glass of water by a bright window. (5 min); day 4: Look for tiny white bumps on the stalk under the water. Change the water. (3 min); day 8: Look for white roots. Draw them. Change the water. (4 min); day 14: Look at the roots. Plant one sprig in a pot of soil if you have one. (8 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pull the leaves off the bottom part of each stalk. 2. Stand them in a clear glass of water. 3. Put the glass by a bright window, out of hot sun.

  *Child mode:* Take off the bottom leaves. Put the mint in water. Put it by the window.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the stalks under the water together. Change the water.  
  *Parent says:* Can you see any tiny white bumps on the stalk under the water?  
  *Child mode:* Look under the water. Any white bumps?
  - Note: Change the water every 3 or 4 days so it stays clean.
  - Wait: Leave the glass for 3 days.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look for white roots. Draw them together. Change the water.  
  *Parent says:* Look! Are those roots? Where are they growing from?  
  *Child mode:* Look for roots. Draw them.
  - Note: Mint usually grows roots in about one or two weeks. In cold weather it can take longer. If one sprig goes brown and soft, take it out and keep watching the other.

- **s4 (8 min)**

  *Parent says:* The mint was picked from the plant. Now it has new roots. Is it alive?  
  *Child mode:* The mint grew roots. Is it alive?
  - Ask: How do you know the mint is alive? (good: Because it grew new roots.; Because it is growing, and growing is a sign of life.)
  - Wait: Leave the glass for 6 more days.

## Worksheets
### Alive or Not? (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (sort) Circle the living things in green. Circle the things that were never alive in red.  
   **Answer:** a crow → Alive; a stone → Never alive; a tulsi plant → Alive; a steel spoon → Never alive; an ant → Alive; salt → Never alive. *Why:* The crow, the tulsi plant and the ant eat, grow, breathe and have young. The stone, the spoon and salt never did any of these.
2. (match) Draw a line from each mother to her baby.  
   **Answer:** Hen → Chick; Cow → Calf; Frog → Tadpole; Dog → Puppy. *Why:* Every living thing has young, and the young grow up to be like the parent. A tadpole looks very different from a frog, but grows into one.
3. (mcq) Which one is alive?  
   Options: A toy car / A cat / A ball  
   **Answer:** A cat. *Why:* A cat eats, grows, moves, breathes and has kittens. A toy car and a ball never do these things by themselves.
4. (truefalse) A tree is alive.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A tree grows, makes food in their leaves, breathes through tiny holes in the leaves, and makes seeds. A strong answer gives one of these reasons.
5. (draw) Draw yourself breathing out on a cold steel plate. Draw what you saw on the plate.  
   **Answer:** A child and a plate with a cloudy patch on it.. *Why:* Breath coming out of us is warm and wet. On a cool plate it makes a cloudy patch. A strong drawing shows the patch near the mouth.
6. (odd) Three of these were once alive. Which one was never alive?  
   Options: A wooden spoon / A cotton towel / A steel tumbler / A dry leaf  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The wooden spoon came from a tree. Cotton comes from a cotton plant. The dry leaf was on a tree. The steel tumbler was never part of a living thing.
7. (mcq) The droopy mint stood up in water. The paper leaf did not. Why?  
   Options: The mint was still alive / The paper leaf was too big / The mint was heavier  
   **Answer:** The mint was still alive. *Why:* Living mint fills up with water and goes firm. Paper only soaks up water and goes soggy. So the mint was still alive.
8. (draw) Draw one living thing you can see from your window.  
   **Answer:** Any living thing: a crow, a pigeon, a tree, a plant, a dog or a person.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a real living thing from the window, not a car or a building.
9. (truefalse) The ceiling fan moves, so the fan is alive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The fan moves only when it is switched on. It never eats, grows, breathes or has young. Moving alone does not mean alive.
10. (count) Count the living things in this picture: two crows, one tree, a car, a dog and a house.  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* Two crows, one tree and one dog make 4 living things. The car and the house were never alive.
11. (sort) Put each thing on the right plate: once alive or never alive?  
   **Answer:** a coconut shell → Once alive; a stone → Never alive; a newspaper → Once alive; a glass marble → Never alive. *Why:* A coconut shell grew on a coconut tree. A newspaper is made from wood, from trees. The stone and the marble were never part of a living thing.
12. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: how do you know you are alive? Give two reasons.  
   **Answer:** I eat food. I grow bigger. I breathe. I run and jump.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two different signs of life, such as eating and growing, and says how the child does them.
13. (perspective) Meena and her brother Ravi look at a potted plant on their balcony in Kochi.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with Meena, because the plant grows, makes food and breathes. Ravi is right that the plant does not walk around. But plants do move slowly, turning their leaves to the light.. *Why:* Many young children think plants are not alive because plants stay in one place. Plants show the other signs of life, and they move too, only slowly.
14. (label) Here are three plates. Label each one: alive, once alive or never alive.  
   **Answer:** ['potato and mint: alive', 'wooden spoon and cotton: once alive', 'steel spoon and salt: never alive']. *Why:* The potato and the mint can still grow. The wooden spoon and the cotton came from plants. The steel and salt were never part of a living thing.
15. (draw) Draw a baby animal and the grown-up animal they will become.  
   **Answer:** Any pair, such as a chick and a hen, or a caterpillar and a butterfly.. *Why:* Young living things grow up to be like their parents. A strong drawing shows the same kind of animal, small and grown-up.
### Tricky Cases (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Is a potato alive?  
   Options: No, a potato never moves / Yes, a potato can grow shoots / No, a potato is food  
   **Answer:** Yes, a potato can grow shoots. *Why:* A potato left in a dark cupboard grows shoots after some weeks. A potato is alive, resting until the right time to grow. Being food does not stop a thing being alive.
2. (draw) Draw the mint in water before and after one hour.  
   **Answer:** A droopy mint sprig in the first picture, and the same sprig standing up in the second.. *Why:* Living mint takes in water and stands up firm. A strong drawing shows the change clearly.
3. (truefalse) A dry tej patta from the kitchen is alive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The tej patta was once alive, on a tree. Now it is dry. In our mint test, the tej patta did not stand up in water.
4. (mcq) Fire grows bigger and needs air. Is fire alive?  
   Options: Yes, because fire grows / No, fire only burns the wood up / Yes, because fire is hot  
   **Answer:** No, fire only burns the wood up. *Why:* Fire grows and needs air, but fire never builds a body from food. Fire only burns things up and leaves ash. Scientists say fire is not alive. This one is tricky, so a child who thinks hard about it is doing well.
5. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: a plant does not have a mouth. How does a plant eat?  
   **Answer:** A plant makes their own food in their green leaves, using sunlight, air and water.. *Why:* A strong answer says plants make their own food, and names sunlight or the green leaves.
6. (order) Put the mango story in order: from seed to tree.  
   **Answer:** ['A mango seed in the soil', 'A small seedling with two leaves', 'A big mango tree']. *Why:* The seed grows into a seedling, and the seedling grows into a tree. Growing is a sign of life.
7. (perspective) Lakshmi and her grandfather in Madurai look at a wooden chair.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with Lakshmi: the wood came from a living tree, so the chair was once alive. Grandfather is right that the chair does not eat or grow now.. *Why:* Once alive means the thing was part of a living thing. The chair is not alive now, but the wood was part of a tree.
8. (odd) Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A crow / A mango tree / A cloud / A cat  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The crow, the tree and the cat are alive. A cloud moves and grows bigger, but a cloud does not eat, breathe or have young. So the cloud is the odd one out.
9. (draw) Draw round your hand. Then draw what you think your hand will look like when you are grown up.  
   **Answer:** A hand outline, and a bigger hand drawn round it.. *Why:* Children grow because they are alive. A strong drawing shows a bigger grown-up hand.
10. (match) Match each sign of life to what you saw.  
   **Answer:** Breathing → a cloudy patch on the steel plate; Growing → a baby sock that no longer fits; Having young → the seeds inside a tomato; Eating → a snail scraping a cabbage leaf. *Why:* Each thing we did showed one sign of life. A strong answer can say which activity showed each one.
11. (mcq) An empty snail shell is lying behind a pot. What is it?  
   Options: Alive / Once alive / Never alive  
   **Answer:** Once alive. *Why:* The shell was once part of a living snail, so it is once alive. No snail lives in it now.
12. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: why did we leave one droopy mint sprig on a dry plate?  
   **Answer:** To see what happens without water. It stayed droopy, so the water made the other mint stand up.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that the dry plate sprig shows what water did. Without it, we could not be sure the water made the difference.
13. (truefalse) Only animals have young. Plants do not.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Plants have young too. Seeds grow into new plants, and a mint sprig can even grow roots and become a new plant.
14. (label) Label each part of the snail: shell, eyes, breathing hole.  
   **Answer:** ['round hard part: shell', 'dots on the long horns: eyes', 'small hole near the shell: breathing hole']. *Why:* A land snail carries a shell, has eyes at the tips of the long horns, and breathes through a hole on the side. A strong answer remembers the snail from the story.
15. (count) Priya counted 12 seeds in one spoonful of tomato. She took 2 spoonfuls. About how many seeds did she have?  
   **Answer:** 24. *Why:* 12 and 12 make 24. Every one of those seeds could grow into a new tomato plant.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which one was never alive?  
   Options: A coconut shell / A stone / A dry leaf  
   **Answer:** A stone. *Why:* A stone was never part of a living thing. The coconut shell and the dry leaf came from trees, so they were once alive.
2. (mcq) Which one is alive now?  
   Options: A wooden spoon / A crow on the window / A steel plate  
   **Answer:** A crow on the window. *Why:* The crow eats, flies, breathes and lays eggs. The wooden spoon was once alive. The steel plate was never alive.
3. (mcq) A cotton shirt is…  
   Options: alive / once alive / never alive  
   **Answer:** once alive. *Why:* Cotton comes from the cotton plant. The shirt is not alive now, but it was part of a living plant.
4. (mcq) Which of these is a sign of life?  
   Options: Being shiny / Growing / Being hard  
   **Answer:** Growing. *Why:* Living things grow. A steel spoon is shiny and hard, and was never alive.
5. (mcq) You breathed on a cool steel plate. What did you see?  
   Options: Nothing / A cloudy patch / A red spot  
   **Answer:** A cloudy patch. *Why:* Your breath is warm and wet. On a cool plate it makes a cloudy patch. Breathing is a sign of life.
6. (mcq) Is a tulsi plant alive?  
   Options: No, the plant stays in the pot / Yes, the plant grows and makes seeds / Only in the morning  
   **Answer:** Yes, the plant grows and makes seeds. *Why:* Plants grow, make food, breathe and make seeds. Staying in one place does not mean a thing is not alive.
7. (mcq) What is the young of a cow called?  
   Options: A kid / A calf / A chick  
   **Answer:** A calf. *Why:* A calf is the young of a cow. A kid is a young goat. A chick is a young bird.
8. (mcq) A tadpole grows up to be…  
   Options: a fish / a frog / a snail  
   **Answer:** a frog. *Why:* A tadpole hatches from a frog's egg, grows legs and loses the tail, and becomes a frog.
9. (mcq) In the mint test, what happened to the droopy mint in water?  
   Options: The mint stood up firm again / The mint went soggy like paper / Nothing happened  
   **Answer:** The mint stood up firm again. *Why:* The mint stood up, because the picked mint was still alive and filled up with water. The paper only went soggy.
10. (mcq) A toy robot walks and talks. Is the robot alive?  
   Options: Yes, because the robot walks / No, the robot does not eat, grow or have young / Yes, because the robot talks  
   **Answer:** No, the robot does not eat, grow or have young. *Why:* Moving and making sounds are not enough. A robot does not grow, eat food or have baby robots, so a robot was never alive.

## Applications
- Doctors check if a person is breathing, to see if they need help.
- Farmers and gardeners keep picked greens in water or in a cool, damp cloth so they stay fresh longer.
- Gardeners grow new mint, money plants and roses from cut stalks.
- Scientists looking for life on other planets look for signs of life, such as breathing out gases.

## Open questions
- **Exactly when does a picked leaf stop being alive?** The tiny parts inside a leaf die a few at a time, over days. Scientists do not agree on one moment when the whole leaf is no longer alive.
- **How did the very first living thing begin?** Every living thing today comes from another living thing. But long ago there must have been a first one. Scientists have ideas, but nobody knows for sure.
- **Is there life on other planets?** Scientists are looking, with telescopes and robots on Mars. So far they have not found any living thing beyond Earth.
