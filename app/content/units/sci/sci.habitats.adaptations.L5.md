# Adaptation: how living things fit their place, their hunters and their prey
`sci.habitats.adaptations.L5`

**Summary.** A plain tiger butterfly is bright orange and flies slowly in the open, yet most birds leave her alone. She tastes bitter, and her colour warns every bird who has tried one before. The female danaid eggfly tastes fine, but she wears the same orange and is left alone too. Your child tests when such copying works, using paper prey and a hungry hunter. They also test why Himalayan trees are shaped like cones. And they learn how hunters and the hunted are fitted to each other.

**Intro.** *A tiger is bright orange with black stripes. Yet a spotted deer often fails to notice a tiger lying in the grass. How can such a bright animal hide?* The answer is in the deer's eyes. We have three kinds of colour cells in our eyes, and a deer has two. To a deer, orange looks much like the green of grass. So the tiger's coat is fitted to the eyes of her prey, as well as to the forest. In this unit you look at how plants and animals are fitted to heat, water and cold. Then you look at how hunters and the hunted are fitted to each other. You pour rava snow on paper trees. You also hunt paper prey, to find out when a false warning works.

## Learning outcomes

1. I can explain how plants are adapted to dry, wet and cold places, with an Indian example of each.

## Terms
- **Adaptation**: An inherited feature that helps a living thing survive and have young in their place. *e.g.* A camel's wide feet are an adaptation to soft sand. A deodar's sloping branches are an adaptation to snow.
- **Environment**: Everything around a living thing: the heat, the water, the ground and the other living things. *e.g.* A lotus's environment is a pond. It includes the mud, the water, the fish and the dragonflies.
- **Structural adaptation**: An adaptation in the shape or covering of the body. *e.g.* A snow leopard's wide, furry paws and a cactus's spines are structural adaptations.
- **Behavioural adaptation**: An adaptation in what a living thing does. *e.g.* Spotted deer feed in a herd. A spiny-tailed lizard rests in her burrow in the heat of noon.
- **Physiological adaptation**: An adaptation in how the body works on the inside. *e.g.* A camel's body can warm up by about 6 degrees Celsius in the day without harm, so she sweats less.
- **Predator**: An animal who catches and eats other animals. *e.g.* A tiger, a house gecko, a barn owl and a dragonfly are all predators.
- **Prey**: An animal who is caught and eaten by a predator. *e.g.* A moth is prey for a gecko. The gecko is prey for a cat.
- **Camouflage**: Colours, patterns or shapes that make a living thing hard to notice. *e.g.* A nightjar on dry leaves and a crab spider on a yellow flower are both camouflaged.
- **Disruptive pattern**: Bold stripes or patches that break up the outline of a body. The watcher's eye then does not see one whole shape. *e.g.* A tiger's black stripes cut her body into pieces that look like shadows between grass stems.
- **Masquerade**: Camouflage in which an animal looks like some other thing that hunters ignore. *e.g.* A stick insect looks like a twig. A young common mormon caterpillar looks like a bird dropping.
- **Warning colours**: Bright colours and bold patterns that tell hunters an animal is bitter, poisonous or able to sting. *e.g.* The orange and black of a plain tiger butterfly, and the yellow and black of a wasp.
- **Poison and venom**: Both are harmful chemicals. A poison does harm when it is eaten or touched. A venom is put into the body by a bite or a sting. *e.g.* A plain tiger butterfly is poisonous to eat. A Russell's viper is venomous, because she injects venom with her fangs.
- **Mimicry**: One kind of living thing looking like another kind, and gaining from the likeness. *e.g.* A hoverfly has no sting, but she is striped yellow and black like a wasp.
- **Model and mimic**: The model is the animal with the real defence. The mimic is the animal who copies the model's look. *e.g.* The bitter common rose butterfly is the model. Some female common mormon butterflies are her mimics.
- **Field of view**: All that an animal can see without moving her head. *e.g.* A rabbit's eyes are on the sides of the head. Her field of view goes almost all the way round.
- **Co-evolution**: Two kinds of living things changing over many generations, each because of the other. *e.g.* Many moths have ears that hear the calls of hunting bats. Some bats now call very softly.
- **Endangered**: So few in number that the whole kind may die out. *e.g.* Only about 100 to 150 great Indian bustards are left in the wild.

## Explanations
### What an adaptation is, and its three kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** An adaptation is an inherited feature that helps a living thing survive in their place. It also helps them to have young there.

Adaptations come in three kinds. A structural adaptation is part of the body's shape or covering, such as a camel's wide feet. A behavioural adaptation is something the living thing does, such as a desert lizard resting in a burrow at noon. A physiological adaptation is a way the body works inside. A camel's body can warm up by about 6 degrees Celsius during the day without harm. So she sweats less and saves water. Most living things use all three kinds together. An adaptation is passed from parents to young. That makes it different from getting used to a place within one lifetime. An adaptation also helps only in the right place. Wide feet help a camel on sand and would slow her down on rocks.
- e.g. Structural: the hollow fangs of a Russell's viper.
- e.g. Behavioural: spotted deer feed in a herd and look up often.
- e.g. Physiological: a cactus opens the tiny holes in her skin only at night. The air is cool then, and less water escapes.
### Plants are adapted to dry, wet and cold places · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant stays in one spot for life. So the shape and working of their body must suit the heat, water and cold there.

In a hot, dry place, rain is rare and dries fast. A cactus has spines where other plants have leaves, so little water escapes. Her thick stem stores water under a waxy skin. Her roots are shallow and wide, and catch a short shower before it sinks away. The khejri tree of the Thar does the opposite, and sends one root many metres down to deep water. In a pond, a lotus has other problems. Her wide leaves float, and their waxy tops shed water. Her stems hold air tubes that carry oxygen down to roots in airless mud. In the mangrove forests of Pichavaram and the Sundarbans, the mud is soft and salty. Some mangrove trees stand on arching stilt roots. Others pass extra salt out through their leaves. In the rainy forests of the Western Ghats, many leaves end in a long, narrow drip tip. Rain runs to the tip and falls off, so the leaf dries sooner. A peepal leaf has such a tip. On cold Himalayan slopes, firs, spruces and young deodars grow as narrow cones. Their branches slope down and bend. In the Snow Load Test experiment in this unit, a steep paper cone sheds almost all the rava snow poured on top. A flat paper circle keeps all of the rava. Real snow slides off a cone-shaped tree in the same way, before the weight can snap the branches. The needle leaves are thin and waxy, so they lose little water while the ground is frozen.
- e.g. Dry: a cactus with spines, a thick stem and a waxy skin.
- e.g. Wet: a lotus with floating leaves and air tubes in her stems.
- e.g. Salty mud: a mangrove tree on stilt roots at Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. Heavy rain: a peepal leaf with a long drip tip.
- e.g. Snow: a deodar with sloping branches and needle leaves in Himachal Pradesh.
### Animals are adapted to hot, wet and cold places · *defined*
**Definition:** An animal's body, behaviour and inner working suit the place where their kind has lived for a very long time.

In the Thar desert the problems are heat, thirst and loose sand. A camel stores fat in her hump and can go a week or more without drinking. Long eyelashes and nostrils that close keep the sand out, and wide feet stop her sinking. In a wet place the problems are moving, breathing and feeding in water. A fish has fins to steer and gills that take in oxygen dissolved in the water. A duck has webbed feet, and oil on her feathers keeps them dry. A flamingo wades on long thin legs and strains tiny food from salty water with a bent beak. Seabirds drink sea water, and glands above their eyes pass the extra salt out. In the cold of Ladakh the problem is losing heat. A yak has a thick double coat. A snow leopard has thick fur, wide furry paws, and a long tail that she wraps around her face. One problem often has several answers. In desert heat, a camel lets her body warm up. A spiny-tailed lizard goes underground, and a desert fox loses heat through large ears. Putting these answers side by side shows what the problem really is. In a desert, it is staying alive in great heat with very little water.
- e.g. Hot and dry: the camel and the spiny-tailed lizard of the Thar.
- e.g. Wet: flamingos at Pulicat Lake and in the Rann of Kutch.
- e.g. Cold: the snow leopard and the yak of Ladakh.
### Predators are adapted to find, reach and kill prey · *defined*
**Definition:** A predator is an animal who catches and eats other animals. Those other animals are the prey.

A predator has three problems to solve, in order. The first is to find the prey. An eagle's eyes are far sharper than ours. A barn owl can catch a mouse in complete darkness by hearing alone. Most hunters have both eyes at the front of the face. Both eyes then see the same prey, and the brain uses the two views to judge distance. The second problem is to get within reach. A tiger uses camouflage and creeps close before a short rush. A cheetah uses speed, and can pass 90 kilometres an hour in a short dash. A spider builds a web and waits. Dholes, the wild dogs of Indian forests, hunt as a pack and tire out a deer. The third problem is to kill. Hunters have long pointed teeth, claws or talons, and some snakes inject venom. A few plants are predators too. The pitcher plant of Meghalaya traps insects in a cup of liquid and digests them. Many predators are also prey. A house gecko hunts moths, and a cat hunts the gecko.
- e.g. Finding: a barn owl hears a mouse under dry leaves.
- e.g. Reaching: a tiger stalks to within a few leaps before she rushes.
- e.g. Killing: a Russell's viper injects venom through hollow fangs.
- e.g. Teamwork: a pack of dholes brings down a sambar deer.
### Prey are adapted to notice, escape and resist · *defined*
**Definition:** Prey animals have adaptations that help them notice a hunter early, avoid being found, get away, or fight back.

Each prey adaptation answers one of the predator's three problems. To notice a hunter early, a deer has eyes on the sides of her head. She sees almost all the way round, though she judges distance less well. Her large ears turn towards a sound. Spotted deer feed below langurs, who see far from the treetops and bark at a tiger. To avoid being found, many prey animals use camouflage and keep still. To get away, a blackbuck can run at up to about 80 kilometres an hour and swerves sharply. A house gecko drops her tail, and the wriggling tail holds the hunter's eye while she runs. An octopus squirts ink and jets away. To resist, a pangolin rolls into a ball of hard scales. A porcupine raises her quills, and bees sting together. Living in a group helps in two more ways. Each animal is one among many, so the chance that the hunter picks her is small. Animals in the middle are also shielded by those outside. In the Edge or Middle activity in this unit, a rolling marble plays a tiger, and 19 chickpeas, called chana, play a herd of deer. The marble tiger nearly always touches a chana on the outer ring of the herd first. Plants are prey too. They resist with thorns, tough leaves and bitter chemicals.
- e.g. Noticing: a spotted deer's side-facing eyes and turning ears.
- e.g. Escaping: a house gecko's tail that breaks off and wriggles.
- e.g. Resisting: an Indian pangolin's ball of scales.
- e.g. A plant resisting: the bitter leaves of a neem tree.
### Camouflage: four ways to go unnoticed · *defined*
**Definition:** Camouflage is any colour, pattern or shape that makes a living thing hard to notice or hard to recognise.

There are four main kinds. The first is matching the background, as a nightjar matches dry leaves. The second is a disruptive pattern. Bold stripes or patches break the body's outline into pieces, so the watcher does not see one animal shape. A tiger's stripes work this way among grass stems and shadows. The third is countershading. Many animals are dark on top and pale below. This cancels the shadow on the belly and makes the body look flat. The fourth is masquerade. The animal can be seen, but looks like something not worth eating. A stick insect looks like a twig. The Indian oakleaf butterfly looks like a dead leaf when her wings are closed. Hunters use camouflage as much as prey do. A crab spider on a yellow flower is yellow, and bees land within reach. Camouflage is always fitted to a particular eye. A tiger looks bright orange to us. A deer's eyes have two kinds of colour cells, and ours have three. To a deer, orange looks much like green. So the same coat is easy for us to see and hard for a deer to see.
- e.g. Matching: a green vine snake among green stems.
- e.g. Disruptive pattern: the stripes of a tiger.
- e.g. Countershading: a mackerel with a dark back and a silver belly.
- e.g. Masquerade: a young common mormon caterpillar who looks like a bird dropping.
### Warning colours: easy to see and easy to remember · *defined*
**Definition:** Warning colours are bright colours and bold patterns on an animal who is poisonous, bitter or able to sting.

Warning colours are the opposite of camouflage. The animal gains by being noticed. A plain tiger caterpillar eats milkweed, a plant with poison in the sap. She keeps the poison in her body for life. The butterfly is orange, black and white, and flies slowly in the open. A young bird who eats one is sick, or spits her out. The bird then avoids that pattern, often for a long time. Red, orange and yellow with black stand out against green leaves and brown ground. A bold pattern is also easy to remember, and that helps the bird as well as the butterfly. Wasps, blister beetles, ladybirds and the banded krait all carry warning colours. A cobra spreads her hood and a saw-scaled viper rubs her scales to make a rasping sound. These are warnings too. A warning works because hunters learn from a bad meal. That means the warning protects the kind of animal as a whole, and not every single one. A few of them are still tasted and die while the hunters are learning.
- e.g. A plain tiger butterfly on a milkweed plant by the road.
- e.g. A yellow and black paper wasp at her nest under a window ledge.
- e.g. A banded krait with wide yellow and black bands.
- e.g. An orange and black blister beetle on a flower.
### Mimicry: copying a warning · *defined*
**Definition:** Mimicry is one kind of living thing looking like another kind, and gaining from the likeness. The animal who is copied is called the model, and the copier is called the mimic.

In the first kind of mimicry, a harmless animal copies a harmful one. It is called Batesian mimicry, after Henry Walter Bates, who described it in 1862. The female danaid eggfly tastes fine, but she looks like the bitter plain tiger. A hoverfly has no sting, but she is striped like a wasp. Some female common mormon butterflies copy the bitter common rose. Birds who have learned to avoid the model leave the mimic alone as well. This works only while birds keep meeting the real, harmful kind. In the Copycat Test experiment in this unit, a hunter usually stops eating striped squares when most of them are bitter. When most patterned squares are harmless, the hunter learns that the pattern is safe and eats them. So mimics are safest when they are fewer than their models. In the second kind of mimicry, two harmful kinds look alike. It is called Müllerian mimicry, after Fritz Müller. Many wasps and bees share yellow and black stripes. A bird then has only one pattern to learn, and fewer of each kind die while birds are learning. In the common mormon, only females are mimics. In 2014 Krushnamegh Kunte's team in Bengaluru found that a single gene, called doublesex, switches the copied pattern on.
- e.g. Model: the plain tiger. Mimic: the female danaid eggfly.
- e.g. Model: the common rose. Mimic: one form of the female common mormon.
- e.g. Model: a paper wasp. Mimic: a hoverfly.
- e.g. Two harmful kinds that share stripes: a paper wasp and a rock bee.
### Hunters and hunted shape each other · *defined*
**Definition:** Co-evolution is two kinds of living things changing over many generations, each because of the other.

In every generation, some deer are a little quicker to notice a tiger. They are more likely to live and have fawns. The fawns inherit their parents' features. In the same forest, the tigers who stalk a little better eat more often and raise more cubs. Over thousands of generations, both kinds change. A deer's speed is partly the work of tigers, and a tiger's stealth is partly the work of deer. Scientists call this an arms race. In 2018 Alan Wilson's team put recording collars on lions, zebras, cheetahs and impalas in Botswana. The hunters were faster and stronger than their prey. The prey could still escape, by running a little slower and turning sharply at the last moment. Bats and moths are in the same kind of race. Many moths have ears that hear a bat's calls, and some bats have taken to calling very softly. Plants and their eaters shape each other too. Milkweed makes poison, and the plain tiger caterpillar eats milkweed all the same and uses the poison herself. How these changes build up is called natural selection, and a later unit explains it.
- e.g. Cheetah and blackbuck: speed against sharp turns.
- e.g. Bat and moth: calls against ears.
- e.g. Milkweed and plain tiger caterpillar: poison against a body that can store poison.
### When a place changes fast, an adaptation can stop fitting · *defined*
**Definition:** An adaptation fits the place where it arose. If the place changes quickly, the same feature may stop helping, or may even do harm.

The peppered moth of England is pale and speckled. On tree trunks covered with pale lichen, birds seldom found her. In the 1800s, smoke from factories killed the lichen and blackened the trunks around Manchester. Pale moths now stood out, and birds ate them. A rare dark form was hard to see on the dark bark. By 1895 almost all the moths near Manchester were dark. When the air was cleaned after 1956, pale moths became common again. Bodies can follow a change in this way only if it is slow enough, and only over many generations. The great Indian bustard is a large bird of dry grassland. Like many prey birds, she has eyes on the sides of her head. She watches the ground and the sky for foxes and eagles. She sees poorly straight ahead while flying. That did no harm for thousands of years. Now power lines cross her grassland, and bustards fly into the wires and die. Only about 100 to 150 are left. Vultures can eat rotting meat safely. But a cattle medicine called diclofenac poisoned them. India lost more than 95 of every 100 vultures before the medicine was banned for cattle in 2006.
- e.g. The peppered moth: pale wings stopped hiding her on sooty bark.
- e.g. The great Indian bustard: side-facing eyes do not show her the power lines ahead.
- e.g. Indian vultures: a strong stomach was no defence against a new medicine.
### Why does a zebra have stripes? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists do not yet agree on what a zebra's stripes are for.

Not every adaptation has an agreed explanation. A zebra's stripes are bold and easy to see on an open plain. Scientists have argued about them for 150 years. The question is still open, though one answer now has the strongest evidence. It is a reminder to test an explanation, however sensible it sounds.
- e.g. A zebra standing in short grass is easy for a person to see from far away.
- e.g. Biting flies land less often on striped surfaces than on plain ones.
- **Tim Caro and his team:** The stripes keep biting flies away. In 2019 the team dressed horses in striped coats. Flies came near, but few managed to land on the stripes.
- **Scientists who study heat, such as Alison Cobb and Stephen Cobb:** Black stripes warm up more than white ones in the sun. This may stir small currents of air over the skin and help a zebra to cool.
- **An older view, held since the 1800s:** The stripes are camouflage, or they dazzle a lion when a herd runs. Tests have found little support for this, because lions see the stripes only when they are already very close.

## Mostly agreed
- An adaptation is inherited, and it helps a living thing survive and have young in their own place. *How we know:* Scientists compare the bodies of related plants and animals from different places. The differences match the problems of each place, such as heat, water, cold, hunters and food.
- Bright warning colours go with a real defence, and hunters learn to avoid them. *How we know:* In 1869 John Jenner Weir reported that the birds in his aviary ate dull caterpillars and refused bright ones. In the 1960s Lincoln Brower's blue jays vomited after eating monarch butterflies raised on poisonous milkweed, and then refused monarchs.
- A harmless mimic is protected only by hunters who have met the model. *How we know:* In 1958 Jane Van Zandt Brower reported tests with Florida scrub jays. Jays who had tasted bitter monarch butterflies refused the look-alike viceroy. Jays who had never met a monarch ate viceroys.
- Camouflage lowers the chance of being eaten, and it works only against the right background. *How we know:* Bernard Kettlewell released pale and dark peppered moths in clean and sooty woods in the 1950s. Birds took more of whichever form stood out. From 2001 to 2007 Michael Majerus repeated the work with thousands of moths near Cambridge and found the same.
- Predators and prey shape each other over many generations. *How we know:* Collars on wild lions, zebras, cheetahs and impalas showed hunters built for speed and prey built for sharp turns. Moths that live with bats have ears tuned to bat calls.
- Living in a group lowers the risk for each animal. *How we know:* In 1971 William Hamilton showed with simple geometry that the edge of a group is more dangerous than the middle. Sheep fitted with trackers move towards the middle of the flock when a dog comes near.
- When people change a place quickly, adaptations can fail. *How we know:* Great Indian bustards die on power lines that cross their grassland. Indian vultures died in millions after eating cattle treated with diclofenac.

## How we found out
**Henry Walter Bates, Charles Darwin, Alfred Russel Wallace and John Jenner Weir in England, and Jane Van Zandt Brower in the United States, Bates wrote in 1862. Darwin and Wallace exchanged letters in 1867, and Weir reported his tests in 1869. Jane Brower published her jay tests in 1958..** Henry Walter Bates spent eleven years in the Amazon forest. In 1862 he wrote about a puzzle. Some butterflies from quite different families had almost the same wings. Some naturalists said the same forest and climate had coloured them alike. Bates suggested that harmless kinds were copying kinds that birds avoid. In 1867 Charles Darwin had a puzzle of his own. He thought bright colours helped animals to win mates. But many caterpillars are bright, and caterpillars do not mate. He asked Alfred Russel Wallace. Wallace guessed that bright caterpillars taste bad, and that the colour helps birds to remember them. John Jenner Weir tested this in his aviary and reported in 1869. His birds ate dull caterpillars and refused bright ones. Nobody tested the copying itself with live birds until the 1950s. Jane Van Zandt Brower fed bitter monarch butterflies to Florida scrub jays. Jays who had tried a monarch then refused viceroy butterflies, which look like monarchs. Jays who had never met a monarch ate the viceroys.  
*What people believed before:* That look-alike butterflies were coloured alike by their surroundings, and that bright colours were only for winning mates. Weir's birds showed that bright colours can be warnings. Brower's jays showed that a copy protects only against birds who have met the bitter kind.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A chameleon changes colour to match whatever she sits on.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Chameleons do change colour, and their usual greens and browns match leaves and bark well.  
  Against: In 2008 Devi Stuart-Fox and Adnan Moussalli studied dwarf chameleons. The biggest and fastest colour changes were signals to other chameleons. Chameleons also change colour as they warm up or cool down.
- **A porcupine can shoot her quills at an attacker.** (A common belief in many countries.; *rejected*)  
  For: A dog who attacks a porcupine comes away with quills stuck in the face, as if they had been fired.  
  Against: The quills are loosely fixed in the skin. The porcupine raises them, rattles them and runs backwards into the attacker. The quills come off when they touch.
- **The viceroy butterfly is a harmless mimic of the bitter monarch.** (Textbooks for about a hundred years.; *rejected*)  
  For: The two butterflies look almost alike, and Jane Brower's jays treated them alike.  
  Against: In 1991 David Ritland and Lincoln Brower fed both butterflies to red-winged blackbirds. The birds found viceroys as bad-tasting as monarchs. So both are harmful, and each protects the other.
- **An adaptation makes an animal perfect for her place.** (A common way of talking about nature.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many adaptations fit so well that they look designed, such as a camel's nostrils that close.  
  Against: Every adaptation has a cost, and each one fits the place as it was. A bustard's side-facing eyes suit open grassland and fail her among power lines.
- **Plants are helpless against the animals who eat them.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: A plant stays in one spot while a goat or a caterpillar feeds on her.  
  Against: Plants defend themselves with thorns, tough leaves and bitter chemicals. In 2014 Heidi Appel and Rex Cocroft played the vibrations of a chewing caterpillar to cress plants. When caterpillars later fed on those plants, the plants made more defence chemicals than plants kept in silence.
- **A mimic stays safe however many mimics there are.** (An easy thing to assume.; *debated*)  
  For: When the model is very nasty, hunters stay away from the pattern even if mimics are common. Jane Brower found this with starlings and bitter mealworms in 1960.  
  Against: When the model is only mildly unpleasant, or is missing, hunters soon learn that the pattern is safe. In places with no coral snakes, clay copies of their harmless mimic were attacked more often.

## Roots
In this unit you poured rava snow on paper trees and hunted paper prey with false warnings. Here we ask a bigger question. Does a warning pattern belong only to the animal who carries the pattern, or does the protection also depend on other living beings?

**Deep truth.** In the first hunt of the Copycat Test, most striped squares were bitter, so the hunter left the harmless striped squares alone too. In the second hunt, most dotted squares were harmless, so the hunter ate them. A copied warning works only while hunters keep meeting the real, bitter kind. So one animal's safety can depend on the bodies of other animals.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about an adaptation as something an animal owns, like a tool in a bag. But the copied pattern worked in one hunt and failed in the other. The copycat did not change. What changed was how many bitter squares were on the table around it.  
*Evidence:* In the Copycat Test only the share of copycats changed, from 2 in 12 to 10 in 12. Jane Brower's jays refused viceroy butterflies only after they had tasted monarchs. David Pfennig's team put out clay copies of a harmless banded snake. In places with no coral snakes, the copies were attacked more often.

- *who am i:* You depend on shared warnings too. A red traffic light keeps you safe only while most drivers stop at red.
- *what is life:* No living being is adapted alone. A deer's speed answers the tiger, and a tiger's stripes answer the deer's eyes.
- *what is this world:* A place is more than rock, water and weather. The other living beings are part of every place.

A camel is suited to sand, and a deodar is suited to snow. A mimic butterfly is suited to something you cannot see, which is a bird's memory of a bad meal. That memory exists only because another butterfly is bitter. So one wing pattern ties three living beings together. Nagarjuna taught that each thing is what it is through other things. Mimicry is a clear case that science can test.

- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18 to 24.19 (about the 2nd century CE):** Nagarjuna taught that nothing has its nature all by itself. Whatever exists has arisen in dependence on other things. (Nagarjuna was writing about all things, and he did not mention butterflies. A warning pattern is a small case that fits. Orange and black have no power by themselves. Their power comes from a poison in one body and a memory in another.)
- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), the last paragraph:** Darwin asks the reader to look at a tangled river bank with many plants, singing birds, insects and worms. He says these living forms are very different from each other, and depend on each other in complex ways. (Darwin wrote this three years before Bates described mimicry. Mimicry became one of the clearest examples of what he meant. A mimic's wings cannot be explained without the model and the bird.)
- Science (settled): Many moths have ears that hear the calls of hunting bats. The barbastelle bat calls very softly, and catches moths who would hear a louder bat. Each body has been shaped by the other.
- Science (open): In the common mormon butterfly only females are mimics. Scientists have found the gene that switches the pattern on, but they still argue about why males never use it.
- Other view, Many biologists who follow the gene's-eye view that Richard Dawkins set out in The Selfish Gene (1976): The mimic and the model are not partners. The mimic gains, and the model loses a little whenever a bird is fooled into trying the pattern again. On this view each body is built by genes that helped their own carriers. The ties between living things are real, but they are mostly ties of use and conflict.
- Practice, One minute at the window: what problem does each part of this body solve? (3 min): Stand at a window and choose one living being, such as a crow, a tree or an ant. Look at her quietly for one minute. For each part of her body, ask what problem outside her body it solves. The wing solves the problem of moving through air. The beak solves the problem of picking up a seed or a scrap. The watchful eye solves the problem of spotting a cat. Now look at your own hand, and ask the same question.
- Ask: In the Copycat Test the pattern on the copycat stayed the same, but how well it protected the copycat changed. So where did the protection really come from?
- Ask: A deer is fast because tigers hunt deer. What does the deer owe to the tiger?
- Ask: Which things that keep you safe work only because other people keep to them too?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A mimic butterfly is safe because another butterfly is bitter. Whose adaptation is the warning pattern? (hint: Think about three animals. The pattern is on the mimic's wings. The poison is in the model's body. And the memory of a bad taste is in the bird.)
- *deep:* A tiger's coat hides her from a deer and stands out to you. Is the tiger camouflaged or not? (hint: Ask whether camouflage belongs to the coat, or to the coat and an eye together.)
- *big:* Deer make tigers stealthy, and tigers make deer watchful. Does either side ever win? (hint: Think about what would happen to tigers if they caught every deer.)
- *big:* People can change a place in ten years. A body changes over thousands of years. What follows from that? (hint: Think about the great Indian bustard and the power lines.)
- *deep:* A plant makes her leaves bitter and her ripe fruit sweet. Is she choosing who eats what? (hint: In the Bitter Leaf, Sweet Fruit activity in this unit, you taste a leaf and a ripe fruit and compare them. Also think about a chilli, which burns a rat's mouth but has no effect on a bird's.)
- *big:* Humans have small teeth, weak claws and slow legs. What are a human's adaptations? (hint: Look at your hands and your forward-facing eyes. Think about sweating, talking and making tools.)

## Story: The Third Butterfly
*Yazhini, her mother, Vasantha Teacher and the bulbul's visits are made up. These facts are real. The common mormon butterfly lays her eggs on curry leaf and lemon plants all over India. Her young caterpillars look like bird droppings. Older caterpillars are green, with marks like eyes and a forked organ that gives off a sharp smell. The chrysalis is green or brown. The males and some females are black with a row of white spots. Other females copy the common rose, a butterfly who is poisonous because she eats birthwort as a caterpillar. The common rose has a red body, and the copy has a black body. In 2014 Krushnamegh Kunte's team in Bengaluru found that one gene, called doublesex, switches the copied pattern on.*

Yazhini's mother wanted eight curry leaves for the sambar. Yazhini went to the pot on the balcony and came back with seven leaves and a complaint.

"A bird has dirtied our plant, Amma. In three places."

Her mother came to look. On three leaves sat three small blobs, shiny and brown, each with a smear of white. Then one of the blobs raised a tiny head and began to eat the leaf.

They were caterpillars. Yazhini ran next door to fetch Vasantha Teacher, who taught zoology at the women's college and knew the name of every wing in Madurai.

"Common mormons," said Vasantha Teacher, bending over the pot. "Their mother laid her eggs here last week. Look how well they are dressed. No bulbul wants to eat a bird dropping."

"All three have the same mother?"

"The same mother, on the same morning. I saw her from my window. She was black, with a row of white spots across her wings."

Yazhini's mother agreed to buy her curry leaves from the shop for one month. Yazhini began a diary.

Day 9. They have changed their clothes. They are green now, as green as the leaf, with a brown belt and two painted eyes that are not eyes. I touched one with a twig. A soft red fork shot out of her head, and the whole balcony smelled sharp and sour. I said sorry.

Day 16. They have stopped eating. Each one has spun a belt of silk and is hanging from a twig. Now they are chrysalises. The two on the green stems are green. The one on the dry brown stick is brown. How did she know?

Then nothing happened for eleven days, and Yazhini learned that nothing is the hardest thing to write about.

On the twelfth morning the first chrysalis was empty. A butterfly hung beside the shell, black, with a row of white spots across his wings like a necklace. By evening there was a second butterfly, exactly the same. Yazhini wrote: Day 28. Two brothers. Both like their mother.

The third came out the next morning. Yazhini looked at her for a long time. Then she went next door without her chappals.

"Teacher, the third one is wrong. She has red spots on her wings and a big white patch. She is not a common mormon. Some other butterfly's egg got mixed up in our pot."

Vasantha Teacher did not answer. She took a thick book from her shelf and opened it on the table. On the page was Yazhini's third butterfly: black, with red spots and a white patch.

"There," said Yazhini. "What is she called?"

"That is the common rose. As a caterpillar she eats a poisonous creeper, and she keeps the poison all her life. A bird who eats one is sick. That bird does not touch a second. Now look at the body in the picture."

The body of the common rose was bright red.

"Go and look at yours."

Yazhini went. The third butterfly was still drying her wings on the brown stick. Red spots. White patch. And a body as black as her brothers'.

She walked back slowly, thinking it through.

"She is ours," she said. "She is a mormon. But she is wearing the rose's wings."

"And why would that help her?"

Yazhini thought of the bulbul who sat on the balcony rail every morning, looking into the pot with one eye and then the other.

"Because some day that bulbul will eat a common rose, and be sick. After that she will not stop to check the colour of a body. She will see red spots and remember."

"So what keeps your third butterfly safe?"

"Not her taste. She tastes fine." Yazhini frowned. "The other butterfly keeps her safe. And the bulbul's memory. She is safe because of two animals she has never met."

"Only the daughters dress like this, and not all of them," said Vasantha Teacher. "Nobody is quite sure why. Perhaps you will be the one who finds out."

That evening the two brothers left first, straight up and over the neem tree. Their sister stayed on the rail a little longer. The bulbul landed a metre away. She looked at the red spots with one eye, and then with the other. Then she flew down to the guava tree instead.

Yazhini opened her diary. Day 29, she wrote. Three butterflies, and all of them ours. One is carrying a warning that belongs to somebody else.

**For the parent:** The third butterfly had the wings of the poisonous common rose and the black body of a common mormon. Yazhini saw that the wings were a copied warning. A copy protects only because another animal is truly bitter and a hunter remembers.

- Ask: Why did the black body show that the third butterfly was a common mormon?
- Ask: What two things must be true in the garden for the copied wings to protect her?
- Ask: What might happen to the copycats if common rose butterflies disappeared from Madurai?

## Games
### g1 · Catch, Escape or Place? The adaptation cards (20 min)
- **rules:** One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and reads the front aloud. The reader keeps the back hidden.; Every other player puts one job token face down. Catch means it helps a hunter. Escape means it helps against being eaten. Place means it helps with heat, cold, water or mud.; Everyone turns the tokens over together. The reader reads the back. Each player whose token matches scores 1 point.; A player who also gives the reason, before the back is read, scores 1 more point. The reader decides, using the back of the card.; With two players, swap the reader's role after every card. The reader does not guess on their own card.; Playing alone? Read a card, say the job and the reason aloud, then turn the card over. Score 1 point for each right job.; After all 24 cards, the player with the most points wins. If you play alone, your score out of 24 is your result.
- **Printable cards: 24 adaptation cards: a feature on the front, its job and the reason on the back:** A tiger's two eyes both face forward. (back: Catch. Both eyes see the same deer, so the tiger can judge exactly how far to leap.); A spotted deer's eyes sit on the sides of her head. (back: Escape. She sees almost all the way round, so a hunter has few places to hide.); A barn owl's wing feathers have soft edges like a comb. (back: Catch. The soft edges quieten the air, so a mouse does not hear the owl coming.); A house gecko drops her tail when a cat grabs hold. (back: Escape. The tail keeps wriggling and holds the cat's eye while the gecko runs. A new tail grows.); A camel can close her nostrils to narrow slits. (back: Place. They also keep grazing mouths away. Either token scores.); A deodar's branches slope downwards and bend. (back: Place. Snow slides off before its weight can snap the branch.); A dhole hunts together with her whole pack. (back: Catch. Together the pack can tire out a sambar deer who is far bigger than one dhole.); An Indian pangolin rolls into a ball of hard scales. (back: Escape. A leopard's teeth cannot get through the scales.); A lotus stem is full of air tubes. (back: Place. The air keeps the leaf afloat and carries oxygen down to roots in airless mud.); A Russell's viper has hollow fangs that inject venom. (back: Catch. The venom soon stops a rat, so the snake does not have to wrestle her prey.); A plain tiger butterfly is bright orange and flies slowly in the open. (back: Escape. The colour warns birds that she tastes bitter. A bird who has tried one leaves the rest alone.); A snow leopard has wide, furry paws. (back: Place. Wide paws spread her weight on soft snow, and the fur keeps the pads warm.); A crab spider sitting on a yellow flower is yellow too. (back: Catch. A bee does not notice the spider and lands within reach.); A herd of spotted deer feeds below a troop of langurs. (back: Escape. The langurs see far from the treetops and bark when a tiger comes.); A flamingo has long thin legs and a bent beak that strains water. (back: Place. She wades in shallow salty lakes and strains tiny food from the water.); An Indian crested porcupine rattles her quills and runs backwards at a leopard. (back: Escape. The quills come loose and stick in the attacker's face. A porcupine cannot shoot them.); A mangrove tree stands on arching roots like stilts. (back: Place. The stilt roots hold the tree up in soft mud when the tide pulls.); A cheetah has a bendy spine and a long tail. (back: Catch. The spine stretches each stride, and the tail keeps her balanced in a sharp turn.); A blackbuck leaps and swerves as she runs. (back: Escape. A sharp turn at the last moment makes a faster hunter overshoot.); A female danaid eggfly butterfly looks like the bitter plain tiger. (back: Escape. She tastes fine, but birds who have tried a plain tiger leave her alone. This is mimicry.); A yak has long outer hair over a thick woolly undercoat. (back: Place. The double coat traps air and holds her body heat in the cold of Ladakh.); An archerfish spits a jet of water at an insect on a leaf. (back: Catch. The jet knocks the insect into the water. Archerfish live among mangrove roots.); A touch-me-not plant folds her leaves when something touches them. (back: Escape. Scientists think a folded leaf looks small and wilted, so a grazing animal moves on.)
- **Printable tokens: 3 job tokens: Catch, Escape, Place. Print one set for each player:** Catch; Escape; Place
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the tokens. Shuffle the cards into a pile. Give each player one set of three tokens.  
  *Parent says:* Each card describes one real adaptation. Your three tokens are three jobs: catch prey, escape a hunter, or live in a hard place. We will say which job each adaptation does.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take three tokens.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first twelve cards. After each card, read the back aloud. Ask the question below after card six.  
  *Parent says:* The reader reads the front. Choose a token and put it face down. Say your reason. Now turn the token over, and the reader reads the back.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the card. Pick a job token. Say why. Check the back.
  - Ask: How did you decide which job the adaptation does? (good: I asked what problem it solves.; I asked whether the animal eats others or is eaten.; I asked whether the problem was a hunter or the weather.)

- **s4 (4 min)**

  *Parent says:* Let us pair the cards. For each Catch card, find an Escape card that works against it. Which Escape card answers the tiger's forward eyes? Which answers the cheetah's speed?  
  *Child mode:* Pair each Catch card with an Escape card. Which answers which?
  - Note: Good pairs are forward eyes against side eyes, stalking against the langurs' watch, and speed against swerving. Any pair your child can explain is a good pair.
  - Check: Which prey adaptation best answers a cheetah's great speed? → **A sharp swerve at the last moment**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Snow Load Test: which tree shape carries the least snow? (30 min)
- **question:** Ten level teaspoons of rava are poured on the top of a paper tree. How much stays on a flat tree, on a low cone and on a steep cone?
- **predict:** Before you pour, guess. Which paper tree will keep the most rava snow, and which will keep the least? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut all three trees from circles of the same size and the same paper. Pour the same 10 level teaspoons on each tree. Pour from the same height, about 10 centimetres, onto the very top. Stand every tree on the same upturned tumbler. Test each tree twice, because one pour can be a fluke. Only the slope of the tree changes.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows, for the flat tree, the low cone and the steep cone. For each tree, write how many teaspoons stayed on in try 1 and in try 2. Measure to the nearest half teaspoon.
- **results table:** Tree shape | Teaspoons of snow that stayed on, try 1 | Teaspoons of snow that stayed on, try 2
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the paper, the tumbler and the rava. Describe the three trees you will make. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will make three paper trees: a flat one, a low cone and a steep cone. Rava will be our snow. Which tree will keep the most snow on top, and which the least? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three paper trees get rava snow. Which keeps the most? Which keeps least? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that tree will keep the least snow? (good: The snow will slide down the steep sides.; There is no flat place for it to sit.; It is like a slide in the park.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw round the plate three times and cut out the circles. Fold each circle in half three times, then open it. The folds mark eight slices. Leave the first circle flat. On the other two, cut along one fold from the edge to the centre. Slide one cut edge over the next slice on the second circle, and tape it. On the third, slide it over four slices, and tape it.  
  *Parent says:* All three trees start as the same circle. The flat tree stays a flat circle. The low cone has one slice tucked under. The steep cone has four slices tucked under, which is half the circle.  
  *Child mode:* Cut three circles. Fold each into eight slices. Make one flat tree. Make a low cone. Make a steep cone.
  - Note: Stiff paper keeps its shape best. If you have only thin paper, tape two sheets together before you cut.
  - Check: Why are all three paper trees cut from circles of the same size? → **So that only the slope differs**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the tumbler upside down in the middle of the tray. Rest the flat tree on the tumbler. Pour 10 level teaspoons of rava onto the middle, one at a time, from about 10 centimetres above. Lift the tree and tip the rava that stayed on into the bowl. Count it back in level teaspoons and record it. Do this twice.  
  *Parent says:* This is the flat tree. Pour ten level teaspoons on the middle, slowly. Now lift the paper carefully and tip the snow into the bowl. How many teaspoons stayed on?  
  *Child mode:* Pour ten teaspoons on the flat tree. Tip what stayed into the bowl. Count it.
  - Note: Pour the rava from the tray back into the cup after each try, so that none is wasted.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the low cone on the tumbler like a hat. Pour 10 level teaspoons of rava onto the tip, from the same height. Measure what stayed on, and record it. Do this twice.  
  *Parent says:* Now the low cone. Pour onto the very tip, from the same height as before. Watch what the rava does when it lands. How many teaspoons stayed on this time?  
  *Child mode:* Pour ten teaspoons on the low cone. Watch the rava. Count what stayed.
  - Ask: What did you see the rava do on the low cone? (good: Some stayed in a heap at the top.; It piled up and then slid down in a rush.; Some rolled off the edge.)
  - Note: The low cone gives the most varied results. On shiny paper most of the rava slides off. On rough paper a heap stays near the tip.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the steep cone on the tumbler. Pour 10 level teaspoons of rava onto the tip, from the same height. Measure what stayed on, and record it. Do this twice.  
  *Parent says:* Last, the steep cone. Pour onto the tip again. How many teaspoons stayed on?  
  *Child mode:* Pour ten teaspoons on the steep cone. Count what stayed.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Now think of a real tree. What would a heavy load of snow do to a wide, flat branch?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the steep cone keep so little rava? (good: The slope was too steep for the grains to stay.; Each grain slid or rolled down.; There was nothing flat to hold a heap.)
  - Ask: A banyan grows wide and flat-topped. Why is that shape a good one in Chennai? (good: There is no snow there.; A wide tree catches more sunlight.; Her leaves spread out to the light.)
  - Note: In most homes the flat tree keeps nearly all 10 teaspoons and the steep cone keeps less than 1. The low cone is in between. Write down what you really found. The reason: the paper grips each grain, and the grain's weight drags it down the slope. On a steep slope the weight wins, and the grain slides. Firs, spruces and young deodars on Himalayan slopes are narrow cones, and their branches slope down and bend. Snow slides off before its weight can break them. A wide crown catches more light, so trees in warm places can spread out. Wet snow is stickier than rava, so real trees need bendy branches as well as steep ones.
  - Think: A steep cone sheds snow, but it is narrow and catches less light from above. How could a tree do both?
### e2 · The Copycat Test: is a copied warning still safe when copycats are common? (30 min)
- **question:** A hunter learns that a pattern means a bitter taste. Does the hunter leave harmless copycats alone when 2 patterned squares in 12 are copycats? What about when 10 in 12 are copycats?
- **predict:** Before the hunts, guess. One hunt has few copycats among the patterned squares, and the other has many. In which hunt will more copycats be left alive? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use 16 squares of the same paper in each hunt: 4 plain and 12 with a pattern. Keep the scores the same in both hunts: plus 1 for a tasty square and minus 2 for a bitter one. The same person is the hunter both times, with the same rule. Only the share of copycats changes, from 2 in 12 to 10 in 12. The pattern also changes from stripes to dots, so that the hunter starts each hunt without a habit. Say so when you report your result.
- **record:** Draw a table with two rows, one for each hunt. Write how many patterned squares the hunter ate out of 12. Write how many copycats were eaten and how many were left alive. Write the hunter's score.
- **results table:** Hunt | Patterned squares eaten, out of 12 | Copycats eaten | Copycats left alive | Hunter's score
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the hunt. Your child will be the hunter. Tell them the scores, and that one hunt has few copycats and the other has many. Do not say which. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* You will be a hungry bird. A tasty square scores plus 1 and a bitter square scores minus 2. Some bitter squares carry a pattern, and some tasty ones copy it. One hunt has few copycats and one has many. In which hunt will more copycats be left alive? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You are the hunter. One hunt has few copycats. One has many. Where are copycats safer? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think copycats will be safer in that hunt? (good: If most patterned ones are bitter, I will stop eating them.; If most are tasty, I will keep eating them.; The copycats hide among the bitter ones.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Send your child to draw the results table in another room. Cut each sheet into 16 pieces. For hunt 1, draw three thick stripes on 12 pieces and leave 4 plain. Underneath, write B on 10 striped pieces, and T on 2 striped pieces and on the 4 plain ones. For hunt 2, draw three big dots on 12 pieces and leave 4 plain. Underneath, write B on 2 dotted pieces, and T on 10 dotted pieces and on the 4 plain ones.

  - Note: Draw all the patterns first and write the letters afterwards, so that copycats and bitter squares look exactly alike. Write the letters small and in pencil. Lay the hunt 1 squares on the newspaper, pattern side up and well mixed. Keep hunt 2 out of sight.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Call your child. Read out the rules. They turn over one square at a time and keep a running score. They may stop whenever they like. Put the eaten squares in a pile.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the first forest. Turn over one square at a time. T is tasty and scores plus 1. B is bitter and scores minus 2. Stop whenever you like. Try to finish with the highest score you can.  
  *Child mode:* Turn over one square at a time. T scores 1. B loses 2. Stop when you like.
  - Note: A curious child may want to turn over every square. Remind them that the aim is the highest score.
  - Check: In the Copycat Test, why does a bitter square take away 2 points? → **A bad meal costs a real bird something**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the striped squares in the eaten pile, and how many of them were copycats marked T. Then turn over all the squares left on the newspaper. Count the striped copycats left alive. Record the numbers and the score.  
  *Parent says:* Let us count. How many striped squares did you eat out of 12? How many of them were copycats? Now turn over the rest. How many copycats are still alive?  
  *Child mode:* Count the striped squares you ate. Turn over the rest. Count the copycats alive.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Clear the newspaper. Lay out the hunt 2 squares, pattern side up and well mixed. Your child hunts again with the same rules.  
  *Parent says:* This is a different forest. The dotted prey here are a different kind of animal, so you know nothing about them yet. The scores are the same. Find out what you can, and get the highest score.  
  *Child mode:* This is a new forest with dotted prey. Hunt again. Get your best score.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the dotted squares in the eaten pile, and how many of them were copycats. Turn over the squares that are left. Count the dotted copycats left alive. Record the numbers and the score.

  - Ask: Why did you stop eating striped squares in the first hunt? (good: The first ones I tried were bitter.; I lost points, so I learned to avoid stripes.; It was not worth the risk.)
  - Ask: What does a copycat need, in order to be left alone? (good: Plenty of really bitter animals with the same pattern.; A hunter who has tasted the bitter kind.; Not too many other copycats.)
  - Note: Most hunters eat only 1 to 3 striped squares in the first hunt, so both striped copycats usually live. In the second hunt most hunters find that dotted squares are nearly all tasty, and eat most of them. Write down what you really found. A careful hunter may stop early in both hunts, and real birds differ in the same way. The reason: a hunter judges a pattern by the meals it has given so far. When most patterned prey are bitter, the hunter avoids the pattern, and the few copycats are protected. When most are harmless, the hunter learns that the pattern is safe. So a copycat depends on the bitter animals whose pattern she copies. Scientists call the copycat a mimic and the bitter animal a model. Jane Brower tested this in 1960 with starlings and bitter mealworms.
  - Think: Make 12 new squares with a third pattern. How many of them must be bitter before a hunter gives up on that pattern?

### a3 · Bitter Leaf, Sweet Fruit: what a plant lets you eat (15 min)
- **safety:** Taste only the kitchen foods named here. Never taste a wild plant, an unknown berry or milky sap. A grown-up does the cutting.

- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* Each of these is a part of a plant. Which is a leaf, and which is a fruit? A plant stays in one spot while animals feed on her. Which of these parts do you think the plant most needs to protect? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Name each plant part. Which part does a plant most need to protect? Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child tastes a tiny piece of the ripe fruit, then the leaf, then the unripe fruit. They sip water in between. For each one they write the taste and a score from 1 to 5.  
  *Parent says:* Taste a tiny piece of the ripe fruit first. Then the leaf. Then the unripe fruit. After each one, say the taste. Then give a score from 1 to 5 for how much an animal would want to eat more.  
  *Child mode:* Taste a tiny piece of each. Sip water in between. Score each from 1 to 5.
  - Note: Your child may spit out the bitter piece. The ripe fruit usually scores 4 or 5. The leaf and the unripe fruit usually score 1 or 2.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the lemon twig, the brinjal stalk or the lady's finger. Find thorns, hairs or a tough skin. Do not taste these.  
  *Parent says:* Taste is one defence. Look for others. Can you find thorns, tiny hairs or a tough skin? Which animals would each one keep away?  
  *Child mode:* Look for thorns, hairs and tough skin. Who would they keep away?
- **s4 (4 min)**

  - Ask: What does a plant gain when an animal eats her ripe fruit? (good: The animal carries the seeds away.; The seeds come out in droppings somewhere new.)
  - Ask: Why is an unripe fruit sour or bitter, and green? (good: The seeds are not ready yet.; Green hides the fruit among the leaves.; The plant keeps eaters away until the seeds can grow.)
  - Note: Leaves make the plant's food, so many plants defend them with bitter chemicals, hairs or thorns. A ripe fruit is different. It is sweet and brightly coloured, and it invites animals who will carry the seeds away. An unripe fruit is sour and green until the seeds are ready. The chilli is a special case. The burning chemical in a chilli hurts the mouths of rats and other mammals, who would crush the seeds. The sensors in a bird's mouth do not respond to that chemical. Birds swallow the seeds whole and spread them.
  - Check: Which part of a plant is most often made sweet for animals to eat? → **The ripe fruit**

  *Parent does:* Mark the start and the finish. The runner stands 3 big steps ahead of the chaser. Ask for a guess.

### c1 · Stand in the Mud: build a mangrove that stays up (20 min)
- **problem:** A mangrove tree grows in soft, wet mud. Deep in the mud there is no air, so her roots cannot go far down. Twice a day the tide pushes and pulls at her. Build a model tree that stands in loose rice. The model must stay up when the tray is slid from side to side 10 times.
- **your turn:** Now design your own roots. Draw the design first. Test it with 10 slides of the tray, then with 20. Write which roots failed first and what you changed.

- **creative:** Tie three sticks into a tripod halfway up the trunk, so that the tree stands on three legs.; Build two trees side by side and tie their roots together, as mangroves in a forest hold each other.; Use many short roots all the way round in place of a few long ones, and compare.

## Worksheets
### Adaptations: the main ideas (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is an adaptation?  
   Options: An inherited feature that helps survival / A skill an animal learns by practice / Any change in the weather / A place where an animal lives  
   **Answer:** An inherited feature that helps survival. *Why:* An adaptation is inherited from parents and helps a living thing survive and have young in their place. A learned skill is not inherited.
2. (mcq) Why are a tiger's eyes at the front of her face?  
   Options: To judge the distance to her prey / To see behind her / To keep dust out / To look fierce  
   **Answer:** To judge the distance to her prey. *Why:* Both eyes see the same deer from slightly different places. The brain uses the two views to judge the distance. A hunter needs that to leap exactly.
3. (mcq) Which of these is a behavioural adaptation?  
   Options: A lizard resting in a burrow at noon / A camel's wide feet / A cactus's spines / A yak's thick coat  
   **Answer:** A lizard resting in a burrow at noon. *Why:* A behavioural adaptation is something a living thing does. Wide feet, spines and a thick coat are parts of the body, so they are structural adaptations.
4. (fill) Bright colours that tell hunters an animal is bitter or can sting are called ____ colours.  
   **Answer:** ['warning']. *Why:* Warning colours are easy to see and easy to remember. A hunter who has had one bad meal avoids the pattern afterwards.
5. (fill) The animal who is copied is the model. The animal who copies her look is the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['mimic']. *Why:* A mimic gains from looking like the model. The female danaid eggfly is a mimic of the bitter plain tiger.
6. (truefalse) A porcupine can shoot her quills at an attacker.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The quills are loosely fixed in her skin. She runs backwards into the attacker, and the quills come off when they touch.
7. (truefalse) Camouflage is used by hunters as well as by prey.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A tiger's stripes and a crab spider's colour help them to get close to prey without being noticed.
8. (match) Match each plant to the place she is adapted to.  
   **Answer:** A cactus with spines and a thick stem → A hot, dry desert; A lotus with air tubes in her stems → A pond; A mangrove on stilt roots → Soft, salty mud by the sea; A deodar with sloping branches → A snowy mountain slope. *Why:* Each feature answers a problem of the place. Spines save water, and air tubes carry oxygen to roots in mud. Stilt roots hold a tree up in soft ground, and sloping branches shed snow.
9. (sort) Sort these adaptations. Does each one help a predator, or help prey?  
   **Answer:** hollow fangs with venom → Helps a predator; silent wing feathers → Helps a predator; eyes at the front of the face → Helps a predator; eyes at the sides of the head → Helps prey; a tail that breaks off → Helps prey; a ball of hard scales → Helps prey. *Why:* Ask what problem each one solves. Fangs, silent wings and forward eyes help to catch. Side eyes, a tail that breaks off and armour help against being caught.
10. (order) Put a tiger's hunt in order, from her first problem to her last.  
   **Answer:** ['Find a deer by sight, sound or smell', 'Creep close without being noticed', 'Rush and catch the deer', 'Kill with teeth and claws']. *Why:* A hunter must find prey before she can get near, and get near before she can catch and kill. Each step has adaptations of its own.
11. (mcq) Why does snow slide off a steep, cone-shaped tree?  
   Options: Snow cannot grip so steep a slope / The tree is warm / The needles push the snow away / Wind always blows it off  
   **Answer:** Snow cannot grip so steep a slope. *Why:* On a steep slope, the weight of the snow drags it downhill more strongly than the surface can hold it. In the Snow Load Test, rava slides off a steep paper cone in the same way.
12. (mcq) A hoverfly has no sting, but she is striped like a wasp. What is this called?  
   Options: Mimicry / Countershading / Masquerade / Co-evolution  
   **Answer:** Mimicry. *Why:* Mimicry is one kind of animal looking like another kind and gaining from it. Birds who have been stung by a wasp leave the hoverfly alone.
13. (write) Explain in two sentences why a plain tiger butterfly gains by being easy to see.  
   **Answer:** She tastes bitter. A bird who has tried one remembers the bright colours and leaves the others alone. If she were hidden, birds would not learn to avoid her kind.. *Why:* A strong answer says that she has a real defence, and that the bright colour helps hunters to remember it.
14. (write) A camel and a spiny-tailed lizard both live in desert heat. Describe how each one deals with it.  
   **Answer:** The camel stays out in the heat. Her body can warm up by about 6 degrees without harm, so she sweats little. The lizard avoids the heat by resting in her burrow in the hottest hours.. *Why:* A strong answer shows two different answers to one problem: one animal puts up with the heat, and the other avoids it.
15. (draw) Draw a hunter's head and a prey animal's head from the front. Show the eyes.  
   **Answer:** The hunter's head, such as a cat's, has both eyes at the front. The prey's head, such as a goat's or a deer's, has one eye on each side.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the hunter's eyes side by side, facing forward. The prey's eyes are far apart, on the sides of the head.
### Fitting a place, and fitting each other (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A deer's eyes have two kinds of colour cells. How does a tiger's orange coat look to her?  
   Options: Much like the green of grass / Bright red / Pure white / Bright blue  
   **Answer:** Much like the green of grass. *Why:* With two kinds of colour cells, a deer does not tell orange from green well. So the coat that looks bright to us blends with the grass for a deer.
2. (mcq) Which kind of camouflage does a stick insect use?  
   Options: Masquerade: she looks like a twig / Countershading / Warning colours / A disruptive pattern  
   **Answer:** Masquerade: she looks like a twig. *Why:* In masquerade the animal can be seen, but she looks like something not worth eating. A bird sees a twig and moves on.
3. (mcq) A chilli burns a rat's mouth and has no effect on a bird's. How does this help the chilli plant?  
   Options: Birds spread the seeds whole / Rats water the plant / Birds eat the leaves / The burn makes the seeds grow  
   **Answer:** Birds spread the seeds whole. *Why:* Rats crush the seeds with their teeth, and the burn keeps them away. Birds swallow the seeds whole and drop them somewhere new.
4. (mcq) Why do many leaves in rainy forests end in a long, narrow tip?  
   Options: Rain runs to the tip and drips off / The tip stores water / The tip stings insects / The tip catches more sun  
   **Answer:** Rain runs to the tip and drips off. *Why:* A leaf that stays wet catches less light, and moulds grow on it. A drip tip helps the leaf to dry sooner.
5. (truefalse) A chameleon's biggest colour changes are signals to other chameleons.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Scientists who studied dwarf chameleons found the biggest and fastest changes during displays to rivals and mates. Hiding is only part of the story.
6. (truefalse) In the common mormon butterfly, both males and females are mimics.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Only some of the females copy the poisonous common rose. The males, and the other females, are black with a row of white spots.
7. (sort) Sort these adaptations into three kinds.  
   **Answer:** a snow leopard's wide paws → Structural (body); a cactus's spines → Structural (body); deer feeding in a herd → Behavioural (what they do); a gecko hunting near a light at night → Behavioural (what they do); a camel's body warming by 6 degrees → Physiological (inner working); a seabird's body passing out extra salt → Physiological (inner working). *Why:* A structural adaptation is a part of the body. A behavioural one is an action. A physiological one is a way the body works inside.
8. (odd) Three of these animals use warning colours. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A plain tiger butterfly / A yellow paper wasp / A banded krait / A nightjar on dry leaves  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The butterfly, the wasp and the krait gain by being noticed. A nightjar gains by not being noticed, which is camouflage.
9. (count) A herd has 20 deer. Each hunt takes 1 at random. How many of 100 hunts take one chosen deer?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* About 5. Each deer has 1 chance in 20, and one twentieth of 100 is 5. Alone, she would be the target in all 100 hunts.
10. (match) Match each model to her mimic.  
   **Answer:** Plain tiger butterfly → Female danaid eggfly; Common rose butterfly → Female common mormon; Paper wasp → Hoverfly; Common krait → Common wolf snake. *Why:* Each model has a real defence: bitter taste, a sting or venom. Each mimic is harmless and looks like her model.
11. (write) In the Copycat Test, a hunter eats few striped squares and many dotted ones. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Most striped squares were bitter, so the hunter lost points and learned to avoid stripes. Most dotted squares were tasty, so the hunter learned that dots were safe and kept eating.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the hunter judges a pattern by the meals it has given so far.
12. (write) A great Indian bustard has eyes on the sides of her head. How does this help her, and harm her?  
   **Answer:** Side-facing eyes let her watch the ground and the sky all round for foxes and eagles. But she sees poorly straight ahead when she flies, so she does not see power lines in time.. *Why:* A strong answer shows that the same feature helps against the old danger and fails against the new one.
13. (write) Many people think bright colours in nature always mean danger. Give one example that fits and one that does not.  
   **Answer:** It fits a wasp, whose yellow and black warn of a sting. It does not fit a ripe mango, whose colour invites animals to eat the fruit and carry the seed.. *Why:* A strong answer shows that a colour is a signal. The meaning depends on who is sending the signal and why.
14. (perspective) Power lines cross the grassland where the last great Indian bustards live. People are deciding what to do.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that side-facing eyes do not show the bird a wire ahead. Another is that flaps make the wire easier to see from the side.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses the bustard's field of view, and takes the cost and the local knowledge seriously.
15. (label) Draw a cactus. Then label three adaptations, and write what each one does.  
   **Answer:** ['Spines: they lose far less water than leaves, and keep grazing animals away', 'Thick stem: it stores water', 'Waxy skin: it slows the escape of water']. *Why:* A strong answer links each part to the problem of a dry place: little water, and animals who want it.
### Testing ideas about adaptation (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Jays who had never met a monarch butterfly ate the look-alike viceroy. What does this show?  
   Options: A mimic is protected only by experienced hunters / Viceroys taste sweet / Jays do not notice orange / Monarchs are harmless  
   **Answer:** A mimic is protected only by experienced hunters. *Why:* The pattern meant nothing to jays who had never had a bad meal from it. Only jays who had tasted a monarch refused the viceroy.
2. (mcq) Factory smoke blackened the tree trunks. Why did pale peppered moths become rare?  
   Options: Birds found them easily on dark bark / The smoke poisoned them / Each moth turned dark in the soot / They flew away to the cities  
   **Answer:** Birds found them easily on dark bark. *Why:* Pale wings no longer matched the trunks, so birds ate most pale moths. Dark moths were born dark. No moth changed colour in her own lifetime.
3. (mcq) Two bitter kinds of butterfly share one wing pattern. How does each kind gain?  
   Options: Hunters need to learn only one pattern / Hunters stop hunting completely / Each kind becomes more bitter / Both become harder to see  
   **Answer:** Hunters need to learn only one pattern. *Why:* Some butterflies die while young birds are learning. If two bitter kinds share a pattern, they share those losses.
4. (mcq) Collars on cheetahs and impalas recorded their chases. How did impalas usually escape?  
   Options: By turning sharply at a lower speed / By running faster than the cheetah / By hiding in burrows / By fighting back  
   **Answer:** By turning sharply at a lower speed. *Why:* The cheetahs were faster. A fast body needs more room to turn, so a sharp turn at the last moment made the cheetah overshoot.
5. (truefalse) Over many generations, hunters and their prey change each other.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Prey who notice or escape a little better have more young, and so do hunters who catch a little better. This is co-evolution.
6. (count) Ten bitter squares score minus 2 each. Two tasty squares score plus 1 each. How many points are lost overall?  
   **Answer:** 18. *Why:* The bitter squares lose 10 times 2, which is 20 points. The tasty squares win 2 points. 20 minus 2 is 18 points lost. Eating striped squares in the first hunt of the Copycat Test does not pay.
7. (count) A paper tree keeps 4 of the 10 teaspoons of rava poured on it. What percentage slides off?  
   **Answer:** 60. *Why:* 10 minus 4 is 6 teaspoons that slide off. 6 out of 10 is 60 out of 100, which is 60 per cent.
8. (fill) Two kinds of living things changing over many generations, each because of the other, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['co-evolution', 'coevolution']. *Why:* Bats and moths have shaped each other in this way. So have cheetahs and antelopes, and milkweed and the plain tiger caterpillar.
9. (order) Put these events in order, to show how peppered moths changed near Manchester.  
   **Answer:** ['Pale moths rest unseen on pale, lichen-covered trunks', 'Factory smoke kills the lichen and blackens the trunks', 'Birds find and eat most of the pale moths', 'Dark moths survive and have more young', 'Almost all the moths in the area are dark']. *Why:* The place changed first. Then the birds' hunting changed which moths lived, and the dark form spread over many generations.
10. (write) The steep cone in the Snow Load Test is narrower than the flat tree. Why is the test still fair?  
   **Answer:** All the rava is poured on the very top of each tree, so every tree receives the same 10 teaspoons. The paper, the circle size and the pouring height are also the same.. *Why:* A strong answer notices that width could matter, and then shows how pouring on the tip removes that difference.
11. (write) Design a test: do birds near your home avoid yellow and black food? Say what you would keep the same.  
   **Answer:** For example: put out ten plain rice balls and ten rice balls striped with haldi and black sesame. Use the same size, the same place and the same time each day. Count how many of each are eaten over five days, and swap their positions each day.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the colour, keeps size, place and time the same, repeats the test, and counts.
12. (write) Only female common mormon butterflies are mimics. Suggest one reason, and say how you could test it.  
   **Answer:** Nobody knows for certain. One idea is that a female carrying eggs flies slowly and needs more protection. Another is that males need their own pattern so that females recognise them. A test could compare how often birds attack paper models of each pattern.. *Why:* This is an open question. A strong answer gives a reason that could be true and a test that could show it to be wrong.
13. (perspective) A village beside a forest keeps losing goats to a leopard at night. People are deciding what to do.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that a leopard's camouflage and night vision work only when she can get close unseen. Another is that the owner's loss is real and must be met.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses the leopard's adaptations, and takes the family's loss seriously.
14. (draw) Invent a prey animal for a snowy mountain where one hunter lives. Draw her, and label three adaptations.  
   **Answer:** For example: a hare-like animal with a white winter coat for camouflage and wide furry feet for soft snow. Her eyes are on the sides of the head, to watch for the hunter.. *Why:* A strong drawing has one adaptation for the cold or the snow, and one against the hunter. Each has a reason.
15. (match) Match each kind of camouflage to its example.  
   **Answer:** Matching the background → A nightjar on dry leaves; Disruptive pattern → A tiger's stripes in tall grass; Countershading → A fish with a dark back and a pale belly; Masquerade → A butterfly who looks like a dead leaf. *Why:* Matching blends with the background. A disruptive pattern breaks the outline. Countershading cancels the belly's shadow. Masquerade makes the animal look like some other thing.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is an adaptation?  
   Options: A cactus's waxy skin / A dog learning to sit / A puddle drying up / A scar on a knee  
   **Answer:** A cactus's waxy skin. *Why:* An adaptation is inherited and helps a living thing survive in their place. Learning to sit is learned, and a scar is not passed on to young.
2. (mcq) Which of these is a physiological adaptation?  
   Options: A cactus opening her skin holes at night / A tiger's claws / Deer living in herds / A stick insect's shape  
   **Answer:** A cactus opening her skin holes at night. *Why:* A physiological adaptation is a way the body works inside. Claws and shape are structural, and living in herds is behavioural.
3. (mcq) A tree lives in soft, salty mud where the tide pulls. Which adaptation suits her?  
   Options: Stilt roots / One very deep root / Needle leaves / A cone shape  
   **Answer:** Stilt roots. *Why:* Stilt roots prop a mangrove from many sides in soft mud. A deep root would find no air in the mud. Needles and a cone shape suit snow.
4. (mcq) Why does a yak have a thick double coat?  
   Options: To hold her body heat in the cold / To keep sand out / To float in water / To hide from eagles  
   **Answer:** To hold her body heat in the cold. *Why:* The coat traps a layer of air, and trapped air lets little heat through. In the cold of Ladakh that saves her energy.
5. (mcq) Why do many prey animals have eyes on the sides of the head?  
   Options: To see almost all the way round / To judge distance exactly / To see more colours / To see in the dark  
   **Answer:** To see almost all the way round. *Why:* Side-facing eyes give a wide field of view, so a hunter is noticed from almost any direction. Judging distance exactly needs two eyes on the same thing, as a hunter has.
6. (mcq) What does living in a herd give each deer?  
   Options: A smaller share of the risk / Sharper teeth / Better camouflage / A warmer coat  
   **Answer:** A smaller share of the risk. *Why:* A hunter takes one animal, and in a herd each deer is one among many. Many eyes also notice the hunter sooner.
7. (mcq) A tiger's stripes break up her outline. What is this kind of camouflage called?  
   Options: A disruptive pattern / Masquerade / Mimicry / Warning colours  
   **Answer:** A disruptive pattern. *Why:* A disruptive pattern cuts the body's shape into pieces, so the watcher does not see one animal. Masquerade is looking like some other thing, such as a twig.
8. (mcq) Why does a bitter butterfly gain from bright colours?  
   Options: Birds remember her and avoid her kind / Bright wings fly faster / The colour itself is poisonous / Birds are afraid of orange  
   **Answer:** Birds remember her and avoid her kind. *Why:* A bold pattern is easy to notice and remember. A bird who has had one bad meal then leaves that pattern alone. The poison is in her body, not in the colour.
9. (mcq) When is a harmless mimic safest?  
   Options: When harmful models are common / When mimics outnumber models / When there are no models / When hunters forget quickly  
   **Answer:** When harmful models are common. *Why:* Hunters avoid a pattern that usually gives a bad meal. If mimics outnumber models, or the models vanish, hunters learn that the pattern is safe to eat.
10. (mcq) In a fair test of paper tree shapes, what should be the only thing that changes?  
   Options: The slope of the tree / The amount of rava / The kind of paper / The height of pouring  
   **Answer:** The slope of the tree. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same. Then any difference in the result must come from the slope.
11. (mcq) Why do great Indian bustards die on power lines?  
   Options: They see poorly straight ahead in flight / They like to sit on wires / The wires are warm / They fly only at night  
   **Answer:** They see poorly straight ahead in flight. *Why:* Their eyes are on the sides of the head, which suits watching for foxes and eagles. Wires straight ahead are a new danger that those eyes do not show in time.

## Applications
- Road signs, safety vests and danger labels use red, yellow and black, the same warning colours that wasps use. They can be seen from far away and are easy to remember.
- Soldiers' uniforms and bird-watchers' hides use camouflage patterns that break up the outline, as a tiger's stripes do.
- In Rajasthan, people hang shining flaps called bird diverters on power lines. The flaps help great Indian bustards to see the wires.
- Farmers in Botswana painted eyes on the rumps of cattle. Lions hunt by ambush, and during the four-year study no painted cow was killed.
- In the Sundarbans in the 1980s, honey collectors wore face masks on the back of the head. Tigers are said to attack from behind.
- Farmers in Assam hang ropes smeared with chilli around fields. The burning chemical that protects the chilli plant also turns elephants away.
- Houses in Himachal Pradesh and Kashmir have steep sloping roofs. Snow slides off before the weight can break the roof.

## Open questions
- **Why are only female common mormon butterflies mimics?** Scientists have found the gene that switches the pattern on. They have several ideas about why males never use it, and no test has settled the matter.
- **What are a zebra's stripes for?** The best evidence says the stripes keep biting flies from landing. But scientists still argue about cooling and about dazzling a hunter.
- **How does a plant tell a caterpillar's chewing from the shaking of the wind?** Cress plants raise their defences after feeling the vibrations of chewing, and not after wind or insect song. Nobody yet knows how the plant tells them apart.
- **Why do some poisonous animals stay hidden while others advertise?** Some poisonous toads and caterpillars are dull and camouflaged. Scientists have several ideas about when a warning pays, and the evidence is mixed.
- **How fast can living things adapt to places that people are changing?** Peppered moths changed within about fifty years. Large animals with few young, such as bustards, may not have that much time. Nobody can predict which kinds will keep up.

## Connected maths
- In the Snow Load Test, 10 teaspoons of rava were poured on each paper tree. The flat tree kept 10, the low cone kept 5 and the steep cone kept 1. What percentage of the rava slid off each tree? **Flat tree: 0 per cent. Low cone: 50 per cent. Steep cone: 90 per cent.**. Take away what stayed on from 10. The flat tree lost 0, the low cone lost 5 and the steep cone lost 9. Out of 10, these are 0 tenths, 5 tenths and 9 tenths. As percentages they are 0, 50 and 90. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Snow Load Test, each paper circle is folded into 8 equal slices. What is the angle of one slice at the centre? The steep cone has 4 slices tucked under. How many degrees of paper are left showing? **One slice is 45 degrees. The steep cone shows 180 degrees, which is half the circle.**. A full turn is 360 degrees. 360 divided by 8 is 45. Four slices are 4 times 45, which is 180 degrees tucked under. 360 minus 180 leaves 180 degrees showing. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L5`)
- In the Copycat Test a tasty square scores plus 1 and a bitter square scores minus 2. Hunt 1 has 12 striped squares: 10 bitter and 2 tasty. Hunt 2 has 12 dotted squares: 2 bitter and 10 tasty. What would a hunter score for eating all 12 patterned squares in each hunt? **Hunt 1: minus 18. Hunt 2: plus 6.**. Hunt 1: 10 bitter squares lose 20 points and 2 tasty squares win 2, which makes minus 18. Hunt 2: 2 bitter squares lose 4 points and 10 tasty squares win 10, which makes plus 6. So eating the pattern pays only in hunt 2. (queues `math.number-sense.negative-numbers.L5`)
- A hunter faces 12 patterned squares, with plus 1 for tasty and minus 2 for bitter. How many of the 12 must be bitter before eating all of them scores 0 or less? **4 or more. With 4 bitter and 8 tasty, the score is exactly 0.**. Try the numbers in order. With 3 bitter and 9 tasty, the score is 9 minus 6, which is plus 3. With 4 bitter and 8 tasty, it is 8 minus 8, which is 0. With 5 bitter it is 7 minus 10, which is minus 3. So the pattern stops paying when one third of the squares are bitter. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L5`)
- In Edge or Middle, the herd has 19 chana. There are 12 on the edge, 6 in the inner ring and 1 in the centre. The marble tiger attacked 12 times and caught an edge chana every time. What fraction of the herd was never in danger? If a deer stood alone, how many of 12 attacks would be aimed at her? **7 of the 19 were never in danger. A deer alone is the target of all 12 attacks.**. The inner ring and the centre make 6 plus 1, which is 7 chana out of 19. The 12 attacks were shared among the 12 edge chana, about 1 each. A deer alone has nobody to share with, so all 12 attacks are hers. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- A cheetah runs 28 metres each second, and a blackbuck runs 22 metres each second. The blackbuck starts 60 metres ahead and runs straight. How long does the cheetah take to catch up? A cheetah can sprint for about 20 seconds. Does she catch the blackbuck? **10 seconds. Yes, if the blackbuck runs straight.**. Each second the cheetah gains 28 minus 22, which is 6 metres. 60 divided by 6 is 10 seconds. That is less than 20 seconds, so the cheetah catches up. This is why a blackbuck swerves, as the runner does in Turn or Sprint. (queues `math.big-ideas.change-and-motion.L5`)
