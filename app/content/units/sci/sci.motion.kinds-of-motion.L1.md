# Rolling, sliding, spinning, swinging and bouncing: five ways things move
`sci.motion.kinds-of-motion.L1`

**Summary.** Give a round steel dabba the same push twice. On its side, it rolls far. Standing flat, it slides a little and stops. Things move in five main ways: they roll, slide, spin, swing and bounce. Your child moves in all five ways, hunts for them at home, and finds that rolling goes much further than sliding.

**Intro.** *Can you move your body in five different ways? Try to roll, slide, spin, swing and bounce.* Everything that moves moves in some way. A ball rolls. A chappal slides on a wet floor. A fan spins. A swing swings. A ball bounces. In this unit you move in all five ways, find them at home, and test one thing two ways: does it go further when it rolls or when it slides?

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say the five ways things move: roll, slide, spin, swing and bounce.
1. By the end I can show each of the five ways with my own body.
2. By the end I can find things at home that roll, slide, spin, swing or bounce.
3. By the end I can say that rolling goes further than sliding with the same push, and why.
4. By the end I can say that a thing bounces when it squashes and springs back.
5. By the end I can make a guess, test it, and say what I saw.

## Terms
- **Motion**: Motion means moving. Anything that is moving is in motion. *e.g.* A running dog, a falling leaf and a turning fan are all in motion.
- **Roll**: To roll is to turn over and over while moving along. Only part of a rolling thing touches the floor at a time. *e.g.* A ball rolls across the floor. A lemon rolls off the table.
- **Slide**: To slide is to move along while the same part keeps touching the floor and rubbing on it. *e.g.* A plate slides when you push it across the table. Your socks slide on a smooth floor.
- **Spin**: To spin is to turn round and round in one place. A spinning thing turns round its middle. *e.g.* A ceiling fan spins. A top spins on its point.
- **Swing**: To swing is to move back and forth, hanging from one point. *e.g.* A swing on a rope goes back and forth. Your arms swing when you walk.
- **Bounce**: To bounce is to hit something and spring back off it. A bouncing thing squashes a little, then springs back to its shape. *e.g.* A rubber ball bounces up from the floor. A ball of atta dough does not.

## Explanations
### Five ways to move · *defined*
**Definition:** Most moving things roll, slide, spin, swing or bounce. Many things can move in more than one of these ways.

Rolling is turning over while going along. Sliding is going along while rubbing. Spinning is turning round in one place. Swinging is going back and forth from a hanging point. Bouncing is hitting something and springing back. A ball can roll, spin and bounce. So the kind of motion is about how a thing moves, not only about what it is.
- e.g. A ball rolls on the floor, spins on a finger and bounces off a wall.
- e.g. Your body can roll on a mat, slide in socks, spin round, swing your arms and bounce on the spot.
- e.g. A steel plate slides on the table, spins when you twist it and rolls when you stand it on its edge.
### Rolling goes further than sliding · *defined*
**Definition:** With the same push, a thing that rolls goes much further than the same thing sliding, because rolling rubs the floor far less.

When the dabba slid, its whole flat bottom rubbed along the floor. That rubbing pushes against the movement and stops it soon. When the dabba rolled, each part touched the floor for a moment and was lifted off again. It hardly rubbed at all, so it kept going. Scientists call the rubbing that slows things down friction. This is why wheels make heavy things easier to move.
- e.g. In the Roll or Slide Test, the dabba on its side rolled many tiles. Standing flat, it slid only one or two.
- e.g. A suitcase with wheels is easy to pull. Without wheels, it scrapes and is hard to drag.
- e.g. Gas cylinders are tipped onto their round edge and rolled, not dragged.
### Bouncing is squashing and springing back · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing bounces when it squashes as it hits the floor and then springs back to its shape, pushing itself up.

A rubber ball squashes a tiny bit when it hits the floor. It springs back to round so fast that it pushes off the floor and jumps up. A ball of atta dough squashes too, but it stays squashed. It does not spring back, so it does not bounce. Things that spring back well bounce well.
- e.g. In the Bounce Test, the rubber ball bounced high and the dough ball went splat.
- e.g. A crumpled paper ball bounced a little, because the paper springs back only a little.
- e.g. Your knees bend and straighten when you bounce on the spot.
### Spinning and swinging go round a still point · *defined*
**Definition:** A spinning thing turns round its middle, which stays in one place. A swinging thing hangs from one point and moves back and forth below it.

When a fan spins, the middle of the fan stays put while the blades go round it. When a bag swings on a door handle, the handle stays still while the bag goes back and forth under it. A swing slows down and stops in the end, unless someone pushes it again.
- e.g. A ceiling fan spins round its middle.
- e.g. A bag on a door handle swings back and forth under the handle.
- e.g. The whole Earth spins round once every day, carrying us with it.

## Mostly agreed
- With the same push, rolling things go further than sliding things. *How we know:* A dabba on its side rolls across a room. The same dabba standing flat slides a short way and stops. Wheels make carts, cycles and suitcases easy to move.
- Things bounce because they squash and spring back. *How we know:* Slow-motion films show a ball squashing flat where it hits the floor, then springing back as it leaves. Dough, which does not spring back, does not bounce.
- The Earth spins round once a day. *How we know:* The Sun, the Moon and the stars all seem to move across the sky once a day. Photos from space show the Earth turning.

## How we found out
**Potters in Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq; early cart-makers in Mesopotamia and Europe; and the people of the Indus Valley cities, such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, About 5,500 to 4,000 years ago.** Many people think the first wheel was made for carts. But archaeologists, people who dig up and study the past, found something different. The oldest wheels we know of did not roll at all. They spun. In Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, potters put clay on a flat round wheel and turned it, more than 5,500 years ago. The clay spun round and round in one place while the potter shaped a round pot. Later, people stood wheels on their edge and fixed them to carts. Now the wheels rolled along the ground. A heavy load that was hard to drag could be pulled easily. The oldest wooden wheel found so far, from a marsh in Slovenia, is about 5,150 years old. In the Indus Valley cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, about 4,500 years ago, children played with small clay toy carts with wheels that turned. The same round shape could spin flat or roll on its edge. Potters in India still spin clay on wheels today.  
*What people believed before:* The wheel was first made for carts, to roll along the ground.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Round things always roll.** (A common first guess among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: Balls, lemons and bottles on their side all roll away.  
  Against: In the Roll or Slide Test, the round dabba standing flat did not roll. It slid. A round thing rolls only when its round side touches the floor.
- **Each thing has only one way of moving.** (A common idea, because we often say 'a ball rolls' or 'a fan spins'; *rejected*)  
  For: We usually see a ball rolling and a fan spinning.  
  Against: A ball can roll, spin, swing in a string bag and bounce. A steel plate can slide, spin and roll. How a thing moves depends on how it is pushed and how it touches the floor.
- **Only balls bounce.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Balls are the best-known bouncers.  
  Against: An empty plastic bottle, a crumpled paper ball and your own body on a mattress all bounce. Anything that squashes and springs back can bounce.

## Roots
We have rolled, slid, spun, swung and bounced, at home and with our own bodies. Now we ask what the Roll or Slide Test tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** With the same push, your dabba rolled far on its side but slid only a little standing flat. Nothing about the dabba changed, only how it met the floor. A rolling thing lifts each part off the floor instead of rubbing it along. So the way a thing moves can matter more than what it is made of.  
*Habit it breaks:* We usually think what a thing is made of, and how heavy it is, decide how far it goes. The test kept the dabba exactly the same and changed only how it touched the floor, and that made the biggest difference.  
*Evidence:* In the Roll or Slide Test, the same dabba with the same push went many tiles rolling on its side and only one or two tiles sliding flat.

- *who am i:* You are a body that can roll, slide, spin, swing and bounce. The same you moves in many ways.
- *what is life:* Living beings move in all five ways. A snake slides on her belly, a bat swings down from a branch, and a frog bounces from leaf to leaf.
- *what is this world:* Everything in the world is moving somehow. Even the floor under you is spinning with the whole Earth.

Rolling, sliding, spinning, swinging and bouncing look like five separate things. But the same dabba or plate can do several of them. The kind of motion grows out of how a thing meets the floor, the air and every push. Everything is conscious, and each moving thing meets the world in its own way, so one thing can show many ways of moving.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes meet at the hub of a wheel. It is the empty space at the centre that makes the wheel useful. (A wheel turns round its middle, and its middle stays where it is. In Spin It, every spinning thing turned round a middle that stayed put.)
- **Plato, Cratylus 402a, reporting Heraclitus:** The Greek thinker Heraclitus taught that everything moves and nothing stays still. You cannot step into the same river twice. (We found motion everywhere: in the kitchen, in our bodies, and in the spinning Earth under a dabba that seems still.)
- Science (settled): Rolling rubs much less than sliding. That is why wheels need only a small push to move heavy loads.
- Science (settled): The Earth spins round once a day. In India, the ground under you is carried round faster than an aeroplane flies.
- Other view, Many physicists: A rolling dabba is fully explained by the forces on it and the way it touches the floor. For them, nothing more is going on in the dabba, and feeling belongs only to living beings with nerves.
- Practice, Still on a spinning Earth (3 min): Sit on the floor and close your eyes. Sit very, very still. Now think: the floor is spinning with the whole Earth. You are moving faster than an aeroplane. Can you feel it? Now roll gently onto your side, and then sit up. Which was moving: you, or the floor?
- Ask: The dabba was the same both times. So what made the difference?
- Ask: When you sit very still, are you really still?
- Ask: Is there anything in the world that is not moving at all?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The dabba was the same both times. Why did it go so much further when it rolled? (hint: Think about which part of the dabba touched the floor each time.)
- *deep:* Why does a ball of dough not bounce, when a rubber ball does? (hint: Look at the dough after it lands. Did it go back to round?)
- *big:* The Earth is spinning right now. Why can't you feel it? (hint: Do you feel a smooth train moving when your eyes are shut?)
- *big:* When you sit very still, is any part of you still moving? (hint: Put a hand on your chest. Feel your breath.)

## Story: Kanmani and the Rice Tin
*Chennai is a real city in Tamil Nadu. Paati means grandmother in Tamil, and akka means elder sister, used for a young woman. Gas delivery workers in India often tip a cylinder onto its bottom edge and roll it. Children should never move gas cylinders. Kanmani, Paati, Amma and Selvi are made up.*

Kanmani was six. She lived on the third floor of a block of flats in Chennai, with her Amma and her Paati. Paati is her grandmother.

One morning the doorbell rang. It was Selvi akka, who brings the gas cylinders. A new red cylinder stood by the lift.

"That looks so heavy," said Kanmani. "How will you get it to the kitchen?"

Selvi akka smiled. She tipped the cylinder a little, so it stood on the round edge of its bottom. Then she turned it with both hands. The cylinder rolled on its edge, round and round, across the floor and into the kitchen.

"You didn't drag it!" said Kanmani.

"Dragging is hard work," said Selvi akka. "When I drag it, the whole bottom rubs on the floor. When I roll it, it just turns, and only a little bit touches the floor at a time."

After Selvi akka left, Kanmani wanted to try. Paati said, "Only grown-ups move gas cylinders. But you can try with something else."

Kanmani found the big steel rice tin. It was round, like a drum, and half full of rice. She stood behind it and pushed. The tin scraped and squeaked on the floor. It moved a tiny bit, then stopped. Her arms ached.

"Paati, it won't go!"

Paati checked that the lid was shut tight. Then she laid the tin down on its side. "Now push," she said.

Kanmani gave it a small push. The tin rolled! It rolled all the way across the kitchen and bumped gently into the door.

Kanmani stared. "It's the same tin. It has the same rice. I'm the same me. But now it goes!"

"It's the same tin," said Paati, "but it is touching the floor in a different way. Standing up, its flat bottom rubbed the floor. Lying down, it rolled."

All afternoon, Kanmani looked for ways that things move. An onion fell off the counter and rolled under the fridge. Her socks slid on the smooth floor. The ceiling fan spun round and round. Amma's cloth bag swung on the door handle. Her rubber ball bounced off the wall.

"Everything has its own way of moving," said Kanmani. "Balls roll and fans spin."

"Watch," said Paati. She put a steel plate flat on the floor and pushed it. It slid. She twisted it, and it spun. She stood it on its edge and gave it a push, and it rolled away in a wobbly line.

Kanmani laughed. "The same plate did three things!"

"It did," said Paati. "How a thing moves depends on how you push it and how it touches the floor."

That night, Kanmani lay in bed and rolled over. "I can roll," she thought. "I can slide on the floor in my socks. I can spin, and swing my arms, and bounce on my toes." She rolled over once more, and fell asleep.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kanmani thinks the heavy tin will not move. It slides only a little standing up, but rolls easily on its side. The tin did not change, only how it touched the floor. Then the plate shows that one thing can slide, spin or roll. How a thing moves depends on how it is pushed and how it meets the floor.

- Ask: How did Selvi akka move the heavy cylinder?
- Ask: Why did the rice tin go further when it was lying down?
- Ask: What three things did Paati's plate do?
- Ask: Which of the five ways can you move?

## Games
### g1 · Five Ways: the sorting card game (10 min)
- **rules:** Write ROLL, SLIDE, SPIN, SWING and BOUNCE on five sheets of paper. These are the mats.; A grown-up reads one card out loud.; The player shows the motion with their hands, then puts the card on a mat.; The grown-up reads the back. A right answer wins the card.; If the player gives a good reason for a different mat, that wins too.; Play until all 12 cards are won.
- **Printable cards: 12 moving cards:** A ball going down a slope (back: ROLL); A chappal on a wet bathroom floor (back: SLIDE); A ceiling fan (back: SPIN); An oonjal, a swing seat hanging in the hall (back: SWING); A rubber ball dropped on the floor (back: BOUNCE); A coconut falling off a cart and going down the road (back: ROLL); A child going down a slide in the park (back: SLIDE); A bambaram, a wooden top (back: SPIN); Your arms when you walk (back: SWING); You jumping on a mattress (back: BOUNCE); The wheels of an auto (back: ROLL, and they also spin); A box pushed across the floor (back: SLIDE)

  - Ask: Why did you put the auto wheels on ROLL? (good: They go round and round along the road.; They turn as the auto moves.)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Roll or Slide Test: does a dabba go further rolling or sliding? (20 min)
- **question:** With the same push, does a round dabba go further when it rolls on its side or when it slides standing flat?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the dabba go further lying on its side or standing flat? Or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same dabba, the same book and the same push every time. The book always moves from the start line to the stop line, then stops. Only the way the dabba sits changes. We try each way 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Draw the dabba on its side and standing flat. Next to each, draw a line as long as the way it went.
- **results table:** How the dabba sat | My guess | How many tiles it went
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stick two tape lines on the floor, one hand-width apart. 2. Show your child the dabba and the book.  
  *Parent says:* This is our pusher. You hold the book on the first line. Then you slide it to the second line and stop. That is one push.  
  *Child mode:* This book is your pusher. Slide it from one line to the next. Then stop.
  - Note: Stopping the book at the second line keeps every push about the same. A short, wide dabba stands flat without tipping over. Close it tight. A roll of cello tape works just as well.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the dabba on its side, then standing flat. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write or draw the guess.  
  *Parent says:* First we lie the dabba on its side. Then we stand it flat. Which way will it go further? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which way will the dabba go further? On its side or standing flat? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: On its side it can roll.; Standing up it is flat, so it will stop.; It is the same dabba, so the same.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Write it down as your child says it. Do not explain yet.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the dabba on its side just past the stop line, pointing down the room. 2. Your child pushes with the book 3 times. 3. Put a slip where it stops each time.  
  *Parent says:* Lie the dabba on its side. Push it with the book, and stop at the line. Watch where it stops. Put a slip there.  
  *Child mode:* Lie the dabba on its side. Push with the book. Mark where it stops.
  - Note: If the dabba curves into a wall, just try again. A rolling dabba often goes many tiles.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the dabba flat at the same place. 2. Your child pushes the same way 3 times. 3. Mark where it stops each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now stand the dabba flat. Push it the very same way. Where does it stop this time?  
  *Child mode:* Stand the dabba up flat. Push it the same way. Mark where it stops.
  - Ask: What did you see? (good: It slid a tiny bit and stopped.; It scraped on the floor.; It went much less far.)
  - Note: Standing flat, the dabba usually slides only a short way, often less than two tiles. Let your child hear the scrape.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the tiles to each slip together. 2. Fill in the table. 3. Ask what your child saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Let us count the tiles. Which way went further? What did you see? Why do you think it went further that way?  
  *Child mode:* Count the tiles. Which way went further? Why, do you think?
  - Ask: Why did it go further on its side? (good: It rolled.; Standing up, it rubbed on the floor.; Rolling only touches a little bit of the floor.)
  - Note: Now you can explain. Sliding rubs the whole bottom along the floor, and the rubbing stops the dabba. Rolling sets each bit down and lifts it up again, so it hardly rubs. Scientists call the rubbing friction.
  - Check: The same dabba got the same push. Which way did it go further? → **Lying on its side, rolling**
  - Think: What else in our home has wheels to make it roll?
### e2 · The Bounce Test: which balls bounce back up? (15 min)
- **question:** Which of these bounce back up: a rubber ball, a ball of socks, a paper ball, a ball of atta dough, and an empty plastic bottle?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which ones will bounce high, which a little, and which not at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We drop every ball from the same height, the top of a table, onto the same floor. We let go without throwing. We drop each one 2 times.
- **record:** Draw each thing and a curved line showing how high it bounced. Draw no line for things that did not bounce.
- **results table:** What I dropped | My guess | What it did: high, a little, or no bounce
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Line up the five things. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason for each. 3. Write or draw the guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Here are five things to drop. Which will bounce high, which a little, and which not at all? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the five things. Which will bounce? Which will not? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the dough will do that? (good: It is soft.; It is sticky.; It is squishy, so it will squash.)
  - Note: Write each guess down as your child says it. Do not explain yet.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds each thing at the edge of the table and lets go. 2. Drop each one twice. 3. Say together: high, a little, or no bounce.  
  *Parent says:* Hold it at the edge of the table. Let go, do not throw. Did it bounce high, a little, or not at all?  
  *Child mode:* Hold it at the table edge. Let go. Did it bounce?
  - Note: The rubber ball bounces high. The paper ball and the bottle bounce a little. The sock ball and the dough hardly bounce. Results can differ a little, and that is fine.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick up the dough ball and the rubber ball. 2. Look at their shapes. 3. Ask what is different.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the dough ball now. Look at the rubber ball. What is different about them after they landed?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the dough. Look at the rubber ball. What changed?
  - Ask: What happened to the dough? (good: It went flat.; It stayed squashed.; It did not go back to round.)
  - Note: Now explain. Both squash when they hit the floor. The rubber ball springs straight back to round, and that pushes it up. The dough stays squashed, so it does not bounce.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Squeeze the rubber ball, then the dough, in your child's hand. 2. Let go and watch.  
  *Parent says:* Squeeze the rubber ball, then let go. Now squeeze the dough. Which one springs back?  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze the ball. Let go. Now squeeze the dough. Which springs back?
  - Check: Why did the rubber ball bounce and the dough did not? → **The ball springs back to its shape, and the dough stays squashed**
  - Think: What else at home springs back when you squeeze it?

## Activities
### a1 · Five Ways With My Body: roll, slide, spin, swing, bounce (8 min)
- **safety:** Move the furniture with corners out of the way. Slide slowly in socks, holding a grown-up's hand. Spin only 3 turns, then stop and sit if you feel dizzy.
- **record:** Draw yourself doing your favourite one of the five.
- **s1 (2 min)**

  *Parent says:* Lie down on the mat. Now roll over and over, like a log. That is rolling.  
  *Child mode:* Lie on the mat. Roll over and over. That is rolling!
  - Ask: Which part of you touched the mat as you rolled? (good: My back, then my side, then my tummy.; A different part each time.)

  *Child mode:* Hold a hand. Slide in your socks. Your feet stay on the floor.
  - Ask: How is sliding different from rolling? (good: My feet stayed on the floor.; The same part touched the floor all the time.)
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child spins round slowly 3 times, arms out. 2. Then stands still and swings both arms back and forth.

  *Child mode:* Spin round three times. Stop. Now swing your arms back and forth.

### a2 · The Kitchen Motion Hunt (10 min)
- **safety:** Use only safe, unbreakable things. Keep away from the stove, knives and the mixie.
- **record:** Draw one kitchen thing on each of the five slips.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the five slips. 2. Read each word with your child.  
  *Parent says:* Here are five slips: roll, slide, spin, swing and bounce. Let us find kitchen things for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Here are five slips. Find a kitchen thing for each one.
  - Note: A lemon or onion rolls. A steel plate slides on the floor and spins when twisted. A ladle swings on a hook. A ball bounces.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child tries each thing. 2. They put it on the slip for how it moved.  
  *Parent says:* Try the lemon. How did it move? Put it on that slip. Now try the plate.  
  *Child mode:* Try each thing. How did it move? Put it on that slip.
  - Ask: Can one thing go on two slips? (good: Yes, the plate slid and spun.; The ball rolled and bounced.)
  - Note: Let your child find that some things fit more than one slip. That is the main idea.
- **s3 (2 min)**

5. (mcq) Why did the dabba standing flat stop so soon?  
   Options: Its flat bottom rubbed on the floor / It was heavier standing up / It got tired  
   **Answer:** Its flat bottom rubbed on the floor. *Why:* When a thing slides, the same part rubs along the floor all the way, and the rubbing slows it down.
6. (mcq) A ball of atta dough falls on the floor. What happens?  
   Options: It goes flat and does not bounce / It bounces high / It rolls away  
   **Answer:** It goes flat and does not bounce. *Why:* Dough squashes and stays squashed. It does not spring back, so it does not bounce.
7. (mcq) What does a ball need to do to bounce?  
   Options: Squash and spring back to its shape / Be very big / Be dropped very gently  
   **Answer:** Squash and spring back to its shape. *Why:* A ball bounces when it springs back after squashing, pushing itself off the floor.
8. (mcq) Which of these does a steel plate NOT do easily?  
   Options: Bounce high / Slide / Spin  
   **Answer:** Bounce high. *Why:* A steel plate slides when pushed and spins when twisted. It hardly springs back, so it does not bounce high.

   Options: Rolling needs a much smaller push than sliding / Wheels make things lighter / Wheels stop things moving

## Open questions
- **Why does a rolling thing stop in the end?** We know rolling hardly rubs. But a rolling ball still slows down and stops. Scientists know some reasons: the ball and the floor squash a tiny bit, and air pushes against it. Working out exactly how much each reason matters, for every kind of ball and floor, is still being studied.
- **Who made the very first wheel, and where?** The oldest wheels found are more than 5,000 years old, from several parts of the world. Nobody knows whether one group of people invented the wheel and others copied it, or whether people in different places found it on their own.

## Connected maths
- In the Roll or Slide Test, Meena's rolling dabba went 9 tiles. Her sliding dabba went 2 tiles. Which went further, and how many more tiles? **The rolling dabba went further, by 7 tiles.**.
