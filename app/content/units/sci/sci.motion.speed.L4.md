# Fast and slow: speed
`sci.motion.speed.L4`

**Summary.** A toy car rolls two metres across the floor. Did it go at one speed, or at many? In this lesson you time a car over a measured track and work out its speed, which is the distance covered in a time. You find out that a speed is always measured against something else: a floor, a clock, another mover. Then you follow speed outwards, from a snail and a cheetah to sound, the slow drift of India, the Earth in its orbit, and light, the one speed nothing overtakes.

**Intro.** *Slide one finger across the table as slowly as you can. Now slide it across again as quickly as you can. The table did not get longer. So what was different?* Both times your finger covered the same distance. The slow slide took more time, and the quick slide took less. Speed is the idea that joins those two things, how far and how long. Today you will measure a speed with a toy car, a tape and a stopwatch. Then you will ask an odd question: you are sitting still right now, so how fast are you going?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what speed means: the distance covered in each second, minute or hour.
1. I can time a toy car over a measured track and work out its speed by dividing the distance by the time.
2. I can tell an average speed over a whole trip from the speed at one moment.
3. I can say what a speed is being compared with, and show that the same thing has different speeds for different watchers.
4. I can put a snail, sound, an orbiting spacecraft, a drifting continent and light in order of speed, and say how each was measured.
5. I can explain why the speed of light is special, and name one question about speed that nobody can answer yet.

## Terms
- **Speed**: How much distance something covers in each second, minute or hour. *e.g.* A girl who walks 60 metres in one minute has a speed of 60 metres per minute.
- **Distance**: How far it is from one place to another along the path taken. *e.g.* The track in the toy car test is 200 centimetres from the start line to the end line.
- **Time taken**: How long something lasts from its start to its finish. *e.g.* The stopwatch shows that the car needed 3 seconds to go from the start line to the end line.
- **Unit of speed**: The way a speed is written: a measure of distance and a measure of time, such as metres per second. *e.g.* Centimetres per second for a toy car, kilometres per hour for a bus, centimetres per year for a continent.
- **Average speed**: The whole distance of a trip divided by the whole time, including the slow parts and the stops. *e.g.* A train that covers 500 kilometres in 10 hours has an average speed of 50 kilometres per hour, though it was faster between stations.
- **Steady speed**: A speed that stays the same, so that equal distances take equal times. *e.g.* The tip of a clock's second hand moves at a steady speed. A toy car rolling on the floor does not.
- **Reference point**: The thing that a speed is measured against. *e.g.* A passenger asleep in a moving bus has no speed measured against the seat, and 40 kilometres per hour measured against the road.
- **Speed of light**: The speed at which light travels through empty space, about 3 lakh kilometres every second. *e.g.* Light from the Sun takes a little over 8 minutes to reach your window.

## Explanations
### Speed is the distance covered in each second, minute or hour · *defined*
**Definition:** Speed is the distance something covers divided by the time it takes.

To know how fast something goes, you need two measurements. One is how far it went, and the other is how long that took. Divide the distance by the time, and you get the speed. A toy car that covers 200 centimetres in 4 seconds covers 50 centimetres in each second. We write that as 50 centimetres per second. The word 'per' means 'in each'. There are two fair ways to compare movers. Give them the same distance, and the one who takes less time is faster. Or give them the same time, and the one who goes farther is faster. A running race uses the first way. A game of 'how far can you hop in ten seconds' uses the second.
- e.g. A child walks 4 kilometres in 1 hour. Her speed is 4 kilometres per hour.
- e.g. A garden snail covers about 1 millimetre each second, so a 30 centimetre ruler takes them about 5 minutes.
- e.g. A bus that goes 90 kilometres in 3 hours has a speed of 30 kilometres per hour.
- **School science books, following Galileo Galilei's book 'Two New Sciences' of 1638:** Speed is distance divided by time. A bigger number means a faster mover.
- **Runners, and people giving directions in any Indian city:** It is often more useful to turn the sum over and say time for each distance. A runner speaks of 6 minutes for each kilometre. A neighbour says 'the market is ten minutes away'. Here a smaller number means faster. Both ways carry the same information.
- **The Vaisheshika school of India, in Prashastapada's commentary of about the 6th century:** These thinkers described 'vega' as a quality that a moving thing carries inside it, which keeps the thing going after the hand lets go. On this view, speed belongs to the thing. Physics today keeps part of this idea under the name momentum, and says that the number for speed still depends on who measures it.
### Average speed over a trip, and speed at one moment · *defined*
**Definition:** Average speed is the whole distance divided by the whole time. The speed at one moment is how fast something is going just then.

In the toy car test, the car took longer over the second metre than over the first. So the car did not have one speed. It was a little slower at every moment, because the floor and the air rubbed against it. When you divided 200 centimetres by the whole time, you found the average speed. That is the one steady speed that would have covered the same track in the same time. The needle of a scooter's speedometer shows something else, which is the speed at this moment. To get close to the speed at a moment, measure a very short piece of the trip. The shorter the piece, the closer you get.
- e.g. A train from Chennai to Coimbatore covers about 500 kilometres in about 6 hours. Its average speed is about 83 kilometres per hour, though the train stood still at each station.
- e.g. A cricket ball's speed on television is measured over a tiny part of its flight, just after the ball leaves the bowler's hand.
- e.g. On the way to school an autorickshaw may show 0, then 30, then 12 on its meter. The average for the trip is a different number again.
- **Bhaskara II, an astronomer of the Deccan, in the Siddhanta Shiromani, about 1150:** He saw that a planet's speed across the sky keeps changing, so a day's average was not good enough. He worked out its 'tatkalika gati', which means its motion at that very time, by using very small pieces of time.
- **Zeno of Elea, a Greek thinker of about 450 BCE, as reported in Aristotle's Physics, book 6, chapter 9:** At one single instant, a flying arrow covers no distance, because an instant has no length. Zeno asked how something that goes nowhere in each instant can be moving at all.
- **Mathematicians since Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz in the 1600s:** The speed at a moment is the number that the average speed gets closer and closer to, as the piece of time is made smaller and smaller. Most mathematicians take this as the answer to Zeno. Some philosophers reply that it tells us how to calculate the speed, and still does not say what moving at an instant is.
### Every speed is measured against something · *defined*
**Definition:** A speed is always stated against a reference point. Change the reference point, and the same mover has a different speed.

Walk down the aisle of a moving train. Measured against the seats, you go at about 4 kilometres per hour. Measured against the platform, you go at the train's speed plus your own, perhaps 104 kilometres per hour. Both numbers are correct. Each needs the words 'compared with' to be complete. Now sit still on the floor. Compared with the floor, your speed is zero. But the floor is on the Earth, and the Earth turns once a day. In Chennai that carries you eastward at about 1,600 kilometres per hour. In Srinagar, which is nearer the pole and goes round a smaller circle, it is about 1,400. The Earth also goes round the Sun at more than one lakh kilometres per hour. You feel none of it, because everything around you moves along with you.
- e.g. Two buses side by side at the same speed: a passenger in one sees the other bus as standing still.
- e.g. A ball tossed straight up in a moving train comes straight back to the hand, though someone on the platform sees it travel forward in a curve.
- e.g. At a station, the train beside yours starts to move, and for a moment you cannot tell which train is moving.
- **Everyday speech:** The ground is still, and speeds are measured against it. This works well for buses, races and road signs, which is why nobody adds 'compared with the road'.
- **Aryabhata, in the Aryabhatiya, Gola section, verse 9, written in 499 CE:** A person in a boat going forward sees the unmoving things on the bank as going backward. In the same way, he wrote, the unmoving stars seem to move west because the Earth turns. Most astronomers of his time, in India and elsewhere, disagreed and held that the Earth stands still.
- **Galileo Galilei, in his 'Dialogue' of 1632:** Shut yourself in a cabin of a ship that sails smoothly. Drip water, throw a ball, watch a butterfly. No test inside the cabin can tell you if the ship is moving or at rest. So steady motion and rest cannot be told apart from inside.
- **Astronomers who study the oldest light in the universe, since George Smoot's team in 1977:** This faint glow comes from every direction and is slightly warmer on one side of the sky. That shows our Sun moving at about 370 kilometres each second compared with the glow. Some call this the nearest thing to a reference point for the whole universe. Others answer that it is one more thing to compare with, and no law of nature prefers it.
### Light: the speed that is the same for everyone, and the top speed · *defined*
**Definition:** Light in empty space travels at 299,792 kilometres per second for every watcher, however the watcher or the lamp is moving. Nothing that has mass can reach this speed.

Throw a ball forward from a moving train, and the train's speed is added to the ball's. Light does not follow this rule. Switch on a headlamp on a moving train, and the light leaves at the speed of light, with nothing added. In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley looked for a change in the speed of light as the Earth moved through space. They found none. In 1905 Albert Einstein took this result seriously. If everyone measures the same speed for light, he reasoned, then moving watchers must disagree about lengths and about time. A clock that moves past you ticks more slowly than your own. The faster something goes, the more energy each extra bit of speed costs. Reaching the speed of light would need more energy than there is, so light's speed is a limit.
- e.g. Light could go round the Earth more than 7 times in one second.
- e.g. Moonlight is about 1.3 seconds old when it reaches your eyes.
- e.g. A phone call through a satellite high above the Earth has a small pause, because the signal travels up and down at the speed of light and no faster.
- **Isaac Newton, in the Principia of 1687:** Time flows at the same rate for everyone, everywhere. Speeds simply add. This view predicts the motion of balls, trains and planets very well, and it fails only when things move extremely fast.
- **Hendrik Lorentz and George FitzGerald, between 1889 and 1904:** Space is filled with a still, unseen 'ether' that carries light. Things that move through it shrink a little along their path, and that hides the change in light's speed. Their sums give the same answers as Einstein's. Most physicists dropped the ether because nothing has ever detected it.
- **Albert Einstein, in 1905:** There is no ether and no single time for everyone. The speed of light is the same for all, and time and length are what differ between watchers. Every test so far agrees with this.
### Living beings each move at speeds that fit their bodies and their lives · *idea*
A garden snail glides at about 1 millimetre each second. A cheetah in the wild has been measured at 93 kilometres per hour, and she can keep that up for only a few seconds. A shaheen falcon, the peregrine of the Indian hills, dives at more than 300 kilometres per hour. The biggest animals are not the fastest. An elephant and a blue whale are both slower than animals of middle size. Speeds run inside bodies too. The fastest nerve signals in you travel at about 100 metres each second, and the slowest at about 1. That is why you feel the knock of a stubbed toe first and the ache a moment later. Plants move as well. A bamboo shoot can rise several centimetres in a day, and a root tip feels their way round a stone.
- e.g. A bacterium such as E. coli swims about 10 of their own body lengths each second, turning towards food and away from harm.
- e.g. A human sprinter at full speed covers about 6 body lengths each second.
- e.g. Your fingernails grow about 3 millimetres in a month, which is a speed too.
- **Myriam Hirt and her team in Germany, 2017:** They compared about 470 kinds of animals and found one pattern for runners, swimmers and fliers. Top speed rises with body size, then falls again. Their explanation is that a big body needs a long time to speed up, and the quick energy in the muscles runs out first.
- **Biologists who measure speed in body lengths:** Kilometres per hour suit an animal of our size. Measured by the mover's own length, some bacteria cover 50 or more body lengths each second and beat a cheetah, who manages about 20. On this view, which being is fastest depends on the ruler you pick.
- **Jagadish Chandra Bose in Kolkata, with his crescograph of the 1910s:** Bose built an instrument that magnified a plant's growth ten thousand times, so that the growth could be watched from second to second. Plants move all the time and respond to what happens to them. Their movements only seem absent to watchers who live at our pace.
### The same sum works for sound, for the ground under India and for the sky · *defined*
**Definition:** Any speed, however great or small, is found in the same way: a measured distance divided by a measured time.

Sound travels through air at about 340 metres each second, a little faster on a hot day. Light is so much faster that a lightning flash reaches you almost at once. So count the seconds from the flash to the thunder. Every 3 seconds means the lightning was about 1 kilometre away. The land of India is moving too. India rides on a plate of rock that pushes north-east at about 5 centimetres a year. In a child's lifetime that cannot be seen. In a million years it adds up to 50 kilometres, and the push has raised the Himalayas. Above us, the Moon goes round the Earth at about 1 kilometre each second. The International Space Station goes round at about 7.7 kilometres each second, and circles the Earth about 16 times a day. The Earth goes round the Sun at about 30 kilometres each second. The Sun carries us round the centre of our galaxy at about 230.
- e.g. At a cricket ground, you see the bat hit the ball before you hear the crack from the far stand.
- e.g. Satellites measure the distance between two points on different plates year after year, and find it changing by a few centimetres.
- e.g. A spacecraft must reach nearly 8 kilometres each second sideways to stay in orbit close to the Earth.
### Can anything go faster than light? · *debated*
Einstein's limit says that nothing with mass can be pushed up to the speed of light. In 2011 a team in Italy reported tiny particles called neutrinos arriving a little sooner than light would. Physicists did not cheer. They checked. In 2012 the team found a loose cable in their timing equipment, and the early arrival vanished. Still, some questions about the limit are open. Very distant galaxies are getting farther from us faster than light could travel. This does not break the limit, because the galaxies are not racing through space. The space between us and them is stretching. Nobody knows why the limit has the value it has.
- e.g. A torch beam swept across the Moon can make a spot of light that crosses the surface faster than light. No thing and no message travels along with the spot, so the limit holds.
- e.g. In water, light slows to about three-quarters of its top speed, and fast particles can overtake it there. They give off a blue glow, seen in the water around nuclear reactors.
- **Most physicists, following Albert Einstein:** The limit has passed every test for more than a hundred years. A particle with mass would need endless energy to reach it.
- **E. C. George Sudarshan, born in Kerala, with Olexa-Myron Bilaniuk and V. K. Deshpande, in 1962:** Einstein's sums forbid crossing the speed of light. They do not forbid particles that have always been faster than light and can never slow down to it. Such particles were later named tachyons. Nobody has found one, and most physicists doubt that they exist.
- **Cosmologists, who study the universe as a whole:** The limit is about motion through space. Space itself can stretch at any rate. So there are galaxies whose light, sent out today, will never reach us.

## Mostly agreed
- A clock that travels fast runs slow compared with a clock that stays behind. *How we know:* In 1971 Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew very exact atomic clocks round the world on passenger planes and compared them with clocks left on the ground. The clocks disagreed by a few hundred billionths of a second, as Einstein's theory predicts. Satellite navigation systems correct for this every day.
- The sea floor is spreading, so the continents on either side are moving apart. *How we know:* In the 1950s the map-maker Marie Tharp plotted thousands of depth soundings of the Atlantic and found a long crack running down the middle of an undersea mountain chain. Her colleague Bruce Heezen at first dismissed it. Later measurements of the rock on both sides, and then direct measurements by satellite, showed the two sides moving apart by a few centimetres a year.
- Sound travels faster through water and through solids than through air. *How we know:* Measured speeds are about 340 metres each second in air, about 1,500 in water and about 5,000 in steel. Put your ear to a long railing while someone taps the far end, and the tap arrives through the metal before it arrives through the air.
- Animals, including us, settle into a walking speed that costs the least energy for each step of distance. *How we know:* In 1958 the doctor Henry Ralston measured the oxygen people used while walking at different speeds. The speed people chose freely was close to the one that used the least energy for each metre. Later studies found the same in horses.
- Stars far from the centre of a galaxy go round about as fast as stars near the centre. *How we know:* In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured the speeds of stars and gas in the Andromeda galaxy and many others. The planets of our Sun behave differently: the far ones are slower. Why galaxies do not follow that pattern is an open question.

## How we found out
**Ibn al-Haytham, Galileo Galilei, Ole Rømer, Giovanni Cassini, James Bradley, About 1021 to 1728.** For a very long time most thinkers held that light takes no time to arrive. It fitted what everyone saw: open your eyes at night and the stars are there at once. Around 1021, in Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham argued in his Book of Optics that light must take time, but he had no way to test it. In 1638 Galileo described a test with two people uncovering lanterns on distant hills. The delay was far too short for human hands. The answer came from the sky. At the Paris Observatory, the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer timed Io, a moon of Jupiter, as it slipped into Jupiter's shadow. The eclipses came early when the Earth was near Jupiter and late when the Earth was far away. In 1676 he predicted that one eclipse would be ten minutes late, and it was. The light had farther to travel. The observatory's director, Giovanni Cassini, did not accept this. In 1728 James Bradley found a second, separate sign of light's speed in the positions of stars, and the old idea was given up.  
*What people believed before:* Light arrives instantly, with no travel time at all. It was believable because no delay can be noticed over any distance on Earth with eyes and hands alone.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The speed of light was much greater when the universe was very young.** (John Moffat in 1993, and Andreas Albrecht and João Magueijo in 1999; *minority*)  
  For: The young universe was the same temperature everywhere, though its parts seem to have been too far apart for light to pass between them. A faster early light would explain that.  
  Against: No measurement has found any change in the speed of light. Most cosmologists explain the even temperature by a very fast early stretching of space, called inflation.
- **The universe is expanding faster than the early universe says it should, so some physics is missing.** (Adam Riess and his team, using nearby stars and exploding stars; *debated*)  
  For: Their measurements give about 73 kilometres each second for every megaparsec of distance. Measurements of the oldest light give about 67. Each team has checked its work for years, and the gap has not closed.  
  Against: Wendy Freedman's team, using other kinds of stars, finds values in between. That hints that a hidden error in measuring distances may explain the gap, with no new physics needed.
- **India once drifted north much faster than plates do today because the plate was unusually thin.** (Prakash Kumar and colleagues at the National Geophysical Research Institute, Hyderabad, in 2007; *debated*)  
  For: Before reaching Asia, India moved at about 15 to 20 centimetres a year, several times its speed now. Their measurements suggest the Indian plate is about half as thick as other old plates, so it could be pulled along more easily.  
  Against: Other teams explain the same speed by a hot plume of rock pushing from below, or by two sinking slabs pulling at once. The evidence does not yet choose between them.
- **A medieval Indian commentary gives the speed of light.** (Subhash Kak and others, writing since the 1990s about Sayana's 14th-century commentary on Rig Veda 1.50.4; *minority*)  
  For: Sayana writes that the Sun travels 2,202 yojanas in half a nimesha. With one common value for the yojana and one for the nimesha, this comes close to the measured speed of light.  
  Against: The line speaks of the Sun, not of light. The yojana and the nimesha had several different values, and other choices give very different answers. No method of measuring is described. Most historians of science treat the match as a coincidence.
- **The speed of light in one direction cannot be measured at all. Only the there-and-back speed can.** (The philosopher Hans Reichenbach in 1928, building on a remark in Einstein's 1905 paper; *debated*)  
  For: To time light going one way, you need two clocks set to the same time in two places. Setting them already assumes how fast signals travel between them. Every real measurement sends light out and back to one clock.  
  Against: Many physicists say that taking the same speed both ways is the simplest choice and that every other choice predicts the same results, so the question makes no difference.

## Roots
You have timed a toy car and worked out its speed. Now we ask what a speed really is, and what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You timed the toy car with a stopwatch, and then with a swinging key. The stopwatch counts the shakes of a tiny crystal, and the key counts its own swings. Each time, you set the car's movement beside another movement and compared the two. Nobody has ever measured a speed in any other way. So a speed always belongs to two movements together, and never to one thing alone.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the car's speed' as if speed sat inside the car, the way its colour does. That way of speaking makes each moving thing look separate and complete by itself. The test shows that the number appears only when one movement is held against another.  
*Evidence:* The same run of the car came out as about 3 seconds on the phone, about 3 swings of the long thread and about 6 swings of the short thread. The car did one thing. The number changed each time the clock changed.

- *who am i:* Your heartbeat, your breath and your steps are movements, and you use them as clocks all day without noticing. When you call something fast, you are comparing it with your own beat. So you are one of the movements doing the measuring, and you are inside what you measure.
- *what is life:* Every living being keeps a pace of their own. A bacterium divides in twenty minutes, and a banyan spreads for centuries. A life goes well when its movements keep time with others: a heart with a breath, a flower with the sunrise, a bee with the flower.
- *what is this world:* From a drifting continent to a beam of light, each speed is one movement measured by another. No clock has been found that stands outside all of them. Even the metre is now defined by how far light travels in a fixed time. The world measures itself, using its own parts.

Take away every other moving thing, and the question 'how fast is the car?' has nothing to answer it. The car needs the floor, the clock and the watcher for its speed to mean anything. The same is true of the Earth, a star and you. That is a plain fact about measuring, and it points somewhere. The things of the world are joined in such a way that one cannot be fully described without the others. Consciousness is the ground from which every movement arises, and the mover, the clock and the watcher are that one ground meeting itself. Many scientists do not accept this last step, and their view is given below.

- **Isha Upanishad, verses 4 and 5:** The One does not move, and yet is swifter than the mind. Standing still, it passes those who run. It moves and it does not move. It is far and it is near. (The sage speaks of the ground of everything, and not of a toy car. The lesson's science gives a small picture of the same shape: one child on a floor is at rest and is moving at more than a lakh kilometres per hour, and both statements are correct.)
- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika, chapter 2, 'Examination of going', about the 2nd century CE:** Nagarjuna, a Buddhist philosopher of South India, argues that we cannot find the going apart from the goer, and we cannot find a goer apart from the going. Each has meaning only through the other. (In the test, a speed could not be found in the car alone. It appeared only between the car, the track and a clock.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra of Umasvati, chapter 5 (verse 5.17 in the common numbering):** This Jain text says that the universe holds a substance whose work is to make motion possible and another whose work is to make rest possible. Later teachers compared the first to water, which lets a fish swim without pushing the fish. (Jain thinkers saw that motion and rest each need something beyond the mover. Physics does not use these two substances. It agrees that motion and rest have no meaning for one thing by itself.)
- Science (settled): Since 1967 the second has been defined by counting 9,192,631,770 vibrations of the light given off by caesium atoms. Since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in a fixed fraction of a second. Both units rest on movements.
- Science (settled): Steady motion cannot be detected from inside, so no experiment can say that one thing is truly at rest.
- Science (open): Time may exist apart from all change, or it may be only our name for change. Physics has not settled this, and it cannot yet test any link between time and consciousness.
- Other view, Isaac Newton, in the Scholium of the Principia (1687), and physicists today such as Lee Smolin: Newton held that true time flows by itself at an even rate, with no relation to anything outside it, and that clocks only copy it more or less well. On this view a thing does have a real speed, and our comparisons are attempts to find it. Smolin argues in a similar spirit that time is real and basic. Many physicists would add that needing a clock to measure speed is a fact about measuring, and tells us nothing about oneness or consciousness.
- Practice, Three clocks (3 min): Sit comfortably and switch on the fan at its slowest, or hang a key from a thread and set it swinging. Put two fingers on the side of your neck and find your pulse. Count ten beats quietly. Now notice your breath, without changing it, for three breaths. Then watch the fan or the key for ten turns or swings. Ask yourself: which of the three is the fast one? Now pick a different one to compare with, and ask again.
- Ask: If a speed needs two movements, was there any speed before there was a second thing to compare with?
- Ask: Your heartbeat is one of your clocks. If your heart beat twice as quickly, would the world seem slower, or would you seem faster?
- Ask: Is there anything about you that is not moving at all? How could you check?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* At one single instant a moving car covers no distance in no time. So in what sense does the car have a speed at that instant? (hint: Think about what you would have to measure, and how short the piece of track could get.)
- *deep:* You are sitting still, and you are also moving at more than one lakh kilometres per hour round the Sun. Are both true, is one truer, or is neither true? (hint: Ask what each number is compared with. Then ask if any one thing deserves to be the reference point.)
- *deep:* If every clock is a movement, what would 'speed' mean in a universe with only one moving thing in it? (hint: Try to describe how you would time that one thing, and with what.)
- *deep:* Why should the universe have a top speed at all? (hint: Imagine light arriving with no delay from everywhere. What would the night sky show, and could causes still come before their effects?)
- *deep:* The star you see tonight sent its light long ago. Are you seeing the star, or the star's past? (hint: Light from your friend's face takes time too, only much less. Is there anything you see as it is right now?)
- *big:* A cheetah can sprint for twenty seconds. A tortoise can live for more than a hundred years. Is faster better? (hint: Think about what each of them needs to do to eat, stay safe and raise young.)
- *big:* A banyan grows for centuries and a mayfly flies for a day. Does a slow life hold more than a quick one, or the same amount? (hint: Decide first what you would count: years, heartbeats, things noticed, or something else.)
- *big:* When a road is widened so that cars can go faster, who gains time and who loses it? (hint: Think about a child crossing, a vegetable seller with a cart, a dog, a tree at the edge, and a person in an ambulance.)
- *big:* People once thought continents stood still because nobody could see them move. What else might be moving too slowly, or too fast, for us to notice? (hint: Think about things that change over a year, over a lifetime, and in less than a blink.)

## Story: The Fly Who Had All the Time in the World
*A made-up story set in Madurai, Tamil Nadu. The people and the shop are imagined, and so is the watchmaker's blinking lamp. What is real: a person stops seeing a light flicker at about 60 flashes a second, and some flies still see flicker at about 250. The story of King Kakudmi and Revati is told in the Bhagavata Purana, book 9, chapter 3. What it is like to be a fly, a snail or a peepal tree is not known to anyone.*

It had rained on Madurai all afternoon, and the lane smelt of wet stone and jasmine. Yazhini, who was nine, sat on the step of Fathima aunty's watch-repair shop. Her brother Kathir, who was six, stood over a bunch of hill bananas with a folded newspaper. A fly sat on the top banana, washing their face with two front legs.

Whap. The fly was already in the air. Whap. Gone again. After the fourth miss Kathir stamped his foot. 'This fly is too fast!'

Fathima aunty had a watch open under her lens. She did not look up. 'Or you are too slow,' she said. 'Why not ask the fly which one it is?'

'Flies do not talk,' said Kathir.

'Their eyes do,' said Fathima aunty. She took a small lamp from a drawer. It had a knob on the side, and it blinked. 'Yazhini, turn the knob. Tell me when the blinking stops.'

Yazhini turned it. Blink, blink, blink. Faster. The blinks ran together into a shiver. Then the lamp simply shone, steady as a tube light. 'It has stopped,' she said.

'It has not,' said Fathima aunty. 'It is blinking about sixty times each second. Your eyes cannot keep the blinks apart any more, so they join them up. People who test the eyes of flies find that a fly keeps them apart up to about two hundred and fifty. In one second you get about sixty looks at the world. This fly gets four times as many.'

Yazhini thought about that. Kathir's newspaper took about a quarter of a second to come down. For her, that was hardly time to see anything. For the fly it held as many looks as a whole second held for her. 'So when Kathir swings,' she said slowly, 'the fly watches the paper coming, and coming, and still coming.'

'And strolls off,' said Fathima aunty.

Kathir did not like this. 'But the paper has a real speed. Akka showed me. You divide how far by how long.'

'You do,' said Fathima aunty, 'and with a stopwatch everybody gets the same answer. That is why people agreed on stopwatches. But nobody lives by a stopwatch, Kathir. You live by your own beat, and the fly lives by theirs. Your paper is quick by your beat and slow by the fly's. The stopwatch does not say which beat is the right one.'

On the wet wall beside the drainpipe, a snail was out for the rain. Yazhini made a chalk mark at the front of their shell and counted to sixty. The snail had gone the width of her finger. 'Then what are we to the snail? A blur?'

'Nobody knows what the world is like for a snail,' said Fathima aunty. 'We know that their eyes and nerves work at a slower beat than ours. What that is like from the inside, I cannot tell you, and neither can anyone else.'

Alagammal, the flower seller, had been waiting for her watch strap. She laughed. 'My mother told me this one. King Kakudmi and his daughter Revati went up to see Brahma. Brahma was listening to music, so they waited until the song was over. When they came back down, whole ages had passed. Everyone they knew was long gone.'

'Is that true?' asked Kathir.

'It is an old story,' said Fathima aunty. 'It is in the Bhagavata Purana. Here is a separate thing that has been measured. A clock that travels very fast ticks a tiny bit slower than one that stays at home. The story and the measurement are two different kinds of knowing. I only notice that the old tellers had already wondered if a little while in one place could be a long while in another.'

Across the lane, a peepal sapling grew out of a crack in the wall of the old bank. Yazhini had seen that same sapling last year. It had been smaller, and the crack had been thinner. All year the roots had been feeling their way into the brick, choosing the damp side, and she had never once seen them move.

'If the peepal watched this lane,' she said, 'we would be the flies. Here, gone. Here, gone.'

Kathir looked at the fly, who had come back to the banana. He looked at the snail, and at the tree. He put the newspaper down. 'So the fly is not fast,' he said, 'and I am not slow. We are just next to each other.'

He broke off the black end of a banana and left it on the step. The fly came down to it at once.

Fathima aunty clicked the back onto the watch and held it to her ear. 'Then tell me this,' she said. 'If the fly, the snail, the peepal and you each keep a different beat, is there one time that all of you share? Or is there only the one we agreed on, so that we can catch the same bus?'

**For the parent:** A stopwatch gives one answer for everybody, but every being meets the world at their own beat. 'Fast' is what each of us calls a movement that is quick compared with our own.

- Ask: Fathima aunty's last question has no agreed answer. What do you think, and what makes you think so?
- Ask: If you could take four times as many looks at the world each second, what in your day would seem different?
- Ask: The peepal's roots moved all year and nobody saw. What is moving near you right now that is too slow for you to see?

## Games
### g1 · The Hour Journey: a card game about average speed (25 min)
- **rules:** Every journey lasts exactly one hour, which is 60 minutes. Any minutes you do not fill with journey cards are spent standing still and cover no distance.; A journey card shows how many minutes the leg takes and how many kilometres it covers. A delay card adds 10 minutes of waiting and 0 kilometres.; Deal 6 cards to each player. Keep your cards hidden. Put the rest face down as the draw pile.; Each player takes 3 turns, going round the table. On your turn, either draw one card and discard one card, or play a delay card from your hand in front of another player.; A delay card in front of you takes 10 minutes from your hour. A delay card you still hold at the end is thrown away.; After everyone has had 3 turns, build your journey. Lay down any of your journey cards whose minutes, plus 10 for each delay in front of you, add up to 60 or less.; Add up your kilometres. The player with the most kilometres wins the round and takes 1 point. Tied players each take 1 point.; Say it aloud: the kilometres in a one-hour journey are the journey's average speed in kilometres per hour.; If the draw pile runs out, shuffle the discard pile and carry on. Put all 36 cards back together for each new round.; Play 3 rounds. The player with the most points wins. If there is a tie, play one more round.
- **Printable cards: 30 journey cards:** 10 minutes. Walk to the corner shop. 1 kilometre.; 10 minutes. Bullock cart on a village track. 1 kilometre.; 10 minutes. Pedal a bicycle along a lane. 2 kilometres.; 10 minutes. Autorickshaw in a busy market. 2 kilometres.; 10 minutes. Autorickshaw on a clear road. 4 kilometres.; 10 minutes. Ferry across a wide river. 3 kilometres.; 10 minutes. Scooter on a city road. 5 kilometres.; 10 minutes. Metro train between two stations. 7 kilometres.; 10 minutes. Bus on a highway. 8 kilometres.; 10 minutes. Express train on a main line. 12 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Walk along a canal path. 2 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Jog in a park. 3 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Cycle up a long slope. 3 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Cycle on a flat road. 5 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Autorickshaw in slow traffic. 5 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Bus in city traffic. 6 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Autorickshaw on a clear road. 8 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Scooter on a ring road. 10 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Bus on a highway. 16 kilometres.; 20 minutes. Express train on a main line. 22 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Walk to the temple fair. 3 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Bullock cart on a field road. 4 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Cycle to the next village. 8 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Ferry along the coast. 9 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Autorickshaw in slow traffic. 9 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Bus in city traffic. 12 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Scooter on a ring road. 15 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Metro train across the city. 20 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Bus on a highway. 24 kilometres.; 30 minutes. Express train on a main line. 33 kilometres.
- **Printable cards: 6 delay cards:** Closed railway gate. Wait 10 minutes. 0 kilometres.; A cow sits in the road. Wait 10 minutes. 0 kilometres.; A flooded lane. Wait 10 minutes. 0 kilometres.; A flat tyre. Wait 10 minutes. 0 kilometres.; Waiting for the ferry. Wait 10 minutes. 0 kilometres.; A wedding procession blocks the road. Wait 10 minutes. 0 kilometres.
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet, to copy by hand: three rows for rounds 1 to 3, with columns for each player's kilometres and points:** Round 1. Kilometres for each player, then points.; Round 2. Kilometres for each player, then points.; Round 3. Kilometres for each player, then points.; Total points for each player.
- **g1s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 36 cards from the printed sheet and shuffle them. Read the three kinds of card together: a leg of a journey, a delay and the one-hour rule.  
  *Parent says:* Every journey in this game lasts one hour. Each card is one part of the journey. Whoever goes farthest in the hour has the best average speed.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and shuffle them. Every journey lasts one hour. The farthest wins.
  - Note: Cutting is for the grown-up if the child is under 8. Keep the cards in a bowl or an envelope after the game for next time.
- **g1s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 1. Deal 6 cards each, take 3 turns each, then build and add up the journeys. Say aloud which cards you chose and why.  
  *Parent says:* You have 6 cards and each one has minutes and kilometres. Which card gives you the most kilometres for its minutes? Pick your best journey of up to 60 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your cards. Which give the most kilometres for the time? Pick your best hour.
  - Ask: A 10 minute card gives 4 kilometres. A 30 minute card gives 9 kilometres. Which is the faster leg? (good: The 10 minute card. In 30 minutes it would give 12 kilometres, which is more than 9.; It covers 24 kilometres per hour and the other covers 18.)
  - Think: Is the card with the most kilometres always the best one to use?
- **g1s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play rounds 2 and 3 the same way. Keep the score sheet up to date.  
  *Parent says:* Now you know the rules. Try to guess which cards are best before the others pick.  
  *Child mode:* Play two more rounds. Write the score after each round.
  - Ask: A delay card makes you stand still for 10 minutes. What does it do to your average speed? (good: It lowers it, because the time grows and the distance does not.; It takes away 10 minutes you could have used to cover distance.)
- **g1s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the speed of your best leg. Multiply the kilometres by 6 for a 10 minute card, by 3 for a 20 minute card, or by 2 for a 30 minute card.  
  *Parent says:* Pick the best leg you played. How many kilometres would it cover in a whole hour at that speed?  
  *Child mode:* Pick your best card. Work out its kilometres for a whole hour.
  - Ask: Why do you multiply by 6 for a 10 minute card? (good: Because six lots of 10 minutes make an hour.; Because the hour is six times as long.)
  - Think: Which card in the pile do you think has the highest speed?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Two-Metre Track: does a toy car keep one speed? (35 min)
- **question:** A toy car rolls off a ramp and along 2 metres of floor. Does the car cover the second metre in the same time as the first?
- **predict:** Before the first run, guess. Will the second metre take the same time as the first, less time, or more time? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The car, the ramp, the height of the ramp, the floor and the place where the car is let go all stay the same. The car is let go and never pushed. The only thing compared is the first metre against the second metre. The run is done five times, because a finger on a stopwatch is never exactly on time, and the middle value of the five is used.
- **record:** Fill in the table for five runs. Circle the middle value in each column. Work out three speeds in centimetres per second: for the first metre, the second metre and the whole track. Then write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Run | Time for the first metre (seconds) | Time for the second metre (seconds) | Time for the whole 2 metres (seconds)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build the ramp with one end 5 to 8 centimetres high. Draw a pencil line across the top of the ramp. Mark the Start line on the floor about one hand-length beyond the foot of the ramp, so that the car is on flat floor when timing begins. Measure 100 centimetres and 200 centimetres from the Start line along the car's path, and mark both.  
  *Parent says:* We will let the car go from the same pencil line every time. Why do you think we start timing on the flat floor and not on the ramp?  
  *Child mode:* Build the ramp. Mark a Start line on the floor just past the ramp. Mark lines at 100 and 200 centimetres.
  - Note: The car speeds up on the ramp. Starting the clock on flat floor means we time only the roll along the floor.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do three practice runs without writing anything. Check that the car crosses the 2 metre line. If the car stops before the line, add one thin book under the ramp. If the first metre takes less than 1 second, take one book away, because the times will be too short to press. If the car curves off the track, turn the ramp a little and try again. Let your child practise with the Lap button.  
  *Parent says:* Press Start when the nose of the car reaches the first line. Press Lap at the middle line and Stop at the last line. Keep your eyes level with the floor.  
  *Child mode:* Try three runs for practice. Press Start, Lap and Stop as the car's nose crosses each line.
  - Note: On most phones the list then shows two lap times. If yours shows only the total, subtract the first lap from the total to get the second metre. After this step, do not change the ramp again.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the prediction and the reason. Write both at the top of the results page.  
  *Parent says:* Will the second metre take the same time as the first, less time, or more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the second metre take the same time, less or more? Write your guess and your reason.
  - Ask: What could make the car change its speed after it has left the ramp? (good: The floor rubs on the wheels.; The air pushes against the car.; Nothing is pushing the car any more, so friction slows it.)
  - Note: Do not say who is right. The runs will answer.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do five timed runs. One person lets the car go from the pencil line without pushing. The other works the stopwatch. After each run, write the time for the first metre, the second metre and the whole track.  
  *Parent says:* We do five runs because a finger is never exactly on time. Five tries let us see past the slips.  
  *Child mode:* Do five runs. Let the car go. Do not push. Write three times after each run.
  - Note: With a ramp of about 6 centimetres on tiles, expect about 1.2 to 1.6 seconds for the first metre and 1.5 to 2.2 for the second. Your numbers will differ with your car and floor. The second metre should take longer in most runs.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* In each column, put the five times in order and circle the middle one. With the phone's calculator, divide 100 by the middle time of the first metre, then 100 by the middle time of the second metre, then 200 by the middle time of the whole track. Round each answer to a whole number and write 'centimetres per second' after it.  
  *Parent says:* Speed is distance divided by time. What do the three answers tell you? Which of them is the car's speed?  
  *Child mode:* Circle the middle time in each column. Work out 100 ÷ time for each metre and 200 ÷ time for the whole track.
  - Ask: You found three different speeds for one car. How can that be? (good: The car was slowing down all the way.; The whole-track number is an average of the fast part and the slow part.; Each number is for a different piece of the trip.)
  - Check: A car takes 1.4 seconds for the first metre and 1.8 seconds for the second. Which is true? → **The car was faster in the first metre.**
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tie the key to the thread. Hold the thread so that there are about 25 centimetres from your fingers to the key, and set the key swinging gently. One swing is there and back. Let the car go again, and count the swings between the Start line and the 2 metre line. Then hold the thread only about 6 centimetres above the key, and count again for another run. Write both counts beside the stopwatch time. Finish with the three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* The car did the same thing each time. What changed? Could you say the car's speed in 'centimetres per swing'? What can this test not tell us about the car?  
  *Child mode:* Swing a key on a 25 centimetre thread. Count the swings while the car goes 2 metres. Try again with a 6 centimetre thread. Write both counts.
  - Note: A key on 25 centimetres of thread takes about 1 second for each swing in any home, so the count is close to the stopwatch time. The short thread swings about twice as often. The test cannot tell us the car's speed at any single point, only averages over each piece. It also cannot tell us how much of the slowing comes from the floor and how much from the air.
  - Think: Whose clock gave the true speed: the phone, the long thread or the short thread?
### e2 · My Own Pace: is your ordinary walk the same speed every time? (15 min)
- **question:** If you walk the same 3 metres five times without trying to be quick or slow, do you take the same time each time?
- **predict:** Guess how much your five walks will differ: by less than half a second, by about a second, or by more. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The distance, the shoes or bare feet, the floor and the person timing stay the same. The walker starts two steps before the Start line, so that the walk is already under way when timing begins. Five ordinary walks are timed. Then five walks are timed with the walker placing heel against toe, to compare.
- **record:** Write the ten times in the table. Circle the middle time of each kind of walk. Work out both speeds as 300 ÷ time, in centimetres per second, and write them beside the toy car's speed.
- **results table:** Walk | Ordinary walk: time for 3 metres (seconds) | Heel-to-toe walk: time for 3 metres (seconds)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark two lines 3 metres apart, with at least two steps of clear floor before the first line and after the second. If your longest clear strip is shorter, use 2 metres and divide 200 by the time later.  
  *Parent says:* Guess first. How much will your five ordinary walks differ? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Mark two lines 3 metres apart. Guess how much your five walks will differ.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Time five ordinary walks. Your child starts two steps before the first line and keeps walking past the second. Start the stopwatch when the front foot crosses the first line and stop it when a foot crosses the second. Write each time.  
  *Parent says:* Do not try to be fast or slow. Walk as if you were going to fetch a glass of water.  
  *Child mode:* Walk the track five times, just as you always walk. Write each time.
  - Note: Most children take between 2 and 4 seconds for 3 metres. The five times usually fall within about half a second of each other, though no one told the legs what speed to keep.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Time five heel-to-toe walks over the same track in the same way. Write each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now put the heel of one foot against the toes of the other on every step.  
  *Child mode:* Walk five more times with your heel touching your toes. Write each time.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Circle the middle time of each set. Divide 300 by each middle time with the calculator. Compare the two walking speeds with the toy car's speed for the whole track.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? Who or what chose the speed of your ordinary walk? What can this test not tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Work out 300 ÷ time for each kind of walk. Are you faster or slower than the toy car?
  - Ask: Your five ordinary walks came out close together. What might be keeping your pace so steady? (good: My legs swing at their own beat, a bit like the key on the thread.; My body picks the speed that feels easiest.; I am not sure, and I would test it with longer or shorter steps.)
  - Note: The test shows that the pace is steady. It cannot show why. Studies of walking suggest that bodies settle on the speed that uses the least energy for each metre, but a home test cannot measure energy.
  - Think: Would your ordinary pace be the same on sand, uphill, or when you are sleepy?

## Activities
### a1 · Walk, tiptoe and hop: how far in ten claps? (15 min)
- **record:** Draw three footprints or three arrows on a paper strip, one for each way of moving. Make the longest arrow for the one that went farthest.
- **a1s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a start line, such as a strip of paper or a chalk mark. Practise the clapping. Clap once about every second, for ten claps, at a steady pace.  
  *Parent says:* I will clap ten times, slowly and steadily. You walk in your ordinary way until I stop clapping.  
  *Child mode:* Stand on the line. Walk until the claps stop.
  - Note: A steady clap is about one clap for each second. Count 'one-and, two-and' to keep time. If you are alone with the child, use a song with a steady beat.
- **a1s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to walk for ten claps and place a chappal where she or he stops. Repeat on tiptoe, and then hopping on both feet. Place a different chappal each time.  
  *Parent says:* Which one do you think will go farthest in ten claps? Why do you think so? Let us see.  
  *Child mode:* Move for ten claps three times. Put a chappal where you stop each time.
  - Ask: Which went farthest and which went the shortest? (good: Walking farthest and hopping or tiptoe the shortest, or any honest answer from the chappals.; The one with the longest gap from the line.)
- **a1s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pick the chappal for the walk. Ask your child to tiptoe and then hop to that same chappal while you clap. Count the claps each time.  
  *Parent says:* The finish is the same this time. Which way takes the most claps?  
  *Child mode:* Go to the walking chappal on tiptoe and then by hopping. Count the claps.
  - Ask: Did hopping or tiptoeing take more claps to reach the same place? (good: The slower way took more claps.; The one that moved less far in each clap.)
- **a1s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the three arrows on the paper strip together. Make the longest arrow for the movement that went farthest in ten claps.  
  *Parent says:* Draw one arrow for each way. The farthest gets the longest arrow.  
  *Child mode:* Draw an arrow for each movement. Make the longest one for the farthest.
  - Think: If you could move for 20 claps, what would happen to the arrows?
### a2 · The fair head start: a race both people can win (20 min)
- **record:** Draw the path as a line. Mark the finish, the faster walker's start and the slower walker's start. Write one sentence about what the head start shows.
- **a2s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark the start with a chappal and the finish about 10 steps away with another chappal. Both of you stand at the start. Say 'go' and walk to the finish at your ordinary pace. Do not hurry.  
  *Parent says:* Walk at your usual pace and do not try to win. We want to see who naturally arrives first.  
  *Child mode:* Both walk at your usual pace. Who arrives first?
  - Note: If the child is the faster walker, that is fine. Either way, one will arrive first by a small amount.
- **a2s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Give the person who arrived second a head start by moving them forward a few tiles. Walk again at the same ordinary paces. If one still arrives first, move the head start. Try up to four times.  
  *Parent says:* How far ahead should the slower walker start so that we arrive together? Guess, and then try it.  
  *Child mode:* The slower walker starts ahead. Move the start until you arrive together.
  - Ask: What makes the tie happen? (good: The head start makes up for the time the faster walker saves on the rest of the path.; The slower walker covers less distance in the same time.)
  - Note: If the two of you walk at the same pace, the head start is nothing. Then one of you walks faster on purpose, like a brisk walk against a slow walk.
- **a2s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When you tie, look at the path. The faster walker covered the whole path and the slower walker covered only part of it in the same time. Ask how the two distances compare.  
  *Parent says:* We both walked for the same time. Who covered more path? About how many times more?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the two distances. Who went farther in the same time?
  - Ask: If the slower walker starts half way along the path and you tie, how many times faster is the other walker? (good: Twice as fast, because she covers the whole path in the time the other covers half.; Two times as fast.)
- **a2s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap places and try walking at the speed of the other person. See if you can tie without a head start.  
  *Parent says:* Can you walk at my pace, so that the race is a tie from the same start?  
  *Child mode:* Try to walk exactly at the other person's speed. Can you tie?
  - Think: Is it easier to keep the same speed as someone else, or to keep your own speed the same each time?
### a3 · Trip notes: the speed of your own errand (25 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up comes along and holds the phone near roads. Cross roads only with the grown-up.
- **record:** Write the mode of travel, the distance, the total time, the time standing still, the average speed and the speed while moving.
- **a3s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a trip you would make today anyway, such as to a shop, the market, a school gate or a bus stop. Look up the distance on a phone map and write it down. If there is no map, walk 20 steps in the hall, measure that distance once with a tape, and use that for each step.  
  *Parent says:* Before we leave, how far do you think it is? What do you think our speed will be?  
  *Child mode:* Write how far the trip is. Guess how fast you will go.
  - Note: A child's step is about 50 centimetres, so 2,000 steps make about a kilometre. The map's walking distance is better when you have it.
- **a3s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* During the trip, write the time you leave, the time of each stop, such as a signal or a queue, and the time you arrive.  
  *Parent says:* Please write down the time each time we stop. We want to know how long we were standing still.  
  *Child mode:* Write the time at the start, at every stop and at the end.
  - Note: Keep the notebook in a pocket so the child can write at stops only.
- **a3s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At home, find the whole time and the time standing still. Divide the distance by the whole time to get the average speed. Then take away the standing still time and divide the distance by the time moving.  
  *Parent says:* Which is bigger, the speed for the whole trip or the speed while moving? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Work out two speeds. Which is bigger?
  - Ask: Why is the average speed for the whole trip lower? (good: The time standing still counts in the trip but adds no distance.; The stops take time and carry no distance.)
  - Think: If you took a different road or a different way of travelling, which of the two speeds would change most?

## Projects
### p1 · The slowest racers: a sprout and climbing water (80 min, 6 days)
- **record:** Keep a table for the water front (minutes and centimetres) and another for the sprouts (day and the length of the longest shoot in millimetres). Draw a distance and time graph for each.
- **schedule:** day 1: Soak the moong or chana in water in the evening. (10 min); day 2: Drain the seeds and wrap them in a damp cloth. Then time how fast water climbs a paper strip. (25 min); day 3: Measure the shoots, rinse them and keep them warm. (10 min); day 4: Measure the shoots again at the same time of day, and rinse. (10 min); day 5: Measure the shoots a third time, and rinse. (10 min); day 6: Draw the graphs and work out the two speeds. Then compare them. (15 min)
- **p1s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* In the evening, put 2 tablespoons of moong or chana in a steel bowl. Cover with water by about 2 finger-widths. Leave them for 8 to 10 hours.  
  *Parent says:* Seeds are alive. They will wake up when they drink. Where do you think they will grow from?  
  *Child mode:* Put the seeds in water. They need to drink all night.
  - Note: The seeds swell as they drink. In cool weather, soak for 12 hours.
  - Wait: Leave the seeds overnight
- **p1s2 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drain the seeds and wrap them in a damp cloth inside the bowl. Put the cover on and keep the bowl in a warm, dark place. Then set up the strip. Pour 2 centimetres of water into a tumbler and stir in a few drops of ink or a pinch of turmeric. Hang one end of the strip in the water so that it touches. Draw a line at the water's edge on the strip every minute for 10 minutes, with a pencil and a clock.  
  *Parent says:* Where will the water reach after one minute? After ten? Do you think it will go up at the same speed all the time?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the strip in the water. Every minute, draw a line where the water has got to. Do this for 10 minutes.
  - Ask: Does the water climb the same distance each minute? (good: No. It climbs farther in the first minutes and slows down later.; Not steady. It slows down.)
  - Note: If the strip climbs less than 2 centimetres in 10 minutes, use a thinner strip or a fresh one. If the water climbs all the way up before 10 minutes, mark every 30 seconds and say so in the record. If the strip flops, lean it against a pencil laid across the tumbler.
- **p1s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Open the cloth and look at the seeds. Measure the longest shoot with the ruler in millimetres. If there is no shoot yet, write 0. Rinse the seeds gently with water, wrap them again and keep them warm.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your seeds. Have any of them started to grow? How long is the longest bit?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the longest shoot in millimetres. Write the number. Rinse and cover the seeds.
  - Note: Do not worry if there is no shoot on day 3. In cool weather, put the bowl near a stove that is lit or on top of a fridge. Shoots can take another day. Write 0 and carry on.
- **p1s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At the same time as yesterday, measure the longest shoot again, write the number, rinse and cover the seeds.  
  *Parent says:* How much longer is the shoot than yesterday?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the longest shoot again. How much longer is it?
- **p1s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure the longest shoot a third time and rinse and cover the seeds as before.  
  *Parent says:* Is the shoot growing at the same speed each day?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the shoot one more time. Write the number.
- **p1s6 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a graph for the water with minutes across and centimetres up. Draw another for the shoot with days across and millimetres up. ### ch1 · A timer you can trust: time a toy car without finger errors (30 min)
- **problem:** Find a way to time a toy car over a 2-metre track so that two different people who time the same run get answers within one tenth of a second of each other. There is more than one good answer.
- **your turn:** Build your own timer from things at home and test it on the toy car. What did you change that made the times closer together? Do you think any timer is ever exactly right?
- **constraints:** Use only things you can find at home. A phone is allowed, but not a special timing app made for races.; The car must be let go and never pushed, and it must run the same each time.; Time at least five runs of your method and write them down.; Compare your result with the plain stopwatch method on the same car.
- **general:** Have a second person call 'now' at the start while another presses the stopwatch, and use the middle of five runs.; Count the swings of a key on a 25-centimetre thread. One swing takes about one second, so the count is the time.; Time over a longer distance, so that the finger's error is a smaller part of the whole time.
- **creative:** Film the run and count frames. A video of 30 frames each second gives a clock that never gets tired or late.; Race the toy car against a ball that you roll at a steady pace, and use the ball's known time as a clock.; Put one person at each line. Each presses a stopwatch to start when the car passes the first line and the second person presses a stopwatch to stop when it passes the second line. Both fingers are late by about the same amount, so the lateness cancels.

## Worksheets
### Distance, time and speed (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meera runs 100 metres in 20 seconds. Lakshmi runs 60 metres in 10 seconds. Who is faster?  
   Options: Meera, because she ran farther / Lakshmi / They are equally fast  
   **Answer:** Lakshmi. *Why:* Meera covers 5 metres in each second. Lakshmi covers 6. Choosing Meera compares only the distances and forgets the times.
2. (fill) A bus goes 120 kilometres in 3 hours. Its speed is ____ kilometres per hour.  
   **Answer:** ['40']. *Why:* 120 ÷ 3 = 40. The bus covers 40 kilometres in each hour.
3. (truefalse) A car that covers 10 metres in its first second must cover 20 metres in two seconds.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. That is true only if the speed never changes. A car that is speeding up covers more, and a car that is slowing down covers less.
4. (mcq) A scooter covers 20 kilometres in one hour, with stops at signals. Its needle showed 40 for a while. What was its average speed?  
   Options: 40 kilometres per hour / 20 kilometres per hour / 60 kilometres per hour  
   **Answer:** 20 kilometres per hour. *Why:* Average speed is the whole distance divided by the whole time, so 20 ÷ 1 = 20. The 40 was the speed at one moment, not the average.
5. (sort) Sort each one by what it tells you.  
   **Answer:** the number on a scooter's speedometer right now → Speed at one moment; a train's 400 kilometres divided by its 5 hours → Average speed; a cricket ball's speed just as it leaves the bowler's hand → Speed at one moment; a girl's 3 kilometres to school divided by her 36 minutes → Average speed; the speed shown on a bus's display as it passes a toll gate → Speed at one moment. *Why:* An average uses a whole trip, with its fast parts, slow parts and stops. A single reading uses a tiny piece of the trip.
6. (match) Match each speed to the mover it suits best.  
   **Answer:** about 1 millimetre per second → a garden snail; about 5 kilometres per hour → a person walking; about 60 kilometres per hour → a bus on a main road; about 5 centimetres per year → a drifting continent. *Why:* Each pair fits the size of the distance and the time. A continent needs a whole year to move a few centimetres, and a bus needs only an hour to cover many kilometres.
7. (order) Put these movers in order from slowest to fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['A garden snail', 'A child walking', 'A bicycle', 'A train on a main line']. *Why:* A snail covers about a millimetre each second. A walking child covers about 1 metre or more each second. A bicycle and a train cover more again.
8. (write) Rohan says, 'Ria ran 50 metres and I ran 40 metres, so Ria is faster.' What is missing from what he says?  
   **Answer:** The time each of them took. Faster means more distance in the same time, so 50 metres in 20 seconds could be slower than 40 metres in 10 seconds.. *Why:* A strong answer asks for both measurements and gives an example where the longer distance is the slower run.
9. (write) Why do you think the second metre of a rolling toy car often takes longer than the first? Give your idea and say how you could test it.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A good idea is that the floor and the air rub against the car and slow it down. A test is to time two pieces of the track and compare the speeds, or to roll the car on a rougher floor and see if the slowing grows.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason and a way to check it, and does not say the car 'just gets tired'.
10. (draw) Draw the track for a toy car test. Mark where the car starts, where the stopwatch starts and stops, and the distance between the lines.  
   **Answer:** A start line, a finish line a measured distance apart such as 200 centimetres, an arrow for the car's path, and the words 'start the clock' at the start line and 'stop the clock' at the finish line.. *Why:* A speed needs a measured distance and a time for exactly that distance, so the clock must start and stop at the lines.
11. (perspective) Two ways to decide who is faster.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says each way gives the same order when speeds stay steady, so neither is wrong. The same-time way suits a hopping game because nobody has to wait for the slowest finisher.. *Why:* Both are fair ways to compare. One keeps distance the same and compares times. The other keeps time the same and compares distances.
12. (truefalse) A passenger asleep in a moving bus has a speed of zero.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False unless we say what the speed is compared with. Compared with the seat it is zero. Compared with the road it is the bus's speed.
13. (mcq) Gita and Anu hop for 10 seconds with steps of the same size. Gita makes 9 hops and Anu makes 12. Who is faster?  
   Options: Gita / Anu / They are equally fast  
   **Answer:** Anu. *Why:* The time is the same, so the one who went farther is faster. Anu covered 12 hop-lengths and Gita covered 9.
14. (fill) A snail covers 3 centimetres in 10 seconds. In one minute, at the same speed, the snail covers ____ centimetres.  
   **Answer:** ['18']. *Why:* One minute is 6 lots of 10 seconds, and 6 × 3 = 18 centimetres.
15. (count) A toy car covers 2 metres in 4 seconds. How many centimetres does it cover in each second?  
   **Answer:** 50. *Why:* 2 metres is 200 centimetres, and 200 ÷ 4 = 50 centimetres per second.
16. (mcq) Which of these is a complete way to state a speed?  
   Options: 40 / 40 kilometres / 40 kilometres per hour  
   **Answer:** 40 kilometres per hour. *Why:* A speed needs a distance and a time together. '40' and '40 kilometres' leave out the time.
### Averages, graphs and different watchers (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A girl runs the first 5 kilometres of a 10 kilometres run at 10 kilometres per hour and the last 5 kilometres at 15 kilometres per hour. What is her average speed?  
   Options: 12 kilometres per hour / 12.5 kilometres per hour / 15 kilometres per hour  
   **Answer:** 12 kilometres per hour. *Why:* The first half takes 30 minutes and the second half takes 20 minutes, so 10 kilometres takes 50 minutes. 10 kilometres in 50 minutes is 12 kilometres per hour. Adding 10 and 15 and halving it forgets that she spent longer going slowly.
2. (fill) A car goes at 72 kilometres per hour. In metres per second, that is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['20']. *Why:* 72 kilometres is 72,000 metres and one hour is 3,600 seconds. 72,000 ÷ 3,600 = 20.
3. (truefalse) Two trains on parallel tracks both go 80 kilometres per hour in the same direction. A passenger in one sees the other going 80 kilometres per hour.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Compared with the passenger, the other train is not moving, because both go along together. The 80 is its speed compared with the ground.
4. (mcq) A bus goes 40 kilometres per hour. A passenger walks towards the back at 4 kilometres per hour. Compared with the road, her speed is  
   Options: 44 kilometres per hour / 36 kilometres per hour / 4 kilometres per hour  
   **Answer:** 36 kilometres per hour. *Why:* Her walking takes away from the bus's speed, since she goes the opposite way. 40 - 4 = 36. Adding is for walking towards the front.
5. (order) Put these in order from slowest to fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['A continent drifting', 'A garden snail', 'Sound in air', 'A spacecraft orbiting Earth', 'Light in empty space']. *Why:* A continent moves a few centimetres in a year. A snail moves about a millimetre in a second. Sound moves about 340 metres in a second. A spacecraft in orbit moves several kilometres in a second. Light moves about 3 lakh kilometres in a second.
6. (match) Match each speed of the same sleeping bus passenger to what it is compared with.  
   **Answer:** zero → the bus seat; about 40 kilometres per hour → the road; more than one lakh kilometres per hour → the Sun. *Why:* One passenger has three correct speeds because each one answers a different 'compared with what'.
7. (sort) Sort each mover by how its speed behaves.  
   **Answer:** the tip of a clock's second hand → Steady speed; a toy car rolling to a stop → Changing speed; a ceiling fan just after it is switched on → Changing speed; a ceiling fan on one setting for an hour → Steady speed; an autorickshaw in a market lane → Changing speed. *Why:* A steady speed means equal distances take equal times. A mover that starts, stops, speeds up or slows down does not keep one speed.
8. (write) A boat's rower pulls at 5 kilometres per hour in still water. The river flows at 3 kilometres per hour. Explain why her speed is different compared with the bank and compared with the water.  
   **Answer:** Compared with the water she goes 5. Compared with the bank it depends on the direction. Going with the river she goes 8, and against it she goes 2. The water itself moves compared with the bank.. *Why:* A strong answer names both reference points and shows the adding or taking away for each direction.
9. (write) How could two friends measure the speed of sound across a field with a stopwatch and a clapper made of two flat sticks? Say what they would measure and what could go wrong.  
   **Answer:** One friend stands a measured distance away, such as 200 metres, and claps the sticks. The other starts the stopwatch when she sees the clap and stops it when she hears it. Speed is distance divided by time. The time is only about half a second, so a late finger spoils it. They should repeat it many times and use the middle value, or use a longer distance.. *Why:* A strong answer includes a measured distance, a time, the sum, and the problem that light arrives almost at once while the finger is slow.
10. (draw) Draw a graph with time across and distance up for a bus that moves steadily, stops for 10 minutes at a dhaba, then moves steadily again.  
   **Answer:** A sloping straight line, then a flat line for the stop, then another sloping line. The two sloping parts can have different steepness.. *Why:* On a distance and time graph, steepness shows speed. A flat line means the distance is not changing, so the speed is zero.
11. (perspective) A driver and a police officer look at the same journey.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says each one answers a different question. The average answers 'when will I get there' and the moment answers 'how risky is this place now'. Neither makes the other useless.. *Why:* An average hides the fast and slow parts of a trip, and a single reading hides the rest of the trip. Which one is the right one depends on the question.
12. (mcq) Car A covers 30 metres in 5 seconds. Car B covers 42 metres in 7 seconds. What is true?  
   Options: Car A is faster, it took less time / Car B is faster, it went farther / They go at the same speed  
   **Answer:** They go at the same speed. *Why:* Both go 6 metres in each second. Looking only at the time or only at the distance gives a wrong answer.
13. (count) A cyclist goes 18 kilometres in 90 minutes. How many kilometres per hour is that?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 90 minutes is 1.5 hours, and 18 ÷ 1.5 = 12 kilometres per hour.
14. (truefalse) If you run fast enough beside a beam of light, you can catch up with it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Everyone who measures a beam of light finds the same speed, however fast they themselves are moving. This is different from the bus and the walking passenger, and nobody has found a way round it.
15. (fill) A continent drifts 5 centimetres each year. In 2 crore years it moves ____ kilometres.  
   **Answer:** ['1000']. *Why:* 2 crore is 20,000,000. 5 × 20,000,000 = 100,000,000 centimetres, which is 1,000 kilometres.
16. (mcq) A radio signal, which travels at the speed of light, takes 20 minutes to reach Earth from a spacecraft. About how far away is the spacecraft?  
   Options: 36 crore kilometres / 3.6 lakh kilometres / 3,600 kilometres  
   **Answer:** 36 crore kilometres. *Why:* 20 minutes is 1,200 seconds. Light covers 3 lakh kilometres in each second, so 3 lakh × 1,200 = 36 crore kilometres.
### Faulty sums and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A bus covers 60 kilometres at 30 kilometres per hour and then 60 kilometres at 60 kilometres per hour. A friend says its average speed is 45. What is right?  
   Options: 45, the middle of 30 and 60 / 40, because 120 kilometres took 3 hours / 30, because the slow part rules  
   **Answer:** 40, because 120 kilometres took 3 hours. *Why:* The first 60 kilometres takes 2 hours and the second takes 1 hour. 120 ÷ 3 = 40. The bus spent longer going slowly, so the average is closer to 30 than to 60.
2. (truefalse) You can find a car's exact speed at one instant by dividing the distance by the time for that instant.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. At one instant no time has passed and no distance is covered, so the sum has nothing to work with. We get close by timing shorter and shorter pieces, and the answer settles towards one number.
3. (fill) A toy car takes 1.5 seconds for the first metre and 2.5 seconds for the second metre. Its average speed over both metres is ____ centimetres per second.  
   **Answer:** ['50']. *Why:* 200 centimetres in 4 seconds is 50 centimetres per second.
4. (mcq) A boat goes 12 kilometres per hour in still water. The river flows at 4 kilometres per hour. How fast does the boat go down the river, compared with the bank?  
   Options: 8 kilometres per hour / 12 kilometres per hour / 16 kilometres per hour  
   **Answer:** 16 kilometres per hour. *Why:* Going with the flow, the two speeds add, so 12 + 4 = 16. Going up the river would give 12 - 4 = 8.
5. (write) A friend says slow things do not matter. Give two slow movers, one from the ground and one from a living being, and say why their slowness matters.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A drifting continent builds mountains such as the Himalayas over millions of years because it moves steadily. A root or a sprout grows a few millimetres each day, and their slowness lets them keep adjusting to light, water and soil.. *Why:* A strong answer names the mover, says how slow it is, and says what its slowness makes possible.
6. (order) Put these in order of the time each takes to cover 1 kilometre, from least time to most time.  
   **Answer:** ['An autorickshaw at 30 kilometres per hour', 'A bicycle at 15 kilometres per hour', 'A runner at 10 kilometres per hour', 'A walker at 5 kilometres per hour']. *Why:* They take 2, 4, 6 and 12 minutes. The faster the mover, the less time each kilometre needs.
7. (match) Match each speed with the same speed in other words.  
   **Answer:** 18 kilometres per hour → 5 metres per second; 7.2 kilometres per hour → 2 metres per second; 108 kilometres per hour → 30 metres per second; 3.6 kilometres per hour → 1 metre per second. *Why:* One metre per second is 3.6 kilometres per hour, because 1,000 metres make a kilometre and 3,600 seconds make an hour.
8. (mcq) You see a firework flash and hear the bang 9 seconds later. The firework is 3 kilometres away. About how fast did the sound travel?  
   Options: 3 metres per second / 333 metres per second / 3 lakh kilometres per second  
   **Answer:** 333 metres per second. *Why:* 3,000 metres ÷ 9 seconds is about 333 metres per second. The light arrived almost at once, so the 9 seconds belong to the sound.
9. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Kilometres per hour, centimetres per year, litres per minute, metres per second.  
   **Answer:** Litres per minute, because it measures how much water or air flows and not how far something moves. The other three are distance in some time.. *Why:* A speed always has a distance on top and a time below. Litres measure an amount, not a distance.
10. (perspective) A cheetah can sprint at about 100 kilometres per hour. A tiny beetle moves far slower. Who is faster?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says each way answers a different question and no one way is the only fair one. Top speed ignores size, body lengths ignore how far an animal needs to go, and the third ignores that a comparison is wanted.. *Why:* Which animal is 'faster' depends on what you count. All three ways are real, and scientists use each one for different questions.
11. (write) Predict how a steeper ramp changes the time a toy car takes over 2 metres of floor. Say how you would test your idea fairly.  
   **Answer:** A steeper ramp lets the car start the floor with more speed, so the time should be shorter. A fair test changes only the ramp height, keeps the same car, floor and release point, and repeats each run about five times and uses the middle value.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a guess with a reason and keeps everything but the ramp the same.
12. (draw) Draw a distance and time graph for a car that keeps speeding up. Say how the steepness of the line shows this.  
   **Answer:** A line that starts flat and curves upwards, getting steeper. The steeper the line, the more distance in each second, so the speed grows.. *Why:* The steepness of a distance and time graph is the speed. A line that bends steeper means the speed is growing.
13. (count) The Moon is about 3.8 lakh kilometres away. Light covers 3 lakh kilometres in each second. About how many seconds does moonlight take to reach us? Give one decimal place.  
   **Answer:** 1.3. *Why:* 380,000 ÷ 300,000 is about 1.3 seconds. When you look at the Moon, you see it as it was just over a second ago.
14. (mcq) A train going 100 kilometres per hour passes another going 60 kilometres per hour on the next track, in the opposite direction. How fast does one train's passenger see the other going?  
   Options: 40 kilometres per hour / 100 kilometres per hour / 160 kilometres per hour  
   **Answer:** 160 kilometres per hour. *Why:* Going towards each other, the speeds add, so 100 + 60 = 160. The 40 would be right for trains going the same way.
15. (truefalse) A snail at 1 millimetre per second moves more than 100,000 times faster than a continent drifting at 5 centimetres each year.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A year has about 31 million seconds, so a snail covers about 31 million millimetres, which is 3 million centimetres. 3 million ÷ 5 is about 600,000 times.
16. (write) Write one question about speed that nobody can answer yet. Say how someone might begin to find out.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A good one is 'Why does the universe have a top speed?' A way to begin is to test, with better and better experiments, whether anything carrying a message ever goes faster than light, and to look for reasons in the theories that predict the limit.. *Why:* A strong answer is a real open question and suggests a test or an observation, not a guess dressed as a fact.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Car A covers 3 metres in 2 seconds. Car B covers 5 metres in 4 seconds. Which is faster?  
   Options: Car A / Car B, because it went farther / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Car A. *Why:* Car A covers 1.5 metres in each second and Car B covers 1.25. If you chose Car B, you compared only the distances. Divide each distance by its time before you compare.
2. (mcq) A cyclist rides 30 kilometres and a walker walks 6 kilometres. Is the cyclist surely faster?  
   Options: Yes, she covered more distance / No, we need each time too / Yes, a bicycle beats walking  
   **Answer:** No, we need each time too. *Why:* Speed needs distance and time together. If the walker took 1 hour and the cyclist took 10, the walker was faster. If you chose yes, ask for both numbers first.
3. (mcq) A toy car covers 150 centimetres in 3 seconds. Which working finds its speed?  
   Options: 3 ÷ 150 / 150 × 3 / 150 ÷ 3, so 50 centimetres per second  
   **Answer:** 150 ÷ 3, so 50 centimetres per second. *Why:* Speed is distance divided by time. 3 ÷ 150 gives seconds for each centimetre, which is the upside-down question. Multiplying gives nothing useful.
4. (mcq) You want to test if a heavier toy car rolls faster down a ramp. Which test is fair?  
   Options: Use a steeper ramp for the heavy car / Same ramp, add weight, repeat the run / Run each car once and compare  
   **Answer:** Same ramp, add weight, repeat the run. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing, here the weight, and repeats the run. A steeper ramp changes two things. A single run can be spoiled by a late finger on the stopwatch.
5. (mcq) You time a car five times and get 3.1, 3.0, 3.4, 3.1 and 3.2 seconds. Why use the middle value?  
   Options: One run is as good as five / A late or early finger spoils some runs / The car speeds up on every run  
   **Answer:** A late or early finger spoils some runs. *Why:* A finger on a stopwatch is never exactly on time, so single runs are a little off. The middle value is not pulled away by one odd run.
6. (mcq) A bus covers 120 kilometres in 3 hours. Its speedometer never showed exactly 40. Is that possible?  
   Options: No, the average must appear on the speedometer / Yes, it was faster and slower than 40 at times / Only if the bus stopped  
   **Answer:** Yes, it was faster and slower than 40 at times. *Why:* The average is the whole distance divided by the whole time. A bus can be faster than 40 for some of the trip and slower for the rest, so the needle may never rest on 40.
7. (mcq) Why does timing a very short piece of track give a better speed at one moment?  
   Options: Short pieces give higher speeds / The speed has little time to change inside it / A stopwatch works better on short times  
   **Answer:** The speed has little time to change inside it. *Why:* Over a short piece, the speed hardly changes, so the average over that piece is close to the speed at that moment. A stopwatch is actually worse on very short times.
8. (mcq) On a graph with time across and distance up, a line rises steadily, goes flat, then rises more steeply. What happened?  
   Options: Steady, stopped, then faster / Fast, stopped, then slower / Steady, going back, then slower  
   **Answer:** Steady, stopped, then faster. *Why:* The steepness shows the speed. Flat means the distance did not change, so the mover stopped. A steeper line afterwards means a higher speed.
9. (mcq) A girl sleeps in a moving train. Which is true?  
   Options: Zero compared with everything / Zero compared with the seat, large compared with the ground / She has no speed while asleep  
   **Answer:** Zero compared with the seat, large compared with the ground. *Why:* A speed always needs 'compared with what'. She is still compared with the seat, and she is moving compared with the ground. Being asleep changes nothing about the numbers.
10. (mcq) A cyclist rides beside a bus and keeps exactly the same speed. Compared with the bus, her speed is  
   Options: Double the bus's speed / The same as the bus's speed / Zero  
   **Answer:** Zero. *Why:* Compared with the bus, she does not get nearer or farther, so her speed is zero. The bus's speed is her speed compared with the road, and that is a different question.
11. (mcq) A snail, a spacecraft and a drifting continent all have speeds. Why can we compare them?  
   Options: Each speed is distance divided by time / Only things we can see have real speeds / Speed means something different for each  
   **Answer:** Each speed is distance divided by time. *Why:* The sum is the same for all of them. Only the size of the distance and the time changes, which is why we use millimetres per second for one and kilometres per second for another.
12. (mcq) How could anyone show that a continent moves 5 centimetres each year?  
   Options: Film it for an hour / Measure one spot for years with satellites / Measure it once with a long tape  
   **Answer:** Measure one spot for years with satellites. *Why:* A speed that small needs a long time to build up a distance we can measure. A film of one hour would show nothing, and one measurement gives no change to divide by a time.
13. (mcq) A spaceship moving at half the speed of light shines a torch forwards. Someone at rest measures the beam. What do they find?  
   Options: One and a half times light's speed / Light's usual speed / Half of light's speed  
   **Answer:** Light's usual speed. *Why:* Everyone who measures light in empty space finds the same speed. That is different from a passenger walking in a bus, where speeds add. Scientists tested this many times and it holds.
14. (mcq) Which question about speed has no agreed answer yet?  
   Options: How fast sound travels in air / Why the universe has a top speed at all / How far a car goes in an hour at 30 kilometres per hour  
   **Answer:** Why the universe has a top speed at all. *Why:* The other two can be measured or worked out with a sum. Nobody has an agreed reason why light's speed is the limit, so the question is still open.

## Applications
- Railway timetables are built on average speeds. Find one train on a timetable at a station or online, and divide the distance by the hours. The answer is lower than the train's top speed, because of the stops.
- Satellite navigation, including India's NavIC, works by timing radio signals that travel at the speed of light. The clocks on the satellites run at a slightly different rate from clocks on the ground, and the system corrects for this, or your position on the map would drift by kilometres in a day.
- The India Meteorological Department tracks how fast a cyclone in the Bay of Bengal or the Arabian Sea is moving towards the coast. Distance divided by that speed tells fishing families and coastal towns how many hours they have.
- Speed guns used at cricket matches and by traffic police bounce radio waves off a moving ball or bus. The change in the returning waves gives the speed at that moment, not an average.
- Earthquake warnings use a race between two speeds. Shaking travels through rock at a few kilometres each second, and a radio message travels almost a lakh times faster. A sensor near the earthquake can warn a city some seconds before the shaking arrives.
- Doctors measure how fast signals travel along a nerve in an arm or a leg. A signal that is slower than usual helps them find where a nerve is pinched or hurt.
- A rocket from Sriharikota must give a satellite a sideways speed of nearly 8 kilometres each second. At a lower speed the satellite falls back, and that speed decides how much fuel the rocket carries.

## Spotted around us
- On a scooter or in an autorickshaw, watch the speed needle or the numbers for one minute. Notice that the reading keeps changing, though the driver would say 'I was going at about thirty'.
- In the monsoon, when you see lightning, count the seconds until the thunder. Notice that the count gets shorter as the storm comes nearer. Every 3 seconds is about 1 kilometre.
- At a railway station, look at the train beside yours when one of them begins to move. Notice the moment when you cannot tell which train is moving. Then look at the platform to find out.
- Look up at a ceiling fan on its slowest setting. The whole blade goes round once in the same time. Notice that the tip travels a big circle while the part near the middle travels a small one, so the tip is much faster.
- On a phone map, look up the way to a relative's home. Notice that the map gives a time as well as a distance, and that the time changes with the hour of the day. Divide one by the other to find the average speed that the map expects.
- Mark the base of one fingernail with a dot of pen or mehendi, and look again after two weeks. Notice how far the dot has moved up. The land under your feet is moving north-east only a little faster than that.

## Open questions
- **Why does the speed of light have the value it has?** Physicists can measure it and use it, but no accepted theory explains the number or why there is a limit. Some suspect that the answer lies in what space and time are made of, which is not known.
- **What makes the outer stars of galaxies go round so fast?** Most astronomers think an unseen kind of matter, called dark matter, adds extra pull. Decades of searching have not caught a particle of it. A smaller group thinks the law of gravity changes over great distances. Neither side has settled it.
- **Is time a basic part of the world, or does it come from something deeper?** Speed needs time. Some physicists, such as Carlo Rovelli, argue that time is not basic and appears only when we describe many things changing together. Others, such as Lee Smolin, argue that time is the most real thing there is. No experiment can yet decide.
- **When did the Earth's plates begin to move?** Estimates range from more than 4 billion years ago to less than 1 billion. The oldest rocks have been melted and remade so many times that the clues are faint, and geologists read them differently.

## Questions to ask kids
- You walked to the shop today. About how long did it take, and about how far is it? So about how fast did you go?
- When you sit in a bus, are you moving or not moving? What would you say if someone asked 'compared with what?'
- Two movers cover the same distance, and one takes half the time. How many times faster is that one? How do you know?
- What is the slowest thing you have watched today, and what is the fastest? How would you compare them?
- A speedometer says 30, but the trip took longer than you thought. What could have happened during the trip?
- If you could make one thing faster and one thing slower in your day, which would you choose? Who else would it change things for?
- When you see lightning and then hear thunder, what does the gap in time tell you?
- What would make a very short piece of a journey tell you more about the speed at that moment?

## Connected maths
- In the toy car test, a car takes 2 seconds for the first metre and 3 seconds for the second metre. Find its speed in centimetres per second for each metre and for the whole 2 metres. Then say why the whole-track speed is not exactly in the middle of the other two. **First metre 50, second metre about 33, whole track 40 centimetres per second. The middle of 50 and 33 is about 42, but the car spent longer going slowly.**. First metre: 100 ÷ 2 = 50. Second metre: 100 ÷ 3 = 33.3. Whole track: 200 ÷ 5 = 40. The car spent 3 seconds at the slower speed and only 2 seconds at the faster, so the average is pulled towards the slower speed. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A child walks at 1.5 metres per second. What is that in kilometres per hour? Show how the numbers 1,000 and 3,600 come in. **5.4 kilometres per hour.**. In one hour she walks 1.5 × 3,600 = 5,400 metres. 5,400 metres is 5.4 kilometres. The 3,600 is the seconds in an hour and the 1,000 is the metres in a kilometre. So one metre per second is 3.6 kilometres per hour. (queues `math.time.L4`)
- A bus makes a trip of 180 kilometres in 4 hours, including a 30 minute tea stop. Find its average speed for the whole trip, and its speed while it was moving. Why are they different? **Average 45 kilometres per hour. Moving speed about 51 kilometres per hour.**. Whole trip: 180 ÷ 4 = 45. Moving time is 4 hours minus half an hour, which is 3.5 hours. 180 ÷ 3.5 is about 51.4. The stop adds time but no distance, so it pulls the average down. (queues `math.time.L4`)
- A girl rides a bicycle and notes her distance every 10 minutes. After 10 minutes: 5 kilometres. After 20 minutes: 10 kilometres. After 30 minutes: 10 kilometres. After 40 minutes: 18 kilometres. Find her speed in kilometres per hour in each 10 or 20 minute stretch. Which stretch would be the steepest on a graph? **First 20 minutes: 30 kilometres per hour. Next 10 minutes: 0. Last 10 minutes: 48 kilometres per hour. The last stretch is the steepest.**. First 20 minutes: 10 kilometres in a third of an hour is 30 kilometres per hour. Next 10 minutes: the distance did not change, so 0. Last 10 minutes: 8 kilometres in one sixth of an hour is 48 kilometres per hour. The steepest line is the one with the highest speed. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- India is about 3,200 kilometres long from north to south. How long would light take to cross it, at 3 lakh kilometres per second? How long would a bus at 50 kilometres per hour take? How many times longer is the bus's time? **Light about one hundredth of a second. The bus 64 hours. The bus takes about 22 crore times as long.**. Light: 3,200 ÷ 300,000 is about 0.0107 seconds. Bus: 3,200 ÷ 50 = 64 hours, which is 64 × 3,600 = 230,400 seconds. 230,400 ÷ 0.0107 is about 21.6 crore. A whole country is a tiny trip for light. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A toy car is 10 centimetres long and goes 50 centimetres per second. A bus is 10 metres long and goes 10 metres per second. Which one covers more of its own length each second? How many times more? **The toy car. It covers 5 of its own lengths each second. The bus covers 1. So the toy car covers 5 times as many.**. Toy car: 50 ÷ 10 = 5 lengths each second. Bus: 10 ÷ 10 = 1 length each second. 5 ÷ 1 = 5. The bus is the faster mover in metres per second, and the toy car is faster in lengths per second. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)

## Warm-up
- A bus goes 60 kilometres in 2 hours. What is its speed? → 30 kilometres per hour. 60 ÷ 2 = 30.
- Which two measurements do you need to find a speed? → A distance and the time it took.
- What does the word 'per' mean in 'metres per second'? → It means 'in each'. So metres per second means metres in each second.
- True or false: a speedometer shows the average speed of the whole trip. → False. It shows the speed at that moment.
- You sit in a moving bus. Name something your speed is zero compared with. → The bus seat, or anything else that moves along with the bus.
- A girl walks 2 kilometres in 30 minutes. How far does she walk in one hour at the same pace? → 4 kilometres. One hour is two lots of 30 minutes.
- Which is faster, 5 metres per second or 15 kilometres per hour? → 5 metres per second. It is 18 kilometres per hour.
- Light takes about 1 second to cover 3 lakh kilometres. About how long for 6 lakh kilometres? → About 2 seconds.
