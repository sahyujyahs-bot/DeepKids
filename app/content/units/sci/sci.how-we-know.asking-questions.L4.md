# Two ideas, one question: how to tell explanations apart
`sci.how-we-know.asking-questions.L4`

**Summary.** A steel tumbler of ice water is wet on the outside. One friend says the water leaked through the steel. Another says it came from the air. Both friends saw exactly the same drops. In this unit your child learns to split what they see from what they think it means. They list the rival ideas, and ask the one question that the ideas answer differently. They test the tumbler with coloured water, search for a pattern in arm spans and heights, and meet Jocelyn Bell, who used the same kind of question to find a signal from space.

**Intro.** *Fill a steel tumbler with ice water and wait ten minutes. The outside is wet. Nobody poured water there. Where did it come from?* Ask three people and you may hear three answers. One says it leaked through the steel. One says it came from the ice. One says it came out of the air. All three looked at the same wet tumbler. They saw the same drops and told three different stories. In this unit you learn how to tell stories apart. You ask what each story would predict, and then you look for the one result that the stories disagree about.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can tell what I saw (an observation) from what I think it means (an inference).
1. I can write down two or more rival explanations for something I noticed.
2. I can ask a deciding question, one that two explanations would answer differently.
3. I can write a prediction as an if-then sentence for each explanation, and say what result would show an idea wrong.
4. I can explain why drops form on the outside of a cold tumbler, and how I tested it.
5. I can look for a pattern by measuring many people or things, and say what a pattern does and does not show.
6. I can choose a way of finding out that suits my question: a fair test, a pattern search, a long watch, or looking it up.
7. I can say what to do when a result surprises me.

## Terms
- **Observation**: What you notice with your senses or with a tool, without adding why. Anyone standing beside you can check it. *e.g.* The outside of the steel tumbler is covered in small clear drops.
- **Inference**: A guess or conclusion you work out from what you noticed. It goes beyond what you saw, so it can be wrong. *e.g.* The water leaked through the steel. Nobody saw water crossing the steel, so this is an inference.
- **Explanation**: A statement of why or how something happens. *e.g.* The drops come from water in the air that turned to liquid on the cold steel.
- **Rival explanations**: Two or more explanations for the same thing you saw. They cannot all be right, so you have to find a way to choose between them. *e.g.* Leaked through the steel and came out of the air are rival explanations for the drops.
- **Hypothesis**: The scientists' word for an explanation that you can test. A hypothesis has to be something that could turn out wrong. *e.g.* The drops come from the air is a hypothesis. We can colour the water inside and see whether the drops are coloured.
- **Prediction**: What you expect to see if an idea is true. A good prediction is written as an if-then sentence, before the test. *e.g.* If the water leaked through the steel, then the drops on the outside will be the colour of the water inside.
- **Evidence**: What you saw or measured that counts for or against an idea. Evidence is not the same as the idea. *e.g.* The drops were clear while the water inside was yellow. That is evidence against 'it leaked through'.
- **Deciding question**: A question that two explanations answer differently. When you get the answer, one explanation is in trouble. *e.g.* Is the water on the outside the same colour as the water inside? Leaking says yes, and the air idea says no.
- **Water vapour**: Water as an invisible gas, mixed into the air. There is some in the air of every room, even when it feels dry. *e.g.* Wet clothes on a line dry because the water leaves them and goes into the air as vapour.
- **Condensation**: Water vapour turning back into tiny drops of liquid water when it touches something cold. *e.g.* The mist on a mirror after a hot shower, and the drops on a cold tumbler, are condensation.
- **Pattern**: Something that repeats or goes together in the same way in many cases. *e.g.* In most families, the taller people also have the longer arm spans.
- **Arm span**: The distance from the tip of one middle finger to the tip of the other, when your arms are stretched out wide. *e.g.* A girl who is 130 centimetres tall may have an arm span of about 130 centimetres too.
- **Measurement error**: The small wobble in any measurement, from where you hold the tape, how you read it, or how still the person stands. It is not the same as a mistake. *e.g.* Two people measure the same child and get 131 and 133 centimetres. The 2 centimetres is measurement error.
- **Sample**: The group of people or things you measure, in order to learn about a much bigger group. *e.g.* Measuring eight people from your street is a sample of everyone in your city.
- **Unexpected result**: A result that does not match what your idea predicted. It is information. It is not a failure. *e.g.* You thought the warm tumbler would sweat, but it stayed dry. That tells you something about warm air and cold steel.
- **Signal**: A pattern in measurements that carries real information, such as a regular beat. *e.g.* A radio hiss that comes back at the same time every evening is a signal.
- **Noise**: Random wobble in measurements that carries no message. *e.g.* The crackle on a radio when a mixer grinder is switched on nearby is noise.
- **Interference**: Unwanted noise from things nearby, which gets mixed into a measurement. *e.g.* A phone buzzing near a speaker is interference.
- **Pulsar**: A star that has collapsed into a tiny, spinning ball and sends out a beam that sweeps past the Earth like a lighthouse, so we get a pulse each turn. *e.g.* The first pulsar was found by Jocelyn Bell in 1967 as a regular blip on a paper chart.

## Explanations
### What I saw and what I think it means · *defined*
**Definition:** An observation is what you can see, hear, touch, smell or measure. An inference is what you work out from it. They are different, and we keep them apart.

Look at a cold tumbler with small clear drops on the outside. 'There are small clear drops on the outside' is an observation. Anyone standing beside you can check it. 'The water leaked through the steel' is an inference. It sounds like something you saw, but nobody saw water cross the steel. Words like 'because', 'leaked', 'made' and 'wants' are clues that an inference has slipped in. Inferences are not bad. Science is full of them. The trouble starts when we forget we made one. Then we stop asking whether it is right. A good habit is to write two columns, 'I saw' and 'I think', and move each sentence into the right one.
- e.g. A crow turns their head towards our plate. That is an observation. 'The crow is hungry' is an inference.
- e.g. A friend is not smiling. That is an observation. 'She is angry with me' is an inference. She may only be tired.
- e.g. The fan blades turn slowly. That is an observation. 'The fan is about to break' is an inference.
### Rival explanations: many stories for one sight · *defined*
**Definition:** Rival explanations are different explanations for the same observation. Usually at most one of them is right.

One sight can have many stories. A wet patch appears on the wall under the bathroom. Is it a leaking pipe? Is it rain coming through the terrace? Is it damp from the cold water pipe? Each story fits the wet patch. So the wet patch alone cannot choose between them. This is why we write the rival explanations down side by side before we test anything. It stops us falling in love with the first story we thought of. It also reminds us to look for a fourth story, because the right one is sometimes the one nobody has said yet.
- e.g. Drops on a cold tumbler: the water leaked through, it came out of the air, or it was my own wet hand.
- e.g. A dosa batter rises overnight: it was the warm kitchen, the time, or the microbes already in the batter.
- e.g. A crow keeps visiting the balcony: for the water pot, for the dry rice, or because someone feeds them.
### A deciding question tells two ideas apart · *defined*
**Definition:** A deciding question is a question that two explanations answer differently. Whichever answer you get, at least one explanation is in trouble.

Watching more of the same thing often cannot choose between two ideas, because both ideas fit. You need a question where the ideas disagree. For the tumbler, ask: what colour will the drops be if the water inside is coloured? If the water leaked through the steel, the drops will be coloured. If the water came from the air, the drops will be clear. The ideas disagree, so the colour decides. To find a deciding question, take each idea in turn and ask: if this were true, what else would I see, that the other idea would not give me?
- e.g. Bell or dusk: do the pigeons still leave on an evening when the bell does not ring?
- e.g. Leak or air: is the water on the outside the same colour as the water inside?
- e.g. Wet patch from a pipe or from rain: does the patch grow on a dry day?
### If-then predictions · *defined*
**Definition:** A prediction written as 'If this idea is true, then I will see this' is a prediction you can check. It names the idea and the result together.

Writing the prediction before the test matters. After a result arrives, it is easy to believe we expected it all along. The if-then shape keeps us honest, because it ties each result to one idea. Write one sentence for each idea, so that two ideas give two sentences. Make them as clear as you can, with a colour, a number or a time, so that nobody can argue afterwards about what it meant. If both sentences would give the same result, the test cannot choose between them. Go back and find a better deciding question.
- e.g. If the water leaked through the steel, then the drops will be yellow when the water inside is yellow.
- e.g. If the pigeons leave because of the bell, then they will stay when the bell does not ring.
- e.g. If arm span is the same as height, then the difference between the two will be close to zero for everyone.
### An idea you can test must be able to fail · *defined*
**Definition:** A testable idea says something that could turn out wrong. If no result could ever show an idea wrong, nobody can use a test to learn about it.

Suppose someone says: the drops are made by a tiny invisible helper who hides whenever we look. Whatever we see, the helper explains it. If the drops are clear, the helper made them clear. If they are yellow, the helper made them yellow. An idea that fits every possible result tells us nothing about this tumbler. It might even be true, but we cannot test it. A good scientific idea sticks its neck out. It says: if I am right, you will see this. If you do not see it, I am wrong. That is what makes it useful, because it can be checked by anyone.
- e.g. 'The drops come from the air' sticks its neck out. A coloured-water test could show it wrong.
- e.g. 'Pigeons leave because of the bell' sticks its neck out. A bell-free evening could show it wrong.
- e.g. 'Mangoes taste best because that is how mangoes are' cannot be tested, because no result could ever show it wrong.
### Five ways of finding out · *defined*
**Definition:** Different questions need different ways of finding out. The main ways are a fair test, a pattern search, a long watch, looking it up or asking, and a model.

A fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same. Use it for 'what happens if I change this?' A pattern search measures many people or things and looks for what goes together. Use it for 'do taller people have longer arms?' A long watch looks at the same thing again and again over time. Use it for 'when does the streetlight come on?' Looking it up or asking an expert answers questions you cannot test at home, such as how far away the Sun is. A model is a drawing, a toy or a computer program that copies the real thing, so you can try ideas safely. Many investigations use more than one way.
- e.g. Does more salt make a potato float? A fair test, changing the amount of salt.
- e.g. Do taller people have longer feet? A pattern search across many people.
- e.g. How far away is the Moon? Look it up, because we cannot test it at home.
### Why drops form on the outside of a cold tumbler · *defined*
**Definition:** The air around us holds invisible water called water vapour. When air touches something cold, some of its vapour turns into tiny drops of liquid water. This is called condensation.

Air is a mix of gases, and one of them is water vapour. Warmer air can hold more vapour than colder air. When air touches the cold steel, the air right next to the steel cools. It can no longer hold all its vapour as a gas. Some of the vapour turns into liquid and lands on the steel as tiny drops. The drops grow and join. The water does not come from inside the tumbler. The steel is a closed wall, and coloured water inside does not cross it. A warm tumbler does not sweat, because the air next to it stays warm. On a very dry day the drops come slowly, because there is less vapour to collect.
- e.g. A cold bottle taken from the fridge is wet within minutes, though its cap is shut tight.
- e.g. Mist forms on a bathroom mirror after a hot shower, because the warm moist air touches the cooler glass.
- e.g. On a very dry winter day a cold tumbler may stay almost dry for a long time, because there is little vapour to collect.
### Looking for a pattern in many cases · *defined*
**Definition:** A pattern search measures the same thing in many people or things and looks for what goes together. The more cases you measure, the steadier the pattern.

Some questions cannot be answered by changing one thing. You cannot change how tall a person is to see what happens to their arms. So you measure many people and look. Are the taller people the ones with the longer arm spans? Draw the numbers in a table or on a graph and look for a pattern. One person tells you very little, because every person is a little different. Six or ten people tell you more, and a hundred tell you more again. Every measurement also wobbles a little, so a good pattern is one that stays visible through the wobble. A pattern tells you what goes together. It does not tell you what causes what.
- e.g. Measure the arm span and height of eight people and see whether the two numbers are close.
- e.g. Count the petals on twenty flowers of the same kind and see whether the number is nearly always the same.
- e.g. Note the time each school bus arrives on twenty mornings and look for the usual time and the wobble.
### A pattern is not the same as a cause · *defined*
**Definition:** When two things go together, one may cause the other, or a third thing may cause both, or it may be chance. A pattern alone cannot tell you which.

Taller people have longer arms. Does being tall make your arms long? Partly, because the same growing body makes both. Something else goes along with height too: older children are taller, and they also have bigger shoes. Bigger shoes do not make children taller. Age makes both bigger. When we find a pattern, we should say 'these go together' and then ask why. To find out whether one thing causes another, we need a fair test, where we change one thing ourselves. If we cannot, we keep looking for other things that could explain the pattern.
- e.g. Shoe size and reading ability go together in primary school, because both grow with age.
- e.g. Ice cream sales and sunburn cases go up together, because both happen on hot sunny days.
- e.g. Tall people have long arms because one growing body makes both, not because one causes the other.
### When the result is a surprise · *defined*
**Definition:** A surprising result is a result that does not match your prediction. Check it, repeat it, write it down as it is, and then ask what it could mean.

Sometimes a test gives a result that nobody expected. The first thing to do is not to throw it away. First check that you did the test the way you planned, and read the measurement again. Then repeat the test. If the surprise happens again, it is real, and you have learned something new. It may mean the idea was wrong, or that a detail you did not think of matters. If it does not happen again, write down that the first result was probably a slip. Many important discoveries began as a surprising result that someone kept and looked at. Jocelyn Bell noticed a strange mark on a paper chart and would not let it go.
- e.g. You expect the warm tumbler to stay dry, but it gets a few drops. Perhaps the room is very humid.
- e.g. One person's arm span is 10 centimetres more than their height. Measure again before you decide it is real.
- e.g. A pigeon stays on the tank after the usual time. Write it down. It may be one pigeon, or a clue.
### Why we see what we expect · *defined*
**Definition:** People notice and remember what fits their idea more than what does not. Writing the prediction first, and asking what would show us wrong, helps us fight this habit.

When an idea feels right to us, we tend to notice the evidence for it and overlook the evidence against it. This happens to children and grown-ups, and to scientists too. A person who thinks the bell scares the pigeons will notice the evenings when the bell and the leaving happen together. They may not even notice the evening the bell was late. Psychologists have shown this in many experiments. The cure is not to stop having ideas. The cure is to write the predictions before the test, to ask 'what would show me wrong?' and to ask someone who disagrees to look at the results too.
- e.g. Two friends who each hope their team wins see the same close match and each count different fouls.
- e.g. A child who expects the lemon slice to stay white may not look closely at a brown edge.
- e.g. Francis Bacon, an English thinker, wrote in 1620 that a mind that has taken up an idea draws everything else in to support it.
### Signal or noise? Jocelyn Bell's star-time test · *defined*
**Definition:** A signal is a pattern that carries information. Noise is random wobble. To check whether a strange mark is a signal from far away or noise from nearby, ask when it comes back.

In 1967 Jocelyn Bell was a research student at Cambridge in England. She was checking long paper charts from a radio telescope, a set of wires that listens for radio waves from space. She noticed a small strange mark, which she called a 'bit of scruff'. It came again. At first the team asked if it was interference from something on Earth, such as a car or a radio. Here was a deciding question. A thing in the sky rises and sets with the stars, and comes back about four minutes earlier by the clock each day. A car or a machine follows the clock on the wall. The mark followed the stars. So it came from space. We now know that it came from a pulsar.
- e.g. A noisy machine next door follows the clock. A star follows star time, about four minutes earlier each day.
- e.g. A radio crackle every evening at six may be a neighbour's machine. A crackle that moves earlier each day may not be.
- e.g. Astronomers still check strange signals by asking if they come back at star time.
### How do pigeons tell that evening is coming? · *idea*
**Definition:** Many town birds settle for the night as the light fades, and a dark cloudy evening can send them to roost early. Scientists know that light matters. They are still working out exactly how birds judge it.

Birds have eyes, and they also have light-sensitive cells deep in the brain. Scientists have found that these help a bird tell day length and the time of year. Evening roosting seems to depend on how bright the sky is, and also on the bird's own inner body clock. Birds in cities also meet street lights, which can change when they sleep. We do not yet know exactly how all these signals are added up by the bird. This is a good example of an idea where we know a part and are still asking.
- e.g. Many crows gather to roost in the same trees around dusk in Indian cities.
- e.g. Street lights can keep some city birds singing late into the night, which scientists have studied.
- e.g. A very dark rain cloud in the afternoon can send birds to roost early.
### Can every question be answered by a test? · *debated*
Some questions can be answered by looking, counting or testing, such as how many drops formed in ten minutes. Other questions are about taste, kindness or what is fair. A test can tell us whether a cheaper medicine works. It cannot by itself tell us whether it is fair that some people cannot afford any. People disagree about where the line between testable questions and other questions falls.
- e.g. Which tumbler keeps water coldest? A test can answer it.
- e.g. Which song is nicest? Each person's answer is true for them, so a test does not settle it.
- e.g. Is it fair to test a new medicine on people? A test can tell us how it works. People must still argue about what is fair.
- **Many scientists:** Science answers only the questions that a test or an observation can settle. Questions about what is right, beautiful or fair belong to other kinds of thinking, and the two should not be mixed up.
- **Many philosophers and some scientists:** Science and values meet all the time. What scientists choose to ask, which tests are allowed, and what we do with the results all depend on what people care about. Evidence helps us argue about fairness, even if it cannot settle it alone.

## Mostly agreed
- What we observe can be checked by others. What we infer is a guess until it has been tested. *How we know:* When two people look at the same wet tumbler, they agree on the drops. They often disagree on where the water came from, until a test is done.
- Drops on the outside of a cold tumbler come from water vapour in the air, which turns into liquid when it touches the cold wall. *How we know:* Coloured water inside leaves the outside drops clear. A warm tumbler stays dry. A very cold glass left on a sensitive scale slowly gets heavier as drops collect.
- When two explanations fit everything you have seen, you need a new observation where they disagree to choose between them. *How we know:* This is how scientists have separated rival explanations for centuries. Francesco Redi, an Italian doctor, did it in 1668 by covering some jars of meat with cloth and leaving others open, to see whether maggots came from the meat itself or from flies.
- An idea is useful to science only if some result could show it wrong. *How we know:* Ideas that can fit every possible result cannot be checked by anyone, so they cannot be improved by testing.
- A regular signal that returns at star time comes from far away, and not from machines on Earth. *How we know:* Jocelyn Bell's team found that the pulses followed star time, and other radio telescopes found the same signals.
- Arm span and height are close in most people, but not equal in everyone. *How we know:* Measurements of many people show that arm span is usually within a few centimetres of height. Everyone's measurements also wobble a little.

## How we found out
**Jocelyn Bell (now Jocelyn Bell Burnell), a research student at Cambridge, with her supervisor Antony Hewish and their team, 1967, and published in 1968.** In 1967, Jocelyn Bell was a research student at Cambridge in England. Her job was to read long paper charts from a new radio telescope that listened to the sky. One day she saw a small strange mark, which she called a bit of scruff. It came back. The team first asked if it was interference from something on Earth, such as a passing car or a radio. Then Bell noticed something clever. The mark returned about four minutes earlier each day by the clock. Things in the sky do that, because the stars rise earlier each day. Machines on Earth follow the clock on the wall. So the mark came from space. Looking closer, the mark was a very regular pulse. The team half-jokingly wondered if it was a message. In fact, it was a new kind of star, now called a pulsar. In 1974 the Nobel Prize for this work went to her supervisor and another senior scientist, and not to her.  
*What people believed before:* Nobody expected a star to pulse so fast and so evenly, so the first guess was that a regular beat must come from machines on Earth. Bell's team showed that the beat followed star time, so it came from far beyond the Earth.

## Challenging perspectives
- **I saw the water leak through the steel.** (Many children, and some grown-ups.; *rejected*)  
  For: Water appears on the outside of a cold tumbler. It is easy to think it came from the inside.  
  Against: Nobody can see water crossing steel. What was seen was drops on the outside. A drop of coloured water inside leaves the outside drops clear, which shows the water came from somewhere else.
- **If I watch carefully enough, the answer will appear on its own.** (A common idea about how scientists work.; *rejected*)  
  For: Careful watching helps us notice what is there, and many discoveries began with someone noticing something.  
  Against: Watching gives us observations, not explanations. When two explanations fit the same observations, watching more of the same cannot choose. A good question can.
- **There is one scientific method, with fixed steps that every scientist follows in the same order.** (Many school books.; *debated*)  
  For: Questions, predictions, tests and conclusions do appear again and again in science, and a list of steps is easy to teach.  
  Against: Real scientists mix their ways of working. Astronomers cannot change a star. Fossil hunters cannot repeat the past. Many discoveries began with a surprise, not a plan. The steps are a useful guide, but not a rule.
- **One good test can prove an idea true for ever.** (Many people.; *debated*)  
  For: A clear result, repeated by many people, can make an idea very strong.  
  Against: A test can show an idea wrong at once. It can only add to the case for an idea being right. A new test in a new place might still show a problem. This is why scientists say 'the evidence supports' more often than 'it is proved'.
- **A surprising result means the test went wrong, so it can be thrown away.** (Many students, and sometimes experienced people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sometimes a surprise really is a slip, such as a misread number.  
  Against: Some surprises are the discovery. The only way to know is to check, repeat and keep the record. Rubbing out an odd result hides the very thing that might teach you something.
- **If two things go together, one must cause the other.** (A very common idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sometimes it is true. Fire and smoke go together, and fire causes smoke.  
  Against: A third thing can cause both, as age makes feet and reading skill grow together. Or it can be chance. Only a fair test, or careful extra evidence, can tell which.
- **Questions about what is fair or good are science questions, because science can answer everything.** (Some enthusiasts of science.; *debated*)  
  For: Evidence can help us argue about fairness, for example by showing who is harmed by a rule.  
  Against: A test shows what happens. It does not by itself say what we ought to do. People have to talk and decide about that, using evidence as one part of the talk.

## Roots
We have tested a tumbler, sorted what we saw from what we thought, and learned to ask the question that tells ideas apart. Now we ask what this says about seeing, and about how we know anything.

**Deep truth.** Everyone beside the tumbler saw the same drops. Some said the water leaked through the steel, and some said it came from the air. What we see is shared. The story we add is ours, and we usually forget that we added it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'I saw the water leak through' as if the leaking were something we saw. We saw drops. The leaking was a story we added so quickly that it felt like seeing.  
*Evidence:* In the Weeping Tumbler, the red water stayed inside and the drops outside were clear. Everyone saw the same drops, and only a question about colour could separate the stories.

- *who am i:* You tell yourself stories all day about what you see. When a friend does not smile, you think she is cross. The smile was seen. The 'cross' was added.
- *what is life:* Many beings read the world and guess. A crow turns their head and decides if your hand holds food. A seedling leans towards the light. Living beings act on guesses about what they sense, and they learn when a guess fails.
- *what is this world:* The air, the steel, the ice and your eyes took part in one happening. We cut it into 'what I saw' and 'what I think' afterwards. The world did not come with that cut.

The cold tumbler, the air, the drops and your eyes were all part of one happening. Nothing in it came labelled 'observation' or 'explanation'. We make that cut ourselves, so that we can check our stories one at a time. Everything we know comes from bodies meeting the world, and every body adds a little of its own. A bee sees the same flower as you, and in a different way. When we pull the seeing and the story apart and test the story, we are not leaving the world. We are looking at it more carefully.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 17, Autumn Floods:** Zhuangzi and his friend Huizi stood on a bridge over a river. Zhuangzi said, 'The fish swim about so freely. They are happy.' Huizi said, 'You are not a fish. How do you know they are happy?' Zhuangzi answered, 'You are not me. How do you know I do not know?' ('The fish are happy' is a story added to what Zhuangzi saw, which was fish swimming about. Huizi asked the question of an investigator: how do you know? The answer might be a way to check, or it might be an honest 'I do not know'.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta:** The hymn of creation asks where everything came from. It ends by saying that perhaps even the one who looks down from the highest heaven knows, or perhaps even that one does not know. (It is very old and it shows honest not-knowing. 'I do not know yet' is where a good question begins, before any story is chosen.)
- **Nyaya Sutra 1.1.1:** The Nyaya school opens with a list of sixteen topics for good enquiry. The list includes doubt and the settling of a question, as well as discussion and the spotting of faulty reasoning. (Doubt comes before settlement. Rival explanations are a kind of doubt, and a deciding question is how to move from doubt to a settled answer.)
- Science (settled): Observations can be checked by anyone, while explanations are tested step by step. Scientists keep the two apart when they report results.
- Science (settled): People add expectations to what they see, without noticing. Psychologists have shown that what we expect changes what we notice and remember.
- Other view, Some philosophers of science: No observation is ever free of ideas. What we choose to look at, and the words we use for it, already carry theories. So the line between what I saw and what I think is never sharp. It is a useful habit, but not a clean split.
- Practice, Seeing and storying (3 min): Look at a steel tumbler or a plate. Say three things you see, in the plainest words you have. Do not use the word because. Now notice a voice inside that wants to say why. Let it speak, and then go back to looking. Breathe in slowly three times.
- Ask: When you see a friend frown, what did you see, and what did you add?
- Ask: Could you ever see something without adding any idea to it?
- Ask: Zhuangzi said the fish were happy. How could you find out?
- Ask: What do you wonder about that you have never asked out loud?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Everyone at the tumbler saw the same drops, yet people told different stories about them. Is the story part of what you see? (hint: Try to describe the drops without using the word 'because'. How much is left?)
- *deep:* Is there anything you have ever seen without adding some idea to it? (hint: Think about a face. When you see someone frowning, what did your eyes give you and what did you add?)
- *deep:* If two ideas fit all the evidence for a hundred years, do we have to choose one? (hint: Think about what the two ideas would each predict about a new test nobody has done yet.)
- *big:* The air in your room is full of water you cannot see. What else is around you that you cannot see? (hint: Think about radio waves, smells and the air itself.)
- *big:* Jocelyn Bell was a student when she noticed the strange mark, and a more senior person received the Nobel Prize. Who gets to ask the questions, and who gets the credit? (hint: Think about who looked at the charts every day, and who was able to have their name on the prize.)
- *big:* Why do people sometimes hold on to an explanation after a test has shown it wrong? (hint: Think about how it feels to be wrong, and about who else shared the idea with you.)

## Story: The Pigeons and the Bell
*A made-up story. The science in it is real: many town birds settle for the night as the light fades, and a dark cloudy evening can send them to roost early. The flat, the bell, the pigeons' timings and the two weeks are imagined.*

Chitra lived with her Ajji on the third floor of a flat in Mysuru. From their balcony you could see the water tank on the roof of the next building. Every evening, forty or fifty pigeons sat along the edge of the tank.

That November her cousin Kiran came to stay for two weeks. He was seven and he asked questions about everything. How many windows are there? Why does the lift hum? Where do the pigeons go?

On the first evening, the two of them stood on the balcony with glasses of warm milk. Across the road, the little temple began to ring its evening bell. At that very moment all the pigeons lifted off the tank together. They circled once and flew away over the roofs.

"The bell scared them," said Kiran. "I saw it."

Ajji was in the kitchen, but she had good ears. "What did you see," she called, "and what did you add?"

This was Ajji's rule. Whenever somebody said "because" in her house, she asked the same thing. Chitra grinned. She knew what to do.

"You saw the bell ring," she told Kiran. "You saw the pigeons fly. You did not see the bell scare them. The scaring is the part you added."

Kiran thought about this with his milk moustache. "Then what is your idea?"

"Pigeons go to sleep at dusk," said Chitra. "Maybe they leave because the light is going." She opened her notebook and wrote two ideas, one under the other. The bell. The dusk.

"It is the bell," said Kiran. "The bell is loud."

Ajji came out, wiping her hands. "Two ideas," she said. "Good. Now, if the first one is true, what else will you see? And if the second is true, what else?"

Chitra chewed her pencil. Then she wrote carefully. If the bell scares them, they will leave when the bell rings. If the dusk sends them, they will leave when the light goes.

She stopped. "But the bell rings at six, and the light goes at about six. The two ideas say exactly the same thing!"

"Aha," said Kiran. "So I win?"

"No. Nobody wins." Chitra was thinking fast now. "We could watch every evening for a year. Every evening would fit both ideas. Watching more of the same cannot choose between them."

"So what can?" asked Ajji, in her quiet teacher voice.

"An evening when the two ideas disagree," said Chitra. "One where the light goes at a different time from the bell."

"We cannot make the bell ring at a different time," said Kiran. "The priest will not listen to me."

Chitra looked up at the sky. The grey November clouds were already piling up in the west. "Maybe the sky will do it for us."

For three evenings they watched and wrote. Day, bell, pigeons, sky. The pigeons left just as the bell began, and the sky was bright until the very end. On the fourth evening, black rain clouds rolled in before half past four. By five the streetlights were on, and the balcony was dim.

They stood at the door with the notebook and a pencil. Kiran held his breath. At twelve minutes past five, the pigeons lifted off the tank, circled and left. The bell had not rung.

"They went early," whispered Kiran. They waited. At six the bell rang. The tank was empty and nothing moved.

"So it is not the bell," said Kiran, a little sadly.

"Not on this evening," said Chitra. "But you have a good point coming, I can see it."

Kiran jumped. "What if the rain scared them? Rain is loud, too!"

Chitra beamed. "A third idea. Good. Now we need a dark evening with no rain." She wrote it down.

Two evenings later the sky was thick and grey all afternoon, but no drop fell. The pigeons left early again, when the light had gone dim. And one more evening, the temple priest was away and the bell did not ring at all. The pigeons left at the usual time.

Ajji read the notebook for a long time. "So, what can you say?"

Chitra wrote slowly. "On the evenings we watched, the pigeons left when the light went. They did not leave when the bell rang. We watched one tank for two weeks. We cannot say this is true for every pigeon in Mysuru."

"Good," said Ajji. "You said what your evidence says and not one word more."

Kiran was frowning at the empty tank. "But how do the pigeons know the light is going? They do not have clocks."

Chitra shrugged. "I do not know. That is the next question."

Kiran took the notebook, turned to a fresh page and wrote the question at the top. Under it he wrote, in large letters, "Idea one." Then he left a lot of space.

**For the parent:** When two ideas both fit what you have seen, watching more of the same cannot choose between them. You need a day when the ideas predict different things. Then the day itself gives the answer.

- Ask: Why could Chitra not choose between the bell and the dusk after the first few evenings?
- Ask: What was the deciding question that Chitra found, and what did each idea predict about it?
- Ask: Kiran suggested a third idea, that the rain scared the pigeons. How did Chitra test it?
- Ask: Chitra said she could not be sure about every pigeon in Mysuru. Why was that a good thing to say?
- Ask: What is the next question that Kiran asked, and how might you find out?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Weeping Tumbler: where do the drops come from? (40 min)
- **question:** A steel tumbler of ice water gets wet on the outside. Did the water leak through the steel, or did it come from the air?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. What will happen to the outside of a tumbler filled with ice water? Where do you think the water will come from? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** This test puts two ideas against each other. It does not change one thing to see what it does. The deciding question is the colour of the drops. Leaking predicts coloured drops, and water from the air predicts clear drops. To keep it fair, both tumblers are the same size and start dry. They stand on the same kind of plate in the same place and are checked at the same times. The warm tumbler is the control, because it shows what the room does when the steel is not cold.
- **safety:** Use warm tap water, never hot water from a kettle. A grown-up should pour for young children, and handle glass tumblers, since glass can break. Food colour and haldi stain clothes, so work on a tray or a table you can wipe.
- **record:** Fill in the results table at 5, 10 and 15 minutes. Write the minute you first saw drops on the cold tumbler. Draw both tumblers at 15 minutes, and colour in the water inside and the drops outside.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Cold tumbler: what the outside looks like | Cold tumbler: colour of the drops | Warm tumbler: what the outside looks like
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two tumblers and the ice. Ask what will happen to the outside of the ice-water tumbler. Ask why. Write the guess and the reason at the top of the page.  
  *Parent says:* We will fill this tumbler with ice water. In ten minutes, what do you think the outside will look like? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A tumbler of ice water. What will the outside look like in ten minutes? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen on the outside? Why? (good: It will get wet, because the ice makes it sweat.; Nothing, because the steel is shut.; It will get wet, because the water leaks through.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write exactly what your child says. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child that people disagree about where the water comes from. Write two ideas. Idea A: the water leaked through the steel. Idea B: the water came from the air. Ask for a third idea. Then ask for an if-then sentence for each idea about the colour of the drops.  
  *Parent says:* Some people say the water leaks through the steel. Some say it comes from the air. Do you have another idea? If we colour the water inside red, what will we see outside if idea A is true? And if idea B is true?  
  *Child mode:* Two ideas. A: it leaks through. B: it comes from the air. What colour will the drops be for each?
  - Ask: If the water leaked through, what colour would the drops be? (good: Red, like the water inside.; The same colour as the water inside.)
  - Ask: If the water came from the air, what colour would the drops be? (good: Clear.; No colour, because the air has no colour.)
  - Note: If your child says 'sweat from my hands' or 'from the ice', write it down as Idea C. A good deciding question for Idea C is: do the drops also appear when no hand has touched the tumbler? The coloured water in the next step will help with that too.
  - Check: Why do we write the if-then sentences before the test? → **So we cannot say afterwards that we expected the result**

  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the first tumbler three-quarters with chilled water. Add 6 to 8 ice cubes. Stir in the colour until the water is strongly coloured. 2. With one child, you take the timer and the writing. Hold the tissue to a drop and look at the damp spot it leaves. A red spot means colour, and a spot with no colour means a clear drop. If there are no drops after 15 minutes, add more ice and wait 10 more minutes. You can also breathe gently onto the cold steel. Your breath is full of water vapour, so mist forms at once. The warm tumbler should stay dry or show only a very faint mist at the start. Drops that look slightly pink mean there was a little colour on the outside. Wipe it and look again.
  - Check: The water inside is red. The drops on the outside are clear. What does this show? → **The drops did not come from the water inside**
  - Wait: Wait 5 minutes between looks. Read the explanation card 'Rival explanations' while you wait.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On the cold tumbler, wipe one patch of the outside dry with a tissue. 2. Watch that patch for three minutes. 3. Ask your child where the new drops come from, if there is no hole.  
  *Parent says:* I will wipe this patch dry. Watch it for three minutes. What do you notice? If the steel leaked, where would the water come from now?  
  *Child mode:* Wipe one patch dry. Watch it. Do new drops come back?
  - Ask: Where do the new drops on the dry patch come from? (good: From the air.; From the air touching the cold steel.; Not from inside, because the steel is shut.)
  - Note: The wiped patch fogs over again within a few minutes. A leaking tumbler would need tiny holes, and the drops would reappear only at those spots. Here the drops appear everywhere on the cold wall, and also low down on the plate edge. This supports the air idea.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. Then read the explanation 'Why drops form on the outside of a cold tumbler'.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think happened? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened that way?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? What did you see? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What do you think, what did you see, and why? (good: The water came from the air. The drops were clear but the water inside was red. The air has water vapour that turns to drops on the cold steel.; I was wrong. I thought it leaked, but the colour stayed inside.)
  - Ask: Was your first guess right? Does it matter if it was wrong? (good: It does not matter. The test shows what happens.; I was surprised, and that is useful.)
  - Note: Say well done for any honest result. If the drops were coloured, look for colour on the outside from pouring, or for a crack in the tumbler. Repeat with a clean tumbler.
  - Check: The warm tumbler stayed dry. What does this tell us about the drops on the cold one? → **The cold steel matters, because the same air did not make drops on the warm one**
### e2 · Arm Span and Height: is Leonardo right? (45 min)
- **question:** Is a person's arm span (finger tip to finger tip, arms stretched out) the same as their height? Is the pattern the same for children and grown-ups?
- **predict:** Before you measure, guess. Will your arm span be shorter than your height, the same, or longer? Will it be the same for a grown-up? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** This is a pattern search, not a fair test, because we cannot change a person's height to see what happens. We keep the measuring fair in other ways. Everyone is measured in bare feet, standing straight against the same wall. The same helper reads the tape, and we measure to the nearest centimetre. We write down every person we measure and we leave nobody out because the number looks odd.
- **safety:** Ask each person before you measure them. Do not stand on chairs to reach the top of the wall.
- **record:** Fill in the results table for each person. Draw a number line from minus 10 to plus 10 and put a dot for each person's span minus height.
- **results table:** Person | Height (cm) | Arm span (cm) | Span minus height (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess whether arm span will be shorter than height, the same, or longer. Ask why. Write the guess. Draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* Stretch your arms out wide. Do you think the distance from fingertip to fingertip is shorter than your height, the same or longer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Arms wide. Is your span shorter, the same, or longer than your height? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Arms are long, so the span is longer.; My arms are shorter than my body, so the span is shorter.; They look about the same.)
  - Note: Write the guess and reason at the top. Do not say what Leonardo drew yet.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take off shoes. 2. Stand with the back of the head, shoulders and heels against the wall, looking straight ahead. 3. A helper lays a flat book on the head, level against the wall. The helper sticks a sticky note just under the book. 4. Measure from the floor to the mark. 5. Do this twice, and write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Stand tall with your back against the wall. Look straight ahead. Now I will mark the top of your head. We will measure twice. Do you think we will get the same number?  
  *Child mode:* Stand against the wall. Your helper marks the top of your head. Measure twice.
  - Ask: The two heights were not exactly the same. Why might that happen? (good: The tape was not straight.; I stood a little differently.; We read the tape in a different place.)
  - Note: A difference of 1 or 2 centimetres between measurements is normal, because the head moves and the tape bends. Do not worry about it. Say that this wobble is why we measure several people.
  - Check: You measure the same child twice and get 128 cm and 129 cm. What is the best thing to do? → **Write both numbers and use the bigger one or the middle, and say there is about a centimetre of wobble**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand with your back flat against the wall and your arms stretched out wide, like a letter T. 2. A helper puts a sticky note at the tip of each middle finger. 3. Step away. 4. Pull the tape straight and measure from one note to the other. 5. Measure twice and write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Back against the wall. Arms out wide like a T. Hold still. I will put a sticky note at each fingertip. Then we measure between them.  
  *Child mode:* Back to the wall. Arms out like a T. Your helper marks your fingertips.
  - Note: The wall must be wider than the arm span. If it is not, stand in a corner with one fingertip touching the corner and mark the other on the wall. Keep the tape level, not sagging.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure the other people the same way. Write each person's name or initial, their height and their arm span in the table. Subtract height from span to fill the last column. A negative number means span is shorter than height.  
  *Parent says:* Now we measure everyone else the same way. Write each number in the table. Then take away the height from the span. If the span is shorter, the answer is a number below zero.  
  *Child mode:* Measure at least five more people. Write each person in your table.
  - Note: Roles for 2 to 4 children: one stands, one holds the book and the sticky note, one holds the tape, one writes. Swap so each child measures and is measured. With one child, you are the helper and measure the child, then yourself with your child's help. You need at least six people. If your home has only four, ask two neighbours or friends. You can also measure again on another day. Measure at least one child and at least one grown-up. Children grow, so their spans may differ from adults' spans in a different way.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the last column. Count how many people are within 3 centimetres of zero. 2. Find the person farthest from zero. 3. Put the dots on a number line. 4. Look at the children and grown-ups separately.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the last column. How many people have a difference close to zero? Who is farthest? Do the children look different from the grown-ups?  
  *Child mode:* Count how many people are close to zero. Who is the farthest? What do you notice?
  - Ask: What pattern do you see? (good: Most people have a span close to their height.; The span is a little longer for the grown-ups.; One person is far from the rest.)
  - Ask: What would you do about the person who is far from the rest? (good: Measure them again.; Check the tape was straight.; Write them in anyway if it is the same twice.)
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say a claim, the evidence and the reason. Then ask what the numbers cannot tell us. Read the explanation 'Looking for a pattern in many cases'.  
  *Parent says:* What can you say about span and height from your numbers? What can you not say?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? What can't you say from only a few people?
  - Ask: What can't we say from only a few people? (good: That it is true for everyone in the world.; That it is true for babies or for very old people.; That one thing causes the other.)
  - Note: A typical result is that most people are within a few centimetres of zero, with some a little over and some under. Grown-ups, especially men, often have a span a few centimetres longer than their height, and the numbers wobble more with a small group, so do not be surprised if only half your people are within 3 centimetres. Leonardo drew arm span equal to height, but real people vary. If your child's numbers are all over the place, check the measuring before you decide there is no pattern.
  - Check: Six people have an arm span within 4 centimetres of their height. Which claim goes too far? → **Everybody in the world has an arm span exactly equal to their height**

### c1 · Does the Fridge Light Stay On? A puzzle you cannot look at (30 min)

## Worksheets
### Saw It, Thought It (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which sentence is an observation about a steel tumbler of ice water?  
   Options: The tumbler leaked / There are small clear drops on the outside of the tumbler / The ice made the steel sweat  
   **Answer:** There are small clear drops on the outside of the tumbler. *Why:* An observation is what you can see, and anyone can check it. 'Leaked' and 'made the steel sweat' are stories about why the drops are there. They are inferences.
2. (sort) Sort each sentence about a bowl of curd on the shelf. Is it something you saw, or something you think?  
   **Answer:** There is a thin watery layer on top of the curd → I saw; The curd has gone sour → I think; The bowl is cold to touch → I saw; Someone left the fridge door open → I think; A few small bubbles are in the curd → I saw; The curd was made with too much starter → I think. *Why:* Sentences that report what you see, feel or count belong in 'I saw'. Sentences about why something happened, or what someone did, are stories. They belong in 'I think', until a test checks them.
3. (truefalse) Mira says, 'Every inference is wrong, so scientists should never make one.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Inferences are guesses made from what we saw, and science is full of them. The point is to know which sentences are inferences, so we can test them.
4. (mcq) Kavya sees her money plant leaning towards the window. Which sentence is an inference?  
   Options: The leaves are pointing to the window / The plant wants the sunlight / The pot is on the sill  
   **Answer:** The plant wants the sunlight. *Why:* 'Wants the sunlight' is a guess about why the plant leans. The leaves pointing and the pot on the sill can both be seen. Plants do bend towards light, and a lamp test could check this.
5. (fill) Two different explanations for the same thing you saw are called ____ explanations.  
   **Answer:** ['rival']. *Why:* Rival explanations compete. Usually at most one of them is right, so we look for a way to choose between them.
6. (write) Meenakshi's dosa batter did not rise overnight. Write two different explanations that could both fit.  
   **Answer:** For example: the kitchen was too cold. Or: there was not enough time. Or: the batter was too thick. Any two sensible, different explanations score.. *Why:* A good answer gives two ideas that are different from each other and that could each be checked, for example by keeping batter in a warm place and in a cold place.
7. (mcq) Farhan says ants find the sugar by smell. Arjun says they follow a trail left by other ants. Which check can tell the ideas apart?  
   Options: Count how many ants arrive / Wipe a short piece of the ants' path and watch what they do / Give the ants a bigger piece of sugar  
   **Answer:** Wipe a short piece of the ants' path and watch what they do. *Why:* If the ants follow a trail, wiping a piece of it will confuse them. If they find the sugar alone by smell, they will carry on. Counting or giving more sugar gives the same result under both ideas.
8. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Observation → what you notice, without adding why; Inference → a guess you work out from what you noticed; Prediction → what you expect to see if an idea is true; Evidence → what you saw that counts for or against an idea; Hypothesis → an explanation you can test. *Why:* These words come up in every investigation. A strong answer could give an example for each one.
9. (order) Put the steps of telling two ideas apart in the best order.  
   **Answer:** ['Notice something odd and write what you saw', 'Write two rival ideas', 'Write an if-then sentence for each idea', 'Find a question the ideas answer differently', 'Do the check and write what you saw']. *Why:* You need something to explain first. Then you need ideas, then what each one predicts. Only then can you find the question where they differ and do the check.
10. (truefalse) If both ideas predict exactly the same result, a test cannot choose between them.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. If both ideas give the same result, any result fits both. You need a check where the two ideas give different results.
11. (mcq) Which is the best if-then prediction for the idea 'The pigeons leave because of the bell'?  
   Options: If the bell scares the pigeons, they will leave later / If the bell scares the pigeons, they will stay on an evening when the bell does not ring / If the bell scares the pigeons, they will fly high  
   **Answer:** If the bell scares the pigeons, they will stay on an evening when the bell does not ring. *Why:* A good prediction says what you will see if the idea is true, and could be shown wrong. If the pigeons leave without the bell, the idea is in trouble.
12. (odd) Three of these ideas can be tested. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: The drops on the tumbler come from the air / The pigeons leave because of the dusk / The tumbler is wet because an invisible helper makes it wet, and hides when we look / The curd sets faster in a warm place  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The invisible helper fits every possible result, so no test could show it wrong. The other three say something that a check could contradict.
13. (draw) Draw two columns headed 'I saw' and 'I think'. Write three sentences about a crow on your railing in the correct columns.  
   **Answer:** A page with two columns. Under 'I saw': for example, 'A crow sits on the railing' and 'The crow turns their head'. Under 'I think': for example, 'The crow is hungry'.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps what can be seen apart from the reasons we add. 'The crow is waiting for food' belongs in the second column.
14. (mcq) In a tumbler test, a girl colours the water inside red. The drops on the outside are clear. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: The drops did not come from the water inside / Colour cannot go through steel / The red colour was too weak  
   **Answer:** The drops did not come from the water inside. *Why:* If the water inside had crossed the steel, the drops would carry the colour. They are clear, so the water came from somewhere else. The air is the likely source.
15. (perspective) A girl's test gives a result she did not expect. She wants to rub it out and write what she thought would happen. Her brother wants to keep it and repeat the test. Who do you agree with?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer keeps the odd result, writes it down as it is and repeats the test, and also gives the girl's worry a fair hearing.. *Why:* Pick-a-side questions have no single right side. Marks go for using the idea of evidence and for stating the other view fairly.
16. (write) Why is it better to write a prediction before the test than after it?  
   **Answer:** Once you have seen the result, it is easy to believe you expected it all along. A written prediction is fixed, so the test can really show you wrong.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the written prediction cannot change after the result, so we can trust it as a test of the idea.
### Patterns, Causes and Surprises (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) On a normal day, why does a tumbler of warm water stay dry on the outside?  
   Options: The air next to a warm tumbler stays warm, so its water vapour does not turn into drops / Warm water has no vapour / Warm water is too heavy to leak  
   **Answer:** The air next to a warm tumbler stays warm, so its water vapour does not turn into drops. *Why:* Drops form when air touches something cold enough to turn some of its vapour into liquid. A warm tumbler does not cool the air next to it.
2. (mcq) A cold bottle from the fridge gets wet outside in a damp kitchen but stays almost dry in a very dry winter room. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Dry air has less water vapour to collect / The bottle leaks more in summer / The fridge is cold only in summer  
   **Answer:** Dry air has less water vapour to collect. *Why:* Condensation needs vapour in the air. When the air is dry, there is less vapour, so fewer drops form and they come more slowly.
3. (sort) Choose the best way of finding out for each question.  
   **Answer:** Does more salt in water help a potato float? → Fair test; Do taller children have longer feet? → Pattern search; When does the school bus come each morning? → Long watch; How far away is the Sun? → Look it up; Does a bigger paper helicopter fall slower? → Fair test; How many petals do hibiscus flowers have? → Pattern search. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing. A pattern search measures many cases. A long watch looks again and again over time. Looking it up suits questions we cannot check at home.
4. (truefalse) If two things go together, one must cause the other.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A third thing can cause both, or it can be chance. Shoe size and reading skill go together in primary school because both grow with age.
5. (write) Priya measured 8 people. Seven had an arm span within 4 centimetres of their height. One had a span 12 centimetres more than the height. What should Priya do about the odd one, and why?  
   **Answer:** Measure that person again, check the tape was straight, and write down both results. If it is the same again, keep it. Do not rub it out.. *Why:* A good answer says to check and repeat before deciding, and to keep the record honest whatever the second measurement shows.
6. (mcq) Which gives a steadier pattern about arm span and height?  
   Options: Measuring 3 people / Measuring 12 people / Measuring only the tallest person  
   **Answer:** Measuring 12 people. *Why:* More cases give a steadier pattern, because any one person may be a little odd. Measuring only one kind of person is also unfair.
7. (fill) The small wobble in any measurement, from where you hold the tape or how you read it, is called measurement ____.  
   **Answer:** ['error']. *Why:* Measurement error is not a mistake. It is the small difference you get when you measure the same thing twice. Every measurement has some.
8. (mcq) Ice cream sales and sunburn cases both go up in summer. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Ice cream causes sunburn / Hot sunny weather causes both / Sunburn makes people buy ice cream  
   **Answer:** Hot sunny weather causes both. *Why:* A third thing, the hot sunny weather, can explain both. Two things that go up together do not have to cause each other.
9. (order) Put the steps of a long watch in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Choose what to watch and why', 'Choose when and how often to look', 'Look and write each result with the time', 'Lay the results side by side', 'Say what the pattern shows and what it cannot show']. *Why:* A long watch needs a plan first, then regular looks recorded with the time, and only at the end the pattern and what it can and cannot say.
10. (draw) Eight people had these differences between arm span and height, in centimetres: 0, +2, −1, +3, −4, +1, 0, +12. Draw a number line from −5 to +15 and put a dot for each person. Circle the one that does not fit.  
   **Answer:** A number line from −5 to +15 with seven dots near zero and one dot at +12, circled.. *Why:* A dot graph shows that most people are close to zero and one is far away. The far dot should be checked and repeated, not hidden.
11. (match) Match each question to the best way of finding out.  
   **Answer:** Does the streetlight come on earlier on cloudy days? → a long watch; Do taller people have longer arms? → a pattern search; Does lemon juice slow browning of cut fruit? → a fair test; How old is the Moon? → look it up. *Why:* Questions about change over time need regular looks. Questions about what goes with what need many cases. Questions about what one thing does need a fair test. Questions we cannot test at home can be looked up.
12. (odd) Three of these are good ways to handle a surprising result. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Check how the test was done / Repeat the test / Rub it out because it does not fit / Write it down exactly as it was  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Rubbing out an odd result hides the very thing that may teach us something. The other three are what careful scientists do.
13. (mcq) The warm tumbler also gets a few drops after 15 minutes. What should you do first?  
   Options: Check whether the room is very humid and whether the water has cooled / Rub out the observation / Say the idea about air is wrong  
   **Answer:** Check whether the room is very humid and whether the water has cooled. *Why:* A surprising result needs checking. A humid room or water that has cooled can make a few drops on a warm tumbler. You write it down and compare with the cold tumbler.
14. (perspective) Two friends measure many people and find that arm span and height are close, but never exactly equal. One says the old drawing by Leonardo, which showed them equal, was wrong. The other says it was a good rule of thumb. Who do you agree with?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that 'close' and 'exactly' are different claims, and that a good claim says which one it makes. Both friends have a point.. *Why:* Pick-a-side questions have no single right side. Marks go for using evidence from the measuring and for stating the other view fairly.
15. (write) Two ideas both fit everything you saw so far. Explain in your own words why watching more of the same cannot choose between them.  
   **Answer:** Both ideas expect the same results, so every new result fits both. You need a situation where the ideas expect different results.. *Why:* A good answer says that the ideas have to disagree about something for a test to separate them.
16. (truefalse) A surprising result should be rubbed out so the page looks tidy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The surprising result may be the discovery. Check it, repeat it and keep the record.
### Telling Ideas Apart (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A strange mark on a chart comes back about four minutes earlier each day by the clock. What does that suggest?  
   Options: It follows star time, so it may come from the sky / It follows the clock on the wall, so it is a machine on Earth / It was drawn by someone  
   **Answer:** It follows star time, so it may come from the sky. *Why:* Things in the sky return about four minutes earlier each day. Machines on Earth follow the clock on the wall. This is the test Jocelyn Bell's team used.
2. (mcq) A crackle on a radio comes at exactly 7 pm every day by the clock. What does that suggest?  
   Options: It probably comes from something that works on the clock, such as a neighbour's machine / It comes from a distant star / It is a message from space  
   **Answer:** It probably comes from something that works on the clock, such as a neighbour's machine. *Why:* Anything that returns at the same clock time each day follows human routines. A star would drift earlier each day.
3. (write) A damp patch grows on the wall under the terrace. Idea A: rain is seeping in. Idea B: a pipe is leaking. Write a deciding question and say what each idea predicts.  
   **Answer:** Does the patch grow on a long dry spell? If rain, it will stop growing on dry days. If a pipe, it will keep growing on dry days too.. *Why:* A strong answer chooses something about which the ideas disagree and says what each would show.
4. (sort) Which of these ideas could a test show wrong?  
   **Answer:** The drops come from the air → Could be shown wrong; The light is switched on by a timer → Could be shown wrong; Something invisible makes everything happen, whatever we see → Could never be shown wrong; The pigeons leave because of the bell → Could be shown wrong; It happens because it was always going to happen → Could never be shown wrong; Heavier balls always roll further → Could be shown wrong. *Why:* An idea that fits every possible result cannot be tested. A testable idea says what you will see, so a different result can show it wrong.
5. (perspective) A research student sees a strange mark on a chart and her supervisor receives the biggest prize for the discovery. Should the person who first noticed the mark have her name on the prize?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer weighs both views and says what counts as discovering something. Marks go for fairly stating both views and giving a reason.
6. (mcq) Which sentence is a good if-then prediction?  
   Options: If the light is a timer, it will come on at the same clock time on a very cloudy evening / If the light is a timer, it will come on / If it is cloudy, I will be sad  
   **Answer:** If the light is a timer, it will come on at the same clock time on a very cloudy evening. *Why:* A good prediction names the idea, the situation and the result you expect. It could turn out wrong.
7. (truefalse) An idea that fits every possible result is the strongest kind of idea.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An idea that fits every result tells you nothing about this case and cannot be tested. A strong idea says what you will see, so it can be wrong.
8. (fill) A question that two explanations answer differently is called a ____ question.  
   **Answer:** ['deciding']. *Why:* A deciding question is the heart of telling ideas apart. If both ideas give the same answer, the question cannot decide.
9. (odd) Three of these questions could tell the tumbler ideas apart. Which one cannot?  
   Options: What colour are the drops if the water inside is coloured? / Do the drops appear on a warm tumbler? / Do the drops come back when you wipe a patch dry? / How many ice cubes did we use?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The number of ice cubes does not separate leaking from water in the air. The other three give different results under the two ideas.
10. (order) Put these in a good order for handling a surprising result.  
   **Answer:** ['Check how the test was done', 'Repeat the test', 'Write down exactly what you saw', 'Ask what the surprise could mean']. *Why:* Write it down first so it cannot disappear. Then look for a slip, then repeat, and only then think about what it could mean.
11. (match) Match each word about measurements to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Signal → a pattern in measurements that carries information; Noise → random wobble with no message; Interference → unwanted noise from things nearby; Sample → the group of people or things you measure; Pattern → something that goes together in many cases. *Why:* These words help us talk about measurements that are hard to read. A strong answer could give an example for each.
12. (mcq) A scientist wants to know how old the Moon is. Which way of finding out is best?  
   Options: A fair test at home / Looking it up in the work of other scientists / Asking five friends to vote  
   **Answer:** Looking it up in the work of other scientists. *Why:* We cannot change the Moon or watch its whole history at home. Other scientists have measured the age with rock samples and careful reasoning, and we can read what they found.
13. (write) Describe a pattern you could look for with a measuring tape and ten people. Say what you would measure, and what you could not say from the result.  
   **Answer:** For example: foot length and height. We could say whether taller people tend to have longer feet. 14. (draw) Draw a model of the mist that appears on a bathroom mirror after a hot shower. Show the air, the water vapour, the mirror and where the drops come from.  
   **Answer:** A mirror with warm moist air near it, dots of vapour, and tiny drops forming on the cooler glass.. *Why:* A good model shows invisible vapour in the air and the cooler glass turning some of it into drops. It is the same idea as the cold tumbler.
15. (mcq) A girl says, 'I have watched the same thing for a year and it always fits both my ideas, so I know both are true.' What is wrong with this?  
   Options: Fitting both ideas does not show either is true, because the watching cannot separate them / A year is too short / She should have watched less  
   **Answer:** Fitting both ideas does not show either is true, because the watching cannot separate them. *Why:* When every result fits both ideas, the results cannot tell the ideas apart. She needs a day when the ideas disagree.
16. (write) A friend says a rain dance brings rain because it rained twice after a dance. Write two rival explanations and one deciding question.  
   **Answer:** Explanation A: the dance brought the rain. Explanation B: it was going to rain anyway. Deciding question: does it also rain on days when nobody dances, and how often?. *Why:* A good answer compares days with and without the dance. Two cases cannot decide, because rain is common.

## Applications
- A doctor deciding between two possible illnesses by choosing the test that would give different results for each.
- An engineer checking whether a damp patch on a wall is from rain or from a leaking pipe, by looking at whether it grows on dry days.
- A farmer asking whether a crop failed because of too little water or too much pest damage, by comparing fields.
- A forest officer finding out whether birds leave a forest because of noise or because of light, by watching quiet and noisy days.
- A detective separating what a witness saw from what the witness thinks happened.
- An astronomer deciding whether a strange signal is from a star or from a machine on Earth, by checking whether it follows star time.
- A cook working out whether a dish failed because of the heat or the amount of salt, by changing one at a time.
- A mechanic finding out whether a noise comes from the engine or the wheels, by listening with the engine off.
- A teacher checking whether a child's wrong answer comes from not knowing the idea or from misreading the question.
- A scientist writing a prediction before a trial so the result can really show the idea wrong.
- A weather forecaster comparing two computer models by finding a day when they predict different weather.

## Open questions
- **How exactly do birds judge that evening is coming, and how do they add up light, their body clock and the street lights?** Scientists know that light matters and that birds have light-sensitive cells in the eye and deep in the brain. How the pieces combine is still being studied.
- **How do pulsars make their beams of radio waves?** Astronomers know pulsars are spinning, tiny, dense stars. How the radio beam is made is still debated.
- **Is there any question that no test could ever answer?** Some thinkers say yes, for example questions about what happened before time began. Others say we have not yet found the right test.
- **Do other animals ask questions and test their own ideas?** Some animals, such as crows and apes, solve new puzzles in ways that look like trying out ideas. Whether this is asking a question as we do is still debated.
- **Can a very good question be asked by someone who does not yet know any science?** Children ask some of the deepest questions. Whether asking well can be taught, and how, is still being studied by people who research learning.
- **Why do people stay with an idea after a test has shown it wrong?** Psychologists have found many reasons, such as habit and pride. Nobody has a complete answer or a sure cure.

- Aadhira is 132 centimetres tall and her arm span is 129 centimetres. What is her span minus her height? **Minus 3 centimetres.**. 129 minus 132 is minus 3. The negative sign means her span is 3 centimetres shorter than her height. (queues `math.number-sense.negative-numbers.L4`)
- Eight people had these differences between arm span and height, in centimetres: 0, +2, −1, +3, −4, +1, 0, +12. How many were within 3 centimetres of zero? Which one is the odd one out, and by how much is it more than the next biggest? **Six were within 3 centimetres of zero. The odd one is +12, which is 9 more than +3.**. The numbers 0, +2, −1, +3, +1 and 0 are within 3 of zero. That is six. The number −4 is 4 away from zero, so it is outside. The biggest is +12 and the next biggest is +3. 12 minus 3 is 9. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A cold tumbler had 12 drops on its outside after 5 minutes, 30 after 10 minutes and 55 after 15 minutes. How many new drops formed between each look? What does this tell you about the speed? **18 new drops in the second five minutes, and 25 new drops in the third. The drops are forming faster.**. 30 minus 12 is 18. 55 minus 30 is 25. The first five minutes gave 12 drops, then 18, then 25. The number of new drops each five minutes is going up. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- A height of 125 centimetres has a measurement wobble of 2 centimetres. About how many in every 100 centimetres is that wobble? **About 2 in every 100.**. 2 out of 125 is 1.6 out of 100, which is about 2 out of 100. It is a small wobble compared with the height. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A bright star crosses a window at 8:00 pm tonight. A star comes back about 4 minutes earlier each day. At about what time will it cross in 5 days? **About 7:40 pm.**. 4 minutes earlier each day for 5 days is 4 times 5, which is 20 minutes. 20 minutes before 8:00 pm is 7:40 pm. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- A star crossed a window at 8:00 pm. After 14 days, about how much earlier will it cross, and about what time? **About 56 minutes earlier, so about 7:04 pm.**. 4 times 14 is 56 minutes. 8:00 pm take away 56 minutes is 7:04 pm. After about two weeks the star comes back nearly an hour earlier. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- On a dark cloudy evening the streetlight came on at 5:35 pm. On a clear evening it came on at 6:10 pm. How many minutes earlier was it on the cloudy evening? **35 minutes earlier.**. From 5:35 pm to 6:00 pm is 25 minutes. From 6:00 pm to 6:10 pm is 10 more minutes. 25 plus 10 is 35. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- In a sample of 6 people, 5 had an arm span within 3 centimetres of their height. If the same share held for 60 people, how many would that be? **50 people.**. 5 out of 6 is the same share as 50 out of 60, because 6 times 10 is 60 and 5 times 10 is 50. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In one class, 3 of 4 nine-year-olds wore big shoes, and 1 of 4 six-year-olds did. Older children also read better. Compare the two shares. Could age explain both big shoes and better reading? **Big shoes were three times as common among the nine-year-olds. Yes, age could explain both.**. 3 out of 4 is 75 out of 100 and 1 out of 4 is 25 out of 100. 75 is three times 25. Older children have bigger feet and also read better, so age can sit behind both. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A helper reads a tape measure and gets 128 centimetres. Another gets 130 centimetres for the same child. They agree to give the middle value. What is the middle value, and by how much does each reading differ from it? **129 centimetres. Each reading differs from it by 1 centimetre.**. The middle between 128 and 130 is 129, because 128 plus 130 is 258, and half of 258 is 129. 129 minus 128 is 1 and 130 minus 129 is 1. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is the difference between an observation and an inference? → An observation is what you can see or measure. An inference is a guess about what it means.
- What are rival explanations? → Different explanations for the same thing you saw, which cannot all be true.
- What is a deciding question? → A question that two explanations answer differently.
- How do you write a prediction? → As an if-then sentence: if this idea is true, then I will see this.
- Why do drops form on the outside of a cold tumbler? → Water vapour in the air turns into drops when it touches the cold steel.
- What colour would the drops be if the water had leaked through the steel? → The colour of the water inside.
- Why can't watching more of the same choose between two ideas that both fit? → Both ideas expect the same results, so every new result fits both.
- What does a pattern search do? → It measures many people or things and looks for what goes together.
- What should you do with a surprising result? → Check it, repeat it and write it down as it is.
- Do two things that go together always cause each other? → No. A third thing may cause both, or it may be chance.
