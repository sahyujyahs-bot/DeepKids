# How animals send messages: smell, colour, sound, touch and echoes
`sci.animal-behaviour.animal-communication.L4`

**Summary.** A female silk moth sits still on a leaf and makes no sound. Yet a male moth finds her in the dark. She gives off a smell called a pheromone, and his feathery antennae catch it. Animals, including humans, send messages in four ways: by smell, by sight, by sound and by touch. Your child tests how long a smell message lasts after the sender has left the room. They also find a steel plate with their eyes covered, using only echoes, as bats and dolphins do.

**Intro.** *A tigress sprays a tree and walks away. Three days later, another tiger sniffs the tree and learns that she lives here. How did a message pass between two animals who never met?* The tigress left a smell on the bark, and the smell stayed after she had gone. A smell is one way to send a message. A peacock uses colour. A frog uses a call. A monkey uses her fingers. Each way works well in some places and badly in others. In this unit you leave smell marks on paper and test how long they last. You also cover your eyes and find a steel plate by listening for echoes, as a bat finds a moth.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the four channels of animal signals and give an example of each.
1. I can explain what a pheromone is, and why a smell mark still works after the sender has gone.
2. I can test fairly how long a smell mark lasts, and say what my results show.
3. I can explain how colours, displays and dances send messages, and why they need light and a clear view.
4. I can explain what calls and songs do well, and name sounds too high or too low for humans.
5. I can explain how touch and vibration carry messages, such as grooming and the shaking of a spider's web.
6. I can explain how bats and dolphins find things by echolocation, and test whether I can hear an echo myself.
7. I can choose the best channel for a dark, noisy or distant place, and say how signals are overheard.

## Terms
- **Signal**: Something one animal does or makes that carries a message to another animal. *e.g.* A dog's growl is a signal. So is a peacock's raised train.
- **Sender and receiver**: The sender is the animal who gives the signal. The receiver is the animal who picks it up. *e.g.* When a frog calls, he is the sender. The female frog who hears him is the receiver.
- **Channel**: The way a signal travels from sender to receiver. The four main channels are smell, sight, sound and touch. *e.g.* A call travels on the sound channel. A scent mark travels on the smell channel.
- **Pheromone**: A chemical that an animal gives off, which changes what other animals of the same kind do. *e.g.* A female silk moth gives off a pheromone, and male silk moths walk towards it.
- **Scent mark**: A smell that an animal leaves on a tree, a wall or the ground for others to find later. *e.g.* A dog who wets a lamp post is leaving a scent mark.
- **Display**: A signal made for the eyes, using colour, shape or movement. *e.g.* A peacock spreading his train in front of a peahen is giving a display.
- **Warning colours**: Bright colours, such as orange with black, that tell hunters an animal tastes bad or can sting. *e.g.* The plain tiger butterfly is orange and black, and birds learn not to eat them.
- **Ultrasound**: Sound that is too high for human ears to hear. *e.g.* Most bats call in ultrasound, so a bat flying past you seems silent.
- **Infrasound**: Sound that is too low for human ears to hear. *e.g.* Elephants make deep rumbles, and part of each rumble is infrasound.
- **Vibration**: A fast shaking back and forth. Animals can feel vibrations through a web, a leaf or the ground. *e.g.* A spider feels the vibration when a fly lands on her web.
- **Echo**: A sound that bounces off something and comes back to you. *e.g.* Shout in an empty hall, and you hear your shout come back as an echo.
- **Echolocation**: Finding where things are by making a sound and listening to its echoes. *e.g.* A bat calls, and the echo from a moth tells her where the moth is.
- **Eavesdropping**: Picking up a signal that was meant for someone else. *e.g.* A hunting bat hears a frog calling for a mate and flies towards the call.

## Explanations
### Signals travel on four channels · *defined*
**Definition:** A signal is something one animal does or makes that carries a message to another animal. A channel is the way the signal travels: smell, sight, sound or touch.

Every signal needs a sender and a receiver. The sender must make the signal, and the receiver must have a body that can pick it up. A smell needs a nose or an antenna. A colour needs an eye. A call needs an ear. A touch needs skin or hairs that feel. Most animals use more than one channel. A street dog growls, shows her teeth, raises the hair on her back and leaves scent marks. Humans are animals too, and we use all four channels. We speak, we smile, we hug, and our noses can tell family from strangers. This unit takes the channels one at a time, and then compares them.
- e.g. An ant leaves a scent trail to food. That is a message on the smell channel.
- e.g. A fan-throated lizard flashes a blue, black and orange throat. That is a message on the sight channel.
- e.g. A koel calls from a mango tree before the rains. That is a message on the sound channel.
- e.g. A bonnet macaque picks through her friend's fur. That is a message on the touch channel.
### Pheromones are messages made of smell · *defined*
**Definition:** A pheromone is a chemical that an animal gives off, which changes what other animals of the same kind do.

A pheromone is made of molecules, which are tiny particles far too small to see. The molecules leave the sender's body and drift on the air or stay on a surface. When they reach the antenna or nose of the right animal, that animal acts. A female silk moth gives off a pheromone called bombykol, and a male walks upwind until he finds her. A honeybee who stings gives off an alarm pheromone that smells like bananas, and other bees rush to the spot. Ants know their nestmates by the smell on their bodies. Some pheromones are made of small, light molecules. These spread fast and fade within minutes, which suits an alarm. Others are heavy and oily. These stay on a tree for days, which suits a mark that says who lives here. A smell also goes round corners and works in the dark. Its weak point is speed. A smell is slow to spread, and the sender cannot take it back or change it quickly.
- e.g. A tigress sprays a tree, and other tigers can read the mark for days.
- e.g. An ant who finds a dead beetle lays a scent trail, and her sisters follow it.
- e.g. A house cat rubs her cheek on a chair leg and leaves her own smell there.
### Colours and displays are messages for the eyes · *defined*
**Definition:** A display is a signal made for the eyes. It uses colour, shape or movement.

Light travels very fast and in straight lines. So a display reaches the receiver at once, and the receiver sees exactly where the sender is. A male fan-throated lizard in Maharashtra stands on a stone and flicks out a bright throat fan. A male fiddler crab on a Kerala mudflat waves one huge claw. Both displays are big, bright and repeated, because a small or dull signal is missed from far away. Some colours are warnings. The plain tiger butterfly is orange and black, and tastes bad to birds. A bird who tries one remembers the colours. A display has two weak points. It needs light, and it needs a clear view. A leaf, a wall or the night can block it. A hunter can see it too, so a bright animal is easier to catch. Many animals show their colours only when they need to. The lizard folds his fan away, and the peacock lowers his train.
- e.g. A peacock raises his train and shakes it in front of a peahen.
- e.g. A plain tiger butterfly's orange and black wings warn birds not to eat them.
- e.g. A street dog tucks her tail and lowers her body to show she does not want a fight.
### A dance is a message made of movement · *defined*
**Definition:** A dance is a display in which the order and the timing of the movements carry the message.

A pair of sarus cranes in a wet field bow, leap and call together. The dance tells each crane that the other is healthy and ready to nest. A male jumping spider lifts his front legs and steps from side to side in front of a female. If his steps are wrong, she may treat him as food. A honeybee dances on the comb in the dark hive. The bees who follow her learn where the flowers are. You will learn to read the bee dance in a later unit. Small frogs called dancing frogs live beside loud streams in the Western Ghats. A male calls, and he also stretches out a back leg and spreads his white foot. Scientists think the foot wave helps because the stream is so loud that a call alone is often lost. A dance costs energy and time. A dancer who can keep dancing shows that they are healthy and strong.
- e.g. Sarus cranes in Uttar Pradesh leap and bow to each other.
- e.g. A male jumping spider on a wall waves his front legs at a female.
- e.g. A dancing frog in Karnataka waves a white foot beside a noisy stream.
### Calls and songs, and the sounds humans cannot hear · *defined*
**Definition:** A call or a song is a signal made of sound. Sound spreads in every direction, passes round trees and works in the dark.

A sound is fast, and the sender can change it or stop it in a moment. That makes sound good for alarms and for calling over long distances. Its weak point is that a sound is gone as soon as it ends, and anyone nearby can hear it. Human ears hear only a part of all sound. Sound too high for us is called ultrasound. Bats, mice and many moths use it. Sound too low for us is called infrasound. Elephants make deep rumbles that can reach other elephants several kilometres away. Male humpback whales sing songs that last many minutes, and whales far away can hear them. Water carries sound more than four times as fast as air does. Each bottlenose dolphin has their own whistle, called a signature whistle. Other dolphins copy that whistle to call them, a little as we use a name.
- e.g. A male cricket rubs his wings together, and females walk towards the sound.
- e.g. Frogs in a paddy field call after the first monsoon rain, and each kind has a different call.
- e.g. An elephant mother rumbles, and her family far across the forest turns towards her.
### Touch and vibration · *defined*
**Definition:** A touch signal passes when two animals are in contact. A vibration signal passes through something they both touch, such as a web, a stem or the ground.

Touch is the most private channel, because only the animal who is touched receives it. Bonnet macaques sit and pick through each other's fur. This is called grooming. Grooming removes dirt and insects, and it also keeps friendships strong. Elephants greet by touching trunks to each other's mouths. A honeybee asks a sister for food by tapping her with her antennae. Vibration lets a touch travel a little further. A spider rests at the middle of her web with her feet on the threads. A struggling fly shakes the web one way, and the wind shakes it another way. A male spider who comes to visit plucks the threads in his own rhythm. That way she does not mistake him for food. Elephants also feel deep rumbles through the ground with their feet and trunks. The weak point of touch is distance. The sender has to be close.
- e.g. Two langurs sit on a temple wall, and one grooms the other's back.
- e.g. A mother dog licks her newborn pups.
- e.g. A spider runs along the exact thread that a trapped fly is shaking.
### Echolocation: finding things with echoes · *defined*
**Definition:** Echolocation means making a sound and using its echoes to find where things are.

An echo is a sound that has bounced off something and come back. A hunting bat makes very short, very loud calls in ultrasound. Each call bounces off a moth, a leaf or a wall. The echo from a near thing comes back sooner than the echo from a far thing. So the time between call and echo tells the bat the distance. Bats are not blind. They echolocate because eyes are little use for catching a small insect in the dark. Large fruit bats, such as the Indian flying fox, do not echolocate. They find fruit with their eyes and noses. Dolphins echolocate with clicks. A dolphin makes the clicks inside her nose passages, and a pad of fat in her forehead aims them forward. The echoes return through her lower jaw to her ears. The Ganges river dolphin is almost blind and lives in muddy water, so she finds fish almost entirely by echoes. Echolocation is not a message to another animal. The same animal is the sender and the receiver. Yet others can overhear it.
- e.g. A small bat circles a street lamp and catches insects that she cannot see.
- e.g. A Ganges river dolphin in the muddy water near Bhagalpur finds a fish by clicking.
- e.g. Some blind people click their tongues and hear where walls and doors are.
### Every channel has gains and costs · *defined*
**Definition:** No channel is the best everywhere. Each one works well in some conditions and badly in others.

Compare the four. A smell lasts after the sender leaves and goes round corners, but it is slow and cannot be changed quickly. A display is instant and shows exactly where the sender is, but it needs light and a clear view. A sound is fast, works in the dark and goes round trees. But it ends at once, and everyone nearby hears it. A touch is private and hard to mistake, but the sender must be close. So the place decides the channel. Moths fly at night in forests, and they use smell. Lizards live on open ground in bright sun, and they use colour. Frogs call at night, when colours are hard to see. Animals who live in a difficult place often use two channels together. The dancing frog beside a loud stream does this.
- e.g. In a thick forest at night, smell and sound work, but a display does not.
- e.g. On open ground at noon, a display works well.
- e.g. In muddy river water, sound works, but sight does not.
### A signal can be overheard, and a signal can be false · *defined*
**Definition:** Eavesdropping means picking up a signal that was meant for someone else. A false signal is one that leads the receiver to do something that helps the sender and harms the receiver.

A signal goes out into the world, and the sender has no control over who receives it. In Central America a male túngara frog calls to females. A hunting bat hears the same call and flies to him. Many tiger moths have ears that hear bat calls. When a bat comes near, such a moth dives away, and some kinds click back and spoil the bat's echoes. Signals can also be false. A koel lays her egg in a crow's nest. The koel chick begs so well that the crows feed them. In the Kalahari Desert, fork-tailed drongos give alarm calls when no danger is near. Then they take the food that frightened meerkats drop. False signals cannot become too common. If most alarm calls were false, the receivers would stop responding to them.
- e.g. A bat hears a calling frog and hunts him.
- e.g. A tiger moth hears a bat and drops out of the way.
- e.g. A koel chick in a crow's nest begs, and the crows feed them.
### Does a signal tell, or does it push? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that signals change what receivers do. They disagree about the best way to describe what a signal is.

When a monkey gives an alarm call and the others climb, what has passed between them? There are two main ways to describe it, and both are used by working scientists.
- e.g. A vervet monkey gives a 'leopard' call and the others climb trees.
- e.g. A male frog's deep call draws a female towards him.
- **Scientists who speak of information, such as Dorothy Cheney and Robert Seyfarth:** A signal carries information. The alarm call tells the listeners that a leopard is near. They use what they have learned to choose what to do.
- **Scientists who speak of influence, such as Richard Dawkins and John Krebs in 1978:** A signal is a way for the sender to change the receiver's behaviour. On this view we should ask what the signal makes the receiver do, and whom that helps.
- **A third view:** Both are true. Animals think, feel and choose. A sender signals for their own reasons, and a receiver weighs the signal and decides. Most signals last only because both sides gain from them.

## Mostly agreed
- Animals signal by smell, sight, sound and touch, and many use several channels at once. *How we know:* Scientists have watched and recorded signals in thousands of kinds of animals, from ants to whales.
- Pheromones are real chemicals, and each has a particular make-up. *How we know:* In 1959 Adolf Butenandt's team purified the silk moth pheromone bombykol. A tiny amount of this one chemical made male moths flutter their wings.
- Bats and toothed whales such as dolphins find things by echolocation. *How we know:* From 1938 Donald Griffin used a detector that picks up ultrasound, and found that flying bats call all the time. He and Robert Galambos then tested bats among wires. Bats with their ears or mouths covered crashed. Bats with their eyes covered did not.
- Many animals use sounds that humans cannot hear. *How we know:* Special microphones for very high and very low sounds record bat calls, mouse songs and elephant rumbles.
- A signal can be overheard by animals it was not meant for. *How we know:* Michael Ryan and Merlin Tuttle showed that fringe-lipped bats fly to loudspeakers playing túngara frog calls.
- The place where an animal lives shapes the signals they use. *How we know:* Dancing frogs beside loud streams add a foot wave to their call. Animals of the night and of muddy water depend on smell and sound.

## How we found out
**Jean-Henri Fabre in France, then Adolf Butenandt and his team in Germany, and Peter Karlson and Martin Lüscher, who made the word pheromone, Fabre published his moth tests in 1900. Butenandt's team named bombykol in 1959, and the word pheromone was made in the same year..** For a long time people thought a male moth found a female by seeing her or hearing her. More than a hundred years ago, in the south of France, the insect watcher Jean-Henri Fabre tested this. A female great peacock moth came out of her cocoon in his study. He put her under a wire cover. That night dozens of large male moths flew in through the window. Fabre then tried a female under a glass cover, where males could see her. He wrote that they ignored her, and went to the place where she had rested before. So sight was not what brought them. Something that passed through wire, but not through glass, was drawing them. Fabre could not say what. The answer came in 1959. The German chemist Adolf Butenandt and his team had worked on the problem for about twenty years. They used the scent glands of around 500,000 female silk moths. They purified one chemical and named it bombykol. A trace of bombykol alone made male silk moths flutter and walk towards it. In the same year Peter Karlson and Martin Lüscher gave such chemicals the name pheromones.  
*What people believed before:* That a male moth finds a female by seeing or hearing her. Fabre showed that males ignored a female they could see under glass. Butenandt's team then showed that one chemical from her body is enough to draw the males.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Bats are blind.** (A common saying: 'as blind as a bat'.; *rejected*)  
  For: Bats fly at night and many have small eyes.  
  Against: All bats have working eyes. Large fruit bats find food mainly by sight and smell. Small bats echolocate as well as see.
- **A peahen chooses the peacock with the most eyespots on his train.** (Marion Petrie and her team in England, in 1991.; *debated*)  
  For: In their studies, males with more eyespots won more mates, and males with some eyespots cut off did worse.  
  Against: A seven-year study in Japan by Mariko Takahashi's team, published in 2008, found no such choice. The train matters, but what peahens look for is still argued.
- **Humans have pheromones that change how other people behave.** (Many perfume advertisements, and some early studies.; *debated*)  
  For: Humans have many scent glands, and babies can find their mother by smell.  
  Against: No human pheromone has yet been purified and shown to work in careful tests. The zoologist Tristram Wyatt reviewed the evidence in 2015 and found none that held up.
- **Animal signals are only outbursts of feeling and say nothing about the world.** (Many scientists before the 1970s.; *rejected*)  
  For: A scream of fear and a purr of comfort do come with strong feelings.  
  Against: Vervet monkeys give different alarm calls for leopards, eagles and snakes, and listeners respond to each in the right way. A honeybee's dance tells her sisters where food is.
- **Plants send and receive signals too.** (Plant scientists such as Richard Karban.; *debated*)  
  For: Sagebrush plants that are cut give off chemicals, and neighbours that sense them are eaten less by insects. Plants signal, sense and respond.  
  Against: Some scientists say the neighbour is only picking up a leak. They keep the word 'signal' for messages that evolved to be sent.

## Roots
In this unit you left smell marks on paper and found a steel plate by its echo. Here we ask what a message is, and whether a message needs its sender to be there.

**Deep truth.** In the Smell Mark Test, your nose found the clove mark long after the sender had left the room. A call or a wave ends when the sender stops. A smell mark goes on giving its message while the sender is far away. So one tigress can warn others off many trees at once, without being at any of them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a message as something that passes between two beings who are together, as when we talk. A scent mark needs no meeting. The sender and the receiver may never see each other.  
*Evidence:* In the Smell Mark Test the grown-up who made the marks stayed away. The clove mark was usually still found after 60 minutes. Tigers, dogs and cats leave marks that others read hours or days later. Fabre wrote that male moths went to the place where a female had rested, after she had been moved.

- *who am i:* You send signals all day on all four channels. You choose some of them on purpose, and you send others without meaning to. You are also an animal who writes. A written note carries your message while you are away.
- *what is life:* No living being lives without sending and receiving. Even bacteria give off chemicals that other bacteria sense and act on.
- *what is this world:* The air, the water and the ground are full of messages. Most were not meant for us, and many are beyond our senses.

A signal is never the work of one being. A moth's pheromone does nothing without an antenna built to catch it. A peacock's train does nothing without a peahen's eye. Over millions of years, senders and receivers have shaped each other's bodies. Many sages taught that all beings are one life in many bodies. The science here says something close. All animals share ancestors, and that is partly why a dog's growl makes sense to you.

- **Rig Veda 1.164.45:** “चत्वारि वाक्परिमिता पदानि तानि विदुर्ब्राह्मणा ये मनीषिणः । / गुहा त्रीणि निहिता नेङ्गयन्ति तुरीयं वाचो मनुष्या वदन्ति” The hymn says that speech is measured out in four parts. Three parts are kept hidden, and humans speak only the fourth. (The hymn is about sacred speech, and it is not about animals. Yet its picture fits what this unit found. The talk that humans can hear is a small part of all the signalling around us. Bat calls, elephant rumbles and pheromones were hidden from us until instruments found them.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 17, 'Autumn Floods':** “莊子曰：「鯈魚出游從容，是魚樂也。」 / 惠子曰：「子非魚，安知魚之樂？」 / 莊子曰：「子非我，安知我不知魚之樂？」” Zhuangzi and his friend Huizi stand on a bridge over the Hao river. Zhuangzi says the fish are enjoying themselves. Huizi asks: you are not a fish, so how do you know what fish enjoy? (Scientists meet Huizi's question with every signal. They cannot ask a frog what his foot wave means. They can only test what other frogs do when they see it.)
- Science (settled): Even bacteria signal. Many kinds give off chemicals and sense how much has built up around them. When there are enough of them, they act together. This is called quorum sensing.
- Science (open): In 1974 the philosopher Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat. He argued that we could learn everything about echolocation and still not know how it feels to the bat.
- Other view, Many biologists, following a rule that C. Lloyd Morgan set out in 1894: A tigress who sprays a tree may not intend to tell anyone anything. Marking may be an urge passed down from her ancestors, because tigers who marked their land had fewer fights. On this view we should not say she 'sends a message' unless simpler explanations fail.
- Practice, Two minutes of receiving (3 min): Sit still together and close your eyes. For half a minute, notice every sound that reaches you. Now notice every smell. Now notice everything that is touching your skin. Open your eyes and notice what you see. Each of these reached you from something else. Which ones were meant for you?
- Ask: In the Smell Mark Test, the clove mark gave its message with nobody there to send it. Where was the message during that hour?
- Ask: A bat hears a night full of echoes that you cannot hear. Is the bat's night the same night as yours?
- Ask: When you write a note and leave the room, the note carries your message without you there. What part of you do you think stays in it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A tigress marks a tree and no other tiger ever passes by. Was the mark a message? (hint: Think about a letter that is written but never opened.)
- *deep:* A bat's call is overheard by a moth, who escapes. Did the bat send the moth a message? (hint: The bat did not gain from it. Does a message need the sender to want it received?)
- *big:* Elephants and bats use sounds we cannot hear. What else might be passing around us, unnoticed? (hint: People found these signals only after building new instruments.)
- *big:* If you could add one new sense to your body, which signals would you want to pick up? (hint: Think about what a dog learns at a lamp post, or a bat on a dark night.)
- *deep:* Writing lets a human message last after the sender has left. Is writing a kind of scent mark? (hint: Compare what you found in the Smell Mark Test with a note left on the fridge.)
- *big:* People have beamed radio messages into space. If someone is out there, would their bodies be able to receive them? (hint: A signal needs a receiver whose senses fit it. A moth's pheromone means nothing to a crow.)

## Story: The White Foot at the Stream
*Nethra, Sudha Chikkamma and their frog count are made up. These facts are real: dancing frogs live beside fast streams in the Western Ghats, they are active by day, the males call and also stretch out a back leg and spread a white foot, and one kind is named after Kottigehara in Karnataka. Scientists who studied these frogs found that the foot wave goes together with the call beside noisy water.*

Nethra had come to the hills near Kottigehara to count frogs with her aunt. So far she had counted none.

Her aunt, Sudha Chikkamma, studied frogs for a college in Mysuru. Every year, when the big rains ended, she came to this stream with a notebook, a torch and a tin of ragi biscuits. This year she had brought Nethra, who was nine, and who had promised not to complain about leeches.

The stream was loud. It came down over black rocks, white and fast, and its noise filled the whole valley. To say anything, they had to put their mouths close to each other's ears.

"They are here," Chikkamma shouted. "Each one is as small as your thumb and as brown as the rock. The males call, tick-tick-tick, like a tiny clock being wound. A call tells the females where he is. It tells other males that his rock is taken."

Nethra listened. She heard the stream, and nothing else.

"I am going across to look upstream," Chikkamma shouted. "Sit on this flat rock and watch. If you find one, call me." She walked over a fallen tree to the far bank, and soon she was twenty steps away, bent over her notebook.

Nethra sat. Sunlight came down through the leaves in small round patches. She stared at the wet rocks until her eyes ached. Leaf. Rock. Rock. Moss.

Then one piece of rock blinked.

He was sitting right at the edge of the water, no bigger than the top of her thumb. As she watched, the skin under his chin swelled into a small white bubble, and sank, and swelled again. He was calling. She could see him calling. She could not hear one tick of it.

She stood up carefully. "Chikkamma!" she shouted. Her aunt did not turn.

"CHIKKAMMA!" The stream took her voice the way it had taken the frog's. Twenty steps away, her aunt turned a page.

Nethra thought of throwing a pebble, but a pebble might frighten him. She thought of running across the fallen tree, but then she would lose the spot. Her hand went to the white cotton towel round her neck. She pulled it off, held it high in both hands, and swept it slowly from side to side.

Chikkamma's head came up at once. She waved, shut her notebook, and started back across the tree.

Nethra lowered the towel and looked for the frog. He was still there. And now he did something she had never seen a frog do.

He lifted one back leg. He stretched it out behind him, straight as a twig. Then he opened his toes. The skin between them was white, as white as her towel. He held the foot there, a small bright fan against the dark rock, and folded it away. Then he did it again.

On a rock on the other side of the water, something white opened and closed. A second frog had answered.

Chikkamma dropped down beside her, breathing hard. Nethra pointed at the frog. Then she pointed at her towel. Then she pointed at the white foot, which was opening again.

Her aunt laughed. Nethra could see the laugh but not hear it. Chikkamma put her mouth to Nethra's ear. "Yes," she shouted. "The very same reason."

That evening they sat on the verandah of the forest guard's house, where it was quiet enough to talk. "He does call," Chikkamma said. "A female very near him can hear it. But beside that water, most of a call is lost. So he sends the message a second way. A stream cannot drown a white foot."

"Why white?" asked Nethra.

"What colour was your towel? Would I have looked up for a brown one?"

Nethra thought about it. "But at night nobody could see his foot."

"That is right. A wave needs light. These frogs are busy in the daytime, so a wave works for them. Most frogs call at night, and they never wave at all."

Nethra opened her own notebook. Under the date she wrote: Frogs seen, 2. Messages seen, 3.

"Three?" said Chikkamma, reading over her shoulder.

"Two feet," said Nethra, "and one towel."

The next morning she went back to the flat rock alone and sat very still. After a while the frog lifted his leg and spread his white foot. Nethra kept her towel in her lap. She did not know yet what a wave from her would say to him.

**For the parent:** The stream drowned Nethra's shout, so she waved a white towel. Then she saw the frog do the same thing with his foot. When one channel is blocked, a signal has to travel on another.

- Ask: Why did the wave reach across the stream when the shout did not?
- Ask: Why does a white foot work better than a brown one on a dark rock?
- Ask: Where in your home would a wave fail and a call work?

### g2 · Nestmate or Stranger? The smell pairs game (15 min)
- **rules:** A grown-up is the packer. The packer cuts eight paper squares about 8 centimetres wide from a notebook page.; The packer puts a smell on each square and folds it shut. Two squares get one crushed clove each. Two get a pinch of cumin seeds (jeera). Two get a pinch of coffee or tea powder. Two get one crushed cardamom pod (elaichi).; The packer sticks a letter on each packet and secretly writes down which letters are pairs. Then the packer lays the packets in two rows.; On your turn, pick up two packets and sniff each one. Do not open them.; If you think they smell the same, say 'nestmates'. The packer checks the list. Then the next player has a turn.; The game ends when all four pairs are found. The player with the most pairs wins. If you play alone, count your turns and try to use fewer next time.

- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet: 4 pairs to find:** Pair 1: packet ___ and packet ___. Found by ___; Pair 2: packet ___ and packet ___. Found by ___; Pair 3: packet ___ and packet ___. Found by ___; Pair 4: packet ___ and packet ___. Found by ___

- **s3 (3 min)**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Smell Mark Test: how long does a smell message last? (30 min)
- **question:** A smell mark is left on paper and the sender goes away. Can a nose still find the mark after 10 minutes, after 30 minutes and after 60 minutes? Does a vinegar mark last as long as a clove mark?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After one hour, will you still find the vinegar mark? Will you still find the clove mark? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same paper for all eight squares, and make every square the same size. Put the same amount of liquid on every square in the vinegar set: 5 drops of vinegar on one, and 5 drops of water on each of the other three. Keep both sets in the same room, away from the fan and the window. The same person sniffs every time, with eyes covered, and the squares come in a mixed-up order. Sniff the vinegar set first each time, because clove is strong. Only two things change: the kind of mark, and the time since it was made.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows, for 0, 10, 30 and 60 minutes. For each time, write a tick or a cross for every try. There are two tries with the vinegar set and two with the clove set.
- **results table:** Minutes since the marks were made | Vinegar mark found? Try 1 and try 2 | Clove mark found? Try 1 and try 2
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the vinegar and the clove. Let them sniff each once. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will leave a vinegar mark on one paper square and a clove mark on another. Then we walk away. After one hour, will your nose still find the vinegar mark? Will it find the clove mark? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Sniff the vinegar and the clove. Which smell will last one hour? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that mark will last longer? (good: Clove smells stronger.; Vinegar is wet and will dry up.; The clove leaves bits and oil on the paper.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. If you have no vinegar, use fresh lemon juice.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Write V1 to V4 on four squares and C1 to C4 on the other four. Send your child out. Put 5 drops of vinegar on one V square and 5 drops of water on the other three. Crush the clove on one C square with the spoon and rub for 10 seconds. Tip the clove bits into the bin. Note the two marked numbers and the time.  
  *Parent says:* Please wait outside for two minutes. I am the sender. I will mark one square in each set, and I will not tell you which.  
  *Child mode:* Number the squares. Then wait outside. A grown-up marks one square in each set.
  - Note: Put each set on its own plate, with the squares not touching. Keep the two plates about two metres apart. Use the spoon on the clove, not your fingers. Then your hands will not carry the smell to the plain squares. The water on the plain V squares makes all four equally damp.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover your child's eyes. Lift each V square by one corner, in a mixed-up order. Hold the middle about 2 centimetres under the nose for two slow breaths. Ask which of the four carried the mark. Do this twice. Then do the same with the C squares. Record ticks and crosses for 0 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes covered. I will bring four squares, one at a time. One of them carries the mark. Tell me if it is the first, second, third or fourth. You may also say that none of them smells.  
  *Child mode:* Cover your eyes. Sniff four squares. Say which one has the smell.
  - Note: By guessing alone, a child would be right about 1 time in 4. Two right answers out of two is good evidence that the mark can still be found.
  - Check: Why are your eyes covered during the Smell Mark Test? → **So only my nose can find the mark**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When 10 minutes have passed since marking, test both sets again, twice each, in a new mixed-up order. Record the results.  
  *Parent says:* Ten minutes have passed, and the sender has been away all that time. Eyes covered again. Vinegar set first.  
  *Child mode:* Ten minutes have passed. Cover your eyes and sniff again.
  - Wait: Wait 10 minutes after the marks were made
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When 30 minutes have passed since marking, test both sets again, twice each. Record the results.  
  *Parent says:* Thirty minutes now. Take two breaths of fresh air first. Then tell me which square carries the mark, or say none.  
  *Child mode:* Thirty minutes have passed. Sniff both sets again.
  - Note: Use the waiting time for the term cards or the story. If your child says 'none', record a cross. That is a real result.
  - Wait: Wait until 30 minutes after the marks were made
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When 60 minutes have passed since marking, test both sets once more, twice each. Record the results. Then show your child which squares were marked.  
  *Parent says:* This is the last test, one hour after I made the marks. Vinegar set first, then clove.  
  *Child mode:* One hour has passed. Sniff both sets once more.
  - Wait: Wait until 60 minutes after the marks were made
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they found, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your ticks and crosses. Say what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think it happened. Which mark would suit an alarm that must end quickly? Which would suit a mark that says 'I live here'?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you found. Say why.
  - Ask: Why might one smell fade faster than another? (good: Its smell particles leave the paper faster.; It dried up.; The clove left oil that stays.)
  - Ask: The sender was out of the room. How did the message still reach you? (good: The smell stayed on the paper.; The mark does not need the sender to be there.)
  - Note: In most homes the vinegar mark is hard to find after 30 to 60 minutes. The clove mark is still clear the next morning. Write down what you really found, even if it differs. The reason: a smell is made of molecules that leave the paper and float into the nose. The molecules of vinegar's smell are small and leave quickly, so the mark soon runs out. Clove oil has heavier molecules that leave slowly, so the mark lasts. Animals use both kinds. In some ants an alarm pheromone fades in about a minute. A tiger's mark on a tree lasts for days. Leave the clove square out and sniff it again tomorrow.
  - Think: Where would an animal put a scent mark so that it lasts as long as possible?
### e2 · The Echo Plate Test: can you find a thing by sound alone? (25 min)
- **question:** Your eyes are covered. Can you tell whether a steel plate is in front of your face by saying 'shhh' and listening? Does it still work when the plate is 30 centimetres or 60 centimetres away?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. With your eyes covered, will you be able to tell when the plate is there? At which distance will it stop working: 10, 30 or 60 centimetres? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same plate, the same room and the same 'shhh' every time. Each round has 10 turns: 5 with the plate and 5 without, in a mixed-up order that the listener cannot know. The helper steps forward the same way on every turn, so that footsteps give nothing away. Keep the room quiet. Do one round at 10 centimetres with no 'shhh' at all, to check that the echo is what helps. Only the distance and the sound change.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows, one for each round. Write the distance, whether you made the 'shhh' sound, and how many of the 10 answers were right.
- **results table:** Round | Distance of the plate | Sound made | Right answers out of 10
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the plate and the cloth. Explain the test. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* You will cover your eyes and say a long 'shhh'. Sometimes I will hold this plate in front of your face, and sometimes I will not. Will you be able to tell? Up to what distance? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Your eyes will be covered. Can you tell when a plate is near? Why?
  - Ask: What could tell you that the plate is there? (good: The 'shhh' might sound different.; The sound might bounce back.; I might feel the air change.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Children often say they will feel the plate. The last round tests that idea.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child hold the plate with eyes open. They say a steady, loud 'shhh'. They move the plate slowly from arm's length to 5 centimetres from the mouth, and back. Do this five times.  
  *Parent says:* Keep your 'shhh' steady, like air leaking from a tyre. Move the plate slowly towards your mouth and away again. Listen hard. Does anything about the sound change?  
  *Child mode:* Say 'shhh'. Move the plate close, then away. Listen for a change.
  - Note: Most people hear the 'shhh' become sharper or more hollow when the plate is within about 20 centimetres. Let your child find their own words for the change. A quick tongue click also works, if your child prefers it.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 1, at 10 centimetres. Write P five times and N five times in a mixed-up order. Cover your child's eyes. On each turn they start the 'shhh'. Step forward. On P turns hold the plate upright 10 centimetres from the face. On N turns keep it behind your back. Count three, ask 'plate or no plate?', and step back. Record each answer.  
  *Parent says:* Round 1. Start your 'shhh' when I say go. I will step forward every time. Tell me 'plate' or 'no plate'. Go.  
  *Child mode:* Round 1. Say 'shhh'. Listen. Say 'plate' or 'no plate'.
  - Note: Measure 10 centimetres once with the ruler, and then judge it by eye. Do not let the plate touch hair or clothes. Do not say whether each answer is right until the round ends.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 2, at 30 centimetres. Write a new mixed-up order of five P and five N. Do 10 turns the same way and record the score.  
  *Parent says:* Round 2. The plate will now be further away, about as long as your ruler. Same rules. Go.  
  *Child mode:* Round 2. The plate is further away now. Listen carefully.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 3, at 60 centimetres. Write a new mixed-up order. Do 10 turns the same way and record the score.  
  *Parent says:* Round 3. The plate will be two ruler lengths away. It may be hard. Give your best answer every time.  
  *Child mode:* Round 3. The plate is far now. Give your best answer.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 4, at 10 centimetres with no sound. Your child keeps their mouth closed and stays silent. Do 10 turns in a new mixed-up order and record the score.  
  *Parent says:* Round 4. The plate is back at 10 centimetres. This time make no sound at all. Keep your mouth closed. Tell me 'plate' or 'no plate'.  
  *Child mode:* Round 4. Make no sound this time. Say 'plate' or 'no plate'.
  - Check: Why does the Echo Plate Test have one round with no 'shhh' sound? → **To check that the echo is what helps**
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the four scores together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they found, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Guessing alone gives about 5 right out of 10. Look at your four scores. Say what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you found. Say why.
  - Ask: Which scores are clearly better than guessing? (good: The ones well above 5 out of 10.; The 10 centimetre round with 'shhh'.)
  - Ask: Why might the far plate be harder to find? (good: The echo is weaker.; The echo mixes with the echoes from the walls.)
  - Note: With the 'shhh', many people score 8 to 10 at 10 centimetres and less at 30 centimetres. At 60 centimetres and in silence, most score about 5. Write down what you really found. The reason: the 'shhh' bounces off the plate and returns to the ears, mixed with the sound coming straight from the mouth. The mix sounds different from the 'shhh' alone. From further away the echo is weaker, and a room's own echoes hide it. A bat does the same thing far better. She uses short, loud calls and large ears, and her brain times each echo.
  - Think: A bat can find a moth several metres away. What does she have that you do not?

### a1 · Signal Spotting: ten minutes at the window (10 min)
- **record:** A table with four rows: smell, sight, sound and touch. Put one stroke for every signal you notice. Write who sent each one if you can.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the table with four rows. Choose a place to watch from. If the street is empty, watch your own family.  
  *Parent says:* A signal is anything one animal does that carries a message to another. Humans are animals, so our waves, horns and hugs count. Which row do you think will fill up fastest?

  *Parent does:* Watch for five minutes. Each time anyone sends a signal, your child says what it was and puts a stroke in the right row.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me each signal as you spot it. A crow calling is sound. A person waving is sight. A dog sniffing a wall is smell. A mother holding a child's hand is touch.  
  *Child mode:* Watch for five minutes. Put a stroke for every signal.
  - Note: There is no garden needed. A street, a corridor or your own kitchen will give plenty of signals. Do not worry about missing some.

  *Parent does:* Count the strokes in each row. Talk about the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Which row has the most strokes? Which has the fewest? Do you think there were really so few smell signals, or did we miss them?  
  *Child mode:* Count each row. Which channel won? Which signals did you miss?
  - Ask: Why is the smell row nearly empty? (good: Our noses are not good enough to notice them.; We cannot see a smell.; The dog noticed smells that we missed.)
  - Note: The count shows what a human watcher can notice. A dog at the same window would fill the smell row first.
### a2 · Three Strings: feel a message the way a spider does (15 min)
- **record:** Write how many of the 9 plucks you named correctly. Then write the two codes you used and whether they were understood.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tie one end of each string to a different chair. Bring the three free ends to one spot. Your child loops one string round each of three fingers: the first, the middle and the ring finger. They pull all three tight.  
  *Parent says:* A spider sits at the middle of her web with her feet on the threads. You are the spider. Your three fingers are three of her feet. Keep the strings tight.  
  *Child mode:* Loop one string on each of three fingers. Pull them tight.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child shuts their eyes. Pluck one string gently near the chair with the pencil. Your child says which finger felt it. Do 9 plucks, three on each string in a mixed-up order. Count the right answers.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes shut. I will pluck one string. Tell me which finger felt it most: first, middle or ring.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Feel the pluck. Say which finger felt it.
  - Note: The plucked string shakes much more than the other two, so most children get 8 or 9 right. Pluck softly and walk quietly.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Agree two codes: one slow pluck means 'wind', and three quick plucks means 'fly'. Send six messages in a mixed-up order on any string. Your child names the string and the message. Then swap places.  
  *Parent says:* A spider must tell a trapped fly from the wind. A fly struggles in quick bursts. Now each pluck has a meaning. One slow pluck is wind. Three quick plucks are a fly.  
  *Child mode:* One slow pluck means wind. Three quick plucks mean fly. Which is this?
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the strings down. Talk about the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* You could not see or hear the message, yet you read it. What carried it? Could I have sent it if you had let go of the strings?  
  *Child mode:* What carried the message to your fingers? What if you let go?
  - Ask: What does a vibration signal need that a call does not? (good: Something that joins the sender and the receiver.; A thread, a stem or the ground between them.)
  - Check: A spider is at the middle of her web. How does she know where a trapped fly is? → **She feels which thread is shaking most**
### a3 · The Waving Test: how big must a signal be? (15 min)
- **record:** A table with three rows: one finger, whole hand, and arm with a bright cloth. Write how many of the 3 signals were read correctly for each.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the curtains or switch off the lights so that the room is dim but safe to walk in. Stand at the two ends. Agree the three movements. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* A fiddler crab waves a huge claw, and a peacock raises a huge train. Why so big? We will send three movements with a finger, a hand and a whole arm with a cloth. Which will you read most easily, and why?  
  *Child mode:* We will send signals across a dim room. Which size will be easiest? Why?
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The sender shows each signal for a count of two. Send three with one finger, three with the whole hand and three with the arm and cloth. Mix up the movements. The receiver calls out what they saw. Record right or wrong. Then swap places.  
  *Parent says:* Watch me. I will show each movement only for a count of two. Call out 'up and down', 'side to side' or 'circle'.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the sender. Call out the movement you saw.
  - Note: If every signal is easy to read, make the room dimmer or stand further apart. The corridor outside your flat may be longer. The finger signals should become hard first.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the three rows. Talk about the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Which size did you read best? A big bright signal is easy for a mate to see. Who else can see it easily?  
  *Child mode:* Which size worked best? Who else could see a big signal?
  - Ask: What is the danger of a big, bright signal? (good: A hunter can see it too.; It takes more energy.)
  - Check: Why does a male fiddler crab wave his biggest claw, and not a small leg? → **A big thing is seen from far away**

### p1 · Our Family Signal Code: six signals in three days (30 min, 3 days)
- **record:** A code page with six signals and their meanings. A log with one line for each time a signal was sent: which signal, where, and whether it was understood.

  *Parent does:* Day 1. Choose six messages your family often needs. Some ideas are 'food is ready', 'come here', 'I need help', 'be quiet', 'yes' and 'no'. Invent one signal without words for each. Use at least three channels. Write the code and teach it to everyone at home.  
  *Parent says:* We need six signals with no words. Some must work when the receiver is in another room. Some must work when the mixer is running. Which channel suits each message?  
  *Child mode:* Invent six signals with no words. Use sight, sound and touch.
  - Note: Good codes mix channels: two taps on a tumbler, a cloth hung on a door handle, a squeeze of the hand. Let your child choose, even if a signal looks likely to fail. Finding out is the point.

  *Child mode:* Use your signals all day. Log a tick or a cross each time.
  - Wait: Do this the day after you invent the code
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 3. Count the ticks and crosses for each signal. For each failure, work out why: was it missed, or mixed up with another signal? Change the signals that failed and say why the new ones should work better.

  *Child mode:* Count the ticks. Which signal failed? Why? Make it better.
  - Ask: What made your best signal work so well? (good: It was big or loud enough to notice.; It was different from all the others.; It worked even when the receiver was not looking.)

### c1 · Message Through the Noise: reach a listener who cannot hear you (20 min)
- **problem:** A dancing frog lives beside a stream so loud that his call is often lost. He adds a second signal: a white foot. Your problem is like his. A receiver sits five big steps away, facing a wall, with music playing. Send them one of three messages, 'come', 'stay' or 'danger', so that they get it right every time.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own way. It must still work if the lights go out, or if someone trips over the string. Draw your design. Test it with 9 messages in a mixed-up order, and write how many the receiver got right.
- **constraints:** No voice and no phone.; The receiver faces the wall and does not turn round.; Music or a mixer is on the whole time.; The sender stays five big steps away.; The receiver must name the message within 10 seconds.
- **general:** Shine a torch on the wall in front of the receiver, with one, two or three flashes.; Tie a string to the receiver's wrist or chair and send tugs along it.; Stamp on the floor so that the receiver feels it through their feet.
- **creative:** Make shadow shapes on the receiver's wall with a torch and your hand: a different shape for each message.; Use two channels together, such as tugs and flashes, so that the message arrives even if one channel fails.; Roll a ball of newspaper past the receiver's feet: one ball, two balls or three.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Set up the chair, the wall and the music. Ask which channels are blocked and which are still open.  
  *Parent says:* The receiver cannot hear you and is not looking at you. Which channels are blocked? Which are still open? List every idea, even odd ones.  
  *Child mode:* The receiver cannot hear or see you. Which channels are left?
  - Ask: Which channels can still reach the receiver? (good: Touch, through a string or the floor.; Sight, if the signal lands on the wall in front of them.; Smell, but it would be slow.)
  - Note: Keep the music at a normal volume. It only needs to cover a tap or a whisper.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child chooses one idea and builds it. Agree a code for the three messages: 'come', 'stay' and 'danger'.  
  *Parent says:* Choose your best idea and build it. The three messages must be easy to tell apart. How will 'danger' differ from 'come'?  
  *Child mode:* Build your best idea. Make three signals that differ clearly.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test it. Send 9 messages, three of each kind, in a mixed-up order. The receiver calls out each message within 10 seconds. Record right or wrong.  
  *Parent says:* Now we test. I will send nine messages. Call out each one as soon as you know it.  
  *Child mode:* Test it with nine messages. Count how many arrive correctly.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the score. Change one thing to make it better, or add a second channel. Test three more messages. Then talk about the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Which messages went wrong, and why? Change one thing and try again. Would your signal still work in the dark?  
  *Child mode:* What went wrong? Change one thing. Test it again.
  - Ask: Why might an animal use two channels for one message? (good: If one channel is blocked, the other still gets through.; Two signals together are harder to miss.)

## Worksheets
### Signals and channels (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a signal?  
   Options: Something an animal does that carries a message / Any loud noise in a forest / A kind of animal that lives in groups / The food an animal carries home  
   **Answer:** Something an animal does that carries a message. *Why:* A signal carries a message from a sender to a receiver. It can be a smell, a colour, a movement, a sound or a touch. It does not have to be loud.
2. (mcq) Which body part does a male moth use to pick up a female's pheromone?  
   Options: His antennae / His eyes / His wings / His feet  
   **Answer:** His antennae. *Why:* A male moth's feathery antennae are covered with tiny hairs that catch the pheromone from the air. If you chose eyes, remember that moths find mates in the dark.
3. (fill) A chemical that an animal gives off, which changes what others of the same kind do, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pheromone']. *Why:* A pheromone is a smell signal made of one or more chemicals. Silk moths, ants and honeybees all use pheromones.
4. (truefalse) All bats are blind.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every kind of bat has working eyes. Large fruit bats find food mainly by sight and smell. Small bats echolocate as well as see.
5. (match) Match each signal to its channel.  
   **Answer:** A tigress sprays a tree → Smell; A lizard flicks out a bright throat fan → Sight; A cricket rubs his wings together → Sound; A macaque picks through her friend's fur → Touch. *Why:* Ask which body part of the receiver picks the signal up. A nose picks up the spray, an eye the fan, an ear the cricket's song, and skin the grooming.
6. (sort) Sort these signals. Does each one work in the dark, or does it need light?  
   **Answer:** a scent mark on a tree → Works in the dark; a frog's call → Works in the dark; a bat's echolocation → Works in the dark; a peacock's display → Needs light; a lizard's throat fan → Needs light; a butterfly's warning colours → Needs light. *Why:* Smells and sounds do not need light. Colours and displays are picked up by eyes, and eyes need light.
7. (mcq) In the Smell Mark Test, three plain squares got 5 drops of water. Why?  
   Options: So all four squares were equally damp / So the vinegar smelled stronger / So the paper would not burn / So the clove would stick  
   **Answer:** So all four squares were equally damp. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. With water on the plain squares, the only difference between the four squares was the smell of vinegar.
8. (truefalse) A scent mark can be read after the animal who made it has gone away.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The smell stays on the tree or the wall. That is what makes a scent mark different from a call or a wave.
9. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how a bat finds a moth.  
   **Answer:** ['The bat makes a short, high call', 'The sound travels out to the moth', 'The sound bounces off the moth', "The echo reaches the bat's ears", 'The bat turns towards the moth']. *Why:* Echolocation is a call followed by an echo. The bat can only turn towards the moth after the echo has come back.
10. (fill) Sound that is too high for human ears to hear is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ultrasound']. *Why:* Ultrasound is above the highest sound we can hear. Most bats call in ultrasound. Sound too low for us is called infrasound.
11. (mcq) How does a spider at the middle of her web know that a fly has landed?  
   Options: She feels the threads shake / She sees the fly's colours / She smells the fly's pheromone / She hears the fly call  
   **Answer:** She feels the threads shake. *Why:* A struggling fly makes the threads vibrate, and the spider feels this through her feet. This is the touch channel, carried by the web.
12. (write) Explain in two sentences why a peacock's display would not work on a dark night.  
   **Answer:** A display is a signal for the eyes, and eyes need light. On a dark night the peahen could not see the colours or the shaking train. So the message would not reach her.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the display travels on the sight channel, and that this channel needs light.
13. (write) In the Smell Mark Test the sender was out of the room. Explain how the message still reached the receiver.  
   **Answer:** The sender left a smell on the paper. The smell stayed there after the sender had gone, and my nose picked it up later.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the signal was left on a surface. So the sender did not need to be there when it was received.
14. (draw) Draw a bat's call going out to a moth and the echo coming back. Label the call and the echo.  
   **Answer:** A bat and a moth with one arrow from the bat's mouth to the moth, labelled 'call', and one arrow from the moth back to the bat's ears, labelled 'echo'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the sound going both ways. The echo must start at the moth and end at the bat's ears.
15. (mcq) Which signal could reach an elephant family three kilometres away?  
   Options: A deep rumble / A touch of the trunk / A flap of the ears / A raised tail  
   **Answer:** A deep rumble. *Why:* Low sounds travel far. Elephant rumbles can reach other elephants several kilometres away. A touch needs contact, and a flap or a raised tail must be seen.

12. (write) Write one gain and one cost of a big, bright display.  
   **Answer:** Gain: a mate or a rival can see it from far away. In the Waving Test, the biggest signal is usually the easiest to read from far away.

   **Answer:** A human nose misses most smell signals, and smells cannot be seen from a window. A count of what we noticed is not a count of what was there.
14. (perspective) A busy road runs through a forest where frogs breed. People are deciding whether to stop lorries on monsoon nights.

A strong answer uses the fact that noise blocks the sound channel, and takes the other needs seriously.
15. (label) Label three parts of this dolphin's head: where clicks are made, the forehead's pad of fat, and the lower jaw.  
   **Answer:** ['Where the clicks are made: the nose passages, just below the blowhole', 'The pad of fat in the forehead: it aims the clicks forward', 'The lower jaw: the echoes come in through it and pass to the ears']. *Why:* A dolphin sends clicks out through her forehead and takes the echoes in through her jaw. Sound goes out by one path and comes back by another.
### Testing signals (challenge, 15 questions)

## Applications
- Farmers hang pheromone traps in cotton, vegetable and coconut fields. The trap gives off an insect pheromone, so the insects fly in, and the farmer sprays less poison.
- Ships and fishing boats use sonar. The machine sends a sound into the water and times the echo, as a dolphin does.
- A hospital ultrasound scan sends very high sound into the body and builds a picture from the echoes.
- Sniffer dogs at airports and railway stations find things by smells that people cannot notice.
- Road signs, school buses and auto-rickshaws use yellow and black, because these colours are easy to see. Wasps wear the same warning colours.
- Some blind people find their way by clicking their tongues. They listen to the echoes from walls, doors and parked cars.

## Open questions
- **What do the songs of humpback whales mean?** Only males sing, and all the males in one area sing the same song, which slowly changes. Scientists do not agree on what the song tells other whales.
- **What are sperm whales saying with their patterns of clicks?** Sperm whale families share patterns of clicks called codas. Teams of scientists are recording thousands of them, and nobody can yet say what they mean.
- **Do humans have pheromones?** Humans have many scent glands, but no human pheromone has been purified and shown to work in careful tests.
- **How much do plants signal to each other?** Plants give off chemicals when they are eaten, and neighbours respond. Scientists argue about how much of this is signalling and how common it is.
- **What is echolocation like for a bat?** We can measure a bat's calls and her brain. We have no way yet to find out how her echoes feel to her. They may feel like hearing, like seeing, or like something we have no word for.

## Connected maths
- In the Smell Mark Test, one square out of four carries the mark. Suppose you had no sense of smell and guessed all 8 times. About how many would you get right? If you got 7 right, was it luck? **About 2 right by guessing. Getting 7 right is very unlikely to be luck.**. One square in four is marked, so a guess is right about 1 time in 4. 8 divided by 4 is 2. A score of 7 is far above 2, so the nose was doing the work. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- Here are four scores out of 10 from the Echo Plate Test. At 10 centimetres the score was 9. At 30 centimetres it was 7. At 60 centimetres it was 5. In silence it was 5. Guessing gives about 5. At which distances did the echo clearly help? **At 10 centimetres and at 30 centimetres.**. 9 and 7 are both above 5, and 9 is far above it. The scores of 5 are what guessing gives. So at 60 centimetres and in silence there is no sign that the echo helped. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- Sound travels about 34 centimetres in one thousandth of a second. A bat hears the echo from a moth 10 thousandths of a second after her call. How far away is the moth? **170 centimetres, which is 1 metre 70 centimetres.**. In 10 thousandths of a second, sound travels 10 times 34, which is 340 centimetres. The sound went to the moth and back, so the moth is half of that distance away. Half of 340 is 170. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In one tenth of a second, sound travels 34 metres in air and 150 metres in water. A bat and a dolphin each hear an echo one tenth of a second after calling. How far away is each one's target? **The bat's target is 17 metres away. The dolphin's target is 75 metres away.**. The sound goes there and back, so halve each distance. Half of 34 is 17. Half of 150 is 75. Sound is faster in water, so the same wait means a target more than four times as far. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In Signal Spotting, a watcher counted 20 signals: 9 sound, 7 sight, 3 touch and 1 smell. What fraction were sound or sight? A dog watched the same street. Would the dog's fractions be the same? **16 out of 20, which is four fifths. The dog's fractions would probably be very different, with far more smell.**. 9 plus 7 is 16. 16 out of 20 is the same as 4 out of 5. The count shows what a human can notice. A dog's nose picks up many smell signals that we miss. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A child rates the strength of a vinegar mark from 0 to 5. The ratings are 5 at 0 minutes, 4 at 10 minutes, 2 at 30 minutes and 0 at 60 minutes. Did the smell fade by the same amount every 10 minutes? When did it fade fastest? **No. It fell fastest in the first 30 minutes, by about 1 point every 10 minutes, and more slowly after that.**. From 0 to 10 minutes it fell by 1. From 10 to 30 minutes it fell by 2, which is 1 for each 10 minutes. From 30 to 60 minutes it fell by 2, which is less than 1 for each 10 minutes. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Name the four channels of animal signals. → Smell, sight, sound and touch.
- What is a pheromone? → A chemical that an animal gives off, which changes what others of the same kind do.
- Which kind of signal still works after the sender has gone? → A smell, such as a scent mark on a tree.
- What is an echo? → A sound that has bounced off something and come back.
- Are bats blind? → No. All bats can see. Small bats also find things by echoes.
- What is sound too high for humans called? → Ultrasound.
- Why does a display not work at night? → It is a signal for the eyes, and eyes need light.
- What is eavesdropping? → Picking up a signal that was meant for someone else.
