# Fresh water and salt water: why so little of Earth's water can be drunk
`sci.water.water-sources-and-uses.L5`

**Summary.** Most of Earth's water is sea water, so very little of it can be drunk. How little? In this unit you will share out one litre of Earth in numbers, freeze salty water to see if the ice keeps the salt, and test what salty water does to a potato.

**Intro.** *From space the Earth looks blue, because water covers most of the surface. So why does a sailor in a lifeboat, with water all around, have nothing to drink?* The water around the sailor is sea water, and sea water is salty. A person who drinks sea water ends up with less water in the body than before. In this unit you measure how much of Earth's water is fresh. You freeze salty water to see where the salt goes. You put sticks of potato in salty water to see what salt does to living cells. You melt ice that is already floating in water. You also melt ice that rests above the water, the way ice on land does. Then you see which one raises the level. Then you take water from a jar of fresh and salty layers, as the people of an island do.

## Terms
- **Fresh water**: Water with very little salt dissolved in it: less than 1 gram in a litre. Rain, rivers, most lakes, most groundwater and glacier ice are fresh water. *e.g.* The Kaveri carries fresh water. A litre of river water usually holds only a small part of a gram of salts.
- **Sea water**: The salty water of the seas and oceans. A litre of sea water holds about 35 grams of dissolved salts, mostly common salt. *e.g.* A litre of sea water, dried in the sun, leaves about 6 level teaspoons of salt.
- **Brackish water**: Water that is saltier than fresh water but less salty than the sea. It is found where rivers meet the sea. *e.g.* Chilika in Odisha is brackish. Rivers bring fresh water into Chilika and the tide brings sea water in.
- **Salinity**: How salty water is. It is measured as grams of dissolved salts in one litre of water. *e.g.* The salinity of sea water is about 35 grams in a litre. The salinity of the Dead Sea is about 340.
- **Dissolve**: To spread out in a liquid as particles too small to see. A dissolved substance is still there. *e.g.* Salt stirred into water dissolves. The water looks clear but tastes salty.
- **Solid, liquid and gas**: The three forms, or states, that water takes. Ice is the solid form, water is the liquid form and water vapour is the gas. *e.g.* An ice cube, the water in a tumbler and the invisible vapour above a wet cloth are all water.
- **Water vapour**: Water in the form of a gas. It is invisible and is mixed into the air everywhere. *e.g.* The drops on the outside of a cold bottle come from water vapour in the room's air.
- **Freeze and melt**: To freeze is to change from liquid to solid when heat leaves. To melt is to change from solid to liquid when heat enters. Pure water does both at 0 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Water in the freezer freezes into ice. Ice on a plate in the room melts into water.
- **Evaporate and condense**: To evaporate is to change from liquid to gas. To condense is to change from gas back to liquid. *e.g.* Wet clothes dry because their water evaporates. A cold tumbler gets wet because vapour condenses on it.
- **Glacier**: A very large, thick body of ice on land, made from snow pressed down over many years. A glacier moves slowly downhill. *e.g.* The Gangotri glacier in Uttarakhand is about 30 kilometres long. The Bhagirathi river begins where the glacier melts.
- **Ice sheet**: A layer of glacier ice that covers a whole land. Earth has two: one on Antarctica and one on Greenland. *e.g.* The ice on Antarctica is about 2 kilometres thick on average.
- **Sea ice**: Ice that forms when the surface of the sea freezes. It is much less salty than the sea water it formed from. *e.g.* In winter the sea around the North Pole is covered with floating sea ice.
- **Iceberg**: A large piece of ice that has broken off a glacier or an ice sheet and floats in the sea. An iceberg is fresh water. *e.g.* An iceberg floats with about nine tenths of the ice under the water.
- **Sea level**: The height of the surface of the sea. It rises when more water flows into the sea, or when sea water warms and takes up more room. *e.g.* Ice sliding off Antarctica into the sea raises the sea level. Ice that already floats on the sea does not.
- **Surface water**: Water that lies on top of the land: rivers, lakes, ponds and reservoirs. *e.g.* Mumbai's drinking water comes from surface water, held in lakes such as Tansa and Vihar.
- **Groundwater**: Water under the ground. It fills the small gaps between grains of soil, sand and rock. *e.g.* A borewell is a narrow pipe drilled down to groundwater.
- **Osmosis**: The movement of water through the thin skin of a living cell, from the less salty side to the more salty side. *e.g.* A stick of potato in very salty water goes limp, because water leaves the potato's cells by osmosis.
- **Cell**: The tiny living unit that the bodies of plants, humans and other animals are built from. Each cell is a small bag of watery liquid inside a thin skin. *e.g.* A potato is built of millions of cells. So is your tongue.
- **Estuary**: The wide mouth of a river, where fresh river water meets salt water from the sea. *e.g.* The Hooghly meets the Bay of Bengal in an estuary. The water there is brackish.
- **Desalination**: Taking the salt out of sea water to make fresh water. It needs a lot of energy. *e.g.* A plant at Minjur, near Chennai, makes drinking water from the Bay of Bengal.
- **Blind test**: A test in which the person who tastes or judges does not know which sample is which. It stops a guess from changing the result. *e.g.* In Freeze the Sea, a grown-up gives you three drops to taste and does not tell you which is which.

## Explanations
### Fresh water, brackish water and sea water · *defined*
**Definition:** Fresh water holds less than 1 gram of dissolved salts in a litre. Sea water holds about 35 grams. Brackish water is in between.

All natural water has something dissolved in it. The amount is what matters. A litre of rain or river water holds a small part of a gram of salts, and you cannot taste them. A litre of sea water holds about 35 grams, which is about 6 level teaspoons. Most of it is common salt, the same salt as in your kitchen. Water between the two is called brackish. Scientists call the saltiness of water its salinity. India's standard for drinking water says half a gram of dissolved salts in a litre, or less, is best. So sea water is about 70 times too salty to drink.
- e.g. Rain water has almost no salt in it.
- e.g. The Kaveri at Srirangam is fresh. A litre of its water holds only a small part of a gram of salts.
- e.g. Chilika lagoon in Odisha is brackish. It is saltier in summer than in the monsoon.
- e.g. The Arabian Sea holds about 36 grams of salts in a litre.
- e.g. The Dead Sea, between Jordan and Israel, holds about 340 grams of salts in a litre.
### How Earth's water is shared out · *defined*
**Definition:** Out of every 1,000 parts of Earth's water, about 975 are salty and about 25 are fresh. Most of the fresh water is ice or groundwater.

Water covers about 71 out of every 100 parts of Earth's surface. Scientists have added up all of that water. Out of every 1,000 parts, about 975 are salty. Nearly all of the salty water is in the seas, and a little is salty groundwater and salt lakes. Only 25 parts are fresh. Of those 25, about 17 are frozen in glaciers and ice sheets. About 7 and a half are groundwater. The small amount left is less than half of one part. It holds frozen soil, lakes, rivers, the water in the air and the water in all living beings. All the fresh lakes and rivers together make about 7 hundredths of one part. Yet most cities take their water from lakes and rivers.
- e.g. In One Litre Earth, a 1 litre bottle stands for all of Earth's water. Of that bottle, the fresh water is only about 5 teaspoons.
- e.g. About 3 and a half of those 5 teaspoons are ice. About 1 and a half are groundwater.
- e.g. All the fresh lakes and rivers together are only about one drop.
- e.g. Lake Baikal in Russia alone holds about a fifth of the fresh water in all the world's lakes and rivers.
### The three forms of water, and where each is found · *defined*
**Definition:** Water is found on Earth as a solid (ice), a liquid (water) and a gas (water vapour). Most of Earth's water is liquid.

Ice, liquid water and water vapour are the same substance in three forms. Scientists call these forms states. About 98 out of every 100 parts of Earth's water are liquid, in the sea and under the ground. Nearly 2 parts are ice, in glaciers, ice sheets and sea ice. Water vapour is only about 1 part in 100,000. It is mixed into the air everywhere, and it is invisible. If all the vapour in the air fell as rain at once, it would cover the Earth about 2 and a half centimetres deep. That small amount matters a great deal, because all rain and snow form from it.
- e.g. Solid water is in the Siachen glacier, the ice on Antarctica, hail and the frost in a freezer.
- e.g. Liquid water is found in the Indian Ocean, in the Godavari, in a well and inside a coconut.
- e.g. Water as a gas is in the air above wet clothes and in the air you breathe out.
- e.g. A cloud is tiny drops of liquid water or tiny bits of ice. A cloud is water that has condensed from vapour.
### What changes water from one form to another · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat entering or leaving changes water's form. Ice melts and water evaporates when heat enters. Vapour condenses and water freezes when heat leaves.

Pure water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius, and ice melts at the same temperature. Water evaporates at any temperature, and faster when it is warm. Vapour condenses when it touches something cool. None of these changes makes new water or destroys water. In One Bottle, Three Forms, the water freezes and then melts. It comes back to the line you marked at the start. One thing does change. When water freezes, it takes up about one tenth more room. So a cupful of ice weighs less than a cupful of water, and that is why ice floats. A lake in Ladakh freezes from the top, and fish live in the liquid water below the ice. Water pipes in cold hill towns can burst in winter, because the freezing water pushes the pipe apart.
- e.g. An ice cube on a plate melts into a puddle. That is melting.
- e.g. Water in a tray turns into ice in the freezer. That is freezing.
- e.g. A wet floor dries because its water goes into the air. That is evaporating.
- e.g. Drops form on a cold bottle taken from the fridge. Water vapour in the air turns back into liquid on it. That is condensing.
### Why freezing and evaporation leave the salt behind · *defined*
**Definition:** When water freezes or evaporates, water particles leave the salty liquid. Almost all the salt particles stay in the liquid.

Water is made of tiny particles called molecules. In ice, the water molecules are locked in a regular pattern. A salt particle does not fit that pattern. So as ice grows, the salt stays in the liquid that is left, and that liquid gets saltier. This is what you taste in Freeze the Sea. The ice is not perfectly fresh. Small pockets of very salty liquid get trapped between the ice crystals. Saltier water also freezes at a lower temperature. Sea water starts to freeze at about minus 2 degrees Celsius. Evaporation sorts water from salt even better. Only water molecules fly off into the air. So rain is fresh, although most of it rose from the salty sea.
- e.g. In Freeze the Sea, the melted ice tastes less salty than the liquid left in the bowl.
- e.g. New sea ice holds less salt than the sea water under it. The rest sits in tiny pockets of salty liquid.
- e.g. Sea ice that has lasted several summers is nearly fresh, because the salty pockets drain out.
- e.g. Salt pans at Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu use evaporation. The water leaves and the salt stays.
### Ice on Earth: glaciers, ice sheets, icebergs and sea ice · *defined*
**Definition:** Glaciers, ice sheets and icebergs are made from snow, so they are fresh water. Sea ice is frozen sea water and holds a little salt.

About 17 of Earth's 25 fresh parts are ice. Nearly all of that ice lies on land. It began as snow. Each year's snow pressed down on the snow below, until it became hard ice. An ice sheet covers a whole land. About nine tenths of the world's ice is on Antarctica, where the ice is about 2 kilometres thick on average. A glacier is a river of ice in a mountain valley. The Himalayas hold thousands of glaciers. In summer their melt water feeds rivers such as the Ganga, the Indus and the Brahmaputra. An iceberg is a piece of glacier ice that has broken off into the sea. Sea ice is different. It forms when the sea's own surface freezes. This difference matters for the sea level. Ice on land adds water to the sea when it slides in and melts. Ice that already floats does not, as you see in Ice on Land, Ice on Sea. Warm sea water also takes up a little more room, and this adds to the rise.
- e.g. The Gangotri glacier is about 30 kilometres long. The Bhagirathi river flows out of an ice cave called Gaumukh.
- e.g. The Siachen glacier in the Karakoram is more than 70 kilometres long.
- e.g. If all the ice on Antarctica melted, the sea would rise by about 58 metres.
- e.g. Aditi Pant, an ocean scientist, and Sudipta Sengupta, a geologist, are credited as the first Indian women to reach Antarctica, in 1983 and 1984.
- e.g. In Ice on Land, Ice on Sea, the water level rose when ice above the water melted into it. The level stayed the same when ice that was already floating melted.
### Groundwater: the largest store of liquid fresh water · *defined*
**Definition:** Groundwater fills the gaps between grains of soil, sand and rock. It holds about 100 times as much fresh water as all the lakes and rivers.

Rain soaks into the ground and trickles down until it reaches rock that it cannot pass. There the water fills the gaps between the grains. Wells and borewells take water from this store. In One Litre Earth, groundwater is 1 and a half teaspoons, and all the lakes and rivers are one drop. So most of the liquid fresh water on Earth is out of sight. More than half of India's farm water and most village drinking water come from groundwater. Groundwater moves slowly. Some of it fell as rain last year. Some has been under the ground for thousands of years. Not all groundwater is fresh. Under parts of the Thar desert and near some coasts, groundwater is brackish or salty.
- e.g. A borewell in Bengaluru may go down 300 metres or more to reach water.
- e.g. In a mine in Canada, a team that included the scientist Barbara Sherwood Lollar found water that had been shut in rock for more than a billion years.
- e.g. Water pumped from deep wells in the Thar desert is often too salty to drink.
### Rivers and lakes, and why some lakes are salty · *defined*
**Definition:** Rivers and most lakes hold fresh water, because water keeps flowing through them. A lake with no river flowing out becomes salty.

Rivers and lakes are the smallest store, and the one people use most, because their water is easy to reach. A river carries a very small amount of dissolved salts, taken from rock and soil. In most lakes, a river flows in and a river flows out, so the salts are carried on to the sea. Some lakes have no way out. Water leaves them only by evaporation, and evaporation leaves the salts behind. Year after year the salts build up. Sambhar Lake in Rajasthan is such a lake, and people make salt from it. The sea is salty for the same reason. Rivers have carried salts into the sea for hundreds of millions of years, and only the water leaves.
- e.g. Wular Lake in Kashmir is fresh. The Jhelum river flows in at one side and out at the other.
- e.g. Loktak Lake in Manipur is fresh, with floating mats of plants called phumdis.
- e.g. Sambhar Lake in Rajasthan has no river flowing out, and is salty.
- e.g. Lonar Lake in Maharashtra fills a crater made by a meteorite. The lake has no outlet, and the water is salty.
### Why we cannot drink sea water · *defined*
**Definition:** Sea water is saltier than the liquid inside our cells. By osmosis, the salt draws water out of the cells, and the kidneys use up more water to remove it.

Your body is built of cells, and each cell is a small bag of watery liquid in a thin skin. The liquid in your cells and blood holds about 9 grams of salt in a litre. Water passes through a cell's skin easily, and salt passes with difficulty. Water moves through the skin from the less salty side to the more salty side. This movement is called osmosis. In the Potato Test, potato sticks in very salty water lose water and go limp. Sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in a litre. If a person drinks sea water, the blood becomes saltier, and water leaves the cells. The kidneys must remove the extra salt in urine. But human urine cannot become as salty as sea water. So to remove the salt from one litre of sea water, the kidneys use more water than the person drank. The person ends up thirstier than before.
- e.g. In the Potato Test, the stick in plain water stays stiff and the stick in very salty water bends.
- e.g. Salt sprinkled on cut cucumber draws water out, and a puddle forms on the plate.
- e.g. Sailors in lifeboats are told never to drink sea water.
### Animals and plants who can drink the sea · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals and plants have body parts that remove salt, so they can live on sea water.

Humans cannot live on sea water, but many other beings can. An albatross can spend months at sea and drink only sea water. She has a salt gland above each eye. The gland takes salt out of her blood and makes a liquid saltier than the sea. The liquid drips from the tip of her beak. Sea turtles have salt glands near their eyes, so a sea turtle on a beach seems to cry. Sea fish drink sea water and push the salt out through their gills. Mangrove trees grow with their roots in brackish water. Some mangroves keep most of the salt out at the root. Others let the salt in and push it out through their leaves, where it dries as white crystals.
- e.g. Gulls, albatrosses and penguins have salt glands.
- e.g. An olive ridley turtle nesting at Gahirmatha in Odisha sheds salty tears.
- e.g. Grey mangrove leaves at Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu have white salt crystals on them after a sunny day.
- e.g. A camel can drink water that is a little salty. A person would find that water too salty to drink.
### Where fresh water meets salt water · *defined*
**Definition:** Fresh water is lighter than salt water, so fresh water floats on top where the two meet. Stirring or hard pumping mixes them.

A litre of fresh water has a mass of 1,000 grams. A litre of sea water has a mass of about 1,025 grams, because of the salt. So fresh water floats on sea water, as you see in The Island Well. Where a river enters the sea, the river water spreads over the sea water. Under a small coral island, the rain that soaks into the sand floats on the sea water below. A floating layer of fresh water is a little like an iceberg, because most of it lies below the sea level. About 40 parts in every 41 are under the sea level. So a layer that rises 1 metre above the sea can reach about 40 metres below it. Real layers are thinner, because tides and pumping mix them. People on the islands of Lakshadweep take this floating rain water from shallow wells. Along a coast, fresh groundwater pushes towards the sea and holds the sea water back. If people pump too much fresh water out, sea water creeps inland under the ground. Wells near the shore then turn salty. This has happened along parts of the coast near Chennai.
- e.g. In The Island Well, clear fresh water stays as a layer above blue salty water until it is stirred.
- e.g. A well on Agatti island rises and falls a little with the tide, because the fresh water floats on the sea.
- e.g. At Minjur, north of Chennai, groundwater has turned salty up to about 16 kilometres from the shore.
### What people use fresh water for · *defined*
**Definition:** Worldwide, about 7 in every 10 litres of fresh water that people take go to farms, about 2 to industry and about 1 to homes.

People take fresh water from rivers, lakes and groundwater. Most of it grows food. A farm field needs far more water than a family drinks, because a growing plant takes up water all day and gives most of it back to the air. Industry uses water to cool machines, wash and make things such as paper, cloth and steel. Homes use the smallest share. A person needs only 2 to 3 litres a day to drink. India's planners work with about 135 litres a person each day in cities with sewers, for drinking, cooking, washing and the toilet. India pumps more groundwater than any other country, mostly for farms. The same water can be used more than once. Water that cools a machine or washes rice can often water a plant.
- e.g. Rice paddies in the Kaveri delta are flooded with river water for much of the season.
- e.g. A cloth mill in Tiruppur uses water in dyeing and washing cloth.
- e.g. A family in a flat fills the overhead tank each morning for cooking, washing and the toilet.
### Should cities make their drinking water from the sea? · *debated*
**Definition:** Desalination is the removal of salt from sea water. It works, and people disagree about how much cities should depend on it.

A desalination plant pushes sea water at very high pressure against sheets with holes so fine that water passes and most salt does not. Chennai's first large plant opened at Minjur in 2010. It makes about 100 million litres of drinking water a day. At Kavaratti in Lakshadweep, a plant has made fresh water from the sea since 2005. Desalination gives water even when the rains fail. But it uses a lot of electricity, and it sends very salty waste water back into the sea.
- e.g. Chennai has plants at Minjur and Nemmeli.
- e.g. Saudi Arabia and Israel get a large share of their drinking water from the sea.
- e.g. A lifeboat's hand pump can make a little fresh water from sea water.
- **An engineer at a city water board:** The sea does not run dry. When the monsoon fails and the lakes are empty, a sea-water plant still gives water every day.
- **A woman who fishes near the plant:** The waste water is much saltier than the sea. Near the pipe, the fish and the prawns have gone, and my catch is smaller.
- **A water scientist:** Saving rain, cleaning used water and mending leaking pipes cost far less energy for each litre. A city should do these first, and use the sea last.
### Is there water deep inside the Earth? · *idea*
All the numbers in this unit count the water at Earth's surface: the sea, the ice, the groundwater and the air. There may be more, far below. In 2014, the scientist Graham Pearson and his team studied a small diamond from Brazil. Inside the diamond was a speck of a mineral called ringwoodite, which forms hundreds of kilometres down. The speck held water, locked inside the mineral. This water is not a liquid and nobody could drink it. If much of the deep rock is like that speck, the rock could hold as much water as all the oceans. One speck is very little evidence, so this is still an idea.
- e.g. A mineral can hold water locked inside its own structure and still look dry, as plaster does.
- e.g. Volcanoes give off steam, so some water does come up from deep rock.

## Mostly agreed
- About 975 of every 1,000 parts of Earth's water are salty, and about 25 are fresh. *How we know:* Scientists measured the depth of the seas with echo sounders and satellites, and the thickness of the ice with radar. The Russian scientist Igor Shiklomanov added up the stores in 1993, and later counts agree closely.
- Most of Earth's fresh water is frozen, and most of the rest is under the ground. *How we know:* Radar from aircraft shows ice about 2 kilometres thick over Antarctica. Wells, borewells and satellite measurements show how much water the ground holds.
- When salt water freezes, the ice holds much less salt than the water it came from. *How we know:* In 1776 Edward Nairne froze sea water in London and melted the ice. The water had no taste of salt. Scientists who drill into sea ice measure the same thing today.
- Water moves through the skin of a living cell towards the saltier side. *How we know:* Pieces of potato, beetroot and many other tissues lose mass in salty water and gain mass in plain water. Anyone can repeat this with a kitchen scale.
- Drinking sea water makes a person lose more water than they drank. *How we know:* Human urine cannot become as salty as sea water, which holds about 35 grams in a litre. Records of shipwrecks show that people who drank sea water died sooner than those who did not.
- Fresh water floats on salt water, because a litre of fresh water has less mass. *How we know:* A litre of sea water has a mass of about 1,025 grams, against 1,000 grams for fresh water. Wells on coral islands hold fresh water that rises and falls with the tide.

## How we found out
**Edward Nairne, a maker of scientific instruments in London, who tested whether the ice of sea water is fresh, Nairne froze sea water in 1776, in the years when British explorers were sailing north..** In the 1770s, British explorers wanted to know if ships could sail to the North Pole across open sea. Some thinkers said yes. Some thinkers doubted that sea water could freeze hard, because it is salty. Any ice that explorers met at sea, they said, must have come from rivers and the land. Edward Nairne, a London maker of scientific instruments, tested the idea. In 1776 he set sea water out in the winter cold. It froze, though only a little below the cold that freezes pond water. He then melted the ice, and the water had no taste of salt. The salt had stayed in the liquid that was left. So sea water does freeze, and freezing takes most of the salt out. Later explorers found that the polar sea was covered with ice that the sea itself had made.  
*What people believed before:* That sea water cannot freeze hard, so ice found at sea must have come from rivers or from the land. Nairne froze sea water and melted the ice. It was nearly fresh, so the sea can freeze and the ice leaves most of the salt behind.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Earth has so much water that people can never run short.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Water covers about 71 out of every 100 parts of the surface, and the water cycle never stops.  
  Against: Only 25 of every 1,000 parts are fresh, and most of that is ice. People in a place can use only the rain, river water and groundwater that reach that place.
- **Sea water cannot freeze, so all the ice at sea must come from rivers.** (Some European thinkers in the 1700s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Ice taken from the sea melted into fresh water. Sea water also stays liquid when ponds on land have frozen.  
  Against: Edward Nairne froze sea water in 1776. It froze at a slightly lower temperature than fresh water, and the melted ice had no taste of salt.
- **Deeper wells always give better water.** (Many well owners.; *debated*)  
  For: A deeper well reaches water when a shallow well has dried up, and deep water usually carries fewer germs.  
  Against: Near the sea and on islands, salt water lies under the fresh water. In some places, deep groundwater also holds more dissolved salts, fluoride or arsenic.
- **Icebergs could be towed to dry countries to give them drinking water.** (Some engineers, since the 1970s.; *minority*)  
  For: An iceberg is fresh water, and one large iceberg holds a very great amount of fresh water. Icebergs melt into the sea unused.  
  Against: A towed iceberg melts on the way through warm seas, and the towing would cost a great deal of fuel. Nobody has yet done it.
- **When the ice at the North Pole melts, the sea will rise a great deal.** (A common belief, repeated in many news stories.; *rejected*)  
  For: The Arctic ice is melting fast, and the sea is rising.  
  Against: The ice at the North Pole is sea ice. It already floats, so melting it hardly changes the sea level. The sea rises because of ice on land, such as Greenland, and because warm water takes up more room.
- **Desalination will end the world's shortage of drinking water.** (Some governments and companies.; *debated*)  
  For: The sea holds far more water than people could ever use, and the cost of taking out the salt has fallen.  
  Against: It still needs much more energy than cleaning river water. Towns far from the sea or high above it would also have to pump the water a long way. The salty waste harms life near the outlet.

## Roots
In this unit you froze salty water and found that the ice left most of the salt behind. Here we ask what that tells us about fresh water, the sea and ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In One Litre Earth, all the fresh lakes and rivers of the world fill less than one drop. Even so, rivers have kept running for millions of years. The Sun lifts fresh water out of the sea and leaves the salt behind. Then rain brings the water back. So a place can keep using water for ever only if it takes no more than the rain refills.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call Earth a water planet, so it seems that water can never run short. That counts the sea, which people cannot drink. The water that people can actually use is only a small amount. It is what the Sun lifts out of the sea and the rain brings back.  
*Evidence:* In One Litre Earth, the sea and the ice fill almost the whole bottle. All the lakes and rivers together are only one drop. In Freeze the Sea, ice leaves its salt behind, and evaporation does the same over the sea.

- *who am i:* More than half of your body is water, and that water holds about 9 grams of salt in a litre. Your body keeps that amount steady every hour, with every drink and every visit to the toilet.
- *what is life:* Every living cell is a small bag of slightly salty water. An albatross, a mangrove and a child each have their own way of keeping that water at the right saltiness.
- *what is this world:* The sea, the clouds, the glaciers and the rivers hold the same water at different times. The Sun and the cold keep taking water out of the sea, and the rivers keep taking it back.

A drop of water in the Bay of Bengal carries salt. The Sun lifts the water into the air and the salt stays in the sea. The drop falls as snow on a Himalayan glacier and melts into the Ganga. It may pass through a well, a tumbler and a child. Then it flows back to the bay and mixes with salt again. The same water passes through the sea, a cloud, a glacier, a river and a child, one after another.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.10:** “इमाः सोम्य नद्यः पुरस्तात्प्राच्यः स्यन्दन्ते पश्चात्प्रतीच्यः / ताः समुद्रात्समुद्रमेवापियन्ति / स समुद्र एव भवति / ता यथा तत्र न विदुरियमहमस्मीयमहमस्मीति” The rivers in the east flow east and the rivers in the west flow west. They rise from the sea and go back to the sea, and they become the sea. Once there, they do not know 'I am this river' or 'I am that river'. (The verse says that rivers come from the sea and return to it. This unit's science agrees: rain rises from the sea by evaporation, and rivers carry it back. The teacher Uddalaka uses the rivers to teach his son about the self. That part is a teaching, and the science neither proves it nor denies it.)
- **Tirukkural, verse 17, in the chapter on rain, by Thiruvalluvar:** “நெடுங்கடலும் தன்நீர்மை குன்றும் தடிந்தெழிலி / தான்நல்கா தாகி விடின்.” Even the wide sea will lose its wealth, if the cloud does not draw up its water and give it back as rain. (Thiruvalluvar, about 1,500 years ago by the usual dating, describes the cloud taking water from the sea and returning it. He is praising rain. He does not say that the salt stays behind, which is what Freeze the Sea and the salt pans show.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, No Death, No Fear (2002):** A cloud never dies. It can become rain, snow or ice, but it cannot become nothing. (This is the same journey told from the cloud's side. The fresh water in a tumbler was vapour, and before that it was sea. It is a way of looking. The measurements in this unit stand without it.)
- Science (settled): Matter is not made or destroyed when water changes form. The water that freezes, melts, evaporates and condenses is the same water.
- Science (settled): Nearly all the water in rivers, lakes, glaciers and groundwater fell as rain or snow, and most of that vapour rose from the sea.
- Science (open): Deep rock inside the Earth may hold as much water as all the oceans, locked inside minerals. Nobody yet knows how much.
- Other view, A doctor who treats people with dehydration: For a living body, fresh water and sea water are truly different, because one keeps a person alive and the other dries them out. Calling them the same water ignores the difference that matters to a thirsty person. So, on this view, what water does to a living body is part of what water is.
- Practice, One slow sip (3 min): Pour a small tumbler of drinking water. Hold it and look into it for a few breaths. This water was once in the sea, and it carried salt then. The Sun lifted it out and left the salt behind. Take one small sip and hold it in your mouth. Notice that it has almost no taste. Swallow, and sit quietly. Where will this water go next?
- Ask: In One Litre Earth, the sea is nearly all of the bottle and the rivers are one drop. Does a planet with this much sea have plenty of water?
- Ask: The water in your body is a little salty, like a very weak sea. Why do you think that is?
- Ask: An albatross can drink the sea and you cannot. Whose word should decide what counts as drinking water?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* An albatross drinks sea water and stays healthy. Is 'drinking water' a kind of water, or does it depend on who is drinking? (hint: Think about the albatross's salt glands and your own kidneys. What would each of you call fresh enough?)
- *deep:* In Freeze the Sea, the ice and the leftover liquid came from one bowl. Were they ever two different waters? (hint: The water particles in the ice and in the liquid are the same kind. Only the salt is shared out differently.)
- *deep:* The rain that fills a river rose from the sea, and the river carries it back. Is the water in a river really fresh water, or is it sea water on its way home? (hint: Follow one drop from the Bay of Bengal to a cloud, to the Kaveri, and back.)
- *big:* Most fresh water is ice far from where people live. Does anyone own the ice on Antarctica? Should anyone? (hint: By a treaty of 1959, no country owns Antarctica, and it is kept for science and peace.)
- *big:* Groundwater under a village took thousands of years to collect. Is it fair for one farm to pump as much as it likes? (hint: Think of the people nearby, the people not yet born, and the trees whose roots reach that water.)
- *big:* Humans use most of the reachable fresh water. How much should be left in rivers for fish, otters, trees and everyone else who lives by fresh water? (hint: A river that is pumped dry before the sea has no estuary, and the fish who breed there have nowhere to go.)

## Story: The Well That Moved with the Tide
*Ayesha, Ummumma, Yusuf, Hamza and Zubaida are made up. These facts are real: Agatti is a narrow coral island in Lakshadweep, about 7 kilometres long and under a kilometre across. On such islands, rain water soaks into the sand and floats as a thin layer on the sea water below. The water in island wells rises and falls with the tide. Wells that are dug too deep or pumped too hard turn salty. The monsoon reaches Lakshadweep around the start of June. Ummumma is a word for grandmother used by many Muslim families in Kerala and Lakshadweep.*

Ayesha was ten, and she drew the water for her grandmother every evening.

The well stood behind their house on Agatti. The island was so narrow that she could hear the lagoon on one side and the open sea on the other. It was not a deep well. The rope had seven knots, one for each arm's length, and the bucket touched water near the fourth knot. Ummumma called the water sweet. On Agatti, sweet water meant water with no salt in it.

That year their neighbour Hamza built a new house and dug a new well. He dug far down past the wet sand. He lined the well with nine rings of cement and set an electric pump on top.

"Deep water is clean water," he told everyone.

For a month his tank filled in ten minutes. His wife Zubaida watered a whole row of banana plants with a hose.

Then, in April, Zubaida came to Ummumma's door with an empty pot. "Our water has turned," she said. "The tea tastes of the sea."

Hamza was sure of the reason. "The well is not deep enough yet," he said. "The sweet water must be further down. After Friday I will dig three more rings."

Ayesha heard this while she drew the evening water. As she listened, she looked at the rope in her hands. The bucket had touched water just below the fourth knot. That morning it had touched water just above the knot. She had seen this many times and had never thought about it.

She tied a strip of red cloth to the rope at the place where it met the water. Next morning, before school, she let the bucket down again. The red cloth hung dry, a hand's width above the water. In the evening the water was back at the cloth.

For four days she kept a page in her maths notebook. She wrote the height of the water in the well, morning and evening. After school she walked to the jetty, where the boatmen had painted lines on a post to read the tide, and she wrote that down too. When the tide was high at the jetty, the well was high about an hour later. When the tide was low, the well was low.

"The well goes up and down with the sea," she told Ummumma.

"It always has," said Ummumma. "My mother knew it. Draw at high tide, and the bucket has less far to go."

"But then the sea is under our well. So why is our water not salty?"

Ummumma had no answer. She had never needed one.

Ayesha found the answer on Sunday, and she found it by accident. Her little brother Yusuf kept a hermit crab in a glass jar of sea water for a day, before he put her back on the beach. That morning it rained hard for an hour, the first rain since December. The jar stood under the edge of the roof. Rain ran off the dusty tiles, brown as weak tea, and trickled down the inside of the jar.

When Ayesha picked up the jar, there were two waters in it. At the bottom was clear sea water, where the crab sat. On top lay a brown layer as thick as her finger. That was the rain from the roof. The line between the two was as straight as a line drawn with a ruler.

She tilted the jar. The brown layer tilted too, and stayed on top.

Then Yusuf took the jar and shook it to wake the crab. The crab did not like it, and Yusuf said sorry to her. The whole jar went pale brown. They waited a long time. The two waters did not come apart again.

Ayesha sat on the step and thought about the rope, the tide and the jar. Then she filled a second jar with sea water. She let tea-coloured water run slowly down the inside until there was a brown layer on top, and she carried the jar to Hamza's yard. He was mixing cement for the new rings.

"Hamza uncle, please don't dig," she said.

She showed him the notebook first. "The wells rise and fall with the tide. So the sea is under the whole island, under the sand." Then she held up the jar. "Rain is lighter than sea water. It soaks into the sand and floats on the sea, the way this brown water floats. Ummumma's bucket takes a little from the top. Your well goes down close to the line. Your pump pulls hard, and it pulls the sea up from below. It does what Yusuf did when he shook the jar."

Hamza looked at the jar for some time. "So if I dig three more rings..."

"You will have a well of sea water."

He said nothing. He had paid for nine rings of cement, and he was not a man who liked being wrong. Behind him, Zubaida said, "Try what the child says. It costs nothing to stop digging."

Hamza did not dig. He filled the bottom of his well with coral stones and sand, until it was no deeper than Ummumma's. He lifted the pump's pipe so that it hung just under the surface. He ran the pump for ten minutes in the morning and ten in the evening, and no more. The banana plants got the washing water.

For two weeks the tea still tasted of the sea. Then the monsoon reached Agatti, and it rained for nine days.

On the tenth day Zubaida came to the door with a full tumbler. Ummumma tasted it and passed it to Ayesha.

It was sweet.

Ayesha went on keeping her page of marks long after anyone needed it. She liked knowing what she was doing each time the bucket came up. She was lifting rain off the top of the sea.

**For the parent:** For the parent: on a small coral island, rain soaks into the sand and floats on the heavier sea water underneath. Ayesha's two clues are the well rising with the tide, which shows that the sea is under the island, and the brown rain floating in the jar. A shallow well and a bucket take only the top of the fresh layer. A deep well and a strong pump draw the salt water up, and once the two are mixed they do not separate by themselves. Only new rain rebuilds the fresh layer.

- Ask: The well rose and fell with the tide. What did that tell Ayesha about what lies under the island?
- Ask: Why did the brown rain water stay on top of the sea water in the jar?
- Ask: Hamza thought a deeper well would reach sweeter water. Why was a deeper well the wrong answer on this island?
- Ask: After Hamza changed his well, the water stayed salty for two weeks. What had to happen before it turned sweet?

### e1 · Freeze the Sea: does ice leave the salt behind? (40 min)
- **question:** A bowl of salty water is left in the freezer until about half of it is ice. Is the ice as salty as the liquid that is left? Does salty water freeze as soon as plain water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will taste saltiest: the water you started with, the melted ice, or the liquid left over? Which bowl will show ice first, plain or salty? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two bowls of the same size and material. Put the same amount of water in each, and put both on the same freezer shelf at the same time. Only one thing differs: bowl B has salt.   *Parent says:* The salt did not leave the bowl. So where is it now?

  - Ask: The leftover liquid tastes saltier than the water you started with. Where did the extra salt come from? (good: From the water that froze. The ice left its salt behind.; The same salt is now in less water.)
  - Ask: Why does the melted ice still taste a little salty? (good: Some salty liquid was trapped inside the ice.; The rinse did not remove all the salty liquid.)
  - Note: The reason: in ice, water particles lock together in a regular pattern, and salt does not fit into it. So the growing ice takes water and leaves salt in the liquid, which gets saltier. Saltier water freezes at a lower temperature, so bowl B froze later than bowl A. Some salty liquid is trapped in small pockets in the ice, so the ice is fresher but not fresh. Sea ice in the Arctic forms in the same way.
  - Think: People of the Arctic choose old sea ice to melt for drinking, and not new sea ice. Why might old ice be fresher?
### e2 · The Potato Test: what does salty water do to living cells? (35 min)
- **question:** Sticks of raw potato sit for one hour in plain water, in home sea water and in very salty water. Which sticks stay stiff and which go limp? Do any change in length?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After one hour, which stick will be stiffest and which will bend most easily? Will any stick get longer or shorter? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut all six sticks from one potato, to the same length and thickness. Use the same amount of water in each tumbler, and leave all the sticks in for the same time. Only the amount of salt differs. Put two sticks in each tumbler, because one stick alone could be odd.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the cutting. Nobody eats the raw potato sticks or drinks the salty water.
- **record:** Fill in the table, with one row for each tumbler. Use the average of the two sticks. Take a photo of the three sticks being bent, or draw them.
- **results table:** Tumbler | Length before, in millimetres | Length after, in millimetres | Bend test: snaps, bends a little, or bends into a curve
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the potato and the three labelled tumblers. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* A potato is made of living cells, and each cell is a tiny bag of watery liquid. We will leave potato sticks in three kinds of water for an hour.  
  *Child mode:* Which water will make a stick bend the most? Which will keep it stiffest? Why do you think so?
  - Note: Many children expect the salty sticks to swell, because they are sitting in water. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill each tumbler with water. 2. Stir 1 level teaspoon of salt into SEA and 4 into VERY SALTY. 3. Peel the potato and cut 6 sticks, each 60 mm long and as thick as a pencil. 4. Your child measures each stick and writes the length. 5. Put 2 sticks in each tumbler and write the time.  
  *Parent says:* Measure each stick in millimetres before it goes in. Then put two sticks in each tumbler.  
  *Child mode:* Measure each stick in millimetres. Put two sticks in each tumbler.
  - Ask: Why two sticks in each tumbler? (good: One stick could be odd, or cut badly.; If both sticks do the same, we can trust the result more.)
  - Note: Thin sticks matter. A stick as thick as a finger changes too slowly to show much in an hour. If your salt is coarse, stir until no grains are left.
  - Wait: Leave the sticks in the water for 1 hour.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 1 hour, lift out the sticks, one tumbler at a time. Measure each stick. Then hold a stick at both ends and bend it gently. Write whether it snaps, bends a little or bends into a curve.  
  *Parent says:* Measure each stick again. Now bend it gently between your fingers. Does it snap, or does it bend? Tell me what you feel.  
  *Child mode:* Measure each stick again. Bend it gently. Does it snap or bend?
  - Note: Expect the plain-water sticks to be crisp and to snap. The sea-water sticks bend a little. The very salty sticks bend into a curve and are a few millimetres shorter. The length changes are small, so the bend test is the clearer result.
  - Check: In the Potato Test, the stick from very salty water bends like rubber. What has happened inside the stick? → **Water has left the potato's cells**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. What did the sticks in each tumbler do?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Which way did water move in the very salty tumbler? What is your evidence? (good: Out of the potato. The stick got shorter and limp.; Towards the saltier side.)
  - Note: The reason: each potato cell is a bag of slightly salty liquid inside a thin skin. Water passes through the skin easily, and salt does not. Water moves through the skin towards the saltier side. This is called osmosis. In very salty water, water leaves the cells and they go slack. In plain water, water enters the cells and they swell tight, so the stick is stiff.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now link the result to drinking sea water. Ask the question below, and then read the numbers in the note.  
  *Parent says:* Now think about drinking sea water. Your body is made of cells too, just like the potato.  
  *Child mode:* Your cells are like the potato's cells. Think about the sticks in very salty water.
  - Ask: What happens to a person's cells if they drink sea water? (good: The blood gets saltier, and water leaves the cells.; The cells shrink, like the cells of the potato stick.; The person gets thirstier.)
  - Note: The numbers: blood holds about 9 grams of salt in a litre, and sea water about 35. Human urine can hold only about 20. So the kidneys need more than a litre of water to remove the salt in a litre of sea water. A person who drinks sea water loses water.
  - Think: A sea turtle drinks sea water every day. What must her body be able to do that yours cannot?
### e3 · Ice on Land, Ice on Sea: which melting ice raises the water? (45 min)
- **question:** Two tumblers hold the same amount of water. In one, ice floats in the water. In the other, ice sits above the water on a strainer. When all the ice has melted, does the water level change in either tumbler?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After the ice melts, will the water be higher, lower or the same in each tumbler? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two clear tumblers of the same size, the same amount of water at the same warmth, and the same number of ice cubes from one tray. Only the place of the ice differs: it floats in tumbler A and sits on a strainer above the water in tumbler B. Mark both water levels before the ice melts.
- **safety:** Wipe up any spilled water at once, so that nobody slips.
- **record:** Take a photo of both tumblers before and after, with the band, or draw them. Fill in the table. Under it, write one sentence that says which ice raised the water, and one that says why.
- **results table:** Tumbler | Water level before the ice melted | Water level after the ice melted | Change in millimetres
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two tumblers, the strainer and the ice. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Ice floats in one tumbler and sits above the water in the other. When all of it has melted, which water will be higher? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at tumbler A. Its ice floats. Look at tumbler B. Its ice sits above the water.
  - Note: Many children say both rise, because melting adds water. Some say neither. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour 150 ml of water into tumbler A. Use lukewarm tap water for both tumblers. 2. Put 4 ice cubes into tumbler A. 3. Put a rubber band round tumbler A at the water line, or mark the line with a pen. 4. Pour water into tumbler B until it reaches the same height as the band on A. 5. Mark B in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Tumbler A has ice floating in it. We fill tumbler B to the same height, with no ice in it.  
  *Child mode:* Add 4 cubes to tumbler A. Mark its water line. Fill B to the same height. Mark it.
  - Ask: Why must both water lines start at the same height? (good: So we can tell which one rose and which did not.; To make it a fair test.)
  - Note: The ice in A pushes the water up as it floats, so B needs more water than A to reach the same line. That is why you fill B to the band, and do not pour 150 ml into each. A 150 ml pour reaches about 4 cm in a tumbler 7 cm wide.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rest the strainer across the top of tumbler B. Put 4 ice cubes in the strainer. Check that the bottom of the strainer is at least 1 cm above the water. If it is not, pour a little water out of tumbler B and mark the line again. Write the time. Leave both tumblers in a warm room, out of the Sun's glare.  
  *Parent says:* These cubes are above the water, like the ice on top of a mountain. Where will the water from this ice go when it melts?  
  *Child mode:* Put 4 cubes in the strainer above B. Write the time. Wait.
  - Note: In a warm Indian room, 4 cubes melt in about 30 to 60 minutes. The ice in tumbler A melts a little sooner, because it sits in water. If ice is still left after an hour, wait until it has all gone. Do not measure before then.
  - Wait: Wait about 45 to 60 minutes, until no ice is left in either tumbler.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When no ice is left, look at both tumblers from the side, with your eyes level with the water line. Hold the ruler against the glass. Measure the gap between each band and the new water line, in millimetres. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Put your eyes level with the water. Is the water in A still at its band? What about B? Measure each gap in millimetres.  
  *Child mode:* Look from the side. Measure how far each water line moved.
  - Note: Tumbler A should stay within 1 or 2 millimetres of its band. Tumbler B should rise by about 10 to 20 millimetres. If A has dropped a little, some water has evaporated or clung to the glass. A change of 1 or 2 millimetres is too small to count.
  - Check: In Ice on Land, Ice on Sea, which tumbler's water rose when the ice melted? → **Tumbler B, with the ice above the water**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Which tumbler is like the sea ice, and which is like an ice sheet on land?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Tumbler A is like sea ice. Why did its water not rise? (good: The ice was already in the water, pushing aside water.; The ice turned into just enough water to fill the space it took up.)
  - Ask: Which ice is like the ice on Antarctica, and what would it do to the sea? (good: The ice above the water, in tumbler B.; It would add water to the sea and raise it.)
  - Note: The reason: a floating cube pushes aside as much water as the whole cube weighs. When it melts, it turns into just that much water, and fills the space. Ice above the water has pushed nothing aside. Its melt water is new water, so the level rises. Ice sheets on Antarctica and Greenland sit on land, like ice in the strainer. Sea ice floats already, like the cubes in A. Warm sea water also takes up a little more room, and this raises the sea too. Real sea water is salty, so floating ice makes a very small change, which can be ignored here.
  - Think: A glacier slides off the land into the sea. At what moment does it start to raise the sea: when it slides in, or later when it melts?

### p1 · The Roof and the Tap: how much water falls on our own home? (45 min, 3 days)
- **safety:** Do not climb on any roof. Measure from the ground or from the plan of the building, or ask a grown-up.
- **record:** A table with five rows: the source of your water, the litres used in a day, the roof area, the rain in a year and the share the roof could give. Add one sentence at the end.
- **schedule:** day 1: Find out where your home's water comes from and how many litres the family uses in a day. (15 min); day 2: Find the area of the roof over your home and work out the litres of rain that fall on it in a year. (15 min); day 3: Compare the rain with the family's use, and write what share the rain could give. (15 min)
- **s1 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. 1. Ask where your tap water comes from, for example a lake, a river, a borewell or a tanker. 2. Find the tank size, or the daily use on the water bill. 3. If you cannot find it, use 135 litres for each person each day. 4. Multiply by the number of people at home.  
  *Parent says:* Where does our tap water come from? How many litres do we use in a day? Guess first. Then we will find out.  
  *Child mode:* Find where your tap water comes from. Find how many litres you use a day.
  - Ask: Where do you think the water in our tap fell as rain? (good: On a lake or river far away, or on the ground above a borewell.)
  - Note: Day 1 answers set up the sum. A common value for a family of 4 is 135 litres for each person, which is 540 litres a day. The real figure may be lower or higher.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. 1. Measure the length and width of the roof over your home in metres. If you live in a flat, measure the floor of your flat instead. 2. Multiply length by width to get the area in square metres. 3. Find your town's yearly rain in millimetres. 4. Multiply the area by the rain. The answer is litres.  
  *Parent says:* One millimetre of rain on one square metre of roof is exactly one litre. How many square metres is our roof? How many litres of rain fall on it in a year?  
  *Child mode:* Work out the roof area. Multiply it by the yearly rain in millimetres.
  - Note: Why one millimetre on one square metre is one litre: a square metre is 100 cm by 100 cm, which is 10,000 square centimetres. One millimetre is a tenth of a centimetre. 10,000 times 0.1 is 1,000 cubic centimetres, which is 1 litre. In a flat, divide the building's roof area by the number of homes. Rough yearly rain, rounded: Chennai about 1,400 mm, Bengaluru about 900, Mumbai about 2,300, Delhi about 800, Kochi about 3,000, Jaisalmer about 200. Look up your own town if you can.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 3. 1. Say that a roof tank catches about 8 tenths of the rain, because some splashes away. Multiply your rain by 8 tenths. 2. Divide by the family's use in a year, which is the daily use times 365. 3. Write the share as a fraction.  
  *Parent says:* If we caught 8 tenths of the rain from our roof, what share of the family's water would that be? Is it more than half? Less than half?  
  *Child mode:* Find 8 tenths of your roof's rain. Compare it with the family's yearly use.
  - Note: With made-up numbers, a 100 square metre roof, 900 mm of rain and 8 tenths caught give 72,000 litres. A family of 4 at 135 litres each uses 540 litres a day, or 197,100 litres a year. The roof could give 72,000 out of 197,100, which is about 4 tenths. It is a large share, and the rest must come from elsewhere.
  - Check: A roof of 100 square metres gets 900 millimetres of rain a year. How many litres fall on it? → **90,000**
  - Think: Rain falls in a few weeks, and the family needs water all year. What would the roof's water need, to be useful in the dry months?

## Challenges
### c1 · Fresher Ice: use freezing to take more salt out (40 min)
- **problem:** Start with 1 tumbler of sea-strength water, made with 1 level teaspoon of salt in 200 ml. Use only a freezer, bowls, a strainer and plain water. Make the freshest melted ice you can. You may freeze it more than once, and you may rinse it.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own way of making fresh ice from salty water. Write the steps of your method, and draw it if you like. Say how many tablespoons you ended with, and where the salt went.
- **constraints:** Start with the same sea-strength water as Freeze the Sea.; No boiling. Use only freezing, straining and a quick rinse.; You must end with at least 3 tablespoons of melted ice to taste.; A grown-up tastes your sample and the single-freeze ice from Freeze the Sea without knowing which is which. The fresher one wins.
- **general:** Freeze a shallow bowl until it is half ice. Pour off the liquid, rinse the ice quickly, and melt it.; Break the ice into small pieces before you rinse it, so that more of the salty liquid washes away.; Freeze slowly, on a low shelf, and stop at half ice, because the last part to freeze holds the most salt.
- **creative:** Freeze the melted ice a second time, and keep only half of the new ice.; Freeze in a tilted tray, so that the salty liquid runs to one end and can be poured off.; Throw away the first drops and the last drops that come off a thawing block, because they are the saltiest.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Remember Freeze the Sea. Ask your child for three ideas, and which one they expect to work best.  
  *Parent says:* In Freeze the Sea, the melted ice was fresher than the sea water, but still a little salty. Tell me three ways to make it fresher. Which will work best? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Think of three ways to make the ice fresher.
  - Ask: In Freeze the Sea, why was the melted ice still a little salty? (good: Some salty liquid was trapped in the ice.; Salty liquid clung to the outside of the ice.)
  - Note: The salty liquid is trapped in tiny pockets between the ice crystals. Anything that opens those pockets or drains them, such as breaking the ice, rinsing it or freezing a second time, helps.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose one design. Make it and write each step. Freeze for as long as it needs, and look every half hour. Write the time of each step.  
  *Parent says:* Choose your design. Write down each step before you start. Then do it, and write the times.  
  *Child mode:* Choose a design. Write the steps. Then do it and write the times.
  - Note: Do the single-freeze version of Freeze the Sea at the same time, so that the two samples are made from the same water. If you want to save time, freeze both on the same shelf.
  - Wait: Freezing takes 2 to 4 hours for each round. A second round takes about the same again.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Melt your ice in a clean katori. A grown-up gives your child one drop of each sample, without saying which is which. Your child says which is fresher.  
  *Parent says:* I will give you two drops, and I will not say which is which. Which one is fresher? Rinse your mouth between drops.  
  *Child mode:* Taste two drops. Which is fresher? Rinse in between.
  - Note: If the two tastes seem equal, your design did not take out more salt than a single freeze. That is a result, and a useful one. Try a different change, and test again.
  - Check: In Fresher Ice, why must the taster not know which sample is which? → **So a guess cannot change the result**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results together. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Which design made the freshest ice? How much melted ice was left? What do you give up to get fresher ice?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your results together.
  - Ask: What do you give up to get fresher ice? (good: Less water, because some is thrown away each time.; More time, because each freeze takes hours.)
  - Note: A second freezing leaves less water but fresher. This is how freeze desalination works, in plants that use cold to take the salt out of sea water. It costs a great deal of energy.
### c2 · A Water Plan for a Small Island (40 min)
- **problem:** A made-up coral island has 300 people. Each person needs 40 litres a day for drinking, cooking and washing. The island has 3,000 square metres of roof and 1,500 millimetres of rain a year. Plan where the water comes from for the whole year.
- **your turn:** Now make your own plan for the island. List the litres from the roofs, the wells and the plant. Say how you would keep the wells from turning salty. Draw it if you like.
- **constraints:** The yearly need is 300 people times 40 litres times 365 days.; Roof tanks catch 7 tenths of the rain that falls on the roofs.; The island's shallow wells can safely give 2,000 litres a day in all. More than that turns the water salty.; A sea-water plant makes fresh water at 2,000 litres a day. It uses fuel, and sends salty water back to the sea.
- **general:** Collect rain from every roof into tanks, and use the tank water first.; Use the wells for the rest, and never pump more than 2,000 litres a day.; Run the sea-water plant only when the tanks and the wells are not enough.
- **creative:** Flush toilets with sea water, so that fresh water is used only for drinking, cooking and washing.; Use washing water for the coconut palms and the vegetable beds.; Mend leaking pipes and taps first, because a litre that is saved costs nothing to make.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Work out how many litres the island needs in a year. Write the sum and the answer.  
  *Parent says:* The island has 300 people, and each person needs 40 litres a day. How many litres is that in a day? How many in a year? Which source do you think will give the most?  
  *Child mode:* 300 people use 40 litres each a day. How many litres is that in a year?
  - Note: 300 times 40 is 12,000 litres a day. 12,000 times 365 is 4,380,000 litres a year.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out each source. 1. Rain on the roofs: 3,000 times 1,500 litres. 2. Multiply by 7 tenths. 3. Wells: they can safely give 2,000 litres a day, so 2,000 times 365. 4. Add the two. How many litres are still missing?  
  *Parent says:* One millimetre of rain on one square metre is one litre. How many litres of rain fall on all the roofs in a year? How much do the tanks catch? How much can the wells give?  
  *Child mode:* Work out the rain from the roofs. Work out the wells. How much is still missing?
  - Note: Rain on roofs: 3,000 square metres times 1,500 mm is 4,500,000 litres. Seven tenths of that is 3,150,000. Wells: 2,000 times 365 is 730,000. The gap is 500,000 litres a year, which is about 1,400 litres a day.
  - Check: The island's roofs catch 3,150,000 litres a year, and its wells give 730,000. The need is 4,380,000. Which is true? → **About 500,000 litres a year are still missing**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Decide how to fill the gap. The plant turns sea water into drinking water and makes 2,000 litres a day. How many days a year must it run? Then write the plan as a list of sources and litres.  
  *Parent says:* How many litres a year are still missing? The plant makes 2,000 litres a day. How many days must it run? What else could close the gap?  
  *Child mode:* Find how many days the plant must run. Write the plan as a list.
  - Note: 500,000 divided by 2,000 is 250 days. The plant would run most of the year. Saving water, as in the creative ideas, can cut that. Tanks must also store rain for the dry months, so the roofs' water is not all available at once.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the plan to a family member. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Explain your plan. What would happen if the island pumped more?  
  *Child mode:* Explain your plan to a family member.
  - Ask: Why do the wells have a limit of 2,000 litres a day? (good: Pumping harder pulls up the salt water that is under the fresh layer.; The fresh layer is thin, and rain refills it slowly.)
  - Ask: What is the cheapest way to close the gap? (good: Save water first, then use the plant for what is left.; Mend leaks and use water twice.)
  - Note: There is no single right plan. A good plan uses the rain first, keeps the wells below their limit, and runs the plant only for the gap.

## Worksheets
### Fresh, salty and frozen water (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Out of every 1,000 parts of Earth's water, about how many are fresh?  
   Options: About 25 / About 250 / About 500 / About 975  
   **Answer:** About 25. *Why:* About 975 parts are salty and about 25 are fresh. That is 2 and a half parts in 100. If you chose a larger number, remember that most of the water is in the salty sea.
2. (fill) Water with less than 1 gram of salt in a litre is called ____ water.  
   **Answer:** ['fresh']. *Why:* Fresh water holds very little dissolved salt. Sea water holds about 35 grams in a litre, and brackish water is in between.
3. (match) Match each water to how salty it is.  
   **Answer:** Rain on Kochi → Almost no salt; Chilika lagoon → Between fresh and the sea; The Arabian Sea → About 36 grams in a litre; The Dead Sea → About 340 grams in a litre. *Why:* Rain holds almost no salt. A lagoon where rivers meet the sea is brackish. The sea holds about 35 grams, and the Dead Sea holds nearly ten times as much, because no river flows out of it.
4. (truefalse) Most of Earth's fresh water is in lakes and rivers.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. All the fresh lakes and rivers together are only about one drop in One Litre Earth. Most fresh water is ice, and most of the rest is groundwater.
5. (mcq) Where is most of Earth's fresh water?  
   Options: Frozen in ice sheets and glaciers / In the world's rivers / In the clouds / In ponds and lakes  
   **Answer:** Frozen in ice sheets and glaciers. *Why:* About 17 of the 25 fresh parts are ice, mostly on Antarctica. Rivers, lakes and the air hold far less.
6. (order) Put these in order, from the most fresh water to the least:  
   **Answer:** ['Ice and glaciers', 'Groundwater', 'Lakes and rivers']. *Why:* Ice holds about 17 parts, groundwater about 7 and a half, and all the fresh lakes and rivers less than one tenth of one part.
7. (mcq) What changes liquid water into water vapour?  
   Options: Heat entering the water / Heat leaving the water / Adding salt / Stirring the water  
   **Answer:** Heat entering the water. *Why:* Heat entering a liquid lets its particles fly off as a gas. This is evaporation. Heat leaving the water makes it freeze or the vapour condense.
8. (sort) Sort each water by its form.  
   **Answer:** Ice on the Gangotri glacier → Solid; The water in the Godavari → Liquid; The invisible vapour in room air → Gas; Frost in a freezer → Solid; The water in a coconut → Liquid; Hail → Solid. *Why:* Glacier ice, frost and hail are solid water. Rivers and coconut water are liquid. Water vapour is a gas, and it is invisible.
9. (truefalse) When salt water freezes, the ice holds all the salt that was in the water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In Freeze the Sea, the melted ice tasted much less salty than the liquid left in the bowl. Most of the salt stays in the liquid.
10. (write) In Freeze the Sea, the leftover liquid tasted saltier than the water you started with. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** As the ice grew, it took water and left most of the salt in the liquid. The same salt was then in less water, so the liquid tasted saltier than the start.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the ice left salt behind, and that the same salt in less liquid is stronger. It uses what was tasted.
11. (mcq) Rain rose from the salty sea. Why is rain fresh?  
   Options: Only water evaporates, and the salt stays in the sea / Clouds wash the salt out of the rain / The Sun burns the salt away / Wind filters the rain  
   **Answer:** Only water evaporates, and the salt stays in the sea. *Why:* When sea water evaporates, only water particles fly into the air. The salt is left in the sea. So rain, and the rivers it fills, are fresh.
12. (mcq) A person drinks a litre of sea water. What happens to the water in their body?  
   Options: The body loses more water than it drank / The body gains one litre of water / The body stays the same / The body loses only salt  
   **Answer:** The body loses more water than it drank. *Why:* To get rid of the salt, the kidneys need more than a litre of the body's own water. So the person ends up with less water than before.
13. (draw) Draw a potato stick from plain water and one from very salty water, each being bent. Show how each looks.  
   **Answer:** The plain-water stick is stiff and snaps or bends only a little. The very salty stick is limp and bends into a curve. Arrows can show water going out of the limp stick.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the difference between a stiff stick and a limp one, and may show water leaving the cells of the limp stick.
14. (label) Label the three stores of fresh water in One Litre Earth, and write how much each holds.  
   **Answer:** ['Ice: about 3 and a half teaspoons', 'Groundwater: about 1 and a half teaspoons', 'Lakes and rivers: about one drop']. *Why:* These are the heaps on the plate. Together the ice and the groundwater make nearly all of the 5 teaspoons of fresh water.
15. (perspective) A coastal village's well has turned salty. What should the village do first?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason: pumping too hard pulls sea water inland under the ground, so digging deeper may make the water saltier. Another: a tanker is a short-term answer, while rain and gentler pumping fix the cause.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea that fresh water floats on sea water and that strong pumping mixes them. It treats the other views fairly, since each answers a different part of the problem.
### Reading the evidence (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In Freeze the Sea, the plain bowl showed ice first and the salty bowl later. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes / Salt water is warmer to begin with / The salty bowl held more water / The freezer was warmer at that bowl  
   **Answer:** Salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes. *Why:* Sea-strength water starts to freeze at about minus 2 degrees Celsius, while plain water freezes at 0. So the salty bowl had to get colder first.
2. (count) Sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in a litre. How many grams are in 4 litres?  
   **Answer:** 140. *Why:* 4 litres hold 4 times 35 grams, which is 140 grams.
3. (truefalse) Sea ice and an iceberg are made in the same way.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sea ice forms when the sea's own surface freezes, so it holds a little salt. An iceberg is a piece of glacier ice that broke off into the sea. It is made from snow, so it is fresh.
4. (mcq) A glacier slides off the land into the sea. When does the sea start to rise?  
   Options: As soon as the ice is floating in the sea / Only when the ice has fully melted / Never, because ice is already water  
   **Answer:** As soon as the ice is floating in the sea. *Why:* Floating ice pushes aside its own weight of water as soon as it is in the sea. That already raises the level. Melting later changes it very little. This matches what happened to tumbler B.
5. (write) In Ice on Land, Ice on Sea, one tumbler rose and the other did not. Say why they were different.  
   **Answer:** In tumbler A the ice was already in the water, pushing aside its own weight of water. When it melted, it filled that space, so the level stayed. In tumbler B the ice was above the water, so its melt water was new water and the level rose.. *Why:* A strong answer says where the ice was at the start, and that melt water from floating ice fills the room the ice took up, while melt water from ice above adds new water.
6. (odd) Three of these are fresh water. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Ice from the Gangotri glacier / Rain / Water in Sambhar Lake / An iceberg  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Glacier ice, rain and icebergs all began as snow or rain, which are fresh. Sambhar Lake has no river flowing out, so evaporation has left its salt behind.
7. (match) Match each living being to the way they get rid of salt.  
   **Answer:** An albatross → A salt gland above each eye drips salty liquid from her beak; A sea turtle → Salt glands near the eyes make salty tears; A sea fish → Salt is pushed out through the gills; A grey mangrove → Salt leaves through the leaves and dries as crystals. *Why:* Each has its own way of removing the salt that sea water brings in. Humans cannot do this, which is why people cannot live on sea water.
8. (mcq) Why does a lake with no river flowing out turn salty over many years?  
   Options: Water leaves only by evaporation, and the salt stays / Rain brings salt down from the clouds / The fish make salt / The lake bed makes new salt  
   **Answer:** Water leaves only by evaporation, and the salt stays. *Why:* Rivers carry a little dissolved salt into the lake. Water leaves only as vapour, and the salt cannot. So the salt builds up, year after year.
9. (sort) Is each store of fresh water easy or hard for people to reach?  
   **Answer:** A village well → Easy to reach; A river → Easy to reach; The ice on Antarctica → Hard to reach; Water deep in rock → Hard to reach; A lake near a city → Easy to reach; A glacier high in the Karakoram → Hard to reach. *Why:* People can reach rivers, lakes and wells with a pipe or a bucket. Ice far away, and water deep in rock, are too far or too hard to use.
10. (label) Label the three layers under a coral island: fresh water, sea water and dry sand.  
   **Answer:** ['Dry sand: at the top', 'Fresh water layer: floating in the middle, on the sea water', 'Sea water: below and around the fresh layer']. *Why:* Rain soaks through dry sand and floats as a layer on the heavier sea water. A shallow well reaches only the fresh layer.
11. (mcq) Why is a potato stick in very salty water limp after an hour?  
   Options: Water left its cells towards the saltier side / Salt ate away the potato / The stick soaked up the salt / The water was too cold  
   **Answer:** Water left its cells towards the saltier side. *Why:* Water moves through the thin skin of a cell towards the saltier side. The cells lose water and go slack, so the stick bends.
12. (write) Some people think a little sea water each day would keep a sailor alive. Explain why it would not.  
   **Answer:** Sea water is saltier than the body's liquids. To remove the salt, the kidneys need more water than the sea water brought. So each drink leaves the body with less water, and the sailor gets thirstier.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the kidneys can make urine only a little saltier than blood, so they need extra body water to carry the salt out.
13. (truefalse) A deeper well always gives fresher water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Near the sea, the salt water lies under the fresh water. A well that goes too deep, or is pumped too hard, can draw the salt water up.
14. (perspective) Who should decide how much groundwater the farms of a village may pump?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason: groundwater is shared under many fields, so one farm's pumping affects the others. Another: a measured limit stops arguments, but people who use the water should help to set it.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that groundwater is shared, and links the limit to how fast rain refills it. It gives the other views a fair hearing.
15. (draw) Draw the journey of one drop of fresh water from the sea back to the sea. Mark where the salt stays.  
   **Answer:** The drop rises from the sea as vapour and the salt stays in the sea. It forms a cloud, falls as rain or snow, runs down a river or soaks into the ground, and flows back to the sea, where it meets salt again.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows evaporation leaving the salt in the sea, and the river carrying fresh water back to where it picks up salt.
### Weigh the evidence (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) In Freeze the Sea, the taster did not know which drop was which. Say what could go wrong if they knew.  
   **Answer:** A taster who knew which drop was the ice might expect it to taste fresh, and taste it that way. Knowing could change what they report. Not knowing keeps the result honest.. *Why:* A strong answer says that expecting a result can change what a person tastes or sees, and that a blind test removes this.
2. (count) Body liquids hold 9 grams of salt in a litre. Sea water holds 35. About how many times saltier is the sea?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 35 divided by 9 is about 3.9, so the sea is about 4 times as salty as the liquids in the body.
3. (mcq) Sea water holds 35 grams of salt in a litre. Suppose urine holds at most 20. How much urine carries that salt?  
   Options: About 1.75 litres / About half a litre / About 35 litres / Exactly 1 litre  
   **Answer:** About 1.75 litres. *Why:* 35 grams divided by 20 grams in each litre is 1.75 litres. The person drank 1 litre, so the body loses three quarters of a litre more than it took in.
4. (count) If the Earth's water were 2 litres in all, about how many millilitres would be fresh?  
   **Answer:** 50. *Why:* About 25 parts in 1,000 are fresh. 2 litres is 2,000 millilitres. 25 times 2 is 50 millilitres.
5. (truefalse) If all the floating ice at the North Pole melted, the sea would rise by metres.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The ice at the North Pole is sea ice that already floats. It pushes aside its own weight of water, so melting it changes the sea level very little. This matches tumbler A.
6. (mcq) Which melting ice would add the most water to the sea?  
   Options: The ice sheets on Greenland and Antarctica / The sea ice around the North Pole / Ice cubes floating in a tumbler / An iceberg that is already floating  
   **Answer:** The ice sheets on Greenland and Antarctica. *Why:* These ice sheets sit on land, like the ice in the strainer. Their melt water is new water for the sea. The others already float.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the saltiest to the least salty:  
   **Answer:** ['The Dead Sea', 'The Arabian Sea', 'Chilika in the monsoon', 'Rain']. *Why:* The Dead Sea holds about 340 grams in a litre and the Arabian Sea about 36. Chilika in the monsoon is a few grams, and rain has almost none.
8. (write) A coastal town's wells turned salty after years of heavy pumping. Explain how this happens and one way to stop it.  
   **Answer:** Fresh groundwater floats on sea water and holds it back from the land. When too much fresh water is pumped out, the sea water moves inland under the ground and reaches the wells. The town can pump less, catch rain, and let the rain refill the ground.. *Why:* A strong answer says that fresh water floats on salt water, and that taking out too much lets the salt water move in. It gives a fix that lets rain refill the ground.
9. (perspective) Should a river be pumped for a city if the fish and fishers at the river's mouth lose out?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason: a river that no longer reaches the sea changes the estuary, and the fish breed where fresh and salt water meet. Another: millions of people's drinking water matters first, and the plan can still leave some flow for the river.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that taking from a river affects the estuary at its end. It does not treat the engineer, fisher or farmer as wrong, because each has a real need.
10. (mcq) Freezing sea water gives ice that is not perfectly fresh. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Pockets of salty liquid are trapped between the ice crystals / Ice makes a little new salt / The freezer adds salt to the ice / The water did not freeze at all  
   **Answer:** Pockets of salty liquid are trapped between the ice crystals. *Why:* Ice crystals push most salt out, but salty liquid gets trapped in small pockets between them. That is why old sea ice, where the pockets have drained, is nearly fresh.
11. (write) Many people say that a deeper well gives fresher water. Plan a test of this in a village near the sea.  
   **Answer:** Choose wells of different depths in the same village. Take a sample from each in the same way and at the same time of year. Tasting the water is not safe, so test them for salt with a safe method such as a grown-up's meter or the village health office. Compare depth with saltiness for all the wells.. *Why:* A strong answer compares several wells of different depths, keeps everything else the same, and measures saltiness safely, not by drinking.
12. (match) Match each change to what happens to the salt.  
   **Answer:** Sea water evaporates → The salt stays in the sea; Sea water freezes → Most salt stays in the liquid; A lake has no outlet → Salts build up year after year; Rain soaks into rock → It picks up a little salt from the rock. *Why:* Salt is left behind when water leaves as vapour or ice. A lake with no outlet loses water only by evaporation, and rain on rock picks up dissolved salts.
13. (draw) Draw two tumblers after Ice on Land, Ice on Sea. Show each water line, and label the one that rose.  
   **Answer:** Two tumblers with a band marked on each. In tumbler A the water is level with the band. In tumbler B the water is above the band, and the label says it rose.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the band and the water line in both tumblers, with only the tumbler that had ice above the water showing a rise.
14. (mcq) Which statement about the three forms of water is true?  
   Options: Most of Earth's water is liquid / Most of Earth's water is ice / Most of Earth's water is vapour / Most of Earth's water is cloud  
   **Answer:** Most of Earth's water is liquid. *Why:* About 98 of every 100 parts of Earth's water are liquid, in the sea and under the ground. Nearly 2 parts are ice, and vapour is only about 1 part in 100,000.
15. (truefalse) Most of the fresh water that people take from rivers, lakes and the ground goes to homes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Worldwide, about 7 in 10 litres go to farms, about 2 to industry, and about 1 to homes.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) About how many grams of salt does one litre of sea water hold?  
   Options: About 35 / About 3.5 / About 350 / About 0.35  
   **Answer:** About 35. *Why:* A litre of sea water holds about 35 grams of dissolved salts, which is about 6 level teaspoons. Fresh water holds under 1 gram.
2. (mcq) In One Litre Earth, what stands for all the fresh lakes and rivers in the world?  
   Options: One drop / One teaspoon / A full tumbler / Half the bottle  
   **Answer:** One drop. *Why:* All the fresh lakes and rivers together are less than one tenth of one part in 1,000. That is about one drop in a litre.
3. (mcq) A cloud is made of what?  
   Options: Tiny drops of water or tiny bits of ice / Water vapour only / Smoke / Dust  
   **Answer:** Tiny drops of water or tiny bits of ice. *Why:* Water vapour is invisible. A cloud forms when vapour condenses into tiny drops, or freezes into tiny bits of ice, which are big enough to see.
4. (mcq) Which change takes the salt out of sea water?  
   Options: Evaporation / Stirring / Pouring through a cloth / Cooling it a little  
   **Answer:** Evaporation. *Why:* Only the water particles fly into the air. The salt stays behind. Stirring and a cloth do not separate dissolved salt.
5. (mcq) Why does a person who drinks sea water become thirstier?  
   Options: The kidneys need extra water to remove the salt / Sea water is hot / The body keeps the salt and loses nothing / Sea water has no water in it  
   **Answer:** The kidneys need extra water to remove the salt. *Why:* The kidneys can make urine only a little more than half as salty as sea water. So they must use extra body water to carry the salt out.
6. (mcq) Why does fresh water float on sea water?  
   Options: A litre of it has less mass / It is colder / It has no colour / It moves faster  
   **Answer:** A litre of it has less mass. *Why:* A litre of fresh water has a mass of about 1,000 grams and a litre of sea water about 1,025 grams. The lighter water floats.
7. (mcq) Which melting ice raises the sea?  
   Options: Ice on land, such as on Greenland / Ice floating at the North Pole / Ice cubes floating in a tumbler / Sea ice that already floats  
   **Answer:** Ice on land, such as on Greenland. *Why:* Ice on land adds new water to the sea. Floating ice already pushes aside its own weight of water, so it changes the level very little.
8. (mcq) Why does a fair test change only one thing at a time?  
   Options: So that we know what caused the difference / So the test is quicker / So there is less to wash up / So the result is bigger  
   **Answer:** So that we know what caused the difference. *Why:* If two things change, we cannot tell which one caused the result. In Freeze the Sea, only the salt differed between the bowls.
9. (mcq) What takes the largest share of the fresh water that people use, worldwide?  
   Options: Farms / Homes / Industry / Swimming pools  
   **Answer:** Farms. *Why:* About 7 of every 10 litres go to farms, because growing plants take up a great deal of water. Homes use about 1 in 10.
10. (mcq) Which holds more fresh water: all the lakes and rivers, or the groundwater?  
   Options: Groundwater, about 100 times more / Lakes and rivers, about 100 times more / They hold the same amount / Neither holds any  
   **Answer:** Groundwater, about 100 times more. *Why:* In One Litre Earth, groundwater is about 1 and a half teaspoons, and all the lakes and rivers are about one drop. Most liquid fresh water is under the ground.
11. (mcq) An albatross drinks sea water and stays healthy. How?  
   Options: Salt glands above her eyes remove the salt / She drinks only a very little / Her kidneys are the same as ours / The salt falls out of her feathers  
   **Answer:** Salt glands above her eyes remove the salt. *Why:* The glands take salt out of her blood and make a very salty liquid, which drips from her beak. Humans have no such glands.
12. (mcq) Which water is brackish?  
   Options: Water where a river meets the sea / Rain / Glacier ice / Water from a village well in the hills  
   **Answer:** Water where a river meets the sea. *Why:* Brackish water is saltier than fresh water but less salty than the sea. Rivers and the sea mix where a river reaches the coast.

## Applications
- A hospital drip bag holds normal saline, which has 9 grams of salt in a litre. That is the same saltiness as blood, so the cells do not swell or shrink.
- People make pickles and dry fish with a lot of salt. The salt pulls water out of the food and out of germs, so the food keeps for months.
- A sea-water plant such as the one at Minjur near Chennai makes drinking water from the sea. It pushes sea water through very fine sheets at high pressure, which hold back the salt.
- Salt pans at Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu and in Gujarat let the Sun evaporate sea water. The salt is left in the pan. Gujarat makes most of India's salt this way.
- Oral rehydration solution, which is sold in small packets in India, mixes the right amounts of salt and sugar in water. It helps a body that has lost water in a stomach illness to take water in.
- Himalayan glaciers feed the Ganga, the Indus and the Brahmaputra in summer. Cities and farms along these rivers plan their water around the snow and ice.
- Villages on coral islands such as those of Lakshadweep keep wells shallow and pump gently, so that the thin layer of rain water does not mix with the sea water under it.

## Spotted around us
- A white crust of salt on the wall of a house near the coast, or on a coconut tree trunk.
- A label on a bottle of packaged drinking water. It lists the dissolved minerals in milligrams in a litre.
- An ice cube in a glass. A small part of it stands above the water and the rest is below.
- The place where a river meets the sea, if you live near one. The brown river water spreads over the blue sea water.
- A drop on your tongue from a tear that ran down your cheek. It tastes a little salty.
- Salt pans seen from a train window near Thoothukudi or in Gujarat, with white heaps of salt beside shallow ponds.
- A drip bag at a clinic with the words 0.9 per cent sodium chloride.
- The overhead tank on your building, and the float that shuts off the water when the tank is full.

## Open questions
- **How much water is locked deep inside the Earth?** A speck of a deep mineral inside one Brazilian diamond held water. One speck is not enough to say how much water is in all the deep rock, which nobody can drill into.
- **How fast will the ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica melt, and how high will the sea rise?** It depends on how the ice flows and breaks, and on how much warmer Earth becomes. Different computer models give different answers.
- **How much groundwater is left under India and under the world?** The water is hidden underground. Satellites show that it is falling in some places, but measuring the amount that is left is very hard.
- **Where did Earth's water come from?** Some scientists think much of it came with the rock that formed Earth. Others think a large share arrived later on asteroids and comets. Nobody yet knows how much each gave.
- **Can taking salt out of sea water ever be cheap and gentle enough for everyone?** It needs a great deal of energy, and it leaves very salty waste. Engineers are looking for ways to use less energy and to put the waste to use.

## Connected maths
- In One Litre Earth, 25 millilitres of the 1,000 are fresh. What fraction of the bottle is fresh, in its simplest form? What percentage is that? **One fortieth of the bottle, which is 2 and a half out of every 100.**. 25 out of 1,000 is 25 over 1,000. Divide the top and the bottom by 25 to get 1 over 40. As a percentage, 25 divided by 1,000 is 0.025, which is 2.5 in every 100. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- A tumbler holds about 13 tablespoons. After freezing, the melted ice measured 6 tablespoons and the liquid measured 5. How much water was lost, and what fraction froze? **2 tablespoons were lost. A little under half froze, which is 6 out of 13.**. 6 plus 5 is 11. The tumbler held 13, so 13 minus 11 is 2 tablespoons lost in rinsing and straining. The ice was 6 out of 13, which is a little less than half, because half of 13 is 6 and a half. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L5`)
- Sea water holds 35 grams of salt in a litre. A bucket holds 8 litres of sea water, and the Sun dries it all. How many grams of salt are left, and about how many level teaspoons is that if one holds 6 grams? **280 grams, which is about 47 teaspoons.**. 8 times 35 is 280 grams. 280 divided by 6 is about 46.7, so about 47 teaspoons. Evaporation took the water and left every gram of salt. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- SEA holds about 30 grams of salt in a litre. A lagoon mix is 3 parts of sea water and 7 parts of plain water. How salty is it? Is it saltier or less salty than the liquids in your body, which hold 9 grams? **9 grams in a litre. It is about as salty as the liquids in your body.**. 3 parts out of 10 is three tenths. Three tenths of 30 is 9. The body's liquids also hold about 9 grams in a litre, so the mix is about the same. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Suppose urine can hold at most 20 grams of salt in a litre. How many litres of urine does a person need to carry out the 35 grams of salt in 1 litre of sea water? How much more water than the person drank is lost? **1.75 litres of urine, so 0.75 litres more than the person drank.**. 35 divided by 20 is 1.75. The person drank 1 litre, so the body loses 1.75 minus 1, which is 0.75 litres more than it took in. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A roof of 80 square metres gets 1,200 millimetres of rain in a year. How many litres fall on it? A tank catches 7 tenths of that. How many litres is that? **96,000 litres fall. The tank catches 67,200 litres.**. One millimetre on one square metre is one litre. 80 times 1,200 is 96,000 litres. Seven tenths of 96,000 is 9,600 times 7, which is 67,200 litres. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L5`)
- In One Litre Earth, the ice is 17 millilitres. About 9 tenths of the world's ice is on Antarctica. How many millilitres of the ice is that? About how many teaspoons? **About 15 millilitres, which is about 3 teaspoons.**. Nine tenths of 17 is 15.3 millilitres. A teaspoon holds 5 millilitres, and 15.3 divided by 5 is about 3. So the ice of Antarctica is about 3 of the 5 teaspoons of fresh water. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
