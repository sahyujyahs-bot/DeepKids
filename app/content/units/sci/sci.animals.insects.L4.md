# Ant and bee colonies, and how insects pollinate flowers and recycle waste
`sci.animals.insects.L4`

**Summary.** A mango tree opens thousands of tiny flowers. No flower becomes a mango unless pollen is carried to that flower. Insects do most of that carrying. Ants and honeybees live in colonies, which are large families with one mother. The workers collect food for the young. While honeybee workers collect that food from flowers, they pollinate the flowers. Flies pollinate flowers too. Ants, flies, beetles and termites clear away dead things and waste. Your child tests whether a hairy carrier or a smooth carrier moves more haldi 'pollen' from box to box. They also time how fast ants clear 20 crumbs from the floor.

**Intro.** *A bee lands on a pumpkin flower, drinks and flies away. Some weeks later a pumpkin hangs where that flower was. The bee came for a drink. So how did her visit make a pumpkin?* The bee is hairy, and yellow pollen from the flower stuck to her hairs. At the next pumpkin flower, some of that pollen rubbed off. A flower needs pollen from another flower before seeds and a fruit can grow. The bee was collecting food for her colony, which is her family of thousands of sisters. In this unit you find out how a colony lives. You test why a hairy body carries pollen so well. You also watch another kind of insect work: ants clearing crumbs from your floor.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say who lives in an ant colony or a bee colony, and what each member does.
1. I can explain how a new colony begins, and why a colony lives longer than its workers.
2. I can explain what pollination is, using the words pollen, anther and stigma.
3. I can test fairly whether a hairy or a smooth carrier moves more pollen, and say what my results show.
4. I can explain why insects visit flowers, and how flowers draw them.
5. I can name pollinators other than honeybees, and say which foods need insect pollinators.
6. I can explain how ants, flies, beetles and termites recycle dead things and waste.
7. I can say what harms insect pollinators and recyclers, and name one thing my family can do.

## Terms
- **Social insect**: An insect who lives in a large family that shares one nest and shares the work. *e.g.* Ants, honeybees and termites are social insects. A housefly lives alone.
- **Colony**: One family of social insects living in one nest. It can hold a few dozen members or many thousands. *e.g.* All the ants walking in one line on your kitchen wall belong to one colony.
- **Queen**: The mother of the colony. She lays the eggs. *e.g.* A honeybee queen can lay more than 1,000 eggs in one day.
- **Worker**: A daughter of the queen who does the work of the colony and usually has no young of her own. *e.g.* The ants you see carrying crumbs are all workers, and all of them are sisters.
- **Larva (plural: larvae)**: The young of an insect after the egg hatches. A larva looks like a small soft grub and must be fed. *e.g.* Worker bees feed each larva in her wax cell many times a day.
- **Nectar**: A sweet liquid that flowers make. Insects drink it for energy. *e.g.* Pull a red ixora flower from its stalk and taste the tiny sweet drop at the base.
- **Pollen**: A fine powder made by flowers. A grain of pollen must reach another flower before seeds can grow. *e.g.* Touch the middle of a hibiscus flower and yellow pollen comes off on your finger.
- **Anther**: The part of a flower that makes pollen. *e.g.* In a hibiscus, the anthers are the small yellow knobs on the long tube in the middle.
- **Stigma**: The sticky tip in a flower that catches pollen. *e.g.* In a hibiscus, the stigma is the five round red tips at the very end of the tube.
- **Pollination**: The moving of pollen from an anther to a stigma. After pollination a flower can grow seeds and fruit. *e.g.* A bee carries pollen from one pumpkin flower to another. That is pollination.
- **Pollinator**: An animal who carries pollen from flower to flower. *e.g.* Bees, flies, butterflies, moths and some beetles are pollinators. So are some birds and bats.
- **Flower constancy**: The habit of visiting only one kind of flower on one trip. *e.g.* A honeybee working sunflowers flies past the marigolds and goes to the next sunflower.
- **Buzz pollination**: Shaking pollen out of a flower by buzzing against it. *e.g.* A big black carpenter bee buzzes on a brinjal flower, and pollen showers out.
- **Scavenger**: An animal who eats dead animals, dropped food or waste. *e.g.* Ants who carry away a dead moth are scavengers.
- **Recycler**: A living being who breaks down dead things and waste, so that the material can be used again. *e.g.* Termites are recyclers. They turn a dead branch back into soil.
- **Pest**: A name people give to an animal who eats or spoils something people want. *e.g.* A fly is called a pest in a kitchen. The same fly pollinates mango flowers in an orchard.

## Explanations
### A colony is one large family · *defined*
**Definition:** A colony is a family of social insects who share one nest. It has one or a few queens who lay eggs, many workers who do the work, and the young.

In a colony of ants or honeybees, the queen is the mother. The workers are her daughters, so they are all sisters. Workers build the nest, clean it, guard it, find food and feed the young. Most workers never lay eggs that grow into new workers. The young begin as eggs and hatch into larvae, who are soft grubs and must be fed. Males are few. A male honeybee is called a drone. Males do no nest work, and they live only to mate with a new queen. A honeybee colony holds tens of thousands of bees. Some ant colonies hold a few dozen ants, and others hold millions. India has several kinds of honeybee. The rock bee builds one huge open comb under a ledge or a water tank. The Indian hive bee nests in hollows and in bee boxes. The tiny stingless bee nests in cracks in walls. Termites also live in colonies, but they are not ants. A termite colony has a king as well as a queen, and termite workers are both female and male.
- e.g. A line of black ants on a kitchen wall: every ant is a worker, and every worker is a sister.
- e.g. A rock bee comb under a flyover, covered by a curtain of thousands of bees.
- e.g. Red weaver ants in a mango tree, living in nests of leaves glued together with silk.
### How a colony begins, grows and makes new colonies · *defined*
**Definition:** A colony begins with one mated queen. It grows as she lays eggs and her daughters raise them. A grown colony makes new queens, and they start new colonies.

After the first heavy rain, winged ants pour out of many nests on the same evening. These are young queens and males, and they mate in the air. Each queen then lands, breaks off her own wings and digs a small hole. She seals herself in and lays her first eggs. She does not eat for weeks. She feeds the first larvae from her own body, using up the muscles that once moved her wings. When her first daughters grow up, they open the nest and bring food. After that the queen only lays eggs. Honeybees start a new colony in another way. The old queen flies off with about half the workers, in a crowd called a swarm. The bees who stay behind raise a new queen. A worker honeybee lives about six weeks in summer. A queen honeybee lives a few years, and an ant queen can live far longer. One black garden ant queen kept in a laboratory lived for 28 years. Workers die every day and new ones hatch, so the colony goes on.
- e.g. Winged ants and winged termites round a tube light on the first wet evening of the monsoon.
- e.g. A queen ant alone in a sealed hole with a small heap of eggs.
- e.g. A swarm of honeybees hanging from a branch like a brown bag while scouts look for a new home.
### What workers bring home, and what they build · *defined*
**Definition:** Workers collect food for the whole colony, above all for the larvae. They also build a nest that stores the food and protects the young.

A honeybee colony needs two foods from flowers. Nectar is mostly sugar and water, and it gives energy. The bees dry nectar into honey, which keeps for months. Pollen is rich in protein, and it is the food that makes larvae grow. A bee packs pollen into two baskets of stiff hairs on her back legs and carries it home. Honeybees build their comb from wax, which comes out of their own bodies in thin flakes. Each cell has six sides. Some cells hold honey, some hold pollen and some hold one larva each. Ants do not make wax. Most ants dig rooms and tunnels in soil or wood. They carry seeds, dead insects and sweet crumbs home, and they feed the larvae in a nursery room. Weaver ants build in trees. A row of workers pulls two leaves together. Other workers hold larvae in their jaws, and the larvae give out silk that glues the leaves. Many ants also guard small plant-sucking insects and drink the sweet drops they give off.
- e.g. A honeybee flying home with a yellow lump of pollen on each back leg.
- e.g. A wax comb with capped honey at the top and larvae in the middle.
- e.g. A ball of mango leaves in a tree, stitched with white silk, with red ants running over it.
### Pollination: what a flower needs before a fruit can grow · *defined*
**Definition:** Pollination is the moving of pollen from an anther to a stigma. After pollination, a flower can grow seeds, and a fruit grows around the seeds.

A flower is the part of a plant that makes seeds. The anthers make pollen, which is a fine powder of tiny grains. The stigma is a sticky tip that catches pollen. When a grain of the right pollen lands on a stigma, it grows a thin tube down into the flower. The tube reaches a tiny egg cell, and a seed begins. A plant cannot walk, so something must carry the pollen. Most plants do better with pollen from another plant of the same kind, and many must have it. A pumpkin vine has two sorts of flower. One sort has only anthers, and the other has only a stigma with a tiny pumpkin below it. Unless pollen travels between them, the tiny pumpkin turns yellow and drops off. Grasses such as rice, wheat and maize let the wind carry their pollen. Nearly nine out of every ten kinds of flowering plant use animals, and most of those animals are insects.
- e.g. A pumpkin flower with a small green ball under it, waiting for pollen from another pumpkin flower.
- e.g. Yellow dust on a bee who has just left a sunflower.
- e.g. A maize plant shaking pollen from the top of the stem into the wind, with no insect needed.
### Why insects make good pollen carriers · *defined*
**Definition:** Insects visit flowers to feed. Their hairy bodies pick up pollen at one flower and leave some of it at the next.

A flower offers food. Nectar is a sweet drink, and pollen is a rich meal. A flower also shows where the food is. Bright petals, strong scents and lines on the petals all point to the nectar. Bees see ultraviolet light, which human eyes cannot see, and many flowers carry ultraviolet marks. To reach the nectar, the insect must push past the anthers and the stigma. A bee is covered in hairs, and under a microscope each hair is branched like a tiny feather. Pollen grains catch among the branches. A flying bee also picks up a small electric charge, and pollen can jump a short gap onto her body. She combs most of the pollen into the baskets on her legs. The grains that pollinate are mostly the ones left on parts she cannot reach well. One more habit helps the flower. On one trip, a bee usually keeps to one kind of flower. This is called flower constancy. So pumpkin pollen goes to a pumpkin flower, and not to a marigold.
- e.g. A bee crawling deep into a pumpkin flower and coming out yellow all over.
- e.g. Dark lines on a petal that lead towards the middle of the flower.
- e.g. A honeybee who visits twenty sunflowers in a row and ignores every other flower.
### Many pollinators, each suited to some flowers · *defined*
**Definition:** A pollinator is any animal who carries pollen between flowers. Honeybees are only one kind among many thousands.

There are about 20,000 kinds of bee in the world, and most of them do not live in colonies. Each mother builds a small nest alone. Some flowers hold their pollen inside closed tubes with a small hole at the tip. Brinjal and tomato flowers are like this. A honeybee cannot get that pollen out. A carpenter bee can. She grips the flower and buzzes her flight muscles a few hundred times each second, and the pollen showers out. This is buzz pollination. Flies are pollinators too. Mango flowers are small and open, with nectar that a short mouth can reach. In many orchards most of the visitors are flies. Cocoa flowers are pollinated by midges, which are tiny flies, so chocolate depends on them. Hawkmoths pollinate pale, scented flowers that open at night. Butterflies, beetles and wasps carry pollen as well. Every kind of fig tree is pollinated by one special kind of tiny fig wasp. Some birds and bats are pollinators, though they are not insects.
- e.g. A large shiny black carpenter bee buzzing loudly on a brinjal flower.
- e.g. Green flies and striped hoverflies on a bunch of mango flowers.
- e.g. A hawkmoth hovering at a jasmine flower after dark with her long tongue unrolled.
### How much of our food needs insect pollinators · *defined*
**Definition:** About three out of every four main kinds of food crop give more or better food when animals pollinate them. Most of those animals are insects.

In 2007 a team of scientists checked the world's main food crops. Of 115 crops, 87 gained from animal pollinators. That is about three out of every four. Mango, pumpkin, cucumber, apple, mustard, coriander, cardamom, coffee and almonds are all on that list. Some of these crops give almost nothing without pollinators. Others give a smaller or poorer harvest. The same study found something that surprises many people. By weight, only about one third of the world's food comes from those crops. The reason is that people eat huge amounts of rice, wheat and maize, and these are pollinated by the wind. Bananas grow their fruit with no pollination at all, and potatoes grow from pieces of potato. So people would not starve without pollinators. But most fruits, many vegetables, nuts, oil seeds and spices would become rare and costly. Those foods hold many of the vitamins that our bodies need.
- e.g. Cardamom from the hills of Kerala, which sets very few pods without bees.
- e.g. Apple growers in Himachal Pradesh, who rent bee boxes when their trees flower.
- e.g. A plate of plain rice, which needed wind and no insect.
### Insects as recyclers · *defined*
**Definition:** A recycler breaks down dead things and waste, so that the material can be used again. Many insects are recyclers.

Every day, leaves fall, animals leave dung, and animals die. Insects are often the first to arrive. Ants are scavengers, which means they eat dead animals and dropped food. Workers cut up a dead moth, carry the pieces home and feed them to the larvae. Flies lay eggs on dead animals and rotting food. The eggs hatch into larvae called maggots, and the maggots eat the soft parts within days. Dung beetles bury dung in the ground, where it feeds the soil. Termites eat dead wood and dry leaves, with the help of microbes and fungi. Most kinds of cockroach live in forests and eat rotting leaves. These insects cut dead things into small pieces, bury them and spread them. Fungi and bacteria then finish the work, and plants take up what is left. Insects do a great deal of this work in cities too. Scientists in New York studied ants and other small animals on a few streets. In a year these animals ate dropped food equal to 60,000 hot dogs.
- e.g. Ants taking a dead cockroach apart on a kitchen floor.
- e.g. A fallen coconut frond with termites and soil under it.
- e.g. A dung beetle rolling a ball of cow dung along a path.
### One insect can do two jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Many insects have a young stage and an adult stage that eat different foods. So the same insect can be a recycler when young and a pollinator when grown.

A green blowfly lays her eggs on a dead fish or a dead rat. Her maggots eat it and grow fast. In warm weather they turn into flies in about two weeks. The adult fly feeds in a different way. She drinks sweet liquids, and flower nectar is one of them. Her body is covered in bristles, and pollen sticks to them. In a mango orchard she is one of the main pollinators. Hoverflies are striped like bees. The larvae of many hoverflies live in dirty water and rotting plants and feed there. The adults hover at flowers and carry pollen. The same is true of many beetles. A heap of rotting kitchen waste and a tree full of flowers can both belong to one insect's life. People call the fly a pest in the kitchen, because she can carry germs to food. In the orchard she is doing work that the tree needs.
- e.g. A shiny green fly with yellow pollen on her bristles, walking over mango flowers.
- e.g. White maggots clearing a dead fish on a rubbish heap in two or three days.
- e.g. A striped hoverfly hanging in the air in front of a coriander flower.
### What harms insect workers, and what helps them · *defined*
**Definition:** Insects are harmed by poison sprays, by the loss of flowers and nesting places, and by bright lights at night. They are helped by flowers, by unsprayed places and by darkness.

A pesticide is a poison used to kill insects who eat crops. Most pesticides also kill bees, flies and ants, and the insects who hunt the crop-eaters. Some pesticides do not kill a bee at once. They harm her memory, so that she cannot find her way home. A colony needs flowers in every month of the year. When a field grows only one crop, there are flowers for two weeks and then none. Street lights pull moths away from the flowers they would pollinate at night. In 63 nature reserves in Germany, scientists weighed the flying insects they caught each year. After 27 years the weight had fallen by about three quarters. There are few such counts from India, so nobody knows the full picture here. Some farmers use insects in place of poison. Fruit growers in China have put weaver ant nests in their orange trees for about 1,700 years. The ants hunt the insects who eat the fruit. A family can help by growing flowering plants, and by never spraying a plant that is in flower.
- e.g. A tulsi or curry leaf plant left to flower on a balcony, with small bees on it.
- e.g. A farmer who sprays in the evening, after the bees have gone home, and never on open flowers.
- e.g. A weaver ant nest left in a mango tree, because the ants hunt fruit-eating insects.
### Are honeybees enough, or do crops need wild pollinators too? · *debated*
**Definition:** Farmers and scientists agree that crops need pollinators. They disagree about how far kept honeybees can replace wild insects.

Honeybees can be kept in boxes and moved to a field on the day the flowers open. That makes them very useful to farmers. But a study of 600 fields in many countries, published in 2013, gave a surprise. Fruit grew better in every crop that wild insects visited more often. Honeybee visits made a clear difference in only about one crop in seven.
- e.g. An apple grower who rents bee boxes for two weeks each spring.
- e.g. A brinjal field beside a patch of wild shrubs, where carpenter bees nest in dead wood.
- **Many beekeepers and farm advisers:** Kept honeybees are the pollinators we can count on. A farmer can bring 40,000 bees to a field in one box, on the right day. Wild insects come and go, and nobody can order them.
- **Scientists who study wild pollinators, such as Lucas Garibaldi and his team:** Wild bees and flies often pollinate better at each visit. They work in cool or windy weather, and some can buzz pollen out of flowers that honeybees cannot open. Bee boxes add to their work and do not replace it.
- **Scientists who worry about crowding:** Very many kept honeybees in one place may take the nectar and pollen that wild bees need. So bringing more hives can sometimes harm the wild pollinators nearby. This is still being tested.
### Is a colony one animal? · *debated*
**Definition:** A superorganism is a group of animals that lives so closely together that the group behaves like one body. Scientists disagree about whether a colony should be called one.

A worker bee cannot live long alone, and she usually has no young of her own. The colony as a whole feeds, grows, keeps itself warm and makes new colonies. In 1911 the American ant scientist William Morton Wheeler wrote that an ant colony is like one living body.
- e.g. A honeybee colony keeps the nursery at about 35 degrees Celsius, summer and winter, as a body keeps its warmth.
- e.g. A colony that splits into two swarms, as one cell splits into two cells.
- **William Morton Wheeler in 1911, and later Bert Hölldobler and Edward O. Wilson:** The colony is the animal. The queen is like the part of a body that makes young. The workers are like the parts that feed and defend the body. What grows, survives and has young is the colony.
- **Scientists who study single insects:** Each bee and each ant is a whole animal. She senses, learns, remembers and chooses for herself. Workers in one nest sometimes even struggle against each other. Calling the colony one body hides all of this.
- **A third view:** Both ways of looking are useful. Your own body is a colony of billions of cells, and each cell is alive. Whether we see one being or many depends on how closely we look.

## Mostly agreed
- Most flowers cannot make seeds unless pollen is carried to the stigma. Insects carry the pollen for most kinds of flowering plant. *How we know:* Scientists cover flowers with fine net bags, so that insects cannot reach them. Many kinds of plant then set few seeds or none. A count published in 2011 found that nearly nine in ten kinds of flowering plant are pollinated by animals.
- About three out of four of the world's main food crops give more or better food with animal pollinators. *How we know:* In 2007 Alexandra-Maria Klein and her team checked 115 leading crops. They found that 87 of them gain from animal pollination.
- In a honeybee or ant colony the workers are daughters of the queen. *How we know:* Beekeepers and scientists have watched queens laying eggs in hives with glass walls. Tests of the workers' genes show that they share one mother.
- A colony lives much longer than its workers do. *How we know:* Scientists mark young bees with dots of paint. In summer the marked bees are gone in about six weeks, and the colony is still there.
- A hairy body carries more pollen than a smooth one, and bee hairs are branched. *How we know:* Under a microscope, bee hairs look like tiny feathers with pollen grains caught in them. Counts of pollen on insects' bodies show that hairier insects carry more.
- Insects remove a large share of the dead animals, dung and dropped food around us. *How we know:* Scientists put out weighed food in cages that let insects in and keep bigger animals out. The food still disappears, and they weigh how much has gone.

## How we found out
**Christian Konrad Sprengel, a German schoolteacher (1750 to 1816), and later Charles Darwin, Sprengel made his first observation in 1787 and published his book in 1793. Darwin's book on orchids came out in 1862..** About 250 years ago, most people who studied plants thought that a flower pollinated itself. Insects on flowers were seen as thieves who stole the nectar. Christian Konrad Sprengel was the head of a school in Spandau, in Germany. In 1787 he looked closely at a wild geranium and saw tiny hairs at the base of each petal. He worked out that the hairs keep rain off the nectar. Then he asked why a flower should protect its nectar, and for whom. He watched hundreds of kinds of flowers for years. He saw that coloured lines on petals point to the nectar. He saw that in many flowers the pollen is ripe before the stigma is ready. Such a flower cannot use its own pollen. Insects had to be carrying it across. In 1793 he published a book with careful drawings of about 500 kinds of flowers. Hardly anybody believed him, and the next year he lost his post at the school. In 1862 Charles Darwin, who had read the book, published his own tests on orchids. He wrote that Sprengel had been right.  
*What people believed before:* That a flower pollinates itself, and that insects only steal nectar. Sprengel showed that in many flowers the pollen and the stigma are ready at different times. So the pollen must come from another flower, and insects carry it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If bees disappeared, humans would die out within four years.** (A saying often printed with Albert Einstein's name.; *rejected*)  
  For: About three out of four main crops gain from animal pollinators. Without them, fruits, nuts and many vegetables would become scarce.  
  Against: No record shows that Einstein said or wrote this. Rice, wheat and maize are pollinated by wind and give most of the world's food by weight. People would eat a much poorer diet, but they would not die out.
- **Honeybees do nearly all the pollinating that matters.** (A common belief, and the view of some farm advisers.; *debated*)  
  For: Honeybees are many, they visit a great range of flowers, and farmers can move them to a field in boxes.  
  Against: A 2013 study of 600 fields found that wild insects raised the fruit harvest in every crop studied. Honeybee visits did so in about one crop in seven.
- **Flies are dirty pests with no use.** (A common belief in homes and shops.; *rejected*)  
  For: Houseflies walk on waste and then on food, and they can carry germs that make people ill.  
  Against: Fly maggots clear away dead animals and rotting food. Adult flies are main pollinators of mango, cocoa and many other plants. Keeping flies off food is wise. Wiping out flies would cost us fruit.
- **Termites are white ants.** (The everyday name for termites in English and in several Indian languages.; *rejected*)  
  For: Termites are small, live in big colonies with a queen, and build nests in soil, as ants do.  
  Against: Termites are close relatives of cockroaches. Ants are close relatives of wasps and bees. A termite colony has a king, and termite workers are both female and male.
- **An ant colony is really one animal.** (William Morton Wheeler in 1911, and Bert Hölldobler and Edward O. Wilson in their 2008 book The Superorganism.; *debated*)  
  For: The colony feeds, grows, defends itself and makes new colonies. A single worker cannot do these things alone.  
  Against: Each ant senses, learns and chooses. Workers sometimes lay their own eggs, and other workers remove them. So the members do not always act as one.
- **Spraying poison is the best way to protect a crop from insects.** (Many sellers of pesticides, and many farmers since the 1950s.; *debated*)  
  For: A spray works within hours, and it can save a crop that is being eaten.  
  Against: Sprays also kill pollinators and the insects who hunt the crop-eaters. The crop-eaters often return in greater numbers. Farmers who keep weaver ants, or who spray less, can harvest as much fruit.

## Roots
In the Hairy or Smooth test you carried haldi from box to box on a cotton bud. In the Clean-up Count you watched ants clear crumbs from your floor. Here we ask who makes a fruit, when a plant and an insect both have a part in it.

**Deep truth.** In the Hairy or Smooth test, the cotton bud left haldi on far more boxes than the pen. A bee is covered in hairs, and she visits flowers to collect food for her colony. Pollen clings to her hairs and rubs off on the next flower. So many fruits begin with a few grains of pollen that an insect spilled while collecting food.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say that a tree makes fruit, and we call an insect a helper or a pest. We speak as though each lived a separate life. A mango needs the tree and also an insect who was busy feeding herself or her family.  
*Evidence:* In the Hairy or Smooth test, the cotton bud went on leaving haldi for many boxes after one dip. In the Raisin at the Bottom, the hand that only fetched raisins left haldi on the second flower's collar. About three out of four main food crops gain from animal pollinators.

- *who am i:* Much of your body was built from foods that an insect pollinated. You are also a kind of colony: billions of living cells that share one body.
- *what is life:* Living beings feed on each other and pass material on. A dead moth becomes young ants, and a fallen leaf becomes soil for a new plant.
- *what is this world:* Pollinating and recycling go on around you every day. Nobody orders this work and nobody pays for it, and all our food depends on it.

A flower cannot walk to another flower, and a bee cannot make sugar from sunlight. Each has what the other lacks. Flowers and insects have lived together for more than 100 million years, and each has shaped the other's body. Many wise teachers taught that all beings share one life in many forms. This unit shows a similar idea in a practical way. A mango is made by a tree, a fly and the recyclers in the soil, all working together.

- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.5.1:** This part of the text is called the honey teaching. It says: this earth is honey for all beings, and all beings are honey for this earth. (The Sanskrit word is madhu, which means honey. The teaching says that each thing feeds the others and is fed by them. A flower feeds a bee, and the bee's visit starts the flower's seeds.)
- **Dhammapada, verse 49:** A bee takes nectar from a flower and flies away without harming the flower's colour or scent. A wise person should go through a village in the same way. (The verse holds up the bee as someone who takes without harming. This unit adds something the verse does not say: the flower gains from the visit.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper, and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in it. He called this interbeing. (Look at a mango in the same way. The fly who carried the pollen is in the mango. So is the dead fish that fed the fly when she was young.)
- Science (settled): Nearly nine in ten kinds of flowering plant are pollinated by animals, and most of those animals are insects.
- Science (debated): Some scientists call an ant or bee colony a superorganism, one living body made of many animals. Others say this hides the fact that each insect senses and chooses for herself.
- Other view, Many biologists who study flowers and their visitors: A flower and a bee are not partners who look after each other. Each takes as much as possible and gives as little as possible. Carpenter bees often bite a hole in the side of a flower and drink the nectar without touching the pollen. Some orchids offer no nectar at all and are still visited. On this view pollination is a bargain with cheating on both sides, and words like 'helping' mislead us.
- Practice, One bite, traced back (3 min): Hold one raisin or one slice of fruit in your palm. Do not eat it yet. This fruit began as a flower. Close your eyes and think of the insect who visited that flower. Think of the soil under the plant, and the recyclers who made that soil. Now eat slowly. Who had a part in this bite?
- Ask: A bee pollinates a flower while she collects food. Is the pollinating her work, the flower's work, or the work of both?
- Ask: A mango needed a tree, a fly, and the dead fish that fed the fly when she was young. Whose mango is it?
- Ask: Workers die and new ones hatch, and the colony goes on. Your cells do the same. What stays the same in you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A bee goes to a flower for food, and the flower gets pollinated. Who is helping whom? (hint: Think about what each one gains, and whether either of them needs to know about the other's gain.)
- *deep:* A worker bee lives about six weeks, and her colony lives for years. Which one is the animal? (hint: Your skin cells live a few weeks, and you live for many years.)
- *deep:* A fly's young eat a rotting fish, and the grown fly pollinates mango flowers. Is she a pest or a helper? (hint: Think about who is giving the name. A cook in a kitchen and a farmer in an orchard see the same fly very differently.)
- *big:* People call an insect a pest when she eats our crop. What might she call us? (hint: A field of one crop is a huge meal to an insect. A spray is a danger to her whole family.)
- *big:* Wild bees pollinate a whole village's crops for no pay. If one farmer's spray kills them, who has lost something? (hint: Think about things that everybody uses and nobody owns, such as clean air.)
- *big:* If every recycler stopped work for one year, what would your street look like? (hint: Count the leaves, the dung, the dropped food and the dead insects of one day, and multiply.)

## Story: The Flies in the Mango Flowers
*Yazhini, Karthik, Paati and their orchard are made up, and so is the habit of Paati's mother. These facts are real: mango trees around Krishnagiri in Tamil Nadu flower in the cool months and fruit in summer. Mango flowers are tiny and open, and each bunch holds hundreds or thousands of them. In many orchards most of the visitors are flies, including green blowflies and striped hoverflies, with some small bees. Blowfly maggots feed on dead fish and other dead animals. Mango growers in some countries put out rotting fish or meat at flowering time to bring more flies.*

Every February, Paati's forty mango trees near Krishnagiri changed colour. The dark green turned pale gold, because every branch ended in a bunch of flowers. Each bunch was as long as Yazhini's arm, and each one held more tiny flowers than she could count.

This February her cousin Karthik had come from Chennai for a week. He was eleven, two years older than Yazhini, and he did not like the orchard at all.

"It smells," he said on the first morning.

He was right. Behind the cowshed, Paati kept a heap. Fish heads went on the heap, and fruit skins, and old straw from the cow's stall. Shiny green flies sat on it in the sun. The day before, Yazhini had turned the heap with a stick. Fat white maggots were wriggling in the fish heads, and she had covered them again quickly.

"Flies carry germs," Karthik said. "At home we would spray them. Paati, shall I ask Mama to bring a can from town?"

Paati was tying up the calf. "We will see," she said.

Yazhini wanted her cousin to like the orchard. "Tomorrow I will help you bury the heap," she told him. "Today I have school work."

Her teacher, Revathi Miss, had asked every child to choose one tree in flower. They had to watch one bunch of flowers for fifteen minutes and put a stroke in the notebook for every visitor. Yazhini had already ruled three columns. Bees. Butterflies. Others.

She chose the old Neelum tree by the well, because one bunch hung low enough to see. Karthik came too. He held his nose as a joke, though the heap was far behind them.

For a minute, nobody came. Then someone landed, and Yazhini lifted her pencil over 'Bees'.

The visitor was not a bee. She was a green fly, as shiny as a new steel plate. She walked over the tiny flowers and dipped her mouth into one after another.

"Shoo," said Karthik, and raised his hand.

"Don't! I have to count her." Yazhini put a stroke under 'Others'.

Another green fly came. Then a smaller fly, striped like a bee, who hung in the air before she landed. Then two more green ones. A small dark bee arrived, worked quickly along the bunch and left. Ants walked up and down the stalk.

After fifteen minutes Yazhini added up her strokes. Bees, 4. Butterflies, 0. Others, 23. She counted the 'Others' again from memory. Four were ants. Nineteen were flies.

"Your tree is dirty," said Karthik.

Yazhini did not answer. One green fly had stopped on a flower just in front of her nose. The fly's back was covered in short stiff hairs. Stuck all over the hairs was a fine yellow dust.

The fly stepped across to the next flower. A little of the yellow dust stayed behind, on the tip in the middle of the flower she had left.

Yazhini looked back towards the cowshed. She looked at the flies in the tree. She looked at the cowshed again.

"Karthik," she said slowly. "What do bees do for flowers?"

"They carry pollen. Everyone knows that. Then the fruit comes."

"Look at her back."

He bent close and looked for a long moment. "That is pollen," he said. "On a fly."

"These flowers are small and flat, like tiny plates," said Yazhini. "A fly has a short mouth, and she can still reach the sweet drop. A bee is not the only one who can carry pollen." She pointed with her pencil. "And where do green flies come from?"

Karthik thought of the fish heads. "Maggots," he said, and made a face.

"The maggots in our heap. They eat the fish heads until only bones are left. Then they turn into these." She tapped the notebook. "Nineteen of them. On one bunch."

They ran to Paati with the notebook. Paati read the three columns twice. Then she laughed until she had to sit down on the step.

"My mother never let anyone clear that heap before the mangoes had set," she said. "She said the trees liked it. I thought it was only her habit."

"So no can from town?" asked Karthik.

"Nobody sprays a tree in flower," said Paati. "Not in this orchard."

Karthik looked towards the cowshed for a while. "It still smells," he said.

"Yes," said Yazhini. "Like mangoes in May."

That night she opened her notebook again. She crossed out 'Others' and wrote 'Flies' and 'Ants'. Then she ruled a fourth column for tomorrow, and above it she wrote: Where did each visitor grow up?

**For the parent:** The flies that Karthik wanted to spray were pollinating the mango flowers. Their young, the maggots, were clearing the fish heads in the heap. One insect did both jobs: she was a recycler when young and a pollinator when grown.

- Ask: Why can a fly reach the nectar of a mango flower, though her mouth is short?
- Ask: What would happen to the mango harvest if every fly in the orchard were sprayed in February?
- Ask: Which other animal near your home do people call a pest? What work might that animal do?

## Experiments
### e1 · Hairy or Smooth: which carrier takes pollen to more flowers? (30 min)
- **question:** Haldi powder is the pollen, and a row of 15 paper boxes is a row of flowers. One carrier is a fuzzy cotton bud and the other is the smooth end of a plastic pen. After one dip in the haldi, how many boxes does each carrier mark?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many boxes out of 15 will the cotton bud mark with yellow? How many will the smooth pen mark? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same haldi and the same paper for both carriers. Dip each carrier once, press it into the haldi and turn it once. Knock it once on the rim of the katori, so that loose powder falls off. Press and turn once in each box, with the same gentle push. Never dip again in the middle of a row. Do three rows with each carrier, because one row can be a fluke. Use a fresh cotton head for each row, and wipe the pen dry before each row. Only the carrier changes.
- **safety:** Haldi stains cloth and some table tops. Work on newspaper and wear old clothes.
- **record:** Draw a table with two rows, one for the cotton bud and one for the pen. Write the number of boxes marked in each of the three tries. Then write the middle value of the three.
- **results table:** Carrier | Boxes marked in try 1 | Boxes marked in try 2 | Boxes marked in try 3 | Middle value
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the haldi, a cotton bud and the pen. Explain the test. Ask for the two guesses and the reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* The haldi is our pollen. Each box will be one flower. We dip a carrier once and then visit 15 flowers in a row. How many boxes will the cotton bud mark? How many will the pen mark? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Haldi is the pollen. Guess how many boxes each carrier will mark. Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that carrier will mark more boxes? (good: The cotton is fluffy, so more powder will stick.; The pen is smooth, so the powder will slide off.; The cotton has gaps where the powder can hide.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Many children expect both carriers to run out after two or three boxes.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Spread the newspaper. On the white paper, rule 6 rows of 15 boxes, each box 1 centimetre wide. Write Bud beside three rows and Pen beside three rows. Put 1 level teaspoon of haldi in the katori.  
  *Parent says:* We need six rows of flowers. Three rows are for the cotton bud and three are for the pen. Why do you think we give each carrier three rows, and not one?  
  *Child mode:* Rule 6 rows of 15 boxes. Label three rows Bud and three rows Pen.
  - Ask: Why three rows for each carrier? (good: One try can be a fluke.; If three tries agree, we can trust the result more.)
  - Note: A page from a maths notebook with squares saves the ruling. The boxes only need to be about the width of a fingertip.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Press one cotton head into the haldi and turn it once. Knock the stick once on the rim. Press and turn the head once in box 1, then in box 2, and on to box 15. Do the second and third Bud rows with fresh cotton heads.  
  *Parent says:* Dip once, turn once and knock once. Now visit every flower in the row. Press, turn and move on. Use the same gentle push each time, and do not dip again.  
  *Child mode:* Dip the cotton bud once. Press and turn in each box. Do not dip again.
  - Note: Let your child do all the pressing, so that the push is the same for both carriers. The marks get fainter along the row. That is expected.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wipe the back end of the pen with the dry cloth. Press it into the haldi, turn it once and knock it once on the rim. Press and turn once in each box of a Pen row. Wipe the pen and repeat for the other two Pen rows.  
  *Parent says:* Now the smooth carrier. Do everything exactly as before. Dip once, turn once and knock once. Then press and turn in every box.  
  *Child mode:* Wipe the pen. Dip it once. Press and turn in each box.
  - Note: The pen end must be dry. A damp or oily pen picks up a thick coat of haldi, and the test then compares wet with dry.
  - Check: In the Hairy or Smooth test, why do you knock each carrier once on the rim? → **So loose powder falls off both**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the paper at arm's length. In each row, count the boxes where your child can see yellow. Write the six numbers in the table. For each carrier, circle the middle value of the three.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the paper out at arm's length. A box counts if you can see yellow from there. Count each row and write the number. Which of the three numbers is in the middle?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the paper far out. Count the yellow boxes in each row.
  - Note: The middle value is the one left when you cross out the biggest and the smallest. In most homes the cotton bud marks 8 to 15 boxes and the pen marks 1 to 4. Write what you really get.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Then say why you think it happened. Which carrier is more like a bee? What does a bee have that the pen does not?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Ask: Where on the cotton bud was the haldi kept, so that it lasted for so many boxes? (good: Between the threads of the cotton.; Deep inside the fluff, and it came out a little at a time.)
  - Ask: A bee is hairy and a beetle's back is smooth and shiny. Which is likely to carry more pollen? (good: The bee, because pollen catches in her hairs.)
  - Note: The reason: cotton is made of thousands of fine threads, and powder is held in the gaps between them. Each press lets a little out, so the marks go on for many boxes. The smooth pen holds powder only on its skin, and the first one or two presses take it all. A bee is covered in hairs, and each hair is branched like a tiny feather. Pollen catches among the hairs and comes off a little at a time, flower after flower. Keep the sheet. It is used again in the worksheets.
  - Think: The cotton bud did not put all its haldi in box 1. Why is that good for flowers 2 to 15?
### e2 · The Clean-up Count: how fast do ants clear 20 crumbs? (30 min)
- **question:** Twenty small crumbs are left on a paper near an ant line. How many minutes pass before all 20 are gone? Do the crumbs go at a steady speed, or does the work start slowly and then speed up?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many minutes will the ants take to clear all 20 crumbs? Will the crumbs go at the same speed all the time, or faster later? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use 20 crumbs of the same food and the same size on each of two paper squares. Put paper A on the floor, about 30 centimetres from the ant line. Put paper B on an upturned katori standing in a plate of water, so that ants cannot reach it. Paper B shows whether crumbs vanish with no ants. Count every 5 minutes by the clock. Do not touch the papers, blow on the ants or stand in their path.
- **record:** Draw a table with nine rows, for 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 minutes. At each time, write the number of ants on paper A and the number of crumbs left on each paper. Then draw a line graph of the crumbs left on paper A.
- **results table:** Minutes since the start | Ants on paper A | Crumbs left on paper A | Crumbs left on paper B
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find a place where ants are walking. Show your child the crumbs. Ask for the guesses and the reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will leave 20 crumbs near these ants. How many minutes will they take to clear all 20? Will the crumbs go at a steady speed, or faster later? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how long the ants need for 20 crumbs. Steady, or faster later? Why?
  - Ask: What has to happen before the first crumb goes? (good: An ant has to find the paper.; She has to tell the others, or lead them there.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. If you see no ants indoors, try a balcony, a doorstep or the foot of a tree near your gate. Evening is often a busy time for ants.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count 20 crumbs onto each paper and spread them out. Lay paper A on the floor, 30 centimetres from the ant line. Stand the katori upside down in the plate of water, and lay paper B on top. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* Paper A is for the ants. Paper B stands in water, and no ant can reach that paper. Why do you think we need paper B at all?  
  *Child mode:* Put 20 crumbs on each paper. Paper A goes near the ants. Paper B stands in water.
  - Ask: What will paper B tell us? (good: Whether crumbs disappear when no ants come.; If B keeps all 20, then the ants took the crumbs from A.)
  - Note: Each crumb should be small enough for one small ant to carry, about the size of a sesame seed. Fresh grated coconut works well in most homes.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every 5 minutes for 40 minutes, count the ants on paper A and the crumbs left on it. Write both numbers. Between counts, do something else nearby, such as the term cards.  
  *Parent says:* Five minutes have passed. Count the ants on the paper, then the crumbs. Write both. Watch one ant closely. Where does she go with her crumb?  
  *Child mode:* Every 5 minutes, count the ants and the crumbs. Write both numbers.
  - Note: If no ant has found paper A after 15 minutes, slide it to a hand's width from the ant line. Then start the clock again. Some kinds of ant eat at the paper and carry little away. Then the count of ants is your main result. If crumbs are still left after 40 minutes, go on counting for another 20 minutes.
  - Wait: Count every 5 minutes for 40 minutes
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At the end, count the crumbs on paper B. Write the number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now count the crumbs on paper B, the one in the water. How many are left? What does that tell us about paper A?  
  *Child mode:* Count the crumbs on paper B. Are all 20 still there?
  - Check: In the Clean-up Count, paper B still has 20 crumbs. What does that show? → **Ants removed the crumbs from paper A**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a line graph. Put minutes along the bottom, from 0 to 40. Put crumbs left up the side, from 0 to 20. Mark a dot for each count and join the dots.  
  *Parent says:* Put a dot for each count and join the dots. Look at the shape of the line. Where is it flat? Where is it steepest?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the graph of crumbs left. Where is the line steepest?
  - Note: A steep part of the line means crumbs were going fast. A flat part means few crumbs were going.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table and the graph together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Then say why you think it happened. When were the most ants on the paper? When did the crumbs go fastest?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Ask: Why did the crumbs go slowly at first and faster later? (good: At first only one ant knew about the food.; She brought more ants, so more crumbs were carried at once.)
  - Ask: Where are the 20 crumbs now, and what will happen to them? (good: In the nest. The workers will feed them to the larvae.; What is left over will go on the nest's rubbish heap.)
  - Note: In most homes the first ant arrives within 10 minutes, and the crumbs are gone within 30 to 60 minutes. The usual pattern is a slow start and then a rush. The reason: one worker finds the food and goes home, leaving a scent trail. Other workers follow the trail, and each of them strengthens it. So the number of ants at the food grows, and the crumbs go faster. The crumbs are carried into the nest and fed to the larvae. This is recycling: food that fell on your floor becomes new ants, and the leftovers go into the soil. Brush the remaining crumbs outside when you finish.
  - Think: Nobody asked the ants to clean your floor. Why did they do it?

## Challenges
### c1 · The Buzz Flower: get the pollen out of a closed tube (20 min)
- **problem:** A brinjal flower keeps its pollen inside closed tubes with one small hole at the tip. A honeybee cannot get the pollen out. A carpenter bee shakes the pollen out by buzzing. Your problem is like hers. A paper tube holds haldi and has one small hole. Get out as much haldi as you can in 10 seconds.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own way to shake the tube. It must leave the tube whole. Test it for 10 seconds and compare the heap with your best heap so far. Draw your method and write why you think it worked or failed.
- **constraints:** Do not squeeze, tear or open the tube.; Do not make the hole bigger.; Hold the tube with the hole pointing down, over a steel plate.; Each method gets 10 seconds, counted aloud.; Refill the tube to the same level before each method.
- **general:** Flick the side of the tube with a finger.; Shake the tube up and down.; Knock the tube gently against the edge of the plate.
- **creative:** Hold the tube against a phone while it vibrates.; Stretch a rubber band over a steel tumbler, rest the tube on the band and pluck the band.; Press the tube lightly against your throat and hum a low, loud note.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Build the tube as the steps show. Hold it hole-down over the plate without moving, and count to 10.  
  *Parent says:* This tube is the pollen tube of a brinjal flower. Hold it still with the hole down. Does the powder pour out? What could a bee do to get the powder out?  
  *Child mode:* Build the tube. Hold it still with the hole down. Does powder fall?
  - Ask: The hole is open, so why does the powder stay inside? (good: The powder sticks to itself and blocks the hole.; Nothing is shaking it loose.)
  - Note: The hole should be about as wide as a pencil tip. With atta mixed in, the powder clumps and hardly falls while the tube is still. If it pours out freely, make a new tube with a smaller hole.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try the three usual methods, 10 seconds each. Refill the tube before each. Make a separate heap on the plate for each method and label the heaps.  
  *Parent says:* Try a flick, then a shake, then a knock. Count ten seconds for each. Keep the heaps apart, so that we can compare them.  
  *Child mode:* Try a flick, a shake and a knock. Make one heap for each.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try the creative methods. Set the phone to vibrate and hold the tube against it. Try the rubber band, and try humming. Make a heap for each.  
  *Parent says:* A carpenter bee does not flick the flower. She buzzes her flight muscles a few hundred times each second. Which of our methods is most like her buzz? Let us test it.  
  *Child mode:* Now try a buzzing phone, a plucked rubber band and a hum.
  - Note: Fast, small shakes often free more powder than slow, big ones. Results vary with the tube and the phone, so let the heaps decide.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare all the heaps. Your child invents one more method, tests it, and draws it.  
  *Parent says:* Which heap is biggest? Was a fast, small shake better than a slow, big one? Now invent your own method and test it.  
  *Child mode:* Which heap is biggest? Invent your own method and test it.
  - Check: Why can a carpenter bee get pollen from a brinjal flower when a honeybee cannot? → **She shakes the flower by buzzing**

## Worksheets
### Colonies, pollination and recycling (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Who lays the eggs in a honeybee colony?  
   Options: The queen / The workers / The drones / The larvae  
   **Answer:** The queen. *Why:* The queen is the mother of the colony, and laying eggs is her work. Drones are males who only mate. Larvae are the young.
2. (mcq) The worker ants in one line on a wall are mostly what to each other?  
   Options: Sisters / Strangers / Brothers / Mothers and daughters  
   **Answer:** Sisters. *Why:* Workers are daughters of the same queen, so they are sisters. If you chose brothers, remember that worker ants are female.
3. (fill) The moving of pollen from an anther to a stigma is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pollination']. *Why:* Pollination must happen before a flower can grow seeds and fruit.
4. (fill) The sweet liquid that flowers make, and that insects drink for energy, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['nectar']. *Why:* Nectar is mostly sugar and water. Honeybees dry it into honey.
5. (truefalse) All the worker bees in a hive are female.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Workers are daughters of the queen. The few males are called drones, and they do no nest work.
6. (truefalse) Rice needs bees to carry its pollen.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Rice is a grass, and the wind carries its pollen. Rice, wheat and maize need no insect.
7. (match) Match each part of a flower to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Anther → Makes pollen; Stigma → Catches pollen; Nectar → Feeds the visitor; Petal → Shows where the food is. *Why:* The anther and the stigma do the work of making seeds. The nectar and the petals bring the insect who carries the pollen between them.
8. (sort) Sort these foods. Did the plant need an insect pollinator, or not?  
   **Answer:** pumpkin → Needed an insect; mango → Needed an insect; cardamom → Needed an insect; mustard seeds → Needed an insect; rice → Needed no insect; wheat → Needed no insect; banana → Needed no insect; potato → Needed no insect. *Why:* Rice and wheat are grasses, and wind carries their pollen. The bananas we eat form with no pollination, and potatoes grow from pieces of potato. The other four set fruit or seed after insects visit.
9. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how a queen ant starts a colony.  
   **Answer:** ['She flies out after rain and mates', 'She lands and breaks off her wings', 'She digs a hole and seals herself in', 'She lays eggs and feeds the first larvae from her own body', 'Her first daughters open the nest and bring food']. *Why:* The queen does everything alone until her first daughters grow up. After that the workers feed her, and she only lays eggs.
10. (mcq) In the Hairy or Smooth test, why did each carrier get three rows of boxes?  
   Options: One try can be a fluke / To use up the haldi / To make the paper yellow / Because an insect has three body parts  
   **Answer:** One try can be a fluke. *Why:* One row might come out odd by chance. If three rows give similar counts, the result can be trusted more.
11. (mcq) Which insect's young clear away dead animals?  
   Options: A blowfly / A honeybee / A butterfly / A carpenter bee  
   **Answer:** A blowfly. *Why:* A blowfly lays eggs on a dead body, and her maggots eat the body. Young honeybees eat pollen and honey, and caterpillars eat leaves.
12. (mcq) What does a worker bee carry home in the baskets on her back legs?  
   Options: Pollen / Honey / Wax / Water  
   **Answer:** Pollen. *Why:* She packs pollen into baskets of stiff hairs on her back legs. Pollen is the protein food that makes larvae grow. Nectar is carried inside her body.
13. (write) Explain in two sentences how a bee pollinates a pumpkin flower while she collects food.  
   **Answer:** The bee crawls into a pumpkin flower for nectar, and pollen from the anthers sticks to her hairs. At the next pumpkin flower some of that pollen rubs off on the sticky stigma.. *Why:* A strong answer says three things. The bee comes for food, pollen sticks to her body, and the pollen rubs off on another flower's stigma.
14. (write) In the Clean-up Count, paper B stood on a katori in water. Explain what paper B was for.  
   **Answer:** Ants could not cross the water, so paper B had crumbs and no ants. It kept all its crumbs. That showed the ants took the crumbs from paper A.. *Why:* A strong answer says that paper B shows what happens with no ants. Then the ants are the only difference between the two papers.
15. (draw) Draw a bee visiting two flowers. Use arrows to show where pollen is picked up and where it is left.  
   **Answer:** Two flowers of the same kind with a bee between them. One arrow goes from the anthers of the first flower to the bee, labelled 'picked up'. One arrow goes from the bee to the stigma of the second flower, labelled 'left here'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows pollen leaving an anther and arriving on a stigma. The bee is the carrier in between.
### Why insects are good at this work (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does a hairy insect carry more pollen than a smooth one?  
   Options: Pollen catches among the hairs / Hairs make pollen / Hairs smell sweet / Smooth insects fly faster  
   **Answer:** Pollen catches among the hairs. *Why:* Pollen grains are held in the gaps between hairs and come off a little at a time. A smooth body holds pollen only on its surface.
2. (mcq) A bee visits twenty sunflowers in a row and no other flower. What is this habit called?  
   Options: Flower constancy / Buzz pollination / Swarming / Scavenging  
   **Answer:** Flower constancy. *Why:* Flower constancy means keeping to one kind of flower on one trip. It takes sunflower pollen to sunflowers, where it can work.
3. (mcq) A brinjal flower keeps its pollen in closed tubes. Which visitor can shake it out?  
   Options: A carpenter bee / A honeybee / A butterfly / An ant  
   **Answer:** A carpenter bee. *Why:* A carpenter bee grips the flower and buzzes, and the shaking throws the pollen out. Honeybees, butterflies and ants do not buzz flowers.
4. (mcq) A worker bee lives about six weeks. Why is her colony still there a year later?  
   Options: New workers hatch every day / The queen does all the work / The workers sleep all winter / Old workers grow young again  
   **Answer:** New workers hatch every day. *Why:* The queen lays eggs every day in the warm months, and new workers replace those who die. The colony goes on while its members change.
5. (truefalse) Termites are a kind of ant.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Termites are close relatives of cockroaches. Ants are close relatives of wasps and bees. A termite colony has a king as well as a queen.
6. (truefalse) A flower gains when a bee keeps to one kind of flower on a trip.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Pollen works only on a flower of the same kind. A bee who mixes flowers wastes most of the pollen she carries.
7. (sort) Sort these insects by the work they are doing.  
   **Answer:** a carpenter bee buzzing on a brinjal flower → Pollinating; a hawkmoth drinking at a jasmine flower → Pollinating; a midge inside a cocoa flower → Pollinating; a dung beetle burying dung → Recycling; a termite chewing a dead branch → Recycling; a maggot eating a dead fish → Recycling. *Why:* An insect feeding at a flower is likely to be moving pollen. An insect eating dung, dead wood or a dead animal is breaking that material down. Then the material can be used again.
8. (odd) Three of these plants are pollinated by wind. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Rice / Wheat / Maize / Mango  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Rice, wheat and maize are grasses with light pollen that the wind carries. Mango flowers are pollinated by flies and small bees.
9. (count) In the Hairy or Smooth test, a cotton bud marked 12, 10 and 14 boxes. What is the middle value?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* Put the three numbers in order: 10, 12, 14. The middle one is 12. The middle value is not pulled about by one odd row.
10. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how ants clear crumbs from a floor.  
   **Answer:** ['One ant finds the crumbs', 'She goes home, leaving a scent trail', 'More ants follow the trail to the crumbs', 'The ants carry the crumbs into the nest', 'Workers feed the crumbs to the larvae']. *Why:* The work starts slowly, with one ant. It speeds up when her nestmates follow her trail. The food ends up inside young ants.
11. (write) A mango orchard has thousands of flowers and very few bees. Explain how the flowers can still be pollinated.  
   **Answer:** Flies visit mango flowers too. The flowers are small and open, so a fly's short mouth can reach the nectar. Pollen sticks to the fly's bristles and reaches the next flower.. *Why:* A strong answer names another pollinator, such as flies, and says why that pollinator suits a small open flower.
12. (write) Why does a flower gain by keeping its nectar at the very bottom?  
   **Answer:** The insect has to push deep into the flower to reach the nectar. On the way she brushes against the anthers and the stigma, so pollen is picked up and left.. *Why:* A strong answer links the place of the nectar to the insect's path past the anthers and the stigma.
13. (write) Dung beetles bury cow dung in the ground. Explain two ways in which this helps a field.  
   **Answer:** Buried dung feeds the soil near the roots of the grass. The grass under the dung is uncovered and can grow again. The beetles' tunnels also let rain soak in.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two different gains. Three possible gains are food for the soil, cleared grass, and tunnels that let in water and air.
14. (perspective) Green flies sit on the mango flowers in an orchard. Three people say what should be done about flies.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that flies really do carry germs to uncovered food. Another is that most visitors to mango flowers in many orchards are flies. A third is that maggots clear dead animals in days.. *Why:* Each person is right about something. A strong answer uses a fact from the unit and deals fairly with the other side. One fair way is to keep flies off food without killing them everywhere.
15. (label) Label three things in this flower: an anther, the stigma, and the place where the nectar is.  
   **Answer:** ['An anther: one of the small knobs that make pollen', 'The stigma: the sticky tip that catches pollen', 'Where the nectar is: at the bottom of the flower, at the base of the petals']. *Why:* The nectar is deepest. So a visitor passes the anthers and the stigma on the way down and on the way out.
### Testing, counting and deciding (challenge, 15 questions)

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a colony?  
   Options: One insect family sharing one nest / Any crowd of insects on a flower / A single very large insect / A kind of flower  
   **Answer:** One insect family sharing one nest. *Why:* A colony is a family: a queen, her worker daughters and the young, in one nest. Insects who only happen to meet at a flower are not a colony.
2. (mcq) What is the queen's main work in a grown colony?  
   Options: Laying eggs / Giving orders / Finding food / Guarding the entrance  
   **Answer:** Laying eggs. *Why:* The queen is the mother, and she lays the eggs. She does not give orders. The workers find food, guard the nest and feed her.
3. (mcq) How does a new honeybee colony usually begin?  
   Options: The old queen leaves with a swarm / One worker walks away alone / Eggs blow away on the wind / The drones build a new nest  
   **Answer:** The old queen leaves with a swarm. *Why:* The old queen flies off with about half the workers, and the bees who stay raise a new queen. A worker alone cannot start a colony.
4. (mcq) What is pollination?  
   Options: Pollen moving from an anther to a stigma / A bee making honey / A seed falling to the ground / A flower opening its petals  
   **Answer:** Pollen moving from an anther to a stigma. *Why:* Pollination is the journey of pollen from where it is made to the sticky tip that catches it. Honey-making happens later, in the hive.
5. (mcq) Which part of a flower catches pollen?  
   Options: The stigma / The anther / The petal / The stalk  
   **Answer:** The stigma. *Why:* The stigma is sticky and catches pollen. If you chose the anther, remember that the anther makes pollen and gives it away.
6. (mcq) Which result of a Hairy or Smooth test shows that a hairy carrier holds more pollen?  
   Options: The cotton bud marks far more boxes / Both carriers mark the same number / The pen marks more boxes / The haldi changes colour  
   **Answer:** The cotton bud marks far more boxes. *Why:* The cotton bud holds powder between its threads and lets it out box after box. The smooth pen runs out after one or two boxes.
7. (mcq) Why does a bee visit a flower?  
   Options: To collect nectar and pollen as food / To help the flower make seeds / To rest in the shade / To lay her eggs  
   **Answer:** To collect nectar and pollen as food. *Why:* A bee visits flowers to feed herself and her colony. The flower is pollinated because pollen sticks to her and rubs off at the next flower.
8. (mcq) Which of these foods needs no insect pollinator?  
   Options: Rice / Mango / Pumpkin / Cardamom  
   **Answer:** Rice. *Why:* Rice is a grass, and wind carries its pollen. Mango, pumpkin and cardamom all set fruit after insects visit their flowers.
9. (truefalse) Honeybees are the only insects that pollinate crops.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Carpenter bees, stingless bees, flies, moths, butterflies and beetles all pollinate. Flies are main pollinators of mango, and midges pollinate cocoa.
10. (mcq) Which insects turn a dead branch back into soil?  
   Options: Termites / Honeybees / Butterflies / Hawkmoths  
   **Answer:** Termites. *Why:* Termites eat dead wood, with the help of microbes and fungi. The other three feed at flowers.
11. (mcq) When ants clear crumbs, why do the crumbs go faster after a slow start?  
   Options: More ants follow the first ant's trail / The crumbs get lighter / The ants get hungrier / The floor gets warmer  
   **Answer:** More ants follow the first ant's trail. *Why:* At first only one ant knows about the food. She leaves a scent trail, more ants follow, and many crumbs are carried at once.
12. (mcq) Which of these helps pollinators most?  
   Options: Never spraying a plant in flower / Killing every fly outdoors / Cutting down flowering trees / Keeping bright lights on all night  
   **Answer:** Never spraying a plant in flower. *Why:* Insects come to open flowers, so a spray on flowers poisons the very insects the plant needs. The other three all harm pollinators.

## Applications
- Apple growers in Himachal Pradesh and Kashmir rent bee boxes when their trees flower. More flowers are pollinated, so more apples form.
- Beekeepers in India move their bee boxes with the seasons. Many take them to the mustard fields of Rajasthan in winter and to the litchi orchards of Bihar in spring.
- Tomato growers with greenhouses keep boxes of bumblebees. Bumblebees buzz the pollen out of tomato flowers, which honeybees cannot do.
- Vanilla has no natural pollinator in India. Workers pollinate every vanilla flower by hand with a thin stick.
- Some cities and farms feed kitchen waste to the larvae of the black soldier fly. The larvae turn the waste into compost, and they are then fed to hens and fish.
- Fruit growers in parts of Asia keep weaver ant nests in their trees. The ants hunt the insects who eat the fruit, so less poison is needed.
- Doctors sometimes put clean, specially raised maggots on a wound that will not heal. The maggots eat the dead flesh and leave the living flesh.
- Engineers copy the six-sided cells of a honeycomb to make panels for aircraft. The panels are light and very stiff.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of insect are there?** About one million kinds have been named. Estimates of the real number run to five million or more. Most kinds have never been studied by anyone.
- **Are insects becoming fewer everywhere, or only in some places?** Long counts exist mainly from Europe and North America, and many of them show steep falls. There are very few long counts from India, Africa or South America.
- **Why do some honeybee colonies suddenly lose nearly all their workers?** From 2006, beekeepers in several countries found hives with a queen and young but almost no workers. Poisons, mites, diseases and poor food are all suspected. No single cause has been proved.
- **What is it like to be a bee?** Bees sense, learn, remember and choose. In a 2022 study, bumblebees rolled small wooden balls again and again for no reward, which looks like play. Nobody has yet found a way to learn what this is like for the bee.
- **How does a colony decide when to split and send out a swarm?** Crowding, the age of the queen and the season all play a part. How thousands of workers reach that one decision together is still being worked out.

## Connected maths
- In a Hairy or Smooth test, the cotton bud marked 12, 10 and 14 boxes. The pen marked 2, 3 and 1 boxes. Find the middle value for each carrier. How many times as many boxes did the cotton bud mark? **The middle values are 12 and 2. The cotton bud marked 6 times as many boxes.**. In order, the cotton bud's counts are 10, 12, 14, and the middle one is 12. The pen's counts are 1, 2, 3, and the middle one is 2. 12 divided by 2 is 6. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In a Clean-up Count, the crumbs left were counted every 5 minutes. The counts were 20, 20, 19, 17, 13, 7, 2 and 0. In which 5 minutes did the ants take the most crumbs? Why not in the first 5 minutes? **Between 20 and 25 minutes, when 6 crumbs went. In the first 5 minutes no ant had brought her nestmates yet.**. Take each count from the one before it. The drops are 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 5 and 2. The biggest drop is 6, from 13 to 7. That is the fifth drop, between 20 and 25 minutes. Early on, only one ant knew about the crumbs. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In the Kitchen Shelf Test, 6 of the 12 foods needed an insect. In one month a family eats 18 kilograms of rice, atta and potatoes. They also eat 6 kilograms of foods that needed insects. What fraction of the food by weight needed insects? Why is it not one half? **One quarter by weight. It is less than one half because the family eats large amounts of a few foods that need no insect.**. 18 plus 6 is 24 kilograms in all. 6 out of 24 is one quarter. Counted by kind, half the foods needed insects. Counted by weight, only a quarter did, because rice and atta are eaten every day in large amounts. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A honeybee may visit about 100 flowers on one trip, and make about 10 trips in a day. About how many flowers does she visit in a day? A colony sends out 10,000 such bees. About how many flower visits is that in one day? **About 1,000 flowers for one bee. About 10,000,000 visits for the colony, which is ten million.**. 100 times 10 is 1,000. Then 1,000 times 10,000 is 10,000,000. These are rough numbers, and they change with the weather and the flowers. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- A honeybee colony in summer has 42,000 workers. Each worker lives about 42 days. About how many workers die each day? About how many eggs must the queen lay each day to keep the colony the same size? **About 1,000 workers die each day. So the queen must lay about 1,000 eggs a day.**. If 42,000 workers are replaced over 42 days, then 42,000 divided by 42 gives 1,000 a day. To stay the same size, the colony needs 1,000 new workers a day. So the queen must lay at least that many eggs. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In Flower Loyal there are 24 cards: 6 each of four kinds of flower. You turn over a pumpkin flower first. How many of the other 23 cards are pumpkin flowers? By luck alone, is your next card likely or unlikely to match? **5 of the 23 are pumpkin flowers. A match by luck is unlikely: less than 1 chance in 4.**. There were 6 pumpkin cards and you hold 1, so 5 are left among 23 cards. 5 out of 23 is a little less than 1 out of 4. So a player who remembers where the pumpkin flowers are does far better than a player who guesses. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
