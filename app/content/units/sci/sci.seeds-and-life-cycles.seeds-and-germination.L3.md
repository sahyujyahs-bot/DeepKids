# Seeds and how they sprout: what wakes a seed, and what is inside
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.seeds-and-germination.L3`

**Summary.** This unit is about seeds and how they sprout. A seed is a baby plant packed in a coat, with its own lunch box of food. A dry seed is alive, but asleep. It wakes up when it gets three things: water, warmth and air. Most seeds do not need light or soil to start. Your child opens a soaked bean to find the baby plant inside. They run a fair test to find out what wakes a seed. They feel sprouting seeds make warmth, because they breathe. Then they follow the idea out: seeds that slept for two thousand years, roots that know which way is down, and seeds that sprouted on the Moon.

**Intro.** *Here is a puzzle. Hold a dry moong seed in your hand. It does not move. It does not eat. It does not grow. Is it alive or not? How could you find out?* Look in your kitchen. There are jars full of seeds: moong, chana, rajma, rice, rai and jeera. They can sit in a jar for a whole year and never change. But put a few moong seeds on a wet cloth tonight. By the day after tomorrow, each one will have a little white tail. What changed? What was the seed waiting for? This unit is about finding out.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the parts of a seed: the seed coat, the baby plant and its food store.
1. I can say what a seed needs to sprout: water, warmth and air. I can say that most seeds do not need light or soil to start.
2. I can run a fair test with seeds, changing only one thing, and count how many seeds sprout.
3. I can show my results in a table and a bar chart, and work out how long sprouting took.
4. I can explain that a sprouting seed breathes, uses up its stored food and makes a little warmth.
5. I can describe how a seed sprouts: it drinks and swells, the root comes out first and grows down, and then the shoot grows up.
6. I can say what scientists agree on about seeds, and what they still do not know, such as how long a seed can stay alive.

## Terms
- **Seed**: A baby plant packed inside a coat, with a store of food to start it off. Flowering plants make seeds so new plants can grow. *e.g.* Moong, chana, rajma, rice grains, rai and the stone inside a mango are all seeds.
- **Seed coat**: The tough outer skin of a seed. It keeps the baby plant safe and dry until it is time to grow. *e.g.* The green skin on a whole moong seed is its seed coat.
- **Baby plant (embryo)**: The tiny living plant inside a seed. It already has a little root and a little shoot. Scientists call it the embryo. *e.g.* Split a soaked peanut. The small pointed bit at one end is the baby plant.
- **Food store**: The part of a seed that holds food for the baby plant. The baby plant lives on it until its first green leaves can make food from sunlight. *e.g.* The two big halves of a chana are its food store. That is also why chana feeds us.
- **Seed leaves (cotyledons)**: The first leaves of a baby plant, already inside the seed. In beans and chana, they are thick and full of food. Scientists call them cotyledons. *e.g.* When a bean sprouts, its two fat seed halves lift out of the soil and turn green. Those are its seed leaves.
- **Germination**: A seed waking up and starting to grow. It is the scientists' word for sprouting. *e.g.* Moong left on a wet cloth for two days germinates. Each seed grows a little white root.
- **Sprout**: A seed that has just started to grow, or the little root and shoot that come out of it. *e.g.* Moong sprouts in a salad, or in usal for breakfast.
- **Dormant**: Asleep but alive. A dormant seed is waiting, using almost no energy, until the conditions are right. *e.g.* Rajma in a dry jar is dormant. It can wait for a year or more.
- **Root**: The part of a plant that grows down into the soil. It holds the plant in place and drinks water. It is the first thing to come out of a sprouting seed. *e.g.* The white tail on a moong sprout is its first root.
- **Shoot**: The part of a young plant that grows up towards the light. It becomes the stem and leaves. *e.g.* After the root, a green shoot comes out of a bean and grows upwards.
- **Swell**: To get bigger by soaking up water. Seeds swell when they are soaked. *e.g.* A cup of dry chana, soaked overnight, swells to fill about two cups.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air that living things need. Animals and plants use it to get energy from their food. Seeds need it too. *e.g.* You breathe in oxygen all day. A sprouting seed takes in oxygen too, very quietly.
- **Breathing (respiration)**: How living things use oxygen to get energy from food. It makes a little warmth, and it gives out a gas called carbon dioxide. Scientists call it respiration. *e.g.* A heap of sprouting seeds gets warm because millions of tiny cells are breathing.
- **Starch**: A kind of stored food made by plants. It is what makes rice, wheat and potatoes filling. *e.g.* Most of a rice grain is starch. The baby rice plant uses it to start growing.
- **Enzyme**: A helper made by living cells that speeds up a change. Some enzymes turn starch into sugar. *e.g.* Sprouted grains taste a little sweet, because enzymes have turned some starch into sugar.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To test if seeds need water, give one cup water and one cup none. Keep the cups in the same place, with the same seeds.
- **Gravity**: The pull that makes things fall towards the Earth. *e.g.* Gravity pulls a dropped ball down. A root grows the way gravity pulls.
- **Seed bank**: A safe store of many kinds of seeds, kept dry and cold so they stay alive for many years. *e.g.* Farmers in Maharashtra and the big seed vault in Norway both keep seed banks.

## Explanations
### What a seed is · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed is a baby plant inside a protective coat, packed with a store of food. It is made by a flowering plant, or by a cone-bearing tree like a pine.

Every seed has three main parts. The seed coat is the tough skin on the outside. Inside it is the baby plant, which scientists call the embryo. It already has a tiny root and a tiny shoot. The rest of the seed is the food store. It feeds the baby plant until its first green leaves can make food from sunlight. So a seed is like a sleeping traveller with a coat and a packed lunch.
- e.g. A soaked rajma bean: peel off the red coat, open the two halves, and find the tiny baby plant at one edge.
- e.g. A peanut: the little pointed tip between the two halves is the baby plant.
- e.g. A mango stone: the hard shell is the fruit's stone, and the seed with its coat is inside it.
### Two kinds of food store: beans and grains · *defined*
**Definition:** In beans, chana and peanuts, the food is stored inside two thick seed leaves. In rice, wheat and maize, the food is stored in a separate part around the baby plant, and there is only one thin seed leaf.

Seed leaves are the first leaves of the baby plant, already inside the seed. Scientists call them cotyledons. A bean has two seed leaves. They are fat and full of food, so a bean splits neatly into two halves. A grain of rice or maize has only one thin seed leaf. Its food is kept in a separate store beside the baby plant. Scientists call that store the endosperm. When you eat a chapati or a bowl of rice, you are mostly eating the food a grain packed for its baby plant.
- e.g. Chana and moong split into two halves. They have two seed leaves.
- e.g. A soaked maize kernel does not split in two. Its baby plant is a small pale patch on one side.
- e.g. Popcorn pops because water in the starchy food store turns to steam and bursts it open.
### What wakes a seed: water, warmth and air · *defined*
**Definition:** Germination is a seed waking up and starting to grow. Most seeds need three things to germinate: water, warmth and air.

Water comes first. It softens the coat and wakes the cells inside. Warmth matters because the changes inside a seed happen faster when it is warm, and very slowly when it is cold. Air matters because the seed must breathe in oxygen, a gas in the air, to get energy from its food. Take away any one of the three, and most seeds stay asleep or die. In the four-cup test, a dry cup, a cold cup and a cup drowned under water all do badly. The damp, warm cup sprouts. The chart shows typical made-up results.
- e.g. Moong in a dry jar never sprouts. Moong on a wet cloth sprouts in a day or two.
- e.g. Moong on a wet cloth in the fridge sprouts very slowly, or not at all, until it is warmed up.
- e.g. Moong covered with boiled and cooled water, under a layer of oil, gets almost no air. Very few sprout.
### Most seeds do not need light or soil to start · *defined*
**Definition:** Most seeds can sprout in the dark, with no soil at all. They need light and soil only later, once the seedling must make its own food.

A seed carries its own food. So at the start it does not need sunlight to make food, and it does not need soil to give it anything. That is why moong sprouts on a wet cloth inside a closed tiffin. Seeds in the ground sprout in the dark too. Light becomes important later, when the shoot comes up and its green leaves start making food. There are a few exceptions. Some small seeds, like some kinds of lettuce, need a flash of light to wake. In 1952, scientists in the United States, Harry Borthwick and Sterling Hendricks with their team, showed that red light wakes these lettuce seeds. Small seeds have only a little lunch. Light tells them they are near the surface, so they will not run out of food before they reach the sun.
- e.g. Moong sprouts in a dark cupboard just as well as on a sunny window sill.
- e.g. Sprouts sold in markets are grown on wet sacks or trays, with no soil.
- e.g. Some tiny seeds, like some kinds of lettuce, sprout only if light reaches them.
### First the seed drinks: swelling · *defined*
**Definition:** A dry seed soaks up water and swells. This is the first step of germination.

A dry seed holds very little water, often only about one part in ten. When it gets wet, water rushes in. Most enters through a tiny hole next to the scar where the seed was joined to its pod. The scar is called the hilum. The seed swells to about twice its weight, and its coat goes wrinkly and then splits. Swelling seeds push very hard. A jar packed with dry chana and water can push its lid off. Once the cells inside are wet again, they switch back on and start to work.
- e.g. Weigh 50 grams of dry chana. Soak it overnight. It now weighs about 100 grams.
- e.g. Soaked rajma has a wrinkly coat for a few hours, then it goes smooth and tight.
- e.g. Look at a soaked bean. The pale scar on its side, the hilum, is where it was joined to its pod.
### A waking seed breathes and makes warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** A sprouting seed breathes. It takes in oxygen, uses it to get energy from its stored food, and gives out carbon dioxide and a little heat. Scientists call this respiration.

You breathe to get energy from your food. A seed does the same, without lungs. Every tiny cell in it takes in oxygen from the air around it. A dry seed breathes so slowly you could never notice. Once it is wet, it breathes many times faster. One sprouting seed makes too little heat to feel. But a big heap of sprouts, wrapped up, gets warm to the touch. A flask of live sprouts gets a few degrees warmer than a flask of boiled ones. That is also why a seed under water does badly. Water holds only a little oxygen, so the seed cannot breathe well.
- e.g. A cloth bundle of sprouting moong feels warm in the middle.
- e.g. Farmers who sprout paddy seed in wet sacks before sowing can feel the heap warm up.
- e.g. Seeds kept under water for too long go sour and slimy, because they could not breathe.
### The food store turns sweet · *defined*
**Definition:** As a seed sprouts, its cells make enzymes. These helpers break the stored food, such as starch, into sugar that the baby plant can use.

Starch is a store of food made of long chains. It keeps well, but the baby plant cannot use it straight away. When the seed wakes, enzymes cut the long chains into small bits of sugar. The sugar travels to the root and shoot, and feeds them as they grow. In grains like barley, the baby plant even sends a chemical message to the food store, telling it to start. In 1960, Leslie Paleg in Australia and Hiroshi Yomo in Japan each found this message. It is a plant hormone called gibberellin. This is also why sprouted grains taste a little sweet. People in India make ragi malt from sprouted ragi.
- e.g. Chew a two-day-old moong sprout slowly. It tastes a little sweeter than a soaked seed that has not sprouted.
- e.g. Sprouted ragi is dried and ground to make ragi malt.
- e.g. Malt for some sweets and drinks is made from sprouted barley.
### Root first, then shoot · *defined*
**Definition:** In most seeds the root comes out first and grows downwards. The shoot comes out next and grows upwards.

The root comes first so the seedling can hold on and drink. Whichever way up a seed lies, its root turns to grow down. Root tips feel gravity, the pull towards the Earth. Inside the very tip are cells holding tiny heavy grains of starch. The grains sink to the bottom of each cell, like sand in a bottle. So the root can tell which way is down. The shoot grows the other way, up towards the light. In beans, the shoot comes up bent like a hook. The hook protects the soft tip as it pushes through the soil.
- e.g. Put soaked chana in a jar against wet paper, some upside down. Every root bends to grow down.
- e.g. Turn the jar on its side after the roots appear. In a day, the root tips bend down again.
- e.g. A bean shoot pushes up through the soil bent like a hook, then straightens in the light.
### Rice: the seed that can start under water · *defined*
**Definition:** Rice is unusual. Its seeds can start to sprout even under water, with very little oxygen.

Most seeds drown under water. Rice comes from wet, flooded places, and its seeds have a trick. Under water, a rice seed sends up its first shoot quickly, before its root. The shoot grows up like a snorkel until its tip reaches the air. Then air can travel down it, and the root can start. This helps rice grow in flooded fields. Scientists are still studying exactly how rice does this, so they can help farmers sow rice straight into wet fields.
- e.g. Moong seeds under water mostly fail. Rice seeds under water often send up a shoot.
- e.g. In many paddy fields in India, rice grows with its feet in water.
### How long can a seed wait? · *idea*
A dry, cool seed can stay alive for a long time, but nobody knows the longest. In 1879, a scientist in the United States, William James Beal, buried bottles of seeds in sand. Every few years, someone digs one up and plants the seeds. In 2021, more than 140 years later, some seeds still sprouted. Sacred lotus seeds about 1,300 years old have sprouted in China. In 2005, Elaine Solowey grew a date palm in Israel from a seed about 2,000 years old. It came from the ruins of an ancient fort called Masada. She worked with a doctor, Sarah Sallon, and the tree was named Methuselah. Most seeds do not last nearly this long. Scientists are still finding out what lets some seeds wait for centuries.
- e.g. Moong from the kitchen usually sprouts well for a year or two, then fewer seeds sprout each year.
- e.g. Seeds last much longer when they are kept dry and cold. That is why seed banks keep them in freezers.
- **Most seed scientists:** Old seeds slowly get damaged inside, a little more each year. Dry and cold slow this down. Every kind of seed has its own limit.
- **Some scientists:** A few seeds may be far better at repairing themselves than we know. The real limit could be thousands of years. Nobody has had time to test it.
### Is a dry seed alive? · *debated*
A dry seed does not grow, move or seem to eat. It breathes so slowly that it is very hard to measure. Yet add water, and it wakes up. So is a dry seed alive the whole time, or does it come back to life? Scientists and thinkers answer this in different ways. The question matters, because it tests what we mean by 'alive'.
- e.g. A moong seed in a jar for a year, and the same seed sprouting two days after soaking.
- e.g. Tardigrades, tiny water animals smaller than a grain of salt, can dry out completely and wake up again years later when wetted.
- **Most biologists:** A dry seed is alive, but dormant. Its cells are still whole and still working, very, very slowly. It is like a person in a very deep sleep.
- **Some scientists who study drying out:** In a very dry seed, life almost stops. They call this 'hidden life'. The seed is not doing much, but it keeps everything it needs to start again.
- **Some philosophers:** The seed shows that 'alive' and 'not alive' may not be two neat boxes. There may be states in between, and our words do not fit them well.
### Deep down: a seed is stored sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** The food in a seed was made by its parent plant from air, water and sunlight. When the seed sprouts and breathes, it uses that stored sunlight energy and sends the carbon back into the air.

The parent plant's leaves took in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil. Using the energy of sunlight, they built sugar and starch. The plant packed some of this food into each seed. Inside the starch, carbon atoms are joined together. Those joins hold energy that first came from the Sun. When the seed breathes, it breaks the joins and uses the energy. The carbon atoms go back into the air as carbon dioxide. So a sprouting seed is running on old sunshine. That is why a sprouting seed in the dark gets lighter, once you dry it out again. It is burning its lunch.
- e.g. Sprout seeds in the dark for a week, then dry them in the sun. They weigh less than the same number of dry seeds that were never sprouted.
- e.g. The energy in the chana you eat also came from sunlight, stored by the chana plant.
### How a seed knows when to wake · *idea*
Some seeds wake as soon as they are wet. Others wait for a sign that the time is right. Some wait for a long cold winter. Some wait until a fire has passed. In 2004, Gavin Flematti and his team in Australia found the chemical in smoke that wakes many such seeds. Some seeds have a coat so hard that it must be scratched, or pass through a bird's belly, before water can get in. Scientists understand parts of how seeds sense these signs. How a seed decides, using many signs at once, is still being worked out.
- e.g. Many Himalayan wildflower seeds sprout only after they have been through a cold winter.
- e.g. Some plants of Australia and South Africa sprout in great numbers after a bush fire.
- e.g. Gardeners rub very hard seeds with sandpaper, or nick them, so that water can get in.
- **Most seed scientists:** A seed has sensors for water, warmth, light and some chemicals. Its hormones add up these signals, a bit like a set of switches.
- **Some plant scientists:** This adding up of signals is a simple kind of decision-making without a brain. Others say 'decision' is the wrong word for chemistry. They still argue about it.
### Seeds in space and on the Moon · *defined*
**Definition:** Seeds have travelled into space and come back able to sprout. Seeds have even sprouted on the Moon, inside a sealed box.

In 1971, an astronaut called Stuart Roosa carried about 500 tree seeds around the Moon on the Apollo 14 mission. Back on Earth, many were sprouted and planted. They grew into normal trees, called Moon trees. In January 2019, China's Chang'e 4 lander carried cotton seeds to the far side of the Moon in a sealed box with water, air and soil. A cotton seed sprouted there, the first seed to sprout on the Moon. When the long, freezing lunar night came, it died. It had water and air, but not enough warmth. Space farmers will need all three.
- e.g. Moon trees from the Apollo 14 seeds still grow in parts of the United States.
- e.g. The cotton seedling on the Moon died when the temperature fell far below freezing.

## Mostly agreed
- A seed holds a living baby plant, with a coat and a store of food. *How we know:* Split any soaked bean and you can see the baby plant with a hand lens. Scientists have looked at seeds under microscopes for more than 300 years.
- Most seeds need water, warmth and air to sprout, and do not need light or soil to start. *How we know:* Fair tests like the four-cup test give the same results in classrooms and laboratories all over the world.
- A sprouting seed breathes. It uses oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide and heat. *How we know:* A flask of sprouting seeds warms up, while boiled seeds do not. Scientists can measure the oxygen used and the carbon dioxide given out.
- The root comes out first and grows down, and the shoot grows up. *How we know:* Seeds placed any way up in a jar send their roots down. Turn the jar, and the root tips turn down again. In space, where the pull is tiny, roots wander in many directions.
- Dry, cool seeds can stay alive for years, and some for centuries. *How we know:* Seeds from Beal's bottles sprouted after more than 140 years. A date palm seed about 2,000 years old was sprouted in 2005, and its age was checked by carbon dating.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Seeds need sunlight to sprout.** (Many children and grown-ups. It is a very common guess.; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants need light to grow well. And seeds in a garden usually sprout where the sun shines.  
  Against: Moong sprouts in a closed dark tiffin. Seeds in the ground sprout in the dark. A seed carries its own food, so it needs light only once its leaves come up. A few small seeds, like some lettuce, are exceptions.
- **Seeds need soil to sprout.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: We plant seeds in soil, and that is where they grow in nature.  
  Against: Seeds sprout on wet cloth, cotton or paper. Soil holds water and air and keeps the seed dark and safe. It also feeds the plant later. But the seed does not need it to start.
- **The more water a seed gets, the better it sprouts.** (Many new gardeners; *rejected*)  
  For: Water is the first thing a seed needs, and dry seeds never sprout.  
  Against: A seed under water cannot get enough air to breathe. In the four-cup test, the drowned seeds do badly, and some go sour. Damp is just right. Rice is the famous exception.
- **A dry seed is dead until water brings it to life.** (A common way of thinking, because dry seeds do nothing we can see; *rejected*)  
  For: A dry seed does not grow or move, and it seems to use no food.  
  Against: A dry seed is still breathing, very slowly. Boiled seeds never sprout, however much water they get. So something in the dry seed was alive all along.
- **A big seed grows into a big plant.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Big seeds carry more food, so their seedlings often start bigger.  
  Against: A banyan or peepal seed is smaller than a grain of rai, yet it grows into a giant tree. A big rajma grows into a small plant. A seed is only the start. The plant builds itself out of air, water and sunlight.
- **Seeds sprout better if you sow them at the right phase of the Moon.** (Some farmers and gardeners around the world, following old planting calendars; *minority*)  
  For: The custom is very old. Some people believe the Moon pulls water in the soil, like it pulls the sea.  
  Against: The Moon's pull on a little patch of soil is far too tiny to move its water. Careful tests have not found a clear effect. Many farmers still follow such calendars, and they may help in other ways, like keeping planting on time.

## Roots
We have been learning how a seed wakes up when water, warmth and air reach it. Now we ask what a seed tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Life does not start from nothing. A seed is old life, paused and passed on.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things into alive and dead, and say a life 'begins' when a seed sprouts. But a dry seed is already alive, breathing very slowly. Its baby plant was built by its parent and packed with its parent's food. Sprouting is a waking, not a beginning.  
*Evidence:* A dry moong seed seems as dead as a pebble. Give it water, warmth and air, and in a day it breathes fast enough to warm a flask. A boiled seed never does.

- *who am i:* You also grew from something tiny, made and fed by those before you. Your life is a carrying on, too.
- *what is life:* Life can wait. A seed can sleep for years, then wake when water, warmth and air arrive.
- *what is this world:* Nothing grows alone. A sprout is a seed meeting water, warmth, air and the Sun's old energy in its lunch.

Inside a tiny seed is a whole plant, folded small, waiting. The parent plant packed it with sunlight stored as food. When water, warmth and air reach it, the One life that was resting in it unfolds into roots, shoots and leaves. DeepKids believes this is the One becoming the many, again and again. One seed becomes a tree, the tree makes a thousand seeds, and the same life carries on through all of them.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** A father, Uddalaka, asks his son Shvetaketu to split a banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing at all.' The father says: from this finest thing that you cannot see, the great banyan tree grows. That fine essence is in everything. And you are that. (When you split a bean, the baby plant is so small you can hardly see it. Yet a whole plant grows from it. The Upanishad uses a seed to point at what is hidden in everything, including you.)
- **Salistamba Sutra (the Rice Seedling Sutra), on outer dependent arising:** A sprout does not come from the seed alone. It comes when the seed meets earth, water, warmth, air, space and the right season. The seed does not think 'I make the sprout', and yet the sprout comes. (This old Buddhist teaching names almost exactly what the five-cup test found. Take away water, warmth or air, and no sprout comes. A sprout is a meeting of many things.)
- **Mencius, Book 2A, chapter 2:** A farmer from the state of Song was worried his young plants were growing too slowly. So he pulled on each one to help it grow. When his son ran to look, the plants had all withered. (You cannot make a seed sprout by pushing it. You can only give it water, warmth and air, and wait. Growing has its own time.)
- Science (settled): All living things alive today come from earlier living things, in an unbroken line going back billions of years. No seed, plant or animal has been seen to start from non-living matter.
- Science (open): A dry seed's cells are not dead. They are still whole, with their repair tools ready, and breathe at a tiny rate. Exactly how close to 'stopped' life can get, and still restart, is being studied.
- Other view, Many biologists: A seed is a brand new plant, not old life carrying on. It has its own new mix of genes from two parents, so it is a different individual. They see no need for a One life flowing through it, and that is an honest view.
- Practice, Be a waiting seed (3 min): Let's sit and curl up small, like a dry seed. Close your eyes. Breathe so slowly and softly that nobody could tell. Feel how still you are, but alive inside. Now imagine warm rain soaking in. Take one big, slow breath, and let your body uncurl a little. Where did your breath come from? Who gave you your very first food?
- Ask: When did your life begin? Is there a clear start?
- Ask: A seed waits, sometimes for years, until the rain comes. What are you waiting for?
- Ask: Is a sprout the same plant as the seed it came from, or a new one?

## Story: The Warm Bundle
*Noor, her family and the sprouts cart are made up. The science is real: soaked seeds need air, a heap of sprouting seeds gets warm because it is breathing, and seeds kept under water too long go sour.*

Every morning at six, Noor's mother pushed a blue handcart to the corner outside Byculla station in Mumbai. On the cart was a big steel bowl of sprouts. There were green moong with white tails, chopped onion, tomato, lemon and a pinch of red chilli. Office workers stopped on their way to the train. 'One plate, Ammi,' they said, and Ammi spooned sprouts into a leaf bowl. Two hundred plates on a good day. On a bad day, less.

Noor was nine, and she knew how the sprouts were made, or she thought she did. At night, Ammi poured moong into the big dabba and covered it with water. In the morning she drained it. She tipped the fat, wet seeds into an old white cloth and tied it loosely, like a bundle for a journey. Then she hung it from a nail on the window bars. By the next morning, every seed had a tail.

'Why the nail?' Noor once asked. 'Why not leave them in the dabba?'

'Because they are getting ready to travel,' said Ammi, which was not really an answer. Ammi's answers were often like that.

Then came the week of Khala's wedding in Nashik. Ammi had to go for two days. 'Faiz will run the cart on Sunday,' she said. Faiz was thirteen and thought he was eighteen. 'And Noor will make the sprouts. Soak them tonight. Hang them tomorrow. You have seen me do it a thousand times.'

Noor nodded. A thousand times was a lot.

That night she soaked the moong in the dabba, just as Ammi did. But in the morning, before she could drain it, she heard a scratching under the floorboards. Last month, a rat had chewed through Ammi's bundle on the nail. Half the sprouts had been ruined. Ammi had cried a little, when she thought nobody was looking.

Not this time, Noor decided. She left the moong in its water. She pressed the steel lid down hard. Then she put the heavy grinding stone on top. No rat could lift that. Pleased, she went to school.

When she came home, the room smelled strange. She lifted the stone and the lid. The water had gone cloudy, and a grey froth floated on top. The smell was sour, like milk left out in the heat. The seeds were fat and soft, but not one of them had a tail.

'You killed them,' said Faiz, holding his nose.

'I did not,' said Noor. But her stomach went cold. There was no money to buy more moong. The cart would sell nothing on Sunday, and Ammi would come home to an empty tin.

She sat down on the floor beside the dabba. She tried to remember everything Ammi did, every single step. Soak. Drain. Tie loosely. Hang. And one more thing, a thing she had nearly forgotten. Once, last winter, Ammi had let her hold the bundle before dawn. It had been warm. Not warm from the stove, which was cold. Warm from inside, like a sleeping kitten.

'Why is it warm?' she had asked.

'Because they are breathing,' Ammi had said. 'Everything that wakes up has to breathe.'

Noor looked at the dabba with its lid and its stone and its water. Nothing could get in, not a rat, and not a breath of air either. She thought of being held under water in the swimming pool at school. You can only stay down for a few seconds.

'They were drowning,' she said.

She drained the cloudy water at once. She rinsed the seeds under the tap, once, twice, five times, until the sour smell had almost gone. She picked out the ones that were mushy and broken. There were a lot of them. Then she spread the rest on the white cloth and tied it loosely, the way Ammi did, with room for air between the seeds. She hung it from the nail on the window bars, where the sea breeze came in.

'What about the rat?' said Faiz.

Noor fetched Ammi's steel strainer. She hung it upside down on the string above the bundle, like an umbrella. A rat that climbed down the string would slide right off. Faiz looked at it for a long time. Then he said, 'Not bad.'

Noor could not sleep. Twice in the night she got up and touched the bundle. It was cool and damp. At four in the morning she touched it again. And this time, in the very middle, it was warm. Not much. Just a little, like a hand that has been holding another hand.

When the first light came, she untied the cloth. Most of the seeds had split their green coats. Out of each one curled a tiny white tail, thin as a comma in her school book. Not every seed. About two of every three. But that was enough.

On Sunday, Faiz pushed the blue cart to the corner outside the station. Noor chopped the onions and squeezed the lemons. They sold one hundred and thirty plates. It was not two hundred. Faiz said it was a record for a cart run by children, and nobody could prove that it wasn't.

That night, Ammi called from Nashik. Music and laughter roared behind her. 'How were the sprouts?' she shouted.

'They were breathing,' said Noor.

There was a little silence on the line. Then Ammi laughed, the way she laughed when Noor said something she had not expected. 'Of course they were,' she said. 'Did you hang them on the nail?'

'With an umbrella,' said Noor. And she went to the window and laid her hand on the new bundle, soaked and tied and hanging in the dark, already beginning to wake.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the seeds did not fail because they were soaked wrongly. They failed because they could not breathe. A waking seed needs air as well as water, and seeds left sealed under water run out of oxygen and go sour. The warmth Noor remembered was the clue: a heap of sprouting seeds gets warm because millions of cells are breathing, using oxygen to burn their stored food. Water, warmth and air together are what wake a seed. The story also quietly shows how much a child can learn just by watching a parent's ordinary work closely.

- Ask: Noor shut the moong under a heavy lid to keep the rat out. Why did that stop them sprouting?
- Ask: The bundle on the nail felt warm in the night. Where did the warmth come from?
- Ask: Why do you think Ammi always tied the cloth loosely, not tightly?
- Ask: Noor remembered something Ammi did without explaining it. What have you learnt just by watching someone at home?
- Ask: Faiz said 'Not bad' about the strainer umbrella. How did Noor solve two problems at once?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Five-Cup Test: what wakes a seed? (30 min, 3 days)
- **question:** What does a moong seed need to sprout? Water? Warmth? Air? Light?
- **predict:** Before you start, look at the five cups. Guess how many of the 10 seeds will sprout in each cup. Write your guesses down.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, every cup gets the same kind of seeds, the same number of seeds and the same cotton. Each cup changes only one thing compared with the damp, warm cup. We use 10 seeds in each cup, not 1, because one seed might be a dud.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water for cup 4 and lets it cool completely. Do not eat the seeds used in this test. Throw away any seeds that smell sour.
- **record:** Fill in the table each day. On day 3, draw a bar chart with one bar for each cup. Make each bar as tall as the number of seeds that sprouted.
- **results table:** Cup | What is different | My guess (out of 10) | Sprouted on day 2 | Sprouted on day 3
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up the five cups. Write your guesses. (20 min); day 2: Count the seeds with a root in each cup. Keep cups 2, 3 and 5 damp. (5 min); day 3: Count again. Draw a bar chart. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (10 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a thin layer of cotton in each of the 5 cups. 2. Count 10 moong seeds onto the cotton in each cup. 3. Label the cups: 1 Dry, 2 Damp, 3 Fridge, 4 Under water, 5 Light.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to find out what wakes a seed. Each cup is a little different. Let's count 10 seeds into each one.  
  *Child mode:* Put cotton in 5 cups. Count 10 moong seeds into each cup. Label them: Dry, Damp, Fridge, Under water, Light.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cup 1: no water. 2. Cups 2, 3 and 5: add water a spoon at a time until the cotton is wet but no water is lying on top. 3. Cup 4: a grown-up boils some water and lets it cool. Fill cup 4 to the top with it. Pour a thin layer of oil on top.  
  *Parent says:* Boiling water pushes out most of the air in it. The oil stops new air getting back in. So cup 4 has lots of water but almost no air.  
  *Child mode:* Cup 1 stays dry. Make cups 2, 3 and 5 damp, not swimming. Ask a grown-up for cooled boiled water for cup 4. Fill it up. Add a little oil on top.
  - Note: Boiling removes much of the dissolved air from water, and oil slows air getting back in. It is not perfect, so a seed or two may still sprout in cup 4. That is fine, and worth talking about.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cover cups 1, 2 and 4 with plates and put them on a kitchen shelf. 2. Put cup 3 in the fridge, not the freezer. 3. Put cup 5 uncovered in a bright spot on the same shelf or a window sill, out of hot direct sun. 4. Write your guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Cup 2 is our normal cup. Each other cup is different in just one way. Which one is missing water? Which is missing warmth? Which is missing air? Which has extra light?  
  *Child mode:* Cover cups 1, 2 and 4 with plates. Put cup 3 in the fridge. Put cup 5 in a bright spot. Now guess: how many will sprout in each cup?
  - Ask: Why do we compare every cup with cup 2? (good: Cup 2 has everything, so it shows what normal looks like.; Each other cup changes just one thing, so we know what made the difference.)
  - Check: In the five-cup test, which cup tests whether seeds need air? → **Cup 4, Under water with oil**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The next day, count the seeds with a little white root in each cup. 2. Write the numbers in the table. 3. Add a few drops of water to cups 2, 3 and 5 if the cotton feels dry.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count. A seed counts as sprouted when you can see a little white root poking out.  
  *Child mode:* Count the seeds with a white tail in each cup. Write the numbers down. Is any cotton dry? Add a few drops.
  - Note: By day 2, cups 2 and 5 usually have most seeds sprouted. Cup 3 is usually still asleep. Cup 1 seeds look just as they did. Cup 4 seeds are swollen but mostly have no root.
  - Wait: Leave the cups for 1 day
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 3, count again and write the numbers. 2. Help your child draw a bar chart with one bar for each cup.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the numbers into a picture. Each cup gets one bar. The taller the bar, the more seeds sprouted.  
  *Child mode:* Count again. Draw one bar for each cup. Make it as tall as the number that sprouted.
  - Check: In the five-cup test, the damp cup in the light and the damp cup in the dark both sprouted about the same. What does that tell you? → **Moong seeds do not need light to sprout**
  - Wait: Leave the cups for 1 more day
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Then take cup 3 out of the fridge and leave it on the shelf for 2 more days.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like real scientists. Tell me three things. What do you think seeds need? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why. Then take the fridge cup out. Will its seeds wake up now?
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: moong seeds need water, warmth and air, but not light. What I saw: 9 of 10 sprouted in the damp cups, but none in the dry cup, 1 in the fridge and 2 under water. Why: water wakes the seed, warmth makes it work faster, and it needs air to breathe. The fridge seeds usually sprout after a day or two in the warm. That shows cold made them wait, but did not kill them.
  - Think: The fridge seeds woke up when they were warmed. Were they alive all along?
### e2 · The Warm Flask: do sprouting seeds breathe? (25 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Do sprouting seeds make warmth? Is that a sign that they are breathing?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After a day, will the two flasks be the same temperature, or will one be warmer? Which one?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, both flasks get the same kind and amount of seeds, soaked for the same time. Both stay in the same place. The only difference is that one lot is alive and the other has been boiled.
- **safety:** Only a grown-up boils the seeds. Let them cool completely before they go in a flask. Plug the flasks loosely with cotton, never with a tight lid, so air can get in. Don't eat the seeds afterwards.
- **record:** Write the temperatures in the table each time. Then draw two bars for the 24-hour reading: one for the live seeds and one for the boiled seeds.
- **results table:** Time | Live seeds: temperature (degrees Celsius) | Boiled seeds: temperature (degrees Celsius) | Room: temperature (degrees Celsius)
- **schedule:** day 1: Boil and cool one lot of soaked seeds. Fill both flasks. Read both thermometers. (15 min); day 2: Read both thermometers in the morning and evening. (5 min); day 3: Read again. Draw two bars for the 24-hour readings. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (5 min)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Split the soaked moong into two equal bowls. 2. A grown-up boils one bowl for 10 minutes. 3. Let the boiled seeds cool completely, until they feel as cool as the live ones.  
  *Parent says:* Boiling kills the baby plants inside the seeds. So these boiled seeds are just the same as the live ones, except they are no longer alive.  
  *Child mode:* Split the soaked moong into two bowls. Ask a grown-up to boil one bowl. Wait until it is cool.
  - Ask: Why do we use boiled seeds, and not an empty flask? (good: So the only difference is being alive.; The boiled seeds are wet and the same size, but they can't breathe.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill one flask with live seeds and label it LIVE. 2. Fill the other with boiled seeds and label it BOILED. 3. Push a thermometer into the middle of each. 4. Plug each top loosely with cotton. 5. Read both thermometers and the room temperature. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* The cotton lets air in, but keeps the warmth in the flask. Let's read the thermometers now, at the start.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the LIVE flask and the BOILED flask. Push a thermometer into each. Plug the tops loosely with cotton. Read both numbers. Write them down.
  - Note: Read the number at the top of the red line. At the start, both flasks should be about the same as the room.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read both thermometers each morning and evening for 2 days. Write each reading in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Are they still the same? Which one is changing?  
  *Child mode:* Read both thermometers, morning and evening. Write the numbers. Which flask is warmer?
  - Note: Live sprouting seeds usually warm up by a few degrees. The boiled seeds stay close to the room temperature. If the boiled seeds start to smell or warm up after a few days, tiny living things called bacteria have started to feed on them. Bacteria breathe too. So stop the test after 2 days.
  - Wait: Check morning and evening for 2 days
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like scientists. What do you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: sprouting seeds breathe, and breathing makes warmth. What I saw: the live seeds warmed up from 27 to 31 degrees, but the boiled seeds stayed at 27. Why: live seeds use oxygen to get energy from their food, and some of that energy comes out as heat.
  - Check: In the warm flask test, the flask of live sprouts got warmer than the flask of boiled seeds. Why? → **The live seeds were breathing, and breathing makes warmth**
### e3 · The Swelling Push: how much water does a seed drink? (20 min, 2 days)
- **question:** How much water does a seed drink before it sprouts? How hard can a swelling seed push?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After a night in water, will the chana weigh the same, a little more, or twice as much?
- **fair test:** Weigh the chana on the same scale both times. Before you weigh the soaked chana, drain it and pat it dry with a cloth, so you weigh only the water inside the seeds, not the water on the outside.
- **safety:** Use plastic, never glass, for the pushing test. Swelling seeds can crack glass.
- **record:** Write both weights. Draw the full cup before and after.
- **results table:** Chana | Weight (grams) | How full is the cup?
- **schedule:** day 1: Weigh the dry chana and soak it. Fill a small plastic cup to the brim with dry chana and water, and put its lid on. (10 min); day 2: Weigh the soaked chana. Look at the lid of the full cup. Work out how many times heavier the chana is. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Weigh out 50 grams of dry chana. 2. Put it in a bowl and cover it with plenty of water. 3. Write down the weight.  
  *Parent says:* Dry seeds have very little water inside. Let's find out how much they drink overnight.  
  *Child mode:* Weigh 50 grams of dry chana. Write it down. Cover it with lots of water.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill a small plastic cup right to the top with dry chana. 2. Pour in water to the brim. 3. Press the lid on.  
  *Parent says:* This cup is full to the top. What will happen when the seeds swell?  
  *Child mode:* Fill a small plastic cup with dry chana, right to the top. Add water to the top. Put the lid on. What will happen?
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The next morning, drain the chana in the bowl and pat it dry with a cloth. 2. Weigh it again and write the weight. 3. Look at the cup with the lid.  
  *Parent says:* How much does it weigh now? Where did the extra weight come from? And look at that lid!  
  *Child mode:* Drain the chana. Pat it dry. Weigh it again. Is it heavier? Now look at the cup. What happened to the lid?
  - Note: The chana usually weighs about twice as much, close to 100 grams. The extra weight is water that went inside the seeds. The lid is usually pushed up or off, and the cup may bulge. Swelling seeds push very hard.
  - Wait: Leave overnight
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out together how many times heavier the chana is. Then ask your child to say what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* 50 grams became about 100 grams. How many 50s make 100? So how many times heavier is it?  
  *Child mode:* Dry: 50 grams. Soaked: about 100 grams. How many times heavier? Then say what you think, what you saw and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: seeds drink a lot of water before they sprout. What I saw: 50 grams of chana became 100 grams, twice as heavy, and the seeds pushed the lid off. Why: water goes inside the seed and it swells.
  - Check: In the swelling test, 50 grams of dry chana weighed about 100 grams after soaking. Where did the extra weight come from? → **Water that went inside the seeds**

### a1 · Open a Seed: find the baby plant and its lunch (15 min)
- **safety:** Use your fingernails to open the seeds. A grown-up cuts the maize kernel with a knife if you try one.
- **record:** Draw one opened bean, big. Label three parts: seed coat, baby plant and food store.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child one dry bean and one soaked bean. 2. Ask them to feel, look at and compare the two.  
  *Parent says:* This one has been in water all night. This one is dry. What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Hold a dry bean and a soaked bean. Feel them. Look closely. What is different?
  - Ask: What has the water done to the bean? (good: It is bigger and fatter.; It is softer.; The skin is smooth and looser.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the pale scar on the side of the soaked bean. 2. Rub the coat until it slips off. 3. Gently split the bean along its edge into two halves with a fingernail.

  *Child mode:* Find the small pale scar on the bean. Rub off the coat. Use your nail to open the bean in two, like a book.
  - Note: The scar is called the hilum. Splitting is easiest along the curved edge, opposite the scar side. Some halves will break. That is fine. Try another bean.

## Worksheets
### Seed Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What are the three main parts of a seed?  
   Options: Seed coat, baby plant and food store / Root, stem and flower / Skin, juice and stone / Leaf, fruit and bark  
   **Answer:** Seed coat, baby plant and food store. *Why:* Every seed has a coat outside, a tiny baby plant inside, and a store of food for it. Picking 'root, stem and flower' mixes up a seed with a grown plant. Those parts grow later.
2. (fill) The tough outer skin of a seed is called the seed ____.  
   **Answer:** ['coat']. *Why:* The seed coat protects the baby plant and keeps it dry until it is time to grow.
3. (mcq) You split a soaked bean in two. What is the tiny thing at one edge, with a little root and two small leaves?  
   Options: The baby plant / A worm / The food store / A bit of the coat  
   **Answer:** The baby plant. *Why:* That is the baby plant, which scientists call the embryo. It was already inside the seed before you soaked it. Picking 'food store' is close, but the food store is the two big halves around it.
4. (mcq) What do most seeds need to sprout?  
   Options: Water, warmth and air / Sunlight, soil and water / Only water / Soil and fertiliser  
   **Answer:** Water, warmth and air. *Why:* Water wakes the seed, warmth makes the changes inside it go faster, and air lets it breathe. Picking 'sunlight and soil' is the most common mistake. A seed has its own food, so it does not need them to start.
5. (mcq) In the five-cup test, you put 10 moong seeds in each of 5 cups: dry, damp, damp in the fridge, under water with oil on top, and damp in the light. In which cup did the most seeds sprout?  
   Options: Dry / Damp and warm / In the fridge / Under water with oil  
   **Answer:** Damp and warm. *Why:* The damp, warm cup had water, warmth and air together. Picking 'under water' usually means thinking more water is always better. Those seeds could not get enough air.
6. (truefalse) Moong seeds can sprout in a dark cupboard.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A seed carries its own food, so it does not need light to sprout. In the five-cup test, the cup in the dark did as well as the cup in the light.
7. (truefalse) Seeds need soil before they can sprout.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Seeds sprout on wet cotton, cloth or paper. Soil helps later, by holding water and the plant's roots.
8. (mcq) Which part comes out of a sprouting seed first?  
   Options: The root / The leaves / The flower / The fruit  
   **Answer:** The root. *Why:* The root comes out first, so the seedling can hold on and drink. Picking 'leaves' is common, because leaves are what we see first above the soil.
9. (fill) A seed waking up and starting to grow is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['germination', 'sprouting']. *Why:* Germination is the scientists' word for a seed sprouting.
10. (sort) Sort these. Could they still sprout, or not?  
   **Answer:** whole moong → Could sprout; split moong dal → Could not sprout; raw peanut → Could sprout; roasted peanut → Could not sprout; rajma → Could sprout; popped popcorn → Could not sprout. *Why:* A seed can sprout only if its baby plant is whole and alive. Splitting the dal loses the baby plant. Roasting and popping kill it.
11. (mcq) A seed is put in a cup full of water, right up to the top. What usually happens?  
   Options: It sprouts best, because it has the most water / It does badly, because it cannot get enough air / It turns into a plant overnight  
   **Answer:** It does badly, because it cannot get enough air. *Why:* Seeds need air to breathe. Under water there is very little air, so most seeds do badly or rot. Picking 'sprouts best' means thinking more water is always better. Rice is an exception.
12. (mcq) Why does a bean seed have two fat halves full of food?  
   Options: To feed the baby plant until its leaves can make food / To make the seed heavy so it doesn't blow away / So animals can eat it  
   **Answer:** To feed the baby plant until its leaves can make food. *Why:* The food store feeds the baby plant while it is still underground, with no light. Animals do eat seeds, but that is not why the seed has food. The food is for the baby plant.
13. (write) Your friend says, 'Dry seeds in a jar are dead.' What would you tell them? Give one piece of evidence.  
   **Answer:** Dry seeds are alive, but asleep. My evidence: when we soaked moong from a jar, most of it sprouted in two days. Boiled seeds never sprout, so the jar seeds must have been alive.. *Why:* A good answer says the seeds are alive but resting, and gives evidence, such as the seeds sprouting once they got water.
14. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, as a bean sprouts.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* First the seed drinks and swells, which splits the coat. The root comes first, then the shoot. Leaves open last, once the shoot reaches the light.
15. (draw) Draw a seed opened in half. Label the seed coat, the baby plant and the food store.  
   **Answer:** A bean shape split open, with the coat peeling off, two large halves labelled 'food store' and a tiny plant at one edge labelled 'baby plant'.. *Why:* Every seed has these three parts.
16. (mcq) Why do we put 10 seeds in each cup, not just 1?  
   Options: Because one seed might be a dud, and 10 give a fairer answer / Because seeds get lonely / Because 10 is a lucky number  
   **Answer:** Because one seed might be a dud, and 10 give a fairer answer. *Why:* Some seeds don't sprout even when everything is right. If we used only one, a single dud seed could fool us. With 10, one dud matters much less.
### How Seeds Wake Up (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A heap of sprouting moong in a cloth gets warm. Why?  
   Options: The seeds are breathing, and breathing makes a little heat / The cloth is made of wool / The water is hot / The seeds are rotting  
   **Answer:** The seeds are breathing, and breathing makes a little heat. *Why:* Sprouting seeds use oxygen to get energy from their food, and some energy comes out as heat. Picking 'rotting' is a good guess, because rotting things do warm up. But healthy sprouts warm up too, and boiled seeds do not.
2. (mcq) In the warm flask test, why did we use boiled seeds in the second flask?  
   Options: So the only difference between the flasks was whether the seeds were alive / To cook them for dinner / Because boiled seeds sprout faster  
   **Answer:** So the only difference between the flasks was whether the seeds were alive. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Both flasks had the same wet seeds, but only one lot was alive. So any difference in warmth must come from being alive. Picking 'sprout faster' is wrong. Boiled seeds never sprout.
3. (fill) The gas in the air that sprouting seeds need to breathe is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['oxygen']. *Why:* Seeds, like us, use oxygen from the air to get energy from food.
4. (mcq) In the swelling test, 50 grams of dry chana weighed about 100 grams after soaking. How many times heavier was it?  
   Options: 2 times / 50 times / 100 times / It weighed the same  
   **Answer:** 2 times. *Why:* 100 is 2 lots of 50. So the soaked chana was 2 times as heavy. The extra 50 grams is water inside the seeds.
5. (truefalse) A jar packed with dry chana and water can push its lid off overnight.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Swelling seeds push very hard as they soak up water. That is why we use a plastic cup, never glass.
6. (mcq) Your seeds on damp cotton in the fridge hardly sprouted. Then you moved them to the kitchen shelf. What will most likely happen?  
   Options: They will start to sprout, because cold only made them wait / Nothing, because the cold killed them / They will turn into ice  
   **Answer:** They will start to sprout, because cold only made them wait. *Why:* Cold slows the changes inside a seed, but usually does not kill it. Once it is warm, it wakes up. Picking 'the cold killed them' is a common guess. Try it and see.
7. (match) Match each cup in the five-cup test to what it was missing.  
   **Answer:** Dry cup → water; Fridge cup → warmth; Under water with oil → air; Damp cup in the light → nothing missing, it had extra light. *Why:* Each cup was different from the damp, warm cup in just one way. That way we can tell what each thing does.
8. (mcq) A root always grows down, even if the seed is upside down. How does the root know which way is down?  
   Options: Its tip feels gravity, using tiny heavy grains that settle at the bottom of its cells / It can see the ground / It follows the smell of soil / It doesn't know, it just grows any way  
   **Answer:** Its tip feels gravity, using tiny heavy grains that settle at the bottom of its cells. *Why:* Cells in the root tip hold tiny heavy grains of starch. They sink to the bottom, like sand in a bottle, so the root can tell which way is down. Picking 'see' is not right. A root has no eyes, and it turns down even in a dark jar.
9. (mcq) Why do sprouts taste a little sweet?  
   Options: The seed has turned some of its starch into sugar / Someone added sugar / The water was sweet  
   **Answer:** The seed has turned some of its starch into sugar. *Why:* As a seed sprouts, enzymes, helpers made by its cells, cut starch into sugar the baby plant can use. That's why ragi malt is made from sprouted ragi.
10. (mcq) Rice seeds are unusual. What can they do that most seeds can't?  
   Options: Start to sprout under water, sending a shoot up like a snorkel / Sprout without any water / Sprout in a freezer  
   **Answer:** Start to sprout under water, sending a shoot up like a snorkel. *Why:* Rice comes from wet places. Under water, its first shoot grows up to the air like a snorkel, so air can reach the rest of the seedling. Most seeds would drown.
11. (write) Noor's mother hung her moong in a loose cloth bundle, not in a closed tin. Why is that better for sprouting? Use the word air in your answer.  
   **Answer:** In a loose cloth, air can reach every seed, so the seeds can breathe. In a closed tin full of water, they get very little air and go sour.. *Why:* A good answer says that seeds need air to breathe, and a loose cloth lets air in.
12. (mcq) A big rajma bean and a tiny banyan seed. Which grows into the bigger plant?  
   Options: The banyan seed, which grows into a giant tree / The rajma, because its seed is bigger / They grow the same size  
   **Answer:** The banyan seed, which grows into a giant tree. *Why:* The size of a seed does not tell you the size of the plant. A tiny banyan seed can become a huge tree. The plant builds itself from air, water and sunlight over many years.
13. (draw) Draw the seed window jar after 5 days. Show 3 seeds that started in different ways, with their roots and shoots.  
   **Answer:** Three seeds, each with a root curving down and a shoot curving up, whichever way the seed was placed.. *Why:* Roots grow towards gravity and shoots grow away from it.
14. (write) Plan a fair test to find out if methi seeds need warmth to sprout. What will you change? What will you keep the same?  
   **Answer:** I will change only the warmth: one cup in the fridge and one on the kitchen shelf. I will keep the same: the number of methi seeds, the same cotton, the same amount of water, and both cups dark.. *Why:* A good fair test changes just one thing and keeps everything else the same. Keeping both cups dark matters, because the fridge is dark.
15. (truefalse) Old seeds from Beal's bottles, buried in 1879, still sprouted in 2021.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. More than 140 years later, some of the seeds sprouted. Dry seeds in the ground can stay alive for a very long time.
### Seed Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Deep down, where did the energy in a seed's food store come from?  
   Options: From sunlight, captured by the parent plant's leaves / From the soil / From the water the seed drinks / From the seed coat  
   **Answer:** From sunlight, captured by the parent plant's leaves. *Why:* The parent plant used the energy of sunlight to make starch from air and water, and packed it into the seed. Picking 'soil' is a very old idea. Soil gives a plant water and small amounts of minerals, but not its food energy.
2. (mcq) You sprout 50 moong seeds in the dark for a week, then dry them. Do they weigh more or less than 50 dry seeds that were never sprouted?  
   Options: Less, because they burned some of their stored food by breathing / More, because they grew / Exactly the same  
   **Answer:** Less, because they burned some of their stored food by breathing. *Why:* In the dark, sprouts cannot make new food. They use up their stored food by breathing, and the carbon goes into the air. So once the water is dried out, they weigh less. Picking 'more' is the usual guess, because sprouts look bigger. Most of that size is water.
3. (write) A seed sealed under water goes sour. A seed in a dry jar does nothing. A seed on damp cotton sprouts. Explain all three using what a seed needs.  
   **Answer:** Under water, the seed has water but no air, so it cannot breathe, and it goes sour. In a dry jar, it has air but no water, so it stays asleep. On damp cotton, it has water and air, and the room is warm, so it sprouts.. *Why:* A good answer names what each seed has and what it is missing.
4. (mcq) Some small lettuce seeds will sprout only if light reaches them. Why might a tiny seed wait for light?  
   Options: Light tells it it is near the surface, so its small food store won't run out before it reaches the sun / Light gives it food straight away / Light makes it heavier  
   **Answer:** Light tells it it is near the surface, so its small food store won't run out before it reaches the sun. *Why:* A tiny seed has only a little food. If it sprouted deep underground, it might run out before reaching the light. So waiting for light keeps it safe. Scientists found this in 1952.
5. (match) Match each scientist to what they found.  
   **Answer:** William James Beal, 1879 → buried bottles of seeds to see how long they live; Elaine Solowey and Sarah Sallon, 2005 → grew a date palm from a 2,000-year-old seed; Leslie Paleg and Hiroshi Yomo, 1960 → found the message a baby plant sends to its food store; Gavin Flematti and team, 2004 → found the chemical in smoke that wakes some seeds. *Why:* Each of these scientists helped us understand how seeds wait and wake.
6. (truefalse) In 2019, a cotton seed sprouted on the Moon inside China's Chang'e 4 lander.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It sprouted in a sealed box with water, air and soil, then died when the freezing lunar night came. It had water and air, but not enough warmth.
7. (write) Is a dry seed in a jar alive? Give one reason to say yes, and one reason someone might say no.  
   **Answer:** Yes: it is still breathing very slowly, and it sprouts when we add water, unlike a boiled seed. Someone might say no: it does not grow, move or seem to eat.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Scientists mostly say it is alive but dormant. What counts is a reason on each side.
8. (mcq) A heap of sprouting seeds warms up. A heap of boiled seeds stays cool for a day, then starts to warm and smell. Why does the boiled heap start to warm?  
   Options: Tiny living things called bacteria start feeding on it, and they breathe too / The boiled seeds come back to life / The sun shines on it  
   **Answer:** Tiny living things called bacteria start feeding on it, and they breathe too. *Why:* Boiled seeds are dead, but bacteria can feed on them. Bacteria breathe, so they make warmth too. That is why we stop the warm flask test after 2 days.
9. (write) Why do you think seed banks keep seeds dry and very cold?  
   **Answer:** Dry and cold slow everything inside the seed down, so the seed uses its food very slowly and stays asleep. Then it can stay alive for many years, until someone needs it.. *Why:* Warmth and water wake a seed. Taking them away keeps it asleep.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out? Rajma, chana, moong, potato.  
   Options: Rajma / Chana / Moong / Potato  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Rajma, chana and moong are seeds, each with a baby plant inside. A potato is a swollen underground stem. It can grow new plants, but it is not a seed.
11. (mcq) Rahibai Soma Popere, a farmer in Maharashtra, saves hundreds of old kinds of local seeds. Why does keeping many kinds matter?  
   Options: If a disease or drought kills one kind, other kinds may survive / Old seeds always taste better / It makes the seeds sprout faster  
   **Answer:** If a disease or drought kills one kind, other kinds may survive. *Why:* Different kinds of the same crop cope with different problems. If every farmer grew just one kind, one disease could wipe out the whole harvest. For this work, she was given the Padma Shri in 2020.
12. (write) A farmer stores seed paddy in a damp, warm corner of the house. Next season, very few seeds sprout. What went wrong?  
   **Answer:** Damp and warmth woke the seeds up too early, or made them use up their food and spoil while stored. Seeds keep best when dry and cool.. *Why:* Water and warmth wake seeds. In storage, we want them to stay asleep.
13. (draw) Invent a seed for a desert where it rains only once every few years. Draw it and label two features that help it wait and then sprout fast.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful design, for example: a very hard waterproof coat so it can wait for years, a coat that only softens after lots of rain, a big food store so the root can grow fast and deep.. *Why:* Real desert seeds do things like these. Many wait in the sand until a big rain, then sprout and flower in a few weeks.
14. (write) In the story The Warm Bundle, Noor remembered the bundle being warm. How did that clue help her save the sprouts?  
   **Answer:** Ammi said the warmth came from the seeds breathing. So Noor realised the seeds needed air. In the closed dabba under water, they could not breathe. She drained them and hung them in a loose cloth so air could reach them.. *Why:* The warmth showed that sprouting seeds breathe. Breathing needs air.
15. (write) Astronauts on the Moon want to grow beans. List what they must give the seeds to sprout, and what the young plants will need later.  
   **Answer:** To sprout: water, warmth and air, inside a sealed box or base. Later: light for the leaves to make food, and minerals, like those in soil or plant food.. *Why:* A seed needs water, warmth and air to wake. A seedling needs light once its own food store runs out.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is inside every seed?  
   Options: A baby plant and its food store / Only water / A tiny flower / Nothing, it is solid  
   **Answer:** A baby plant and its food store. *Why:* Every seed holds a living baby plant and a store of food for it. Picking 'nothing' is an easy mistake, because the baby plant is tiny. Soak a bean, split it and look closely. It is there.
2. (mcq) What are the two big halves of a split chana?  
   Options: The food store, in its two seed leaves / The roots / Two baby plants / The seed coat  
   **Answer:** The food store, in its two seed leaves. *Why:* They are seed leaves packed with food. The thin seed coat is outside them, and the tiny baby plant sits between them. Picking 'two baby plants' mixes up the food store with the plant it feeds.
3. (mcq) Which three things do most seeds need to sprout?  
   Options: Water, warmth and air / Sunlight, soil and water / Soil, fertiliser and sunlight / Only water  
   **Answer:** Water, warmth and air. *Why:* Water wakes the seed, warmth makes it work faster and air lets it breathe. Picking 'sunlight and soil' is the most common mistake. A seed carries its own food.
4. (mcq) Does a moong seed need light to sprout?  
   Options: No, it sprouts in the dark too / Yes, it can't sprout without light / Only red light  
   **Answer:** No, it sprouts in the dark too. *Why:* Moong sprouts in a dark tiffin. Grown plants need light, which is why many people guess 'yes'. A few tiny seeds, like some lettuce, do need light. Moong does not.
5. (mcq) In the five-cup test, you put 10 moong seeds in each of 5 cups: dry, damp, damp in the fridge, under water with oil on top, and damp in the light. Which cup tests whether seeds need warmth?  
   Options: The fridge cup / The dry cup / The cup under water / The cup in the light  
   **Answer:** The fridge cup. *Why:* The fridge cup has water and air, but is cold. So it tests warmth. Picking the dry cup mixes up warmth and water.
6. (mcq) Why must a fair test change only one thing?  
   Options: So we know which thing made the difference / So the test is quicker / So more seeds sprout  
   **Answer:** So we know which thing made the difference. *Why:* If we changed two things at once, we would not know which one caused the result. Picking 'quicker' misses the point. Fair tests are about trusting the answer.
7. (mcq) In the five-cup test, you put 10 moong seeds in each of 5 cups: dry, damp, damp in the fridge, under water with oil on top, and damp in the light. Look at the chart. How many more seeds sprouted in the damp warm cup than in the fridge cup?  
   Options: 8 more / 9 more / 10 more / 1 more  
   **Answer:** 8 more. *Why:* The damp, warm cup had 9 and the fridge cup had 1. 9 take away 1 is 8. Picking 9 means reading just the damp cup's bar, not the difference.
8. (mcq) You soaked moong at 8 o'clock in the evening. The first roots appeared at 8 o'clock the next evening. How long did that take?  
   Options: 24 hours, one whole day / 12 hours / 8 hours / 2 days  
   **Answer:** 24 hours, one whole day. *Why:* From 8 in the evening to 8 the next evening is a full day, 24 hours. Picking 12 hours means counting only from evening to morning.
9. (mcq) A cloth bundle of sprouting moong feels warm in the middle. Why?  
   Options: The seeds are breathing, which makes warmth / The cloth was ironed / Sprouts are always hot / The water was boiled  
   **Answer:** The seeds are breathing, which makes warmth. *Why:* Sprouting seeds use oxygen to get energy from their stored food. Some energy comes out as warmth. A boiled seed is dead and makes no warmth.
10. (mcq) Why do seeds sealed under water often go sour and fail to sprout?  
   Options: They cannot get enough air to breathe / They have too much food / Water is poisonous to seeds  
   **Answer:** They cannot get enough air to breathe. *Why:* Water holds only a little oxygen. Seeds sealed under it cannot breathe well, so they fail and go sour. Picking 'poisonous' is wrong. Seeds need water, just not too much of it.
11. (mcq) What happens to a seed's food store as the seedling grows?  
   Options: It gets used up, so the seed halves shrink / It grows bigger / It turns into roots  
   **Answer:** It gets used up, so the seed halves shrink. *Why:* The baby plant lives on its stored food until its green leaves can make food. So the seed halves shrink. Picking 'grows bigger' is a natural guess, but the food is being eaten, not made.
12. (mcq) What is the first thing that happens when a dry seed gets wet?  
   Options: It drinks water and swells / Its leaves open / It grows a flower / It turns green  
   **Answer:** It drinks water and swells. *Why:* The seed soaks up water and swells, often to about twice its weight. Then its coat splits and the root comes out.
13. (mcq) A seed is planted upside down. Which way will its root grow?  
   Options: Down, towards the ground / Up, the way the seed was pointing / It will not grow  
   **Answer:** Down, towards the ground. *Why:* Root tips feel gravity, so roots always turn to grow down. Picking 'up' means thinking the root just follows the way the seed points.
14. (mcq) How long can a seed stay alive?  
   Options: It depends on the seed. Some last a year or two, and a few have sprouted after hundreds of years. Nobody knows the limit. / Only one week / For ever, always / Exactly 100 years  
   **Answer:** It depends on the seed. Some last a year or two, and a few have sprouted after hundreds of years. Nobody knows the limit.. *Why:* A date palm seed about 2,000 years old sprouted in 2005. Most seeds last far less. Scientists still do not know the longest a seed can live.
15. (mcq) Do scientists agree that a dry seed is alive?  
   Options: Most say yes, alive but asleep. Some say life almost stops in it, and philosophers still argue about what 'alive' means. / Yes, and there is nothing more to say / No, everyone agrees it is dead  
   **Answer:** Most say yes, alive but asleep. Some say life almost stops in it, and philosophers still argue about what 'alive' means.. *Why:* Most biologists call a dry seed dormant: alive, but asleep. Because it does so little, some scientists and thinkers say it sits between alive and not alive. This is still discussed.

## Applications
- Farmers soak and sprout seed, like paddy, before sowing, so it comes up quickly and evenly.
- Sprouts of moong, chana and methi are sold in markets and eaten in usal, salads and chaat.
- Malt, made by sprouting grains like barley and ragi, is used in ragi malt, some sweets, bread and drinks.
- Seed companies test how many seeds in a batch sprout, and print it on the packet as the germination rate.
- Seed banks keep seeds dry and cold, so they stay asleep and alive for decades.
- Gardeners soak hard seeds like bhindi and bitter gourd overnight, so water gets in faster.
- Gardeners nick or rub very hard seeds with sandpaper to let water in.
- Microgreens, tiny young plants grown from seeds on trays, are a new kind of crop in cities.
- Farmers store seed in dry, cool places, sometimes with neem leaves, so it does not sprout or spoil too soon.
- Scientists breed rice that can sprout in flooded fields, so farmers can sow it straight into wet ground.
- Space agencies test seeds in space, to plan how astronauts could grow food on the Moon and Mars.
- Forest workers collect and sprout seeds of native trees to plant new forests.
- Seed balls, seeds rolled into balls of clay and soil, are thrown onto bare land so they sprout when rain comes.

## Open questions
- **How long can a seed stay alive?** Some have sprouted after 2,000 years. Nobody knows the real limit, and it is different for every kind of seed. Experiments like Beal's bottles take centuries.
- **How does a seed decide when to wake?** Seeds sense water, warmth, light and some chemicals. How they add all these signals together is still being worked out.
- **Why do some seeds of the same plant wake at once, and others wait for years?** Scientists think it spreads the risk, like not putting all your eggs in one basket. How each seed is set to wait is not fully known.
- **How do seeds repair the damage of getting old while they sleep?** Seeds have repair tools for their cells. Scientists are still finding out how they work, and why some seeds are so much better at it.
- **Can we store seeds that do not like being dried, like mango, jackfruit and many rainforest seeds?** These seeds die if dried or frozen, so normal seed banks cannot keep them. Scientists are testing new ways, such as freezing just the baby plant in liquid nitrogen.
- **Could we grow crops from seed on the Moon or Mars?** Seeds have sprouted on the Moon in a sealed box and in Moon soil brought to Earth. Growing whole crops there, through many generations, has never been done.
- **Is a dry seed alive, or something in between?** It depends on what we mean by 'alive'. Scientists and philosophers still discuss it.

## Connected maths
- Look at the chart from the five-cup test. How many seeds sprouted in all five cups together? And how many seeds did not sprout? **21 sprouted and 29 did not.**. Add the bars: 0 + 9 + 1 + 2 + 9 = 21 seeds sprouted. There were 10 seeds in each of 5 cups, so 50 seeds in all. 50 take away 21 is 29. So 29 seeds did not sprout. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the damp, warm cup, 9 out of 10 seeds sprouted. In a bigger test, a farmer sows 100 seeds of the same kind in the same way. About how many would you expect to sprout? **About 90.**. 9 out of every 10 sprouted. 100 seeds is 10 lots of 10. So expect 10 lots of 9 sprouts. 10 times 9 is 90. It may not be exactly 90, because every seed is a little different. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the swelling test, 50 grams of dry chana weighed 100 grams after soaking overnight. How many grams of water did the chana drink? How many times heavier was it? **50 grams of water. It was 2 times as heavy.**. 100 grams take away 50 grams is 50 grams. That is the water inside the seeds. 50 + 50 = 100, so the soaked chana weighs 2 lots of 50 grams. It is 2 times as heavy. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)
- The thermometer in the flask of live sprouts shows 31 degrees Celsius. The flask of boiled seeds shows 27 degrees. How much warmer are the live sprouts? **4 degrees warmer.**. 31 take away 27 is 4. So the live sprouts are 4 degrees Celsius warmer. Count up from 27 to check: 28, 29, 30, 31. That is 4 steps. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- In your seed diary, the root of your bean was 6 millimetres on day 3, 15 millimetres on day 4 and 27 millimetres on day 5. How much did it grow each day? Did it grow faster or slower as the days went on? **9 millimetres from day 3 to day 4, then 12 millimetres from day 4 to day 5. It grew faster.**. 15 take away 6 is 9. So it grew 9 millimetres on the first day. 27 take away 15 is 12. So it grew 12 millimetres on the next day. 12 is more than 9, so the root was growing faster. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- You soaked moong at 7 o'clock in the evening. You drained it at 7 o'clock the next morning. The first roots showed at 7 o'clock that evening. How long did the seeds soak? How long from soaking to the first roots? **They soaked for 12 hours. From soaking to the first roots took 24 hours.**. From 7 in the evening to 7 in the morning is 12 hours. From 7 in the morning to 7 in the evening is another 12 hours. 12 + 12 = 24 hours, which is one whole day. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
