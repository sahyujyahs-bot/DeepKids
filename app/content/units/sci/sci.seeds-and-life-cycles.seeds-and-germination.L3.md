# Seeds: what a seed needs to sprout
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.seeds-and-germination.L3`

**Summary.** A dry moong seed can sit in a jar for a year without changing. On a wet cloth in a warm room, they grow a white root in a day or so. Inside every seed is a tiny living plant with their own store of food. They sprout when they get water, warmth and air, even in the dark and without soil. Your child finds the baby plant in a soaked bean, then tests five cups of moong to find out what wakes a seed.

**Intro.** *Hold a dry moong seed in your hand. They do not move, eat or grow. Are they alive? How could you find out?* Look in your kitchen. There are jars full of seeds: moong, chana, rajma, methi, rai and jeera. They can sit in a jar for a whole year and never change. But put a few moong seeds on a wet cloth tonight. If the room is warm, by tomorrow evening most of them will have a little white root. What changed? What was the seed waiting for?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the parts of a seed: the seed coat, the baby plant and their food store.
1. I can say what a seed needs to sprout: water, warmth and air. I can say that most seeds do not need light or soil to start.
2. I can run a fair test with seeds, changing only one thing, and count how many seeds sprout.
3. I can show my results in a table and a bar chart, and work out how long sprouting took.
4. I can explain that a sprouting seed breathes, uses up their stored food and makes a little warmth.
5. I can describe how a seed sprouts: they drink and swell, the root comes out first and grows down, and then the shoot grows up.
6. I can say what scientists agree on about seeds, and what they still do not know, such as how long a seed can stay alive.

## Terms
- **Seed**: A baby plant packed inside a coat, with a store of food to start them off. Flowering plants and cone trees, like pines, make seeds so new plants can grow. *e.g.* Moong, chana, rajma, rice grains, rai and the big seed inside a mango stone are all seeds.
- **Seed coat**: The tough outer skin of a seed. It keeps the baby plant safe and dry until it is time to grow. *e.g.* The green skin on a whole moong seed is their seed coat.
- **Baby plant (embryo)**: The tiny living plant inside a seed. They already have a little root and a little shoot. Scientists call this the embryo. *e.g.* Split a soaked peanut. The small pointed part at one end is the baby plant.
- **Food store**: The part of a seed that holds food for the baby plant. The baby plant lives on it until their first green leaves can make food from sunlight. *e.g.* The two big halves of a chana are their food store. That is also why chana feeds us.
- **Seed leaves (cotyledons)**: The first leaves of a baby plant, already inside the seed. In beans and chana, they are thick and full of food. Scientists call them cotyledons. *e.g.* When a rajma bean sprouts, their two fat seed halves lift out of the soil and turn green. Those are their seed leaves. A chana's seed leaves stay under the soil.
- **Germination**: A seed waking up and starting to grow. It is the scientists' word for sprouting. *e.g.* Moong left on a wet cloth for two days germinates. Each seed grows a little white root.
- **Sprout**: A seed that has just started to grow, or the little root and shoot that come out of them. *e.g.* Moong sprouts in a salad, or in usal for breakfast.
- **Dormant**: Alive, but resting. A dormant seed uses almost no energy while they wait for the right conditions. *e.g.* Rajma in a dry jar is dormant. They can wait for a year or more.
- **Root**: The part of a plant that grows down into the soil. It holds the plant in place and drinks water. It is the first thing to come out of a sprouting seed. *e.g.* The white tail on a moong sprout is their first root.
- **Shoot**: The part of a young plant that grows up towards the light. It becomes the stem and leaves. *e.g.* After the root, a green shoot comes out of a bean and grows upwards.
- **Swell**: To get bigger by soaking up water. Seeds swell when they are soaked. *e.g.* A cup of dry chana, soaked overnight, swells to fill about two cups.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air that living things need. Animals and plants use it to get energy from their food. Seeds need it too. *e.g.* You breathe in oxygen all day. A sprouting seed takes in oxygen too, though you cannot see it happen.
- **Breathing (respiration)**: How living things use oxygen to get energy from food. It makes a little warmth, and it gives out a gas called carbon dioxide. Scientists call it respiration. *e.g.* A heap of sprouting seeds gets warm because millions of their cells are breathing.
- **Starch**: A kind of stored food made by plants. It is what makes rice, wheat and potatoes filling. *e.g.* Most of a rice grain is starch. The baby rice plant uses it to start growing.
- **Enzyme**: A helper made by living cells that speeds up a change. Some enzymes turn starch into sugar. *e.g.* Sprouted grains taste a little sweet, because enzymes have turned some starch into sugar.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To test if seeds need water, give one cup water and one cup none. Keep the cups in the same place, with the same seeds.
- **Gravity**: The force that pulls things towards the Earth. *e.g.* Gravity makes a dropped ball fall. Roots grow in the direction gravity pulls.
- **Seed bank**: A safe store of many kinds of seeds, kept dry and cold so they stay alive for many years. *e.g.* Rahibai Popere, a farmer in Maharashtra, keeps a seed bank of old local crops. The Svalbard vault in Norway keeps seeds inside a frozen mountain.

## Explanations
### What a seed is · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed is a baby plant inside a protective coat, packed with a store of food. They are made by a flowering plant, or by a cone-bearing tree like a pine.

Every seed has three main parts. The seed coat is the tough skin on the outside. Inside it is the baby plant, which scientists call the embryo. They already have a tiny root and a tiny shoot. The rest of the seed is the food store. It feeds the baby plant until their first green leaves can make food from sunlight.
- e.g. A soaked rajma bean: peel off the red coat, open the two halves, and find the tiny baby plant at one edge.
- e.g. A peanut: the little pointed tip between the two halves is the baby plant.
- e.g. A mango stone: the hard shell is the fruit's stone, and the seed with their coat is inside it.
### Two kinds of food store: beans and grains · *defined*
**Definition:** In beans, chana and peanuts, the food is stored inside two thick seed leaves. In rice, wheat and maize, the food is stored in a separate part around the baby plant, and there is only one thin seed leaf.

Seed leaves are the first leaves of the baby plant, already inside the seed. Scientists call them cotyledons. A bean has two seed leaves. They are fat and full of food, so a bean splits neatly into two halves. A grain of rice or maize has only one thin seed leaf. Their food is kept in a separate store beside the baby plant. Scientists call that store the endosperm. When you eat a chapati or a bowl of rice, you are mostly eating the food store that a grain made for their baby plant.
- e.g. Chana and moong split into two halves. They have two seed leaves.
- e.g. A soaked maize kernel does not split in two. Their baby plant is a small pale patch on one side.
- e.g. Popcorn pops because water in the starchy food store turns to steam and bursts it open.
### What wakes a seed: water, warmth and air · *defined*
**Definition:** Germination is a seed waking up and starting to grow. Most seeds need three things to germinate: water, warmth and air.

Water comes first. It softens the coat and lets the cells inside start working again. Warmth matters because the changes inside a seed happen faster when it is warm. In a cold room, moong still sprouts, but slowly. In a fridge, they hardly start. Air matters because the seed takes in oxygen, a gas in the air, to get energy from their food. Take away any one of the three, and most seeds do not sprout. In the five-cup test, the dry cup, the fridge cup and the cup under water do badly. The damp cup on the shelf sprouts, and the damp cup in the light does no better. The chart shows typical made-up results.
- e.g. Moong in a dry jar never sprouts. Moong on a wet cloth sprouts in a day or two.
- e.g. Moong on a wet cloth in the fridge sprouts very slowly, or not at all, until they are warmed up.
- e.g. Moong covered with water that was boiled and cooled, under a layer of oil, gets very little air. Only a few sprout.
### Most seeds do not need light or soil to start · *defined*
**Definition:** Most seeds can sprout in the dark, with no soil at all. They need light and soil only later, once the seedling must make their own food.

A seed carries their own food. So at the start they do not need sunlight to make food, and they do not need anything from the soil. That is why moong sprouts on a wet cloth inside a closed tiffin. Seeds in the ground sprout in the dark too. Light becomes important later, when the shoot comes up and their green leaves start making food. There are a few exceptions. Some small seeds, like some kinds of lettuce, need light to wake. In 1952, a team in the United States, including Harry Borthwick, Sterling Hendricks and Vivian Toole, showed that red light wakes these lettuce seeds, and a deeper red light, called far-red, stops them again. A small seed has only a little stored food. Light tells them that they are near the surface, so their food will not run out before the shoot reaches the light.
- e.g. Moong sprouts in a dark cupboard just as well as in a bright room.
- e.g. Sprouts sold in markets are grown on wet sacks or trays, with no soil.
- e.g. Some tiny seeds, like some kinds of lettuce, sprout only if light reaches them.
### First the seed drinks: swelling · *defined*
**Definition:** A dry seed soaks up water and swells. This is the first step of germination.

A dry seed holds very little water, often only about one part in ten. When they get wet, water rushes in. Most enters through a tiny hole next to the scar where the seed was joined to their pod. The scar is called the hilum. The seed swells to about twice their weight, and their coat goes wrinkly and then splits. Swelling seeds push very hard. A jar packed with dry chana and water can push its lid off. Once the cells inside are wet again, they switch back on and start to work.
- e.g. Weigh 50 grams of dry chana. Soak them overnight. They now weigh about 100 grams.
- e.g. Soaked rajma has a wrinkly coat for a few hours, then it goes smooth and tight.
- e.g. Look at a soaked bean. The pale scar on their side, the hilum, is where they were joined to their pod.
### A waking seed breathes and makes warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** A sprouting seed breathes. They take in oxygen, use it to get energy from their stored food, and gives out carbon dioxide and a little heat. Scientists call this respiration.

You breathe to get energy from your food. A seed does the same, without lungs. Every tiny cell in them takes in oxygen from the air around it. A dry seed breathes so slowly you could never notice. Once they are wet, they breathe many times faster. One sprouting seed makes too little heat to feel. But a big heap of sprouts, wrapped up, gets warm to the touch. A flask of live sprouts gets a few degrees warmer than a flask of boiled ones. That is also why a seed under water does badly. Water holds only a little oxygen, so the seed cannot breathe well.
- e.g. A cloth bundle of sprouting moong feels warm in the middle.
- e.g. Farmers who sprout paddy seed in wet sacks before sowing can feel the heap warm up.
- e.g. Seeds kept under water for too long go sour and slimy. They could not breathe, and germs grew on them.
### The food store turns sweet · *defined*
**Definition:** As a seed sprouts, their cells make enzymes. These helpers break the stored food, such as starch, into sugar that the baby plant can use.

Starch is stored food made of long chains of sugar. It keeps well, but the baby plant cannot use it straight away. When the seed wakes, enzymes cut the long chains into sugar. The sugar travels to the root and shoot, and feeds them as they grow. In grains like barley, the baby plant even sends a chemical message to the food store, telling it to start. In 1960, Leslie Paleg in Australia and Hiroshi Yomo in Japan each found this message. It is a plant hormone called gibberellin. This is also why sprouted grains taste a little sweet. People in India make ragi malt from sprouted ragi.
- e.g. Chew a two-day-old moong sprout slowly. Many people find it tastes a little sweeter than a soaked seed that has not sprouted.
- e.g. Sprouted ragi is dried and ground to make ragi malt.
- e.g. Malt for some sweets and drinks is made from sprouted barley.
### Root first, then shoot · *defined*
**Definition:** In most seeds the root comes out first and grows downwards. The shoot comes out next and grows upwards.

The root comes first so the seedling can hold on and drink. Whichever way up a seed lies, their root turns to grow down. Root tips feel gravity, the pull towards the Earth. Inside the very tip are cells holding tiny heavy grains of starch. The grains sink to the bottom of each cell, like sand in a bottle. So the root can tell which way is down. The shoot grows the other way, up towards the light. In beans, the shoot comes up bent like a hook. The hook protects the soft tip as it pushes through the soil.
- e.g. Put soaked chana in a jar against wet paper, some upside down. Every root bends to grow down.
- e.g. Turn the jar on its side after the roots appear. In a day, the root tips bend down again.
- e.g. A bean shoot pushes up through the soil bent like a hook, then straightens in the light.
### Rice: the seed that can start under water · *defined*
**Definition:** Rice is unusual. Their seeds can start to sprout even under water, with very little oxygen.

Most seeds drown under water. Rice comes from wet, flooded places, and their seeds have a trick. Under water, a rice seed sends up their first shoot quickly, before their root. The shoot grows up like a snorkel until its tip reaches the air. Then air can travel down it, and the root can start. This helps rice grow in flooded fields. Scientists are still studying exactly how rice does this, so they can help farmers sow rice straight into wet fields.
- e.g. Moong seeds under water mostly fail. Paddy, rice still in the husk, often sends up a shoot under water. Kitchen rice cannot do this, because milling has removed their baby plant.
- e.g. In many paddy fields in India, rice grows with their feet in water.
### How long can a seed wait? · *idea*
A dry, cool seed can stay alive for a long time, but nobody knows the longest. In 1879, a scientist in the United States, William James Beal, buried bottles of seeds in sand. Every 20 years or so, someone digs one up and plants the seeds. In 2021, more than 140 years later, some seeds still sprouted. Sacred lotus seeds about 1,300 years old have sprouted in China. In 2005, Elaine Solowey grew a date palm in Israel from a seed about 2,000 years old. The seed came from the ruins of an ancient fort called Masada. She worked with a doctor, Sarah Sallon, and the tree was named Methuselah. Most seeds do not last nearly this long. Scientists are still finding out what lets some seeds wait for centuries.
- e.g. Moong from the kitchen usually sprouts well for a year or two, then fewer seeds sprout each year.
- e.g. Seeds last much longer when they are kept dry and cold. That is why seed banks keep them in freezers.
- **Most seed scientists:** Old seeds slowly get damaged inside, a little more each year. Dry and cold slow this down. Every kind of seed has their own limit.
- **Some scientists:** A few seeds may be far better at repairing themselves than we know. The real limit could be thousands of years. Nobody has had time to test it.
### Is a dry seed alive? · *debated*
A dry seed does not grow, move or seem to eat. They breathe so slowly that it is very hard to measure. Yet add water, and they wake up. So is a dry seed alive the whole time, or do they come back to life? Scientists and thinkers answer this in different ways. The question matters, because it tests what we mean by 'alive'.
- e.g. A moong seed in a jar for a year, and the same seed sprouting two days after soaking.
- e.g. Tardigrades, tiny water animals about half a millimetre long, can dry out completely and wake up again years later when they get wet.
- **Most biologists:** A dry seed is alive, but dormant. Their cells are still whole and still working, very, very slowly. They are like a person in a very deep sleep.
- **Some scientists who study drying out:** In a very dry seed, life almost stops. They call this 'hidden life'. The seed is not doing much, but they keep everything they need to start again.
- **Some philosophers:** The seed shows that 'alive' and 'not alive' may not be two neat boxes. There may be states in between, and our words do not fit them well.
### Deep down: a seed is stored sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** The food in a seed was made by their parent plant from air, water and sunlight. When the seed sprouts and breathes, they use that stored sunlight energy and sends the carbon back into the air.

The parent plant's leaves took in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil. Using the energy of sunlight, they built sugar and starch. The plant packed some of this food into each seed. When the seed sprouts, they breathe: their cells join the stored food with oxygen. That releases the energy that sunlight first put into the food, and the carbon goes back into the air as carbon dioxide. So a sprout in the dark uses up their food as they grow. Dried out, they weigh less than the seed they came from. They must reach the light, and start making new food, before their store runs out.
- e.g. Scientists sprout seeds in the dark for a week, then dry them fully in an oven. They weigh less than the same number of dry seeds that were never sprouted.
- e.g. The energy in the chana you eat also came from sunlight, stored by the chana plant.
### How a seed knows when to wake · *idea*
Some seeds wake as soon as they are wet. Others wait for a sign that the time is right. Some wait for a long cold winter. Some wait until a fire has passed. In 2004, Gavin Flematti and his team in Australia found the chemical in smoke that wakes many such seeds. Some seeds have a coat so hard that it must be scratched, or pass through a bird's belly, before water can get in. Scientists understand parts of how seeds sense these signs. How a seed decides, using many signs at once, is still being worked out.
- e.g. Apple seeds, and the seeds of many mountain wildflowers, sprout only after weeks of cold.
- e.g. Some plants of Australia and South Africa sprout in great numbers after a bush fire.
- e.g. Gardeners rub very hard seeds with sandpaper, or nick them, so that water can get in.
- **Most seed scientists:** A seed has sensors for water, warmth, light and some chemicals. Their hormones add up these signals, a bit like a set of switches.
- **Some plant scientists:** This adding up of signals is a simple kind of decision-making without a brain. Others say 'decision' is the wrong word for chemistry. They still argue about it.
### Seeds in space and on the Moon · *defined*
**Definition:** Seeds have travelled into space and come back able to sprout. Seeds have even sprouted on the Moon, inside a sealed box.

In 1971, an astronaut called Stuart Roosa carried about 500 tree seeds around the Moon on the Apollo 14 mission. Back on Earth, many were sprouted and planted. They grew into normal trees, called Moon trees. In January 2019, China's Chang'e 4 lander carried cotton seeds to the far side of the Moon in a sealed box with water, air and soil. A cotton seed sprouted there, the first seed to sprout on the Moon. When the long, freezing lunar night came, they died. They had water and air, but not enough warmth. Space farmers will need all three.
- e.g. Moon trees from the Apollo 14 seeds still grow in parts of the United States.
- e.g. The cotton seedling on the Moon died when the temperature fell far below freezing.

## Mostly agreed
- A seed holds a living baby plant, with a coat and a store of food. *How we know:* Split any soaked bean and you can see the baby plant with a hand lens. Scientists have looked at seeds under microscopes for more than 300 years.
- Most seeds need water, warmth and air to sprout, and do not need light or soil to start. *How we know:* Fair tests like the five-cup test give the same results in classrooms and laboratories all over the world.
- A sprouting seed breathes. They use oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide and heat. *How we know:* A flask of sprouting seeds warms up, while boiled seeds do not. Scientists can measure the oxygen used and the carbon dioxide given out.
- The root comes out first and grows down, and the shoot grows up. *How we know:* Seeds placed any way up in a jar send their roots down. Turn the jar, and the root tips turn down again. In space, where the pull is tiny, roots wander in many directions.
- Dry, cool seeds can stay alive for years, and some for centuries. *How we know:* Seeds from Beal's bottles sprouted after more than 140 years. A date palm seed about 2,000 years old was sprouted in 2005, and their age was checked by carbon dating.

## How we found out
**Thomas Andrew Knight in England (1806); Charles and Francis Darwin in England (1880); Gottlieb Haberlandt and Bohumil Němec (1900), The spinning wheel in 1806; the root-tip test in 1880; the starch grains suggested in 1900.** People once thought a root grows down simply because it is looking for soil and water. In 1806, an English gardener and scientist, Thomas Andrew Knight, tested this. He fixed sprouting bean seeds around the rim of a wheel. Water from a stream kept the wheel spinning, day and night. A spinning wheel pushes things outwards, like the push you feel on a fast ride at a mela. If roots only followed water and soil, the spinning would not change them. Instead, every root grew outwards, in the direction of the push, and every shoot grew inwards. So a root grows the way a force pulls on it. On Earth, that force is gravity. In 1880, Charles Darwin and his son Francis cut the very tip off some growing roots. Those roots kept growing, but they no longer turned downwards. So the tip is the part that senses which way is down. In 1900, two scientists, Gottlieb Haberlandt and Bohumil Němec, each suggested how: tiny heavy grains of starch settle at the bottom of the root-tip cells.  
*What people believed before:* That roots grow down only because they are looking for soil and water. On Knight's spinning wheel, the roots grew outwards, the way the push went, even though the water and soil were the same all round.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Seeds need sunlight to sprout.** (Many children and grown-ups. It is a very common guess.; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants need light to grow well. And seeds in a garden usually sprout where the sun shines.  
  Against: Moong sprouts in a closed dark tiffin. Seeds in the ground sprout in the dark. A seed carries their own food, so they need light only once their leaves come up. A few small seeds, like some lettuce, are exceptions.
- **Seeds need soil to sprout.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: We plant seeds in soil, and that is where they grow in nature.  
  Against: Seeds sprout on wet cloth, cotton or paper. Soil holds water and air and keeps the seed dark and safe. It also feeds the plant later. But the seed does not need it to start.
- **The more water a seed gets, the better they sprout.** (Many new gardeners; *rejected*)  
  For: Water is the first thing a seed needs, and dry seeds never sprout.  
  Against: A seed under water cannot get enough air to breathe. In the five-cup test, the seeds under water do badly, and some go sour. Damp is just right. Rice is the famous exception.
- **A dry seed is dead until water brings them to life.** (A common way of thinking, because dry seeds do nothing we can see; *rejected*)  
  For: A dry seed does not grow or move, and they seem to use no food.  
  Against: A dry seed is still breathing, very slowly. Boiled seeds never sprout, however much water they get. So something in the dry seed was alive all along.
- **A big seed grows into a big plant.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Big seeds carry more food, so their seedlings often start bigger.  
  Against: A banyan or peepal seed is no bigger than a grain of rai, yet they grow into a giant tree. A big rajma grows into a small plant. A seed is only the start. The plant builds most of their body from air and water, using the energy of sunlight, with a little from the soil.
- **Seeds sprout better if you sow them at the right phase of the Moon.** (Some farmers and gardeners around the world, following old planting calendars; *minority*)  
  For: The custom is very old. Some people believe the Moon pulls water in the soil, like it pulls the sea.  
  Against: The Moon's pull on a little patch of soil is far too tiny to move its water. Careful tests have not found a clear effect. Many farmers still follow such calendars, and they may help in other ways, like keeping planting on time.

## Roots
We have been learning how a seed wakes up and lives on their stored food. Now we ask what a sprout tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A seed sprouts in the dark, living on the food their parent packed inside them. Their root and shoot are built from that food, and breathing uses up more of it. Dried out, a sprout grown in the dark weighs less than their seed did. So a seedling first grows by spending their store, and must reach light before the store runs out.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think growing always means gaining, getting heavier by taking more in. A seedling in the dark grows by spending what they carry, and only later start to earn their own food from sunlight.  
*Evidence:* In the five-cup test, moong sprouted in a dark covered cup as well as in the light, so they were living on their own food. Inside a soaked bean, almost all of the seed is that food store. The flask of live sprouts warmed up because the sprouts were breathing, using up their food. Scientists who dry sprouts grown in the dark find they weigh less than the seeds did.

- *who am i:* Before you could find food yourself, you grew on food your mother's body gave you. A seed starts the same way, on food from their parent.
- *what is life:* A seed can rest for years, using almost none of their food. Once they wake, they use their food fast to reach the light.
- *what is this world:* A sprout grows using the energy of sunlight that fell on their parent's leaves months ago. The Sun's energy is passed on from plant to plant.

A seed's food was made by their parent from air, water and sunlight. The sprout breathes that food back into the air as they grow. Then their own green leaves catch new sunlight and make food for the seeds they will one day pack. So the same sunlight and the same carbon keep passing from plant to air to plant. One awareness runs through this whole chain, and the seed is aware in their own way: their root senses which way is down and chooses where to grow.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** A father, Uddalaka, asks his son Shvetaketu to split a banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing at all.' The father says: from this finest thing that you cannot see, the great banyan tree grows. That fine essence is in everything. And you are that. (When you split a bean, the baby plant is so small you can hardly see them, yet a whole plant grows from them. Uddalaka used a seed to show that what we cannot see can hold something great. A sprout grows big from a tiny start, fed by what was packed inside them.)
- **Mencius, Book 2A, chapter 2:** A farmer from the state of Song was worried his young plants were growing too slowly. So he pulled on each one to help them grow. When his son ran to look, the plants had all withered. (You cannot make a seed sprout by pushing them. You can only give them water, warmth and air, and wait. Growing has its own time.)
- Science (settled): Seedlings grown in the dark get longer but lose dry weight, because breathing uses up their stored food. Plant scientists have measured this for well over a hundred years.
- Science (open): A dry seed's cells are not dead. They are still whole, with their repair tools ready, and breathe at a tiny rate. Exactly how close to 'stopped' life can get, and still restart, is being studied.
- Other view, Many biologists: A seed's stored food is fuel, a bit like the fuel in a scooter's tank. Using it up is chemistry and timing. They see no need for a shared awareness to explain it, and that is an honest view.
- Practice, Be a waiting seed (3 min): Let's sit and curl up small, like a seed. Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly. Now breathe out, slowly and softly. A sprout breathes out too, and each breath uses a little of their stored food. Hold your hand in front of your mouth. Breathe out once more and feel the warm air on your hand. Where did the food you are using right now come from?
- Ask: A sprout in the dark gets longer but lighter. Are they growing, using themselves up, or both?
- Ask: Is a sprout the same plant as the seed they came from, or a new one?
- Ask: A sprout's food was made from sunlight that fell on their parent. Whose sunlight is your food made of?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A dry seed sits in a jar for a year, doing nothing. Are they alive the whole time? How could you prove it? (hint: What happens to a boiled seed when you soak them? What happens to an unboiled one?)
- *deep:* Why does a seed carry their own food, when a grown plant makes food from sunlight? (hint: Where is a seed when they first wake up? Can they see the Sun from there?)
- *deep:* How does a root know which way is down, even in the dark? (hint: Think about sand in a bottle. Which way does it settle when you turn the bottle?)
- *deep:* A sprouting seed breathes, like you. What else do you and a seed have in common? (hint: Think about food, water, warmth and growing.)
- *big:* When does a plant's life begin? When the seed sprouts, when the seed was made, or before that? (hint: Who made the baby plant inside the seed, and who packed their food store?)
- *big:* Some seeds sprout as soon as they are wet. Others wait for a cold winter or a fire. Why might it help a seed to wait? (hint: What would happen to a seedling that sprouted just before a frost, or in a dry month?)
- *big:* During a terrible war, scientists guarding a seed bank in Leningrad chose to go hungry rather than eat the seeds. Why would anyone do that? (hint: Who were the seeds for? How many people could they feed one day?)

## Story: The Warm Bundle
*Noor, her family and the sprouts cart are made up. The science is real: soaked seeds need air, a heap of sprouting seeds gets warm because the seeds are breathing, and seeds kept under water too long go sour.*

Every morning at six, Noor's mother pushed a blue handcart to the corner outside Egmore station in Chennai. On the cart was a big steel bowl of sprouts. There were green moong with white tails, chopped onion, tomato, lemon and a pinch of red chilli. Office workers stopped on their way to the train. 'One plate, Ammi,' they said, and Ammi spooned sprouts into a leaf bowl. Two hundred plates on a good day. On a bad day, fewer.

Noor was nine, and she knew how the sprouts were made, or she thought she did. At night, Ammi poured moong into the big dabba and covered them with water. In the morning she drained them. She tipped the fat, wet seeds into an old white cloth and tied it loosely, like a bundle for a journey. Then she hung it from a nail on the window bars. By the next morning, every seed had a tail.

'Why the nail?' Noor once asked. 'Why not leave them in the dabba?'

'Because they are getting ready to travel,' said Ammi, which was not really an answer. Ammi's answers were often like that.

Then came the week of Khala's wedding in Vellore. Ammi had to go for two days. 'Faiz will run the cart on Sunday,' she said. Faiz was thirteen and thought he was eighteen. 'And Noor will make the sprouts. Soak them tonight. Hang them tomorrow. You have seen me do it a thousand times.'

Noor nodded. A thousand times was a lot.

That night she soaked the moong in the dabba, just as Ammi did. But in the morning, before she could drain them, she heard a scratching under the floorboards. Last month, a rat had chewed through Ammi's bundle on the nail. Half the sprouts had been ruined. Ammi had cried a little, when she thought nobody was looking.

Not this time, Noor decided. She left the moong in their water. She pressed the steel lid down hard. Then she put the heavy grinding stone on top. No rat could lift that. Pleased, she went to school.

When she came home, the room smelled strange. She lifted the stone and the lid. The water had gone cloudy, and a grey froth floated on top. The smell was sour, like milk left out in the heat. The seeds were fat and soft, but not one of them had a tail.

'You killed them,' said Faiz, holding his nose.

'I did not,' said Noor. But her stomach went cold. There was no money to buy more moong. The cart would sell nothing on Sunday, and Ammi would come home to an empty tin.

She sat down on the floor beside the dabba. She tried to remember everything Ammi did, every single step. Soak. Drain. Tie loosely. Hang. And one more thing, a thing she had nearly forgotten. Once, last winter, Ammi had let her hold the bundle before dawn. It had been warm. Not warm from the stove, which was cold. Warm from inside, like a sleeping kitten.

'Why is it warm?' she had asked.

'Because they are breathing,' Ammi had said. 'Everything that wakes up has to breathe.'

Noor looked at the dabba with its lid and its stone and its water. Nothing could get in, not a rat, and not a breath of air either. She thought of the time she had dipped her face in a bucket of water. She could only hold her breath for a few seconds.

'They were drowning,' she said.

She drained the cloudy water at once. She rinsed the seeds under the tap, once, twice, five times, until the sour smell had almost gone. She picked out the ones that were mushy and broken. There were a lot of them. Then she spread the rest on the white cloth and tied it loosely, the way Ammi did, with room for air between the seeds. She hung it from the nail on the window bars, where the sea breeze came in.

'What about the rat?' said Faiz.

Noor fetched Ammi's steel strainer. She hung it upside down on the string above the bundle, like an umbrella. A rat that climbed down the string would slide right off. Faiz looked at it for a long time. Then he said, 'Not bad.'

Noor could not sleep. Twice in the night she got up and touched the bundle. It was cool and damp. At four in the morning she touched it again. And this time, in the very middle, it was warm. Not much. Just a little, like a hand that has been holding another hand.

When the first light came, she untied the cloth. Most of the seeds had split their green coats. Out of each one curled a tiny white tail, thin as a comma in her school book. Not every seed. About two of every three. But that was enough.

On Sunday, Faiz pushed the blue cart to the corner outside the station. Noor chopped the onions and squeezed the lemons. They sold one hundred and thirty plates. It was not two hundred. Faiz said it was a record for a cart run by children, and nobody could prove that it wasn't.

That night, Ammi called from Vellore. Music and laughter roared behind her. 'How were the sprouts?' she shouted.

'They were breathing,' said Noor.

There was a little silence on the line. Then Ammi laughed, the way she laughed when Noor said something she had not expected. 'Of course they were,' she said. 'Did you hang them on the nail?'

'With an umbrella,' said Noor. And she went to the window and laid her hand on the new bundle, soaked and tied and hanging in the dark, already beginning to wake.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the seeds did not fail because they were soaked wrongly. They failed because they could not breathe. A waking seed needs air as well as water, and seeds left sealed under water run out of oxygen and go sour. The warmth Noor remembered was the clue: a heap of sprouting seeds gets warm because millions of cells are breathing, using oxygen to get energy from their stored food. Water, warmth and air together are what wake a seed. The story also quietly shows how much a child can learn just by watching a parent's ordinary work closely.

- Ask: Noor shut the moong under a heavy lid to keep the rat out. Why did that stop them sprouting?
- Ask: The bundle on the nail felt warm in the night. Where did the warmth come from?
- Ask: Why do you think Ammi always tied the cloth loosely, not tightly?
- Ask: Noor remembered something Ammi did without explaining it. What have you learnt just by watching someone at home?
- Ask: Faiz said 'Not bad' about the strainer umbrella. How did Noor solve two problems at once?

### Seed Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (draw) Draw a seed opened in half. Label the seed coat, the baby plant and the food store.  
   **Answer:** A bean shape split open, with the coat peeling off, two large halves labelled 'food store' and a tiny plant at one edge labelled 'baby plant'.. *Why:* Every seed has these three parts.
2. (fill) The tough outer skin of a seed is called the seed ____.  
   **Answer:** ['coat']. *Why:* The seed coat protects the baby plant and keeps them dry until it is time to grow.
3. (mcq) You split a soaked bean in two. What is the tiny thing at one edge, with a little root and two small leaves?  
   Options: A worm / The food store / The baby plant / A bit of the coat  
   **Answer:** The baby plant. *Why:* That is the baby plant, which scientists call the embryo. They were already inside before you soaked the seed. Picking 'food store' is close, but the food store is the two big halves around them.
4. (mcq) In the five-cup test, you put 10 moong seeds in each of 5 cups: dry, damp, damp in the fridge, under water with oil on top, and damp in the light. In which cup did the most seeds sprout?  
   Options: Damp and warm / Dry / In the fridge / Under water with oil  
   **Answer:** Damp and warm. *Why:* The damp, warm cup had water, warmth and air together. Picking 'under water' usually means thinking more water is always better. Those seeds could not get enough air.
5. (write) Many people think seeds need sunlight to sprout, because plants need light. Use the five-cup test to say whether this is right.  
   **Answer:** It is not right for moong. The damp cup in the dark sprouted just as well as the damp cup in the light. A seed carries their own food, so they do not need light to start. The seedling needs light later, once the food runs out.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the two damp cups as evidence: they were the same except for light, and both sprouted.
6. (truefalse) Seeds need soil before they can sprout.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Seeds sprout on wet cotton, cloth or paper. Soil helps later, by holding water and the plant's roots.
7. (mcq) Which part comes out of a sprouting seed first?  
   Options: The leaves / The flower / The fruit / The root  
   **Answer:** The root. *Why:* The root comes out first, so the seedling can hold on and drink. Picking 'leaves' is common, because leaves are what we see first above the soil.
8. (fill) A seed waking up and starting to grow is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['germination', 'sprouting']. *Why:* Germination is the scientists' word for a seed sprouting.
9. (sort) Sort these. Could they still sprout, or not?  
   **Answer:** whole moong → Could sprout; split moong dal → Could not sprout; raw peanut → Could sprout; roasted peanut → Could not sprout; rajma → Could sprout; popped popcorn → Could not sprout. *Why:* A seed can sprout only if their baby plant is whole and alive. Splitting the dal loses the baby plant. Roasting and popping kill them.
10. (perspective) In the story, a rat keeps chewing the bundle of sprouting moong. Noor, her mother and her brother each have a plan.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses what seeds need: water, warmth and air. Noor's first plan keeps the rat out, but the seeds under water cannot breathe, so they go sour. Ammi's cloth gives the most air, but a rat can reach it. Faiz's tin keeps the rat out and lets some air in, if the seeds are drained first. A strong answer gives each plan its due.. *Why:* There is a real trade-off between keeping a rat out and letting air in. In the story, Noor solved both by hanging a strainer above the bundle, like an umbrella.
11. (mcq) Why does a bean seed have two fat halves full of food?  
   Options: To make the seed heavy so they don't blow away / To feed the baby plant until their leaves can make food / So animals can eat them  
   **Answer:** To feed the baby plant until their leaves can make food. *Why:* The food store feeds the baby plant while they are still underground, with no light. Animals do eat seeds, but that is not why the seed has food. The food is for the baby plant.
12. (write) Think of your own five-cup test. Which cup surprised you most? Say what you guessed, what you saw, and what it shows.  
   **Answer:** Any honest answer with all three parts. For example: I guessed the cup under water would sprout best, because it had the most water. But hardly any seeds sprouted there. 13. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, as a bean sprouts.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* First the seed drinks and swells, which splits the coat. The root comes first, then the shoot. Leaves open last, once the shoot reaches the light.
14. (mcq) Why do we put 10 seeds in each cup, not just 1?  
   Options: Because one seed might be dead, and 10 give a fairer answer / Because seeds get lonely / Because 10 is a lucky number  
   **Answer:** Because one seed might be dead, and 10 give a fairer answer. *Why:* Some seeds don't sprout even when everything is right. If we used only one, a single dead seed could fool us. With 10, one dead seed matters much less.
15. (write) A girl sows a moong seed in a pot of dry soil on a sunny windowsill. She never waters the pot. Will the seed sprout? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. The seed has warmth and air, and sunlight, but no water. Water is what wakes a seed up. Sunlight does not help a seed start, because the seed has their own food.. *Why:* A strong answer names the missing need, water, and says that the sun does not make up for it.
16. (truefalse) A seed that swells up in water must be alive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A boiled seed swells up in water too, like a sponge, but never sprouts. Swelling is only the seed soaking up water. Sprouting a root shows that a seed is alive.

   **Answer:** ['oxygen']. *Why:* Seeds, like us, use oxygen from the air to get energy from food.

   Options: 50 times / 100 times / They weighed the same / 2 times  
   **Answer:** 2 times. *Why:* 100 is 2 lots of 50. So the soaked chana was 2 times as heavy. The extra 50 grams is water inside the seeds.
4. (truefalse) A jar packed with dry chana and water can push its lid off overnight.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Swelling seeds push very hard as they soak up water. That is why we use a plastic cup, never glass.
5. (truefalse) Moong seeds that hardly sprouted in the fridge are dead.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Cold slows the changes inside a seed, but usually does not kill them. Move the fridge cup to a warm kitchen shelf, and most of the seeds sprout within a day or two.
6. (match) Match each cup in the five-cup test to what it was missing.  
   **Answer:** Dry cup → water; Fridge cup → warmth; Under water with oil → air; Damp cup in the light → nothing missing, it had extra light. *Why:* Each cup was different from the damp, warm cup in just one way. That way we can tell what each thing does.
7. (mcq) A root always grows down, even if the seed is upside down. How does the root know which way is down?  
   Options: It can see the ground / Its tip feels gravity, using tiny heavy grains that settle at the bottom of its cells / It follows the smell of soil / It doesn't know, it just grows any way  
   **Answer:** Its tip feels gravity, using tiny heavy grains that settle at the bottom of its cells. *Why:* Cells in the root tip hold tiny heavy grains of starch. They sink to the bottom, like sand in a bottle, so the root can tell which way is down. Picking 'see' is not right. A root has no eyes, and it turns down even in a dark jar.
8. (mcq) Why do sprouts taste a little sweet?  
   Options: Someone added sugar / The water was sweet / The seed has turned some of their starch into sugar  
   **Answer:** The seed has turned some of their starch into sugar. *Why:* As a seed sprouts, enzymes, helpers made by their cells, cut starch into sugar the baby plant can use. That's why ragi malt is made from sprouted ragi.
9. (mcq) Rice seeds are unusual. What can they do that most seeds can't?  
   Options: Sprout without any water / Start to sprout under water, sending a shoot up like a snorkel / Sprout in a freezer  
   **Answer:** Start to sprout under water, sending a shoot up like a snorkel. *Why:* Rice comes from wet places. Under water, their first shoot grows up to the air like a snorkel, so air can reach the rest of the seedling. Most seeds would drown.
10. (write) Arif, a boy in Class 3, feels his bundle of sprouting moong getting warm. He says, 'They must be rotting.' How could you test whether he is right?  
   **Answer:** Set up two heaps the same way: one of live sprouting moong and one of boiled moong. Put a thermometer in each. For the first day, the live heap gets warm and the boiled heap stays cool. The live sprouts also smell fresh and grow tails. So the warmth comes from seeds breathing, not from rotting.. *Why:* A strong answer plans a fair test with boiled seeds, so the only difference is whether the seeds are alive.
11. (mcq) A girl sows one moong seed 1 cm deep in a tall bucket of soil, and another 30 cm deep. Which one is more likely to reach the light?  
   Options: The shallow seed, because the deep seed's food store may run out before their shoot reaches the light / The deep seed, because they have more soil around them / Both the same  
   **Answer:** The shallow seed, because the deep seed's food store may run out before their shoot reaches the light. *Why:* Underground there is no light, so the seedling lives only on the food in their seed. A moong seed has only a little food. From 30 cm down, the shoot may use it all up before reaching the light. Picking 'more soil' is a common guess, but a seed does not need soil to start.
12. (draw) Draw the seed window jar after 5 days. Show 3 seeds that started in different ways, with their roots and shoots.  
   **Answer:** Three seeds, each with a root curving down and a shoot curving up, whichever way the seed was placed.. *Why:* Roots grow towards gravity and shoots grow away from it.
13. (write) Plan a fair test to find out if methi seeds need warmth to sprout. What will you change? What will you keep the same?  
   **Answer:** I will change only the warmth: one cup in the fridge and one on the kitchen shelf. I will keep the same: the number of methi seeds, the same cotton, the same amount of water, and both cups dark.. *Why:* A good fair test changes just one thing and keeps everything else the same. Keeping both cups dark matters, because the fridge is dark.
14. (truefalse) Old seeds from Beal's bottles, buried in 1879, still sprouted in 2021.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. More than 140 years later, some of the seeds sprouted. Seeds buried in the ground can stay alive for a very long time.

But a seed also needs air, and soggy soil leaves no air, so Rao Aunty's worry is real. Selvi's plan gives water without drowning the seed. A strong answer picks one and admits what another gets right.. *Why:* There is a real trade-off. Water wakes a seed, but too much water and too little air make the seed rot. The best plan depends on how wet and cold the soil is.

### Seed Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Deep down, where did the energy in a seed's food store come from?  
   Options: From the soil / From sunlight, captured by the parent plant's leaves / From the water the seed drinks / From the seed coat  
   **Answer:** From sunlight, captured by the parent plant's leaves. *Why:* The parent plant used the energy of sunlight to make starch from air and water, and packed it into the seed. Picking 'soil' is a very old idea. Soil gives a plant water and small amounts of minerals, but not their food energy.
2. (mcq) You sprout 50 moong seeds in the dark for a week, then dry them. Do they weigh more or less than 50 dry seeds that were never sprouted?  
   Options: More, because they grew / Less, because they used up some of their stored food by breathing / Exactly the same  
   **Answer:** Less, because they used up some of their stored food by breathing. *Why:* In the dark, sprouts cannot make new food. They use up their stored food by breathing, and the carbon goes into the air. So once the water is dried out, they weigh less. Picking 'more' is the usual guess, because sprouts look bigger. Most of that size is water.
3. (write) A seed sealed under water goes sour. A seed in a dry jar does nothing. A seed on damp cotton sprouts. Explain all three using what a seed needs.  
   **Answer:** Under water, the seed has water but no air, so they cannot breathe, and they go sour. In a dry jar, they have air but no water, so they stay asleep. On damp cotton, they have water and air, and the room is warm, so they sprout.. *Why:* A good answer names what each seed has and what they are missing.
4. (mcq) Some small lettuce seeds will sprout only if light reaches them. Why might a tiny seed wait for light?  
   Options: Light tells them they are near the surface, so their small food store won't run out before they reach the sun / Light gives them food straight away / Light makes them heavier  
   **Answer:** Light tells them they are near the surface, so their small food store won't run out before they reach the sun. *Why:* A tiny seed has only a little food. If they sprouted deep underground, they might run out before reaching the light. So waiting for light keeps them safe. In 1952, scientists in the United States showed that red light is what wakes these seeds.
5. (perspective) People disagree about whether a dry seed in a jar is alive.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence. For alive: dry seeds from a jar sprouted when soaked, but boiled seeds never did, so something in the jar seeds was still alive. Against: a dry seed shows almost none of the signs of life. A strong answer then gives the other side its due, for example that sleep is a good comparison, but a seed can stay like this for years.. *Why:* Most biologists say a dry seed is alive but dormant. Because they do so little, people still discuss where life begins and ends. There is no single right answer here.
6. (mcq) A heap of sprouting seeds warms up. A heap of boiled seeds stays cool for a day, then starts to warm and smell. Why does the boiled heap start to warm?  
   Options: The boiled seeds come back to life / The sun shines on it / Tiny living things called bacteria start feeding on it, and they breathe too  
   **Answer:** Tiny living things called bacteria start feeding on it, and they breathe too. *Why:* Boiled seeds are dead, but bacteria can feed on them. Bacteria breathe, so they make warmth too. That is why we stop the warm flask test after 2 days.
7. (odd) Which one is the odd one out? Rajma, chana, moong, potato.  
   Options: Rajma / Chana / Moong / Potato  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Rajma, chana and moong are seeds, each with a baby plant inside. A potato is a swollen underground stem. It can grow new plants, but it is not a seed.
8. (mcq) Rahibai Soma Popere, a farmer in Maharashtra, saves the seeds of many old local kinds of crops. Why does keeping many kinds matter?  
   Options: Old seeds always taste better / If a disease or drought kills one kind, other kinds may survive / It makes the seeds sprout faster  
   **Answer:** If a disease or drought kills one kind, other kinds may survive. *Why:* Different kinds of the same crop cope with different problems. If every farmer grew just one kind, one disease could wipe out the whole harvest. For this work, she was given the Padma Shri in 2020.
9. (write) A farmer stores seed paddy in a damp, warm corner of the house. Next season, very few seeds sprout. What went wrong?  
   **Answer:** Damp and warmth woke the seeds up too early, or made them use up their food and spoil while stored. Seeds keep best when dry and cool.. *Why:* Water and warmth wake seeds. In storage, we want them to stay asleep.
10. (draw) Invent a seed for a desert where it rains only once every few years. Draw them and label two features that help them wait and then sprout fast.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful design, for example: a very hard waterproof coat so they can wait for years, a coat that only softens after lots of rain, a big food store so the root can grow fast and deep.. *Why:* Real desert seeds do things like these. Many wait in the sand until a big rain, then sprout and flower in a few weeks.
11. (write) In the story The Warm Bundle, Noor remembered the bundle being warm. How did that clue help her save the sprouts?  
   **Answer:** Ammi said the warmth came from the seeds breathing. So Noor realised the seeds needed air. In the closed dabba under water, they could not breathe. She drained them and hung them in a loose cloth so air could reach them.. *Why:* The warmth showed that sprouting seeds breathe. Breathing needs air.
12. (write) Astronauts on the Moon want to grow beans. List what they must give the seeds to sprout, and what the young plants will need later.  
   **Answer:** To sprout: water, warmth and air, inside a sealed box or base. Later: light for the leaves to make food, and minerals, like those in soil or plant food.. *Why:* A seed needs water, warmth and air to wake. A seedling needs light once their own food store runs out.
13. (write) A banyan seed is about as small as a mustard seed, with only a tiny store of food. Yet the seed can grow into a giant tree. Where does the rest of the tree come from?  
   **Answer:** The seed's food only feeds the seedling for the first few days. After that, the young banyan's green leaves make food from carbon dioxide in the air and water from the soil, using sunlight. Over many years, the tree builds their whole body that way.. *Why:* A strong answer says the seed's food is only a start, and the tree is built mostly from air and water, using sunlight.
14. (mcq) In parts of Australia and South Africa, some seeds sprout only after they meet smoke from a bush fire. Why might a seed wait for a fire?  
   Options: After a fire, the ground is open and sunny, with fewer big plants taking the light and water / Fire makes the seed warm enough to sprout / Smoke is food for seeds  
   **Answer:** After a fire, the ground is open and sunny, with fewer big plants taking the light and water. *Why:* A fire clears away big plants, so a seedling that sprouts just after gets light, water and room. In 2004, scientists found the chemical in smoke that wakes these seeds. Picking 'fire makes the seed warm' is a sensible guess, but it is a chemical in the smoke, not the heat, that wakes them.
15. (fill) A seed packet says that 40 of every 50 seeds sprouted in the company's test. That is ____ out of every 10 seeds.  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* 50 is 5 lots of 10. 40 shared into 5 lots is 8. So 8 out of every 10 seeds sprouted. Sowing 10 seeds, you should expect about 8 seedlings.
16. (order) Put these ways of storing seeds in order, from the one that keeps seeds alive longest to the one that keeps them alive for the shortest time.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Water and warmth wake a seed and make them use up their food. Dry and cold keep the seed resting for the longest. That is why seed banks keep seeds dry and frozen.

## Applications
- Farmers soak and sprout seed, like paddy, before sowing, so they come up quickly and evenly.
- Sprouts of moong, chana and methi are sold in markets and eaten in usal, salads and chaat.
- Malt, made by sprouting grains like barley and ragi, is used in ragi malt, some sweets, bread and drinks.
- Seed companies test how many seeds in a batch sprout, and print it on the packet as the germination rate.
- Seed banks keep seeds dry and cold, so they stay asleep and alive for decades.
- Gardeners soak hard seeds like bhindi and bitter gourd overnight, so water gets in faster.
- Gardeners nick or rub very hard seeds with sandpaper to let water in.
- Microgreens, tiny young plants grown from seeds on trays, are a new kind of crop in cities.
- Farmers store seed in dry, cool places, sometimes with neem leaves, so they do not sprout or spoil too soon.
- Scientists breed rice that can sprout in flooded fields, so farmers can sow them straight into wet ground.
- Space agencies test seeds in space, to plan how astronauts could grow food on the Moon and Mars.
- Forest workers collect and sprout seeds of native trees to plant new forests.
- Seed balls, seeds rolled into balls of clay and soil, are thrown onto bare land so they sprout when rain comes.

## Open questions
- **How long can a seed stay alive?** Some have sprouted after 2,000 years. Nobody knows the real limit, and it is different for every kind of seed. Experiments like Beal's bottles take centuries.
- **How does a seed decide when to wake?** Seeds sense water, warmth, light and some chemicals. How they add all these signals together is still being worked out.
- **Why do some seeds of the same plant wake at once, and others wait for years?** Scientists think it spreads the risk, like not putting all your eggs in one basket. How each seed is set to wait is not fully known.
- **How do seeds repair the damage of getting old while they sleep?** Seeds have repair tools for their cells. Scientists are still finding out how they work, and why some seeds are so much better at it.
- **Can we store seeds that do not like being dried, like mango, jackfruit and many rainforest seeds?** These seeds die if dried or frozen, so normal seed banks cannot keep them. Scientists are testing new ways, such as freezing just the baby plant in liquid nitrogen.
- **Could we grow crops from seed on the Moon or Mars?** Seeds have sprouted on the Moon in a sealed box and in Moon soil brought to Earth. Growing whole crops there, through many generations, has never been done.
- **Is a dry seed alive, or something in between?** It depends on what we mean by 'alive'. Scientists and philosophers still discuss it.

## Connected maths
- Look at the chart from the five-cup test. How many seeds sprouted in all five cups together? And how many seeds did not sprout? **21 sprouted and 29 did not.**. It may not be exactly 90, because every seed is a little different. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)

(queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)

So it grew 9 millimetres on the first day. 27 take away 15 is 12. So it grew 12 millimetres on the next day. 12 is more than 9, so the root was growing faster. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- You soaked moong at 7 o'clock in the evening. You drained them at 7 o'clock the next morning. The first roots showed at 7 o'clock that evening. How long did the seeds soak? How long from soaking to the first roots? **They soaked for 12 hours. From soaking to the first roots took 24 hours.**. From 7 in the evening to 7 in the morning is 12 hours. From 7 in the morning to 7 in the evening is another 12 hours. 12 + 12 = 24 hours, which is one whole day. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What are the three parts of a seed? → The seed coat, the baby plant and the food store.
- Name the three things a seed needs to sprout. → Water, warmth and air.
- Does a moong seed need light to sprout? → No. They sprout in the dark too.
- What comes out of a seed first, the root or the shoot? → The root.
- Which way does a root grow? → Down, towards the pull of gravity.
- Why does a bundle of sprouts feel warm? → The seeds are breathing, and breathing makes warmth.
- Can split dal sprout? → No. It has lost its baby plant.
- Which seed can start sprouting under water? → Rice.
- About how old was the oldest seed ever sprouted into a tree? → About 2,000 years old, a date palm seed.
- What happened to the cotton seed that sprouted on the Moon? → They died when the freezing lunar night came.
