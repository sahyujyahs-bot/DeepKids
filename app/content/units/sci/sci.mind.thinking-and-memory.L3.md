# Thinking and memory: how your brain learns and remembers
`sci.mind.thinking-and-memory.L3`

**Summary.** You can hear ten words once and lose most of them by the next day. Turn the same ten words into a silly story, and most of them stay. Your brain is made of billions of nerve cells called neurons, and every memory is held in the links between them. What you do with something while you learn it decides how strong those links become. Practice makes the links stronger, so a hard skill slowly becomes easy. Even grown-up brains keep changing this way: London taxi drivers grow a bigger part of the brain that finds the way.

**Intro.** *What did you eat for breakfast yesterday? What did you eat for breakfast on the same day last month? Why is one easy and the other so hard?* Your brain kept yesterday's breakfast, but let last month's go. In this unit you find out how your brain holds on to things. You test two ways of learning a list of words, and see which one lasts until tomorrow. You practise something hard with your other hand and watch it get easier. You find out what neurons are, and why practice changes them. And you try the tricks that people with very strong memories use.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what my brain does: it senses, thinks, remembers and moves my body, using nerve cells called neurons.
1. I can name three kinds of memory: memories of things that happened, facts I know, and skills I can do.
2. I can explain that practice makes the links between neurons stronger, so a skill gets easier and a memory lasts longer.
3. I can test a way of remembering fairly, and say what I think, what I saw, and why.
4. I can use three ways to remember better: making a story or a picture, putting things into groups, and testing myself on different days.
5. I can say why some popular ideas about the brain are wrong, such as 'we use only 10% of our brain'.
6. I can give examples of other living beings who learn and remember, such as bees and the touch-me-not plant.

## Terms
- **Brain**: The soft organ inside your skull that senses, thinks, remembers, feels and controls your body. *e.g.* When you catch a ball, your brain sees the ball, works out where it will go, and moves your hands.
- **Neuron**: A nerve cell. Neurons carry messages as tiny electrical signals. Your brain has about 86 billion of them. *e.g.* When you touch a hot tumbler, neurons carry the message from your fingers to your brain in a fraction of a second.
- **Synapse**: The tiny gap where one neuron passes its message to the next. The message crosses the gap as a chemical. *e.g.* Each neuron has synapses with thousands of other neurons, like one person passing notes to many friends.
- **Memory**: Anything you have learned and kept: a thing that happened, a fact, or a skill. *e.g.* Remembering your grandmother's face, knowing that 2 + 2 is 4, and knowing how to ride a cycle are all memories.
- **Short-term memory**: The memory that holds a few things for a few seconds, while you use them. It is also called working memory. *e.g.* Holding an OTP number in your head for the few seconds it takes to type it in.
- **Long-term memory**: The memory that keeps things for days, years or a whole life. *e.g.* Your name, your home, the words of a song you know well.
- **Recall**: Bringing a memory back into your mind without being shown it. *e.g.* Saying all the planets without looking at a list is recall.
- **Forgetting**: Losing a memory, or losing the way to bring it back. Most forgetting happens in the first day after learning. *e.g.* You learn a new friend's name at a wedding and lose it by the next morning.
- **Hippocampus**: A curved part deep inside the brain, one on each side, that helps store new memories of events and places. Its name is Greek for 'seahorse', because of its shape. *e.g.* Your hippocampus helped you remember where you kept your slippers last night.
- **Practice**: Doing something again and again to get better at it. *e.g.* Playing the same sarali varisai on the veena every day until your fingers know the way.
- **Brain plasticity**: The brain's way of changing itself as you learn. Links between neurons that are used get stronger, and new links grow. 'Plastic' here means able to change shape, like clay. *e.g.* After a month of practice, your brain's links for skipping rope are stronger, so you skip without thinking.
- **Chunking**: Grouping many small things into a few bigger pieces, so they are easier to hold in memory. *e.g.* A mobile number like 98410 23456 is easier to remember as two groups of five than as ten separate digits.
- **Skill memory**: The memory of how to do something with your body. You use it without thinking about the steps. *e.g.* Tying your shoelaces or swimming. You can do it, but it is hard to explain each step in words.
- **Habit**: Something you have done so often that you do it without deciding to. *e.g.* Washing your hands as soon as you come home, without anyone telling you.

## Explanations
### What the brain does · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain is the organ that senses the world, thinks, remembers, feels and controls the body.

Your brain sits inside your skull, floating in a liquid that protects it. A grown-up's brain weighs about 1.3 kilograms, about as much as a full one-litre bottle of water and a bit more. Messages come in from your eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin. Your brain puts them together, works out what they mean, and decides what to do. It sends messages out to your muscles to move you. It also runs things you never notice, like your breathing and your heartbeat. Your brain is only about 2% of your body's weight, but it uses about one fifth of the energy from your food.
- e.g. When an auto honks behind you, your brain hears it, works out where it is, and makes you step aside, all in under a second.
- e.g. While you sleep, your brain keeps your heart beating and your lungs breathing.
- e.g. When you read a word, your brain turns black marks on paper into a meaning.
### Neurons: how the brain sends messages · *defined*
**Definition:** Neurons are nerve cells. They carry messages as tiny electrical signals, and pass them on to other neurons across tiny gaps called synapses.

Your brain has about 86 billion neurons. A neuron has many short branches that receive messages, and usually one long tail that sends them on. A message travels along the neuron as a small electrical signal. At the end, the message must cross a tiny gap, the synapse, to the next neuron. It crosses as a chemical. Each neuron has synapses with thousands of other neurons. So the brain is a huge web of links. Every thought, feeling and memory is many neurons sending signals together.
- e.g. Neurons also run from your brain down your back and out to your fingers and toes, so a message can go from your toe to your brain.
- e.g. Brain scientists can record the electrical signals of neurons with small sensors on the head.
- e.g. The longest neurons in your body run from the bottom of your back to your toes.
### How a memory is made · *defined*
**Definition:** A memory is made when a pattern of neurons that fired together becomes linked more strongly, so that the same pattern can fire again later.

First, something must get your attention. Things you do not notice are not stored at all. Then your short-term memory holds it for a few seconds. Most grown-ups can hold about 7 digits this way, and a child of 8 about 5. If you use it, think about it or link it to something you already know, your brain starts to keep it. The hippocampus helps to store new memories of events and places. Over the next days, and especially while you sleep, the memory is linked into the rest of your brain. Memories of skills, like riding a cycle, are kept by other parts of the brain.
- e.g. You remember the day the power went off at your birthday party, because it surprised you and you talked about it.
- e.g. You forget what you ate for lunch on an ordinary day last month, because nothing linked it to anything else.
- e.g. You can still swim after a whole year without swimming, because skill memories last a long time.
### How practice changes the brain · *defined*
**Definition:** When neurons send signals to each other again and again, the synapses between them grow stronger, and new links can grow. This is called brain plasticity.

The first time you do something new, like writing with your other hand, it is slow and wobbly. Your brain is trying many links to find the right way. Each time you practise, the links you use get stronger. The signal passes more easily, so the skill becomes faster and smoother. In 1949 the scientist Donald Hebb suggested that neurons which fire together become more strongly linked. Experiments have since shown this is true. Links you stop using slowly get weaker. That is why a skill you have not practised for a long time feels rusty. Brains keep changing all through life, at any age.
- e.g. Children who took music lessons for over a year showed changes in the parts of the brain for hearing and finger movement.
- e.g. London taxi drivers, who learn thousands of streets, grow a larger back part of the hippocampus, which helps find the way.
- e.g. Practising a little every day works better than practising for a long time on one day.
### Why we forget, and what helps us remember · *defined*
**Definition:** We forget most quickly in the first hours and days after learning. A memory lasts longer when we link it to things we know, picture it, group it, and bring it back again on later days.

In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus, a German scientist, tested his own memory for years. He learned lists of made-up syllables, then tested himself later. He found that most of a new list was lost within a day, and after that the forgetting slowed down. Many later studies found the same. What helps? Making a story or a picture from the things to remember. Putting things into groups, called chunking. And testing yourself, which means trying to bring a memory back without looking. Each time you recall it, especially on a new day, the memory gets stronger. Sleep helps too.
- e.g. A story that links ten words keeps most of them for a day. Repeating the same words over and over keeps far fewer.
- e.g. Your times tables stay because you said them aloud on many different days.
- e.g. Trying to say a poem without looking, then checking, works better than reading it again and again.
### Who else learns and remembers? · *defined*
**Definition:** Learning means changing what you do because of what happened before. Animals, plants and even single cells learn and remember.

Honeybees learn which flower colours give the most nectar, and remember it for days. A touch-me-not plant folds their leaves when they are touched or dropped. In a 2014 experiment, Monica Gagliano and her team dropped touch-me-not plants a short way again and again, without harming them. After many drops, the plants stopped folding. They had learned that the drop was harmless. They still remembered it almost a month later, without a brain or any neurons. Plants, animals and people all learn, each in their own way. Some scientists call the plant's change a simple automatic response, not real learning.
- e.g. A dog learns that the sound of a steel plate means dinner is coming.
- e.g. A crow at your window learns which flat leaves out food.
- e.g. Slime moulds, which are single-celled, find the shortest way through a maze to their food, and leave a trail that shows where they have already been.
### Where is a memory kept? · *debated*
Scientists agree that memories depend on links between neurons. They have even found, in mice, small groups of neurons that bring back one particular memory when they fire. But a single memory seems to be spread across many parts of the brain, not kept in one spot. And nobody knows how signals between neurons become the experience of remembering your grandmother's face. Here are the main views.
- e.g. When you remember a festival, the sights, sounds, smells and feelings all come back together, though they are handled by different parts of the brain.
- e.g. People who lose part of their brain in an accident often keep most of their memories.
- **Most neuroscientists:** A memory is a pattern of strengthened links between neurons, spread across the brain. There is nothing more to it than that.
- **Scientists who study memory without brains:** Plants, single cells and even cells in a dish keep traces of what happened to them. So memory is older and wider than neurons, and the brain is one way of doing it.
- **Many Indian philosophers:** Awareness is the ground of everything, and memory is awareness holding on to what it has met. The brain is the way this happens in a human body.

## Mostly agreed
- The brain is made of billions of neurons that pass messages to each other across synapses. *How we know:* Scientists have seen neurons and synapses with microscopes since the late 1800s, and recorded their electrical signals.
- Practice strengthens links between neurons, and the brain keeps changing all through life. *How we know:* Brain scans of musicians, jugglers and London taxi drivers show changes that grow with practice. Experiments on animals show synapses getting stronger when used.
- We forget most quickly in the first day after learning, and recalling a memory on later days makes it last. *How we know:* Hermann Ebbinghaus found this in 1885, and hundreds of later studies with children and grown-ups found the same.
- Linking things into a story or a picture, and grouping them, makes them much easier to remember. *How we know:* In 1969 Gordon Bower and Michal Clark found that people who made stories from lists of words remembered far more of them than people who just repeated them.
- Sleep helps new memories to settle. *How we know:* People who sleep after learning remember more the next day than people who stay awake for the same time. Recordings from rats show their memory neurons replaying the day's paths while they sleep.

## How we found out
**Eleanor Maguire and her team, and later Katherine Woollett with Eleanor Maguire, at University College London, 2000 (the taxi driver scans) and 2011 (the trainees scanned before and after).** For much of the 1900s, many scientists thought a grown-up's brain was fixed. Children's brains grow and change, they said, but after that a brain only wears out. In the late 1990s, Eleanor Maguire, a brain scientist in London, studied London's taxi drivers. To get a licence, they must learn about 25,000 streets and the way between any two places. This test is called the Knowledge, and it takes most people three to four years. Maguire and her team scanned the brains of 16 taxi drivers, all men. In 2000 they reported that the back part of the hippocampus, which helps us find our way, was bigger in the drivers than in other men. The longer a man had driven a taxi, the bigger it was. But perhaps people born with a big hippocampus chose to drive taxis? So Katherine Woollett and Maguire scanned 79 trainees before they began, and again three to four years later. At the start, their brains matched other people's. The 39 who passed the Knowledge had grown in that part. Those who gave up, and people who never trained, had not. Learning had changed grown-up brains.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought the grown-up brain was fixed and could not change its shape. The trainee drivers started with brains like everyone else's, and those who learned the streets grew a bigger back part of the hippocampus. So learning changes grown-up brains too.

## Challenging perspectives
- **We use only 10% of our brain.** (A very old and popular idea, repeated in films and adverts; *rejected*)  
  For: Many people feel they could do much more if they tried, so it seems there must be unused brain.  
  Against: Brain scans show that every part of the brain is active over a day. Damage to almost any part causes a problem. A brain that wasted 90% of itself would not use a fifth of the body's energy.
- **Your brain stops changing once you are grown up.** (Many scientists for much of the 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: Children learn new languages and skills much faster than most grown-ups. Grown-ups often find new things hard.  
  Against: Brain scans show grown-up brains changing as they learn. London taxi drivers grew a bigger part of the hippocampus while they learned the city's streets.
- **Some people are 'visual learners' and some are 'listening learners', and each learns best only in their own way.** (Many teachers and books on learning; *rejected*)  
  For: People do say they like pictures or words more. It feels true that some lessons suit us better.  
  Against: When scientists tested it, teaching people in their 'own' style did not help them learn more. Most things are best learned with pictures and words and doing, all together.
- **Memory works like a video camera, keeping exactly what happened.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Some memories feel very clear and complete, as if we could play them again.  
  Against: People who remember the same event often remember it differently, and can be sure of things that never happened. Each time we recall a memory, the brain builds it again.
- **Only animals with brains can learn and remember.** (Many scientists, and most people; *debated*)  
  For: Learning in people and animals clearly uses neurons, and plants have no neurons.  
  Against: Touch-me-not plants learned that being dropped was harmless, and kept that memory for weeks. Single-celled beings change what they do because of what happened before.

## Roots
We have tested how stories, groups and practice help us remember. Now we ask what memory tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You heard two lists of ten home words, with the same time for each word. The next day, you remembered far more of the list you made into a story. The only change was what your mind did with the words. So how well you remember depends on what you do while you learn.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about a 'good memory' or a 'bad memory' as if it were a box we are born with, big or small. The Story Trick shows that remembering is something you do, and the same child can do it well or badly.  
*Evidence:* In the Story Trick, the same child, with the same kind of words and the same time, remembered far more of the story list the next day. Gordon Bower and Michal Clark found the same with grown-ups in 1969.

- *who am i:* You are partly made by what you practise. Every skill and memory you build changes your brain a little.
- *what is life:* Living beings learn from what happened to them before, from bees and dogs to touch-me-not plants.
- *what is this world:* The places and people you meet leave lasting changes in you. A taxi driver's brain carries the shape of a city's streets.

Every human brain starts from the same plan, with the same kinds of neurons. Then each life uses some links more than others. The links that are used grow stronger, and each brain takes the shape of the life it has lived. So from one shared plan come billions of different minds, each shaped by what they did and met.

- **Yoga Sutras of Patanjali 1.11:** Memory, smriti, is not letting go of something you have experienced. (In the Story Trick, the story words were the ones your mind did not let go. Patanjali describes memory as holding on, which is what strong links between neurons do.)
- **Yoga Sutras of Patanjali 1.14:** Practice becomes firm when it is done for a long time, without breaks, and with care. (This is what the Other Hand experiment and the 7-Day Practice Diary showed: short practice on many days, done with attention, makes a skill firm.)
- **Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book 2, chapter 1:** We become builders by building, and lyre players by playing the lyre, a small kind of harp. In the same way, we become fair by doing fair things. (Aristotle saw that what we do again and again shapes what we become. Brain science now shows one way it happens: practice changes the links in the brain.)
- Science (settled): Synapses that are used again and again grow stronger, and this is one way the brain stores memories and skills.
- Science (open): How signals between neurons become the experience of remembering is not known.
- Science (debated): Plants and single-celled beings learn and remember without neurons. Scientists are still working out how, and some argue about whether to call it learning.
- Other view, Many neuroscientists: A memory is a pattern of strengthened links between neurons, and nothing more. There is no awareness that holds on to experiences. The brain is all there is to memory, and talk of a mind beyond it adds nothing.
- Practice, Call a memory back (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly, three times. Now think of the smell of your favourite food. Wait for it. Notice the moment it comes back. Did you push it, or did it arrive? Now let it go. Breathe once more and open your eyes.
- Ask: When a memory comes back, where was it while you were not thinking of it?
- Ask: If practice changes your brain, what would you like to practise, to grow into who you want to be?
- Ask: A touch-me-not plant remembered without a brain. What do you think a memory really is?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* If your brain changes every time you learn something, are you the same person you were last year? (hint: Think about what has stayed the same about you, and what has changed.)
- *deep:* Why did the story list last until the next day, when the repeated list faded? (hint: Think about how many links each word had to other things in your mind.)
- *deep:* A touch-me-not plant has no brain, yet learned that being dropped was harmless. So what is a memory made of? (hint: Think about what the plant changed, and what your brain changes.)
- *big:* If you could never forget anything, would that be a good thing? (hint: Think about the things you are glad to forget, and what it would be like to remember every lunch you ever ate.)

- *deep:* Is a skill in your hands or in your brain? (hint: Think about your other hand, which has the same muscles but not the same practice.)

## Story: Paati's Kolam
*The story is made up. The kolam is real: a sikku kolam is drawn as one line that loops round a grid of dots without touching them, and many families in Tamil Nadu draw bigger kolams every morning in the month of Margazhi.*

It was the first morning of Margazhi, and it was still dark in Madurai. Kavya was eight. She had set an alarm for half past five, because this year Paati had promised to teach her the kolam.

Paati lived with them in a flat on the third floor. Every morning she swept the landing outside their door and sprinkled it with water. Then she bent down with a steel bowl of rice flour and drew. Her fingers let out a thin white line that curled round and round a grid of dots. In two minutes there was a pattern like a flower made of one long loop. The neighbours on the landing always stopped to look.

"This one is a sikku kolam," said Paati. "Seven rows of dots, and one line that goes round all of them. The line never touches a dot, and it ends where it began."

Kavya watched very carefully. She watched Paati's hand go round the first dot, then dip between two dots, then swing round the corner. She was sure she had it.

"Now you try," said Paati. She rubbed out a corner of the landing with her foot and put down fresh dots.

Kavya took a pinch of flour. Her line went round the first dot nicely. Then it went round the second. Then she stopped. Was it under the next dot, or over it? Her line wobbled, crossed itself and ran into a dot. It looked like a tangled thread.

Her little brother Arjun had come out in his banian, rubbing his eyes. "That is not a flower," he said. "That is a noodle."

"I watched you so carefully, Paati," said Kavya. "Why did it all fall out of my head?"

Paati sat down on the step. "Watching is not the same as knowing," she said. "You saw my hand for two minutes. My hand has drawn this kolam every Margazhi for sixty years. Do you think I learned it in one morning?"

"How did you learn it, then?"

"My amma taught me with a little song," said Paati. "Round the top, dip down, round the side, come back. Each part has its words. When you know the words, you do not have to remember seventy turns. You only remember four parts, and then you do them again."

She sang it slowly, and drew one corner of the kolam while she sang. Kavya sang with her. Then Kavya drew the same corner, singing. Her line still wobbled, but it went the right way.

"Good," said Paati. "That is enough for today. Tomorrow you will do it again."

"Only one corner?"

"One corner, every morning," said Paati. "The kolam has to grow a path in your head first. A path grows by walking on it."

So Kavya came out every morning. She made a chart on a sheet of paper and stuck it on the fridge with a magnet. Each morning she timed herself on Amma's phone and wrote down how long the corner took.

On the first day, one corner took her four minutes, and she had to start again twice. On the fourth day, it took two minutes. On the seventh day she drew the whole kolam. It was crooked on one side, but it was one line, and it came back to where it began. Arjun looked at it for a long time. "That is a flower," he admitted.

By the middle of Margazhi, something strange had happened. Kavya could draw the kolam while she talked to the neighbour, Lakshmi Akka, about her school. She did not have to sing the song any more. She did not even have to think. Her hand just knew.

"Paati," she said one morning, "the kolam has moved. It used to be in the song. Now it is in my hand."

Paati laughed. "It is not in your hand, kanna. Your hand is the same hand it was on the first day. Try it with your other hand and see."

Kavya tried with her left hand. The line wobbled and ran into a dot, exactly like on the first morning.

"So where is it?" asked Kavya.

"In your head," said Paati, tapping her own forehead. "Every morning you used the same path, and every morning the path got easier to walk. My old teacher used to say that practice is how you build your own brain. I did not understand her when I was eight. I do now."

On the last day of Margazhi, Arjun came out before anyone else. He was holding the bowl of rice flour.

"Akka," he said, "teach me the song."

So Kavya sat him down on the step, put down seven rows of dots, and showed him the first corner. "Only one corner today," she said. "Tomorrow you will do it again."

**For the parent:** Kavya's hand was the same on the first morning and the last. What had changed was inside her head: every morning of practice had made the path for the kolam a little stronger, until her hand could follow it without her thinking.

- Ask: Why did Kavya forget the kolam after watching Paati only once?
- Ask: How did the little song help Kavya remember so many turns?
- Ask: Why did the kolam go wrong when Kavya tried it with her other hand?
- Ask: Is there something you can now do without thinking, that was hard when you started?

## Games
### g1 · I Went to the Santhai (20 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them face down in a pile. Each player takes 5 tokens.; The first player turns over a card and says: 'I went to the santhai and bought tomatoes.' Then they put the card face down in a row.; The next player turns over a new card, says the whole list in order, and adds the new thing.; If you forget a thing or get the order wrong, lose one token. The next player starts a new list.; After each round, say the trick you used to remember: a story, a picture, or grouping.; When a player has no tokens left, they are out. The last player with tokens wins.; Playing alone: turn over one card at a time and say the whole list each time. How long a list can you say? Try again tomorrow and beat it.
- **Printable cards: 30 market picture cards to cut out:** tomatoes; onions; bananas; a jackfruit; drumsticks; curry leaves; coconut; jasmine flowers; a clay pot; a steel tumbler; a broom; brinjals; lemons; green chillies; a pumpkin; groundnuts; jaggery; a mat; a basket; bangles; a kite; a balloon; a bucket; rice; tamarind; a mango; a watermelon; a comb; a towel; an umbrella
- **Printable tokens: 15 memory tokens, 5 for each player:** Cut out 15 small circles. Each player starts with 5. You lose one each time you forget.
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player name; Longest list remembered; Trick I used
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the cards and tokens. 2. Shuffle the cards. 3. Give each player 5 tokens.  
  *Parent says:* This is a market game. Each of us will say the whole shopping list and add one more thing. If you forget, you lose a token.  
  *Child mode:* Say the whole list. Add one thing. Forget? Lose a token!
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play two rounds. Turn the cards face down as you go, so the list is only in your heads.  
  *Parent says:* I went to the santhai and bought tomatoes. Your turn. Say tomatoes, then add your card.  
  *Child mode:* Turn a card. Say the whole list. Add your thing.
  - Note: Most children manage 6 to 10 things in the first round. Do not help with the list, but after the round, ask which trick they used.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After the second round, teach a trick: make a story with the list.  
  *Parent says:* Let's try a trick. Make a story as the list grows: the tomatoes rolled into the onions, and a banana slipped on them. Does a story help you go further?  
  *Child mode:* Try making a story as the list grows. Can you go further?
  - Check: Divya wants to remember a long market list. Which trick will help her most? → **Making a story that joins the things**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play one more round with the story trick, then fill in the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* One more round, with stories this time. Then let's write our longest lists on the score sheet.  
  *Child mode:* One more round with a story. Write your longest list.
  - Think: Was the story round longer than the first round? Why?
### g2 · Number Plate Flash (15 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the plate cards and put them face down.; The shower turns over one card and shows it for five seconds, then turns it back.; The other player writes the plate from memory.; Score one point for each letter or digit in the right place. A full plate scores 10.; Take turns being the shower. After 5 plates each, add up the scores.; For round two, read each plate in groups before writing: 'TN 59, AB, 12, 34'. Does grouping raise your score?
- **Printable cards: 20 number plate cards to cut out:** TN 59 AB 1234; KA 05 MN 4821; KL 07 CD 9036; TG 09 EK 5507; AP 39 BT 7712; TN 01 AX 6380; KA 51 HR 2049; KL 11 PL 3375; TN 38 CQ 8810; MH 12 DE 1468; WB 02 AK 7341; DL 3C AB 9062; KA 03 JN 5190; TN 72 BY 4006; KL 43 F 2208; TG 07 MS 6633; AP 16 CR 0917; TN 22 DT 3591; GJ 01 KP 8024; OD 02 AZ 1157
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player name; Plate; Letters and digits right (out of 10); Did I group it?
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the cards and explain the game.  
  *Parent says:* These are number plates like the ones on cars and autos. The first two letters show the state. TN is Tamil Nadu and KA is Karnataka. I show a plate for five seconds. You write it down.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the plate for five seconds. Then write it down!
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play five plates each and score them.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the first one. Look carefully. Five, four, three, two, one. Now write it.  
  *Child mode:* Look hard. Now write it from memory.
  - Note: Ten letters and digits are more than most people can hold after one look. Most children get 5 to 7 right.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play five more plates each, but this time say each plate in groups before writing.  
  *Parent says:* This time, say it in groups as you look: TN 59, AB, 12, 34. Four groups instead of ten letters. Does that help?  
  *Child mode:* Say the plate in groups. Then write it.

### e1 · The Story Trick: does a story help you remember a list? (30 min, 2 days)
- **question:** If you make a list of words into a story, do you remember more of them than if you just repeat them?
- **predict:** You will learn one list by saying each word again and again, and the other by making up a story. Which list will you remember better tomorrow? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both lists have ten words for everyday things in a home. Each word gets the same time, 10 seconds. After each list, you count backwards from 20 to 1, so you cannot keep repeating the words. Only the way of learning changes. You test both lists the next day too, at the same time, without looking.
- **safety:** No safety risks.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw two bars for the next day: one for list A and one for list B. Write one sentence about what you found.
- **results table:** List | How I learned it | My guess: how many I will remember tomorrow | Words remembered straight away (out of 10) | Words remembered the next day (out of 10)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess first. Learn list A by repeating each word, and list B by making a story. Test both lists straight away. (25 min); day 2: At the same time of day, without warning and without looking, say all the words you remember from both lists. (5 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the question aloud. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write the guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* I am going to read you two lists of ten words. One you will repeat, and one you will turn into a story. Which list will you remember better tomorrow? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Two lists of words. Repeat one. Make a story with the other. Which will you remember tomorrow? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that list will stay longer? (good: Because saying a word many times puts it in my head.; Because a story is easier to remember than separate words.; Because the story joins the words together.)
  - Note: Do not say what you think will happen. Any guess with a reason is a good start. The guess shows what your child thinks memory is.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read list A aloud, one word every 10 seconds. 2. After each word, your child says it aloud again and again until the next word. 3. Do not show the list.  
  *Parent says:* List A. I will say a word. You say it over and over until I say the next one. Ready? Spoon.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to each word. Say it again and again. Keep going until the next word.
  - Note: List A: spoon, pillow, bucket, mango, umbrella, comb, key, tumbler, slipper, clock. Use the stopwatch to keep 10 seconds for each word. Always do the repeat list first. If you do the story list first, your child may keep making stories with list A too.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to count backwards aloud from 20 to 1. 2. Then ask them to say every word they remember from list A, in any order. 3. Tick each one.  
  *Parent says:* Now count backwards from 20 to 1. Then tell me every word you remember from that list. Any order is fine.  
  *Child mode:* Count back from 20 to 1. Then say all the words you remember.
  - Note: Counting backwards stops your child from repeating the words in their head, so both lists wait the same way. Write the number remembered in the table.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read list B aloud, one word every 10 seconds. 2. After each word, your child adds it to a story, aloud. 3. The story can be as silly as they like.  
  *Parent says:* List B is different. This time, make up a story as you go. Each new word must join the story. The first word is broom. What does the broom do?  
  *Child mode:* Make up a story. Every new word must join the story. Silly is good!
  - Note: List B: broom, candle, banana, towel, coin, bangle, plate, ladder, pencil, kite. A story might go: 'A broom lit a candle and slipped on a banana.' If your child is stuck, ask 'What happens next?' but let them choose the words of the story. Keep to 10 seconds a word, the same as list A.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to count backwards from 20 to 1. 2. Then ask for every word they remember from list B. 3. Tick each one and write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Count backwards from 20 to 1 again. Now tell me every word from the story list.  
  *Child mode:* Count back from 20 to 1. Say all the story words.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two numbers side by side. 2. Ask what your child notices. 3. Tell them you will ask again tomorrow, but not when.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the two numbers. Is there a difference? Tomorrow I will ask you both lists again, without the lists. Which do you think will stay?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your two numbers. Which list did better? What about tomorrow?
  - Note: Straight away, many children remember 5 to 8 words from each list. The big difference usually shows the next day. Do not let your child practise the lists tonight, or the test will not be fair.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The next day, at about the same time, ask for all the words from list A, then list B. 2. Do not give any hints. 3. Write both numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Do you remember the two lists from yesterday? Tell me every word you can from the list you repeated. Now every word from the story list.  
  *Child mode:* Remember yesterday's two lists? Say every word you can. First the repeat list. Then the story list.
  - Note: Most children remember far more of the story list the next day, often 7 or more, and only 1 to 4 of the repeat list. If the numbers are close, that is a real result too. Ask your child what they did in their head for each list.
  - Check: Meena remembered 3 words from her repeat list and 8 from her story list the next day. What does this show? → **Making a story helped her remember more**
  - Wait: Do this the next day, at about the same time.
- **s8 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A strong answer: 'I think a story helps me remember. Tomorrow I remembered 8 story words and only 3 repeated words. In the story, each word was joined to the one before, so one word brought back the next.' After your child has answered, read the explanation cards together.
  - Check: Why does a story help you remember a list? → **Each word is joined to others, so one word brings back the next**
### e2 · The Other Hand: does practice make it easier? (30 min, 3 days)
- **question:** If you write with your other hand again and again, does it get faster and neater? How fast does it change?
- **predict:** On the first try, how long will it take you to write 1 to 10 with your other hand? Will your tenth try be faster or slower? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every try uses the same numbers, 1 to 10, in the same boxes, with the same pencil. Time each try with the same stopwatch. Count the numbers that go outside their box. Only the number of tries changes.
- **safety:** No safety risks. Stop if the hand starts to ache, and rest it.
- **record:** Fill in the table after every try. On day 3, draw a line graph: tries along the bottom, seconds up the side.
- **results table:** Day | Try | Time (seconds) | Numbers outside the box
- **schedule:** day 1: Write 1 to 10 once with your usual hand. Then four tries with your other hand, timed. (12 min); day 2: Three more timed tries with your other hand. (8 min); day 3: Three more timed tries. Make a line graph of your times and talk about what changed. (10 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to write 1 to 10 in the first row of boxes with their usual hand, while you time it. 2. Write the time down.  
  *Parent says:* First, write 1 to 10 in the boxes with the hand you always use. I will time you.  
  *Child mode:* Write 1 to 10 with your usual hand. Go!
  - Note: This gives a number to compare with. Most children of 8 take about 10 to 20 seconds.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess about the other hand, and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Now you will use your other hand. How long do you think it will take? Will you get faster if you keep trying? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Now your other hand. How long will it take? Will you get faster? Why?
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Time four tries with the other hand, one row each. 2. After each try, write the time. 3. Count the numbers that touch or cross the box lines.  
  *Parent says:* Off you go with your other hand. Take your time. I will write down the time. Now let's count how many went outside the boxes.  
  *Child mode:* Write 1 to 10 with your other hand. Then count numbers outside the boxes.
  - Note: The first try is often two or three times slower than the usual hand, and wobbly. Do not let your child switch hands halfway. Praise the effort, not the speed.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, do three more timed tries, the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Yesterday you did four tries. Before you start, guess: will today's first try be faster than yesterday's last?  
  *Child mode:* Three more tries today. Is your first try faster than yesterday's last?
  - Note: Often the first try of a new day is about as good as, or better than, the last try the day before. Sleep helps the brain keep what it practised.
  - Wait: Do this the next day.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 3, do three more timed tries. 2. Help your child draw a line graph of all ten times.  
  *Parent says:* This is the last day. Three more tries. Then let's draw a graph of all your times, from the first try to the tenth.  
  *Child mode:* Three last tries. Then draw a line of your times.
  - Wait: Do this on day 3.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your graph. What do you think practice did? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your graph. What changed? What shows it? Why?
  - Note: A strong answer: 'Practice made my other hand faster. My first try took 52 seconds and my tenth took 31. My hand did not change, but the links in my brain for writing with that hand got stronger each time.' Most children's line falls quickly at first, then more slowly.
  - Check: Thara's times with her other hand were 60, 51, 46, 43, 42 seconds. What happens to the time she saves on each try? → **It gets smaller with each try**
### e3 · Letters or Words: how much can you hold at once? (20 min)
- **question:** Can you remember more letters when they make words than when they are jumbled?
- **predict:** The grown-up will read 12 letters in a jumble, then the same 12 letters as four words. How many will you get right each time? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both rounds use the same 12 letters. The grown-up reads one letter each second. You say them back straight after, in order. Only the order of the letters changes. Do two jumble rounds and two word rounds with different letters, so one lucky round does not fool you.
- **safety:** No safety risks.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar for each round.
- **results table:** Round | Jumble or words? | Letters right, in the right place (out of 12)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the test and ask for a guess with a reason.  
  *Parent says:* I will read 12 letters, one each second. You say them back in the same order. First in a jumble, then the same letters as words. Which will be easier? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to 12 letters. Say them back in order. Jumble first, then words. Which is easier? Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read set 1 jumble, one letter each second: N, U, P, G, S, O, C, E, P, D, N, U. 2. Your child says them back in order straight away. 3. Count how many are right and in the right place.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Listen to all twelve, then say them back. N. U. P. G. S. O. C. E. P. D. N. U. Your turn.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to all twelve letters. Then say them back in order.
  - Note: Most children of 8 get about 4 to 6 letters in the right place. Do not repeat the letters.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the set 1 words letter by letter, one each second, with no pauses. 2. Your child says them back. 3. Count the score.  
  *Parent says:* The same letters, in a new order. D. O. G. S. U. N. C. U. P. P. E. N. Your turn.  
  *Child mode:* Same letters, new order. Listen. Say them back.
  - Note: Many children notice the words partway through and get all 12. If they do not notice, that is fine. Write the score down.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do set 2 the same way: jumble first, then words.  
  *Parent says:* One more jumble, then one more set of words. Listen carefully.  
  *Child mode:* One more jumble. Then one more set of words.
  - Note: Set 2 jumble: U, T, N, B, C, A, E, T, S, B, T, A. Set 2 words: C, A, T, B, U, S, N, E, T, B, A, T.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what your child thinks, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think made the second round of each set easier? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Why were the words easier? What did you see?
  - Note: A strong answer: 'Words are easier. I got 5 jumbled letters but all 12 as words. Twelve letters are too many to hold. Four words are only four things.' This is called chunking: grouping many small things into a few bigger ones.
  - Check: Why are 12 letters easier as DOG SUN CUP PEN? → **You hold four words instead of twelve separate letters**

### a3 · Make a Neuron from Dough (15 min)
- **safety:** Wash hands after playing with dough. Do not eat raw dough.
- **record:** Draw your neuron and label the middle, the branches, the tail and the gap.
- **s1 (8 min)**

  *Parent says:* A neuron is a nerve cell. It has a round middle, short branches that receive messages, and one long tail that sends them on. Let's make two.  
  *Child mode:* Make a ball. Add short branches. Add one long tail. Make two!

  *Child mode:* Leave a tiny gap. Drop grains in the gap. That is the message crossing!
  - Ask: What might happen to a synapse that is used many times? (good: It gets stronger.; The message crosses more easily.; It gets faster.)
  - Note: Each real neuron has synapses with thousands of others. When a synapse is used again and again, the message crosses it more easily. That is one way the brain learns.

### How Memory Works (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What are neurons?  
   Options: Nerve cells that carry messages / Tiny bones inside the skull / Drops of blood in the brain  
   **Answer:** Nerve cells that carry messages. *Why:* Neurons are nerve cells. They carry messages as tiny electrical signals, and your brain has about 86 billion of them. The skull is bone, and blood flows through the brain, but neither carries the messages.
2. (truefalse) Your brain keeps working while you sleep.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. While you sleep, your brain keeps your heart beating and your lungs breathing. It also helps the day's new memories settle.
3. (fill) The tiny gap where one neuron passes its message to the next is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['synapse']. *Why:* A synapse is the tiny gap between two neurons. The message crosses it as a chemical.
4. (match) Match each memory to its kind.  
   **Answer:** Remembering your last birthday party → a memory of something that happened; Knowing that Chennai is in Tamil Nadu → a fact; Riding a cycle → a skill memory; Holding an OTP in your head while you type it → short-term memory. *Why:* A strong answer sees that memories differ in what they keep: a time and a place, a fact, a way of moving, or a few things held for a few seconds.
5. (sort) Sort these. Does your brain do it while you notice, or without you noticing?  
   **Answer:** choosing a sweet at a shop → I notice; keeping your heart beating → Without noticing; working out a sum → I notice; breathing while you sleep → Without noticing; remembering a friend's name → I notice; making you shiver in the cold → Without noticing. *Why:* Your brain does many jobs you never notice, like your heartbeat and breathing in sleep. Thinking and remembering are the jobs you notice.
6. (mcq) In the Story Trick, you count backwards from 20 after hearing each list. Why?  
   Options: To make the test harder for the story list / To stop you repeating the words in your head, so both lists wait the same way / Because counting helps memory  
   **Answer:** To stop you repeating the words in your head, so both lists wait the same way. *Why:* Counting backwards keeps your mind busy, so you cannot keep saying the words to yourself. Both lists then wait in the same way, which makes the test fair.
7. (truefalse) Most forgetting happens in the first day after you learn something.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Hermann Ebbinghaus found this in 1885, and many later studies agree. After the first day, forgetting slows down. That is why recalling something the next day helps so much.
8. (mcq) Leela, a girl in Class 3, must remember her mother's mobile number, 9841023456. Which is the best way?  
   Options: As ten separate digits / In two groups, 98410 and 23456 / By saying it very fast  
   **Answer:** In two groups, 98410 and 23456. *Why:* Two groups of five are much easier to hold than ten separate digits. This is chunking. Saying it fast does not help you keep it.
9. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, to show how a new memory is made and kept.  
   **Answer:** ['notice it', 'hold it in your mind for a few seconds', 'link it to something you already know', 'recall it again on another day']. *Why:* First you must notice something. Then short-term memory holds it. Linking it to what you know helps your brain keep it, and recalling it on later days makes it last.
10. (write) Kavya, a girl of eight, practised a kolam with her right hand every morning for a month. Then she tried it with her left hand, and it went wrong. Why?  
   **Answer:** Her left hand has the same kind of muscles, but it had not practised. The practice had made strong links in her brain for drawing with her right hand, not her left.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the skill was built by practice, in the links of the brain for that hand, and that the left hand had not been given that practice.
11. (draw) Draw two neurons with a gap between them. Draw an arrow to show which way the message goes.  
   **Answer:** Two neurons, each with a round middle, branches and a tail. The tail of the first is close to a branch of the second, with a small gap. The arrow goes along the first neuron's tail, across the gap, into the second.. *Why:* A good drawing shows the gap, the synapse, and the message going from the tail of one neuron to the branches of the next.
12. (truefalse) We use only 10% of our brain.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Brain scans show every part of the brain working over a day, and damage to almost any part causes problems. The 10% idea is an old myth.
13. (fill) Grouping many small things into a few bigger pieces, so they are easier to remember, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['chunking']. *Why:* Chunking turns many small things into a few bigger ones, like twelve letters into four words.
14. (write) Write about one thing you can now do without thinking, which was hard when you started. How did it become easy?  
   **Answer:** For example: 'I can ride my cycle without thinking. At first I fell a lot. I practised every evening, and it got easier each week.'. *Why:* A strong answer names a real skill and says that doing it again and again, over many days, made it easy.
15. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Riding a cycle, swimming, tying shoelaces, knowing the capital of Kerala.  
   **Answer:** knowing the capital of Kerala. *Why:* The capital of Kerala is Thiruvananthapuram. Knowing it is a fact. The other three are skill memories: things your body knows how to do.
### Making Memories Last (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In the Other Hand experiment, Shalini wrote 1 to 10 with her other hand ten times over three days. Which result shows that practice worked?  
   Options: Her first try took 55 seconds and her tenth took 33 / Every try took about 50 seconds / Her usual hand took 15 seconds  
   **Answer:** Her first try took 55 seconds and her tenth took 33. *Why:* Her time fell from 55 to 33 seconds with practice. If every try took the same time, practice would not have changed anything. Her usual hand's time is a comparison, not a result of practice.
2. (perspective) Meenakshi and Farhan, two children in Class 4, disagree about memory.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with evidence, for example the Story Trick, where the same child remembered far more with a story. It also says what the other side has right: people do differ a little, and some of that may come from birth.. *Why:* Both have a point. People differ somewhat in memory, partly from birth. But the tricks and practice a person uses make a much bigger difference, and memory champions say they trained, not that they were born that way.
3. (fill) The curved part deep inside the brain, shaped like a seahorse, that helps store new memories of events and places, is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['hippocampus']. *Why:* The hippocampus helps store new memories of events and places. Its name is Greek for 'seahorse'.
4. (truefalse) A grown-up's brain cannot change any more.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Grown-up brains keep changing as they learn. London taxi drivers grew a bigger part of the hippocampus while they learned thousands of streets.
5. (mcq) Anjali, a girl in Class 3, wants to remember a poem for a show in one month. Which plan will help her most?  
   Options: Read it ten times on Monday, then rest / Say it from memory for a few minutes on Monday, Wednesday and Saturday, and check it each time / Read it once the night before the show  
   **Answer:** Say it from memory for a few minutes on Monday, Wednesday and Saturday, and check it each time. *Why:* Saying it from memory on different days makes the memory stronger each time. Reading it many times on one day helps on that day, but most of it fades quickly. One reading the night before is too little.
6. (write) Your friend Sanjana did the Story Trick. Straight away, she remembered 6 words from each list. The next day she remembered 2 repeated words and 7 story words. Why did the difference show only the next day?  
   **Answer:** Straight away, both lists were still fresh in short-term memory. By the next day the repeated words had mostly faded, because each one stood alone. The story words were linked to each other and to pictures, so they lasted.. *Why:* A strong answer explains that short-term memory hides the difference at first, and that links between the story words help them last overnight.
7. (sort) Sort these. Do they help you remember for a long time, or not much?  
   **Answer:** making a story from the list → Helps a lot; sleeping well after learning → Helps a lot; testing yourself without looking → Helps a lot; reading the same page again and again → Not much; staying up all night before a test → Not much; learning a little on many days → Helps a lot. *Why:* Stories, sleep, testing yourself and spreading learning over days all strengthen memories. Reading again and again feels good but adds few new links. Staying up all night takes away the sleep that helps memories settle.
8. (match) Match each memory trick to the reason it works.  
   **Answer:** Making a story → each word leads to the next; A memory walk → each thing is joined to a place you know well; Grouping → you hold a few groups instead of many small things; Testing yourself → bringing a memory back makes it stronger. *Why:* Every trick works by giving the new thing more links, or by giving your short-term memory fewer things to hold.
9. (mcq) A honeybee visits blue flowers that have lots of nectar and yellow flowers that have none. The next day, which flowers will she most likely visit first?  
   Options: Blue / Yellow / Any colour, because bees do not learn  
   **Answer:** Blue. *Why:* Honeybees learn which colours give nectar and remember it for days. She has learned that blue flowers are worth visiting. Bees do learn, so the last option is wrong.
10. (draw) Draw the shape of the line graph you would expect for ten tries of writing with your other hand. Label the bottom 'try' and the side 'seconds'. Write one sentence about the shape.  
   **Answer:** A line that starts high, falls quickly over the first few tries, then falls more slowly. 'I got faster quickly at first, then more slowly.'. *Why:* A strong answer shows the line going down, and steeper at the start than at the end.
11. (truefalse) In an experiment, touch-me-not plants learned that being dropped gently was harmless, and remembered it for weeks.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 2014 Monica Gagliano and her team dropped touch-me-not plants a short way again and again. The plants stopped folding their leaves, and still remembered almost a month later.
12. (order) Put these in order, to show how practice makes a skill easy.  
   **Answer:** ['the first try is slow and wobbly', 'the links you use get a little stronger each time', 'the skill becomes faster and smoother', 'you can do it without thinking']. *Why:* Practice strengthens the links between neurons step by step. 13. (write) Why is it easier to remember the words of a song than the same words written as a plain list?  
   **Answer:** The tune and the rhythm join the words together, and each line leads to the next. In a plain list, each word stands alone.. *Why:* A strong answer says the tune adds links between the words, the same way a story does.
14. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Hippocampus, neuron, synapse, kidney.

## Applications
- Teachers space out revision over weeks and give short quizzes, because recalling on different days makes memories last.
- Phone numbers, bank account numbers and number plates are written in groups, so people can hold them more easily.
- Musicians, dancers and sportspeople practise the same small moves every day until they need no thought.
- Doctors and therapists help people who have had a stroke to practise movements, so the brain can build new links around the damage.
- Pill boxes with one space for each day help people remember whether they have taken their medicine.
- Memory champions use memory walks and stories to remember hundreds of numbers or words.
- Many families in India have passed down songs, stories and recipes by memory, from grandparents to grandchildren, for generations.
- Scientists who study memory are looking for ways to help people with Alzheimer's disease, an illness that slowly takes away memories.

## Spotted around us
- Children chanting times tables aloud, the vaaippaadu in Tamil schools, so the rhythm holds the numbers.
- Vedic chanting, kept accurately by memory for thousands of years using special patterns of repeating the words. UNESCO lists it as part of the world's heritage.
- Avadhanam, a show of memory and quick thinking in Telugu and Kannada, where one poet answers many questioners in turn, and remembers every unfinished answer.
- A Carnatic music student practising sarali varisai, the same simple patterns every day, before learning songs.
- A kolam drawn from memory at a doorstep every morning, especially in the month of Margazhi.
- An auto driver in Chennai who knows the way through hundreds of small lanes without a map.
- A pill box with the days of the week on it, in a grandparent's room.

## Open questions
- **How do signals between neurons become the experience of remembering?** Scientists can see which neurons fire when we remember, but nobody knows how that becomes the feeling of seeing your grandmother's face in your mind.
- **Why do we sleep, and exactly what does sleep do to memories?** Sleep clearly helps memories settle, and memory areas replay the day in sleep. But scientists still argue about exactly how this works and why it needs sleep.
- **How do plants and single-celled beings keep memories without neurons?** Experiments show they learn and remember, but scientists are only beginning to find out what inside them holds the memory.
- **Why do we forget almost everything from before the age of three?** Young children learn huge amounts, but most grown-ups remember nothing from their first years. There are several ideas, and none is proven.

## Connected maths
- In the Story Trick, Meena remembered 3 words from her repeated list and 8 words from her story list the next day. Each list had 10 words. How many more story words did she remember? What fraction of each list did she remember? **5 more story words. She remembered 3 tenths of the repeated list and 8 tenths of the story list.**. 8 - 3 = 5, so 5 more. Each list had 10 words. 3 out of 10 is three tenths. 8 out of 10 is eight tenths. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- Ravi wrote 1 to 10 with his other hand. His times were 50, 44, 40, 38 and 37 seconds. How many seconds did he save on each new try? Guess his time on the sixth try, and say why. **He saved 6, 4, 2 and 1 seconds. A good guess for the sixth try is about 36 or 37 seconds.**. 50 - 44 = 6. 44 - 40 = 4. 40 - 38 = 2. 38 - 37 = 1. The savings get smaller each time, so the next one is probably about 1 second or less. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- A grown-up's brain weighs about 1300 grams. A full one-litre bottle of water weighs about 1000 grams. How much more water must you add to match the brain? About how many 100-gram packets of biscuits weigh the same as a brain? **About 300 grams more water. About 13 packets.**. 1300 - 1000 = 300 grams. 1300 grams is 13 lots of 100 grams, so 13 packets. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)
- The number plate KA 05 MN 4821 has 10 letters and digits. Shreya says it as KA 05, MN, 48, 21. How many groups is that? If she can hold 5 things in her short-term memory, can she hold the plate as separate letters and digits? As groups? **4 groups. As 10 separate letters and digits, no. As 4 groups, yes.**. KA 05 is one group, MN is one, 48 is one and 21 is one, so 4 groups. 10 things are more than 5, but 4 groups are fewer than 5. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Divya practised catching a ball for 7 days. Her catches in a row were 4, 7, 9, 8, 12, 14, 15. On which day did her number go down? What was her biggest jump from one day to the next? Why might a number go down for one day? **It went down on day 4, from 9 to 8. The biggest jump was from day 4 to day 5, 4 more catches. A number can dip on a day when you are tired, distracted or unlucky.**. The jumps are 3, 2, then down 1, then 4, 2 and 1. One bad day does not mean practice stopped working. The whole line still goes up. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
