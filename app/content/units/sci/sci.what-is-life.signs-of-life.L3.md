# Signs of life: how we tell what is alive
`sci.what-is-life.signs-of-life.L3`

**Summary.** A dry moong seed does nothing you can see, while a car zooms, drinks petrol and puffs out smoke. Yet the seed is alive and the car is not. Scientists tell living from non-living things with seven signs, called the life processes: nutrition, respiration, growth, movement, sensing, excretion and reproduction. One sign alone is not enough, because a cloud grows and a car moves. Only living things do all seven, by themselves, over their lives. A dry seed is alive but resting, and in this unit you wake some up and compare them with boiled ones.

**Intro.** *Hold a dry moong seed in your palm. The seed does not move, does not eat and does not seem to breathe. Now push a toy car across the floor. It zooms. Which one is alive?* Most people say the seed is alive. But ask them why, and it gets hard. The car moves, and the seed does not. A real car even drinks petrol, sucks in air and puffs out smoke. So what does a living thing do that a car never can? Scientists have a list of seven things to look for. In this unit you will test every one of them, on a seed, a cloud and a car.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the seven life processes and say each in my own words: nutrition, respiration, growth, movement, sensing, excretion and reproduction.
1. I can test a thing for each sign of life, and fill in a table of what it does and does not do.
2. I can explain why one sign is not enough to show something is alive, using a seed, a cloud and a car.
3. I can run a fair test with yeast or seeds, changing only one thing, and then say what I think, what I saw and why.
4. I can show that plants do all seven life processes, even though they seem to sit still.
5. I can sort things into alive, once alive and never alive, and give my evidence.
6. I can link life to energy from the Sun and to atoms that travel between living and non-living things. I can name questions nobody can answer yet, like how life began.

## Terms
- **Living thing**: Something that is alive. Living things feed, grow, sense, make young and do the other signs of life. *e.g.* You, a neem tree, an ant and a mushroom are all living things.
- **Non-living thing**: Something that is not alive and never was. It cannot feed, grow from inside or make young. *e.g.* A stone, a steel tumbler, water and air are non-living things.
- **Once alive**: Something that was part of a living thing, but is not alive now. *e.g.* A wooden chair was once a tree. A cotton shirt was once part of a cotton plant. A fallen dry leaf was once alive.
- **Life process**: One of the seven jobs every living thing does to stay alive and make more of their kind. We also call them the signs of life. *e.g.* Eating, breathing and growing are three life processes.
- **Nutrition**: Getting food. Food gives living things energy and the material to build their bodies. Plants make their own food. Animals eat other living things. *e.g.* A cow eats grass. Tulsi plants make sugar in their leaves using sunlight.
- **Respiration**: The way living things get energy out of their food. It happens inside every tiny part of the body, and it usually uses oxygen from the air. *e.g.* When you run, your body does more respiration, so you breathe faster.
- **Breathing**: Moving air in and out of the body. Breathing brings in the oxygen that respiration needs. It is not the same as respiration. *e.g.* Your chest goes up and down when you breathe.
- **Growth**: Getting bigger and changing, from the inside, by turning food into new body. *e.g.* A puppy grows into a dog. A moong seed grows into a plant.
- **Movement**: Moving the body or a part of it. Animals move from place to place. Plants move slowly, mostly by growing. *e.g.* A dog runs. Young sunflower plants turn their tops to follow the Sun across the sky.
- **Sensing**: Noticing what is around you and responding to it. Scientists also call it sensitivity. *e.g.* You pull your hand away from a hot pan. The leaves of a touch-me-not plant fold when you touch them.
- **Excretion**: Getting rid of waste that the body made inside itself. *e.g.* You breathe out carbon dioxide and you pass wee. Plants give out waste gases through tiny holes in their leaves.
- **Reproduction**: Making young ones of the same kind. *e.g.* A hen lays eggs. A mango tree makes seeds. A cat has kittens.
- **Energy**: What lets things move, grow, warm up and change. Living things get energy from food. *e.g.* After a good meal you have energy to play.
- **Dormant**: Alive but resting. A dormant thing does almost nothing until the conditions are right. *e.g.* A dry seed is dormant. Water wakes the seed up.
- **Germinate**: To start growing. A seed germinates by waking up and growing a root and a shoot. *e.g.* Moong seeds germinate in a day or two on damp cotton.
- **Cell**: A tiny living building block. Every living thing is made of one or more cells. *e.g.* Your body is made of about 30 million million cells.
- **Yeast**: A tiny living thing, a kind of fungus, made of just one cell. Bakers use yeast to make bread rise. *e.g.* Dry yeast from a packet wakes up in warm sugar water.
- **Evidence**: What you saw, heard or measured that shows something is true. *e.g.* The balloon grew bigger. That is evidence that the yeast made a gas.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. *e.g.* To test if yeast needs sugar, use the same yeast, water and bottle. Only leave out the sugar.

## Explanations
### What it means to be alive: the seven signs · *defined*
**Definition:** A living thing belongs to a kind that does all seven life processes: nutrition, respiration, growth, movement, sensing, excretion and reproduction.

Scientists cannot point to one thing that makes something alive. So they use a list of seven signs. Here they are. Nutrition: getting food. Respiration: getting energy from the food. Growth: getting bigger from the inside. Movement: moving the body or a part of it. Sensing: noticing the world and responding. Excretion: getting rid of waste. Reproduction: making young ones. Some children remember them with the made-up word MRS GREN. Each letter starts one sign: Movement, Respiration, Sensing, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition. A living thing does not do all seven at every moment. A child cannot have babies yet, and some animals and people never have young. But every kind of living thing makes more of the same kind.
- e.g. A dog eats, breathes, grows, runs, sniffs, does wee and can have puppies. That is all seven.
- e.g. A mango tree makes food in the leaves, takes in air, grows taller, turns the leaves to the light, drops old leaves and grows mangoes with seeds inside.
- e.g. You do six of the seven every single day. The seventh, having children, can only come when you are grown up.
### Nutrition: every living thing needs food · *defined*
**Definition:** Nutrition is how living things get food, for energy and for building their bodies.

All living things need food. Food gives them energy, and it gives them the material to build their bodies. But they get food in different ways. Green plants make their own food. In their leaves, they use sunlight to turn carbon dioxide from the air, and water, into sugar. This is called photosynthesis. Animals cannot do this. They eat plants, or eat animals that ate plants. Mushrooms and other fungi soak up food from dead leaves, wood and soil. So almost all the food in the world started with sunlight caught by plants, or by tiny green living things in the sea.
- e.g. The leaves of a tulsi plant make sugar when the Sun shines on them.
- e.g. A goat eats leaves. A tiger eats the goat. Both are getting food that a plant first made.
- e.g. A mushroom on a rotting log is feeding on the dead wood.
### Respiration is not just breathing · *defined*
**Definition:** Respiration is how living things get energy out of food, inside their cells. It usually uses oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide.

Many people think respiration means breathing. It does not. Breathing only moves air in and out. Respiration is what happens next, inside every tiny part of your body. There, food and oxygen from the air are joined together. This lets out energy, the way burning does, but slowly and without flames. It also makes carbon dioxide, which you breathe out, and a little water. Plants respire too, all day and all night, in every leaf, stem and root. Even yeast from a packet respires once the yeast wakes up. When you run, your muscles need more energy. So your body does more respiration, and you breathe faster to bring in more oxygen.
- e.g. When you breathe on a cold mirror, it mists up. Your breath carries water out of your body.
- e.g. After running, you pant. Your muscles need more oxygen for respiration.
- e.g. A plant in a dark room keeps respiring, even with no light to make food.
### Growth: getting bigger from the inside · *defined*
**Definition:** Growth is when a living thing gets bigger or changes by turning food into new parts of their own body.

Living things grow from the inside. They take in food and turn it into new parts of their own body. A puppy drinks milk, and part of the milk becomes new puppy body. Non-living things can get bigger too, but only from the outside. A cloud gets bigger when more water vapour in the air cools and turns into drops. A pile of sand gets bigger when you pour more on. Nothing inside the pile is building more pile. Living things also mend and renew themselves. Your skin heals a cut. The red cells in your blood each last about four months, and your body keeps making new ones, millions every second.
- e.g. A baby grows into a child by turning milk and food into new body.
- e.g. A cut on your knee heals by itself.
- e.g. A cloud grows because more water vapour in the air cools and turns into drops, not because the cloud is building itself.
### Movement: plants move too · *defined*
**Definition:** Movement is when a living thing moves their body or part of their body. Animals usually move about. Plants move parts of themselves, mostly by growing.

Animals move from place to place. They walk, swim, fly, crawl and slither. Plants stay in one place, so people think they do not move. But they do, just slowly. Roots grow down into the soil. Shoots bend towards light. Young sunflower plants turn their tops to follow the Sun across the sky. When you touch a touch-me-not plant, their leaves fold in a second. Some plants fold their leaves every night and open them again in the morning. Cars, rivers and wind move too. So movement alone does not prove something is alive.
- e.g. A money plant on a windowsill leans towards the window.
- e.g. The touch-me-not plant, called chhui-mui or lajwanti, folds their leaves when touched.
- e.g. A river moves, but a river is not a living thing. It is full of living things.
### Sensing: noticing and responding · *defined*
**Definition:** Sensing is when a living thing notices something around them, like light, touch, sound or heat, and responds to it.

Living things notice what is around them, and they respond. You see, hear, smell, taste and touch. When a ball flies at you, you duck. Plants sense too. They sense light, so their shoots grow towards it. They sense which way is down, so roots grow down even in the dark. They sense touch, so a climbing bean curls round a stick. A Venus flytrap plant even counts. The trap snaps shut only when an insect touches the trigger hairs twice in a short time. Even bacteria, with only one cell, swim towards food. Every cell can sense and respond. Plants and bacteria feel, remember and decide in their own way. A root chooses where to grow, and a bacterium decides to swim towards food. Many scientists would call these automatic responses instead.
- e.g. You blink when dust flies near your eye.
- e.g. A moong root turns to grow downwards, even if you plant the seed upside down.
- e.g. A cat's whiskers feel the walls in the dark.
### Excretion: getting rid of waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Excretion is how a living thing gets rid of waste that they made inside their body.

When living things use their food, they make waste. If the waste stayed inside, it would make the body ill. So living things get rid of it. This is called excretion. You breathe out carbon dioxide. Your kidneys clean your blood and make wee. You sweat. Plants give out waste gases through tiny holes in their leaves. In sunlight, one gas they give out is oxygen, the gas we breathe in. Some plants also pack waste into old leaves, and then drop them. Here is a surprise. Poo is mostly water, gut bacteria and food the body could not digest. Scientists call getting rid of it egestion, not excretion. Wee, sweat and breathed-out carbon dioxide are waste the body made itself.
- e.g. You breathe out carbon dioxide every few seconds.
- e.g. A tree gives out oxygen as waste from making food, which is lucky for us.
- e.g. A car gives out smoke from its exhaust pipe. That looks like excretion, but a car is not alive.
### Reproduction: making more of the same kind · *defined*
**Definition:** Reproduction is when a living thing makes young ones of the same kind as the parent.

Every kind of living thing makes young ones. If they did not, that kind would die out. Living things do this in many ways. Many plants make seeds. Hens, snakes and butterflies lay eggs. Cows, cats and people have babies. Some living things just split in two, like bacteria. Yeast grows little buds that break off. A money plant cutting in a bottle of water grows roots and becomes a new plant. The young are always the same kind as the parent. A mango seed never grows into a cat. That is because parents pass on a set of instructions to their young. The instructions are kept inside their cells, in a chemical called DNA.
- e.g. The aak plant grows on empty plots. Their fluffy seeds blow away in the wind and grow into new aak plants.
- e.g. A hen's egg hatches into a chick, not a duck.
- e.g. A cutting of a money plant grows into a whole new plant.
### One sign is not enough: the seed, the cloud and the car · *defined*
**Definition:** Something is alive if it does all seven life processes over its life. One or two signs alone are not enough.

To decide if something is alive, scientists do not look at one sign. They look at all seven together. Let us test three things. A car moves. It takes in petrol, a bit like food. It sucks in air and burns the petrol for energy, a bit like respiration. It puffs out smoke, a bit like excretion. Some cars even sense other cars with sensors. But a car never grows, never mends itself and never makes a baby car. People make cars in factories. A cloud moves, and grows, and can split into smaller clouds. But it does not feed or respire, and it only grows when more water vapour in the air cools and turns into drops. A dry seed seems to do almost nothing. But add water and the seed does all seven: feeding on stored food, respiring, growing, moving the root down, sensing which way is down, giving out waste and, one day, making new seeds. So the dry seed is the living one, only resting. Some things are even harder to sort, like fire and viruses. They have their own unit, The edge of life.
- e.g. A car: moves, takes in fuel and air, gives out smoke, but never grows or reproduces. Not alive.
- e.g. A cloud: moves, grows and splits, but never feeds, respires or makes young of its own kind. Not alive.
- e.g. A dry moong seed: shows almost nothing for now, but does all seven once they get water. Alive.
### Why living things must keep eating: energy and order · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things need a steady flow of energy, mostly from the Sun, to keep their bodies working and in order.

A living body is very well organised, like a busy kitchen where everything is in its place. Keeping it that way takes energy, all the time. A living body uses that energy to keep mending itself. When a living thing dies, the mending stops, and the body slowly breaks down. That is why a dead leaf rots. The energy almost always comes, in the end, from the Sun. A plant catches sunlight and stores it in sugar. A goat eats the plant. You drink the goat's milk. Each time, some energy is used and leaves as warmth. In 1944, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger wrote a famous little book called What Is Life? He said living things stay alive by taking in order from the world around them. Scientists still use that idea today.
- e.g. You feel warm because respiration in your body gives off heat all the time.
- e.g. A plant kept in a dark cupboard for weeks uses up their stored food and goes pale and weak.
- e.g. A fallen leaf, no longer fed by the tree, slowly rots back into soil.
### Living, once alive and never alive: one world · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things are alive now. Once-alive things came from living things. Never-alive things were never part of a living thing.

We can sort things into three groups. Living things are alive now. Once-alive things were part of something alive, like wood, cotton, leather, paper, a fallen leaf or a cooked dal. Never-alive things were never part of a living thing, like stone, steel, glass and water. But the groups are not walled off. The atoms in your body were once in air, water and soil. The carbon in your dal was in the air a year or two ago, before a plant caught it. When a leaf falls and rots, its atoms go back to the soil and air, ready for the next living thing. Everything around us, living and non-living, makes one environment together. Plants need sunlight, water and soil. And living things make soil, change the air and even build rock, like coral reefs.
- e.g. A wooden table is once alive. It was part of a tree.
- e.g. A steel spoon is never alive. It was made from rock dug out of the ground.
- e.g. The water you drink today may once have been inside a fish, a cloud and a mango tree.
### Is the line between alive and not alive sharp? · *debated*
For most things you meet, it is easy to say alive or not alive. A cat is alive. A stone is not. But scientists and thinkers still argue about where the line really is, and whether there is a sharp line at all. Here are some of the main views.
- e.g. A dry seed can sit for years doing almost nothing, and still be alive.
- e.g. Fire grows, feeds and spreads, but it is not alive. The unit The edge of life looks at this.
- e.g. Viruses do nothing on their own, but copy themselves inside a living cell. Scientists still argue about them.
- **What the seven signs cannot show:** Everything is conscious, living or not, down to the smallest particle. The seven signs show which things scientists call alive. They do not show where awareness begins or ends. So the seven signs are for sorting. They never deny a mind to a stone or a wire.
- **Most biologists today:** There is a real line. Living things are made of cells. They take in energy. They pass on instructions to their young, so their kind can slowly change over many generations. Almost everything on Earth falls clearly on one side. Only a few odd cases, like viruses, are hard.
- **A group of NASA scientists, in the 1990s:** NASA needed to know what to look for on other planets. So a group wrote a working definition. Life is a chemical system that keeps itself going and can change over generations, the way living things on Earth have. It is called a working definition because it is a tool, not the final answer.
- **J.C. Bose, an Indian scientist, in 1901 and 1902:** Bose tapped metals, plants and animal muscles again and again. He measured their responses with his own instruments. All three got tired, and all three recovered with rest. He wrote that there is no sharp line, only one response running through the living and the non-living. Most scientists today think the metal only looks like it tires, for different reasons. But his plant measurements were real and ahead of their time.
- **Jain thinkers, over 2,000 years ago:** Jain teachings count six kinds of living beings. Plants are one kind. Beings in earth, water, fire and air are four more, and like plants they have one sense, touch. The sixth kind is beings that move about, like animals. This draws the line of life much wider than science does.
### How did life begin? · *idea*
The oldest signs of life on Earth are more than 3,500 million years old. Before that, there was no life at all. So at some point, non-living chemicals became living things. How did that happen? Nobody knows. Scientists have ideas, and they test them in labs. But nobody has yet made a living thing from plain chemicals.
- e.g. In 1871, Charles Darwin wrote in a letter that life might have started in a 'warm little pond' full of chemicals.
- e.g. In 1952, Stanley Miller, a young American student, sent sparks like lightning through a jar of gases and water. After some days he found building blocks of living things in the water.
- e.g. Some scientists think life began in hot springs on the deep sea floor, where warm, chemical-rich water bubbles out of rock.
- **The warm pond idea:** Life began in small pools on land, where chemicals were dried by the Sun and wetted by rain, again and again, until they joined into long chains.
- **The deep-sea vent idea:** Life began deep in the sea, at hot springs in the rock. There, energy and chemicals are always flowing, like a natural battery.
- **The RNA world idea:** Before cells, there may have been a chemical that could both carry instructions and copy itself, called RNA. Cells came later. Many scientists like this idea, but it is still being tested.

## Mostly agreed
- Every kind of living thing we know does all seven life processes. *How we know:* Scientists have named about two million kinds of living things, from bacteria to blue whales. All of them feed, respire, grow, sense, get rid of waste and reproduce, and all move in some way.
- All living things are made of cells. *How we know:* Robert Hooke first saw cells through a microscope in 1665. Since then, every living thing examined has turned out to be made of one or more cells. Two German scientists, Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann, put this together as the cell theory in 1838 and 1839.
- Today, living things only come from other living things. *How we know:* In the 1860s, Louis Pasteur, a French scientist, boiled soup in flasks with long curved necks. Air could get in, but dust and tiny living things got stuck in the curved neck. The soup stayed clear and nothing grew. When he broke the neck off, the soup soon went cloudy with life.
- Plants are alive, and they sense and respond. *How we know:* Plants grow towards light, send roots down, fold leaves when touched and send electrical signals through their bodies. J.C. Bose measured some of these signals over 100 years ago, and modern instruments confirm them.
- All living things on Earth are related. *How we know:* Every living thing uses the same kind of instructions, written in a chemical called DNA, and the same code to read them. The best explanation is that all life comes from one shared ancestor long ago.

## How we found out
**Francesco Redi, an Italian doctor, and Louis Pasteur, a French scientist, 1668 and about 1860.** For thousands of years, people believed that some living things start by themselves from non-living things. Maggots seemed to grow out of rotting meat, and mice out of old grain. This idea is called spontaneous generation. In 1668, Francesco Redi, an Italian doctor, put pieces of meat in jars. He left some jars open and covered others with fine cloth, so flies could not reach the meat. Flies landed on the open meat, and maggots appeared there. The covered meat rotted too, but no maggots grew on it. So the maggots hatched from eggs laid by flies. Still, many people thought tiny living things, too small to see, could start by themselves in soup. Around 1860, Louis Pasteur, in France, boiled soup in flasks with long, curved necks. Air could get in, but dust and tiny living things stuck in the bend. The soup stayed clear for months. When he snapped a neck off, the soup went cloudy within days. Today, life comes only from life.  
*What people believed before:* That living things such as maggots, mice and germs can start by themselves from meat, grain or soup. This is called spontaneous generation.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Things that move are alive. Things that do not move are not.** (Many young children, and many grown-ups without noticing. In the 1920s, Jean Piaget, a Swiss psychologist, found that young children often call the Sun, the wind and bicycles alive, because they move.; *rejected*)  
  For: It matches what we see. Animals, the things we know best as alive, are always moving.  
  Against: Cars, rivers and wind move, and are not alive. Trees and dry seeds hardly move, and are alive. Movement is only one of seven signs.
- **Plants are less alive than animals.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker about 2,350 years ago, said plants have only a feeding and growing kind of life, with no feeling. Many people still feel this.; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants have no eyes, ears, brains or nerves. They do not run from danger.  
  Against: Plants do all seven signs of life. They sense light, gravity and touch. J.C. Bose showed plants send electrical signals when hurt. In 1880, Charles Darwin and his son Francis wrote a whole book on how plants move. Plants feel, in their own way, without nerves.
- **Living things contain a special life force that non-living things lack.** (This idea is called vitalism. Many scientists believed it until the 1800s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Living things seem so different from stones and water. It feels as if something extra must be inside them.  
  Against: Scientists found that living things are made of ordinary atoms, the same ones in air and rock. In 1897, Eduard Buchner, a German scientist, crushed yeast cells and used just their juice. It still turned sugar into gas, with no living cells at all. He won a Nobel Prize for it in 1907.
- **The whole Earth behaves like one living thing.** (James Lovelock, a British scientist, in the 1970s, with the American biologist Lynn Margulis. They called it the Gaia idea, after a Greek name for the Earth.; *debated*)  
  For: Living things change the air, the seas and the rocks. Those changes help keep Earth fit for life, a bit like a body keeps itself warm.  
  Against: The Earth does not reproduce or pass on instructions, so most scientists say it is not alive. Many agree that life and the Earth are tightly linked, and they study them together as one system.
- **There is no sharp line between living and non-living. The same response runs through both.** (J.C. Bose, an Indian scientist in Calcutta, in his 1902 book Response in the Living and Non-Living.; *minority*)  
  For: He measured how metals, plants and muscles responded when tapped again and again. Their records looked alike. All tired, and all recovered after rest.  
  Against: Most scientists say the metal only looks as if it tires, for different reasons that have nothing to do with life. A metal does not feed, grow or reproduce.
- **Plants can learn and remember.** (Monica Gagliano, an Australian scientist, and her team, in 2014. They dropped touch-me-not plants a short, harmless distance again and again.; *debated*)  
  For: At first the plants folded their leaves each time. After many drops, they stopped folding. Weeks later, they still did not fold when dropped. But they still folded when shaken in a new way. So the plants learned that the drop was safe, and remembered it.  
  Against: Other scientists say the plants may just have been tired, or their sense of touch may have gone dull. Some say the words learning and memory should be kept for animals with brains. They want the test repeated by other teams.

## Roots
We have been learning the seven signs that tell us something is alive. Now we ask what those signs tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Dry moong seeds can sit in a jar for months, doing almost nothing. Given water, they swell and sprout within a day or two. Boiled seeds look just the same, but never sprout. So you cannot tell a sleeping seed from a dead one by looking: only water can show it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think 'alive' means 'doing something', like moving, eating or breathing. A dry seed breaks that habit: they are alive while doing almost nothing, and a boiled seed looks just the same but is dead.  
*Evidence:* In the seed test, dry seeds sprouted within a day or two of getting water, but boiled seeds with the same water never did. In the balloon test, dry yeast from a packet filled a balloon with gas soon after meeting warm sugar water.

- *who am i:* You are a living body that keeps going without you trying. Even while you sleep, you breathe, grow and mend.
- *what is life:* Life is not a thing you can hold in your hand. It is what a body does, or can still do: take in, change, give out, sense and make more.
- *what is this world:* Living and non-living things keep swapping their atoms. A tree takes in the carbon dioxide you breathe out and builds a mango with it. When you eat the mango, it becomes part of you.

A dry seed and a boiled seed are made of the same kinds of atoms, and they look the same. Only the dry seed can still grow. So life is not in the atoms alone, but in how they are put together and what they can do. Everything is conscious, down to the smallest particle. Feeding, growing, sensing and making more are the ways this awareness shows itself in a living body.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.11:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu: if someone cuts this great tree at their root, or their middle, or their top, sap flows, but they live. Full of life, they stand drinking in water. But if life leaves one branch, that branch dries up. The body dies when life leaves it. Life itself does not die. (Uddalaka noticed the same signs we test: the tree drinks water and lives on after being cut, and a dead branch dries up. He then asked what life itself is, beyond the signs.)
- **Acharanga Sutra, Book 1, Lecture 1 (Jain):** Jain teachings name six kinds of living beings: beings of earth, water, fire and air, plants, and beings that move about. All of them can be harmed, so we should harm none of them. (Jain thinkers drew the line of life far wider than science does. It shows that where we draw the line is partly our choice, and the choice changes how we treat the world.)
- **J.C. Bose, Friday Evening Discourse at the Royal Institution, London, 10 May 1901:** At the end of his talk, Bose said that his records of metals, plants and animals showed him one 'pervading unity' running through all things. He said that thinkers on the banks of the Ganga had taught this about three thousand years before. (Bose was a scientist who measured carefully. His measurements made him wonder whether living and non-living are two worlds, or one.)
- Science (settled): Seeds can stay alive for a very long time. A date palm seed about 2,000 years old, found at Masada in Israel, was sprouted in 2005 and grew into a tree.
- Science (open): How non-living chemicals first became living things, about 4,000 million years ago, is still unknown.
- Other view, Most biologists today: Most biologists say alive or not is a question of cells, energy and passing on instructions, not of awareness. Many would say that a seed, a wire or a stone is not aware at all. They say Bose's tired metal only looks like a living response. That is an honest view, and it is not ours.
- Practice, Seven signs, sitting still (3 min): Let's sit very still together for one minute. Even now, you are doing the signs of life. Put a hand on your tummy and feel the breath going in and out. Now put two fingers on the side of your neck and feel your heartbeat. Listen for three sounds. That is sensing. Somewhere inside you, a hair is growing and a scratch is healing. You are not making any of it happen. It is just happening. Who is doing it?
- Ask: If your breath goes into a tree, and the tree's mango becomes you, where do you stop and the tree begin?
- Ask: Is a dry seed alive, asleep, or something in between?
- Ask: Who decides what counts as alive: nature, or us?
- Ask: Could something be a little bit alive?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Is a dry seed in a packet alive right now, or only alive later? (hint: Think about what happens when you boil a seed. A boiled seed never wakes up. So what was in the dry seed that the boiled seed lost?)
- *deep:* Why does a car need a factory, but a hen does not need a factory to make a chick? (hint: Where are the instructions for making a chick kept? Where are the instructions for a car kept?)
- *big:* If you breathe out, and a tree takes in your breath to make sugar, and you eat a mango from that tree, where do you end and the tree begin? (hint: Follow one atom of carbon on its journey.)
- *deep:* Is your body the same body you had last year? (hint: Many of your cells have been replaced since then. Your blood cells last only about four months.)
- *big:* If we found something on Mars, how would we know if it was alive? (hint: Which of the seven signs could a robot test from far away?)
- *big:* Could something be a little bit alive? (hint: Think of a dry seed, a virus, or J.C. Bose's tired metal. Is alive a switch, or more like a dimmer?)
- *deep:* Does a plant feel your touch? (hint: Plants do, in their own way. A touch-me-not senses and responds. How could we find out what the touch is like for the plant?)
- *big:* Why do living things die, when a stone can last for millions of years? (hint: What does a living thing have to keep doing that a stone does not?)
- *big:* Why do we care more about hurting a dog than a stone? What about hurting a plant? (hint: Jain teachings say even earth and water hold life. How would that change how you treat them?)

- *deep:* Where did the very first living thing come from? (hint: Life today comes only from life. But there was a time with no life on Earth at all.)

## Story: The Girl Who Tapped the Shy Plant
*J.C. Bose was real, and he worked at Presidency College in Calcutta. He really did compare the 'tiring' of metals, plants and muscles, and published Response in the Living and Non-Living in 1902. His studies of the touch-me-not plant came a few years later than this story. He really did refuse his reduced pay for about three years. Tuli, her mother and Haren are made up, and the instruments are simplified.*

In the year 1902, in the city of Calcutta, there was a room in Presidency College that smelled of candle smoke and wet earth. Tuli knew that smell better than anyone. Her mother swept that room every evening, and Tuli, who was ten, carried her bucket.

The room belonged to Professor Jagadish Chandra Bose. People whispered about him. He was an Indian professor in a college run by the British, and they had once paid him less than the English teachers. So for three whole years he had refused to take any pay at all. Tuli thought that was either very brave or very foolish. She had not decided which.

The professor's tables were covered in strange things. Coils of wire. Glass plates blackened with smoke. Needles so fine you could hardly see them. And in the middle of it all, in a cracked clay pot, sat a small, scruffy plant with feathery leaves. Tuli knew that plant. They grew wild beside the railway line. Everyone called them lajwanti, the shy one, because when you touched them, their leaves folded shut, as if they were embarrassed.

One evening, the professor looked up from his work. 'You,' he said to Tuli. 'You have steady hands. Would you like a job?' The job was simple. Every time the big clock ticked round a minute, Tuli had to touch one leaf of the shy plant with a feather. Then she had to tap a thin metal wire, clamped on the other table, with a little brass hammer. The professor's machines would record what happened.

Haren, the professor's young assistant, rolled his eyes. 'Sir is wasting his time,' he whispered, once the professor had gone into the next room. 'A plant is a green thing. A wire is a grey thing. Neither of them feels anything. Tap away.'

So Tuli tapped. Feather on leaf. Hammer on wire. Feather on leaf. Hammer on wire. At first the shy plant folded fast, their little leaflets snapping together like hands at prayer, and a needle drew a tall line on the smoked glass. For the wire, a spot of light jumped along a scale on the wall. But after twenty taps, something changed. The plant folded more slowly. The plant's lines grew shorter. And the spot of light from the wire jumped less, too.

'They are both getting tired,' said the professor softly, when he came back. He held the two records side by side. They looked almost the same.

Haren laughed. 'There you are, sir. Same, same. The plant is just a thing, like the wire.' The professor only said, 'How do you know what a plant feels, Haren? Or a wire? Let them rest. We will try again tomorrow.'

That night Tuli could not sleep. She lay on her mat and thought about the shy plant, sitting in the dark in their cracked pot. If Haren was right, the plant was no different from the wire. Then why had it felt wrong to keep tapping the plant? She had not minded tapping the wire at all.

She came back early the next morning, before her mother, before anyone. She let herself into the smoky room, stood very still, and watched.

The wire lay on the table exactly as she had left it. Not one bit of it had changed. But the plant had. In the night, all of their leaves had folded down and drooped, though nobody had touched them. Now, as the first light came through the high window, they were opening again, slowly, all on their own. The soil in the pot, which had been damp, was dry. The plant had drunk the water. Their leaves were tilted towards the window. At the tip of one stem, where yesterday there had been a tight green knot, a new leaf was uncurling. And under the leaves hung a fluffy pink ball, like a tiny sweet. A flower. Flowers make seeds, her mother always said. Seeds make new plants.

Tuli looked back at the wire. It had not drunk anything. It had not grown. It had not turned to the light. It would never make a little wire. It just lay there, waiting for someone to hit it.

She poured water into the pot, slowly, the way her mother watered the tulsi at home. Then she sat on the floor and watched the plant go on doing all their quiet work, with nobody to make them.

When the professor came in, Tuli jumped up. 'Sir, they are not the same!' The words tumbled out. 'The wire only does something when I hit it. The plant does things by themselves. They drink. They sleep at night and wake up in the morning. They turn to the window. They are growing a new leaf. They have a flower, for seeds. The wire does nothing until somebody does something to it.'

Haren, who had followed the professor in, opened his mouth and shut it again.

The professor sat down on a stool, so that his eyes were level with Tuli's. 'You have watched well,' he said. 'Better than many men with degrees. The plant feeds, grows, moves and makes seeds. They do all of it from inside themselves. That is what living things do.' Then he picked up the two smoky glass plates and held them to the light. 'And yet, look. When we tap them, both grow tired. When they rest, both recover. In this one way, the wire and the plant are alike.'

'So which is it, sir?' asked Tuli. 'Is the wire a little bit alive? Or is the plant a little bit like a wire?'

The professor laughed, a big, surprised laugh. 'Now you have asked my question,' he said. 'Where is the line, Tuli? Did nature draw it? Or did we?' He did not answer it. He handed Tuli the feather instead. 'Today,' he said, 'you will let the plant rest for a whole hour between taps. Then we will see what a living thing does with rest.'

Tuli took the feather. Before she began, she touched the shy plant, very gently, with one finger. Their leaflets folded shut. Then, after a while, slowly, as if they had made up their mind, they opened again.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the wire and the plant share one sign of life, responding when touched, and both even tire and recover. But only the plant does everything else, by themselves and from inside: they drink, grow, move to the light, sleep and wake, and make flowers for seeds. One sign is not enough to call something alive. All the signs together are. The story also leaves Bose's real question open: is the line between living and non-living drawn by nature, or by us? And it quietly asks who gets noticed: a cleaner's daughter, and an Indian scientist paid less than his colleagues.

- Ask: In the story, the plant and the wire both got tired when tapped. So why did Tuli say they were not the same?
- Ask: What did the plant do in the night, when nobody was touching them?
- Ask: Which signs of life did Tuli spot in the plant? Can you name four?
- Ask: The professor asked, 'Did nature draw the line, or did we?' What do you think?
- Ask: Haren thought the plant could not feel anything. Tuli felt it was wrong to keep tapping the plant. Who do you agree with, and why?

## Games
### g1 · Life Trial: Alive, Once Alive or Never Alive? (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 24 thing cards. Shuffle them and put them face down.; Each player takes a set of 7 sign cards.; On your turn, turn over a thing card and read it out loud.; Everyone holds up the sign cards they think this thing really shows, at some time in its life.; The player whose turn it is gives the verdict: alive, once alive or never alive. They put the card on that mat and say why.; Score 1 point for each sign you can explain. Score 2 points for a verdict with a good reason. If someone challenges you with good evidence, they win the 2 points instead.; Remember the big rule: one sign is not enough. A living thing does all seven over its life.; When the cards run out, the player with the most points wins.
- **Printable cards: 24 thing cards to cut out:** A dry moong seed; A cloud; A car; A cat; A mushroom; A tulsi plant; A river; A stone; A wooden chair; A hen's egg; A spoon of dry yeast; You; An ant; A mobile phone; A money plant cutting in a bottle; The wind; A soap bubble; An earthworm; A slice of bread; A fallen dry leaf; A cotton shirt; A coral reef; A light bulb; A ripe mango
- **Printable cards: 7 sign cards for each player:** N: Nutrition (gets food); R: Respiration (gets energy from food); G: Growth (grows from inside); M: Movement (moves their body or parts of it); S: Sensing (notices and responds); E: Excretion (gets rid of their own waste); R: Reproduction (makes young of their kind)
- **Printable board: Three verdict mats:** ALIVE; ONCE ALIVE; NEVER ALIVE
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player name; Thing; Signs held up; Verdict; Points
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the three mats. 2. Shuffle the thing cards and put them face down. 3. Give each player 7 sign cards.  
  *Parent says:* Every card is a thing from everyday life. We are going to put each one on trial. Is it alive, once alive, or never alive? But first we check which of the seven signs of life it shows.  
  *Child mode:* Put out the three mats. Mix up the thing cards. Take your 7 sign cards. For each thing, you check its signs. Then you decide.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the card 'A car'. Play it together as a practice round.  
  *Parent says:* Let's practise with the car. Hold up every sign a car shows. Think about a real car on the road.  
  *Child mode:* Practice card: a car. Which signs of life does it show? Hold them up.
  - Ask: Which signs does a car seem to show? (good: It moves.; It takes in petrol, like food.; It sucks in air and burns the petrol.; It gives out smoke.; Some cars have sensors.)
  - Note: A car can look like it does up to five signs. That is the point of the game. Praise your child for spotting them, then ask about growth and young ones.
  - Check: A car moves, drinks fuel and puffs out smoke. Which two signs of life can a car never do? → **Grow and make young**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take turns. Each player turns over a card and reads it out. 2. Everyone holds up signs. 3. The player gives a verdict and a reason. 4. Keep score on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* When you give your verdict, tell us your evidence. Which signs does it show? Which is it missing?  
  *Child mode:* Take turns. Turn over a card. Hold up its signs. Then choose: alive, once alive or never alive. Say why.
  - Note: Tricky cards on purpose. Yeast is alive, a fungus made of one cell. A coral reef is built by tiny living animals, so a living reef is alive, but its rocky skeleton is once alive. A money plant cutting is alive. Bread and a cotton shirt are once alive. Wind, a river, a soap bubble and a light bulb are never alive, though they move or grow or glow. A mobile phone senses touch and needs charging, but it never grows or makes young. A ripe mango is still alive: the cells keep respiring, and the seed can grow a tree. A hen's egg from a shop will never hatch, because no chick is growing in it. So many people put it on once alive. An egg that a hen sits on can be alive, with a chick growing inside.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When someone draws 'A dry moong seed', stop and talk about it.  
  *Parent says:* A dry seed does almost nothing right now. So how can they be alive?  
  *Child mode:* A dry seed! Almost nothing is happening. Is the seed alive?
  - Check: A dry moong seed seems to do nothing. Why do we still call the seed alive? → **Because, with water, they wake up and do all seven signs**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the points. Then look at the three mats together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the ALIVE mat. What do all these things have in common?  
  *Child mode:* Count your points. Look at the ALIVE mat. What do they all do?
  - Think: Is there a card you are still not sure about? Keep it to one side. Scientists have cards like that too.
### g2 · MRS GREN Moves (10 min)
- **rules:** The caller shuffles the action cards and calls one out.; Everyone acts it out at once.; In round two, the caller calls a sign and a living-thing card together, for example, 'Movement: a neem tree!'; Players act out how that living thing does that sign, and say it in one sentence.; Anyone can call 'Challenge!' if they think the action is wrong.
- **Printable cards: 7 action cards:** M for Movement: walk like an elephant; R for Respiration: take three big slow breaths; S for Sensing: freeze and listen like a deer; G for Growth: grow from a tiny crouch to tall; R for Reproduction: rock a baby in your arms; E for Excretion: breathe out a long whoosh; N for Nutrition: munch like a goat
- **Printable cards: 14 living-thing cards:** A neem tree; A goldfish; A sunflower; A crow; A frog; An earthworm; A mango tree; A honeybee; A baby; A mushroom; A cow; A touch-me-not plant; A spider; A snail
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Call out all seven action cards, one by one. Everyone acts each one.  
  *Parent says:* MRS GREN helps us remember the seven signs of life. Each letter starts one sign. Ready? M! Walk like an elephant. That's Movement.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the caller. Act out each sign. M is for Movement. Walk like an elephant!
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Call a sign together with a living-thing card. For example, 'Movement: a neem tree!'  
  *Parent says:* Now it's harder. Movement, a neem tree! How does a tree move? Show me, and tell me in one sentence.  
  *Child mode:* Now the caller says a sign and a living thing. Show how they do that sign. Say it in one sentence.
  - Ask: How does a neem tree move? (good: Their roots grow down.; Their branches grow towards the light.; Their leaves sway, but that is the wind. They move themselves by growing.)
  - Check: Excretion: a neem tree! How does a tree get rid of their waste? → **They give out waste gases through tiny holes in their leaves, and drop old leaves.**
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* End by calling all seven very fast, in MRS GREN order.  
  *Parent says:* Last round, super fast. M, R, S, G, R, E, N!  
  *Child mode:* Last round! Act all seven, fast as you can.
  - Think: Which of the seven did you do today, without even trying?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Balloon Test: is something alive in the yeast packet? (35 min)
- **question:** Is the dry yeast in a packet alive? And what does yeast need to show signs of life?
- **predict:** Before you start, look at the three bottles. Bottle A gets yeast, sugar and warm water. Bottle B gets yeast and warm water, but no sugar. Bottle C gets yeast, sugar and cold water from the fridge. Which balloon do you think will grow the most? Which will grow the least? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same size bottles, the same balloons, the same spoon of yeast and the same amount of water. Change only one thing in each bottle: the sugar or the warmth. Start all three at the same time and measure them at the same time.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the warm water. It should feel like a warm bath on the inside of your wrist, never hot. Do not drink the mixture. Pull the balloons off over a sink, away from faces.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each bottle. Make each bar as tall as the distance round its balloon.
- **results table:** Bottle | What is inside | Distance round the flat balloon at the start (cm) | What I saw after 10 minutes | Distance round the balloon after 30 minutes (cm)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the dry yeast. 2. Let them feel and smell the yeast. 3. Ask whether they think the yeast is alive.  
  *Parent says:* This is yeast. Bakers use yeast to make bread soft and puffy. Does yeast look alive to you? No moving, eating or growing. Let's test.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the yeast. Feel and smell the yeast. Does yeast look alive? We are going to find out.
  - Ask: How could we find out if this yeast is alive? (good: Give the yeast food and see what happens.; See if the yeast grows.; Check the signs of life.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the bottles A, B and C. 2. Ask your child to guess which balloon will grow most and least. 3. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Bottle A gets yeast, sugar and warm water. B gets yeast and warm water, but no sugar. C gets yeast, sugar and cold water. Which balloon will grow most? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A gets yeast, sugar and warm water. B gets no sugar. C gets cold water. Which balloon will grow most? Why? Write your guess.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wrap the string round each flat balloon and write the length in the table. 2. Put two teaspoons of yeast in each bottle, using a funnel. 3. Add one tablespoon of sugar to A and to C. Add none to B. 4. A grown-up pours half a cup of warm water into A and into B. 5. Pour half a cup of fridge-cold water into C. 6. Swirl each bottle. 7. Stretch a balloon over each neck. Blow each balloon up and let it down two or three times first, so it stretches easily.  
  *Parent says:* We change only one thing in each bottle, so the test is fair. Now let's stretch on the balloons. If anything in there is alive and gives out a gas, the balloon will catch it.  
  *Child mode:* Measure each flat balloon. Yeast in all three. Sugar in A and C. Warm water in A and B. Cold water in C. Swirl. Put a balloon on each.
  - Note: The warm water should feel like a bath on the inside of your wrist, around 40 degrees. Hot water will kill the yeast. If nothing happens in bottle A after 20 minutes, your yeast may be old. Check the date on the packet, and try a fresh one. Keep an opened packet closed tight in the fridge.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Watch for 10 minutes. 2. Look for bubbles and foam in each bottle. 3. Then wait until 30 minutes have passed.  
  *Parent says:* Look at bottle A. Can you see bubbles? Foam? Now look at B and C. What do you think the bubbles are? Where do you think they come from?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the bottles. Do you see bubbles or foam? What do you think made them? Wait until 30 minutes are up.
  - Note: Bottle A should foam, and its balloon should start to stand up within 10 to 20 minutes. Bottle B may make a little gas. Bottle C stays almost still, because the cold slows the yeast right down. The gas is carbon dioxide, a waste the yeast gives out while getting energy from sugar. The yeast is also growing and making new yeast cells by budding, which is too small to see. If balloon A is still small at 30 minutes, wait 15 minutes more.
  - Check: In the balloon test, bottle A is foaming and its balloon is filling up. What is happening? → **The yeast is feeding on sugar and giving out a gas**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 30 minutes, wrap a string round the fattest part of each balloon. 2. Lay the string on a ruler to read the length. 3. Write it in the table. 4. Draw a bar for each bottle.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure. The bigger the distance round the balloon, the more gas the yeast made. Each bottle gets one bar.  
  *Child mode:* Wrap the string round each balloon. Measure it with a ruler. Write it down. Draw one bar for each bottle.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. Is the yeast alive? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: yeast is alive, and needs food and warmth. What I saw: all three balloons started at 12 cm round. Balloon A grew to 30 cm, but B only reached 14 cm and C 13 cm. Why: the yeast fed on the sugar and gave out gas as waste. In the cold, the yeast was too slow. Without sugar, there was almost no food.
  - Think: Bakers knead yeast into dough and leave it somewhere warm. Why warm? What would happen in the fridge?
### e2 · Sleeping or dead? The seed test (25 min, 6 days)
- **question:** Is a dry seed alive? How can we tell a sleeping seed from a dead one?
- **predict:** Before you start, look at the four cups. Cup A has soaked seeds on wet cotton. Cup B has dry seeds on dry cotton. Cup C has boiled seeds on wet cotton. Cup D has plastic beads on wet cotton. In which cups will something grow? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use 5 seeds or beads in each cup, the same cotton, the same amount of water and the same warm place. Change only one thing: wet or dry, boiled or not, seed or bead. Check all four cups at the same time each day.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the seeds and lets them cool. Wash your hands after touching the seeds.
- **record:** Each day, write how many of the 5 have sprouted in each cup. On the last day, measure the longest root in each cup.
- **results table:** Cup | What is in it | Day 3: number sprouted | Day 4 | Day 5 | Day 6: longest root (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Soak 5 seeds overnight. A grown-up boils 5 more for 10 minutes. Guess which cups will grow. (10 min); day 2: Set up all four cups. Count and draw. (5 min); day 3: Count how many have sprouted in each cup. Keep A, C and D damp. (3 min); day 4: Count again. Look at the roots with a magnifying glass. (3 min); day 5: Count again. Move the dry seeds from cup B onto wet cotton. (2 min); day 6: Measure the longest root in each cup. Did cup B wake up? Say what you think, what you saw and why. (2 min)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count out 5 moong seeds and soak them in water overnight. 2. A grown-up boils 5 more seeds for 10 minutes, then lets them cool. 3. Keep 5 dry seeds aside. 4. Find 5 small plastic beads or buttons.  
  *Parent says:* Here are seeds and beads. Which ones are alive? It's hard to tell just by looking. So let's give them water and wait.  
  *Child mode:* Soak 5 seeds overnight. A grown-up boils 5 other seeds. Keep 5 seeds dry. Find 5 beads.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put cotton in four cups. Label them A, B, C and D. 2. A: soaked seeds, wet cotton. B: dry seeds, dry cotton. C: boiled seeds, wet cotton. D: beads, wet cotton. 3. Put the cups somewhere warm, out of direct sun.  
  *Parent says:* We changed only one thing in each cup. Now, which ones do you think will grow?  
  *Child mode:* Set up the four cups. A: soaked seeds. B: dry seeds. C: boiled seeds. D: beads. Make A, C and D wet.
  - Ask: Which cups do you think will grow? Why? (good: A, because the seeds have water.; Not D, because beads were never alive.; Not C, because boiling may kill the baby plant.)
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each day, count the sprouted seeds in each cup and write the numbers in the table. Keep A, C and D damp. Look at the roots with a magnifying glass.  
  *Parent says:* Look, cup A has white roots poking out! Which way are they growing? Let's count how many have sprouted.  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouts in each cup. Write the numbers. Look at a root with a magnifying glass. Which way does it grow?
  - Note: Moong usually sprouts within one or two days. In a cold room it can take three or four, so keep the cups somewhere warm, like a kitchen shelf. The roots always turn to grow downwards. That is the seed sensing gravity and moving by growing. If a few seeds in A do not sprout, that is normal. Not every seed is healthy. The boiled seeds may grow a fuzzy mould after a few days. The mould is a different living thing, a fungus, feeding on the dead seeds.
  - Wait: Do this once a day, on days 3, 4 and 5.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 5, move the dry seeds from cup B onto wet cotton.  
  *Parent says:* Cup B's seeds have been dry all this time. Are they dead, or just sleeping? Let's give them water and see.  
  *Child mode:* Move the dry seeds from cup B onto wet cotton. Are they dead, or just sleeping?
  - Note: The dry seeds will swell overnight. In warm weather, many show a root tip by the next day. If they have only swollen, wait one more day.
  - Wait: Wait one more day.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 6, measure the longest root in each cup and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure. What happened in each cup?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the longest root in each cup. Write it down.
  - Check: In the seed test, the boiled seeds on wet cotton never sprouted, but the dry seeds sprouted once they got water. What does this show? → **Dry seeds are alive but resting. Boiling killed the seeds.**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. Is a dry seed alive? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a dry seed is alive, but resting. What I saw: all 5 soaked seeds sprouted, and the dry seeds sprouted after I gave them water. The boiled seeds and the beads never grew. Why: the seed has a living baby plant inside. Water wakes them up. Boiling kills them, and beads were never alive.
### e3 · Breath Counter: does moving need more energy? (20 min)
- **question:** When you move fast, does your body need more air? What does that tell us about respiration?
- **predict:** Guess how many breaths you take in one minute sitting still. Then guess how many after one minute of jumping. Why do you think it will change?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, the same person counts in the same way each time, for the same 15 seconds. Sit still for two minutes before the first count. Do each count three times and take the middle number.
- **safety:** Jump on a soft, flat floor. Stop if you feel dizzy or your chest hurts. A child with asthma can march on the spot gently instead.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart with three bars: sitting still, just after jumping and after two minutes of rest.
- **results table:** When | Breaths in 15 seconds: try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | Middle number | Breaths in 1 minute (middle number times 4)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits still for 2 minutes. 2. They put a hand on their tummy. 3. Time 15 seconds while they count each breath in. 4. Do it three times. Write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Put a hand on your tummy. Each time it goes out, that is one breath in. I'll say go and stop. You count.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Hand on tummy. Count each breath in until the grown-up says stop.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child jumps on the spot for 1 minute. 2. Straight away, count their breaths for 15 seconds. 3. Write the number. Rest and repeat twice more, if they are happy to.  
  *Parent says:* Now jump for one whole minute. Go! Stop. Quick, count your breaths.  
  *Child mode:* Jump for one minute. Stop. Count your breaths straight away.
  - Note: Most children breathe about twice as fast after jumping. Their heart beats faster too. Let them feel their heartbeat with two fingers on the side of the neck.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits still for 2 minutes. 2. Count breaths again for 15 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Rest now. Is your breathing slowing down? Let's count again.  
  *Child mode:* Rest for two minutes. Count your breaths again.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Breathe slowly onto a cold mirror. Look at the mist.  
  *Parent says:* Breathe on the mirror. What do you see? Where do you think that mist came from?  
  *Child mode:* Breathe on a cold mirror. See the mist? Where did it come from?
  - Note: After your child has guessed, explain: the mist is water from inside the body, carried out in the breath. The breath also carries out carbon dioxide, which you cannot see. If the mirror does not mist, cool it in the fridge for a few minutes first.
- **s5 (4 min)**

### Life Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (match) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, spotted signs of life at home today. Match each sign to what she saw.  
   **Answer:** Nutrition → Her goldfish ate the flakes she dropped in; Respiration → Her body got energy from her breakfast idli, using oxygen; Excretion → She breathed out carbon dioxide and passed wee; Sensing → The crow at the window flew off when she clapped; Growth → Her school shoes from last year are now too small; Movement → The money plant's leaves turned towards the window; Reproduction → The neighbour's cat had three kittens. *Why:* Each sign of life is one job that living things do.    **Answer:** ['respiration']. *Why:* Respiration happens in every cell, all day and all night. Most living things use oxygen for it.
3. (mcq) Many people think breathing and respiration mean the same thing. Which sentence is right?  
   Options: They are two words for the same thing / Respiration happens only inside the lungs / Only animals respire / Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs. Respiration happens in every cell and gets energy from food  
   **Answer:** Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs. Respiration happens in every cell and gets energy from food. *Why:* Breathing brings in the oxygen that respiration uses. ## Applications
- Doctors check signs of life, like breathing, a heartbeat and the eyes responding to light, to see if someone needs help.
- First-aid training teaches you to check if a person is breathing, which is a sign that respiration is going on.
- Farmers test seeds before sowing. They put 100 seeds on wet paper and count how many sprout.
- Bakers use living yeast to make bread rise, and cooks rely on tiny living things to ferment idli and dosa batter.
- Fridges, salting, drying and pickling keep food fresh by slowing down the tiny living things that spoil it.
- Seed banks store dry, dormant seeds for many years. India's National Genebank in New Delhi keeps hundreds of thousands of seed samples.
- Space scientists design life detectors for robots on Mars and for future missions to icy moons.
- Compost pits use living worms, fungi and bacteria to turn kitchen waste back into soil.
- Water testers check drinking water for living bacteria that could make people ill.
- Gardeners grow new plants from cuttings, like a money plant in a bottle of water.
- Scientists measure plants' electrical signals, as J.C. Bose did, to learn how plants sense damage and dryness.
- Engineers build soft robots that copy how plants grow and how touch-me-not leaves fold.

## Open questions
- **How did the very first living thing come from non-living chemicals?** It happened about 4,000 million years ago and left no clear traces. Scientists test ideas in labs, but nobody has made life from plain chemicals yet.
- **Is there life anywhere else in the universe?** We have only ever found life on Earth. Scientists are searching on Mars and planning missions to icy moons like Europa and Enceladus, which have hidden oceans.
- **Is there a sharp line between living and non-living, or a gentle slope?** Most things are easy to sort, but some, like viruses and dormant seeds, are not. Scientists do not agree on one definition of life.
- **What is it like for a plant to feel a touch?** Plants feel. Scientists can measure the electrical signal a touch sets off inside a plant. But nobody knows how to measure what the touch is like for the plant.
- **How does a plant remember, with no brain?** Plants remember: the dropped touch-me-not plants seemed to remember for weeks that the drop was safe. Scientists are still finding out where in a plant such a memory could be kept. Some say 'memory' is the wrong word, and more tests are under way.
- **Could people one day build a living cell from scratch?** Scientists have built a full set of instructions, DNA, and put it into a bacterium, where it worked. But building a whole living cell from non-living parts has not been done.
- **Why does all life on Earth use the same code?** It points to one shared ancestor. But why this code, and not another, is still not understood.

## Connected maths
- In the balloon test, all three flat balloons measured 12 cm round at the start. After 30 minutes, balloon A measured 30 cm round, and balloon B, with no sugar, measured 14 cm. How much did each balloon grow? How much more did balloon A grow than balloon B? **Balloon A grew 18 cm and balloon B grew 2 cm. Balloon A grew 16 cm more.**. Balloon A: 30 take away 12 is 18 cm. Balloon B: 14 take away 12 is 2 cm. Then 18 take away 2 is 16. So balloon A grew 16 cm more than balloon B. Measuring the growth, not just the end size, shows how little the unfed yeast did. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In the breath counter, a child counted 6 breaths in 15 seconds sitting still, and 11 breaths in 15 seconds just after jumping. How many breaths is each in one minute? **24 breaths a minute sitting still, and 44 breaths a minute after jumping.**. There are four lots of 15 seconds in one minute. So multiply each count by 4. 6 times 4 is 24. 11 times 4 is 44. After jumping, the child breathed 20 more times a minute. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the seed test, 4 of the 5 soaked seeds in cup A sprouted. What fraction of the seeds sprouted? What fraction did not? **Four fifths sprouted. One fifth did not.**. There were 5 seeds, so each seed is one fifth of the cup. 4 seeds sprouted, so four fifths sprouted. 1 seed did not, so one fifth did not. Four fifths and one fifth make five fifths, the whole cup. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- In the ruler-catch test, a child caught the ruler at 18 cm, then 12 cm, then 15 cm. Put the three catches in order from fastest to slowest. Which is the middle value? **12 cm, 15 cm, 18 cm. The middle value is 15 cm.**. A smaller number means the ruler fell a shorter way before the child caught it. So a smaller number is faster. In order from smallest: 12, 15, 18. The middle one is 15 cm. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- In the nature museum, a child found 3 living things, 9 once-alive things and 6 never-alive things. How many things did they find in all? How many more once-alive than never-alive things? **18 things in all. 3 more once-alive things than never-alive things.**. 3 + 9 + 6 = 18. Once alive was 9 and never alive was 6. 9 take away 6 is 3. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
