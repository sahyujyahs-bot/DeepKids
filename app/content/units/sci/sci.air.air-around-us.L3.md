# Air around us: it fills every gap, has weight, and keeps us alive
`sci.air.air-around-us.L3`

**Summary.** A glass filled to the top with chana still has room for a lot of water. That room was full of air. Air is matter, like water and stone: it takes up space and it has weight. One litre of air weighs about 1.2 grams, so the air in a small bedroom weighs about as much as a child. Air is a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. We breathe air in for its oxygen, which our cells need to get energy from food, and we breathe out carbon dioxide. Plants take carbon dioxide from the air and, in light, give out oxygen. Plants also need oxygen for their own roots, stems and leaves, day and night.

**Intro.** *Push a balloon inside an empty plastic bottle and stretch its mouth over the bottle's neck. Now try to blow the balloon up. It hardly grows, however hard you blow. The bottle looked empty. So what is stopping the balloon?* The bottle was not empty. It was full of air. The air had nowhere to go, so it pushed back on the balloon. In this unit you find out that air is real stuff that takes up space and has weight. You measure how much air hides in a glass of grain. You squash air in a syringe. You also find out why you breathe, and how plants and air need each other.

## Terms
- **Air**: The mixture of gases all around the Earth. We cannot see it, but we can feel it move. *e.g.* When a ceiling fan turns, you feel the air moving on your face.
- **Matter**: Anything that takes up space and has weight. Solids, liquids and gases are all matter. *e.g.* A stone, a glass of water and the air in a balloon are all matter.
- **Gas**: Matter that spreads out to fill whatever it is in. Its particles are far apart and move about freely. *e.g.* The smell of hot sambar spreads through the whole flat, carried by gases in the air.
- **Takes up space**: Fills a place, so nothing else can be in exactly that place at the same time. *e.g.* When you get into a full bucket of water, water spills out, because your body takes up space.
- **Compress**: To squash something into less space. Air can be compressed easily. Water hardly at all. *e.g.* A bicycle pump compresses air into a tyre, so the tyre gets hard.
- **Particles**: The tiny pieces that all matter is made of. They are far too small to see. *e.g.* In air, the particles are far apart, with empty space between them.
- **Weight**: How hard the Earth's gravity pulls something down. We measure it with a weighing scale. *e.g.* One litre of air weighs about 1.2 grams. One litre of water weighs about 1,000 grams.
- **Oxygen**: The gas in air that our body needs to stay alive. About one-fifth of air is oxygen. *e.g.* Every time you breathe in, your lungs take some oxygen out of the air.
- **Nitrogen**: The gas that makes up most of the air, about four-fifths of it. *e.g.* Most of each breath you take is nitrogen. You breathe it in and out again unchanged.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas that living things breathe out. Plants take it in to make their food. *e.g.* The air you breathe out has about 100 times more carbon dioxide than the air you breathe in.
- **Lungs**: Two soft, spongy organs in your chest that fill with air when you breathe in. *e.g.* Put your hands on your ribs and breathe in deeply. You feel your chest grow as your lungs fill.
- **Stomata**: Tiny holes, mostly on the underside of a leaf, that let air in and out. One of them is called a stoma. *e.g.* A single leaf of a money plant has many thousands of stomata, far too small to see.
- **Photosynthesis**: How plants make their own food from carbon dioxide and water, using light. They give out oxygen as they do it. *e.g.* A tulsi plant on a sunny window is photosynthesising all day.
- **Air canals**: Long hollow tubes inside the stems and roots of some water plants. They carry air down to roots stuck in mud. *e.g.* Cut a lotus stem across, and you see a ring of large holes. Those are its air canals.

## Explanations
### Air takes up space · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is matter. It takes up space, so where air is, nothing else can be until the air moves out.

We call a bottle 'empty' when nothing we can see is inside it. But it is full of air. Push a balloon into the bottle and try to blow it up. The air in the bottle has nowhere to go, so the balloon hardly grows. Make a small hole in the bottle, and the air can leave. Now the balloon blows up easily. A glass of chana looks full, but between the grains are gaps. Those gaps are full of air. Pour water in, and bubbles rise as the water pushes the air out.
- e.g. Water poured into a glass of dry rice makes bubbles, because the water pushes out the air between the grains.
- e.g. A plastic bottle with its cap on will not crush flat when you squeeze it, because the air inside has nowhere to go.
- e.g. When you pour water fast out of a bottle, it glugs. Each glug is a bubble of air going in to take the water's place.
### Air can be squashed · *defined*
**Definition:** Air can be compressed, which means squashed into less space. When you let go, it spreads out again.

Pull back the plunger of a syringe to fill it with air. Hold a finger tight over the tip and push. The plunger goes in part of the way, then pushes back on your thumb. Let go, and it springs back. Do the same with water, and the plunger hardly moves. This is because air is made of tiny particles that are far apart, with empty space between them. Pushing moves them closer together. In water, the particles already touch each other, so there is almost no room to squash them into.
- e.g. A bicycle pump squashes air into a tyre, so the tyre becomes hard.
- e.g. A football is full of compressed air, which is why it bounces.
- e.g. Bubble wrap pops when you squeeze it, because the squashed air bursts out.
### Air has weight · *defined*
**Definition:** Air has weight. Near the ground, one litre of air weighs about 1.2 grams.

Air feels like nothing, but the Earth's gravity pulls on it, just as it pulls on you. One litre of air, the size of a big water bottle, weighs about 1.2 grams, about as much as one paper clip. That is very light. But there is a lot of air. A small bedroom, 3 metres long, 3 metres wide and 3 metres high, holds about 32 kilograms of air. That is about as heavy as a child of 9 or 10. We do not notice the weight of the air because it pushes on us equally from every side, and our bodies push back.
- e.g. A football gets a few grams heavier when you pump more air into it.
- e.g. A bottle of soda water gets a few grams lighter after it goes flat, because the gas has escaped.
- e.g. The air above every square centimetre of the ground weighs about 1 kilogram, all the way to the top of the sky.
### What air is made of · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is a mixture of gases. About four-fifths is nitrogen and about one-fifth is oxygen. The rest is a little argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour.

Air is not one thing. It is a mixture of gases, all invisible. If you could sort 100 particles of dry air, about 78 would be nitrogen and about 21 would be oxygen. The last one would almost always be a gas called argon. Carbon dioxide is only about 4 out of every 10,000 particles, but plants depend on it. Air also carries water vapour, which is water as a gas. On a sticky monsoon day in Chennai there is much more water vapour in the air than on a dry winter day in Delhi. Air also carries dust, pollen and smoke, which are not gases.
- e.g. A cold steel tumbler from the fridge gets wet on the outside, because water vapour from the air turns back into drops on it.
- e.g. On a smoky day after Deepavali crackers, the air carries tiny bits of smoke as well as its gases.
- e.g. Divers carry air in tanks because they need its oxygen under water.
### Why we breathe · *defined*
**Definition:** We breathe to take oxygen into our body and to get rid of carbon dioxide.

Every cell in your body needs energy to work. Cells get that energy from food, and they need oxygen to do it. When you breathe in, air goes down your windpipe into your lungs. The lungs are full of tiny air bags with very thin walls. Oxygen passes through the walls into your blood, and the blood carries it all round your body. The cells use the oxygen and make carbon dioxide as waste. The blood brings the carbon dioxide back to the lungs, and you breathe it out. Breathed-out air still has plenty of oxygen, about 16 parts in 100 instead of 21. That is why rescue breathing, breathing into someone's mouth, can help them.
- e.g. After running up the stairs, you breathe faster, because your working muscles need more oxygen.
- e.g. You can stop eating for a day, but you can stop breathing only for a minute or so.
- e.g. Fish also need oxygen. Their gills take it from air that has dissolved in the water.
### Plants and air · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants take carbon dioxide from the air to make food, using light, and give out oxygen. Like us, they also take in oxygen to use their food, day and night.

A plant cannot walk about to find food, so they make their own. Their leaves take in carbon dioxide from the air through tiny holes called stomata. Inside the leaf, they join it with water, using the energy of light, to make sugar. This is called photosynthesis. Oxygen is left over, and goes out into the air. But every part of a plant is also alive and working, so they also need oxygen, day and night, just as we do. In sunlight, a leaf makes much more oxygen than the plant uses. In the dark, the plant only uses oxygen. Roots need oxygen too. That is why a pot that is always soaked with water can make a plant sick: water fills the air spaces in the soil.
- e.g. A money plant on a sunny window gives out oxygen in the day, and uses a little oxygen at night.
- e.g. Seeds need air to sprout. Seeds kept deep under water for days often fail to sprout.
- e.g. Gardeners make holes in the bottom of pots so that extra water drains away and air gets back into the soil.
### Plants whose roots live in mud · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants grow where their roots sit in wet mud with very little air. They have special ways to get air down to their roots.

Mud under a pond or a river has almost no air in it. So a lotus has a problem: their roots and underground stem need oxygen, but they are stuck in airless mud. The lotus solves this with air canals. These are long hollow tubes that run from the leaves, down the stalks, into the stem in the mud. Air travels down them to the parts that need it. That is why a slice of lotus stem, sold in markets as kamal kakdi or thamarai thandu, is full of holes. Mangrove trees grow in salty mud by the sea. Many of them send up breathing roots, like pencils sticking out of the mud. These roots take in air at low tide and pass it down to the roots below.
- e.g. A slice of lotus stem has a ring of big holes, like the spokes of a wheel. They are its air canals.
- e.g. At Pichavaram, near Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu, thousands of mangrove breathing roots stick up out of the mud.
- e.g. Rice plants in a flooded field also have air spaces in their stems that carry air to their roots.
### Where the oxygen in the air came from · *idea*
When the Earth was young, its air had almost no oxygen. Scientists agree that the oxygen came from living things. Tiny beings in the sea, called cyanobacteria, were the first to photosynthesise and give out oxygen. Over hundreds of millions of years, oxygen built up in the air. Rocks show that this had clearly started by about 2,400 million years ago. Today plants on land and tiny floating beings in the sea, called plankton, keep making oxygen. Scientists estimate that about half of today's oxygen is made in the sea, but the exact share is still being worked out.
- e.g. Old rocks called banded iron formations have stripes of iron rust. Many of them formed when oxygen from tiny sea beings met iron in the sea water.
- e.g. Every breath you take holds oxygen that a plant or a tiny sea being once gave out.

## Mostly agreed
- Air is matter: it takes up space and has weight. *How we know:* A sealed bottle stops a balloon from blowing up. A flask weighs less after air is pumped out of it, and more after extra air is pumped in.
- Air can be squashed into a smaller space, and it springs back. *How we know:* A syringe of air can be pushed in when its tip is blocked. Bicycle pumps and footballs work because of this.
- Dry air is about 78% nitrogen and about 21% oxygen, with a little argon and carbon dioxide. *How we know:* Scientists have measured air from all over the world. Since 1958 they have measured its carbon dioxide every day at Mauna Loa in Hawaii.
- Our cells need oxygen to get energy from food, and they make carbon dioxide as waste. *How we know:* Breathed-out air has less oxygen and much more carbon dioxide than the air around us.
- In light, plants take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen. They also take in oxygen to live, day and night. *How we know:* Water plants give off bubbles of oxygen in sunlight. In a dark sealed box, a plant uses up oxygen, just as an animal does.
- The oxygen in the air was made by living things, over a very long time. *How we know:* Very old rocks show almost no oxygen in the air. Rocks younger than about 2,400 million years show oxygen building up.

## How we found out
**Anaxagoras and Empedocles, thinkers in ancient Greece, and later Hero of Alexandria, an engineer in Egypt, About 450 BCE (the wine bags and the water thief), and the first century CE (Hero's book Pneumatica).** About 2,500 years ago, in ancient Greece, many people thought that a pot with nothing you could see in it was empty. They believed the air inside was nothing at all. The thinker Anaxagoras and his followers showed this was wrong, using things anyone had at home. They blew up a leather wine bag and squeezed it. The bag pushed back, because air was inside. They also used a water thief, a metal jar with small holes in the bottom and a narrow neck at the top. Dip it in water, and water flows in through the holes. But close the top with your thumb first, and hardly any water gets in. The air inside is in the way. Another thinker, Empedocles, described a girl playing with a water thief, and used it to explain breathing. About 500 years later, Hero of Alexandria wrote a book about air. He said that vessels which look empty are full of air. He said air is made of tiny pieces with empty space between them, and that is why it can be squashed.  
*What people believed before:* Many people thought that a pot holding only air was empty, because air seemed to be nothing. The squeezed wine bags and the water thief showed that air is a real thing that takes up space and pushes back. The philosopher Aristotle wrote about these tests in his book Physics.

## Challenging perspectives
- **An empty bottle or pot has nothing in it.** (A very common idea, in children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: We cannot see, smell or feel anything inside an empty bottle.  
  Against: A sealed bottle stops a balloon growing, and pouring water into it makes bubbles. The bottle is full of air.
- **Air weighs nothing.** (A common idea, because air feels weightless; *rejected*)  
  For: We cannot sense the air pressing down on our heads, and a blown-up balloon floats about easily.  
  Against: Weigh a football before and after pumping, and it gets a little heavier. Careful scales show that one litre of air weighs about 1.2 grams.
- **Plants breathe in carbon dioxide and animals breathe in oxygen. Plants don't need oxygen.** (A common idea, even in some school books; *rejected*)  
  For: In sunlight, plants clearly take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen.  
  Against: Every living part of a plant also uses oxygen, day and night. A plant's roots die if the soil stays flooded for too long, because they get no oxygen.
- **Plants in a bedroom are dangerous at night because they use up the oxygen.** (An old warning some families still give; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants do use oxygen at night, and give none out in the dark.  
  Against: A plant uses only a very small amount, far less than one sleeping person or a pet. A bedroom with a few plants is safe.
- **A few pot plants clean the air of a whole room.** (A popular idea that grew from a NASA test in 1989, done in small sealed boxes; *debated*)  
  For: In small sealed boxes, plants and the tiny living beings in their soil did take some harmful gases out of the air.  
  Against: In a real home, air leaks in and out all the time. A study in 2019 worked out that a room would need between 10 and 1,000 plants on every square metre to clean the air as well as the fresh air that leaks in and out of an ordinary building.

## Roots
We have found out that air fills every gap, has weight, and keeps us and plants alive. Now we ask what air tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your glass looked full of chana, yet over a third of a glass of water still went in. Tiny mustard seeds left about the same share of air as big chana. Smaller seeds leave smaller gaps, but many more of them. So a glass of round grains is about one-third air, whether the grains are big or small.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call a glass of grain 'full' when we can see no room left, and we expect big grains to leave much more empty space than small ones. Both habits come from what our eyes notice. The test shows the gaps are full of air, and that the share of air hardly depends on grain size.  
*Evidence:* In the Full Glass test, a glass filled to the line with chana, moong or mustard seeds still took in a lot of water, and bubbles of air came out as it did. The amounts for the three grains came out close, though the chana are several times bigger than the mustard seeds.

- *who am i:* Every few seconds you take air into your body, and some of its oxygen becomes part of the way you live. You are not sealed off from the air. You are always sharing it.
- *what is life:* Plants and tiny sea beings put oxygen into the air, and every breathing being uses it. Life made the air we breathe.
- *what is this world:* We live at the bottom of a deep layer of air, many kilometres high. It weighs about a kilogram on every square centimetre of the ground.

Air looks like nothing, and yet it fills every gap in a glass of grain, every bottle and every pair of lungs. It keeps moving and mixing round the whole Earth. The oxygen in it was given out by plants and tiny sea beings. You breathe it in, and your breath goes out to be breathed by others, by crows, by the money plant on the window. One invisible air flows through countless separate lives and joins them.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Clay is shaped into a pot. But it is the empty space inside that makes the pot useful. (Lao Tzu called the inside of a pot empty. Our tests show that this space is filled with air. When we pour water in, the air must make way, just as the bubbles in the chana showed.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 4.3, the teaching of Raikva:** Among the forces of the world, air takes everything into itself: when a fire goes out, it goes into the air. In a person, breath does the same. (The Upanishad sees air outside and breath inside as two forms of one thing. Science agrees that the air of the room and the air in our lungs are the same air, moving in and out.)
- **Anaximenes, a thinker in ancient Greece, about 550 BCE (a saying later writers credit to him):** Just as our breath holds us together, so breath and air surround the whole world. (Anaximenes thought air was the stuff all things are made of. That turned out wrong. But he was right that air surrounds the whole Earth, and that our breath is part of it.)
- Science (settled): Air is matter. One litre near the ground weighs about 1.2 grams, and the air above each square centimetre of ground weighs about 1 kilogram.
- Science (settled): Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by living things through photosynthesis, building up from about 2,400 million years ago.
- Science (open): How much of today's oxygen is made in the sea, and how much on land, is still being measured. Most estimates say about half comes from the sea.
- Other view, Many scientists: Air is a mixture of gas particles, and breathing is gases moving in and out of the lungs. Calling the air one breath that joins all lives is a lovely way of speaking, but it adds no new fact about air.
- Practice, Feel the air you cannot see (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Hold your hand a little in front of your mouth. Breathe in slowly through your nose. Notice the cool air going in. Now breathe out slowly through your mouth onto your hand. Notice the warm air coming out. Do this five times. Now open your eyes and look at the room. All of it is full of air, and you are sharing it with everyone here.
- Ask: Was the air you just breathed out ever part of someone or something else?
- Ask: If a space is full of air, is it empty or full? Who decides?
- Ask: Plants give us oxygen, and we give them carbon dioxide. Are we separate from plants, or part of one living system?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The glass of chana looked full, but it was partly air. Is anything really full? (hint: Even a stone has tiny spaces in it. And inside every particle, there is a lot of empty space.)
- *deep:* You can squash air in a syringe but not water. What does that tell you about how the particles sit in each? (hint: Think of a crowded bus and an empty bus. In which one can more people squeeze in?)
- *deep:* We cannot see air. How many other real things around us can't we see? (hint: Think of sounds, smells, magnetism, and the tiny living beings on your hands.)
- *big:* The air you breathe out goes into the room, and others breathe it in. Where does your breath end and someone else's begin? (hint: Air keeps moving and mixing all round the Earth. Some of the air in your lungs was once in a tree's leaf or a crow's lungs.)
- *big:* Plants give out oxygen and we give out carbon dioxide. Who is helping whom? (hint: Plants also use oxygen. Do they need us, or do we only need them?)
- *big:* Long ago, many people believed that an 'empty' pot held nothing. What ideas do people hold today that might turn out just as wrong? (hint: What did people believe that was obvious, but turned out wrong when someone tested it?)

## Story: The Tumbler That Was Full and Not Full
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: water poured into dry grain fills the gaps between the grains and pushes out the air as bubbles, and a sealed bottle stops a balloon from blowing up.*

It was the morning of Pongal in Madurai, and the whole house smelled of jaggery. Paati, Kavya's grandmother, sat on a low stool in the kitchen. In front of her stood a brass pot, ready for the sweet pongal. Kavya was eight. Her cousin Arjun, who was nine, had come from Bengaluru for the festival.

"Kavya, measure the rice for me," said Paati. "One steel tumbler, filled to the top."

Kavya dipped the tumbler into the rice tin and pulled it out, heaped high. She ran her finger across the top, the way Paati did, so the rice was flat and level with the rim.

"Full," said Kavya.

"Good. Now wash it," said Paati. "Pour water in, right there in the tumbler, and swirl."

Kavya held the tumbler over the sink and began to pour water from a jug. She waited for the water to spill over the top at once. It didn't. The water sank down into the rice and disappeared. She poured more. It disappeared again. Tiny bubbles came up between the grains and popped at the top. She kept pouring until the water was level with the rim at last.

"Paati!" she said. "The tumbler was full of rice. Where did all that water go?"

"Into the empty spaces between the grains," said Paati, smiling.

Arjun leaned over to look. "There aren't any empty spaces. The rice was right up to the top."

"Look closely," said Paati. "Rice grains are long and thin. They don't fit together perfectly. There are little gaps between them."

Kavya looked at the bubbles. They were still coming up, slower now. "But Paati, if the spaces were empty, where did the bubbles come from?"

Paati stopped stirring the jaggery and looked at her granddaughter. "Tell me what you think."

Kavya thought hard. A bubble is air. The bubbles came up from between the grains. So before the water came, the gaps had air in them. "They weren't empty," she said slowly. "They were full of air. The water went in, and the air had to come out."

"Air is nothing," said Arjun. "You can't even see it."

"You can't see it," said Kavya. "But it was in the way. The water had to push it out before it could go in."

Arjun shook his head. "That's just bubbles."

Kavya had an idea. Last week, at her homeschool table, her mother had shown her a trick with a balloon and a bottle. She ran to the cupboard, found an empty plastic water bottle and one of the balloons left over from her birthday. She pushed the balloon down into the bottle and stretched its mouth over the bottle's neck.

"Blow it up," she said, and handed it to Arjun.

Arjun laughed and blew. His cheeks puffed out. His face went red. The balloon grew a tiny bit and stopped. He tried again, harder. The balloon would not grow.

"It's a trick bottle," said Arjun.

"It's an ordinary bottle," said Kavya. "It's full of air. When the balloon tries to grow, it has to push the air out of the way. But the air has nowhere to go. It's trapped in the bottle, so it pushes back."

Paati handed Kavya a safety pin from the end of her sari. "Make a way out for it," she said. Kavya pressed the pin carefully into the bottom of the bottle and made a small hole.

Arjun blew again. This time the balloon swelled up inside the bottle until it pressed against the sides.

"Now put your finger on the hole," said Kavya. Arjun did, and took his mouth away. The balloon stayed blown up inside the bottle, with its mouth wide open.

Arjun stared. "Why doesn't it go down?"

"Because no air can get back in to fill the space," said Kavya. "Take your finger off."

He lifted his finger. With a soft hiss, air rushed in through the hole, and the balloon shrank back to nothing.

Arjun was quiet for a while. Then he picked up the tumbler of wet rice and looked at the last few bubbles. "So the bottle was full before, and the tumbler was full before," he said. "Full of air."

"Air is something," said Kavya. "It just doesn't look like anything."

Paati tipped the washed rice into the brass pot. "Next time you measure rice," she said, "you will know that part of the tumbler is air."

Kavya laughed, and went to fetch the sugarcane.

**For the parent:** Kavya realises that the 'empty' spaces between the rice grains were full of air, because bubbles came out when the water went in. A sealed bottle shows the same thing: the air inside stops the balloon from growing.

- Ask: Why did the water seem to disappear into the full tumbler of rice?
- Ask: Where did the bubbles come from?
- Ask: Why couldn't Arjun blow up the balloon inside the bottle at first?
- Ask: What changed when Kavya made a hole? Why did the balloon stay blown up when Arjun covered the hole?
- Ask: Do you agree with Arjun that air is nothing? What would you show him?

### e2 · Squash It: can you squeeze air into less space? Can you squeeze water? (20 min)
- **question:** If you block the end of a syringe and push the plunger, how far will it go in with air inside? How far with water inside?
- **predict:** Will the plunger go in further with air or with water inside, or not at all with either? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same syringe each time. Start with the plunger at the same mark. Block the tip with the same finger, pressed hard. Push as hard as you can each time. Only what is inside changes: air, then water, then half and half.
- **safety:** Never use a syringe with a needle.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw two syringes. In one, draw dots for air particles far apart. In the other, draw dots for water particles touching.
- **results table:** What is inside | Start mark (ml) | Smallest mark when I push (ml) | What happens when I let go?
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check the syringe has no needle. 2. Show your child the marks on the side, in millilitres. 3. Pull the plunger back to the 10 ml mark, so the syringe fills with air.  
  *Parent says:* The syringe is full of air up to 10. If I put my finger over the end and push, will the plunger move? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Pull the plunger to 10. The syringe is full of air. Will it push in if you block the end? Why?
  - Note: Use a 10 ml or 20 ml syringe with a smooth tip. If you use a 20 ml syringe, start at 20 ml and change the numbers in the table.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press a finger hard over the tip. 2. Push the plunger in as far as you can. 3. Read the mark it reaches. 4. Let go of the plunger and watch.  
  *Parent says:* Push hard. What number does it reach? Now let go. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Block the tip with your finger. Push hard. Read the number. Now let go!
  - Note: Most children can push a 10 ml syringe of air to about 5 or 6 ml, and grown-ups further. When you let go, the plunger springs back almost to 10. If it does not spring back, air is leaking past the finger. Press harder.
  - Check: You let go, and the plunger springs back. Why? → **The squashed air pushes back and spreads out again**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Over the bowl, suck water into the syringe up to the 10 ml mark. 2. Point the tip up and push out any bubbles, then top up to 10. 3. Block the tip hard and push. 4. If you like, try 5 ml of water and 5 ml of air together.  
  *Parent says:* Now the syringe is full of water. Will it squash like the air did? Push as hard as you did before.  
  *Child mode:* Now fill it with water to 10. Push out the bubbles. Block the tip and push hard. Does it move?
  - Note: The plunger should hardly move, perhaps a fraction of a millilitre, because of a tiny bubble or the rubber bending. Some water may squirt past the finger. That is fine. Then try 5 ml of water and 5 ml of air: it squashes only about as much as 5 ml of air would.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why. Then show the drawing of particles in the explanation card.  
  *Parent says:* Air squashed, and water hardly did. What does that tell you about the tiny particles inside air and inside water?  
  *Child mode:* Air squashed. Water didn't. What does that tell you about their tiny particles?
  - Ask: Why can air be squashed but water hardly at all? (good: The particles in air are far apart, so they can be pushed closer.; In water the particles are already touching.)
  - Note: A strong answer: air takes up space, but its particles are far apart with empty space between them. Pushing moves them closer. Water's particles already touch, so there is nowhere for them to go. This is why air can be pumped into a tyre.

### Air Is Real (core) (core, 15 questions)

   **Answer:** Push a balloon into the bottle and stretch its mouth over the neck. Try to blow it up. It hardly grows, because the bottle is full of air, and the air has nowhere to go.. *Why:* A strong answer names a real test and says what you would see, and why it shows air is there.

## Applications
- Cycle, scooter and car tyres are filled with compressed air, so they stay firm and soften the bumps.
- Footballs, volleyballs and basketballs bounce because of the compressed air inside them.
- Bubble wrap and air pillows in parcels protect things, because squashed air pushes back.
- Divers, firefighters and hospital patients get air or oxygen from tanks, where a lot of it is squeezed into a small space.
- Pressure cookers, pumps and the air brakes on buses and trucks all use the push of squashed or heated air and steam.
- Doctors give rescue breathing because breathed-out air still has enough oxygen to help someone.
- Gardeners make holes in pots and loosen soil so roots get air.
- Rice grows in flooded fields in Kerala, Assam and Punjab, because rice plants carry air down to their roots.
- Houses are built with windows and ventilators so that stale air goes out and fresh air comes in.
- Weather scientists measure the weight of the air above us, called air pressure, to forecast rain and storms.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how much of the world's oxygen is made in the sea, and how much on land?** Tiny sea beings are very hard to count across a whole ocean. Most estimates say about half, but the numbers keep changing as scientists measure more.
- **Why did oxygen take so long to build up in the air after tiny sea beings started making it?** Rocks show a gap of perhaps hundreds of millions of years. Scientists argue about what used up the oxygen first, such as iron in the sea and gases from volcanoes.
- **How much does the air in our cities harm children's lungs over many years?** Scientists know that dirty air is harmful, but measuring exactly how much, for each kind of pollution, takes studies of many children over many years.
- **How exactly do plant roots sense that the soil has too little air, and what do they do about it?** Scientists have found some of the signals roots use, but the full story of how they sense and respond is still being worked out.
