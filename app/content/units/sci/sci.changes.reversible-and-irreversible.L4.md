# Changes that go back and changes that do not: what cooking does to food for ever
`sci.changes.reversible-and-irreversible.L4`

**Summary.** You can melt butter and set it again, but you cannot turn toast back into bread. Why not? In this unit you will sort five changes into three piles, stir flour into cold and hot water to see if it comes back, and climb a toast ladder.

**Intro.** *A lump of ghee melts in a hot pan. Take the pan off the stove, and the ghee sets again. Pour dosa batter on the same hot pan, and no amount of cooling brings the batter back. Why does one change go back and the other does not?* Both changes used the same pan and the same heat. The ghee only changed from a solid to a liquid. It was ghee before, and it was ghee after. The batter became something new, with a new colour, a new smell and a new feel. In this unit you sort changes into three piles: back easily, back with work, and no way back. You test flour in cold water against flour in hot water. You toast a ladder of bread squares, and you try to bring them back.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can sort changes into three groups: back easily, back with work, and no way back. I can name the way back.
1. I can explain that in a reversible change the substance stays the same, as in melting, evaporating and dissolving.
2. I can explain that most irreversible changes make new substances, as in cooking, burning, rusting and souring.
3. I can explain what heat does to the starch and the protein in food.
4. I can explain why toast turns brown, and why the browning does not go back.
5. I can test whether a change is reversible by trying a way back, in a fair test.
6. I can explain why tearing, grinding and mixing do not go back by themselves, although no new substance is made.
7. I can say why people cook food, and where people use changes that go back and changes that do not.

## Terms
- **Reversible change**: A change that can be undone, so that you have the same substance as before. *e.g.* Melted ghee sets again when it cools. It is ghee before and after.
- **Irreversible change**: A change that cannot be undone. You cannot get back what you started with. *e.g.* A dosa cannot be turned back into batter.
- **Substance**: One kind of matter, with its own name and its own way of behaving. *e.g.* Water, salt, sugar, iron and wax are five substances.
- **Property**: Something a substance does or is like, which you can test. *e.g.* Salt tastes salty, dissolves in water and has white grains. These are three of its properties.
- **Change of state**: A change between solid, liquid and gas. The substance stays the same. *e.g.* Ice, water and steam are three states of one substance.
- **Dissolve**: To spread through a liquid as particles too small to see. *e.g.* Sugar dissolves in water. The water tastes sweet, so the sugar is still there.
- **Evaporate**: To change from a liquid into a gas that mixes into the air. *e.g.* Water evaporates from a wet towel, and the towel dries.
- **Mixture**: Two or more substances together, each one still itself. *e.g.* Rice and dal in one tin make a mixture. So does salt in water.
- **Physical change**: A change in shape, size or state, with no new substance made. *e.g.* Tearing paper is a physical change. Each piece is still paper.
- **Chemical change**: A change that makes one or more new substances. *e.g.* When wood burns, the wood and oxygen become ash, smoke and gases.
- **Starch**: The substance that plants store as food in grains, potatoes and roots. Rice, wheat and cornflour are mostly starch. *e.g.* A grain of rice is about three quarters starch.
- **Protein**: A substance made of very long chains, folded into exact shapes. Living bodies are built from proteins. *e.g.* Egg white, paneer and dal are rich in protein.
- **Gelatinise**: What starch does in hot water. The tiny starch grains swell, burst and thicken the water. *e.g.* Rice flour and water gelatinise into kanji when they are heated.
- **Denature**: To make a protein chain lose its folded shape, by heat or acid. The chain itself stays whole. *e.g.* Heat denatures the proteins in egg white, and the egg white turns solid.
- **Maillard reaction**: The chemical change that browns hot, dry food. Sugars and parts of proteins react and make new substances. *e.g.* The brown colour and smell of toast come from the Maillard reaction.

## Explanations
### Three piles: back easily, back with work, and no way back · *defined*
**Definition:** A reversible change can be undone, so that you have the starting substance again. An irreversible change cannot be undone.

Many reversible changes go back with almost no effort. Cool the melted ghee, and it sets. Unfold the paper boat, and it is a sheet again. Other reversible changes go back only with work, time or energy. Salt stirred into water is still salt, but to get it back you must evaporate all the water. Rice mixed with dal is still rice, but someone must pick the grains apart. So this unit uses three piles. The first is 'back easily'. The second is 'back with work'. The third is 'no way back', which holds the irreversible changes. For every change, ask two questions. Is it still the same substance? What exactly would I do to get back to the start? If you can name a way back and it works, the change is reversible.
- e.g. Back easily: ice melts, and the water freezes again in the freezer.
- e.g. Back with work: salt dissolves in water, and comes back when the water evaporates.
- e.g. No way back: batter cooks into a dosa.
### In a reversible change the substance stays the same · *defined*
**Definition:** In a reversible change, no new substance is made. The same particles are there before and after, and only their arrangement or their place changes.

A substance is made of tiny particles, far too small to see. When ice melts, the water particles stop holding fixed places and slide past each other. They are the same particles. When water evaporates, the particles leave one by one and mix into the air. When they meet a cold lid, they gather into drops again. When salt dissolves, its particles spread out among the water particles, and each one is still salt. That is why the water tastes salty. In all these changes you can test the substance afterwards, and it behaves as it did before. Going back often means giving back or taking away heat. Ice takes in heat to melt, and gives the same heat out again when it freezes.
- e.g. Candle wax melts near the flame and sets again lower down.
- e.g. Steam from a cooker turns into drops of water on a cold lid.
- e.g. Sea water dries in the salt pans of Thoothukudi, and salt is left.
### Getting a substance back out of a mixture · *defined*
**Definition:** A mixture can be separated when its parts differ in some property, such as size, weight or whether they evaporate.

In Cold Stir, Hot Stir, raw flour is stirred into cold water in glass A. Within half an hour, most of the flour lies in a white layer at the bottom. The water is poured off, the layer is dried, and it is flour again. That is a way back. It worked because raw flour does not dissolve. Its grains only hang in the water for a while, and then sink. Each kind of mixture has its own way back. Sand in water settles. Salt in water does not settle, but water evaporates and salt does not. Iron pins in sawdust come out with a magnet. Rice and dal are picked apart by hand or with a sieve. Every one of these takes work or time. A mixture never separates by itself.
- e.g. Tea leaves are held back by a strainer, and the tea passes through.
- e.g. Farmers winnow grain: the wind carries off the light husk, and the heavy grain falls.
- e.g. Salt is got from sea water by letting the sun evaporate the water.
### Most irreversible changes make new substances · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical change makes one or more new substances. The new substances have properties that the starting substances did not have.

When a matchstick burns, wood and oxygen from the air become ash, smoke and gases. Cooling the ash does not give back wood, because the wood is no longer there. When milk sours into curd, bacteria turn the milk sugar into an acid, and the acid sets the milk. When an iron gate rusts, iron joins with oxygen and water and becomes rust. Wet cement also changes in this way. Cement does not harden by drying. It reacts with the water and grows tiny crystals that lock the sand and stones together. Cement even hardens under water. All the atoms are still present after a chemical change. They are joined in new groups, and cooling or drying does not put them back in the old ones.
- e.g. A dosa has a brown colour and a smell that the batter never had.
- e.g. Rust is brown and crumbly, and iron is grey and hard.
- e.g. A footprint left in wet cement stays in the hard floor for good.
### What heat does to starch · *defined*
**Definition:** To gelatinise is what starch does in hot water: the starch grains take in water, swell and burst.

Rice, wheat, corn and potatoes store their food as starch. Starch is packed into grains so small that hundreds would fit on a full stop. In cold water these grains stay hard and closed, and they sink. That is the white layer in glass A of Cold Stir, Hot Stir. Between about 55 and 80 degrees Celsius, depending on the starch, the grains let water in. They swell to many times their size. Long starch molecules spill out of them and tangle with each other, and the water turns thick. That is the paste in glass B. A swollen, burst grain cannot close up again. So when the paste is dried, it becomes a hard, glassy flake and never a powder. Cooking rice, rotis, idlis and potatoes is mostly this one change.
- e.g. Rice kanji is used to stiffen cotton saris, because cooked starch dries into a stiff film.
- e.g. Vadam and sandige are cooked rice paste dried in the sun into hard chips.
- e.g. A raw potato is hard, and a boiled potato is soft all the way through.
### What heat does to protein · *defined*
**Definition:** To denature a protein is to make its chain lose its folded shape. Heat and acid both do this.

A protein is a very long chain, folded into an exact shape. Raw egg white is about nine parts water and one part protein. Each folded protein floats separately, so the egg white is runny and clear. Heat makes the chains shake harder and harder. At about 62 degrees Celsius, some of the folds start to come open. The opened chains stick to each other, and they join into a net that holds the water still. The egg white turns solid and white. In Tangled Threads you model this with six balls of wool that are shaken into one clump. The chains are not broken. They are only unfolded and joined to each other. There are so many of them, tangled so closely, that cooling does not sort them out. Acid does the same to milk protein. That is how lemon juice turns hot milk into paneer.
- e.g. A clear raw egg white turns solid and white in a hot pan.
- e.g. Lemon juice makes hot milk split into soft white curds and pale whey.
- e.g. Dal turns soft, and its raw smell goes, when its proteins are changed by boiling.
### Why toast is brown: the Maillard reaction · *defined*
**Definition:** The Maillard reaction is a chemical change between sugars and parts of proteins in hot, dry food. It makes brown substances and many new smells and tastes.

In The Toast Ladder, squares of bread sit on a hot tawa for different times. Two things happen to them. First, water leaves the bread as vapour, and the bread goes firm. This drying is reversible. A pale, dry square left overnight in a box of damp air goes soft again. Second, the dry surface gets hotter than about 140 degrees Celsius. Then the sugars and proteins in the bread react. They make hundreds of new substances. Some are brown, and some have the smell of toast. This is the Maillard reaction, which the French doctor Louis-Camille Maillard described in 1912. It does not go back. A brown square stays brown after cooling and after a night in damp air. Boiling water never gets hotter than 100 degrees Celsius. That is why boiled rice and steamed idlis stay white. Sugar heated alone, at about 160 to 170 degrees Celsius, breaks up and browns in a different reaction and becomes caramel.
- e.g. A dosa is brown on the tawa side and pale on the top side.
- e.g. Roasted coffee beans, roasted peanuts and a roti's dark spots are all browned by the Maillard reaction.
- e.g. A steamed idli stays white, because steam is not hot enough to brown it.
### No new substance, and still no easy way back · *defined*
**Definition:** Some physical changes are irreversible. Tearing, breaking and grinding make no new substance, yet they cannot be undone.

A torn sheet of paper is still paper. Atta is still wheat. A broken glass is still glass. No new substance was made, yet nobody can join the pieces as they were. So 'irreversible' and 'chemical' are two separate ideas. Mixing is a special case. A drop of ink spreads through a glass of water in a few minutes. It never gathers into a drop again. The Slip Shuffle shows why. With 2 dotted and 2 blank slips, the slips come out sorted about 1 time in 3. With 3 and 3, it is 1 time in 10. With 5 and 5, it is 1 time in 126. The more pieces there are, the more mixed orders there are, and the sorted orders become a tiny share. A drop of ink has more particles than anyone could count. Their random moving almost always leads to one of the mixed orders. A mixture can be separated, but only with work, such as evaporating the water. The Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann gave this explanation in the 1870s.
- e.g. Wheat ground into atta cannot be made into grains again.
- e.g. A drop of ink spreads through water and does not gather again.
- e.g. A shuffled pack of cards almost never comes back in order by more shuffling.
### Why people cook · *defined*
**Definition:** Cooking is heating food so that it changes for good. It makes food softer, safer and easier to digest.

Cooking is the oldest chemistry that people do. It does three useful things, and all three are irreversible. It softens food. Swollen starch and loosened fibres are easier to chew. It makes food safer. The heat denatures the proteins of most harmful microbes, and these microbes die. Raw rajma holds a protein that makes people ill, and about half an hour of hard boiling changes that protein for good. It also lets the body take in more of the food. In one careful study, people's bodies took in about 91 parts in 100 of the protein from cooked egg. From raw egg they took in only about 51 parts in 100. Cooking has a cost too. Heat destroys part of the vitamin C in vegetables, which is one reason to eat some fruit and salad raw. Cooks use reversible changes as well. They melt ghee, dissolve salt and boil off water.
- e.g. Raw rice is too hard to chew, and cooked rice is soft.
- e.g. Milk is boiled in most Indian homes to kill microbes before it is drunk.
- e.g. Lemon juice is squeezed on food after cooking, so that its vitamin C is not destroyed by heat.
### Both kinds of change in living bodies, on the Earth and in the stars · *defined*
**Definition:** Reversible changes let the same substance be used again and again. Irreversible changes happen once.

Your blood carries oxygen by a reversible change. A protein in red blood cells holds oxygen in the lungs and lets it go in the muscles. Each red blood cell makes this round trip about once a minute. Growing is different. A sprouted seed cannot go back to being a seed, and your body will not be a baby's body again. The Earth also runs on both kinds. The same water has been cloud, rain, river and sea many times, because evaporating and condensing leave water unchanged. Rock melts deep underground and sets again as new rock. People copy these cycles. Glass, steel and aluminium can be melted and shaped again and again, which is why they are worth collecting. A phone battery holds a chemical change that charging pushes backwards. The Sun runs on a change that goes one way. Every second it joins hydrogen into helium, and it cannot turn the helium back.
- e.g. Water goes round as vapour, cloud, rain and river without being used up.
- e.g. A steel tumbler can be melted down and made into a new one.
- e.g. A chana seed who has grown a root cannot draw the root back in.
### Can an irreversible change ever be undone? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that a cooked egg does not uncook itself. They disagree about whether 'irreversible' means 'impossible' or only 'far too hard'.

In the kitchen, 'no way back' is a safe rule. In laboratories the line is less sharp. Around 1960 the American scientist Christian Anfinsen unfolded a protein with chemicals, and it stopped working. When he took the chemicals away, the chains folded back by themselves and worked again. In 2015 a team in California and Australia took egg white that had been boiled for 20 minutes. They made it liquid with a chemical and spun it very fast in a thin tube. One kind of protein in it folded back into its working shape. They did not make a raw egg.
- e.g. A protein unfolded gently with chemicals can fold back by itself.
- e.g. A battery's chemical change is pushed backwards by charging.
- e.g. Nobody has turned a dosa back into batter.
- **Many chemists:** No atom is lost in any change. So with enough energy and the right tools, any substance could in principle be made again. 'Irreversible' describes what we can do today.
- **Many physicists:** You can undo a change in one place, but only by spending energy. Spending that energy makes more disorder somewhere else. So the world as a whole never goes back to how it was. This is called the second law of thermodynamics.
- **Cooks, potters and builders:** For anyone who makes things, the difference is real and sharp. You can melt the ghee again, but you cannot take back the toast. Good work means knowing which step is which before you take it.

## Mostly agreed
- Melting, freezing, evaporating, condensing and dissolving do not change what a substance is. *How we know:* The substance can be got back and tested. Water from melted ice freezes at the same temperature as before. Salt from dried salt water tastes and looks like salt.
- Cooking makes changes that do not go back, because heat changes the starch and the proteins in food. *How we know:* Under a microscope, raw starch grains are small and closed, and cooked ones are swollen and burst. Cooked egg white stays solid when it is cooled.
- A denatured protein has lost its shape, and its chain is still whole. *How we know:* The Chinese biochemist Hsien Wu proposed this in 1931. Around 1960 Christian Anfinsen unfolded a protein with chemicals and watched it fold back and work again.
- Browning needs dry heat well above the temperature of boiling water. *How we know:* Food boiled or steamed at 100 degrees Celsius stays pale. The same food browns on a tawa or in hot oil, where its surface gets much hotter.
- No atoms are made or destroyed in any change, reversible or irreversible. *How we know:* When chemists carry out a change in a sealed container, the weight before and after is the same. Antoine Lavoisier showed this in the 1770s and 1780s.
- Mixed things do not sort themselves out again, because there are far more mixed-up ways to arrange the pieces than sorted ways. *How we know:* Ludwig Boltzmann worked this out in the 1870s by counting arrangements. Anyone can check a small case with slips of paper, as in the Slip Shuffle.

## How we found out
**Hsien Wu and Daisy Yen Wu in Beijing, and Christian Anfinsen in the United States, Wu published his theory in 1931. Anfinsen did his folding tests in the late 1950s and early 1960s, and won the Nobel Prize in 1972..** A hundred years ago, many chemists thought that heat destroyed a protein. A boiled egg white could not be made runny again. So they supposed that its proteins had been broken up or turned into something else. In Beijing, the biochemist Hsien Wu tested proteins for years. He did this work with the biochemist Daisy Yen Wu, who became his wife in the 1920s and wrote many of the papers with him. In 1931 he proposed a different idea. A protein is a long chain folded into an exact shape, he said. Heat does not break the chain. It only makes the chain lose its folds, and the loose chains then stick to each other. Few scientists noticed his paper. Around 1960 the American scientist Christian Anfinsen found a way to test the idea. He took a protein from a cow's pancreas and unfolded the protein with chemicals. The protein stopped working. Then he took the chemicals away. The chains folded back by themselves and worked again. A broken chain could never have done that. Anfinsen won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1972.  
*What people believed before:* That heating a protein breaks it up or destroys it. Wu proposed that the chain only loses its folded shape. Anfinsen showed that an unfolded protein can fold back and work again, which a broken chain could not do.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A physical change can always be undone.** (Many school books say this in a short form.; *rejected*)  
  For: Melting, freezing, dissolving and bending a wire all go back, and none makes a new substance.  
  Against: Tearing paper, grinding wheat and breaking a glass make no new substance, and they do not go back. Whether a change goes back is a separate question from whether it is physical.
- **A change that makes a new substance can never go back.** (A common rule of thumb.; *rejected*)  
  For: Burning, cooking and rusting make new substances and do not go back in daily life.  
  Against: Charging a battery pushes its chemical change backwards. Blood picks up oxygen and lets it go again about once a minute. Some chemical changes go back and forth easily.
- **Cooking destroys the protein in food.** (A belief found in some diet advice.; *rejected*)  
  For: A cooked egg white looks and feels nothing like a raw one, so something has clearly been changed.  
  Against: Heat unfolds the chains and leaves them whole. In a study of 1998, people took in far more of the protein from cooked egg than from raw egg.
- **Raw food is always healthier than cooked food.** (Some followers of raw-food diets.; *rejected*)  
  For: Heat destroys part of the vitamin C in vegetables. Fresh fruit and salads keep it.  
  Against: Cooking kills harmful microbes and changes the harmful protein in raw rajma. It lets the body take in more of the starch and protein. Most people stay healthiest with some raw food and some cooked food.
- **Cement hardens because it dries.** (Most people who have watched a wet floor turn hard.; *rejected*)  
  For: Wet cement is soft and dark, and hard cement is pale and dry.  
  Against: Cement hardens by reacting with water, and it hardens even under water. Masons keep new concrete wet for days so that the reaction can finish. Dried mud softens again in rain, and set cement does not.
- **Cooking is what made our ancestors human.** (The scientist Richard Wrangham, in his book Catching Fire (2009).; *debated*)  
  For: Cooked food gives more energy for less chewing. About 1.8 million years ago our ancestors' teeth and guts became smaller and their brains larger.  
  Against: The oldest widely accepted traces of fire used by our ancestors are about 1 million years old. They come from Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa. Many scientists think cooking began too late to explain the first of those changes.

## Roots
In this unit you stirred flour into cold and hot water, and you toasted a ladder of bread squares. Here we ask what it means that some changes go back and some do not.

**Deep truth.** In The Toast Ladder, every square of bread could still be toasted darker. No square could be made paler again. So with a change that goes only one way, too little can be fixed and too much cannot. That is why a careful cook heats in small steps and checks each time.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat 'a bit too much' and 'a bit too little' as mistakes of the same size. With a change that has no way back, they are different kinds of mistake. After too little, the right amount can still be reached. After too much, it cannot.  
*Evidence:* In The Toast Ladder, the pale 1-minute square went back on the tawa. It caught up with the brown 4-minute square. The 6-minute square stayed dark after cooling and after a night in damp air. In Cold Stir, Hot Stir, the heated flour could not be made thin again either.

- *who am i:* Many of the changes that made you do not go back. Every meal you have digested is one of them, and so is every centimetre you have grown. At the same time, changes that do go back keep you alive every minute. Your blood picks up oxygen and lets it go again.
- *what is life:* A living being uses both kinds of change. A dry seed can soak, dry and wait again. Once they grow a root, they have chosen a change with no way back.
- *what is this world:* The world uses the same water again and again through changes that go back. But the world also has a past and a future, because some changes never go back.

Changes that go back let the world use one substance many times. The water in your glass has been cloud, rain and river before. Changes with no way back are how new things come to exist: toast, a pot, a grown tree, a star's helium. Everything is conscious, down to the particles. So each of these changes is something that happens to someone, and not only to a thing. Many sages taught that all things come from one source, which keeps making new things. The science of this unit adds one plain point. New things keep coming into being, and the change can be slowed down or steered, but it cannot be taken back.

- **Tirukkural 481:** “பகல்வெல்லும் கூகையைக் காக்கை இகல்வெல்லும் / வேந்தர்க்கு வேண்டும் பொழுது.” Tiruvalluvar wrote that a crow can defeat an owl by day. In the same way, he said, a ruler who wants to win must choose the right time. (The Kural is about a ruler and an enemy, and it is not about toast. But the lesson fits in the same way. The same act can succeed at one time and fail at another. In The Toast Ladder, taking the bread off the tawa worked only before it turned too dark.)
- **Dhammapada, verse 277:** “Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā, / yadā paññāya passati; / Atha nibbindati dukkhe, / esa maggo visuddhiyā.” The Buddha taught that all things that are put together are impermanent. A person who sees this clearly, he said, turns away from suffering. (The verse says that nothing made stays as it is. This unit agrees and adds a detail. Some things change and come back, as water does. Others change once, as toast does.)
- **Heraclitus, as quoted by Plato in the Cratylus, 402a:** “δὶς ἐς τὸν αὐτὸν ποταμὸν οὐκ ἂν ἐμβαίης.” The Greek thinker Heraclitus said that you cannot step twice into the same river. New water is always flowing in. (A river looks the same from day to day, yet its water is always new. Even changes that 'go back' do not return the world to exactly what it was. The ghee that sets again has lost a little heat to the room.)
- Science (settled): A protein that has lost its shape still has a whole chain. Some proteins fold back by themselves when the cause is taken away. Christian Anfinsen showed this around 1960.
- Science (open): Nobody knows why the universe began in a state so orderly that changes have run one way ever since.
- Other view, Many chemists and physicists: They point out that no atom is lost in any change. So in principle, with enough energy and skill, toast could be taken apart and built into bread again. On this view nothing is truly irreversible. 'No way back' only describes the tools we have today. Many of them also say that bread and wax are not aware of anything.
- Practice, The pause before the tear (3 min): Hold a small piece of scrap paper in both hands, ready to tear it. Stop there. Breathe in and out slowly three times. Right now the paper is still whole, and you could still choose to keep it whole. Now tear it slowly and listen. Hold the two pieces. The whole paper is gone, and only the torn paper is left.
- Ask: A cooked egg holds almost every atom the raw egg held. What exactly cannot be got back?
- Ask: Before you tore the paper, you could still choose to keep it whole. After you tore it, the whole paper was gone. Where did the whole paper go?
- Ask: The Sun turns hydrogen into helium and cannot turn it back. Is the Sun running down, or is it making something?

## Story: The Blue Drop on the White Sari
*Yazhini, Vasanthi Periyamma, Sundar and the sari are made up. Periyamma is a Tamil word for a mother's elder sister, and anna means elder brother. These things are real. Batik is made by drawing on cloth with melted wax and then dyeing the cloth in a cool dye bath. The wax keeps the dye off the cloth beneath it. At the end the wax is taken out with heat. The cloth is ironed between sheets of paper or boiled, and the wax can be used again. Many cotton dyes join chemically to the cotton fibre and do not wash out.*

The sari had to be ready by six o'clock. At four, Yazhini ruined it twice in one minute.

It hung over the long table in Vasanthi Periyamma's shed, six metres of white cotton. Periyamma made batik. She drew on cloth with melted wax, and then she dipped the cloth in dye. Where the wax sat, the dye could not reach, so the cloth under the wax stayed white.

This sari was for a bride in Mylapore. It was to have a border of blue fish and a wide white middle. The border was finished. Each fish had been left bare, and the cloth between the fish was sealed under wax. The middle was still bare too. Periyamma would cover it with wax after her tea, and then the whole sari would go into the blue vat.

Sundar anna was nineteen, and he had been learning from Periyamma for a year. He was stirring the vat with a long stick. Yazhini, who was nine, was only supposed to carry the tea.

She carried it too fast. Her elbow caught the wax pen, a little brass cup on a wooden handle. It rolled, and a line of hot wax drops fell across three fish. Sundar jumped to catch it. His stick swung out of the vat, and one fat blue drop landed in the middle of the white cloth.

Nobody spoke. The wax drops turned from clear to cloudy as they cooled. The blue drop sank into the threads and spread to the size of a one-rupee coin.

"Three fish will have white spots," Sundar whispered. "And the white part has a blue one. We have to start again, and there is no time and no more cloth."

Periyamma was at the gate, paying the dye seller. They had perhaps five minutes.

Yazhini stared at the wax drops. They looked like something she had seen that morning. At breakfast, a spoon of ghee had dripped on her steel plate and set into pale, cloudy beads. Then Amma had put a hot dosa on top of them. When Yazhini lifted the dosa, the beads were gone. The ghee had turned runny and soaked into the dosa.

"Anna," she said. "Is wax like ghee? When it is hot it runs, and when it is cold it sits. Is that all that has happened to it?"

Sundar looked at her, and then at the iron that Periyamma used for finished cloth. He laid a sheet of old newspaper over the three fish and pressed the warm iron down. When he lifted the paper, it had three greasy spots. He pressed again with a fresh sheet, and then again, until the paper came up clean. On the cloth, the drops were gone.

"It went back," Yazhini said. "It only needed to be warm again."

"Now the blue," said Sundar. He put fresh newspaper over the blue spot and pressed the iron. The paper came up clean, and the spot had not changed. He dabbed it with a wet cloth. He rubbed it with soap. The cloth around it got wet, and the spot stayed as blue as before.

"Warm it more," said Yazhini.

"It is not sitting on the cloth, kanna," said a voice behind them. Periyamma had come in. She did not shout. She bent over the spot and looked at it for a long moment, the way she looked at a drawing she had not finished.

"Wax sits on top of the threads," she said. "I send it where I like, and heat calls it back. Dye joins the cotton. Once they have joined, I cannot part them. Why do you think I do all my drawing in wax, and dip in the dye last of all?"

"Because you can change your mind about wax," said Sundar.

"And about dye, never," said Periyamma. "So I do not waste time wishing a blue spot white. I ask what a blue spot can become."

She picked up the wax pen and filled it. Around the blue spot she drew a small, quick shape and left it bare: a body, a tail, a fin. The spot sat exactly where an eye would be. Then she covered the rest of the white middle with wax, as she had always meant to do.

The sari went into the vat at half past four. It came out blue wherever the cloth had been bare. Sundar boiled it in the big pot, and the wax melted off and floated to the top in a yellow skin. Periyamma skimmed that skin into a tin. She would use the same wax tomorrow.

At six, the bride's mother unfolded the sari across both arms. A row of blue fish swam along the border. Far out in the white middle, one small fish swam alone, with a dark blue eye.

"That one has left the others," the bride's mother said. "Did you plan it?"

Periyamma glanced at Yazhini. "It happened," she said, "and then we planned it."

That night Yazhini helped to scrape the cold wax out of the tin. It broke into yellow flakes in her hands. It had been melted and set more times than anyone had counted, and it was ready to be melted again.

**For the parent:** The wax had only melted and set, so warmth took it out again. The dye had joined the cotton, and nothing in the shed could take it out. Periyamma's craft depends on knowing which change goes back and which does not. She does the one that goes back first.

- Ask: Why could a warm iron take out the wax drops and not the blue drop?
- Ask: Why is it safer to do the step that goes back first, and the step with no way back last?
- Ask: Where at home is there a job with one step that goes back and one that does not?

## Experiments
### e1 · Cold Stir, Hot Stir: can the flour come back? (30 min, 2 days)
- **question:** One spoon of flour is stirred into cold water. Another spoon is stirred into water and heated for a few minutes. After both have stood for 30 minutes, will the flour settle out of each one? Can dry flour be got back from each?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the flour settle to the bottom of the cold glass? Will it settle in the heated glass? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same flour in both glasses, one level tablespoon in each. Use the same amount of water, 150 millilitres in each. Use two glasses of the same size, and stand them side by side for the same time. Only one thing differs: the mix for glass B is heated for a few minutes, and the mix for glass A is not. Heating boils away a little water from B. That is a small second difference, so write it down.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the heating and pours the hot paste. Hot starch paste sticks to skin and burns, so let the pan cool for two minutes before pouring.
- **record:** Draw a table with rows for 0, 10 and 30 minutes, and one more row for the dried plates. For each glass, write whether there is a layer at the bottom and how thick it is in millimetres. Write whether the liquid pours.
- **results table:** Minutes standing | Glass A, cold: what you see | Glass B, heated: what you see
- **schedule:** day 1: Mix both glasses and heat the mix for glass B. Watch both for 30 minutes, then set the two plates to dry. (25 min); day 2: Test the two dried plates and say what the results show. (5 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the flour and the water. Let them rub a pinch of flour between their fingers. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* One spoon of flour goes into cold water. One spoon goes into water that we heat. After half an hour, will the flour settle in each glass? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Rub a pinch of flour. Will it settle in cold water? In heated water? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think it will, or will not, settle? (good: Flour is heavier than water, so it sinks.; Hot water might make it dissolve.; Heat might cook it, like rice.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Cornflour gives the clearest result, and rice flour or maida also work.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child measures 150 millilitres of cold water into glass A. They add 1 level tablespoon of flour and stir 20 times. Note the time.  
  *Parent says:* Ten tablespoons of water, then one level tablespoon of flour. Stir twenty times. Now leave glass A alone and write down the time.  
  *Child mode:* Put water in glass A. Add one spoon of flour. Stir twenty times.
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Mix the same amounts in the pan. Heat on a low flame, stirring all the time, until the mix thickens and turns glossy. This usually takes 2 to 4 minutes. Cool for 2 minutes, then pour it into glass B.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same flour and water go into the pan. Watch closely and tell me the moment something changes.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up heats the same mix. Watch. Say when it changes.
  - Note: The change is sudden. The thin milky liquid turns thick and slightly see-through within a few seconds. Keep stirring so that it does not stick. Let your child watch from a safe distance.
  - Check: Why does each glass get exactly one level tablespoon of flour? → **So that only the heating differs**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When glass A has stood for 10 minutes, look at both glasses from the side. Measure any layer at the bottom with the ruler. Record what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Ten minutes have passed. Look from the side, without moving the glasses. What do you see at the bottom of each one?  
  *Child mode:* Ten minutes have passed. Look from the side. Is there a layer?
  - Wait: Wait until glass A has stood for 10 minutes
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When glass A has stood for 30 minutes, look and measure again. Then tilt each glass a little. Record whether each one pours.  
  *Parent says:* Thirty minutes now. Measure the layer again. Now tilt each glass gently. Which one moves like water?  
  *Child mode:* Thirty minutes have passed. Measure again. Tilt each glass gently.
  - Note: Use the waiting time for the term cards or the story. Glass A should show a white layer under thinner water. With rice flour or maida the water above stays a little milky.
  - Wait: Wait until glass A has stood for 30 minutes
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour the water off glass A slowly into the sink. Scrape the white layer on to plate A and spread it thin. Spread 1 tablespoon of the paste from glass B on plate B, as thin as you can. Leave both plates under a fan.  
  *Parent says:* Now we try the way back. We take away the water and see what is left. What do you think each plate will hold when it is dry?  
  *Child mode:* Pour the water off A. Spread each one thin. What will be left?
  - Note: Spread both as thin as a coin, or they will take more than a day to dry. A sunny windowsill is faster than a fan. Keep the rest of the paste from glass B in a closed box in the fridge. It is a real glue, and The Tell-Tale Seal can use it.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When both plates are fully dry, rub each one with a finger. Stir a pinch from each plate into a little water in two katoris. Then ask the three questions in your words to say.  
  *Parent says:* Rub plate A. Now try plate B. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Rub each plate. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Ask: Which glass gave the flour back, and how do you know it is flour? (good: Glass A. It is a white powder again.; In water it turns cloudy and settles, like the flour at the start.)
  - Ask: Glass B was cool again by the time you tested it. Why did it not turn back? (good: The heat changed the flour into something new.; It was cooked.; Cooling does not undo what the heat did.)
  - Note: Plate A usually holds a white powder. In water it clouds and settles, as fresh flour does. Plate B holds a hard, clear or whitish flake. In water the flake stays a flake and slowly goes soft. The reason is that flour is mostly starch, packed in tiny hard grains. In cold water the grains stay closed, and they sink. In hot water they take in water, swell and burst. Long starch molecules spill out and tangle, and the water turns thick. A burst grain cannot close again. So the cold stir was reversible, and the hot stir was not.
  - Think: Cotton saris are stiffened with cooked starch. Why would raw flour in cold water not work?
  - Wait: Leave both plates until they are fully dry, by the next morning
### e2 · The Toast Ladder: can toast go back to bread? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Squares of bread sit on a hot tawa for 0, 1, 2, 4 and 6 minutes. Can cooling, or a night in damp air, turn any square back into soft white bread? Can a pale square still be made darker?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which squares, if any, will go back to soft white bread after a night in damp air? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut all the squares from the same loaf, and make them the same size. Use the same tawa on the same low flame. Turn every square over once each minute, so that both sides are heated. Keep one square off the tawa, to compare the others with. Only one thing differs between the squares: the number of minutes on the tawa.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the toasting. The tawa stays hot for several minutes after the flame is off. Do not eat the squares that spent the night in the damp box.
- **record:** Draw a table with five rows, for 0, 1, 2, 4 and 6 minutes. Give each square a colour score: 0 for white, 1 for cream, 2 for golden, 3 for brown and 4 for dark brown or black. Write its feel too: soft, firm or crisp. Fill in the table twice: straight after toasting, and after the night in damp air.
- **results table:** Minutes on the tawa | Colour score and feel, straight after | Colour score and feel, after a night in damp air
- **schedule:** day 1: Toast the squares and score them. Send one pale square back to the tawa, and shut the rest in the damp box. (21 min); day 2: Score the squares again and say what the results show. (4 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut two slices of bread into 8 squares. Lay out the six labels. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Five squares will be toasted for different times. Then we will try to turn them back into soft white bread. Which ones will go back? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Cut the bread into squares. Which toasted squares will go back? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will, or will not, go back? (good: The pale one is only dried, so water might soften it.; The brown one is cooked, and cooking does not go back.; Once it is toast it stays toast.)
  - Note: Two squares are spare. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat the tawa on a low flame for 1 minute. Put five squares on it and start the timer. Turn every square once each minute. Take two squares off at 1 minute, and one each at 2, 4 and 6 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* You are the timekeeper. Call out one minute, two minutes, four minutes and six minutes. Sniff each square as it comes off the tawa.  
  *Child mode:* Call out the times. Sniff each square as it comes off.
  - Note: The square marked 0 never touches the tawa. If the 2-minute square is already dark brown, the flame is too high. Lower it and start again with the spare squares and fresh bread.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put each square on its label. Your child gives each one a colour score from 0 to 4, presses it with a finger, and records both.  
  *Parent says:* Score each colour: 0 is white and 4 is nearly black. Now press each one. Is it soft, firm or crisp? What did you smell?  
  *Child mode:* Score each colour from 0 to 4. Press each square. Write it down.
  - Check: Why is one square of bread kept off the tawa? → **To compare the toasted squares with it**
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Let the squares cool for 3 minutes and look again. Then put one of the two 1-minute squares back on the tawa for 3 more minutes, turning it each minute. Compare it with the 4-minute square.  
  *Parent says:* The squares are cool now. Has any colour gone back? Now watch this pale square go back on the tawa. Can it catch up with the brown one?  
  *Child mode:* The squares are cool. Did any colour go back? Can a pale square get darker?
  - Note: Cooling changes no colours. The pale square, toasted for 3 more minutes, reaches about the same brown as the 4-minute square. So any square can still go darker.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the 0, 1, 2, 4 and 6 minute squares in the steel box, in order. Stand the katori with the wet cloth beside them, not touching. Close the lid and leave the box overnight.  
  *Parent says:* Toasting drove water out of the bread. So tonight we give the squares damp air. Tomorrow we see which ones went back.  
  *Child mode:* Put five squares in the box with the wet cloth. Close the lid.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Open the box. Your child scores the colour of each square again, presses each one, and records both. Then ask the three questions in your words to say.  
  *Parent says:* Score the colours again and press each square. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Score and press each square again. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What went back, and what did not? (good: The softness came back in the pale squares.; No colour went back.; The brown squares still smell of toast.)
  - Ask: Yesterday, could every square still be made darker? Could any be made paler? (good: Yes, the pale one caught up with the brown one.; No square got paler, whatever we did.)
  - Note: In most homes the pale squares are soft again, and the brown ones are bendy. Every colour score is the same as yesterday. The reason is that two changes happened on the tawa. Water left the bread, and damp air gave it back. That change is reversible. On the hot, dry surface, sugars and proteins also reacted and made new brown substances. That is the Maillard reaction, and it has no way back. Throw the squares away afterwards.
  - Think: Boiled rice never turns brown, however long it boils. Why not?
  - Wait: Leave the box closed overnight

## Worksheets
### Can it go back? (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a reversible change?  
   Options: A change that can be undone / A change that makes a new substance / A change that needs a stove / A change that happens fast  
   **Answer:** A change that can be undone. *Why:* A reversible change can be undone, and you have the same substance again. A change that makes a new substance usually has no way back.
2. (mcq) Which of these changes is irreversible?  
   Options: Toasting bread / Melting ghee / Freezing water / Folding paper  
   **Answer:** Toasting bread. *Why:* Toasting makes new brown substances that stay. Melted ghee sets, ice melts and folded paper unfolds.
3. (fill) Salt stirred into water can be got back by letting the water ____.  
   **Answer:** ['evaporate', 'dry up', 'dry']. *Why:* The water goes into the air as vapour and the salt cannot, so the salt is left on the plate.
4. (truefalse) Melting changes candle wax into a new substance.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Melted wax is still wax. Its particles slide past each other, and they take fixed places again when the wax cools.
5. (match) Match each change to its way back.  
   **Answer:** Water froze into ice → Warm it; Candle wax melted → Cool it; Salt dissolved in water → Evaporate the water; Iron pins fell into sawdust → Use a magnet. *Why:* Each way back uses something the substance does. Ice melts when warm, wax sets when cool, water evaporates and salt does not, and a magnet attracts iron.
6. (sort) Sort these changes. Is each one reversible or irreversible?  
   **Answer:** an ice cube melting → Reversible; an egg boiling → Irreversible; a towel drying → Reversible; a matchstick burning → Irreversible; sugar dissolving in water → Reversible; dosa batter cooking → Irreversible. *Why:* Melting, drying and dissolving leave the same substance, so they go back. Boiling an egg, burning and cooking batter make changes that cooling does not undo.
7. (mcq) Raw flour is stirred into cold water and left to stand. What happens?  
   Options: Most of it settles to the bottom / It turns into a thick paste / It dissolves and cannot be seen / It turns brown  
   **Answer:** Most of it settles to the bottom. *Why:* Raw starch grains do not dissolve or swell in cold water. They hang in the water for a while and then sink. Flour turns to paste only when it is heated.
8. (truefalse) Cooked flour paste turns back into dry flour when it cools.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Heat made the starch grains swell and burst, and a burst grain cannot close again. Cooled paste stays paste.
9. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how salt comes back from salty water.  
   **Answer:** ['Pour the salty water into a wide plate', 'Leave the plate in a warm, airy place', 'The water evaporates into the air', 'White salt is left on the plate']. *Why:* The salt can only appear after the water has gone. A wide plate helps, because more of the water touches the air.
10. (fill) Heat makes the long protein chains in an egg unfold and ____ together.  
   **Answer:** ['tangle', 'stick', 'join']. *Why:* The unfolded chains catch on each other and make a net that holds the water still. That is why a cooked egg white is solid.
11. (mcq) Why does boiled rice never turn brown in the pot?  
   Options: Boiling water is not hot enough / Rice has nothing in it to brown / The lid keeps the light out / The water washes the brown away  
   **Answer:** Boiling water is not hot enough. *Why:* Browning needs a dry surface hotter than about 140 degrees Celsius. Boiling water stays at 100 degrees Celsius. Rice does brown when it is roasted in a dry pan.
12. (mcq) Wet cement becomes a hard floor. What kind of change is this?  
   Options: Irreversible: cement reacts with the water / Reversible: the cement only dries / Reversible: the cement freezes / No change at all  
   **Answer:** Irreversible: cement reacts with the water. *Why:* Cement reacts with water and grows tiny crystals. Rain softens dried mud again, and rain does not soften set cement.
13. (write) Explain in two sentences why melted ghee is still ghee.  
   **Answer:** Melting only changes ghee from a solid to a liquid. When it cools it sets again, and it looks, smells and tastes as it did before.. *Why:* A strong answer says that no new substance was made, and gives the evidence: the ghee comes back when it cools.
14. (write) A pale square of toast can be made darker. Can a dark square be made paler? Explain your answer.  
   **Answer:** No. The brown colour comes from new substances made by heat. Cooling, wetting or waiting does not turn them back into bread.. *Why:* A strong answer says that browning makes new substances. It also says that a square can go darker and cannot go paler.
15. (draw) Draw raw egg white and cooked egg white as tiny threads. Show what heat changed.  
   **Answer:** Raw: several separate threads, each folded into its own small ball. Cooked: the same threads opened out and joined to each other in a net.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps the same number of threads in both pictures. Nothing is added or broken. Only the folding and the joining change.

   Options: The torn fibres cannot be rejoined / The paper turns into ash / The paper dissolves in the air / Torn paper is a new substance  
   **Answer:** The torn fibres cannot be rejoined. *Why:* Paper is a mat of tiny plant fibres. Tearing pulls them apart, and nothing puts each fibre back in its old place. The pieces are still paper.

   Options: Pushes a chemical change backwards / Fills the battery with new metal / Melts the inside of the battery / Dries the battery out

   Options: Extra salt cannot be taken out again / Salt melts slowly / Salt costs a lot / Salt burns quickly

   Options: Ice and steam / Bread and toast / Milk and curd / Iron and rust

6. (truefalse) Every change that makes no new substance can be undone.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Tearing paper, grinding wheat and breaking a glass make no new substance, and none of them goes back.

   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 2 sorted orders in 20 is 1 in 10. 40 divided by 10 is 4. So about 4 shakes in 40 give a sorted row.

12. (write) Some people think dissolved salt is gone for ever. What test would show that the salt is still there?

   **Answer:** The drying goes back. Pale toasted squares went soft again in damp air. The browning does not go back: the colour scores stayed the same.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the two changes and gives evidence for each one from a test.
14. (write) Explain why a drop of ink never gathers itself again in a glass of water.  
   **Answer:** The ink particles move about at random. ### Testing the way back (challenge, 15 questions)

6. (mcq) What does heat do to the starch grains in flour and water?  
   Options: They swell and burst / They melt into oil / They turn into protein / They sink faster  
   **Answer:** They swell and burst. *Why:* Hot water gets into the grains, and they swell to many times their size and burst. This is called gelatinising, and it thickens the water.
7. (mcq) Why does egg white turn from runny to solid when it is heated?  
   Options: Proteins unfold and join into a net / The water in it freezes / The shell presses on it / Air leaves the egg  
   **Answer:** Proteins unfold and join into a net. *Why:* Heat shakes the folded protein chains open. The open chains stick to each other and hold the water still.
8. (mcq) A square of brown toast is left to cool. What happens to its colour?  
   Options: It stays brown / It fades to white / It turns to steam / It soaks into the plate  
   **Answer:** It stays brown. *Why:* The brown comes from new substances made by the Maillard reaction. Taking the heat away does not turn them back.
9. (mcq) How can you find out whether a change is reversible?  
   Options: Try a way back and compare / Ask whether heat was used / See how fast it happened / Weigh the thing once  
   **Answer:** Try a way back and compare. *Why:* Heat and speed are poor guides. Melting uses heat and goes back. You have to try a way back and compare with what you started with.
10. (mcq) Wheat ground into atta is still wheat. Can the grinding be undone?  
   Options: No, the bits cannot be rejoined / Yes, by cooling the atta / Yes, by adding water / Yes, by sieving the atta  
   **Answer:** No, the bits cannot be rejoined. *Why:* No new substance was made, but nothing joins the powder into whole grains again. A physical change can be irreversible.
11. (mcq) Why does a shuffled pack of cards almost never come back in order?  
   Options: Mixed orders far outnumber sorted ones / The cards stick together / Shuffling makes new cards / The pack is too heavy  
   **Answer:** Mixed orders far outnumber sorted ones. *Why:* Each shuffle gives another order of the cards. Nearly all the possible orders are mixed ones, so the sorted order almost never comes up.
12. (mcq) Why can an old steel tumbler be made into a new one?  
   Options: Melting steel is a reversible change / Steel grows back by itself / Rust turns into steel in the sun / Steel never changes at all  
   **Answer:** Melting steel is a reversible change. *Why:* Steel melts in a furnace and sets again as steel. Because this change goes back, steel can be used again and again.

## Applications
- Recycling works best for materials whose melting is reversible. Glass, steel and aluminium are melted and shaped again and again.
- Salt makers at Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu and in Kutch in Gujarat let the sun evaporate salty water. Dissolving goes back, so the salt is left.
- A rechargeable battery holds a chemical change that goes both ways. Using the battery runs it forwards, and charging runs it backwards.
- Milk is pasteurised by heating it. The heat denatures the proteins of harmful microbes, and that change does not go back when the milk cools.
- Builders use the setting of cement, which has no way back. It gives roofs, bridges and dams that stay hard in the rain.
- Medicine bottles, water bottles and jam jars have seals that snap or pop once. The seal uses an irreversible change to show that the pack was opened.
- Batik makers draw with wax, which can be melted off again, before they use dye, which stays for good.

## Open questions
- **How does a protein chain find its folded shape so fast?** A chain could fold in more ways than anyone can count, yet most fold correctly in less than a second. Computers can now predict the final shape of many proteins. How the chain gets there is still being worked out.
- **When did our ancestors first cook their food?** The oldest widely accepted traces of fire used by our ancestors are about 1 million years old. Some scientists think cooking began much earlier, and old fireplaces are very hard to find.
- **Why did the universe begin in such an orderly state?** Changes run one way because the world moves from more orderly arrangements to less orderly ones. That only works if things were very orderly to begin with. Nobody knows why they were.
- **Can a strong plastic be made that goes back fully to its starting substances?** Most plastics get weaker each time they are melted, and some cannot be melted at all. Chemists are testing new plastics that can be taken apart and made again. None is in wide use yet.
- **Can old concrete be turned back into fresh cement?** Making cement gives off a great deal of carbon dioxide. Engineers are testing ways to reheat the cement from old concrete so that it can react with water again. It is not yet known how well this works for large buildings.

## Connected maths
- In The Slip Shuffle, 3 dotted and 3 blank slips can lie in 20 different orders. Only 2 of those orders are sorted. If you shake and draw 40 times, about how many rows will be sorted? **About 4 rows.**. 2 sorted orders out of 20 is the same as 1 out of 10. 40 divided by 10 is 4. Your real count may be a little more or less, because each try is a matter of chance. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- With 5 dotted and 5 blank slips, only 1 row in 126 comes out sorted. One try takes 5 seconds. About how long would you shake before you saw one sorted row? Give the answer in minutes and seconds. **About 10 minutes and 30 seconds.**. 126 tries at 5 seconds each is 126 times 5, which is 630 seconds. 600 seconds is 10 minutes, and 30 seconds are left over. With only four more slips, the wait grows from under a minute to over ten minutes. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- Here are made-up results from Cold Stir, Hot Stir. One tablespoon of flour weighed 9 grams. After settling and drying, 8 grams of flour came back from glass A. What fraction came back? Where could the missing gram be? **Eight ninths came back. The missing gram stayed in the water that was poured off, or stuck to the glass and the plate.**. 8 grams out of 9 grams is eight ninths. No flour was destroyed. The finest grains had not settled yet, so they went down the sink with the water. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Here are made-up colour scores from The Toast Ladder. At 0 minutes the score was 0. At 1 minute it was 0. At 2 minutes it was 1. At 4 minutes it was 3. At 6 minutes it was 4. Did the colour rise by the same amount each minute? What score would you expect at 3 minutes? **No. Nothing changed in the first minute, and the colour rose fastest between 2 and 4 minutes. At 3 minutes the score would be about 2.**. From 0 to 1 minute the score rose by 0. From 2 to 4 minutes it rose by 2, which is 1 for each minute. Halfway between a score of 1 and a score of 3 is 2. The bread has to dry before it can brown, so the start is slow. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Egg white starts to set at about 62 degrees Celsius. Tap water is at 28 degrees. A cup of hot tea is at 70 degrees. Boiling water is at 100 degrees. In which of these would egg white start to set? How many degrees above the setting point is boiling water? **In the hot tea and in the boiling water. Boiling water is 38 degrees above the setting point.**. 70 and 100 are both more than 62, and 28 is less. 100 minus 62 is 38. The change does not need boiling. It needs the egg white to pass 62 degrees Celsius. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
- Here are made-up results from Soak, Dry, Sprout. A row of 10 dry chana was 8 centimetres long. After soaking it was 12 centimetres. After two days of drying it was 9 centimetres. What fraction of the swelling was undone? **Three quarters of the swelling was undone.**. The row grew by 12 minus 8, which is 4 centimetres. It shrank back by 12 minus 9, which is 3 centimetres. 3 out of 4 is three quarters. The seeds were probably not fully dry yet. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a reversible change? → A change that can be undone, so that you have the same substance again.
- Name two changes that go back easily. → Melting and freezing. Folding and unfolding is another.
- How do you get salt back from salty water? → Let the water evaporate. The salt is left behind.
- What does heat do to the starch in flour and water? → The starch grains swell and burst, and the water turns thick.
- Why does a cooked egg white stay solid when it cools? → Its protein chains have unfolded and joined into a net.
- Why does a steamed idli stay white? → Steam is not hot enough for browning. Browning needs a dry surface above about 140 degrees Celsius.
- Name a change with no way back that makes no new substance. → Tearing paper, grinding wheat or breaking a glass.
- Does cement harden by drying? → No. It hardens by reacting with water.
