# Infectious and non-infectious diseases: how they differ, how they spread and how to prevent them
`sci.microorganisms.germs.L5`

**Summary.** You may sit beside a grandmother who has diabetes all day. But you are kept away from a cousin who has chickenpox. Chickenpox is an infectious disease: a germ causes it, and the germ passes to other people. Diabetes is a non-infectious disease: no germ causes it, and nobody catches it. **Intro.** *You may sit beside a grandmother who has diabetes all day. But you are kept away from a cousin who has chickenpox. Both are ill. Why is one illness passed on and the other not? And how did anyone find out which is which?* Some diseases are caused by germs, and many of those pass from one person to the next. They are called infectious diseases. Colds, chickenpox, cholera, malaria and tuberculosis are infectious. Other diseases have no germ behind them, and nobody catches them. They are called non-infectious diseases. Diabetes, asthma, heart disease and most cancers are non-infectious. In this unit you find out how people tell the two kinds apart. ## Terms
- **Disease**: A condition in which part of the body, or the mind, does not work as it should, for some time. *e.g.* A cold, malaria, diabetes and asthma are all diseases. A cut finger is an injury, which is different.
- **Germ**: A microbe who can make a person ill. A microbe is a living being too small to see. Germs can be bacteria, viruses, fungi or tiny parasites. *e.g.* The cholera germ is a bacterium. The measles germ is a virus. The malaria germ is a tiny parasite.
- **Infectious disease**: A disease caused by a germ who gets into the body and multiplies there. *e.g.* Typhoid, dengue, tuberculosis and chickenpox are infectious diseases.
- **Non-infectious disease**: A disease that no germ causes. Nobody catches it from another person. *e.g.* Diabetes, asthma, anaemia, heart disease and most cancers are non-infectious diseases.
- **Contagious**: Passing from one person straight to another. A contagious disease is also called a catching disease. *e.g.* Measles is very contagious. Malaria is infectious but not contagious, because a mosquito must carry the germ.
- **Symptom**: A change that an ill person feels or shows. Fever, cough, pain, rash and loose motions are symptoms. *e.g.* The symptoms of dengue are high fever, pain behind the eyes and aching joints.
- **Incubation period**: The time between a germ getting into the body and the first symptom. The germ is multiplying during this time. *e.g.* Chickenpox has an incubation period of 10 to 21 days. Flu has 1 to 4 days.
- **Carrier**: A person who has a germ in their body and can pass the germ on, but has no symptoms. *e.g.* Some people carry the typhoid germ for years and feel well. Food they cook can still make others ill.
- **Vector**: An animal who carries a germ from one person to the next. Most vectors are insects or ticks. *e.g.* A female Anopheles mosquito is the vector of malaria. A female Aedes mosquito is the vector of dengue.
- **Droplets and aerosols**: The drops that leave the mouth and nose in a cough, a sneeze or speech. Big droplets fall within about a metre. Very small ones, called aerosols, float in the air. *e.g.* The germs of flu, tuberculosis and measles ride in droplets and aerosols.
- **Ventilation**: The movement of fresh air through a room, which carries the old air out. *e.g.* Two open windows on opposite sides give good ventilation. The air comes in at one and goes out at the other.
- **Dehydration**: The state of a body that has lost too much water and salt. Loose motions and vomiting cause it. *e.g.* The signs are a dry mouth, sunken eyes, very little urine and great tiredness. ORS replaces the lost water and salt.
- **Epidemic**: A time when many more people than usual in one place have the same disease. *e.g.* Cities in India often have a dengue epidemic after the monsoon. An epidemic that crosses many countries is a pandemic.
- **Deficiency disease**: A non-infectious disease caused by too little of one nutrient in the food. *e.g.* Too little iron causes anaemia. Too little vitamin A causes night blindness. Too little iodine causes goitre.
- **Inherited disease**: A disease passed from parents to a child through their genes. Genes are the instructions inside every cell. *e.g.* Sickle cell disease and thalassaemia are inherited diseases of the blood. Nobody catches them.
- **Lifestyle disease**: A non-infectious disease made more likely by the way a person lives over many years. Genes and surroundings matter too. *e.g.* Type 2 diabetes and heart disease are more likely with tobacco, little movement and a lot of sugary, fried food.
- **Risk factor**: Anything that raises the chance of a disease. A risk factor does not decide who falls ill. *e.g.* Tobacco is a risk factor for cancer and for heart attack. Some smokers stay well, and some people who never smoked fall ill.
- **Insulin**: A substance made by the pancreas, an organ behind the stomach. Insulin lets sugar pass from the blood into the cells. *e.g.* In diabetes the body makes too little insulin or cannot use insulin well. So sugar stays in the blood.
- **Artery**: A tube that carries blood from the heart to the body. The heart muscle and the brain each have their own arteries. *e.g.* A heart attack happens when an artery of the heart is blocked. A stroke happens when an artery of the brain is blocked or bursts.
- **Cancer**: A disease in which some of the body's own cells keep dividing when they should stop. They form a lump or spread. *e.g.* Tobacco can cause cancer of the mouth, throat and lungs. Cancer is not catching.
- **Vaccine**: A safe preparation that shows the body a harmless form or part of a germ. The body then gets ready for the real germ. *e.g.* Vaccines protect against measles, polio, tetanus, typhoid and some kinds of pneumonia.
- **Epidemiology**: The science of who falls ill, where and when. It studies many people together to find the cause. *e.g.* An epidemiologist marks cases on a map and on a calendar. The pattern often shows how the disease spreads.

## Explanations
### Two kinds of disease: infectious and non-infectious · *defined*
**Definition:** An infectious disease is caused by a germ who gets into the body and multiplies. A non-infectious disease has no germ as its cause, and nobody catches it.

Germs are bacteria, viruses, fungi and tiny parasites who can live in a human body and harm it. When they get in and multiply, the person has an infection. Many infectious diseases pass on to other people, so one case can become many. A non-infectious disease begins inside the person's own body. The cause may be a missing nutrient, a gene from the parents, or slow damage over many years. It may also be something harmful in the air, water or food. These causes do not move from one person to the next. So the two kinds need different protection. For an infectious disease, we stop the germ from reaching the next person. For a non-infectious disease, we remove the cause or lower the risk. Doctors treat the two kinds differently too. Medicines that kill germs do nothing for diabetes or asthma.
- e.g. Infectious: common cold, flu, chickenpox, measles, cholera, typhoid, malaria, dengue, tuberculosis and HIV infection.
- e.g. Non-infectious: diabetes, heart disease, stroke, most cancers, asthma, anaemia, goitre, sickle cell disease and cataract.
- e.g. A broken arm and a snake bite are injuries. They are neither kind of disease.
### Infectious does not always mean catching from a person · *defined*
**Definition:** Every contagious disease is infectious, but some infectious diseases never pass straight from one person to another. Their germs arrive by another road.

A contagious disease passes from person to person through the air, by touch, or through shared food and water. Colds, measles and chickenpox are contagious. Malaria is infectious, because a germ causes it. But a person with malaria is safe to sit beside. The germ must travel inside a mosquito. Tetanus is infectious too. The tetanus bacteria live in soil and enter through a deep cut. They do not pass from an ill person to a carer. Rabies reaches people through the bite of an infected dog or other animal. This difference matters in daily life. A person with malaria, tetanus or dengue does not need to be kept alone. The family needs mosquito nets or clean wounds instead. Keeping people away from someone who is not contagious helps nobody and hurts the ill person.
- e.g. Contagious: measles, chickenpox, flu, the common cold, tuberculosis of the lungs.
- e.g. Infectious but not contagious: malaria, dengue, tetanus, rabies and Kyasanur Forest disease, which ticks carry in Karnataka.
- e.g. A dog bite must be washed with soap and running water for 15 minutes. The person must see a doctor for the rabies vaccine the same day.
### The five roads germs travel · *defined*
**Definition:** Germs reach a new person by five main roads: the air, water and food, insects and ticks, touch, and blood and other body fluids.

In this unit we call each way a germ can travel a road. A road is simply the route from one body to the next. Each germ uses mostly one road, and the road decides the protection. Air: the germs leave in coughs, sneezes and speech, and the next person breathes them in. Water and food: the germs leave in the stools of an ill person and reach water, hands, flies or food. The next person swallows them. Insects and ticks: a mosquito or tick takes the germ in with blood and passes the germ on with her next bite. Touch: the germs pass by skin, or by things such as towels, combs and door handles. Blood and body fluids: the germs pass when the blood of an infected person enters another body. A doctor who knows the road can stop a disease without a medicine. Boiling water blocks the water road. A net blocks the mosquito road. An open window thins out the germs on the air road.
- e.g. Air: cold, flu, measles, chickenpox, tuberculosis, pneumonia, COVID-19.
- e.g. Water and food: cholera, typhoid, diarrhoea, dysentery, hepatitis A, polio.
- e.g. Insects and ticks: malaria, dengue, chikungunya, filariasis, Kyasanur Forest disease.
- e.g. Touch: ringworm, scabies, sore eyes, head lice.
- e.g. Blood and body fluids: HIV infection, hepatitis B.
### Symptoms: what an illness shows, and what causes them · *defined*
**Definition:** A symptom is a change that an ill person feels or shows. Many symptoms of infection are made by the body's own defences.

A germ gets in and multiplies for some days before anything is felt. This time is the incubation period. Then symptoms begin. Fever is the body raising its own temperature, which slows many germs. A cough and a runny nose push germs out of the airways. Loose motions flush germs out of the gut. Swelling and redness show that blood and defender cells have arrived. Some symptoms help the germ too. A cough carries the germ to the next person, and loose motions carry the germ to water. Doctors read the set of symptoms to guess the disease, and then test to be sure. A fever with shivering every second day points to malaria, and a blood test confirms it. Non-infectious diseases have symptoms too, but they usually come slowly. Great thirst and much urine point to diabetes. Some people have a germ and no symptoms at all. They are carriers, and they can still pass the germ on.
- e.g. Measles: fever, cough, red eyes, then a red rash that starts on the face.
- e.g. Typhoid: a fever that climbs over several days, with headache and stomach pain.
- e.g. Diabetes: great thirst, much urine, tiredness and wounds that heal slowly.
### Diseases of dirty water and food · *defined*
**Definition:** Cholera, typhoid, diarrhoea, dysentery and hepatitis A spread when germs from the stools of an ill person reach water or food.

The germs leave the body in stools. They reach drinking water where sewage leaks or where people have no toilets. Unwashed hands and flies carry them to food. Cholera causes sudden, watery loose motions, many times a day. Diarrhoea means loose, watery stools from any cause. Dysentery is loose stools with blood or mucus. All three can dry the body out within hours, and this dehydration is the danger. Small children are at most risk. The treatment is oral rehydration solution, called ORS, given in sips again and again. A doctor is needed if the child cannot drink or passes blood. Typhoid causes a fever that climbs over days, with headache and stomach pain. It needs a doctor and an antibiotic. Hepatitis A harms the liver and turns the eyes and skin yellow, which is called jaundice. Protection is the same for all. Drink boiled or filtered water, wash hands with soap, cover food, and use a toilet. Vaccines protect against typhoid, hepatitis A and rotavirus. Rotavirus is the commonest cause of severe diarrhoea in babies. Gagandeep Kang, a doctor and scientist in Vellore, took a leading part in testing the first rotavirus vaccine made in India.
- e.g. Cholera: many watery stools, vomiting and fast dehydration.
- e.g. Typhoid: a slowly rising fever for a week or more.
- e.g. Hepatitis A: fever, no wish to eat, dark urine and yellow eyes.
### Diseases carried by mosquitoes · *defined*
**Definition:** Malaria, dengue, chikungunya and filariasis are infectious diseases whose germs travel inside mosquitoes. They do not pass straight from person to person.

Only female mosquitoes bite, because they need blood to make eggs. When a female bites an infected person, she takes in the germ. The germ multiplies inside her for some days. Then she passes the germ on with her bites. Malaria is caused by a parasite called Plasmodium. The female Anopheles mosquito carries the parasite, and she bites mostly at night. The symptoms are fever with shivering and then sweating, often every second or third day. Dengue is caused by a virus. The Aedes mosquito carries the virus. She has striped legs, bites in the daytime, and lays eggs in clean standing water. Dengue brings high fever, pain behind the eyes, joint pain and sometimes a rash. Chikungunya brings fever and very painful joints. Filariasis is caused by thread-like worms, carried by Culex mosquitoes. After many years the legs can swell greatly. Any fever that lasts more than two days needs a blood test. Protection means sleeping under a net and covering arms and legs. It also means emptying standing water every week from coolers, pots and tyres. Mosquitoes need about a week to grow from egg to adult.
- e.g. Malaria: fever with shivering, then sweating, then a gap.
- e.g. Dengue: sudden high fever with pain behind the eyes and in the joints.
- e.g. Filariasis: no early symptoms, then swelling of a leg after years.
### Diseases that travel through the air · *defined*
**Definition:** Colds, flu, measles, chickenpox, tuberculosis and many kinds of pneumonia spread when a person breathes in germs that another person breathed out.

Coughing, sneezing, singing and talking send out drops of every size. Big droplets fall within about a metre. Very small ones, called aerosols, float for minutes or hours in a closed room. Colds and flu are caused by viruses. They bring a runny nose, sore throat, cough and, with flu, fever and body ache. Rest and warm drinks help, and antibiotics do not. Getting wet in the rain does not cause a cold, because a cold needs a virus. Pneumonia is an infection deep in the lungs. The signs are fast breathing, fever and chest pain, and the person needs a doctor at once. Tuberculosis, or TB, is caused by a slow-growing bacterium. The signs are a cough for more than two weeks, evening fever and loss of weight. TB is cured by taking medicines every day for at least six months. In India the tests and medicines are free at government health centres. Measles is one of the most contagious diseases known. Protection on the air road is fresh air, a mask on the ill person, a covered cough and vaccines. The BCG, measles and pneumonia vaccines are given to babies in India.
- e.g. Cold: runny nose, sneezing and sore throat for about a week.
- e.g. TB: a cough for more than two weeks, with evening fever and weight loss.
- e.g. Pneumonia: fast, hard breathing with fever.
### HIV: how it spreads, and how it does not · *defined*
**Definition:** HIV is a virus who attacks the body's defender cells. HIV passes only through blood and a few other body fluids, never through everyday contact.

HIV stands for human immunodeficiency virus. Over years, the virus destroys defender cells in the blood. Without treatment, the body can no longer fight other infections. That late stage is called AIDS. HIV spreads in a few ways only. One is infected blood, for example on a shared needle or blade. Another is from a mother to her baby during pregnancy, at birth or through breast milk. The third is close physical contact between grown-ups. HIV does not spread by touching, hugging, shaking hands or sharing food. It does not spread by coughing, by toilets, or by mosquitoes. So a person with HIV can live, study, play and eat with everyone else. Medicines taken every day keep the virus very low, and the person can live a long life. In India these medicines are free at government centres. Medicines for the mother also protect her baby. There are two rules for children. Never touch a used needle or blade. Treat a person with HIV as kindly as anyone else.
- e.g. Safe: hugging, playing together, sharing a classroom, a meal or a toilet.
- e.g. Not safe: sharing needles, blades or anything with blood on it.
- e.g. A mosquito does not pass HIV on. The virus does not live or multiply inside mosquitoes.
### Non-infectious diseases and their causes · *defined*
**Definition:** Non-infectious diseases have causes inside the body or in the surroundings. The main groups are deficiency, inherited, long-term lifestyle, allergy, and harm from surroundings.

A deficiency disease comes from too little of one nutrient. Too little iron causes anaemia, with tiredness and pale skin. An inherited disease comes through the genes from both parents. Sickle cell disease bends some red blood cells into a curved shape, so they block small blood vessels. It is common in many Adivasi communities of central India. Long-term diseases build up over years. Heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and many cancers belong here. In an allergy, the body's defences react strongly to something harmless, such as dust or pollen. In asthma the airways of the lungs narrow, and breathing becomes hard. An inhaler opens them. Some diseases come from the surroundings. Too much fluoride in well water damages teeth and bones in parts of Telangana and Rajasthan. Dirty air harms the lungs and the heart. Illnesses of the mind, such as depression, are non-infectious diseases too, and they can be treated. Non-infectious diseases now cause about six in every ten deaths in India.
- e.g. Deficiency: anaemia, night blindness, goitre, rickets.
- e.g. Inherited: sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, haemophilia.
- e.g. Long-term: heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, many cancers.
- e.g. Allergy and surroundings: asthma, hay fever, fluorosis.
### Heart attack and stroke: a blocked artery · *defined*
**Definition:** A heart attack happens when an artery that feeds the heart muscle is blocked. A stroke happens when an artery in the brain is blocked or bursts.

Arteries are tubes that carry blood from the heart. Over many years, a fatty layer can build up on the inside wall of an artery. The tube gets narrower. If a clot of blood forms on the fatty layer, the artery is blocked. The part beyond the block gets no oxygen, and its cells begin to die within minutes. In the heart, this is a heart attack. The signs are a heavy pain in the chest, pain in the arm or jaw, sweating and breathlessness. In the brain, this is a stroke. The signs come suddenly. One side of the face droops, one arm is weak, and speech is unclear. Both are emergencies, and every minute counts. In most Indian states the ambulance number is 108. The chance of both goes up with tobacco, high blood pressure, diabetes and little movement. Much salt and much fried food raise the chance too. It goes down with daily movement, plenty of vegetables and fruit, and no tobacco.
- e.g. Heart attack: chest pain that spreads to the arm or jaw, with sweating.
- e.g. Stroke: a drooping face, a weak arm and unclear speech, all starting suddenly.
- e.g. High blood pressure has no symptoms for years, so grown-ups need to have it measured.
### Diabetes: sugar that stays in the blood · *defined*
**Definition:** In diabetes, the level of sugar in the blood stays too high. The body makes too little insulin or cannot use insulin well.

Food is broken down into sugar, and the blood carries the sugar to every cell. Insulin is made by the pancreas. Insulin lets the sugar pass from the blood into the cells. In type 1 diabetes, the body's own defender cells destroy the cells that make insulin. It often begins in childhood. Sweets do not cause it, and the person needs insulin injections every day. In type 2 diabetes, the body still makes insulin, but the cells no longer respond well. It usually begins in adults and builds up slowly. It is more likely with extra weight, little movement, and when parents have diabetes. The symptoms of both are great thirst, much urine, tiredness, and wounds that heal slowly. High sugar over many years damages the eyes, kidneys, nerves and arteries. About 100 million people in India have diabetes. Type 2 can often be delayed or controlled by daily movement, less sugar and medicines. Old Indian doctors called the disease madhumeha, which means honey urine. They saw ants gather at the urine of people who had it.
- e.g. Type 1: begins suddenly, often in a child, and needs insulin for life.
- e.g. Type 2: builds up over years, usually in adults.
- e.g. A finger-prick blood test shows the sugar level in a few seconds.
### Cancer: cells that do not stop dividing · *defined*
**Definition:** In cancer, some of the body's own cells keep dividing when they should stop. They form a lump called a tumour, or spread to other parts.

Every cell carries instructions called genes. Some genes tell the cell when to divide and when to stop. If these genes are damaged, the cell can go on dividing. Most damage is repaired, and most damaged cells die. Cancer begins when a damaged cell escapes both. Damage adds up over a lifetime, so cancer is more common in older people. Some things cause more damage. Tobacco is the biggest one. Smoking causes cancer of the lungs. Chewing tobacco and gutka cause cancer of the mouth, which is common in India. Too much strong sunlight, alcohol and some chemicals add to the damage. Cancer is not catching. But a few cancers are started by a germ who stays in the body for years. One virus, called HPV, starts cancer of the cervix, a part of the womb. A vaccine against that virus prevents most of those cancers. An Indian-made vaccine of this kind was approved in 2022. Many cancers can be cured when they are found early. Warning signs include a lump, a sore that does not heal, or bleeding with no cause.
- e.g. Mouth cancer: a white or red patch or a sore in the mouth that does not heal.
- e.g. Lung cancer: most cases are in people who smoked for many years.
- e.g. Some people get cancer with no known risk factor. Nobody is to blame for it.
### Risk: what raises the chance, and what it cannot tell · *defined*
**Definition:** A risk factor raises the chance that a person gets a disease. It does not decide what happens to any one person.

Infectious diseases have one necessary cause, which is the germ. Most non-infectious diseases have many causes that add up. Each one raises or lowers the chance. So two things are true together. People who use tobacco get lung cancer far more often than people who do not. Yet some smokers live long, and some people who never smoked get lung cancer. One person's story cannot settle the question. Only a comparison of large groups can. In 1950 the British scientists Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill compared hospital patients in London. Almost all the lung cancer patients had smoked, and heavy smokers were at most risk. Then they followed about 40,000 doctors for many years. The doctors who smoked died of lung cancer far more often. Some risk factors can be changed, such as tobacco, food, movement and dirty air. Others cannot, such as age and genes. Many depend on where a person lives and how much money the family has. So a risk is a reason to change what can be changed. It is never a reason to blame a person who is ill.
- e.g. Risk factors a person can change: tobacco, alcohol, little movement, much sugar and salt.
- e.g. Risk factors a person cannot change: age, genes, the air of the city they live in.
- e.g. In the Risk Dice Test, about half the people in the tobacco group stay well.
### How people find out what kind of disease it is · *defined*
**Definition:** People find out how a disease spreads by studying many cases together: where they are, when they began, and what the ill people share.

This science is called epidemiology. A contagious disease leaves a pattern. Cases come in chains, and each new case had contact with an earlier one. The number of cases climbs faster and faster, and then falls. People who nurse the ill fall ill too. A disease that is not contagious leaves other patterns. The cases may be scattered. Or they may cluster around one shared thing, such as a well, a factory, a forest or a kind of food. A cluster alone does not prove that a disease is catching. A cluster shows only that the ill people share something. The next step is to find out what they share. Then comes a test. If the cause is removed, do the cases stop? Finding a germ in ill people is strong evidence. The German doctor Robert Koch set out rules for this in the 1880s. A germ must be found in the ill, grown alone, and shown to cause the disease.
- e.g. In the Two Maps Test, every new ill house on the catching map stands beside an earlier ill house.
- e.g. People who drink from one well with too much fluoride all get stained teeth. That cluster is not catching.
- e.g. Nurses who cared for pellagra patients never got pellagra. That was a clue that pellagra is not infectious.
### Prevention: block the road, or lower the risk · *defined*
**Definition:** An infectious disease is prevented by blocking the germ's road or by a vaccine. A non-infectious disease is prevented by removing its cause or lowering the risk.

Each protection works against some diseases and not others. Soap and clean hands block the touch road and part of the water and food road. Boiled or filtered water, covered food and toilets block the water and food road. Nets, covered skin and no standing water block the mosquito road. Fresh air, masks and covered coughs thin out the air road. Clean needles and tested blood block the blood road. A vaccine protects against one disease by any road. So a mask does nothing against malaria, and a mosquito net does nothing against cholera. For non-infectious diseases, the protection is different. Mixed food with iron, iodised salt and vegetables prevents deficiency diseases. An hour of movement each day, little sugar and salt, and no tobacco lower the risk of heart disease and diabetes. Clean air protects the lungs. Much protection cannot be done by one family alone. Clean piped water, sewers, vaccines, mosquito control and clean air are the work of a whole town. In India about a million women health workers called ASHAs carry vaccines, ORS and advice to homes. The World Health Organization honoured them in 2022.
- e.g. Cholera in a town stops when the water supply is made clean.
- e.g. Iodised salt has made goitre rare in most of India.
- e.g. A person with a cough who wears a mask protects everyone in the room.
### Where the two kinds of disease meet · *defined*
**Definition:** The two groups are useful, but some diseases belong partly to both. A germ can start a disease that is not catching, and a non-infectious condition can open the way for a germ.

Most stomach ulcers were blamed on worry and spicy food. In the 1980s two Australian doctors showed that a bacterium causes most of them. A virus starts cancer of the cervix, and another virus starts much liver cancer. So vaccines against germs now prevent some cancers. The link runs the other way too. A child who gets too little food catches infections more easily and takes longer to recover. A person with diabetes is about three times as likely to fall ill with TB. One inherited condition even protects against an infection. People with one sickle cell gene are well, and they are less likely to die of malaria. The scientist Anthony Allison showed this in East Africa in 1954. This is why the sickle cell gene is common where malaria has been common. A body is one whole, so anything that changes one part can change how the body meets a germ.
- e.g. A bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, causes most stomach ulcers. Antibiotics cure them.
- e.g. The hepatitis B vaccine, given to babies in India, prevents much liver cancer later in life.
- e.g. Good food is part of the treatment of TB.
### Is it possible to be too clean? · *debated*
**Definition:** Allergies and asthma have become much more common in the last hundred years. Some scientists think one cause is that children now meet fewer microbes early in life.

This idea is called the hygiene hypothesis. It does not say that washing hands is harmful. Hand washing, clean water and vaccines save millions of lives, and nobody disputes that. The debate is about which microbes a child's defences need to meet, and when.
- e.g. Children who grow up on farms with cows have less asthma than children in the nearby towns.
- e.g. Hay fever was rare two hundred years ago and is common now.
- **David Strachan, a British doctor, in 1989:** He studied thousands of British children. Those with more older brothers and sisters had less hay fever. He suggested that infections caught from older children in early life protect against allergy.
- **Graham Rook, a British scientist, from the early 2000s:** He argued that colds and other infections are not what protects. What matters is meeting the harmless microbes of soil, farm animals and other people. Human bodies lived with these for millions of years. He calls them 'old friends'.
- **Many doctors today:** They say that allergy has several causes together, such as dirty air, changed food and genes. They warn that the phrase 'too clean' misleads people. Nobody should give up soap, clean water or vaccines because of it.

## Mostly agreed
- Infectious diseases are caused by germs: particular bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites. *How we know:* In the 1870s and 1880s Robert Koch found the bacteria of anthrax, tuberculosis and cholera in ill humans and other animals. He grew each one alone and showed that it caused the disease. The same has since been done for hundreds of diseases.
- Malaria is carried from person to person by female Anopheles mosquitoes, and not by air or touch. *How we know:* In 1897 in Secunderabad, Ronald Ross found malaria parasites in the stomach wall of a mosquito who had fed on a patient. Where mosquitoes are controlled and people sleep under nets, malaria falls.
- Tobacco causes lung cancer, mouth cancer and heart disease. *How we know:* In 1950 Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill compared lung cancer patients with other patients in London hospitals. They then followed about 40,000 British doctors for decades. Smokers died of lung cancer many times more often, and those who stopped smoking lowered their risk.
- Germs of tuberculosis and measles travel through the air in particles small enough to float for a long time. *How we know:* In the 1950s Richard Riley and William Wells piped air from a tuberculosis ward in Baltimore to cages of guinea pigs. The guinea pigs caught tuberculosis from the air alone. When the air was treated with ultraviolet light first, they did not.
- HIV does not pass on through touch, shared food, toilets, coughs or mosquito bites. *How we know:* Studies of thousands of families living with a person who has HIV found no spread through everyday contact. The virus does not multiply inside mosquitoes.
- In India, non-infectious diseases now cause more deaths than infectious diseases. *How we know:* A large study of every Indian state counted the causes of death. In 1990, non-infectious diseases caused about 38 in every 100 deaths. In 2016 they caused more than 60 in every 100.

## How we found out
**Joseph Goldberger, a doctor in the United States Public Health Service. Mary Farrar Goldberger, his wife, who volunteered. Conrad Elvehjem, an American chemist., Goldberger began in 1914. The volunteer tests were in 1916. Niacin was identified in 1937..** In the early 1900s a disease called pellagra spread across the southern United States. It caused a rough red rash, loose motions and confusion, and thousands died each year. Cases crowded together in orphanages, prisons and poor villages. So most doctors were sure that a germ caused it. In 1914 the government sent Dr Joseph Goldberger to find the germ. He noticed something else. In the orphanages and hospitals, the children and patients got pellagra. The nurses and staff, who touched them every day, never did. The staff ate meat, milk and vegetables. The patients ate mostly maize bread and syrup. Goldberger gave children in two orphanages fresh meat, milk and vegetables, and their pellagra went away. In 1916 he, his wife Mary and other volunteers swallowed capsules of scabs from patients. They also took injections of patients' blood. None of them fell ill. Pellagra was a deficiency disease. In 1937 Conrad Elvehjem found the missing nutrient, a B vitamin now called niacin.  
*What people believed before:* That pellagra was an infectious disease, because the cases crowded together. The cases crowded together because poor people in one place ate the same poor food. Staff who shared the rooms but not the food stayed well, and volunteers who took in material from patients stayed well.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Bad-smelling air from rubbish and marshes causes diseases such as cholera and malaria.** (Most doctors in Europe and India until the late 1800s. The idea was called miasma.; *rejected*)  
  For: Diseases were common in dirty, crowded and marshy places. Cleaning those places did reduce disease.  
  Against: Germs were found for each disease, and their roads were traced to water and mosquitoes. A bad smell alone makes nobody ill. Yet the people who believed in miasma were right that fresh air matters. Germs of some diseases do float in the air of closed rooms.
- **Getting wet in the rain or going out with wet hair gives you a cold.** (A common belief in many countries; *rejected*)  
  For: Colds are more common in the rainy and cold months. People often remember being wet before a cold began.  
  Against: A cold is caused by a virus, and nobody catches a cold without meeting the virus. In wet and cold months people stay close together indoors with the windows shut, so viruses pass more easily. Some studies suggest a chilled nose fights viruses less well, but that is not settled.
- **A lifestyle disease is the ill person's own fault.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Tobacco, little movement and sugary food do raise the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers.  
  Against: Risk also depends on genes, age, dirty air and the food a family can afford. Many people fall ill with no risk factor that they could have changed. A risk raises the chance for a group and does not explain one person's illness.
- **Cancer never has anything to do with germs.** (Most doctors before the 1980s; *rejected*)  
  For: Cancer does not pass from one person to another, and it begins in the body's own cells.  
  Against: The German scientist Harald zur Hausen found a virus, HPV, in cancers of the cervix in 1983 and 1984. A vaccine against that virus now prevents most of these cancers. The cancer itself is not catching, though the virus who starts it passes between people.
- **Becoming very overweight spreads from friend to friend, like an infection.** (Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler, two American scientists, in 2007; *debated*)  
  For: They studied about 12,000 people over 32 years. When a person became very overweight, their close friends were more likely to become so too.  
  Against: Other scientists used the same method on things that cannot spread, such as height and headaches. The method seemed to show that those spread too. Friends also choose each other because they are alike, and they share the same shops and streets.
- **Flu and COVID-19 spread only by big droplets that fall within a metre or two.** (Many health agencies until 2020 and 2021; *rejected*)  
  For: People closest to an ill person are the most likely to catch these diseases.  
  Against: In 2020, 239 scientists led by Lidia Morawska and Donald Milton wrote to the World Health Organization. They showed that people had been infected across large rooms with poor ventilation. Health agencies now agree that these viruses also float in small particles, so fresh air matters.

## Roots
We have made two maps with dice and learned how people tell a catching illness from one that is not. Now we ask what this tells us about one person and many people.

**Deep truth.** In the Two Maps Test, both maps had the same number of ill houses. Any one ill house, looked at alone, could belong to either map. On the catching map, every new ill house stood beside a house that fell ill earlier. So people learn whether an illness is catching by studying many patients together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of an illness as something inside one body, to be found by examining that body. But whether an illness is catching is not found inside one body. It is found by looking at how the cases are connected to each other. This shows in the pattern of who fell ill, where and when. The same is true of a risk. One smoker's life cannot show what tobacco does. The lives of thousands of people can.  
*Evidence:* In the Two Maps Test, the two maps have equal numbers of ill houses, and only the pattern differs. In the Risk Dice Test, no single ill person shows whether the risk made them ill, but the two groups of 30 differ clearly. Goldberger found out that pellagra was not infectious by comparing patients with their nurses. If your own maps or groups came out differently, write down what you got and run them again.

- *who am i:* Your health depends partly on you and partly on the people around you. The water, the air and the vaccines that you share with your town protect you as much as your own habits do.
- *what is life:* Germs are living beings, and they need a way to reach the next body. People, mosquitoes, ticks and germs all live close together. An illness is one thing that can happen when their lives meet.
- *what is this world:* Some facts cannot be seen by looking at one thing alone. They show up only when you look at many things together. The pattern on the map was one example.

A doctor examines one patient, and that is how most of us think about illness. But the two maps showed that some facts are about the connections between people. A catching illness passes from one person to the next. You cannot see that by looking at one ill person. A risk is a difference between two groups. You cannot see that in one person either. So health is something people share, as well as something each person has alone. Clean water, clean air and vaccines work this way: each protects a person by protecting everyone around them.

- **Charaka Samhita, Vimana Sthana, chapter 3:** A student asks how one disease can strike a whole town at once, when the people differ in body, food and habits. The teacher answers that they share four things: the air, the water, the place and the season. (This is the reasoning of the Two Maps Test, written down in India roughly two thousand years ago. When many different people fall ill together, look for what they share.)
- **The story of Kisa Gotami, in the commentary on the Dhammapada, verse 114:** A mother whose child has died asks the Buddha for medicine. He asks her to bring a mustard seed from a house where nobody has ever died. She goes from door to door and finds no such house. (Kisa Gotami learned something about her own loss only by visiting many houses. In this unit too, some truths about one illness appear only when many homes are looked at together.)
- **Hippocrates, On Airs, Waters and Places, opening section:** A doctor who arrives in a new town should first study its winds, its water, its soil and the way its people live. (The Greek doctors did not know about germs. But they saw that the diseases of a town follow what the whole town breathes, drinks and eats.)
- Science (settled): Whether a disease passes from person to person is found by studying the pattern of many cases, and then confirmed by finding the germ.
- Science (settled): A vaccinated person protects the people around them, because the germ cannot pass on through that person to the next one.
- Science (debated): Some scientists think that habits such as smoking and overeating also spread between friends, somewhat as germs do. Others think the studies were misread.
- Other view, Many doctors who treat patients one at a time: They would say that a pattern in thousands of people tells little about the one person in front of them. An average says nothing certain about this patient's body or this patient's future. For them, the truth about an illness is found by listening to the ill person and examining them. They warn that people treated as numbers are easily forgotten.
- Practice, The air we share (3 min): Sit near an open window with someone from your family. Breathe in slowly through your nose, and breathe out slowly. Do this ten times. The air you breathe out mixes into the room. The other person breathes some of that air in. The window carries part of it away to the street. Now sit quietly for one minute. Think of everyone who shares this air, this water and this street with you.
- Ask: One ill person looks the same whether the illness is catching or not. So how can anyone find out that an illness is catching? What do they have to look at?
- Ask: Your own habits protect you, and so do your town's water and air. Which part of your health belongs only to you?
- Ask: A doctor studies one patient, and a map studies thousands. What does each one see that the other misses?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* One ill person looks the same whether their illness is catching or not. So how can anyone find out that an illness is catching? What do they have to look at? (hint: Think about the Two Maps Test. What did you have to look at before you could tell the two maps apart?)

- *deep:* A person with one sickle cell gene is protected from malaria. A person with two has a serious disease. Is that gene good or bad? (hint: Think about where the person lives, and about how many copies of the gene they have.)
- *big:* Clean water for a town prevents more illness than any medicine. Who should pay for it, and who should decide? (hint: Think about who is protected when one family's water is clean, and who is protected when everyone's is.)
- *big:* Tobacco raises the chance of cancer, but it does not decide it. Is it fair to blame an ill person for a disease that a risk made more likely? (hint: Think about the Risk Dice Test. Could you tell which ill people in the tobacco group would have fallen ill anyway?)
- *big:* People once drove away those who had leprosy or HIV, though neither spreads by everyday contact. Why does fear travel faster than facts? (hint: Think about how people act before they know the road a germ travels.)
- *deep:* A germ who makes people very ill may stop them from meeting others. Is it better for a germ to be mild? (hint: Compare a cold, which lets people walk about, with a disease that keeps people in bed.)

## Story: The Tally on the Wall
*Gowri, her family, Sharadakka and the tally are made up. These facts are real. In 1957, monkeys were found dead in the Kyasanur forest in Shimoga district, in what was then Mysore State and is now Karnataka. People in the villages nearby fell ill with a high fever. Scientists from the Virus Research Centre in Poona, now Pune, found a new virus in the dead monkeys and in the patients. They found that forest ticks carry the virus, and that the disease does not pass from one person to another. It is called Kyasanur Forest disease, or monkey fever. Most people recover, some die, and a vaccine now exists.*

The langurs began to die in the month of March, when the forest floor above Gowri's village was deep in dry leaves.

Gowri's brother Shivu found the first one. He grazed the family's cattle under the trees, and he came home that evening very quiet. 'A big grey one,' he said. 'Lying on the leaves as if asleep. And another by the stream.'

Ajji, their grandmother, did not like it. 'When monkeys die, something is wrong in the forest,' she said.

Ten days later, the fever came to the village. Thimmappa the woodcutter had it first. His head hurt so badly that he could not open his eyes, and his whole body ached. Then two women from the far lane fell ill, and then the boy who herded goats.

People were afraid. 'It jumps from one person to the next,' they said. 'Do not go near those houses.' Nobody visited Thimmappa's wife any more. Nobody would lend her a pot.

Then Gowri's mother came back from gathering leaf litter in the forest, lay down, and did not get up. By night she was burning hot.

'Keep away from her,' the neighbours called from the gate. 'You will all get it.'

'Somebody must give her water,' said Gowri. She was eleven, and she was not going to leave her mother alone. So Gowri and Ajji nursed her. They wiped her face, fed her ragi gruel from a spoon and slept on the floor beside her mat.

Gowri waited to fall ill. She did not. Ajji did not. But Shivu, who had hardly gone near the sickroom, came home from the cattle one evening with the same fever.

That puzzled Gowri so much that she could not sleep. In the morning she took a piece of charcoal from the hearth and went to the back wall of the house. She drew a short line for every person in the village who had fallen ill. There were fourteen lines.

Beside each line she made a second mark if someone in that house had nursed the ill person. Then she walked from house to house and asked one question at each gate. 'Did the one who nursed fall ill too?'

Thimmappa's wife had nursed him for twelve days. She was well. The goat boy's mother was well. An old woman called Sharadakka had gone into four sick houses to help, because she said she was too old to be afraid. She was well.

'If it jumped from person to person,' Gowri said to Ajji, 'Sharadakka would have had it four times over.'

'Then where does it come from?' asked Ajji.

Gowri looked at her fourteen lines again. This time she asked a different question. What did these fourteen people do that the others did not?

Thimmappa cut wood. The two women gathered leaves. The goat boy took his goats under the trees. Her mother gathered leaves. Shivu grazed cattle. One man lived alone at the edge of the forest and met nobody for weeks, and he had the fever too.

Every one of the fourteen went into the forest. The weaver, the potter and the schoolmaster met ill people every day in the lanes. They never went into the forest, and not one of them was ill.

'It is in the forest,' said Gowri. 'It is where the langurs died.'

A week later a jeep came up the cart track with doctors from the laboratory in Poona. They went from house to house and took a little blood from each ill person. A young doctor stopped at the back wall and stared at the charcoal lines.

'Who made this?' he asked.

'I did,' said Gowri. She explained the lines and the second marks. She told him about Sharadakka, and about the man who lived alone. The doctor wrote for a long time in his notebook.

'We have been asking who met whom,' he said. 'You asked who went where. That is the better question.'

The doctors walked into the forest in tall boots. They found a dead langur and looked through the fur with a lens. They dragged a white cloth over the dry leaves and held the cloth up to the light. Small dark specks were walking on the cloth. They were ticks, each no bigger than a grain of ragi.

'The ticks drink the blood of the monkeys,' the young doctor told the village. 'Something in that blood makes monkeys and people ill. When a tick who carries it bites a person, the person gets the fever. An ill person cannot give it to you. A tick can.'

After that, things changed in the village. Shivu tied cloth round his ankles before he took the cattle out. The women rubbed oil on their legs and looked each other over for ticks when they came home. Nobody went near a dead monkey. They sent word to the forest guard instead.

And people went back into the sick houses. They carried gruel to Thimmappa's wife and lent her two pots.

Gowri's mother sat up on the ninth day and asked for buttermilk. Shivu was on his feet a week after her.

The rains washed most of the charcoal off the back wall that June. Gowri did not mind. She had already copied every line into the schoolmaster's old notebook, with the second marks beside them.

**For the parent:** The illness was caused by a germ, yet nobody caught it from an ill person. Gowri saw this in the pattern. The people who nursed the ill stayed well, and everyone who fell ill had been in the forest. Asking what the ill people shared showed the road the germ travelled.

- Ask: The people who nursed the ill stayed well. What does that tell you about how the fever spread?
- Ask: Why did one ill person alone not show where the fever came from?
- Ask: The villagers stayed away from the sick houses. Whom did that protect, and whom did it hurt?
- Ask: Think of an illness in your own home. What would you ask to find out how it arrived?

On the map where the illness is not catching, will any ill houses stand side by side? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Both maps have 36 houses, the same die and the same number of ill houses at the end. Only the rule for falling ill changes. On map A a house can fall ill only from an ill neighbour. On map B the die picks houses anywhere.
- **record:** Fill in the table for map A and map B. Draw a bar chart of the new ill houses on each day, one chart for each map. Then say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Write one sentence for each.
- **results table:** Map | Ill houses in all | Days it took | New ill houses on each day | Ill houses beside an earlier ill house
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw two grids of 6 rows and 6 columns. 2. Number the rows 1 to 6 down the side and the columns 1 to 6 along the top. 3. Label one grid 'A: catching' and the other 'B: not catching'. 4. Read the question aloud and ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Each square is one house. Both maps will end with the same number of ill houses. What do you think will be different about them? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two grids of 36 houses. Number the rows and the columns. Label them A and B. Now guess what will be different. Write your reason.
  - Note: Many children guess that nothing on the map can show it, or that the catching map will simply have more ill houses. Write the guess down and do not correct it.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On map A, write 1 in the house at row 3, column 3. 2. For day 2, find each well house that shares a side with the house marked 1. 3. Roll once for each. On 1, 2, 3 or 4, write 2 in that house. 4. For day 3, do the same around every house marked 2. 5. Go on, one day at a time. Finish the day on which the map reaches 12 ill houses, then stop.  
  *Parent says:* On this map the illness passes only to a house next door. A house passes the illness on for one day. Then the people there are getting better.  
  *Child mode:* Write 1 in row 3, column 3. Roll for each well house beside it. On 1 to 4, that house falls ill. Write the day number. Stop when 12 houses or more are ill.
  - Ask: Why does a house marked 1 not pass the illness on again on day 3? (good: In this model a house passes the illness on for one day only.; The people there are getting better.)
  - Note: Houses that touch only at a corner are not neighbours. If two ill houses share a well neighbour, roll once for each of them. About once in 30 maps the illness stops before 12 houses. Real outbreaks sometimes stop this way too. If that happens, start map A again.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the ill houses on map A. Map B must reach the same number. 2. On map B, roll twice: the first roll is the row, the second is the column. Write 1 in that house. 3. Roll twice again for a second house on day 1. 4. On each new day, pick two houses in the same way and write the day number. 5. If the die picks a house that is already ill, nothing happens. 6. Stop as soon as map B has as many ill houses as map A.  
  *Parent says:* On this map nobody catches anything. Each day the die picks two houses anywhere in the town. Their neighbours make no difference at all.  
  *Child mode:* Count the ill houses on map A. On map B, roll for a row and a column. That house falls ill. Pick two houses each day. Stop at the same number as map A.
  - Note: Map B usually takes 7 to 10 days. That is about twice as long as map A.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the maps side by side. Ask what your child notices first. 2. On each map, circle in red every ill house that has a smaller number in a house beside it. 3. Count the circles on each map. 4. For each map, list how many houses fell ill on day 1, day 2, day 3 and so on. 5. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Both maps have the same number of ill houses. Look at where they are. Now look at the day numbers. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both maps. What is different? Circle each ill house beside an earlier ill house. Count the circles. Then count the new ill houses for each day.
  - Ask: On map A, how many ill houses stand beside an earlier ill house? (good: All of them except the first house.)
  - Ask: Map B has some ill houses side by side too. How did that happen? (good: By chance. The die happened to pick houses that are neighbours.)
  - Ask: How do the numbers of new ill houses change from day to day on each map? (good: On map A they grow each day.; On map B they stay at about two a day.)
  - Note: On map A every ill house but the first is circled. On map B usually between 3 and 9 are circled, by chance alone. So one pair of ill neighbours proves nothing. The whole pattern is the evidence.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Send your child out of the room. 2. Make two new maps, one by each rule, with the same number of ill houses. Do not label them. 3. Call your child back and hand over both maps. 4. Ask which map shows the catching illness, and what the evidence is.  
  *Parent says:* One of these maps was made by the catching rule and one by the other rule. Which is which? Show me the evidence on the map.  
  *Child mode:* Someone makes two secret maps for you. Find the catching one. Use the circles and the day numbers. Say your evidence. Working alone? Make a new pair of maps and check your first result.
  - Note: This takes you about 8 minutes. To save time, your child can make the second pair while you make the first. A child working alone repeats steps 2 to 4 and sees whether the result holds.
  - Check: A map has 14 ill houses. Only 5 stand beside an earlier ill house. About 2 fell ill each day. Which rule made this map? → **The not-catching rule**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to point at any one ill house on map B. 2. Ask whether that one house alone shows which map it is on. 3. Ask for the three sentences: what I thought, what I saw, why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Point at one ill house. Looking at that house alone, could you tell which map this is? So what did you have to look at?  
  *Child mode:* Point at one ill house. Does that house alone show which map this is? Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What did you have to look at to tell the maps apart? (good: Many houses together.; Where the ill houses were and when each one fell ill.)
  - Note: A strong answer says that one ill house looks the same on both maps. The difference is in how the ill houses are joined in place and in time. This is how real diseases are sorted into catching and not catching.
  - Think: A doctor sees one ill person at a time. Who would be able to see the whole map?
### e2 · The Risk Dice Test: does a risk decide who falls ill? (35 min)
- **question:** Tobacco raises the chance of serious illness. Does that mean every tobacco user falls ill, and nobody else does? What can a risk tell us about one person?
- **predict:** Guess first. Each group has 30 people. In the group without tobacco, a roll of 1 means illness. In the tobacco group, a roll of 1, 2 or 3 means illness. How many people will fall ill in each group? Will anyone without tobacco fall ill? Will anyone with tobacco stay well? Why do you think so? Write your guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** Both groups have 30 people and use the same die, with one roll for each person. Only the numbers that mean illness change. The chances are made up to suit a die. Real chances differ from disease to disease.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the two groups of 30. Then make a second table for six rounds with groups of 6. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Write one sentence for each.
- **results table:** Group | People | Fell ill | Stayed well | Fraction who fell ill
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw two blocks of 30 circles, in 5 rows of 6. 2. Label one 'No tobacco' and the other 'Tobacco'. 3. Explain the two rules. 4. Ask for the guesses and reasons, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Each circle is one person, followed for many years. Without tobacco, only a 1 means illness. With tobacco, a 1, 2 or 3 means illness. What will happen in each group? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two groups of 30 circles. Label them No tobacco and Tobacco. Guess how many fall ill in each group. Will anyone without tobacco fall ill? Write your reasons.
  - Note: Many children expect nobody in the first group to fall ill and nearly everyone in the second. Write the guess down.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start with the 'No tobacco' group. Roll once for each circle. 2. If the roll is 1, colour that circle red. 3. Then do the 'Tobacco' group. Colour a circle red on a roll of 1, 2 or 3. 4. Count the red circles in each group and fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Roll once for each person. The die stands for everything we cannot see: genes, luck and the rest of that person's life.  
  *Child mode:* Roll once for each person. In the first group, colour a 1 red. In the tobacco group, colour 1, 2 and 3 red. Count the red circles.
  - Note: Expect about 5 red circles in the first group, and anything from 2 to 9 is common. Expect about 15 in the tobacco group, and 10 to 20 is common.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to compare the two groups. 2. Point to one red circle in the 'No tobacco' group and ask how that happened. 3. Point to one uncoloured circle in the 'Tobacco' group and ask the same. 4. Point to one red circle in the 'Tobacco' group. Ask whether tobacco made this person ill.  
  *Parent says:* Look at this ill person in the tobacco group. Did the tobacco make them ill? Or would they have fallen ill anyway? How could we know?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the groups. Find an ill person without tobacco. Find a well person with tobacco. Now point at one ill tobacco user. Was it the tobacco? Could you ever tell?
  - Ask: Did everyone in the tobacco group fall ill? (good: No. About half stayed well.)
  - Ask: For one ill person in the tobacco group, can you tell whether tobacco was the cause? (good: No. A roll of 1 would have made them ill in either group.; Only a 2 or 3 was because of the tobacco, and a real person does not show a number.)
  - Note: This is the heart of the test. The die shows what real life hides. Of about 15 ill people in the tobacco group, about 5 would have been ill without tobacco. Nobody can say which 5.
  - Check: A man smoked all his life and stayed well until he was 90. What does this show? → **A risk raises the chance but does not decide**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now use small groups. Draw 6 circles for 'No tobacco' and 6 for 'Tobacco'. 2. Roll for all 12 people by the same rules. 3. Write how many fell ill in each small group. 4. Do this six times. 5. Count the rounds in which the 'No tobacco' group had as many ill people as the 'Tobacco' group, or more.  
  *Parent says:* A scientist with little money studies only 6 people in each group. Will the tobacco group always come out worse? Let's test that six times.  
  *Child mode:* Now test small groups of 6 people. Roll for 6 without tobacco and 6 with. Write the two numbers. Do six rounds. Did tobacco always come out worse?
  - Ask: In how many of the six rounds did the tobacco group not come out worse? (good: Often one round, sometimes none or two.)
  - Ask: Why are 30 people better than 6 for this test? (good: With few people, chance can hide the real difference.; Big groups even out the luck.)
  - Note: With groups of 6, the 'No tobacco' group has as many ill people or more in about one round in six. With groups of 30, that almost never happens. This is why health studies need thousands of people.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look back at the guesses from the first step. 2. Ask for the three sentences: what I thought, what I saw, why it happened. 3. Ask what a risk factor is, in your child's own words.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then tell me what a risk does, and what it does not do.  
  *Child mode:* Read your first guess again. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened. Then say what a risk does.
  - Note: A strong answer says that a risk changes how many people in a group fall ill. It does not say who. So nobody should be blamed for an illness, and nobody should feel safe because one smoker stayed well.
  - Think: If you could not see the dice, how would you find out that tobacco was a risk at all?
### e3 · One Opening or Two: which bottle clears smoke fastest? (30 min)
- **question:** Fine particles from coughs can float in a closed room for a long time. Is one open window enough to clear them, or does a room need two openings?
- **predict:** Guess first. A bottle full of smoke is tested three ways: closed, open at the top only, and open at the top and at a side window. Which will clear fastest? Will the bottle with one opening clear much faster than the closed bottle? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Use the same bottle, the same 30 seconds of smoke and the same spot on the table each time. Keep the fan at the same slow speed and the torch in the same place. Only the openings change.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights the agarbatti, holds the bottle over the smoke and does the cutting. Keep the glowing tip at least 5 centimetres from the plastic. Work near an open window and do not breathe the smoke on purpose. Anyone with asthma watches from across the room. Dip the agarbatti in water at the end.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three trials. Write 'thick', 'thin' or 'gone' for the haze at each minute. Then say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Write one sentence for each.
- **results table:** Trial | Openings | Haze at 1 minute | Haze at 2 minutes | Haze at 3 minutes | Time until the haze was gone
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the bottle, the cap and where the side window will be cut. 2. Explain that the bottle stands for a room and the smoke for fine particles in the air. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* This bottle is a small room. We will test it closed, with one opening and with two. Which will clear fastest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The bottle is a small room. The smoke is like fine particles in air. Guess which test clears fastest. Write your reason.
  - Note: Many children expect one opening to work nearly as well as two. Write the guess down.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the lit agarbatti in its holder near an open window. 2. Hold the bottle upside down with its mouth 5 centimetres above the tip. Count 30 seconds. 3. Screw the cap on while the bottle is still upside down. 4. Stand the bottle on the table under the slow fan, in front of the dark cloth. 5. Shine the torch through the side. 6. Leave the cap on. At 1, 2 and 3 minutes, write whether the haze is thick, thin or gone.  
  *Parent says:* This is trial A, the closed room. Watch the grey haze in the torch beam. Is it getting thinner? Where could the smoke go?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up fills the bottle with smoke and caps it. Shine the torch through the side. Look at the haze each minute. Write thick, thin or gone.
  - Ask: The bottle is closed. Why might the haze still get a little thinner? (good: Some smoke sticks to the inside of the bottle.; Some particles settle.)
  - Note: The haze shows best from the side, with the room light dim. In the closed bottle it should still be easy to see after 3 minutes. If you see no haze at the start, hold the bottle over the smoke for a full minute.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the bottle with smoke for 30 seconds again and cap it. 2. Stand it in the same spot. 3. Take the cap off and start the clock. 4. Write the haze at 1, 2 and 3 minutes. 5. Write the time when no haze shows in the torch beam. Stop at 5 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* This is trial B, a room with one open window. The smoke can leave only through the top. Watch the beam and tell me when the haze has gone.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bottle again. Put it in the same spot. Take the cap off and start the clock. Write the haze each minute. Stop when it is gone.
  - Note: With one opening, the air inside has no easy way to be replaced. So the haze usually thins slowly. It may still be there at 5 minutes.
- **s4 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts a window about 4 centimetres square, low in the side of the bottle. 2. Cover the window with tape. 3. Fill the bottle with smoke for 30 seconds and cap it. 4. Stand it in the same spot, with the window facing the moving air. 5. Take off the cap and the tape together and start the clock. 6. Write the time when the haze has gone.  
  *Parent says:* This is trial C, a room with two openings. Air can come in at one and go out at the other. Watch closely, because this may be quick.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts a side window and tapes it shut. Fill the bottle again. Take off the cap and the tape together. Time the haze.
  - Ask: What can the air do now that it could not do with one opening? (good: It can flow through: in at one opening and out at the other.; Fresh air can push the smoky air out.)
  - Note: With two openings the haze usually clears in well under a minute. If it does not, turn the window towards the fan or the open room window.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dip the agarbatti in water. 2. Compare the three times in the table. 3. Ask for the three sentences: what I thought, what I saw, why it happened. 4. Walk through your home and find a room with two openings and a room with one.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Now, which room in our home is like bottle B? Which is like bottle C?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the three times. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened. Find a room at home with two openings.
  - Note: A strong answer says that smoke leaves when fresh air can flow through and push it out. Smoke is not germs. But the fine particles from a cough float and move in the same way.
  - Check: A person with a cough rests in a room. Which gives the people who visit the best protection? → **A window and the door on the far side open**
  - Think: Smoke itself harms the lungs. Which non-infectious diseases does smoky air make more likely?

## Activities
### a1 · Our Family's Illness List: which illnesses were catching? (25 min)
- **record:** Draw a table with four columns: the illness, did someone else at home get it too, the road or cause, and catching or not. Under the table, write one sentence about which road came up most.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to ask two grown-ups which illnesses the family has had. 2. They write eight, such as colds, fevers, loose motions, chickenpox, asthma or blood pressure. 3. Skip anything a person would rather not share.  
  *Parent says:* Which illnesses have we had in our family? Ask whoever you like. Write down eight. Nobody has to share anything private.  
  *Child mode:* Ask two grown-ups which illnesses the family has had. Write eight. Skip anything private.
  - Note: Good finds are colds, fever, loose motions, chickenpox, mumps, dengue, headaches, asthma, high blood pressure and sugar. Ordinary illnesses count. Write no names.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each illness, ask whether another person at home fell ill soon after. 2. Decide which road fits: air, water and food, insects, touch or blood. 3. Write NO GERM if no germ causes it. 4. Write catching or not.  
  *Parent says:* Did someone else at home fall ill soon after? Which road did the germ probably use? Or is there no germ behind it?  
  *Child mode:* For each illness, did someone else at home fall ill too? Name the road, or write no germ.
  - Ask: Does one family's list prove that an illness is catching? (good: No. A few cases at home could be chance.; We would need many families, as in the Two Maps Test.)
  - Note: If you are unsure, say so and write a question mark. A doctor or the earlier cards will settle it. Colds are air. Loose motions are usually water and food. Asthma and blood pressure have no germ.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the catching illnesses and the not-catching ones. 2. Find the road that came up most. 3. Ask which protection would have blocked it.  
  *Parent says:* How many were catching? How many were not? Which road came up most? What would have blocked it?  
  *Child mode:* Count the catching illnesses. Count the others. Which road came up most?
  - Check: Most of a family's illnesses are colds and coughs. Which road do these mostly use? → **Air**
### a2 · Incubation Detective: when did the germ get in? (25 min)
- **record:** Draw one day line from 0 to 30 with three rows of dots, one row for each group. Beside it, write the illness you think each group had, and one sentence of reasons.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a line of days from 0 to 30. 2. Read the table: flu 1 to 4 days, typhoid 6 to 30 days, chickenpox 10 to 21 days. 3. Explain that each visitor came on day 0 and was ill.  
  *Parent says:* Each group met one ill visitor on day 0. The days between the visit and the first symptom are the incubation period. Each illness has its own usual range.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a day line from 0 to 30. Read the three ranges. Day 0 is the visit.
  - Note: Typhoid most often shows up after one to two weeks, so the range is wide. Chickenpox is passed on from about one or two days before the rash. Many people can pass on a germ before they feel ill.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Group P has first symptoms on days 2, 2, 3 and 4. 2. Group Q has them on days 7, 9, 12 and 21. 3. Group R has them on days 11, 13, 14 and 17. 4. Mark each as a dot on the line, one row for each group.  
  *Parent says:* Here are the days when each person in each group first felt ill. Put a dot on the line for each. Then tell me what you notice.  
  *Child mode:* Mark each first-symptom day as a dot. Use one row for each group. What do you notice?
  - Ask: Which group can you name from the days alone? (good: Group P. Only flu shows its first symptoms in 1 to 4 days.)
  - Ask: Groups Q and R overlap. What else would you ask to tell them apart? (good: The symptoms: a slowly rising fever with stomach pain, or itchy blisters.; Where the people ate, and whether they had a rash.)
  - Note: Group P is flu. Group Q is typhoid, and group R is chickenpox. The ranges overlap, so the days alone cannot name Q and R. A doctor uses days and symptoms together.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask how long a visitor with flu leaves children at risk. 2. Ask the same for chickenpox. 3. Ask why a person well on day 5 may still be at risk after a chickenpox visit.  
  *Parent says:* A child is well on day 5 after a visitor with flu. Is that child probably safe from that visit? What about after a visitor with chickenpox? Why is it different?  
  *Child mode:* Well on day 5 after flu: probably safe. Well on day 5 after chickenpox: not yet. Why?
  - Note: Flu is over its incubation period in about 4 days, so day 5 is nearly safe. Chickenpox needs up to 21 days. This is why a child exposed to a disease may be kept at home for a set number of days.
  - Check: A child is well 22 days after meeting a visitor who had chickenpox. What is most likely? → **The child did not catch it from that visit**
### a3 · Our Emergency Card: who to call and what to say (25 min)
- **record:** Make the card. It has the emergency numbers, the address in words, the warning signs for a heart attack and a stroke, and the signs of dehydration. Stick it near the phone or on the fridge.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write 108 for an ambulance and 112 for any emergency. 2. Write the address in words, with a landmark. 3. Add the name and number of a neighbour and the nearest hospital. 4. Check which numbers work in your state.  
  *Parent says:* Which number do we call for an ambulance? Write it large. Now write our address in words, with a landmark that a stranger could find.  
  *Child mode:* Write 108 and 112 large. Write our address and a landmark. Add the nearest hospital.
  - Note: In most Indian states 108 calls an ambulance, and 112 is the national emergency number. Check what works where you live. 1098 is Childline for a child in danger.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Heart attack: heavy chest pain that spreads to the arm or jaw, with sweating. 2. Stroke: face droops, one arm is weak, speech is unclear, all of a sudden. 3. Dehydration: dry mouth, sunken eyes, very little urine. 4. Write each on the card with the action: call 108 now, or give ORS in sips.  
  *Parent says:* A stroke comes suddenly. Look at the face, the arms and the speech. If any one is wrong, call 108 at once. Every minute counts.  
  *Child mode:* Write the warning signs on the card. For a heart attack or stroke, call 108 now. For dehydration, give ORS in sips.
  - Ask: Why does time matter so much in a heart attack or a stroke? (good: The part of the body beyond the blocked artery gets no oxygen.; Its cells begin to die within minutes.)
  - Note: Add the dog bite rule: wash the bite with soap and running water for 15 minutes, then see a doctor the same day. A fever of more than two days, or a cough of more than two weeks, needs a test.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up plays the operator. 2. Your child calls. 3. They say their name, the address, who is ill and what happened, in four short sentences. 4. Swap roles once.  
  *Parent says:* Hello, this is the ambulance. What is your emergency? Your child answers with four sentences: who they are, where they are, who is ill and what happened.  
  *Child mode:* Pretend to call 108. Say your name. Say where you are. Say who is ill and what happened.
  - Check: A grown-up's face droops, one arm is weak and speech is slurred, all of a sudden. What do you do first? → **Call 108 at once**
### a4 · The Clinic Register: which chart shows a catching illness? (25 min)
- **record:** Draw three bar charts side by side. Under each, write its shape in two words, and your guess for the illness. Then write one sentence on what a chart cannot tell you.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Chart X is children absent with fever at one school, on days 1 to 14: 1, 1, 3, 5, 9, 14, 16, 12, 8, 4, 2, 1, 1, 0. 2. Chart Y is new asthma visits at one clinic, on days 1 to 14: 4, 3, 5, 4, 6, 4, 3, 5, 4, 5, 3, 4, 5, 4. 3. Chart Z is new dengue cases in one city, in weeks 1 to 10: 2, 3, 6, 12, 25, 40, 48, 30, 15, 6.  
  *Parent says:* A clinic writes down its numbers each day. Here are three sets. We will draw each as a chart, and then ask what each shape could mean.  
  *Child mode:* Read the three sets of numbers. Chart X is a school. Chart Y is a clinic. Chart Z is a city.
  - Note: Take the numbers one set at a time. Draw each chart with its own title. All numbers are made up, to show shapes.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the three charts on squared paper. 2. For each, write the days or weeks along the bottom. 3. Colour the tallest bar of each chart red.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a bar for each number. Draw all three. Which chart goes up and then down? Which one stays about the same?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each number. Draw all three charts. Colour the tallest bar red.
  - Ask: Which chart stays about the same from day to day? (good: Chart Y, the asthma visits.)
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what shape chart X has. 2. Ask why a catching illness climbs fast and then falls. 3. Ask what could keep chart Y steady. 4. Ask what chart Z cannot tell you about how dengue spreads.  
  *Parent says:* Chart X climbs, peaks and falls. Why would a catching illness do that? Chart Y stays flat. What could cause that? What can chart Z not tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Chart X climbs and falls. Why? Chart Y stays flat. Why? What can a chart not tell you?
  - Ask: Why does a catching illness first climb and then fall? (good: Each ill person passes it on, so cases grow.; Later, fewer people are left who can catch it, or people take protection.)
  - Ask: Charts X and Z have the same shape. Does that show that both pass from person to person? (good: No. Dengue passes by mosquito bites.; A chart shows that many cases are linked. It does not show the road.)
  - Note: Chart X fits flu. Chart Y fits a not-catching illness such as asthma. Chart Z fits dengue after the monsoon. The shape tells you that cases are joined, and not which road joins them.
  - Check: A clinic sees about 4 new patients with the same illness every day for two weeks. Which is most likely? → **An illness that is not catching, or one steady shared cause**
### a5 · Fact or Fear? Answer back kindly (20 min)
- **record:** Write the numbers 1 to 6 and your answer beside each. For two false claims, write a two-sentence reply. The first sentence gives the fact, and the second is kind.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read each claim and wait for true or false. 2. Claim 1: You can catch HIV from a hug. 3. Claim 2: Cancer is catching. 4. Claim 3: A person with diabetes should eat apart from the family. 5. Claim 4: Once all the blisters of chickenpox have dried into scabs, the person is safe to sit beside. 6. Claim 5: A mosquito who bit a person with HIV can pass HIV to you. 7. Claim 6: A person with TB must stay away from everyone for ever.

## Worksheets
### Two kinds of disease, five roads and their shields (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these diseases is caused by a germ?  
   Options: Diabetes / Typhoid / Anaemia / Asthma  
   **Answer:** Typhoid. *Why:* Typhoid is caused by a bacterium who travels in water and food. Diabetes, anaemia and asthma begin inside the body, and no germ causes them.
2. (sort) Sort these diseases into infectious and non-infectious.  
   **Answer:** Cholera → Infectious; Diabetes → Non-infectious; Measles → Infectious; Heart attack → Non-infectious; Malaria → Infectious; Anaemia → Non-infectious. *Why:* Cholera, measles and malaria are each caused by a germ. A heart attack comes from a blocked artery, anaemia from too little iron, and diabetes from sugar that stays in the blood. No germ causes those three.
3. (fill) A disease that passes straight from one person to another is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['contagious']. *Why:* Contagious means passing from person to person. Every contagious disease is infectious, but malaria is infectious and not contagious, because a mosquito must carry the germ.
4. (match) Match each road to a disease that travels by it.  
   **Answer:** Air → Tuberculosis; Water and food → Typhoid; Insects and ticks → Dengue; Touch → Ringworm; Blood and body fluids → Hepatitis B. *Why:* Tuberculosis germs ride in the breath of an ill person. Typhoid germs reach water and food from stools. Dengue needs an Aedes mosquito. Ringworm passes by skin and shared towels. Hepatitis B passes by blood.
5. (truefalse) You can catch malaria by sitting beside a person who has malaria.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A malaria parasite must be carried by a female Anopheles mosquito. A person with malaria is safe to sit beside. A net and no standing water protect the family.
6. (mcq) Which protection blocks the mosquito road?  
   Options: A net over the bed / A mask / Boiled water / Washing hands  
   **Answer:** A net over the bed. *Why:* A net keeps the mosquito from biting. A mask works on the air road, boiled water on the water road, and hand washing on the touch road.
7. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, for an infectious disease.  
   **Answer:** ['The germ gets into the body', 'The germ multiplies while the person feels well', 'The first symptom appears', 'The person gets better or is treated']. *Why:* The germ gets in and multiplies for days before anything is felt. That gap is the incubation period. Then the first symptom appears, and the person recovers or is treated.
8. (truefalse) An antibiotic cures a cold.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A virus causes a cold, and antibiotics kill bacteria only. Rest and drinks help while the body deals with the virus.
9. (mcq) Which of these diseases does a mosquito net not protect against?  
   Options: Malaria / Filariasis / Cholera  
   **Answer:** Cholera. *Why:* Cholera travels by water and food, so a net does nothing against it. Malaria and filariasis are both carried by mosquitoes.
10. (fill) Too little iron in the food causes ____.  
   **Answer:** ['anaemia']. *Why:* Iron is needed to make red blood cells. Too little leaves a person tired and pale. It is a deficiency disease, and no germ causes it.
11. (write) Boiling water protects against cholera but not against diabetes. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Cholera is caused by a germ who travels in dirty water, and boiling kills the germ. Diabetes has no germ in the water behind it. It comes from the body's use of sugar, so a different shield is needed.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the protection must match the road or the cause.
12. (mcq) On a map of a catching illness, which ill houses stand beside an earlier ill house?  
   Options: All except the first one / Only about half of them / None of them / Only the last one  
   **Answer:** All except the first one. *Why:* On the catching map of the Two Maps Test, a house could fall ill only from an ill neighbour. So every ill house except the first had an earlier ill neighbour. On the not-catching map only some did, by chance.
13. (label) Label the five roads that germs travel, with one disease for each.  
   **Answer:** ['Air: for example tuberculosis', 'Water and food: for example typhoid', 'Insects and ticks: for example malaria', 'Touch: for example ringworm', 'Blood and body fluids: for example hepatitis B']. *Why:* Any true disease for each road is a good answer. The roads decide the protection, so naming them is the first step of stopping a disease.
14. (perspective) Six children in one class have a fever and a cough. What should the school do?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. The doctor's view fits the air road: fresh air thins the germs, ill children stay home, and a long cough may be TB. Sending healthy children home helps little. Doing nothing ignores that a cough spreads germs.. *Why:* Strong answers use the road the germ travels, and do not just say the first thing that comes to mind.
15. (draw) Draw a child with a cough. Show two ways to stop the germs reaching the next person.  
   **Answer:** A child coughing into an elbow or a cloth, with a window open behind, or a mask on the child. Two clear labels, such as covered cough and fresh air.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows two real shields on the air road and labels each one.
### Risk, causes and reading patterns (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What does a risk factor do?  
   Options: Raises the chance of a disease / Decides who falls ill / Makes a disease catching / Cures a disease  
   **Answer:** Raises the chance of a disease. *Why:* A risk factor changes how many people in a group fall ill. It does not decide what happens to any one person. Picking 'decides who falls ill' is the most common mistake.
2. (truefalse) A man smoked all his life and stayed well until he was 90. So tobacco is not a risk.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the tobacco group of the Risk Dice Test, about half the people stayed well, and tobacco still tripled the number who fell ill. One person's story cannot show what a risk does.
3. (mcq) What causes a heart attack?  
   Options: A germ in the blood / A blocked artery of the heart / Too much sleep / Cold weather  
   **Answer:** A blocked artery of the heart. *Why:* A fatty layer narrows an artery, and a clot can block it. The heart muscle beyond the block gets no oxygen. Germs and cold weather are not the cause.
4. (sort) Sort these diseases by their main cause.  
   **Answer:** Sickle cell disease → Inherited; Goitre → Missing nutrient; Fluorosis from well water → Surroundings; Type 2 diabetes → Builds up over years. *Why:* Sickle cell disease comes through the genes. Goitre comes from too little iodine. Fluorosis comes from too much fluoride in water. Type 2 diabetes builds up slowly with age, weight, little movement and genes.
5. (count) Each group has 30 people. 5 fall ill without tobacco and 15 with tobacco. How many times as many is that?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 15 divided by 5 is 3. Three times as many fell ill in the tobacco group, and yet half of that group stayed well.
6. (odd) Three of these are non-infectious. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Diabetes / Asthma / Tuberculosis / Anaemia  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Tuberculosis is caused by a bacterium who passes through the air. Diabetes, asthma and anaemia are non-infectious.
7. (mcq) On a map, no ill house stands beside an earlier ill house. What does this suggest?  
   Options: The illness does not pass from person to person / The illness is very contagious / Everyone has the same germ  
   **Answer:** The illness does not pass from person to person. *Why:* A catching illness leaves chains, with each new ill house beside an earlier one. Scattered houses suggest the cases are not linked by contact. Mosquito diseases can look like this too, so the next step is to find what the ill people share.
8. (order) Put these steps in order for finding out whether a disease is catching.  
   **Answer:** ['Mark the ill houses on a map', 'Look for what the ill people share', 'Remove the shared thing and watch the cases', 'Find the germ in the ill people']. *Why:* A map shows the pattern. Looking for what the ill share gives a suspect. Removing the suspect and watching the cases is a test. Finding the germ in the ill people confirms the cause.
9. (truefalse) Many ill people living around one well proves that the illness is catching.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A cluster shows only that the ill people share something. It may be the water in the well, which is not catching from person to person. The next step is to test what they share.
10. (mcq) A child sits beside a classmate who has HIV. Which of these is safe?  
   Options: Sharing a tiffin box / Sharing a used needle / Sharing a bandage with blood on it  
   **Answer:** Sharing a tiffin box. *Why:* HIV does not pass by food, touch, toilets or mosquitoes. It passes by infected blood, a few body fluids and from mother to baby. A used needle or a bloody bandage can carry blood.
11. (fill) The time between a germ getting into the body and the first symptom is the ____ period.  
   **Answer:** ['incubation']. *Why:* During the incubation period the germ multiplies while the person feels well. Chickenpox has 10 to 21 days, and flu has 1 to 4 days.
12. (write) Explain why a person with no symptoms can still pass a germ to someone else.  
   **Answer:** A germ can be in the body and multiplying before any symptom shows. Some people are carriers, who carry the germ for a long time and feel well. Their breath, hands or food can still pass the germ on.. *Why:* A strong answer names the incubation period or a carrier.
13. (write) Why can a map of many cases show something that one ill person alone cannot?  
   **Answer:** One ill person looks the same whether the illness is catching or not. Only many cases together show a pattern, such as chains of houses, a growing number each day, or a cluster around one shared thing.. *Why:* A strong answer says that 'catching' is a fact about how cases are joined, and one case alone has no links to see.
14. (perspective) A neighbour has TB and has started medicine. Should the family stay away?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. The nurse's view fits what is known: TB travels on the air road, so fresh air and treatment block it, and staying away from a person who is on treatment does little. The person with TB needs care. Fear is understandable, but facts show what helps.. *Why:* Strong answers use the air road and the fact that medicine lowers the chance of spreading.
15. (label) Label the three parts of this artery.  
   **Answer:** ['Part A: a fatty layer on the wall', 'Part B: a clot', 'Part C: the space left for blood']. *Why:* A fatty layer narrows the tube. A clot on the layer can block the space that is left for blood. The part of the body beyond the block gets no oxygen.
### Telling them apart, arguing and planning (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Many ill people live around one well, and the nurses who care for them stay well. What is the best next step?  
   Options: Find what the ill share, and test the well / Send everyone out of the village / Give every person an antibiotic  
   **Answer:** Find what the ill share, and test the well. *Why:* A cluster and well nurses suggest a shared cause and not person-to-person spread. The next step is to test what they share and see if the cases stop when it is removed.
2. (mcq) Which fact is the best evidence that a disease does not pass from person to person?  
   Options: Nurses who care for patients every day stay well / Many patients live in one town / Patients feel very ill  
   **Answer:** Nurses who care for patients every day stay well. *Why:* If the disease passed from person to person, the nurses would fall ill. Dr Goldberger used this clue for pellagra. Many patients in one town, or feeling very ill, tell us nothing about the road.
3. (mcq) A catching illness spreads through a school. Why do the daily cases first climb fast?  
   Options: Each ill child passes it on to more children / Children get tired of school / The germ gets stronger each day  
   **Answer:** Each ill child passes it on to more children. *Why:* Each ill child can pass the germ to several others, so the number grows each day. It slows when fewer children are left to catch it, or when people take protection.
4. (mcq) One sickle cell gene protects against malaria. Why is the gene common where malaria has been common?  
   Options: Carriers of one copy survived malaria more often / Malaria causes the gene / Mosquitoes carry the gene  
   **Answer:** Carriers of one copy survived malaria more often. *Why:* People with one copy are well and are less likely to die of malaria. So they had more children, and the gene stayed common. People with two copies have a serious disease.
5. (truefalse) Stomach ulcers are caused by worry and spicy food, and by nothing else.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed in the 1980s that a bacterium causes most stomach ulcers. Antibiotics cure them.
6. (truefalse) A vaccine protects only the person who gets it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A vaccinated person cannot pass the germ on, so the chain stops at that person. This protects people around them, such as babies who are too young for the vaccine.
7. (match) Match each cause to a disease it can lead to.  
   **Answer:** Too little iodine → Goitre; Tobacco → Mouth cancer; Genes from both parents → Sickle cell disease; Standing water → Dengue; A virus who stays for years → Cancer of the cervix. *Why:* Iodine is needed to make a hormone, and too little swells the neck. Tobacco damages the cells of the mouth. Sickle cell disease is inherited. Standing water breeds Aedes mosquitoes. The HPV virus starts most cancers of the cervix.
8. (count) Suppose 20 of 400 non-smokers and 80 of 400 smokers get lung disease. How many times as likely are smokers?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* Smokers: 80 out of 400, which is 1 in 5. Non-smokers: 20 out of 400, which is 1 in 20. One in 5 is 4 times 1 in 20.
9. (fill) A mosquito who carries a germ from one person to the next is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['vector']. *Why:* A vector is an animal who carries a germ between people. Female Anopheles mosquitoes are the vectors of malaria, and female Aedes mosquitoes of dengue.
10. (write) Soap and water help against cholera but not against malaria. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Cholera germs travel on hands, food and water, so clean hands block part of that road. Malaria germs travel inside a mosquito and enter by her bite. Soap does nothing to the mosquito's bite.. *Why:* A strong answer matches the protection to the road.
11. (write) In groups of 6, tobacco did not always look worse. What does this say about how many people a study needs?  
   **Answer:** With few people, luck can hide the real difference between the groups. With many people, luck evens out and the real effect shows. So a health study needs hundreds or thousands of people.. *Why:* A strong answer says that chance matters most in small groups.
12. (write) Some scientists think children need to meet harmless microbes early in life. What evidence would support this idea?  
   **Answer:** Children who grow up on farms or with older brothers and sisters would need to have less allergy and asthma. The pattern should also hold after other causes, such as dirty air, are taken into account.. *Why:* A strong answer asks for a pattern across many children and for other causes to be ruled out.
13. (perspective) Is it fair to say that a person with a lifestyle disease caused it themselves?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A risk raises the chance for a group and does not decide for one person. Many causes add up, and some cannot be changed. So a risk is a reason to change what can be changed, and not a reason to blame a person who is ill.. *Why:* Strong answers use the idea that a risk raises the chance but does not decide.
14. (draw) Draw a map of nine houses with an illness that is not catching. Put all the ill houses around one well.  
   **Answer:** A grid of nine houses with a well marked in the middle. The ill houses are the ones closest to the well, and they are not joined in a chain of earlier and later cases.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a cluster around one shared thing, marked as the well, and not a chain from house to house.
15. (mcq) Which of these would lower the chance of a heart attack?  
   Options: Daily movement and no tobacco / A mosquito net / Boiled water  
   **Answer:** Daily movement and no tobacco. *Why:* A heart attack comes from a blocked artery, and movement, no tobacco and less salt and fried food lower the chance. A net and boiled water protect against infections.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a vector?  
   Options: An animal who carries a germ between people / A medicine that kills germs / A symptom of a fever  
   **Answer:** An animal who carries a germ between people. *Why:* A vector carries a germ from one person to the next. A female Anopheles mosquito is the vector of malaria. A medicine is not a vector, and a fever is a symptom.
2. (mcq) Which disease passes through the air?  
   Options: Tuberculosis / Cholera / Dengue  
   **Answer:** Tuberculosis. *Why:* Tuberculosis germs ride in droplets and aerosols from a cough. Cholera travels by water and food, and dengue by an Aedes mosquito.
3. (mcq) How does dengue reach a new person?  
   Options: By a mosquito bite / By dirty water / Through the air  
   **Answer:** By a mosquito bite. *Why:* A female Aedes mosquito carries the dengue virus and passes it on when she bites. A person with dengue is safe to sit beside.
4. (mcq) Which shield protects against typhoid?  
   Options: Boiled water and washed hands / A mosquito net / An open window  
   **Answer:** Boiled water and washed hands. *Why:* Typhoid travels by water and food. Boiling water and washing hands block that road. A net blocks mosquitoes, and an open window thins germs in the air.
5. (truefalse) Hugging a person who has HIV can pass the virus on.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. HIV passes only by infected blood, a few other body fluids and from mother to baby. Hugging, shared food and toilets do not pass it on.
6. (mcq) Which of these diseases is non-infectious?  
   Options: Measles / Asthma / Chickenpox  
   **Answer:** Asthma. *Why:* Asthma is the airways reacting to smoke, dust or pollen, and no germ causes it. Measles and chickenpox are caused by viruses.
7. (mcq) What happens in a stroke?  
   Options: An artery in the brain is blocked or bursts / A germ enters the brain through water / The heart beats too slowly  
   **Answer:** An artery in the brain is blocked or bursts. *Why:* Brain cells beyond a blocked or burst artery get no oxygen and begin to die within minutes. This is why a stroke is an emergency.
8. (mcq) In diabetes, what stays too high in the blood?  
   Options: Sugar / Iron / Germs  
   **Answer:** Sugar. *Why:* Insulin lets sugar pass from the blood into the cells. In diabetes there is too little insulin, or the cells do not respond to it, so sugar stays in the blood.
9. (truefalse) Cancer passes from one person to another by touch.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Cancer begins in a person's own cells. A few cancers are started by a germ, such as HPV, but the cancer itself is not catching.
10. (mcq) Which pattern on a map suggests a catching illness?  
   Options: Each new ill house is beside an earlier ill house / Ill houses are spread evenly / Ill houses are all in one colour  
   **Answer:** Each new ill house is beside an earlier ill house. *Why:* A catching illness passes from house to house, so new cases appear beside earlier ones and form chains. An even spread or one colour says nothing about chains.
11. (mcq) Two groups of 30 people have 5 and 15 ill people. What does this show about the second group?  
   Options: The risk is higher, but not everyone fell ill / Everyone in it falls ill / The risk is the same  
   **Answer:** The risk is higher, but not everyone fell ill. *Why:* Three times as many fell ill, so the chance was higher. But half the group stayed well, so the risk did not decide who fell ill.
12. (mcq) Which of these is something scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Whether too little early contact with microbes raises allergies / Whether hand washing prevents cholera / Whether mosquitoes carry malaria  
   **Answer:** Whether too little early contact with microbes raises allergies. *Why:* The hygiene hypothesis is still being tested. Hand washing and the mosquito road of malaria are settled facts.

## Applications
- Health workers mark new cases on a map and a calendar to find the source of an outbreak, and to decide where to send nets, vaccines and clean water.
- Clean piped water and sewers protect a town from cholera, typhoid and hepatitis A better than any medicine.
- In India, tests and medicines for tuberculosis are free at government health centres. A person who takes every dose is cured.
- ASHA workers visit homes in villages and towns with vaccines, ORS packets and advice. India has about a million of them.
- Doctors measure blood sugar with a finger prick and blood pressure with a cuff, to find diabetes and heart disease before they cause harm.
- Iodised salt has made goitre rare in most of India, and iron tablets given by health programmes help to prevent anaemia.
- A district health office draws a chart of new fever cases each week. A sharp rise after the monsoon tells it to look for standing water.

## Open questions
- **Does meeting more microbes early in life protect children from allergies and asthma?** Children on farms have less asthma, but many other things differ between farms and cities. Nobody has yet separated the causes.
- **Why do some people infected with the same germ stay well while others become very ill?** Age, genes, food and earlier infections all matter. Nobody can yet predict who will become very ill.
- **How many long-term diseases, such as heart disease and some cancers, have a germ among their causes?** A few do, such as HPV and the stomach bacterium. Scientists are testing others, and for most the evidence is still weak.
- **Can a vaccine give full and lasting protection against malaria?** Two malaria vaccines are now in use. Both give only partial protection, and the malaria parasite changes her coat in the body.
- **Can the habits of friends spread from person to person, somewhat as germs do?** Some studies say yes. Others found the same 'spread' for height and headaches, so the method may be misleading.

## Connected maths
- In the Risk Dice Test, 5 of 30 people without tobacco fell ill, and 15 of 30 with tobacco. Write each as a fraction in its simplest form and as a percentage. **1 in 6, about 17 percent, and 1 in 2, which is 50 percent.**. 5 out of 30 is 1 out of 6, because both numbers divide by 5. 1 divided by 6 is about 0.17, which is 17 percent. 15 out of 30 is 1 out of 2, which is 50 percent. The risk is about 3 times as high. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Risk Dice Test, a person in the tobacco group falls ill on a roll of 1, 2 or 3. What fraction of rolls make a person ill? In a group of 600 people, about how many fall ill? **3 out of 6, which is 1 half. About 300 people.**. The numbers 1, 2 and 3 are 3 of the 6 faces on the die, so the chance is 3 out of 6, or 1 half. Half of 600 is 300. In real life the number will be near 300, and not exactly 300. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- In a made-up pair of maps for the Two Maps Test, both maps had 14 ill houses. The catching map took 4 days. The not-catching map took 8 days. How many ill houses a day was that on each map, on average? Why does the catching map go faster? **3.5 a day on the catching map and 1.75 a day on the other. On the catching map each ill house makes more ill houses.**. 14 divided by 4 is 3.5. 14 divided by 8 is 1.75, which is half as many. On the catching map each new ill house can pass the illness to its neighbours, so the number each day grows. On the other map the die picks two houses a day, whatever the neighbours do. (queues `math.division.L5`)
- In a made-up record, four people first felt ill on days 12, 14, 15 and 17 after meeting a visitor with chickenpox. What are the shortest and longest incubation periods? What is the difference? **The shortest is 12 days and the longest is 17 days. The difference is 5 days.**. The earliest first-symptom day is 12 and the latest is 17. 17 minus 12 is 5. The usual range for chickenpox is 10 to 21 days, so these numbers fit inside it. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- About 100 million people in India have diabetes, and India has about 1,400 million people. About what fraction of all people is that? About what percentage? **About 1 in 14, which is a little over 7 percent.**. 100 out of 1,400 is 1 out of 14, because both numbers divide by 100. 1 divided by 14 is about 0.07, which is 7 percent. These are rounded numbers, so the answer is a rough guide. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- In India, non-infectious diseases caused about 38 in every 100 deaths in 1990 and more than 60 in every 100 in 2016. Did the share double? How much did it rise by? **No. It rose by more than 22 in every 100, which is about 1.6 times as large.**. 60 minus 38 is 22, and the real rise is a little more. Double 38 would be 76, which is far more than 60. So the share did not double. 60 divided by 38 is about 1.6. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A mosquito can take about a week to grow from an egg to an adult. A family empties a bucket every 5 days. Another family empties theirs every 9 days. Which family stops mosquitoes from growing in the bucket? Why? **The first family. An egg laid just after emptying has 5 days to grow, which is less than a week, and then the bucket is emptied again.**. Eggs can become adults in about a week. If the water is thrown out every 5 days, no egg lasts long enough. If it is thrown out every 9 days, eggs laid just after emptying have 9 days and some grow into adults. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- In a clinic register for flu, the new cases each day were 1, 3, 9 and 27. What pattern is it? If the pattern went on, how many new cases would there be on day 6? **Each day has 3 times as many as the day before. On day 6 there would be 243.**. 1 times 3 is 3, and 3 times 3 is 9, and 9 times 3 is 27. Day 5 is 81 and day 6 is 243. Real outbreaks slow down as fewer people are left to catch the illness, so the pattern cannot go on for long. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is the difference between an infectious and a non-infectious disease? → A germ causes an infectious disease. No germ causes a non-infectious disease, and nobody catches it.
- Name the five roads that germs travel. → Air, water and food, insects and ticks, touch, and blood and body fluids.
- Is malaria contagious? → No. It is infectious, but a mosquito must carry the germ.
- What does a mosquito net protect against? → Malaria, filariasis and other diseases that mosquitoes carry.
- What is an incubation period? → The time between a germ getting in and the first symptom.
- What happens in a heart attack? → An artery that feeds the heart muscle is blocked.
- What does a risk factor do? → It raises the chance of a disease. It does not decide who falls ill.
- Can you catch HIV from a hug? → No. HIV passes only by infected blood, a few body fluids and from mother to baby.
