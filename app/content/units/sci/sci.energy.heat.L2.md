# Hot and cold: what makes warmth, and what only keeps it in
`sci.energy.heat.L2`

**Summary.** We call a sweater warm, but does a sweater make any warmth? Your child puts two katoris of fridge-cold coconut oil on a table, and wraps one of them in a sweater. The bare oil melts first, and the oil inside the sweater stays white and hard for longer. So a sweater makes no warmth. It only slows warmth moving in or out. The warmth you feel inside a sweater comes from your own body, which makes it from food. Your child also hunts for the things that really make warmth: the Sun, fire, electricity in an iron box, rubbing, and living bodies.

**Intro.** *Hold a sweater, a shawl or a blanket against your cheek. Is it warm?* Most people say yes, because we call these warm clothes. But a sweater lying on a chair feels only as warm as the chair. Put it on, and in a minute or two it feels warm. So where did that warmth come from? In this unit you find out which things make warmth and which only keep it in.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say whether things are hot, warm, cool or cold, and that temperature is how hot or cold something is.
1. By the end I can name things that make warmth: the Sun, fire, electricity in an iron or a kettle, rubbing, and living bodies.
2. By the end I can say that a sweater or a blanket makes no warmth, and only slows warmth moving in or out.
3. By the end I can explain that my body makes its own warmth from food, all day and all night.
4. By the end I can do a fair test with two katoris of cold coconut oil, and say what I saw.
5. By the end I can say that hot things cool down and cold things warm up, until they are as warm as the room.
6. By the end I can name ways that people and other animals keep warm and cool down.

## Terms
- **Hot and cold**: Hot and cold are words for how much warmth a thing has. Hot things have a lot of warmth. Cold things have little. *e.g.* An idli straight from the steamer is hot. An ice cube is cold.
- **Warm and cool**: Warm is a little hot. Cool is a little cold. *e.g.* A bath can be warm. Water from a clay matka is cool.
- **Temperature**: Temperature is how hot or cold something is. A thermometer measures it. *e.g.* On a summer afternoon in Chennai, the air has a high temperature. On a winter morning in Ooty, it has a low one.
- **Heat**: Heat is warmth on the move, from a hotter thing to a cooler thing. *e.g.* When you hold a cup of hot milk, heat moves from the milk into your hands.
- **Body heat**: Body heat is the warmth a living body makes from food, all the time. *e.g.* A dog curled up asleep on a mat is warm, because their body makes warmth.
- **Melt**: To melt is to change from a solid into a liquid when warmed. *e.g.* Cold coconut oil is white and hard. When it warms, it melts into a clear liquid.
- **Insulator**: An insulator is a material that slows heat moving through it. Wool, cloth and feathers are insulators. *e.g.* A woollen sweater is an insulator. It slows your body heat from getting out.
- **Shiver**: To shiver is to shake without meaning to. Your muscles do it when you are cold, and it makes warmth. *e.g.* Getting out of a cold bath, you may shiver until you are dry and warm.

## Explanations
### Hot, cold and temperature · *defined*
**Definition:** Temperature is how hot or cold something is.

Your skin can tell hot from cold. A tumbler of fridge water feels cold, and a cup of tea feels hot. Scientists use one word for how hot or cold something is: temperature. A high temperature means hot, and a low one means cold. To measure it exactly, people use a thermometer.
- e.g. Your forehead feels warm to the back of your hand.
- e.g. A steel plate left in the sun at noon feels hot.
- e.g. A tumbler of fridge water feels cold.
### Things that make warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** Warmth is made when sunlight lands on things, when things burn, when electricity flows through an iron or a kettle, when things rub, and inside living bodies.

Some things make warmth. The Sun does, and its light warms what it lands on. A gas flame or a diya burns, and burning makes warmth. An iron box and a kettle turn electricity into warmth. Rubbing your hands together makes them warm. Living bodies make warmth from food all the time, yours, a dog's and a hen's.
- e.g. The sunny spot on the floor where a cat likes to sleep.
- e.g. The flame under the dosa tawa.
- e.g. Your two palms, after you rub them fast.
### A sweater keeps warmth in, but makes none · *defined*
**Definition:** A sweater, a blanket or a quilt is an insulator. It slows heat moving through it, in either direction.

In the Sweater and Coconut Oil test, the bare oil melted first, and the oil in the sweater stayed hard for longer. The room's warmth was moving into both katoris, and the sweater slowed it down. If the sweater made warmth, the wrapped oil would have melted first. When you wear a sweater, your body makes the warmth, and the sweater slows it from leaving you. Wool holds still air between its hairs, and still air lets heat through slowly.
- e.g. A quilt keeps you warm at night because it keeps your body heat in.
- e.g. Chapatis wrapped in a cloth stay warm, because the cloth keeps their heat in.
- e.g. An ice lolly wrapped in newspaper melts more slowly on the way home.
### Your body makes its own warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body makes warmth from food, all day and all night. Inside, you are about 37 degrees Celsius, about as warm as a warm bath.

Food is your body's fuel. Your cells use it slowly, together with the air you breathe, and this gives out warmth. That is why you are warm even while you sleep. When you run or jump, your muscles work harder and make more warmth. Other animals with fur or feathers make their warmth in the same way.
- e.g. After running up the stairs, your cheeks feel warm.
- e.g. A hen sits on her eggs, and her body keeps them warm.
- e.g. Puppies sleep in a heap and keep each other warm.
### Hot things cool down, and cold things warm up · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat moves from a warmer thing to a cooler thing. In the end, both are equally warm.

A hot dosa left on a plate slowly gets cool. Its heat moves out into the plate and the air. A cold tumbler from the fridge slowly gets warmer. Heat from the room moves into it. Each one ends up as warm as the room. That is why the bare coconut oil melted. It had been in the cold fridge, and the warmer room was heating it.
- e.g. A cup of filter coffee forgotten on the table is cool an hour later.
- e.g. Curd taken out of the fridge is no longer cold after a while.
- e.g. An ice cube on a plate melts into a puddle of water.
### Who decides when to shiver? · *debated*
When you get cold, you start to shiver. Tiny bumps called goosebumps come up on your skin. This happens without you planning it. Your skin feels the cold, and your body acts. Your cells and nerves sense the cold and decide what to do about it. Thinking is not only something brains do.
- e.g. Shivering after a swim.
- e.g. Goosebumps on your arms on a cold morning.
- e.g. A crow fluffing up their feathers on a cold window ledge.
- **Many scientists who study living things:** Every living cell senses what is around it and responds. A body is full of these sensing parts, working together. Shivering is the body taking care of itself.
- **Many brain and body scientists:** Shivering is a reflex. Nerves send signals to a part of the brain, and it switches on shivering, like a switch on a heater. They say no one part of the body is deciding.
### Cooling down · *defined*
**Definition:** We cool down when heat leaves our body faster than we make it.

On a hot day, your body still makes warmth. To stay comfortable, that warmth must leave you. A fan blows air past your skin, and the moving air carries warmth away. When sweat dries on your skin, it takes warmth with it. Shade keeps the Sun's light off you. A dog pants, and a cow looks for shade under a tree.
- e.g. Sitting under a ceiling fan in the afternoon.
- e.g. A wet cloth on your forehead on a hot day.
- e.g. A buffalo lying in a pond to cool off.

## Mostly agreed
- A sweater, a blanket or a quilt makes no warmth. It slows heat moving in or out. *How we know:* A thermometer wrapped in a sweater on a chair reads the same as the room. Cold things wrapped in wool stay cold for longer.
- Humans and other animals with fur or feathers make warmth inside their bodies from food. *How we know:* Measurements of breathing and body heat show that the more food a body uses, the more warmth it gives out.
- Heat moves by itself from warmer things to cooler things. *How we know:* Hot food always cools to the warmth of the room, and cold food warms up to it. No one has ever seen it go the other way by itself.
- The inside of a healthy human body stays close to 37 degrees Celsius. *How we know:* Doctors have measured the temperature of millions of people. It changes a little through the day and from person to person.

## How we found out
**Edgar Rey Sanabria, a doctor in Bogotá, Colombia. Later, Sarmila Mazumder, Nita Bhandari and their team in India, 1978 (in Bogotá) and 2019 (the study in Haryana).** For a long time, hospitals kept very small babies warm in incubators. An incubator is a warm box with a clear lid. Many doctors thought a very small baby needed one to stay warm. In 1978, a big hospital in Bogotá, the capital of Colombia, was crowded and had too few incubators. A doctor there, Edgar Rey Sanabria, tried something new. Mothers held their small babies upright against their bare chest, skin to skin, under their clothes, for many hours each day. The mother's body made the warmth, and her clothes kept it around the baby. A mother kangaroo carries her baby in a pouch, so people called this care kangaroo mother care. Many studies tested it after that. In Haryana, in India, a team led by Sarmila Mazumder and Nita Bhandari helped families do it at home. In 2019, they reported that more of the small babies survived. Today the World Health Organization advises it for small babies everywhere, and fathers can do it too.  
*What people believed before:* Many doctors thought a very small baby could only be kept warm safely in an incubator. Skin-to-skin care showed that a parent's own body heat can keep the baby warm.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A sweater or a blanket makes heat.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: When we put on a sweater, we feel warm, and we call woollens warm clothes.  
  Against: In the coconut oil test, the oil inside the sweater stayed cold and hard longest. A sweater on a chair is as cool as the chair.
- **You lose most of your body heat through your head, so a cap matters most.** (A common saying in many countries; *rejected*)  
  For: Your head often sticks out of your clothes, so it does lose heat on a cold day.  
  Against: Tests show the head loses about as much heat as any other bare part of the body of the same size. A bare head loses a lot only because the rest of you is covered.
- **Being cold gives you a cold.** (A common idea in many homes; *debated*)  
  For: Colds are more common in cool, wet weather. In one test in Britain, people who sat with their feet in cold water got cold symptoms more often in the next few days.  
  Against: A cold is caused by tiny germs called viruses, not by cold air. Ellen Foxman, Akiko Iwasaki and their team found that one common cold virus grows better in a cool nose. So cold may help a virus, but the virus is needed.
- **Some foods heat the body and some cool it, like mango and curd.** (A common idea in many Indian homes, from Ayurveda and Siddha; *debated*)  
  For: People say they feel hot or cool after some foods. Hot chilli can make you sweat.  
  Against: Scientists have not found that a mango or a bowl of curd changes body temperature by much. In these traditions, 'hot' and 'cold' foods may describe other effects on the body, not temperature.

## Roots
We have hunted for warm things, wrapped cold coconut oil in a sweater and felt our own warmth. Now we ask what the sweater test tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your sweater kept the cold coconut oil hard, while the bare oil melted. So the sweater made no warmth. It only slowed the room's warmth getting in. When you wear it, the warmth you feel is your own body's.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call sweaters, shawls and quilts 'warm', as if warmth were stored in them. The coconut oil test shows a sweater keeps a cold thing cold just as well. It only slows heat moving in or out.  
*Evidence:* In the Sweater and Coconut Oil test, the bare oil melted first, and the oil inside the sweater stayed white and hard for longer. The same sweater, put on, felt warm within two minutes. In the Warm Spot Race, ice melted fastest in your own hand.

- *who am i:* You are a warm body. Your cells make warmth from food all day and night, even while you sleep.
- *what is life:* Living bodies make warmth and keep it. A hen warms her eggs with her body, and a dog curls up to keep their warmth in.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of warmth on the move: from the Sun, from fire and from bodies into the cooler things around them.

The Sun, a diya flame and your body look like very different things. Each of them is giving out energy as warmth. Your warmth came from your food, and your food got its energy from sunlight that a plant caught. So the warmth inside your sweater began as sunshine on a rice field. A sweater, a nest or a parent's arms keep that warmth near.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2:** Bhrigu asked his father Varuna what the world comes from. After thinking hard, he understood: from food, living beings are born, by food they live. (Your body heat is made from food. Without food, a body has nothing to make warmth from.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 15.14:** Krishna says he is the fire inside the bodies of living beings, which digests their food, joined with the breath going in and out. (Long ago, people called the body's warmth a fire inside. Science agrees that your cells slowly use food with the air you breathe, and this gives out warmth.)
- Science (settled): Your cells use food and oxygen to get energy, and much of that energy comes out as warmth. This is why a living body stays warm.
- Science (idea): Why animals like us keep our insides at about 37 degrees is not known for sure. One idea is that it keeps most fungi from growing in us.
- Other view, Many brain and body scientists: Shivering and goosebumps are reflexes, switched on by nerves and one part of the brain, like a switch on a heater. They say the cells in your skin only send signals, and only the brain feels warm or cold.
- Practice, Feel your own warmth (3 min): Sit still and shut your eyes. Cup your hands in front of your face, without touching it. Breathe out slowly through your mouth. Feel your breath warm your hands. Now rest both hands on your tummy. Feel the warmth there. You made that warmth yourself, from your breakfast. Where was it before it was in your breakfast?
- Ask: A small baby is held skin to skin on a parent's chest. Whose warmth is keeping the baby warm?
- Ask: The Sun, a flame and your body all make warmth. What do they have in common?
- Ask: Shivering and goosebumps happen without you planning them. Who or what decides to start them?

## Story: Arjun's Cold Idli
*Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Ooty, also called Udhagamandalam, is a hill town in the Nilgiris in Tamil Nadu, where winter mornings can bring frost. Meenakshi, Arjun, Paati and Rosie are made up.*

Meenakshi was eight. She lived in Chennai, where it is warm nearly all year. In December, she and her little brother Arjun went to stay with their Paati in Ooty. Ooty is a town high up in the Nilgiri hills. Arjun was five.

On the first morning, Meenakshi woke up and gasped. The air in the room was cold. Outside the window, the grass was white with frost. Frost is tiny bits of ice that form on very cold nights.

'Sweaters on!' called Paati from the kitchen.

Meenakshi's red sweater lay folded on the chair, where it had been all night. She pulled it over her head. 'Brrr! Paati, my sweater is cold!' she said.

'Wait two minutes,' said Paati, smiling.

Meenakshi waited. Slowly, the sweater began to feel warm and soft. She frowned. On the chair it had been cold. On her it was warm. What had changed?

At breakfast, Paati gave them hot idlis with coconut chutney. Arjun ate slowly, because he was watching a big black crow on the wall. The crow had puffed up their feathers until they looked twice as fat. By the time Arjun looked back at his plate, his last idli was cold.

'I know!' said Arjun. 'Sweaters are warm.' He took off his blue sweater and wrapped his idli in it. Then he put the bundle on the chair.

Ten minutes later, he unwrapped it and took a bite. 'Still cold! This sweater is broken!'

Meenakshi laughed. Then she stopped laughing, because she remembered her own cold sweater on the chair. 'Paati,' she said, 'is a sweater warm or not?'

Paati put down her coffee. 'Feel your sweater now, on your arm,' she said. 'Where did that warmth come from?'

Meenakshi thought about it. The chair was not warm. The room was not warm. 'From me?' she said slowly.

'From you,' said Paati. 'Your body makes warmth all day and all night, from the food you eat. Your sweater makes none of its own. It only keeps your warmth close to you. So it does not escape into the cold air.'

'Then why did my idli stay cold?' asked Arjun.

'Because your idli had no warmth left to keep,' said Meenakshi. 'The sweater kept it just as cold as it was.' Paati nodded.

After breakfast, the Sun climbed over the hill. They took their tumblers of milk out to the verandah. Paati's cat, Rosie, was already lying in the one bright patch of sunlight. She had found the warmest spot in the house.

Meenakshi decided to count the things that warmed them. The Sun on the verandah was one. The gas flame under Paati's milk pan was two. Then Arjun rubbed his hands together fast, and they felt warm. That was three. He ran three times round the garden and came back pink and puffing. Running makes a body give out even more warmth. 'And our bodies,' said Meenakshi. 'That is four. And Rosie's body is five.'

'What about our sweaters?' asked Arjun.

'Sweaters do not make warmth,' said Meenakshi. 'They only keep it in.'

At lunch, Paati made dosas. Appa was coming home late from the tea factory. So Paati laid his hot dosas in a steel box lined with a cotton cloth. She folded the cloth over them and closed the lid.

'Arjun, watch,' said Meenakshi. 'The cloth is like your sweater. The dosas were hot when they went in, so the cloth will keep them hot.'

When Appa came home half an hour later, Paati opened the box. A little steam rose out. The dosas were still warm.

Arjun looked at the box, then at his sweater. 'So a sweater keeps hot things hot,' he said.

'And cold things cold,' said Meenakshi. She hugged her sweater around her. 'I'm the heater,' she said, 'and my sweater is the lid.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: Meenakshi notices that her sweater was cold on the chair and warm on her. Arjun's cold idli stayed cold inside a sweater, while hot dosas stayed warm inside a cloth. She works out that a sweater or a cloth makes no warmth. It keeps whatever warmth is already there, and the warmth in her sweater came from her own body.

- Ask: Meenakshi's sweater was cold on the chair. Why did it feel warm after two minutes?
- Ask: Arjun wrapped his cold idli in his sweater. Why did it stay cold?
- Ask: Meenakshi counted five things that make warmth. Can you name three of them?
- Ask: Why did Appa's dosas stay warm in the cloth-lined box?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Sweater and the Coconut Oil: does a sweater warm things up? (45 min)
- **question:** If a sweater makes warmth, the oil inside it should melt first. Does it?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which oil will melt first: the bare one, or the one wrapped in the sweater? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both katoris are the same, with the same amount of oil, and both come out of the fridge at the same time. They sit on the same table, out of the sun and away from the fan. Only the sweater is different. We check both at the same times.
- **record:** Draw the two katoris at each check. Colour the hard oil white and the runny oil yellow.
- **results table:** Katori | My guess | At 10 minutes | At 20 minutes | At 30 minutes
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take both katoris out of the fridge. 2. Let your child tilt them gently and look at the oil. 3. Hand them the sweater to hold.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the oil. It is white and hard, because the fridge made it cold. Now hold the sweater. We call sweaters warm clothes. What do you think a sweater does?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the oil. It is white and hard. Now hold the sweater. What does a sweater do?
  - Ask: What do you think a sweater does? (good: It makes you warm.; It keeps the warm in.; Any answer is fine here. The test will check it.)
  - Note: Coconut oil is white and hard when it is cooler than about 24 degrees, and a clear liquid when it is warmer. In a warm home the oil in the bottle is already runny, and an hour in the fridge makes it hard. If your room is cooler than about 24 degrees, the oil will not melt on the table. Then use two steel tumblers of the same warm tap water instead. Wrap one in the sweater, and feel which one is warmer after 30 minutes.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will wrap one katori in the sweater and leave the other bare. Which oil will melt first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which oil will melt first, the bare one or the wrapped one? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The wrapped one, because sweaters are warm.; The bare one, because the sweater keeps the cold in.)
  - Note: Most children guess the sweater one, because sweaters are 'warm'. Do not say what will happen.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the sweater into a thick pad. 2. Put one katori on it and fold the sweater over the top, so it is covered all round. 3. Put the bare katori beside it. 4. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* Let's wrap this one all round, under and on top. The other one stays bare. Now we start the timer.  
  *Child mode:* Wrap one katori all round in the sweater. Leave the other bare. Start the timer.
  - Note: Keep both out of sunlight and away from the fan, so only the sweater is different.
- **s4 (30 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At 10 minutes, open the sweater just enough to peep in, then close it. 2. Tilt each katori a little. Is there runny oil? 3. Draw both. 4. Do the same at 20 and 30 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Time to peep. Tilt the bare katori. Is the oil runny? Now peep into the sweater, quickly. Is that oil runny?  
  *Child mode:* Peep into both katoris. Tilt them gently. Which oil is runny?
  - Note: While you wait between checks, do the warmth hunt (the Make It or Keep It activity). In a warm room, the bare oil usually turns clear at the edges within about 10 to 15 minutes. It is mostly runny by about 20 to 30 minutes. The wrapped oil stays white much longer. If it is still hard at 30 minutes, that is your answer: the sweater made it slower. You can look again later. In a cooler room, keep checking until the bare oil has melted.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the two katoris side by side, both open. Ask what your child saw, and why. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which oil melted first? What did the sweater do to the cold oil? Did it warm it up, or keep it cold?  
  *Child mode:* Which oil melted first? Did the sweater warm the oil, or keep it cold?
  - Ask: Why did the bare oil melt first? (good: The room was warmer than the oil, and the room's warmth got in.; The sweater stopped the warmth getting in, so that oil stayed hard.)
  - Note: Wait for your child's own claim and reason first. Then say it plainly: the room's warmth was moving into both katoris. The sweater slowed it down. A sweater makes no warmth. It only slows heat moving in or out.
  - Check: The oil in the sweater stayed hard for longer. What does that show? → **The sweater slowed the room's warmth from getting in.**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to put the same sweater on for two minutes, then feel inside it.  
  *Parent says:* Now put the sweater on. Wait two minutes. Does it feel warm now? The sweater makes no warmth. So where is this warmth coming from?  
  *Child mode:* Put the sweater on. Wait two minutes. Is it warm? Where does the warmth come from?
  - Ask: Where does the warmth come from? (good: From me.; From my body. The sweater keeps it in.)
  - Think: If you wrapped a hot dosa in a cloth, what would the cloth do?
### e2 · The Warm Spot Race: where does ice melt fastest? (20 min)
- **question:** Which place melts ice fastest: your hand, a sunny spot, the table or the floor?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which ice cube will melt first, and which last? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The four ice cubes are the same size and come from the same tray. All four go to their places at the same moment. Only the place is different.
- **record:** Write 1, 2, 3 and 4 next to each place, in the order the ice cubes melted.
- **results table:** Place | My guess (1st to 4th) | Minutes until no ice is left | What came 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose four places with your child: your child's hand, a sunny spot, the table and the floor in the shade. 2. Take four ice cubes of the same size from the freezer tray, only when everyone is ready.  
  *Parent says:* We will race four ice cubes. One will sit in your hand. Which one will melt first? Which last? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Which ice cube will melt first? Which will melt last? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: My hand, because I am warm.; The sunny spot, because the Sun is hot.)
  - Note: Keep the tray in the freezer until you start. If no sunlight comes into your home, use a spot next to a warm machine instead, such as a television or a wifi box that has been on for a while.
- **s2 (12 min)**

## Applications
- Families keep chapatis and dosas warm in a cloth or a hot case, which slows their heat from escaping.
- A flask keeps tea hot for hours, and keeps cold water cold, because it slows heat moving in or out.
- Hospitals keep very small babies warm skin to skin on a parent's chest. This is called kangaroo mother care.
- Soldiers on the cold mountains of Ladakh and Siachen wear many layers, because each layer keeps more body heat in.
- Ice-cream sellers keep their boxes thickly lined, so the ice cream stays frozen in the hot sun.
- In 1948, Mária Telkes, a scientist from Hungary working in America, built the heating for a house warmed by stored sunshine. The house was designed by the architect Eleanor Raymond.

## Spotted around us
- In winter in Kashmir, people wear a long woollen cloak called a pheran, and some carry a small clay pot of hot coals, called a kangri, under it.
- In Kerala and Tamil Nadu, cooked rice and curry are often kept warm in a heavy pot with its lid on.
- On winter mornings in Assam and Meghalaya, families sit around a small fire or in the sunshine, wrapped in shawls.
- Street dogs in Indian cities curl up tight on cold nights, and find warm spots like the bonnet of a car that was just parked.
- In Rajasthan and Gujarat, people wear loose white or light cotton clothes and turbans in the summer heat, which keep the Sun off them.
- In a cowshed in winter, calves lie close to their mothers and to each other.

## Open questions
- **Why is the inside of the human body about 37 degrees, and not cooler or warmer?** Nobody knows for sure. One idea, from the scientist Arturo Casadevall and others, is that this warmth keeps most kinds of fungus from growing inside us, while not costing too much food.
- **How does a plant feel a long winter, and remember the cold?** Some plants flower only after a long cold spell. Caroline Dean and other scientists have found that cold slowly switches off a flowering brake in their cells, and the plant remembers this for months. Exactly how the cold is sensed is still being worked out.
- **How much warmth do grown-ups make from special 'brown fat'?** Babies have brown fat that makes warmth. Scientists found in 2009 that many grown-ups have some too. How much it matters for keeping a grown-up warm is still being studied.

Count in tens. 2 checks is 10, 20, so 20 minutes. 5 checks is 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, so 50 minutes. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- In the Warm Spot Race, the ice cube in the hand of Farhan, a boy in Class 2, melted at 4 minutes. The ice cube on the floor melted at 14 minutes. How many minutes longer did the floor ice take? **10 minutes longer.**. Count on from 4 to 14. That is 10. So the floor ice took 10 more minutes. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- In the warmth hunt, Nila, a girl in Class 2, made a tally: Makes warmth: |||| . Keeps warmth in: |||| | . Which column has more, and by how many? **Keeps warmth in has more, by 1. It has 6 and Makes warmth has 5.**. Count the tally marks. The first column has 5 and the second has 6. 6 is 1 more than 5. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- Divya, a girl in Class 2, says: 'The bare oil melted in 20 minutes. So the oil in the sweater will melt in 20 minutes too.' Will it? What would you expect instead? **No. It will take longer than 20 minutes, because the sweater slows the room's warmth getting in.**. The sweater changes how fast warmth reaches the oil. So the times will not be the same. We expect a bigger number for the wrapped oil. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
