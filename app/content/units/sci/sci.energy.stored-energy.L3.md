# Stored energy: how energy waits in a twist, a height, food and a cell
`sci.energy.stored-energy.L3`

**Summary.** Wind up a toy, and it moves later, after you have let go. In between, the energy waits in the toy. This is called stored energy. Energy can wait in the shape of a thing, such as a twisted rubber band or a coiled clock spring. It can wait in height, such as water in the tank on the roof. And it waits in food, fuel and cells, in how their tiny particles are joined. A store can give its energy back all at once, like a jumping toy, or slowly, like a clock that ticks for a week.

**Intro.** *Roll a jar away from you along the floor and let go. It slows down and stops. Then, with nobody touching it, it rolls back to you. What made it come back?* The jar has a rubber band and a small weight inside. Rolling the jar away twisted the band. The twist held energy, and the energy brought the jar back. In this unit you build this roll-back jar and find out how many turns make it come back furthest. You also test a torch cell that seems finished, read the energy on food packets, and find energy waiting all over your home.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what stored energy is, and give examples of energy waiting in height, in a stretched, squashed or twisted shape, in food and fuel, and in cells.
1. I can build a roll-back jar, test how the number of turns changes how far it rolls back, and explain the result with what I think, what I saw and why.
2. I can explain that a store gives its energy back later, quickly or slowly, and that some of it always spreads out as warmth and sound.
3. I can explain why water or a ball held higher has more energy waiting, using the tank on the roof and a dam as examples.
4. I can explain where the energy in food comes from, and read the energy number on a food packet.
5. I can explain what a cell stores, why a tired cell may still run a clock, and how to handle cells safely.
6. I can tell how Robert Mayer's idea about food and body heat was tested, and what wrong idea it replaced.

## Terms
- **Energy**: What makes things move, light up, heat up or make a sound. *e.g.* A ceiling fan needs energy from electricity to turn.
- **Stored energy**: Energy that is waiting in a thing, ready to come out later. *e.g.* A wound-up toy car holds stored energy until you let go of it.
- **Potential energy**: The scientists' name for stored energy. Potential means 'able to happen later'. *e.g.* Water in the tank on the roof has potential energy. It comes out when you open the tap downstairs.
- **Movement energy**: The energy a thing has because it is moving. Scientists call it kinetic energy. *e.g.* A rolling ball has movement energy. A faster ball has more.
- **Elastic**: Able to spring back to its shape after being stretched, squashed, bent or twisted. *e.g.* A rubber band is elastic. Let go of a stretched band and it snaps back.
- **Twist**: Turning one end of a thing while the other end stays still. *e.g.* Wringing out a wet towel twists it.
- **Spring**: A coil of springy metal that stores energy when it is squashed, stretched or wound up. *e.g.* A wind-up wall clock has a flat coiled spring inside. Winding the key coils it tighter.
- **Chemical energy**: Energy stored in how the tiny particles of a material are joined together. It comes out when the material changes, for example when it burns or is digested. *e.g.* Food, LPG gas, candle wax and the chemicals in a cell all hold chemical energy.
- **Fuel**: A material that is burned to give out its stored energy as heat and light. *e.g.* LPG in the kitchen cylinder is a fuel. So are wood, coal, kerosene and petrol.
- **Cell**: A small store of chemical energy that pushes electricity round a circuit. People often call it a battery. *e.g.* A TV remote has two AA cells inside.
- **Battery**: Strictly, two or more cells joined together. A rechargeable battery can be filled with energy again from the wall socket. *e.g.* A phone battery is charged at night and runs down during the day.
- **Kilocalorie (kcal)**: A unit for measuring how much energy is stored in food. Packets often print it as kcal, or just as Calories. *e.g.* A packet of glucose biscuits says about 450 kcal for every 100 grams.
- **Release**: To let stored energy come out, for example by letting go of a stretched band. *e.g.* Opening the tap releases the water stored up in the tank.

## Explanations
### What stored energy is · *defined*
**Definition:** Stored energy is energy that waits in a thing, because of its height, its shape or what it is made of, until it is released. Scientists call it potential energy.

Energy is what makes things move, light up, heat up or make a sound. Some energy is busy, like the energy of a rolling ball. Some energy is waiting. A rubber band that you hold stretched is still, but let go and it flies. Water in the tank on your roof is still, but open a tap downstairs and it rushes out. The energy was there the whole time, waiting. In 1853 a Scottish engineer, William Rankine, gave this waiting energy the name potential energy. Potential means 'able to happen later'. You cannot see stored energy. You can only see what it does when it comes out.
- e.g. A stretched rubber band on your fingers holds stored energy until you let go.
- e.g. A coconut high on a tree holds stored energy. If the coconut falls, that energy becomes movement.
- e.g. A packet of rice in the kitchen holds stored energy that your body can use after you eat it.
### Energy waiting in a shape: stretch, squash, bend and twist · *defined*
**Definition:** An elastic thing stores energy when it is stretched, squashed, bent or twisted. It gives the energy back as it springs back to its shape.

To twist the band inside the roll-back jar, you had to push the jar along the floor. Your push put energy into the band. The twisted band held that energy, as long as the jar stayed still. When you let go, the band untwisted and turned the jar back. The more turns you wound, the more energy the band held, and the further the jar rolled. A bow, a catapult, a trampoline and the spring inside a wind-up clock all work the same way. Something pushes or pulls to change the shape. The shape holds the energy. The energy comes back out as the thing springs back.
- e.g. An archer pulls back the string of a bow. The bent bow stores energy and gives it to the arrow.
- e.g. A wind-up clock's key coils a flat steel spring tighter. The spring then unwinds slowly and turns the clock's wheels.
- e.g. A mattress squashes when you jump on it, then pushes you back up.
### Energy waiting in height · *defined*
**Definition:** When you lift a thing against the Earth's pull, called gravity, you store energy. The higher and heavier the thing, the more energy is stored. It comes out as movement when the thing falls.

Gravity is the pull of the Earth on everything. To lift a water bottle onto a high shelf, your arm works against gravity, and that work is stored. If the bottle falls, the stored energy turns into movement, and it lands with a thud. A bottle on a higher shelf would land harder, because more energy was stored. This is why many buildings in Indian cities have a water tank on the roof. A motor pumps water up into the tank once or twice a day. Then the water stays there, high up, with energy waiting. When anyone opens a tap, the water rushes down the pipes by itself. Taps on the ground floor usually flow harder than taps on the top floor, because the water above them has fallen further. Scientists say the stored energy belongs to the bottle and the Earth together, because the Earth's pull is what makes the fall.
- e.g. Hydroelectric power stations, such as the one at the Mettur dam on the Kaveri river, let water stored high behind a dam fall through pipes to spin machines that make electricity.
- e.g. A child at the top of a slide has stored energy that becomes speed on the way down.
- e.g. A coconut high on a palm tree has much more energy waiting than one on a low branch.
### Energy waiting in food and fuel · *defined*
**Definition:** Chemical energy is stored in how the tiny particles of a material are joined. When food is digested, or a fuel burns, the particles join up in new ways and the energy comes out.

Everything is made of tiny particles called atoms, joined into groups called molecules. In food and fuel, the atoms are joined in a way that holds energy. When gas burns on the stove, it joins with oxygen from the air, and the energy comes out quickly as heat and light. Your body does almost the same thing with food, but slowly and in many small steps. It joins the food with oxygen from your breath, and the energy comes out as warmth and as the movement of your muscles. That is why you breathe faster when you run. The energy in almost all food was first caught from sunlight by plants. A plant uses sunlight to build sugar from air and water, and the sugar holds the energy. Oils and fats hold the most energy for their weight. Sugar, rice and dal hold less than half as much, and fresh fruit much less, because fruit is mostly water.
- e.g. 100 grams of cooking oil holds about 900 kcal. 100 grams of banana holds only about 90 kcal, because a banana is mostly water.
- e.g. A camel crosses deserts where food can be hard to find for days. Their hump is a store of fat, and their body uses it for energy when there is little to eat.
- e.g. A candle burns its wax, and the wax's stored energy comes out as light and heat.
### Energy waiting in cells and batteries · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell stores chemical energy. When it is joined into a circuit, its chemicals slowly react, and the energy comes out as a push on electricity.

Inside an AA cell are two different chemicals, kept apart. When the cell is in a torch and the switch is on, the chemicals react, a little at a time, and push electricity round the circuit. As the chemicals get used, the push gets weaker. A torch bulb needs a big flow of electricity, so a tired cell makes it dim. A wall clock needs only a tiny trickle, so the same tired cell can often run a clock for weeks more. A phone has a rechargeable battery. Charging uses electricity from the wall to push its chemicals back the way they were, so the energy is stored again. An ordinary AA cell must never be charged, because it can leak, get hot or burst.
- e.g. An AA cell that has stopped lighting a torch brightly may still keep a wall clock ticking.
- e.g. An inverter at home charges big batteries while the power is on. In a power cut, the batteries run the fans and lights.
- e.g. An electric scooter is charged overnight, and the stored energy turns its wheels during the day.
### A store lets energy come out later, quickly or slowly · *defined*
**Definition:** A store keeps energy until it is released. It can release the energy all at once or a little at a time.

When you held the wound jar still for a minute, nothing moved, but the jar still rolled back when you let go. Your push had ended a minute earlier. The energy waited in the band in between. This is what makes stores so useful. Ammamma winds a clock for a few minutes on a Sunday, and the spring lets the energy out a tiny bit at each tick, for eight days. A bow stores energy slowly, as the archer pulls, and gives it all to the arrow in a moment. A flea needs to jump onto a dog many times their own height. Their leg muscles are too slow to throw them that high. So the flea slowly squeezes a pad of very springy material in their body, called resilin, then lets it go all at once.
- e.g. A pendulum wall clock wound once a week keeps ticking all week.
- e.g. A flea stores energy slowly in a springy pad, then jumps in about a thousandth of a second.
- e.g. A phone is charged for two hours at night and uses the energy slowly all day.
### No store gives back everything · *defined*
**Definition:** When stored energy comes out, all of it goes somewhere, but some always spreads out as a little warmth and sound that we cannot get back.

Energy is never made from nothing, and it never vanishes. So a store can give back at most the energy that was put in. In real life it gives back less, because some energy always spreads out. As the roll-back jar rolls, it rubs on the floor and makes a soft rumbling sound. The band rubs inside itself as it twists and untwists, and gets very slightly warm. That warmth and sound are still energy, but they spread into the air and the floor, and nobody can collect them again. So the jar rolls back and forth a few times, each time a little less, and then stops.
- e.g. A bouncing ball bounces lower each time, and stops in the end.
- e.g. Stretch and let go of a thick rubber band ten times quickly, then touch it to your lip. It feels slightly warm.
- e.g. A swing slows down unless someone keeps pushing.
### Stores in living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things store energy, as food or in springy body parts, to use when they need it.

A seed must grow a root and a shoot in the dark soil, before they can catch any sunlight. So their parent plant packs food into them, as starch or oil, and the seed lives on that food until their first leaves open. A groundnut seed is full of oil for this reason. A bear in a cold country sleeps through the winter, when there is little food. So they eat a lot in autumn and store it as fat. Your own body stores some energy as a sugar called glycogen in your muscles and liver, and much more as fat. That is how you can run in the evening on energy from your lunch.
- e.g. A groundnut seed is about half oil, stored by their parent plant to feed the young plant.
- e.g. A camel's hump is fat, used for energy on long trips with little food.
- e.g. Your muscles store a little sugar, ready for a sudden run.
### What is energy, really? · *debated*
Scientists can measure energy very exactly, and the numbers always add up. But they do not agree on what energy is. When it is stored, you cannot see it, weigh it or touch it. The physicist Richard Feynman said in his lectures in the 1960s that in physics today we have no knowledge of what energy is. He meant that we know its rules, but not what it is made of.
- e.g. A stretched band and a loose band look almost the same, but one holds energy and one does not.
- e.g. Physicists can calculate exactly how far a ball will bounce, without knowing what energy is.
- **Many physicists:** Energy is a number we calculate. It always comes out the same before and after any change, so it is very useful. It is not a stuff that sits inside things.
- **Other physicists:** Energy is real. Albert Einstein showed that mass and energy are the same thing in two forms, so energy is part of what everything is made of.
- **Some philosophers:** Energy may be the ability of the world to change. A store is a change that has not happened yet.

## Mostly agreed
- Energy can be stored in a thing's height, its shape, or the chemicals it is made of, and released later. *How we know:* Anyone can wind a toy, lift a ball or eat a meal and see the energy come out later. Scientists measure these stores very exactly.
- Energy is never made or destroyed. It only changes from one form to another. *How we know:* In every careful experiment since the 1840s, the energy before a change has added up to the energy after it, once warmth and sound are counted.
- The energy in food is chemical energy, and almost all of it came first from sunlight caught by plants. *How we know:* Plants grown in the dark use up their seed's food and stop growing. Measurements show that burning food gives out the same energy as the body gets from it.
- When stored energy comes out, some always spreads out as warmth and sound. *How we know:* No bouncing ball, swing or wound toy keeps going for ever. Careful thermometers show the warming.

## How we found out
**Julius Robert Mayer, a German ship's doctor, and later Max Rubner, a German scientist, 1840 (the voyage to Java), 1842 (Mayer's paper) and 1894 (Rubner's measurements with dogs).** In 1840 Julius Robert Mayer, a young German doctor, sailed on a Dutch ship to Java, an island in what is now Indonesia. Doctors then often let a little blood out of sick people. In the hot harbour, Mayer saw that blood from the sailors' veins was bright red. In cold Germany it was dark. Mayer worked out a reason. The body burns food slowly with oxygen to keep warm. In a hot place it needs less warmth, so it uses less of the oxygen in the blood, and the blood stays bright. Many scientists then thought a special life force made a body's heat and movement. Mayer said the warmth and work of a body come from the energy stored in food, and energy is never made from nothing. A science journal would not print his first paper. He published another in 1842, and few people listened. Later scientists tested the idea. In the 1890s Max Rubner, a German scientist, kept dogs in a closed box and measured all the heat they gave out. It matched the energy in the food they had used, almost exactly.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought a special life force made a body's heat and movement, apart from food. Mayer argued that both come from the energy stored in food. Rubner's measurements showed the heat a dog gives out matches the energy in their food.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A thing that is not moving has no energy.** (A very common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: We see energy when something moves, lights up or makes a sound. A still thing seems to be doing nothing.  
  Against: A wound jar held still, a coconut high on a tree and a packet of food are all still, yet they all hold energy that comes out later.
- **The energy of a raised ball is stored in the ball alone.** (How most people, and many books, say it; *accepted*)  
  For: It is easier to say, and the ball is the thing that moves when it falls.  
  Against: Physicists say the stored energy belongs to the ball and the Earth together, because it comes from the pull between them. Far from the Earth, lifting the ball would store almost nothing.
- **Living bodies make their heat with a special life force, not from their food.** (Many scientists before the 1840s; *rejected*)  
  For: A living body stays warm and moves by itself, while a dead one goes cold. It seemed something special to life was at work.  
  Against: Robert Mayer said the warmth and movement come from the energy in food. In the 1890s Max Rubner measured all the heat given out by dogs, and it matched the energy in the food they used.
- **The kcal number on a packet tells you how good a food is for you.** (Many diet adverts; *debated*)  
  For: Eating much more energy than you use, for years, can make a body store too much fat.  
  Against: A kcal number says only how much energy is stored. It says nothing about protein, vitamins or fibre. Groundnuts are high in kcal and full of protein. A sweet drink is lower and has almost nothing else.
- **A cell that will not light a torch is completely empty.** (A common idea at home; *rejected*)  
  For: The torch stays dark, so it seems all the energy has gone.  
  Against: A tired cell still holds some energy, but it can push only a small flow of electricity. A wall clock needs only a tiny flow, so the cell can often still run it.

## Roots
We have wound a jar, tested a tired cell and found energy waiting all over the house. Now we ask what a store of energy tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You wound the jar, held it still for a minute, then let go, and it rolled back. Your push had ended a minute before the rolling began. In between, the energy waited in the twisted band. So a store lets the energy of a push given now come out later, quickly or slowly.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect a cause and its effect to happen together: the push, then straight away the movement. A store breaks that link in time. The cause can end long before the effect begins, like Ammamma's Sunday winding and Thursday's ticking.  
*Evidence:* In the wait test of the Roll-Back Jar, the jar held still for a minute still rolled back about as far as before. In the story, ten turns of a key kept a clock ticking for eight days.

- *who am i:* You are a store too. Today's lunch waits in your body as the energy for this evening's run.
- *what is life:* A seed holds food from their parent plant, sometimes for years, until water tells them it is time to grow.
- *what is this world:* Water held high behind a dam waits in the hills, until the gates open and it lights a city.

Before you wind the jar, the band is loose and nothing is waiting in it. Winding gathers your pushes into one store. Let go, and that one store comes out as many things: rolling, a soft rumble, and a little warmth in the band and the floor. The same store could have spun a wheel or lifted a weight instead. One store of energy can become many different happenings.

- **Thirukkural 490:** When it is time to wait, be still like a heron. When the moment comes, strike as quickly as the heron. (A heron standing still in the water is not idle. She is ready. Your wound jar held still in your hand was ready in the same way, and moved the moment you let go.)
- **Aristotle, Metaphysics, Book 9:** Aristotle said a thing can be something 'in potential' before it is that thing 'in action'. A builder who is resting can still build. Our word energy comes from his Greek word energeia, which means being at work. (The scientists' word for stored energy, potential energy, comes from the same old idea. A wound band is rolling in potential, until you let go and it rolls in action.)
- **Tao Te Ching 64:** A tree too big to hug grows from a tiny shoot. A tower nine storeys high begins with a heap of earth. A journey of a thousand miles begins under your feet. (Big happenings often begin long before we see them. A week of ticking began with a few turns of a key, and a run in the evening began at lunch.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or destroyed. A store can give back only what was put into it, and some of that always spreads out as warmth.
- Science (settled): The energy stored by lifting a thing belongs to the thing and the Earth together, because it comes from the pull between them.
- Science (open): What energy actually is, beyond the numbers that measure it, is still argued about.
- Other view, Many physicists: Stored energy is a number we calculate to make our sums come out right. Nothing is really 'waiting' in a band. There are only atoms pushed out of place, pushing back. Saying it waits, like a heron, is a picture, not a finding.
- Practice, Still like a heron (3 min): Let's stand on one leg like a heron. Bend your knee a little and keep very still. Breathe in slowly and hold your breath for three counts. Feel your leg muscles ready to spring. Now breathe out and jump up gently, as high as you like. Stand still again. Where was the jump while you were waiting?
- Ask: While you held the wound jar still, was anything happening inside it?
- Ask: Ammamma's arm worked on Sunday, and the clock ticked on Thursday. Which day did the ticking really start?
- Ask: A seed can wait for years. What do you think a seed is doing while they wait?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your push ended a minute before the jar rolled back. Is the rolling caused by the push, or by the band? (hint: Think about what would happen with no push, and what would happen with no band.)
- *deep:* Where exactly is the energy in a twisted rubber band? In the band, in its tiny particles, or between them? (hint: Think about what changes inside the band when it twists. Nothing is added to it.)
- *big:* The energy in your breakfast was sunlight, caught by a plant. Is part of you made of stored sunlight? (hint: Think about what your body builds from food, as well as what it burns.)
- *big:* A seed can wait in a dry packet for years, then grow when it is watered. Is a seed alive while they wait? (hint: Think about what a seed is doing in the packet, and what they need to start growing.)
- *deep:* If a still thing can hold energy, how can you tell whether anything is truly at rest? (hint: Think of the tank on the roof and the coconut on the tree. What would you need to know about them?)
- *big:* Coal and petrol are stores of energy from plants and sea creatures that lived millions of years ago. Should we use up stores that took so long to make? (hint: Think about who made the store, how long it took, and who else might need it.)

## Story: Ammamma's Clock
*The story is made up. Everything about the clock is true: many old pendulum wall clocks are 'eight-day clocks', wound with a key once a week, and the key coils a flat steel spring that turns the clock's wheels.*

In Nikhil's flat in Mysuru, the loudest thing in the hall was the clock. It hung high on the wall, in a wooden case with a glass door. Behind the glass, a brass pendulum swung left and right, left and right. Tick, tock. Tick, tock. Every hour it struck the time with deep, round bongs. The clock had belonged to Ammamma's father, and it was older than anyone in the family.

That month, Ammamma went to Chennai for her niece's wedding. She was away for ten days. Nikhil was eight, and he liked having the hall to himself. He did his sums to the tick-tock of the clock.

On the ninth morning, something was wrong. Nikhil sat down with his tumbler of milk and listened. The hall was silent. The pendulum hung straight down, perfectly still. The hands said twenty past four, though it was seven o'clock.

"Amma! Divya! The clock is broken," he shouted.

His big sister Divya came to look. She tapped the glass. Nothing. "Maybe it got too old," she said. "Maybe it is just tired."

Their mother looked up at the clock and smiled. "Wait for Ammamma," she said. "She comes back tomorrow."

The next evening Ammamma came home with a bag of sweets and a jasmine string in her hair. Before she had even had her coffee, Nikhil pulled her into the hall. "Ammamma, your clock has died."

Ammamma looked at the still pendulum. "It has not died, kanna. It is hungry." She opened the little brass box on the shelf below the clock. Inside lay a key, shaped like a small T.

She stood on a stool and opened the glass door. There were two holes in the clock's face. She fitted the key into one and began to turn it. Click, click, click. "Count for me," she said.

Nikhil counted. After about ten turns, the key would not go round any more.

"Now feel this," said Ammamma. She let Nikhil put his fingers on the key and try to turn it one more time. It pushed back against his fingers, hard. "Inside, there is a long, flat strip of steel, coiled up like a snail shell," Ammamma said. "Every turn of the key winds it tighter. Now it is wound right up. It is pushing, all the time, to unwind."

"Then why doesn't it unwind all at once?" asked Nikhil.

Ammamma touched the pendulum gently, and it began to swing. Tick, tock. The clock was alive again. "Because of this," she said. "Each time the pendulum swings, it lets one tooth of a little wheel go past. So the spring can only turn the wheels one tooth at a time, one tick at a time. A few minutes of winding is let out slowly, over eight days. That is why my father called it an eight-day clock. And that is why I wind it every Sunday."

Nikhil thought about this. "But you were away for ten days."

"So it ran for eight, and on the ninth morning the spring had nothing left to give." Ammamma climbed down from the stool. "Nothing in it was broken. Nobody had put energy into it."

Divya laughed. "So it was tired, after all."

"In a way," said Ammamma. "But a clock does not rest and get better. It only gets back what someone gives it."

Nikhil was still looking at the key. "So the ticking on Thursday came from your arm on Sunday?"

Ammamma smiled. "From my arm, from my breakfast, and from the sun that grew the ragi in my breakfast. All of it waiting in that spring."

That night, Nikhil lay in bed and listened to the clock. Tick, tock. He thought of the strip of steel, coiled tight in the dark, giving out a tiny bit of Sunday with every swing. The next Sunday, before anyone else was up, he stood on the stool and asked Ammamma to show him again. This time, he turned the key himself.

**For the parent:** The ticking of a whole week was put into the clock in a few minutes of winding. The energy waited in the spring and came out a tiny bit at each tick.

- Ask: Why had the clock stopped? Was anything broken?
- Ask: Ammamma wound the clock for a few minutes. How long did that energy last?
- Ask: Where did the energy in the spring come from, before Ammamma's arm?
- Ask: Can you think of something at home that stores energy quickly and lets it out slowly?

### g2 · Wind Me Up: an acting game (10 min)
- **rules:** One player is the Winder. Everyone else picks a toy card and freezes.; The Winder turns an invisible key on a player's back, counting the turns out loud, from 1 to 10.; The player must stay frozen until the Winder says 'Let go!'; Then the player acts out their toy: 1 turn gives 1 step, 5 turns give 5 steps. They slow down as they run out, and stop.; A player who moves before 'Let go!' or does more steps than turns becomes the next Winder.
- **Printable cards: 8 toy cards:** A wind-up car: roll along the floor; A jumping frog: hop; A dancing doll: spin; A walking robot: march stiffly; A clock: swing your arm like a pendulum; A roll-back jar: roll away and come back; A flea: crouch slowly, then spring; A bird toy: flap your arms
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the rules, and give each player a toy card.  
  *Parent says:* I am the Winder. I'll wind you with a key. You stay frozen until I say let go. Then you move one step for every turn.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a toy card. Freeze. Wait to be wound up!
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play several rounds. Sometimes wait a long time before saying 'Let go!'  
  *Parent says:* Three turns. Now wait. Wait. Let go! How many steps did you have?  
  *Child mode:* Stay frozen while you wait. When you hear 'Let go!', take your steps. Slow down at the end.
  - Note: The long waits are the point. The energy was put in by the winding, and it waits until it is let go.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what the game showed.  
  *Parent says:* When I wound you and made you wait, where was your energy while you were frozen?  
  *Child mode:* While you were frozen, where was your energy?
  - Check: Gowri was wound 4 turns and had to wait. When she was let go, how many steps should she take? → **4 steps, the same as her winding**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Roll-Back Jar: do more turns make it roll back further? (40 min)
- **question:** Does winding the roll-back jar more turns make it roll back further? And does the energy stay in it if you hold it still for a minute?
- **predict:** We will wind the jar 1, 2, 3 and 4 turns. Guess how far it will roll back each time. Will 4 turns go further than 1 turn, the same, or less? And if we hold it still for a minute before letting go, will it still roll back? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same jar, the same band, the same padlock and the same floor every time. Roll the jar slowly by hand, and let go without pushing. Only the number of turns changes. Try each number of turns 3 times and use the middle result, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes in the lid and the base, because the nail or scissor point can slip. Keep fingers away from the point. If a rubber band snaps, it can sting, so keep your face away from a tight band.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar for each number of turns, as tall as the middle result. Write one sentence under it: what the bars show.
- **results table:** Turns wound | My guess (cm) | Try 1 (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle result (cm)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Roll an empty jar with its lid on along the floor towards your child. 2. Ask what they notice. 3. Then show the jar with the band and padlock inside, before you build it.  
  *Parent says:* Watch this empty jar. I roll it, and it keeps going until it stops. Now we will make a jar that comes back to you by itself.  
  *Child mode:* Roll an empty jar along the floor. It goes away and stops. Now let's make one that comes back!
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make a small hole in the centre of the lid and the centre of the base. 2. Loop three bands into a chain. 3. Push one end through the base hole and slip half a matchstick through the loop outside. Tape it down. 4. Tie the padlock to the middle of the band chain with thread, so it hangs about 1 cm below. 5. Pull the other end through the lid hole, fix it with the other matchstick, and close the lid.  
  *Parent says:* I'll make the holes, because the nail can slip. You loop the bands together and tie the padlock to the middle. The padlock must hang down without touching the side of the jar.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes the holes. You loop the bands. Tie the padlock to the middle. It must hang down, not touch the side.
  - Note: The band should run straight from the middle of the base to the middle of the lid, a little stretched. If the padlock touches the side of the jar, shorten the thread or the band chain. Stick a strip of coloured tape along the outside of the jar, so you can count the turns as it rolls.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the jar on its side on a chalk line on the floor. 2. Ask your child to guess how far it will roll back after 1, 2, 3 and 4 turns. 3. Write each guess in the table. 4. Ask the second question: will it still roll back if we hold it still for a minute?  
  *Parent says:* When I roll this jar forward, what do you think happens to the band inside? How far will it roll back after one turn? After four turns? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how far it rolls back after 1, 2, 3 and 4 turns. Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think more turns will make it roll back further? (good: Because the band gets more twisted.; Because more twisting stores more energy.; Because I push it more.)
  - Note: Do not explain yet. Any guess is good if your child gives a reason. Some children think it will roll back the same however far it goes. That is a fine guess to test.
- **s4 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the jar on the chalk line with the tape strip on top. 2. Roll it forward slowly with your palm, exactly 1 turn, counting by the tape. 3. Hold it still, then lift your hand without pushing. 4. Mark where it first stops. 5. Measure from where you let go to that mark. 6. Repeat 3 times, then do 2, 3 and 4 turns.  
  *Parent says:* Roll it slowly, and count the turns by the tape. Now lift your hand without pushing. Where does it stop first? Let's measure from where you let go.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the jar 1 turn. Let go gently. Where does it stop? Measure. Do it 3 times.
  - Note: Each turn twists the band one more turn, because the padlock stays hanging at the bottom. The jar usually rolls back past the chalk line, slows down, and may wobble back again. Measure only to the place where it first stops. Expect more turns to send it further, for example about 40 cm for 1 turn and over 1 metre for 4 turns. A 10 cm wide jar rolls about 30 cm in each turn, so 4 turns needs about 1.3 metres of floor in front, and the same behind. If the padlock swings right over the band inside and the band unwinds without moving the jar, the band is too strong for the weight. Use a heavier weight or fewer turns.
  - Check: Leela, a girl in Class 3, winds her roll-back jar 1 turn, then 4 turns. What did your own test show will happen? → **It rolls back further after 4 turns**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wind the jar 3 turns again. 2. Hold it still on the floor while you both count slowly to 60. 3. Let go gently. 4. Measure, and compare with your 3-turn results.  
  *Parent says:* This time, wind 3 turns and hold it still. Let's count to 60 together. Is anything moving? Now let go. Did it still come back?  
  *Child mode:* Wind 3 turns. Hold it still. Count to 60. Nothing moves. Now let go!
  - Note: The jar should roll back about as far as your other 3-turn tries, perhaps a little less. While it was held still, the energy waited in the twisted band. This wait test is the heart of the unit, so let your child say what they notice before you say anything.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: more turns make the jar roll back further, and the energy waits in the band. What I saw: 1 turn went about 40 cm, 4 turns went over a metre. After holding it still for a minute, it still came back. Why: rolling the jar twists the band, and the twist stores energy. The more twists, the more energy waits in it.
  - Check: In the wait test, the jar was held still for a minute and then rolled back. Where was the energy during that minute? → **In the twisted rubber band**
### e2 · The Tired Cell: is a cell that dims a torch really empty? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Does an AA cell that makes a torch dim still have energy that can run a wall clock?
- **predict:** This cell makes the torch dim. Do you think it will run the wall clock? Will the clock tick, tick slowly, or stay still? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same torch and the same clock for every cell. Test a fresh cell too, so you can compare. Watch the clock's second hand for a full minute each time.
- **safety:** Never open, cut, heat or try to charge an ordinary cell. If a cell has a white crust or is leaking, do not use it. Wipe it with a dry cloth, put it in a bag for e-waste, and wash your hands. Keep small round button cells away from babies, because a swallowed button cell can badly burn a child's food pipe within two hours.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then finish this sentence: 'A cell that is too tired for a torch...'
- **results table:** Cell | Torch: bright, dim or dark? | My guess for the clock | Clock after 1 minute: ticking or still? | Clock next day: still on time?
- **schedule:** day 1: Test each cell in the torch. Guess, then test it in the clock. Set the clock to the right time. (20 min); day 2: Check whether the clock is still ticking and still on time. Say what you found. (5 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the fresh cell in the torch and switch it on. 2. Then put a tired cell in and switch it on. 3. Compare how bright the torch is.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a new cell and an old one. Let's see how each one lights the torch. Which is brighter?  
  *Child mode:* Try the new cell in the torch. Then try the old cell. Which is brighter?
  - Note: The tired cell should make the torch clearly dimmer, or not light it at all. If it lights the torch as brightly as the new one, it is not tired yet. Use another.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess whether the tired cell will run the clock, and why.  
  *Parent says:* This cell can hardly light the torch. Do you think it can run the clock? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* This cell can hardly light the torch. Will it run the clock? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the clock will not work? (good: Because the cell is finished.; Because the torch did not light, so there is no energy left.)
  - Ask: Why do you think the clock might still work? (good: Because a clock needs less energy than a torch.; Because the torch was dim, not dark, so a little energy is left.)
  - Note: Both guesses are good if your child gives a reason. Do not tell them the answer.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the tired cell in the clock, the right way round. 2. Set the clock to the right time. 3. Watch the second hand for one full minute. 4. Write what happens.  
  *Parent says:* Put the cell in with the flat end on the spring. Now let's watch the second hand together for one whole minute.  
  *Child mode:* Put the cell in the clock. Flat end on the spring. Watch the second hand for one minute.
  - Note: Very often a cell that is too tired for a torch still runs a clock, sometimes for weeks. A torch bulb needs a big flow of electricity, and a clock needs only a tiny trickle. If the clock stays still, the cell is truly flat. Try your second tired cell if you have one.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The next day, compare the clock with a phone. 2. Write whether it is still ticking and still on time.  
  *Parent says:* Is the clock still going? Is it showing the right time, like the phone?  
  *Child mode:* Is the clock still ticking? Does it match the phone?
  - Note: If it is still on time, the tired cell had plenty of energy left for the clock. Keep the cell in the clock and see how many more days or weeks it lasts.
  - Wait: Leave the clock running until tomorrow.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a tired cell is not empty. What I saw: it made the torch dim, but the clock ticked all night. Why: the torch needs a big flow of electricity and the clock needs only a little, so the cell still had enough stored energy for the clock.
  - Check: Rohini's TV remote has stopped working. Her mother says the cells are empty. What could Rohini try before throwing them away? → **Try them in a wall clock, which needs only a tiny flow of electricity**

- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the marble at the top of the door and drop it straight down. 2. Time it.

  *Child mode:* Drop the marble from the top. Time it. Can we make it take longer?
- **s2 (25 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape channels in a zigzag down the door. 2. Test from the top after every two or three pieces. 3. Fix places where the marble jumps out or stops.  
  *Parent says:* Each channel must slope down a little. If it is too steep, the marble goes fast. If it is too flat, it stops.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the channels in a zigzag. Each one slopes down a little. Test as you go.
  - Note: Help with taping high up. Stand on the floor, not on a chair, unless a grown-up holds it. Slopes of about one finger-width drop for every 30 cm work well.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Time the marble 3 times. 2. Compare with the straight drop.  
  *Parent says:* The marble started at the same height both times, with the same energy waiting. Why did the track make it take so much longer?  
  *Child mode:* Time it 3 times. Why does it take longer than the drop?

## Applications
- Wind-up clocks and watches store energy in a coiled spring.
- Water tanks on roofs store energy in height, so taps work without a pump running.
- Hydroelectric power stations, such as those at Mettur and Idukki, store water high behind dams.
- Phones, laptops, electric scooters and inverters store energy in rechargeable batteries.
- Bows, catapults and trampolines store energy in a bent or stretched shape.
- Food packets print the energy stored in the food, in kcal.
- Pull-back toy cars store energy in a spring when they are pulled back.
- Clothes pegs, hair clips and stapler springs hold things by pressing with stored energy.
- Cars and buses carry stores of energy as petrol, diesel, gas or batteries.

## Open questions
- **What is energy, really?** Scientists can measure it very exactly and the numbers always add up, but nobody can say what it is made of.
- **Can we build batteries that store much more energy, cheaply and safely, to keep solar electricity for the night?** Many teams are working on new kinds of batteries, but none yet is cheap, safe, long-lasting and able to hold a lot.
- **Is the total energy of the whole universe exactly zero?** Some physicists think the stored energy of gravity might exactly balance all the other energy. Nobody knows how to check this.
- **How do some seeds stay alive while they wait for years, even hundreds of years?** Scientists know some ways seeds protect themselves while dry, but not why some kinds can wait so much longer than others.

## Connected maths
- Remember the roll-back jar, wound 1, 2, 3 and 4 turns. Divya's middle results were 40 cm, 75 cm, 105 cm and 130 cm. How much further did each extra turn send the jar? What pattern do you see? **35 cm, then 30 cm, then 25 cm. Each extra turn adds a little less than the one before.**. 75 - 40 = 35. 105 - 75 = 30. 130 - 105 = 25. The gaps get 5 cm smaller each time. So more turns always help, but each extra turn helps a little less. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Using Divya's pattern, where each extra turn adds 5 cm less than the one before, predict how far her jar would roll back after 5 turns. **About 150 cm.**. The last gap was 25 cm. The next gap should be 5 cm less, which is 20 cm. 130 + 20 = 150 cm. This is only a prediction. With too many turns the padlock may flip over, so the test must check it. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Ammamma turns the key of her clock 10 times every Sunday. How many turns does she make in 4 weeks? In a year of 52 weeks? **40 turns in 4 weeks. 520 turns in a year.**. 4 lots of 10 is 40. 52 lots of 10 is 520. All the clock's ticking for a whole year comes from those 520 turns of the key. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In Kavya's battery diary, the phone showed 100% at 7 in the morning and 60% at 3 in the afternoon. How much did it drop each hour, if it dropped the same amount each hour? At that rate, at what time will it reach 20%? **5% each hour. It will reach 20% at 11 at night.**. From 7 to 3 is 8 hours. 100 - 60 = 40. 40 shared over 8 hours is 5 each hour. From 60 to 20 is 40 more, which takes another 8 hours. 3 in the afternoon plus 8 hours is 11 at night. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- 100 grams of cooking oil stores about 900 kcal. 100 grams of banana stores about 90 kcal. How many times as much energy does the oil store? How many grams of banana hold as much energy as 100 grams of oil? **10 times as much. 1,000 grams, which is 1 kilogram, of banana.**. 900 is 10 lots of 90. So oil stores 10 times as much for its weight. To match it you need 10 lots of 100 grams of banana, which is 1,000 grams. A banana is mostly water, which stores no energy. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
