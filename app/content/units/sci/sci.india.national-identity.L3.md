# India's national symbols: what they stand for, and how we count our tigers
`sci.india.national-identity.L3`

**Summary.** A country chooses a few things to stand for all its people. India's are the Tricolour flag, the Lion Capital emblem, the anthem Jana Gana Mana, the song Vande Mataram, the tiger, the peacock and the lotus. Real people designed or chose them, such as Pingali Venkayya from Andhra Pradesh, whose flag design grew into ours. To protect the tiger, India must know how many tigers there are. For about 30 years, forest staff counted tigers from their footprints, called pugmarks. In this unit you test whether one print can tell you who made it. You also check a famous claim: that hockey is India's official national game.

**Intro.** *Make a fist and press it gently into a plate of atta. Now smooth the atta and press again, harder. Are the two prints the same size? Forest staff once counted India's tigers by measuring prints like these. Could a print tell them which tiger made it?* The tiger is India's national animal. A national animal is one that a country chooses to stand for all its people. Choosing a symbol is easy. Keeping a real tiger alive is harder, and it starts with knowing how many there are. In this unit you meet India's national symbols and the people behind them. You also test footprints with your own hand, and find out why scientists now count tigers by their stripes.

## Terms
- **National symbol**: A national symbol is a thing a country chooses to stand for all its people. It can be a flag, a song, an animal or a picture. *e.g.* The tiger is India's national animal, and the peacock is India's national bird.
- **Tricolour**: Tricolour means 'three colours'. It is the name for India's flag, which has saffron, white and green bands. In Hindi it is called Tiranga. *e.g.* On 15 August, many buildings in Chennai and Kolkata fly the Tricolour.
- **Ashoka Chakra**: The Ashoka Chakra is the navy-blue wheel with 24 spokes in the middle of India's flag. It is copied from a stone wheel on Emperor Ashoka's pillar at Sarnath. *e.g.* Count the spokes on the wheel of a paper flag. There are 24.
- **Emblem**: An emblem is an official picture that stands for a country. India's emblem is the Lion Capital: four lions standing back to back, carved in stone over 2,000 years ago. *e.g.* The emblem is printed on every rupee note and on passports.
- **Satyamev Jayate**: Satyamev Jayate is a line in Sanskrit that means 'truth alone wins'. It comes from an old text called the Mundaka Upanishad, and it is written under India's emblem. *e.g.* Look under the lions on a rupee note. You will find Satyamev Jayate written in Devanagari letters.
- **National anthem**: A national anthem is a country's official song. People stand still while it is sung. India's anthem is Jana Gana Mana, written by Rabindranath Tagore. *e.g.* Before a cricket match, both teams stand in a line while their anthems are played.
- **Republic**: A republic is a country whose head is chosen, not born into the job like a king or queen. India became a republic on 26 January 1950. *e.g.* India's President is chosen by elected members of Parliament and the state assemblies, not born a ruler.
- **Pugmark**: A pugmark is the footprint of a big cat, such as a tiger or a leopard. It shows four toe pads and one big pad behind them. *e.g.* A tiger walking on a muddy path by a stream leaves a line of pugmarks.
- **Camera trap**: A camera trap is a camera fixed to a tree or a post in a forest. It takes a photo by itself whenever an animal walks past. *e.g.* A camera trap on a forest path in Nagarahole photographed a tigress at night.
- **Census**: A census is a careful count of how many people, or animals, there are in a place. India counts its wild tigers every four years. *e.g.* The tiger census of 2022 found at least 3,167 tigers in India.
- **Endangered**: An animal is endangered when so few are left in the wild that they could disappear for ever. *e.g.* Tigers are endangered. Hunting them is a crime in India.
- **Evidence**: Evidence is what you can see, measure or check that shows whether an idea is true. *e.g.* A photo of a tiger's stripes is evidence that this tiger lives in the forest.

## Explanations
### What a national symbol is · *defined*
**Definition:** A national symbol is something a country chooses to stand for all its people, such as a flag, an anthem, an emblem or an animal.

India has people of many languages, foods, festivals and faiths. A few things are shared by all of them. Every Indian has the same flag, the same anthem and the same Constitution, which is the book of the country's most important rules. These shared things are called national symbols. Together they are part of India's national identity, which means what a country shares and how it shows who it is. A symbol is chosen by people. The tiger did not ask to be a national animal. People chose the tiger, and that choice also made them promise to protect tigers.
- e.g. A family in Kochi and a family in Guwahati speak different languages, but both stand for the same anthem.
- e.g. The Tricolour is flown on government buildings across India.
- e.g. The ₹ symbol on a price tag means the same thing in every state.
### The national flag · *defined*
**Definition:** India's flag has three equal bands: saffron on top, white in the middle and green at the bottom. In the middle of the white band is a navy-blue wheel with 24 spokes, the Ashoka Chakra. The flag's length and height are always in the ratio 3 to 2.

Saffron stands for courage and giving things up for others. White stands for peace and truth. Green stands for the land, growth and plenty. The wheel says that the country must keep moving forward. A ratio of 3 to 2 means that for every 3 parts of length there are 2 parts of height. So a flag 3 metres long is 2 metres high. The flag grew from a design by Pingali Venkayya, a teacher from Andhra Pradesh. In 1921, at Vijayawada, he showed Mahatma Gandhi a flag of red and green. White and a spinning wheel were then added. In 1931 the Congress chose a saffron, white and green flag with a spinning wheel. On 22 July 1947, the Ashoka Chakra took the spinning wheel's place, and the flag became the one we fly today. Official flags have long been made of khadi, cloth spun and woven by hand. Today the main maker of khadi national flags is a khadi centre in Hubballi, Karnataka. The rules now also allow flags of polyester and of cloth made by machines.
- e.g. A paper flag 30 cm long must be 20 cm high.
- e.g. Saffron is always on top. A flag flown with green on top is upside down.
- e.g. On a day of national mourning, the flag is flown halfway down the pole. This is called half mast.
### The emblem and the rupee symbol · *defined*
**Definition:** India's emblem is the Lion Capital of Ashoka: four lions sitting back to back on a round base, with the words Satyamev Jayate written below. The rupee symbol, ₹, joins the Devanagari letter र (ra) and the Roman letter R.

Emperor Ashoka had the Lion Capital carved in stone at Sarnath, near Varanasi, about 2,250 years ago. It sat on top of a tall pillar. It is now in the museum at Sarnath. From the front you can only see three of the four lions. The base has an elephant, a horse, a bull and a lion carved on it, with a wheel between each of them. India took it as its emblem on 26 January 1950. The words Satyamev Jayate mean 'truth alone wins'. The ₹ symbol is much newer. In 2010 the government held an open competition. The winner was D. Udaya Kumar, a young designer from Tamil Nadu. His symbol was chosen on 15 July 2010. The two lines at its top also look like an equals sign.
- e.g. Look at any rupee note or coin. The Lion Capital is on it.
- e.g. A price tag in a Madurai shop says ₹ 40. The ₹ means rupees.
- e.g. Passports and government letters have the emblem printed at the top.
### The national anthem and the national song · *defined*
**Definition:** India's national anthem is Jana Gana Mana, written by Rabindranath Tagore. India's national song is Vande Mataram, written by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay. Both were adopted on 24 January 1950, and both are given equal honour.

Tagore wrote Jana Gana Mana in Bengali. It was first sung in Kolkata, then called Calcutta, on 27 December 1911. In 1919 Tagore stayed at a college in Madanapalle, in Andhra Pradesh, and wrote it out in English there. Margaret Cousins, who taught at the college, wrote down its tune in musical notes. The anthem names parts of India from every direction: Punjab, Sindh, Gujarat, the Maratha land, the Dravida south, Odisha and Bengal, the Vindhya and Himalaya mountains, the Yamuna and Ganga rivers and the sea. The full anthem takes about 52 seconds to sing. While it is sung, people stand still and quiet. Vande Mataram means 'I bow to you, mother'. Bankim Chandra put it in his novel Anandamath in 1882. Freedom fighters sang it at their marches.
- e.g. At school assembly, children stand still with their arms by their sides for Jana Gana Mana.
- e.g. Tagore also wrote Amar Shonar Bangla, the national anthem of Bangladesh.
- e.g. A short version of Jana Gana Mana, the first and last lines only, takes about 20 seconds.
### The national days · *defined*
**Definition:** India has three national days. Independence Day on 15 August remembers the day India became free from British rule in 1947. Republic Day on 26 January remembers the day the Constitution came into force in 1950. Gandhi Jayanti on 2 October is the birthday of Mahatma Gandhi, born in 1869.

The Constitution was finished and agreed on 26 November 1949. That day is now called Constitution Day. The leaders waited two months and started it on 26 January 1950. On 26 January 1930, the freedom movement had asked for complete independence, called Purna Swaraj. Starting the Constitution on the same date honoured that day. Gandhi led millions of people in the freedom struggle without violence. So his birthday is also the International Day of Non-Violence.
- e.g. On 15 August, the Prime Minister raises the flag at the Red Fort in Delhi.
- e.g. On 26 January, a big parade marches in Delhi, and states hold their own parades in their capitals.
- e.g. On 2 October, many schools hold clean-up drives in Gandhi's memory.
### The tiger, the peacock and the lotus in their own lives · *defined*
**Definition:** The tiger is India's national animal, the peacock is the national bird and the lotus is the national flower. Each one's body helps them with a problem in their own life.

A tiger's problem is food. Tigers cannot run fast for long, so they must creep very close to a deer before they leap. Their stripes break up their shape among tall grass and shadows. Deer see colours differently from us. To a deer, a tiger's orange looks much like green. So the deer does not see the tiger until too late. A peacock's problem is to be chosen. Peahens choose which male they mate with. In the rainy season, males spread their long trains and shake them to show themselves. The shining blue and green of the feathers is not paint. It comes from tiny ridges in the feathers that send back light in different ways. The lotus grows in muddy ponds. Their leaves are covered in tiny waxy bumps, so water rolls off in balls and carries the dust away. A clean leaf can catch more sunlight. The tiger became the national animal in 1973, when Project Tiger began. Before that it was the lion. The peacock became the national bird in 1963.
- e.g. Hold a peacock feather and tilt it. The 'eye' changes colour as you turn it.
- e.g. A tiger photographed in tall dry grass is hard to spot, even for people.
- e.g. Drops of water roll off a lotus leaf, or a colocasia (chembu) leaf, like silver balls.
### How India counts its tigers · *defined*
**Definition:** Today India counts its tigers mainly with camera traps. Each tiger's stripe pattern is different from every other tiger's, and it stays the same all their life. So a photo of the stripes shows exactly which tiger walked past.

From the 1970s, forest staff counted tigers from pugmarks. They traced each print on glass or paper and decided which prints came from the same tiger. But a print changes with the ground and with how the tiger walks. In soft wet mud it spreads and looks bigger. In dry dust its edges crumble. A heavy tiger, or one running, presses deeper. So one tiger could be counted as two, and two tigers as one. In the early 1990s, the Indian scientist K. Ullas Karanth began using camera traps in Nagarahole, Karnataka. He recognised each tiger from their stripes. In 2005, scientists found that Sariska Tiger Reserve in Rajasthan had no tigers left at all, though the official records had still shown tigers there. After that, India changed how it counts. Since 2006, every count every four years uses camera traps and careful sums. The count in 2006 found about 1,411 tigers. The count in 2022 photographed at least 3,167. That is about three out of every four wild tigers in the world.
- e.g. A camera trap photographs a tigress's left side. Two weeks later another camera, 5 km away, photographs the same stripe pattern. It is the same tigress.
- e.g. A camera always photographs one side of a tiger. The left and right sides have different stripes, so scientists compare left with left.
- e.g. Forest guides still read pugmarks to know where tigers have walked today.
### Does India have a national game? · *debated*
Many books say hockey is India's national game. In 2012, a schoolgirl asked the government, under the Right to Information law, when hockey was made the national game. The Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports replied that no sport has been declared India's national game. So the claim in many books is not official. People still argue about which game best stands for India.
- e.g. India's men's hockey team won 8 Olympic gold medals between 1928 and 1980.
- e.g. National Sports Day, 29 August, is the birthday of the hockey player Dhyan Chand.
- e.g. Kabaddi grew from village games played across South Asia, including chadugudu in Tamil Nadu.
- **People who say hockey:** Hockey gave India its greatest Olympic years, with 8 gold medals. Many books already call it the national game.
- **People who say cricket:** Cricket is the game most Indians watch and play today, in every street and maidan.
- **People who say kabaddi:** Kabaddi needs no bat, ball or field, and it grew from games children played in Indian villages.
- **People who say none:** A country does not need a national game. Each person can love the sport they play.

## Mostly agreed
- Every tiger has their own stripe pattern, and it stays the same all their life. *How we know:* Camera traps have photographed the same tigers again and again for many years, in Nagarahole and across India. Each one keeps the same stripes.
- The size and shape of a footprint change with the ground and with how the foot pressed. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with their own fist in dry and damp flour. Studies of tiger tracks in different mud showed the same thing.
- India's flag is saffron, white and green, with a 24-spoke navy-blue Ashoka Chakra, and its length and height are in the ratio 3 to 2. *How we know:* This is written in the Flag Code of India, the official rules for the flag.
- India's wild tigers have more than doubled in number since 2006. *How we know:* The national counts, using camera traps, found about 1,411 tigers in 2006 and at least 3,167 in 2022.

## How we found out
**K. Ullas Karanth, a wildlife scientist in Karnataka, and the Tiger Task Force led by Sunita Narain, Camera-trap studies from the early 1990s; Sariska found empty in 2004 and 2005; camera-trap counts across India from 2006.** From the 1970s, India counted its tigers by their pugmarks. Forest staff traced prints on glass or made plaster casts, and decided which prints came from the same tiger. People believed every tiger's pugmark was different enough to tell them apart. In the early 1990s, the Indian scientist K. Ullas Karanth began studying tigers in Nagarahole, Karnataka, with camera traps. Each photo showed a tiger's stripes, which are different on every tiger and never change. He argued that pugmarks could not tell tigers apart reliably, because the same paw leaves different prints on different ground. Many officials disagreed. Then, in 2004 and 2005, scientists searched Sariska Tiger Reserve in Rajasthan and found no tigers at all, though the official records had still counted tigers there. The government set up a Tiger Task Force, led by the environment scientist Sunita Narain. Since 2006, India has counted its tigers with camera traps. Tigers were brought back to Sariska from Ranthambore, starting in 2008.  
*What people believed before:* People thought each tiger's pugmark was different enough to count tigers by footprints alone. But one tiger leaves different prints on different ground, and the empty forest at Sariska showed that the footprint counts could be badly wrong. Stripes photographed by camera traps now tell each tiger apart.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Hockey is India's official national game.** (Many school books and quiz books; *rejected*)  
  For: Hockey brought India 8 Olympic gold medals, and the claim has been repeated for many years.  
  Against: In 2012 the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports said, in reply to a schoolgirl's Right to Information question, that no sport has been declared the national game.
- **Each tiger's pugmark is different enough to count tigers by footprints alone.** (The official tiger counts in India from the 1970s to 2005; *rejected*)  
  For: Skilled trackers can learn a lot from prints: roughly how big the animal is, which way they walked, and when.  
  Against: The same tiger leaves different prints on different ground. In 2005, Sariska was found to have no tigers, though the records still counted some. Camera traps now identify tigers by their stripes.
- **Jana Gana Mana was written to praise the British king, George V.** (Some newspapers in 1911, and some people still today; *rejected*)  
  For: The song was first sung in December 1911, the same month the king visited India.  
  Against: Tagore wrote in a letter in 1937 that the 'giver of India's destiny' in his song could never be a king. The song names the land, its rivers and its people.
- **The tiger has always been India's national animal.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: The tiger is on posters, stamps and school books everywhere.  
  Against: The lion was India's national animal until the early 1970s. The tiger replaced the lion in 1973, when Project Tiger began.

## Roots
We have tested footprints with our own hands, and learned why tigers are now known by their stripes. Now we ask what this tells us about knowing who someone is, and about checking what is true.

**Deep truth.** Your one fist left a wider print when pressed hard, and a blurred one in dry atta. A print is shaped by the ground and the press, as well as the hand. So one print cannot say for sure whose it is. Scientists tell tigers apart by their stripes, which stay the same all their life.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a footprint as belonging to one foot, like a signature. But the same foot leaves a different print on every kind of ground and with every kind of step. To know who someone is, you need a clue that does not change with the moment.  
*Evidence:* In the Paw Print Test, the same fist gave hard prints about 1 or 2 cm wider than gentle ones, and blurred prints in dry atta. A smaller hand pressing hard could match a bigger hand pressing gently. In 2005, Sariska had no tigers left, though footprint records still counted them.

- *who am i:* Your handwriting, your voice and your footprints change from day to day. You are still you. The marks you leave are only part of the story of who you are.
- *what is life:* A tigress walks her forest every night. She does not know she is a symbol. Her stripes are for hiding from deer, and her life is her own.
- *what is this world:* A country is many millions of people who have never met. A flag, a song and a shared animal help them know they belong together.

A tiger leaves thousands of prints in a life, and no two are quite the same. Wet mud spreads them and dry dust crumbles them. Through all of it, the tigress herself stays the same, and so do her stripes. A country works a little like this. Its people speak many languages, and each place leaves its own mark. Some things stay shared through all of it: a flag, an anthem, a constitution. Finding what really stays, and checking it truly, is how both a tiger scientist and a citizen come to know who someone is.

- **Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.6, the line written under India's emblem:** Truth alone wins, not falsehood. (Satyameva jayate nanritam.) (For years the records said Sariska had tigers. Only careful checking, and finally searching for the tigers themselves, showed the truth: there were none. Because the truth was found, tigers could be brought back.)
- **Thirukkural 423, by Thiruvalluvar, in Tamil:** Whatever you hear, from whoever says it, wisdom is to see the truth in it. (Many books and many people said hockey was the official national game. A schoolgirl did not just accept it. She asked the people who would know, and found it was not true.)
- **Rabindranath Tagore, Gitanjali, poem 35 (1912):** Where the clear stream of reason has not lost its way into the dreary desert sand of dead habit. (Counting tigers by pugmarks went on for about 30 years partly out of habit. The writer of our anthem hoped for an India where people keep testing old habits with clear reasons, as Karanth did with his cameras.)
- Science (settled): Every tiger has their own stripe pattern, and it stays the same all their life. The left and right sides are different.
- Science (settled): A footprint's size and shape depend on the ground, the animal's weight and how fast they moved, as well as the foot.
- Science (open): Whether computers can learn to tell individual tigers apart from footprint photos well enough to count them is still being tested.
- Other view, Some experienced forest trackers: Skilled trackers can read much more from pugmarks than size alone: the shape of the toes, the gap between steps, the age of a print. Camera traps are costly and cover only part of a forest. Pugmarks are still the best way to know where tigers have walked today, and where to put the cameras.
- Practice, Be a camera trap (3 min): Let's sit very still by a window, like a camera trap on a tree. We won't move or speak for two minutes. Notice everything that passes: a crow, a sound, a leaf moving. Then tell me what you saw. A camera trap waits like this every night in the forest.
- Ask: A tiger's prints change every day, but the tiger is the same tiger. What makes you the same person from day to day?
- Ask: Many people believed hockey was official, and they were sure. What makes us believe things we have never checked?
- Ask: The tiger does not know they are our national animal. Who is the symbol really for?

## Story: Two Tigers or One?
*Pugmark counts, plaster casts, glass tracings and camera traps in Karnataka's tiger forests are real, and so is the problem of one tiger being counted twice. Meenakshi, her aunt Revathi, the forest guard Shankar and this tigress are made up.*

Meenakshi was nine, and she was spending the summer holidays with her aunt Revathi. Aunt Revathi was a wildlife scientist. She lived in a small house at the edge of a tiger forest in Karnataka. The house smelled of wood smoke and filter coffee, and its walls were covered with photos of tigers.

On the first morning, a forest guard called Shankar came to the door. He was holding a sheet of glass with a drawing on it. 'Madam,' he said, 'good news. We found pugmarks by the stream yesterday. Big ones, fourteen centimetres wide. And this morning we found smaller ones on the dusty path near the old temple. Eleven centimetres. That is two tigers in this part of the forest!'

He put the glass on the table. Someone had laid it over the pugmark and traced the print with a marker. Meenakshi saw four round toe marks in a curve, and one big pad behind them.

'Two tigers would be wonderful,' said Aunt Revathi. 'Let's go and look.'

They walked to the stream first. The mud there was soft and wet. The pugmarks were deep, and their edges were smooth and wide. Then they walked along the path to the old temple. Here the ground was dry and dusty. The pugmarks were shallow, and their edges had crumbled in.

Meenakshi crouched down and looked from one print to the other. Then she had an idea. She pressed her own fist into the wet mud by the stream. Then she ran to the dusty path and pressed the same fist into the dust, just as hard.

'Aunty, look!' she called. 'My fist made a big print in the mud. And the same fist made a small one in the dust. It's the same hand!'

Shankar laughed. 'That's a child's fist, not a tiger's paw.'

'But it's a good test,' said Aunt Revathi. 'Meenakshi, try once more in the mud. This time press very gently.'

Meenakshi pressed gently. The print was smaller than her first mud print. She measured both with the little ruler from Aunt Revathi's bag. 'The hard one is seven centimetres wide. The gentle one is six. Same fist!'

Shankar stopped laughing. He looked at the two tiger pugmarks again. 'So a tiger walking slowly on dry ground,' he said, 'and the same tiger stepping hard into wet mud...'

'...could leave both of these,' said Meenakshi.

'Could,' said Aunt Revathi. 'We don't know yet. A print tells us a tiger walked here. It can't tell us for sure which tiger. For that we need something on the tiger that never changes, whatever the ground is like.'

That evening she took Meenakshi to a tall jamun tree near the stream. A small grey box was strapped to the trunk at knee height. 'This is a camera trap,' said Aunt Revathi. 'When an animal walks past, it takes a photo by itself, day or night. There's another one across the path, so we get both sides.'

For four days, Meenakshi checked the cameras with her aunt every morning. They saw photos of a sambar deer, two wild pigs, a porcupine and a very surprised peacock. On the fifth morning, there was a tiger.

Back at the house, Aunt Revathi opened the photos on her laptop. A tigress was walking past the jamun tree in the moonlight. Then she opened a photo from another camera, near the old temple, taken two nights later.

'Now look at her stripes,' she said. 'Just behind the shoulder. See that one shaped like a fish hook? And the two short ones above it, like a pair of eyebrows?'

Meenakshi leaned close. In the stream photo, there was the fish hook and the two eyebrows. In the temple photo, the fish hook and the two eyebrows were there too, in exactly the same place.

'It's the same tigress!' she said.

'Every tiger's stripes are their own,' said Aunt Revathi. 'No two tigers have the same pattern, and a tiger keeps theirs all their life. The left side and the right side are different, so we always compare left with left.'

Shankar was quiet for a long time. Then he said, 'So we would have counted one tigress as two.'

'Many people did, for years,' said Aunt Revathi kindly. 'You noticed the prints, and that is how we found her. Now we know who she is.'

Shankar asked Meenakshi to show him the fist test again. This time he did it too. His hard print in the mud was wider than his gentle one, just like hers.

'What shall we call her?' asked Meenakshi.

Aunt Revathi smiled. 'You found the clue. You choose.'

Meenakshi thought of the river that flows through Karnataka and all the way to the sea in Tamil Nadu. 'Kaveri,' she said.

Under the tigress's photo, Aunt Revathi wrote: 'Kaveri. Left side: fish hook and two eyebrows behind the shoulder. One tigress, not two.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: a footprint is shaped by the ground and the step as well as by the foot. Wet mud spreads under a paw, dry dust crumbles, and a harder step presses deeper and wider. That is why Meenakshi's own fist gave prints of different sizes, and why one tigress could leave a big print by the stream and a small one on the dusty path. A tiger's stripe pattern does not change with the ground or the day, and no two tigers share one. So a camera photo of the stripes tells you exactly which tiger walked past. This is why India now counts its tigers with camera traps.

- Ask: What did Meenakshi do with her own fist, and what did it show?

- Ask: Why do scientists compare a tiger's left side with left-side photos only?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Paw Print Test: does one hand always leave the same print? (35 min)
- **question:** Does the same fist leave the same size of print every time, on dry and damp ground, pressed gently and pressed hard?
- **predict:** Guess first. If you press the same fist into dry atta and into damp atta, gently and then hard, will all four prints be the same width? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same fist every time, and measure every print the same way, across the four finger marks at the widest part. Only the ground or the press changes. Make each print twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Wash hands before you start, so the atta can still be cooked afterwards. Sweep up spilled flour, because a floury floor is slippery.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw one gentle print and one hard print, side by side, and write the widths under them.
- **results table:** Ground | Press | Width, try 1 (cm) | Width, try 2 (cm) | Edges: sharp or crumbly?
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour about 1 and a half cups of dry atta into the first plate. 2. Make it about 2 cm deep. 3. Smooth the top flat with the edge of the ruler.  
  *Parent says:* This plate is dry ground, like a dusty forest path in summer. Let's make it smooth and flat first.  
  *Child mode:* Wash your hands. Pour dry atta into the first plate. Smooth it flat with the ruler.
  - Note: Steel thalis with a rim work well. Keep the layer about 2 cm deep, so the fist does not hit the plate.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put about 1 and a half cups of atta in a bowl. 2. Add water one tablespoon at a time, rubbing it in with your fingers. 3. Stop when a pinch squeezed in your fingers holds together, like damp sand. 4. Spread it in the second plate, press it down lightly and smooth the top.  
  *Parent says:* This plate is damp ground, like mud by a stream. It should be crumbly, not a dough.  
  *Child mode:* Add water to the atta, one spoon at a time. Rub it in. Stop when a pinch holds together. Spread it in the second plate.
  - Note: About 4 to 5 tablespoons of water is usually enough. If it turns into a sticky dough, add a handful of dry atta and rub again. Damp, crumbly atta holds a sharp print. Use it for chapatis or puris the same day.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child how to make the 'paw'. 2. Make a loose fist. 3. Turn it so the flat backs of the four bent fingers face down. 4. Those four bumps are the toes.  
  *Parent says:* A tiger's pugmark has four toe marks in a curve. Our fingers will be the toes. Now, will all the prints be the same width? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make a fist. Turn it so your four bent fingers face down. Those are your toes! Will all your prints be the same width? Why?
  - Ask: Will the same fist always leave the same print? Why do you think so? (good: Yes, because it is the same hand.; No, because pressing harder might make it bigger.; It might look different in dry and damp atta.)
  - Note: Write the guess and the reason on the sheet. Any guess with a reason is good. Don't say what will happen yet.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press the fist gently into the dry atta, as if resting it there. 2. Lift it straight up. 3. Measure across the four finger marks at the widest part. 4. Smooth the atta and repeat. 5. Now do the same with a hard press.  
  *Parent says:* First a gentle press, like a tiger walking slowly. Then a hard press, like a tiger stepping heavily. Measure each one across the four toes.  
  *Child mode:* Press gently into the dry atta. Lift straight up. Measure across the four toes. Now press hard. Measure again.
  - Note: Hold the ruler just above the print, not on it. Measure to the nearest half centimetre. In dry atta the edges often crumble in, so look closely for where the print really ends.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do the same in the damp atta: two gentle presses and two hard presses. 2. Smooth the atta between prints. 3. Write each width in the table, and whether the edges were sharp or crumbly.  
  *Parent says:* Now the damp ground, like the mud by a stream. Gentle press, then hard press. What do you notice about the edges?  
  *Child mode:* Now press into the damp atta. Gently, then hard. Measure each print. Are the edges sharp or crumbly?
  - Note: Usually the hard prints are 1 or 2 cm wider than the gentle ones. Damp prints have sharp edges, and you can see the finger joints. Dry prints are blurred. If two widths are the same, that is a real result too. Ask your child what else looked different.
  - Check: Your hard press left a wider print than your gentle press. Why? → **The fist went deeper, so more of it touched the atta**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child looks away. 2. You, or another family member with a similar-sized hand, each make one print in the damp atta. 3. Your child turns round and guesses whose print is whose.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's be forest guards. Close your eyes. Two of us will each make one print. Can you tell whose is whose?  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Two people make prints. Open your eyes. Can you tell whose is whose?
  - Note: Choose two people with hands of about the same size. If one press is hard and the other gentle, the smaller hand can leave the bigger print. Ask how sure your child feels, and why.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the same hand leaves different prints. What I saw: my hard prints were about 2 cm wider than my gentle ones, and the dry prints were blurred. Why: pressing harder pushes the fist deeper, and dry atta crumbles in. So one print alone can't tell you whose hand it was.
### e2 · The Anthem Clock: does everyone sing Jana Gana Mana at the same speed? (20 min)
- **question:** The full anthem should take about 52 seconds. Does each person in the family sing it in the same time? Do we get closer when we sing together?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will everyone in your family take the same time to sing the anthem? Will singing together change it? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Everyone sings the full anthem, all the words, from the first word to the last 'Jaya he'. The same person times every try on the same phone or clock. Each person sings twice.
- **safety:** No safety worries. Stand still and straight while you sing, as you would at school.

- **results table:** Who sang | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Difference from 52 seconds
- **s1 (3 min)**

  *Parent says:* The rules say our anthem takes about 52 seconds. Will each of us take that long? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Read the anthem words once. It should take about 52 seconds. Will everyone take the same time? Why?
  - Ask: Will everyone take the same time? Why do you think so? (good: Yes, because the words are the same.; No, some people sing faster.; Singing together might help us keep the same speed.)
- **s2 (8 min)**

- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did the clock show? And why do you think people sing at different speeds?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: people sing the same song at different speeds. What I saw: Amma took 56 seconds and I took 49. Together we took 53. Why: each person has their own pace. When we sing together, we listen and keep the same speed. That is why the official anthem has a fixed tune and timing, so a whole school can sing it as one.

   **Answer:** Saffron → Courage and giving things up for others; White → Peace and truth; Green → The land, growth and plenty; Ashoka Chakra → Keep moving forward. *Why:* These are the meanings given for the flag's parts. A strong answer could also say which band is on top: saffron.

   **Answer:** ['24', 'twenty-four']. *Why:* 24 spokes. The folding activity shows why: half, three, half, half gives 2 × 3 × 2 × 2 = 24.
4. (truefalse) Meera says the flag should be flown with green on top on Republic Day. Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Saffron is always on top, every day. A flag with green on top is upside down.

   Options: Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay / Rabindranath Tagore / Pingali Venkayya / Mahatma Gandhi  
   **Answer:** Rabindranath Tagore. *Why:* Rabindranath Tagore wrote Jana Gana Mana in Bengali. Bankim Chandra wrote Vande Mataram, the national song. Pingali Venkayya designed the flag that ours grew from.
6. (match) Match each national day to what it remembers.  
   **Answer:** 15 August → India became free from British rule in 1947; 26 January → The Constitution came into force in 1950; 2 October → Mahatma Gandhi's birthday. *Why:* A strong answer also knows that the Constitution was agreed on 26 November 1949, and started two months later.

   **Answer:** Pressing hard pushed the fist deeper into the atta. A fist is rounded, so when it goes deeper, more of its sides touch the atta and the print is wider.. *Why:* A strong answer says the hand did not change size: the depth of the press did.
8. (mcq) Forest staff found a big pugmark in wet mud and a smaller one on a dry path. What can they be sure of?  
   Options: Two different tigers walked there / One big tiger and one cub walked there / At least one tiger walked there / The big tiger is older  
   **Answer:** At least one tiger walked there. *Why:* The same tiger can leave a big print in wet mud and a smaller one on dry ground. So the prints only show that at least one tiger walked there. Choosing 'two tigers' is the mistake the old counts made.
9. (fill) Today, scientists tell one tiger from another by their ____, photographed by camera traps.  
   **Answer:** ['stripes', 'stripe pattern']. *Why:* Every tiger has their own stripe pattern, and it stays the same all their life.
10. (draw) Draw two fist prints in damp atta: one from a gentle press and one from a hard press. Write how wide each was.  
   **Answer:** Two prints, the hard one wider, with widths written below, for example 5 cm and 7 cm.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the hard print wider and gives real numbers from the test.
11. (truefalse) Hockey has been officially declared India's national game.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In 2012 the sports ministry said no sport has been declared India's national game. Hockey is often called the national game because of its 8 Olympic gold medals.
12. (sort) Sort these into national symbols of India and things that are not official symbols.  
   **Answer:** the tiger → Official national symbol; the peacock → Official national symbol; Jana Gana Mana → Official national symbol; the Lion Capital → Official national symbol; cricket → Not an official symbol; hockey → Not an official symbol; the elephant as national animal → Not an official symbol. *Why:* The tiger, peacock, anthem and Lion Capital are official. ## Open questions
- **How many tigers can India's forests hold?** Tigers need large forests full of deer and other prey. Scientists study how much more room there is, but it depends on how forests, villages and roads change.
- **Do peahens really choose males by their eye-spots?** Some studies found that males with more eye-spots on their trains were chosen more often. Other studies found no clear link. Scientists still argue about what peahens look for.
- **Why does each tiger get their own stripe pattern?** Stripes form in the skin before a cub is born. Scientists have ideas about how chemicals in the growing skin make the pattern, but the details for tigers are still being worked out.
- **Can footprints alone ever count animals well?** Some scientists are training computers to tell individual animals apart from photos of their footprints. Whether this works well enough for tigers is still being tested.

## Connected maths
- A flag must be 3 parts long for every 2 parts high. Revathi makes a flag 30 cm long. How high must it be? Her brother makes one 60 cm long. How high must his be? **Revathi's: 20 cm. Her brother's: 40 cm.**. 30 cm is 3 groups of 10 cm, so the height is 2 groups of 10 cm, which is 20 cm. 60 cm is 3 groups of 20 cm, so the height is 2 groups of 20 cm, which is 40 cm. Check: a flag twice as long is twice as high. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Paw Print Test, Divya's gentle press in damp atta was 5 cm wide. Her hard press was 7 cm wide. Her mother's gentle press was 6 cm wide. Divya says, 'The 7 cm print must be my mother's, because it is the biggest.' Is she right? **No. The 7 cm print was Divya's own hard press.**. Divya's own prints were 5 cm and 7 cm, a difference of 2 cm. Her mother's gentle print was 6 cm, which is in between. So the biggest print came from the smaller hand, pressed hard. Size alone can't tell you whose print it is. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- India's tiger count found about 1,411 tigers in 2006 and at least 3,167 in 2022. Did the number of tigers more than double? **Yes. Double 1,411 is 2,822, and 3,167 is more than that.**. To double a number, add it to itself. 1,411 + 1,411 = 2,822. 3,167 is bigger than 2,822 by 345. So the tigers more than doubled in those 16 years. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- In the Anthem Clock, Arjun sang Jana Gana Mana in 58 seconds. His grandmother sang it in 50 seconds. Who was closer to 52 seconds, and by how much? **His grandmother, by 4 seconds.**. Arjun was 58 minus 52, which is 6 seconds too slow. His grandmother was 52 minus 50, which is 2 seconds too fast. 2 is less than 6, so she was closer, by 6 minus 2, which is 4 seconds. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
