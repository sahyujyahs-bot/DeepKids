# Separating mixtures: every method uses one difference between the parts
`sci.materials.separating-mixtures.L4`

**Summary.** Stir one mustard seed into a katori of rava and you can pick the seed out again. Stir in a pinch of salt and you cannot, though the salt is still there. Why does one come back so easily and the other not? In this lesson you separate mixtures by picking, sieving, a magnet, filtering and evaporating, and find that each method uses one difference between the parts. Then you ask a harder question: clear water looks like one material, so how would you know if it is?

**Intro.** *Put two spoons of rava in a katori. Drop in one mustard seed and stir. Can you get the seed back? Now stir in a pinch of salt. Can you get the salt back?* The mustard seed is black and round, so your eyes find the seed and your fingers lift the seed out. The salt grains are white and small, like the rava, so your eyes and fingers cannot tell them apart. Yet the salt has not turned into rava. Touch a little of the mixture to your tongue and you taste it. So the salt is still salt, and there must be some way to make it leave. Today you will find the ways people, other animals and the Earth use to take a mixture apart. Each time, ask one thing: what is different about the part that leaves?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a mixture is, and name mixtures that look like one material, such as air, sea water and 22-carat gold.
1. I can show that a material keeps its own properties inside a mixture, and say which properties of the whole mixture can be new.
2. I can separate a mixture by picking, sieving, a magnet, settling, filtering and evaporation, and say which difference between the parts each method uses.
3. I can choose the methods for a mixture of three or four materials, put them in a sensible order, and give my reason for each step.
4. I can tell how living beings and the Earth separate mixtures, with examples such as a kidney, a flamingo's beak and rain.
5. I can explain why 'pure' depends on how closely a thing is tested, and name one question about mixing that nobody can answer yet.

## Terms
- **Material**: What a thing is made of. *e.g.* A steel spoon is made of steel and a wooden spoon is made of wood.
- **Mixture**: Two or more materials put together, each still the same material. *e.g.* Stir a cup of rice into a cup of toor dal and you have a mixture of rice and dal.
- **Property**: Something a material does or has, such as its colour, its taste or being pulled by a magnet. *e.g.* Salt is white, tastes salty and disappears in water. Those are three of its properties.
- **Separate**: To take the parts of a mixture apart so that each part is by itself. *e.g.* Picking the stones out of a plate of rice separates the stones from the rice.
- **Picking**: Taking one part out of a mixture one piece at a time, using a difference your eyes or fingers can tell. *e.g.* A grandmother spreads dal on a plate and picks out the tiny stones.
- **Sieve**: A sheet or net with holes of one size. Small pieces fall through and big pieces stay on top. *e.g.* An atta sieve lets flour fall through and holds back the brown bran flakes.
- **Winnowing**: Letting moving air carry the light pieces of a mixture away while the heavy pieces fall straight down. *e.g.* A farmer pours paddy from a flat basket in a breeze, and the husk blows to one side.
- **Husk**: The dry, light outer covering of a grain. *e.g.* Paddy has a husk around each rice grain, and the husk is removed before the rice is cooked.
- **Settling**: Leaving a mixture still so that heavier solid pieces sink to the bottom of a liquid. *e.g.* Wash rice, wait a few seconds, and the rice sinks while the dust stays in the water.
- **Pouring off**: Tipping the clear liquid from the top of a settled mixture without stirring up the bottom layer. Its other name is decanting. *e.g.* After rice settles, you pour the cloudy washing water into the sink and keep the rice.
- **Gravity**: The pull that makes a dropped stone fall to the ground. Scientists describe it in more than one way, and no theory yet joins gravity with quantum physics. *e.g.* A dropped pin falls to the floor, and gravity also pulls sand to the bottom of a tumbler of water.
- **Dissolve**: To spread out into a liquid as particles so small that you cannot see them. *e.g.* Salt stirred into water dissolves. The water looks clear, but it tastes salty.
- **Solution**: A liquid with something dissolved in it. *e.g.* Salt water is a solution of salt in water, and so is sweet tea.
- **Filter**: A material with holes too small for solid pieces to pass, used to hold the solid back and let the liquid through. *e.g.* A tea strainer is a filter that holds back the leaves and lets the tea pass.
- **Pressure**: How hard something pushes on each small part of a surface. *e.g.* Press a thumb on a table, then press with a fingertip. The fingertip pushes harder on each small spot.
- **Filtrate**: The liquid that has come through a filter. *e.g.* The tea in the cup, after it has been strained, is the filtrate.
- **Residue**: The solid that stays behind on a filter or at the bottom of a dish. *e.g.* The wet tea leaves left in the strainer are the residue.
- **Plankton**: Tiny living beings that drift in sea, river and pond water. *e.g.* Some plankton are green specks that make food from sunlight and some are tiny animals. Flamingos in a salt pan strain them out of the water.
- **Evaporation**: A liquid changing into a gas and leaving the surface. When salt water evaporates, the water leaves and the salt stays. *e.g.* Wet clothes dry on a line because their water evaporates into the air.
- **Distillation**: Evaporating a liquid and then cooling the vapour back into a liquid, so that you keep the liquid as well as leaving the solid behind. *e.g.* Steam from boiling salt water that touches a cold lid turns back into drops of fresh water, and the salt stays in the pot.
- **Energy**: A number that physicists work out and that stays the same while things change. Nobody yet knows finally what energy is, but the number works. *e.g.* The Sun gives the energy that dries a plate of salt water, and your arm gives the energy to stir.
- **Water vapour**: Water as a gas. You cannot see it, and it is always in the air. *e.g.* The steam you can see above a pot is made of tiny drops. The water vapour that makes them is invisible.
- **Condense**: To change from a gas back into a liquid, usually by cooling. *e.g.* A cold glass of water gets tiny drops on the outside when water vapour in the air condenses on it.
- **Attract**: To pull something closer, without needing to touch it first. *e.g.* A magnet attracts a steel pin across a small gap, before they touch.
- **Magnetic**: Able to be attracted by a magnet. Iron and steel are magnetic. *e.g.* A paper clip is magnetic. A plastic comb and a pencil are not.
- **Magnetism**: The pull between magnets, and between a magnet and things such as iron. *e.g.* A fridge magnet that sticks to a steel door shows magnetism.
- **Particle**: A piece of a material so small that you cannot see it. *e.g.* Each grain of salt holds an enormous number of particles, too small to see even with a magnifying glass.
- **Chemical change**: A change in which materials join and become a new material that cannot simply be taken apart again. *e.g.* Lemon juice added to milk makes lumps and a thin liquid, and you cannot get smooth milk back.
- **Chromatography**: A way of separating a mixture by letting a liquid creep through paper, carrying each part along at its own speed. *e.g.* Water climbing a paper strip splits black ink into bands of blue, pink and yellow.
- **Uniform mixture**: A mixture so evenly mixed that it looks the same all through. Scientists call it a homogeneous mixture. *e.g.* A glass of salt water, or the air in a room, is a uniform mixture.
- **Mixture with visible parts**: A mixture whose different parts you can see. Scientists call it a heterogeneous mixture. *e.g.* Bhel puri is a mixture of puffed rice, onion and sev, and you can see each part.
- **Pure**: Made of one substance only. In everyday talk it also means clean and safe. *e.g.* A shop calls a bottle of water pure when it is clean. A chemist asks whether it holds only water.
- **Purity**: How much of a sample is the named substance, written as a number such as 99.9 out of 100. *e.g.* Gold sold as 999 is 999 parts gold in every 1,000, and the other part is something else.
- **Molecule**: A group of atoms joined together as one piece. Water is made of molecules. *e.g.* One water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom joined together.
- **Heavy water**: Water whose hydrogen atoms are a rare heavier kind. It is separated from ordinary water in special plants. *e.g.* India makes heavy water in plants for some of its nuclear power stations.
- **Quantum physics**: The physics of very small things, such as atoms and particles, which behave quite differently from everyday things. *e.g.* Quantum physics says that two particles of the same kind are exactly alike, with no hidden label.
- **Entropy**: A number that tells how many ways the parts of a mixture can be arranged. A mixed state has far more ways than a separated one. *e.g.* There are only a few ways for all the blue ink to sit in one drop. There is a huge number of ways for it to spread through the tumbler.
- **Paramanu**: The smallest piece that old Indian philosophers of the Vaisheshika school said cannot be cut any further. *e.g.* In their account, a clay pot is made when paramanu come together, and it ends when they come apart.
- **Samyoga**: A Sanskrit word for things coming together, one of the two ideas the Vaisheshika thinkers used to explain mixing and making. *e.g.* Rice and dal poured into one bowl show samyoga. Picking them apart again shows vibhaga.
- **Vibhaga**: A Sanskrit word for things coming apart, the partner of samyoga. *e.g.* Winnowing, where the husk leaves the grain, is vibhaga that anyone can see.
- **Brine**: Water with a lot of salt dissolved in it. *e.g.* Sea water is thin brine, and the water in a salt pan gets saltier day by day until it is thick brine.
- **Alloy**: A metal made by mixing two or more metals, or a metal and another material, so that it looks like one metal. *e.g.* A 22-carat gold bangle is an alloy of gold with a little copper or silver.

## Explanations
### A mixture is two or more materials together, and each one is still itself · *defined*
**Definition:** A mixture is two or more materials put together. Each material stays the same material, and can be taken out again without changing into something else.

Tip a cup of rice into a cup of toor dal and stir. You now have a mixture. Every grain of rice is still rice, and every grain of dal is still dal. Nothing new was made. You could sit and pick the two apart, and you would have the same cup of rice and the same cup of dal. The parts of a mixture can be in any amounts. One spoon of dal in a sack of rice is a mixture, and so is half and half. The parts can be solids, liquids or gases. Salt water is a solid mixed into a liquid. Air is several gases mixed together.
- e.g. Sambar powder is a mixture of ground chilli, coriander seed, dal, fenugreek and other spices. Each family chooses different amounts.
- e.g. Soil is a mixture of sand, clay, bits of dead leaves, water, air and many small living beings.
- e.g. A glass of nimbu pani is a mixture of water, lemon juice, sugar and salt.
- e.g. The air in your room is a mixture of gases, with dust and water vapour mixed in.
- **Cooks and shopkeepers in India:** In a kitchen, a mixture is anything made by putting things together, and the amounts are the cook's choice. A masala, a chivda and a fruit salad are all mixtures in this sense. This everyday meaning matches the science meaning well.
- **Chemists, since Joseph Proust's work on fixed proportions in about 1799:** Chemists add one test. In a mixture the amounts can be anything. In a compound, which is a new material made when materials join, the amounts are always fixed. Water is always two parts hydrogen to one part oxygen. So a chemist asks 'can the amounts vary?' to decide if something is a mixture.
### A material keeps its own properties inside a mixture · *defined*
**Definition:** A property is something a material does or has, such as its colour, its taste, or being attracted by a magnet. In a mixture, each material generally keeps the properties it had before.

Mix iron pins into a bowl of sand. Hold a magnet over the bowl, and the pins jump to it just as they did before. Being mixed with sand did not change the iron. Stir salt into water until you cannot see it. The water tastes salty, because the salt is still salt. This is why separating is possible at all. A method can only pull a material out if that material still behaves in its own way. Scientists say 'generally' because the whole mixture can show something new. Salt water freezes at a lower temperature than plain water does. Neither the salt nor the water does that alone.
- e.g. A raisin in a bowl of poha is still sweet and still soft.
- e.g. Sugar stirred into tea has lost its white colour and its crunch, but it has kept its sweetness.
- e.g. Brass is copper and zinc melted together. Brass is harder than either metal, and that hardness belongs to the mixture.
- e.g. Dry salt and plain water each carry electricity very poorly. Salt water carries it well.
- **School science books, including the Cambridge primary course:** They state the rule simply: materials generally keep their properties in a mixture. The rule is right for the properties we use to separate things, such as size, magnetism and dissolving.
- **Materials scientists who make alloys:** An alloy is a metal mixed with other materials. People who make alloys mix on purpose to get a property that no part has alone. Steel, which is iron with a little carbon, is far stronger than iron. For them the interesting part of a mixture is what is new.
- **The Charvaka thinkers of ancient India, as reported in Madhava's Sarvadarshanasamgraha, chapter 1, of the 14th century CE:** They pointed to fermenting. The ingredients of a fermented drink do not make anyone drunk, but the mixture, after it ferments, does. They used this to argue that a new power can appear when things are combined. Today we know that yeast, who are living beings, change the sugar into a new material, so this is more than a mixture.
### Mixing that makes a new material is not a mixture any more · *defined*
**Definition:** When materials join and change into a new material, a chemical change has happened. The new material cannot be taken apart by picking, sieving, a magnet, filtering or evaporation.

Squeeze lemon juice into warm milk. The milk breaks into white lumps and a thin yellow-green liquid. You cannot get smooth milk back. Something new was made. Compare that with salt in water. Leave salt water in a plate in the sun, and the same salt comes back. So here is a test you can use. Ask: 'Can I get the same things back?' If yes, it was a mixture. If the things have changed into something else, a chemical change happened. Many things in the kitchen are both. Dosa batter starts as a mixture of ground rice, urad dal and water. Overnight, bacteria and yeasts living in the batter change part of it, and the sour taste is new.
- e.g. Wet sand dries back into sand. That was a mixture.
- e.g. Cement and water set into a hard solid that never becomes powder and water again. That was a chemical change.
- e.g. A rusty nail is iron that has joined with oxygen. A magnet test and a file will not bring the shiny iron back.
- **Chemists:** They separate two kinds of change. A physical change, such as mixing, dissolving or melting, leaves each material the same material. A chemical change makes new materials. The methods in this lesson undo only the first kind.
- **Chemists who study dissolving closely:** They point out that the line is not perfectly sharp. When salt dissolves, each grain comes apart into two kinds of charged particles, called ions, and water molecules gather round each one. Some call that a small chemical change. It still counts as a mixture in school science, because drying brings the same salt back.
### Every way of separating uses one difference between the parts · *defined*
**Definition:** To separate a mixture, find a property in which the parts differ. Then use something that treats the parts differently because of that property.

A sieve does not know what rice is. It only lets small things through and stops big things. A magnet only attracts some metals. Water only dissolves some solids. Each of these treats the parts of a mixture differently for one reason. So before you choose a method, you ask what is different about the parts. Is one bigger? Is one attracted by a magnet? Does one dissolve in water? Does one sink faster? If the parts differ in size, sieve them. If they differ in dissolving, add water. If you can find no difference at all, no method will work. Then you need a closer look, or a new instrument, to find a difference.
- e.g. Rajma and rava differ in size, so a sieve separates them.
- e.g. Iron pins and brass pins look alike and are the same size. They differ in being attracted by a magnet.
- e.g. Salt and fine sand differ in dissolving. Salt dissolves in water and sand does not.
- e.g. Husk and grain differ in how easily moving air carries them.
### Picking by hand uses a difference the eyes or fingers can tell · *defined*
**Definition:** Picking, also called hand-picking, is taking one part out of a mixture one piece at a time. It uses a difference in colour, shape or size that you can see or feel.

Spread a handful of rice on a steel plate. A small grey stone shows up against the white grains, and you flick it out. That is picking. It needs no tool, only a difference your senses can catch. Picking works well when the unwanted pieces are few and easy to see. It is slow when there are many, and it fails when the pieces are too small or look alike. Picking is still one of the most used methods in the world. Much of India's tea is plucked by hand, two leaves and a bud at a time. Most of the pluckers are women. Fingers can choose the young leaves from the old.
- e.g. Taking small stones and bits of husk out of rice or dal before cooking.
- e.g. Sorting a mixed bag of buttons by colour.
- e.g. Taking the whole red chillies and curry leaves out of your sambar before you eat.
- e.g. A sparrow at a heap of swept-up grain and grit picks up the grain and leaves the grit.
- **Waste pickers in Indian cities:** By different estimates, between 15 lakh and 40 lakh people in India earn their living by picking paper, plastic, glass and metal out of mixed rubbish. They recover a large share of what the country recycles. Their groups, such as the waste pickers' cooperative SWaCH in Pune, ask families to keep wet and dry waste apart. Picking through mixed waste is slow and unsafe.
- **Engineers who build sorting machines for rice mills:** A modern rice mill picks too, with a machine. Grains fall past cameras, and a puff of air knocks out each dark or broken grain. The machine uses the same difference as the eye, which is colour, and checks thousands of grains each second.
### Sieving uses a difference in size · *defined*
**Definition:** A sieve is a sheet or net with holes all of one size. Sieving separates pieces smaller than the holes, which fall through, from pieces bigger than the holes, which stay on top.

Shake atta in a sieve. The fine flour falls through, and the brown flakes of bran stay on the mesh. The holes of an atta sieve are less than 1 millimetre across. A piece gets through only if it is smaller than the hole. So the size of the holes decides what is separated. You choose a sieve with holes bigger than one part and smaller than the other. Sieving only works when the parts have clearly different sizes. Rava and salt are too close in size, so no kitchen sieve can part them. Shaking matters too. It gives each small piece many chances to meet a hole.
- e.g. At a building site, workers throw sand against a sloping wire screen. Sand falls through, and pebbles roll down the front.
- e.g. A colander for washed vegetables is a sieve with large holes. Water and grit pass, and the vegetables stay.
- e.g. Sieving besan before making laddu takes out the lumps.
- **Cooks in Indian kitchens:** A cook thinks of a sieve as sorting big from small, and for round things like mustard seed and rajma that is exactly right.
- **Engineers who test soil and sand:** They point out that a sieve measures the width of a piece and not its length. A rice grain about 6 millimetres long and 2 millimetres wide can stand on end and slip through a hole 3 millimetres across. So for long pieces, 'size' means the second smallest measurement. They stack sieves from coarse to fine to sort a soil into many sizes at once.
### Winnowing uses a difference in how moving air carries the parts · *defined*
**Definition:** Winnowing is separating light pieces from heavy ones by letting moving air carry the light pieces away while the heavy ones fall straight down.

After a paddy harvest, the grain is mixed with husk, bits of straw and dust. A farmer lifts the mixture in a flat basket and pours it slowly from shoulder height while a breeze blows. The heavy grain falls almost straight down into a heap. The light husk is blown sideways and lands farther away. The air pushes on every piece. The push moves a light, flat flake much more than a heavy, compact grain. The flat winnowing basket has many names: muram in Tamil, soop in Hindi, kulo in Bengali. At home it is flicked up and down, which tosses the mixture so that the husk flies off the front edge.
- e.g. Roasted peanuts rubbed between the palms shed their thin red skins. One puff of breath blows the skins away and leaves the nuts.
- e.g. Blowing gently across a plate of jeera to remove dust.
- e.g. Dandelion and milkweed seeds are carried off by wind while heavier seeds fall near the parent plant.
- **Farmers across India:** They speak of light and heavy, and choose the time by the wind. Too little breeze leaves husk in the grain. Too much blows grain away with the husk.
- **Physicists who study drag:** Drag is the push of air on a thing moving through it. They say weight alone does not decide what flies. What matters is weight compared with the area the air can push on. A husk and a grain of the same weight would still separate, because the husk is flat and wide.
### Settling and pouring off use a difference in how fast the parts sink · *defined*
**Definition:** Settling, also called sedimentation, is leaving a mixture still. The heavy pieces sink to the bottom of the liquid. Tipping off the clear liquid from the top, without stirring up the bottom, is called decanting.

Wash rice in a bowl of water. Swirl it, then wait a few seconds. The rice sinks. The cloudy water above carries fine dust and loose starch, and you pour it away. You have just used settling and pouring off. Large, heavy pieces sink in seconds. Fine pieces of mud can take hours, because water drags hard on small things. Some pieces are so small that they never settle by themselves. People have long added something to help. Tiny pieces of mud that touch the helper stick together into bigger clumps, and the clumps sink.
- e.g. Muddy water left in a bucket overnight is clear at the top by morning, with a brown layer at the bottom.
- e.g. Water works add alum, a white mineral salt, to make fine mud clump and sink before the water is filtered.
- e.g. In a jar of home-made ghee, the brown milk solids settle and the clear ghee is poured off.
- **Sushruta Samhita, Sutrasthana chapter 45, an Indian medical text compiled by about the 4th century CE:** It lists ways to make muddy water clear. One is the seed of the kataka tree, also called the clearing nut, which is rubbed on the inside of the water pot. Another is straining through cloth. In Tamil the seed is called thettran kottai, and some families still use it.
- **Samia Al Azharia Jahn, who studied village women's methods in Sudan in the 1970s and 1980s:** She recorded that women there cleared river water with crushed seeds of the moringa tree. The tree came from India and is also called the drumstick tree. Later tests in laboratories showed that a protein in the seeds makes mud and many bacteria clump together and sink.
- **Water engineers:** They agree that settling removes most mud. They add that it does not remove what is dissolved, and it does not remove every microbe. So settled water still needs filtering and boiling or another treatment before drinking.
### Filtering holds back solid pieces and lets the liquid through · *defined*
**Definition:** A filter is a material with holes too small for the solid pieces to pass, such as cloth or filter paper. Filtering separates a solid that has not dissolved from a liquid. The liquid that comes through is called the filtrate, and the solid left behind is called the residue.

Pour tea through a strainer. The liquid runs into the cup and the leaves stay behind. A filter is a sieve for liquids, with much smaller holes. The holes in cotton cloth are too small to see easily. The holes in laboratory filter paper are about one hundredth of a millimetre across, far thinner than a hair. A filter stops only pieces bigger than its holes. Anything dissolved goes straight through with the water. Filter salty, muddy water through paper and the mud stays on the paper, but the water that drips out is still salty. That limit is important. Clear water is not always clean water.
- e.g. A South Indian coffee filter has two steel cups. Hot water drips through the ground coffee and through tiny holes, and the grounds stay in the top cup.
- e.g. Paneer is made by pouring curdled milk through muslin. The cloth holds the solid paneer and lets the liquid whey through.
- e.g. A mask filters dust out of the air you breathe.
- e.g. The filter in a water purifier at home has holes small enough to stop most bacteria.
- **Rita Colwell, Anwar Huq and their team, in villages of Bangladesh from 1999 to 2002:** They asked families to pour pond and river water through an old cotton sari folded at least four times. Cholera, a disease carried in water, fell by about half in those villages. The bacteria that cause cholera are small enough to pass through cloth. But most of them ride on larger plankton, tiny beings drifting in the water, and the folded cloth stops those.
- **Tapputi, a perfume maker in Mesopotamia, on a clay tablet of about 1200 BCE:** The tablet records her method for making perfume for the palace. It includes straining her mixtures again and again. She is one of the first workers with materials whose name we know, and she was the overseer of the palace's perfume makers.
- **Doctors and water engineers:** They warn that a cloth or paper filter cannot stop viruses or anything dissolved, such as salt, arsenic or fluoride. Filtering is one step. It does not make every water safe alone.
### A magnet uses a difference in being attracted · *defined*
**Definition:** Magnetic separation is taking a magnetic material, such as iron or steel, out of a mixture with a magnet. The magnet attracts that material and leaves the others behind.

Mix steel pins into a bowl of rice. Wrap a magnet in a small plastic bag and pass it through the bowl. The pins cling to the bag and the rice does not. Lift the magnet out, hold it over an empty plate and pull the magnet out of the bag. The pins drop. A magnet strongly attracts iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. It does not attract rice, sand, plastic, wood, copper, aluminium or gold. So the method works only when one part is magnetic and the rest are not. Flour mills and spice factories pass their grain over strong magnets. A tiny chip of steel from a machine is caught before it reaches a packet.
- e.g. Stir a wrapped magnet through dry sand from a beach or a building site. Black grains often cling to it. They are a mineral that holds iron.
- e.g. In a scrap yard, a crane with a large magnet lifts steel out of a heap of mixed scrap.
- e.g. On the beaches of Chavara in Kerala and Manavalakurichi in Tamil Nadu, the sand holds heavy minerals that are worth money. The works there use magnets to take out ilmenite. It is a black mineral that a strong magnet can pull a little.
- **School science books:** They sort materials into magnetic and non-magnetic. For choosing a separating method at home, that sorting is all you need.
- **Physicists, since Michael Faraday's tests in 1845:** Faraday found that every material answers a strong enough magnet a little. Most are pushed or attracted so weakly that we never notice. So 'non-magnetic' means 'too weak to matter here'. Mineral works use very strong magnets to separate materials that a fridge magnet ignores.
### Evaporation takes the water away as vapour and leaves the dissolved solid behind · *defined*
**Definition:** Evaporation is a liquid changing into a gas. A solution is a liquid with something dissolved in it. When water evaporates from a solution, the water goes into the air as water vapour. The dissolved solid stays behind.

Pour a little salt water into a steel plate and leave it on a sunny windowsill. In a day or two the water is gone, and a white crust is left. Taste it. It is the salt. The water did not vanish. It went into the air as water vapour, a gas you cannot see. Salt does not turn into a gas at these temperatures, so it could not follow. This is the method for a solid that has dissolved, where no filter can help. One litre of sea water holds about 35 grams of salt, which is about six level teaspoons. Evaporation gives you the solid and loses the water. To keep the water as well, the vapour must be cooled back into liquid. That longer method is called distillation.
- e.g. In the salt pans of Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu and the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, salty water is let into shallow ponds. It comes from the sea or from under the ground. The Sun and the wind take the water. Gujarat makes about three quarters of India's salt.
- e.g. In the Salt March of 1930, Mohandas Gandhi and his companions walked about 385 kilometres to the sea at Dandi. There they picked up salt left by evaporated sea water. A law of the time said Indians could not make their own salt.
- e.g. Jaggery is made by boiling sugarcane juice until most of the water has evaporated.
- e.g. A white ring left inside a steel vessel after water has boiled away is made of minerals that were dissolved in the tap water.
- **The Agariya families of the Little Rann of Kutch:** They have made salt there for many generations. They pump salty water from under the ground into pans they shape by treading with their feet, and rake the crystals for months in great heat. They speak of farming salt, and the knowledge of when a pan is ready is theirs.
- **Kautilya's Arthashastra, Book 2, chapter 12, written between about 300 BCE and 300 CE:** This Indian book on running a kingdom names an officer in charge of salt, who collects the king's share from salt makers. It shows that separating salt from water was already a large, organised trade.
- **Archaeologists, after F. R. Allchin's study of 1979:** Clay vessels found at Shaikhan Dheri in Gandhara, in today's Pakistan, look like parts of stills for distillation. They date from about 150 BCE to 350 CE. Allchin argued that distillation may have begun in ancient India. Other historians think it began in several places. They credit Maria of Alexandria, a woman alchemist of about the 1st to 3rd century CE, with an early still.
### Choosing a method: find the difference, decide what you want to keep, then put the steps in order · *defined*
**Definition:** To separate a mixture of several materials, follow four steps. List the parts and decide which ones you want to keep. Find one difference for each part. Pick the method that uses that difference. Then do the dry methods before the wet ones.

Think of a mixture of steel pins, rajma, sand and salt. No single method can part all four. So you use a chain. First, pass a magnet through it. Only the pins come out. Second, sieve it. The rajma stays on the sieve, and the sand and salt fall through. Third, add water and stir. Only the salt dissolves. Fourth, filter. The sand stays on the paper. Fifth, evaporate the water. The salt comes back. The order matters. A magnet and a sieve work best on dry things, so they come before the water. You also ask what you want to keep. If you want only the sand, you can pour the salty water away and skip the last step.
- e.g. Rava and salt, the puzzle at the start: add water, filter through cloth so the rava stays, then evaporate the water to get the salt.
- e.g. Tea leaves, sugar and water in a cup of black tea: a strainer takes the leaves, and evaporation would bring back the sugar.
- e.g. Muddy water: let it settle, pour off the top, then filter what you poured.
- **Chemists in a laboratory:** They choose the method that gives the purest part, even if it is slow, because their tests need clean materials.
- **Engineers at a factory, a mine or a water works:** They choose by cost. Each step uses energy, water, time and people's work. They often accept a part that is clean enough and not perfect. They also ask where the unwanted part will go, because it does not disappear.
- **Groups that work on waste, such as Hasiru Dala in Bengaluru:** They say the cheapest separation is the one you never have to do. Wet waste and dry waste kept in two bins at home need no separating later.
### Chromatography separates materials that differ only a little, such as the colours in one ink · *defined*
**Definition:** Chromatography is a way of separating a mixture with a liquid that creeps through paper or powder. The liquid carries each part along at its own speed, so the parts spread out into separate bands.

Draw a thick dot with a black sketch pen near the bottom of a strip of filter paper or a white coffee filter. Dip only the bottom edge in water. The water climbs the paper and passes through the dot. As it climbs, the black spreads into bands of blue, pink or yellow. Many black inks are a mixture of dyes. Each dye differs a little in how strongly it clings to the paper and how well it dissolves in water. A dye that clings less travels farther. So a small difference, repeated over the whole climb, becomes a gap you can see. Chromatography is used wherever the parts are too alike for a sieve or a filter.
- e.g. Laboratories use it to check food for colours that are not allowed.
- e.g. Sports bodies use a form of it to test athletes' samples for banned drugs.
- e.g. A permanent marker does not spread in water. Its dyes dissolve in spirit and not in water, so a different liquid is needed.
- **Mikhail Tsvet, a botanist working in Warsaw, in 1903:** He washed an extract of green leaves down a glass tube packed with chalk powder. The green separated into bands of green and yellow. Leaf green was a mixture of several pigments, which are coloured materials. He named the method chromatography, from Greek words for colour and writing.
- **Archer Martin and Richard Synge, in 1941:** For about thirty years few chemists used Tsvet's method. Martin and Synge worked out why it separates and made it work for colourless materials too. Since then it has become one of the most used methods in chemistry.
### Living beings separate mixtures all day, using the same differences · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living being takes what they need out of a mixture, such as air, water or food, and leaves or sends back the rest. Bodies do this with filters, with evaporation and with surfaces that let some materials through and stop others.

You are separating a mixture as you read this. The air you breathe in is about 21 parts oxygen in every 100. The air you breathe out is about 16. Your lungs took some oxygen out of the mixture and left the rest. Your two kidneys filter your blood. Roughly 180 litres of liquid pass through their filters each day. The kidneys take back nearly all of it and send out about one and a half litres as urine. Every cell does the same on a small scale. A cell's skin lets some materials in and keeps others out. A cell chooses what to let in. Many biologists describe this as automatic chemistry, with no choosing in it.
- e.g. A flamingo feeds with their head upside down. Their beak has rows of fine plates like a comb. Their tongue pumps water in and out, and the plates hold back tiny algae and small animals.
- e.g. Honey bees collect nectar, which can be more than half water. In the hive they spread it thin and fan it with their wings until enough water has evaporated. Honey is under one fifth water.
- e.g. An oyster draws sea water across their gills and filters out floating food. One adult oyster can filter more than 100 litres in a day.
- e.g. A mangrove tree of the Sundarbans takes in salty water. Their roots stop most of the salt, and some kinds push the rest out through their leaves.
- **Physiologists, who study how bodies work:** They describe a kidney or a gill by its sizes, pressures and flows, and can build a machine that copies it. A dialysis machine filters the blood of a person whose kidneys have stopped working.
- **Biologists who study how cells sense and respond, such as the microbiologist James Shapiro:** They describe cells as sensing what is around them and acting on it. A bacterium detects sugar outside, makes the right gate in their skin and lets the sugar in. In this view, separating is something a living being does, and is a form of deciding.
### The Earth separates mixtures too: rain, rivers and salt lakes · *defined*
**Definition:** Sunlight, moving water, wind and gravity separate the Earth's materials by the same differences people use. Evaporation leaves salt behind, and flowing water sorts stones by size.

The sea is salty and rain is not. The Sun warms the sea and water evaporates. The salt cannot follow, so the vapour that makes clouds is fresh. Rain is sea water that the Sun has separated. Rivers sort too. A fast mountain stream can roll large stones. As the river slows on the plain, it drops the pebbles first, then the sand, then the silt. The finest clay travels to the delta. So a river lays out its load by size, as a stack of sieves would. Where a lake has no way out to the sea, water leaves only by evaporation. The salts stay and build up year after year.
- e.g. Sambhar Lake in Rajasthan is India's largest salt lake away from the coast. Rivers flow in, no river flows out, and people have collected salt there for well over a thousand years.
- e.g. The Ganga carries boulders near Rishikesh, sand through the plains and fine silt to the Sundarbans delta.
- e.g. Wind blowing across a beach carries the fine, light sand inland to build dunes and leaves the coarse shell pieces behind.
- **Geologists:** They read the sizes of grains in a rock to tell how fast the water was moving when the grains were laid down. Sorting by a river that dried up long ago is still recorded in stone.
- **Chandogya Upanishad, 6.10:** The text says that the rivers flow east and west and enter the sea. The sea's water rises as cloud and falls as rain, and it is all one sea. Once joined, the rivers do not know 'I am this river' or 'I am that river'. The sage uses the water's journey to speak of all beings sharing one ground.
### Some mixtures look like one material: air, sea water, milk, steel and gold jewellery · *defined*
**Definition:** In a uniform mixture, the parts are spread so evenly and are so small that the mixture looks the same all through. Scientists call this a homogeneous mixture. A mixture whose different parts you can see is called heterogeneous.

A plate of bhel puri is plainly a mixture. You can see the puffed rice, the onion and the sev. A glass of salt water is a mixture too, but your eyes see one clear liquid. The parts are single particles, far too small to see. Air is the same. Dry air is about 78 parts nitrogen, 21 parts oxygen and 1 part argon in every 100, with a very small amount of carbon dioxide. No part of a room has its own patch of oxygen. So looking is not a safe test for a mixture. You need another test. Tasting found the salt. Evaporation would find it too.
- e.g. Gold jewellery marked 916 is 22-carat gold. It is 916 parts gold in every 1,000. The rest is copper, silver or zinc, mixed in to make the gold hard enough to wear.
- e.g. Milk looks like one white liquid. Under a microscope it is water with tiny floating drops of fat. Churning curd gathers that fat into butter.
- e.g. A steel tumbler is iron mixed with chromium, nickel and a little carbon.
- e.g. Mist is tiny drops of water mixed into air.
- **Shoppers and cooks:** We call air, milk and steel by one name each and treat each as one material. For cooking, breathing and buying a tumbler, that works.
- **Chemists:** They ask how small the mixed pieces are. In a solution such as salt water, the pieces are single particles. In milk and mist the pieces are larger clumps, still too small for the eye, and this kind of mixture is called a colloid. In bhel puri the pieces are easy to see.
### How pure is 'pure'? Purity is a number, and it is never quite 100 · *defined*
**Definition:** A pure material is made of one substance only. In practice no sample is perfectly pure, so purity is given as a number that says how much of the sample is the named substance.

A packet says 'pure salt'. A bottle says 'pure water'. In a shop, pure means clean and safe, with nothing added that should not be there. A chemist means something stricter: one substance and nothing else. By that meaning, hardly anything you have touched is pure. Bottled drinking water has minerals dissolved in it, and the label lists them. The purest gold bars are stamped 999, which means 999 parts gold in 1,000. The silicon used for computer chips is among the purest materials people make. Its makers reach about 99.9999999 parts in every 100. That is one stray atom among a hundred crore, and still not zero. Each cleaner grade costs more work than the last.
- e.g. Table salt often has a little iodine added on purpose, to keep people healthy. It is a mixture by design.
- e.g. Rainwater has dissolved gases from the air and dust that the drops formed around.
- e.g. Water with nearly everything removed is used in laboratories and chip factories. It is a poor carrier of electricity.
- **Indian food safety rules and shop labels:** Here 'pure' is a promise that nothing harmful or cheap has been mixed in. Pure ghee means no vanaspati has been added. The word is about trust between a seller and a buyer.
- **Chemists:** They never write 'pure' without a number. They give a grade, such as 99.9 in every 100, and say which test measured it. A better instrument can find a smaller trace, so the number depends on how closely anyone has looked.
- **Anaxagoras, in Greece, about 450 BCE, in the fragments numbered 6 and 11:** He taught that in everything there is a portion of everything. A thing is named after what it has most of. He reasoned this from food. Bread and water become hair and bone, so he held that hair and bone must already be in them in small amounts. Chemists today explain that change differently, by materials being rebuilt in the body.
### Is water just H2O? Even one 'pure' substance is a mixture on a closer look · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists and philosophers disagree about whether water is one substance, or a mixture of several kinds of water molecules that differ slightly.

A molecule is a group of atoms joined together. School books say that water is one substance, made of molecules written H2O: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Take water with every other material removed. Look closer, and it still holds a mixture. Hydrogen atoms come in a common light kind and a rare heavy kind. So about 1 water molecule in every 3,200 has one heavy hydrogen atom and weighs more. At any moment, about 2 molecules in every 100 crore have split into two charged pieces. The molecules also cling to each other in groups that form and break very quickly. So is water one thing, or a mixture of kinds? Scientists and philosophers of science do not agree. What would you count as 'the same kind'?
- e.g. Heavy water, in which both hydrogen atoms are the heavy kind, can be separated from ordinary water. India makes it in special plants for some of its nuclear power stations.
- e.g. Ice made of heavy water sinks in ordinary water, while ordinary ice floats.
- **Most chemistry books:** Water is the substance whose molecules are H2O. The heavy kind and the split pieces are small details of that one substance. This view lets chemists name materials simply and it works for nearly every task.
- **The philosopher of science Michael Weisberg, in 'Water is not H2O', 2006:** He argues that the formula alone does not pick out what we call water. What counts as the same substance depends on what you are doing. For drinking, heavy and light water are different, because a large amount of heavy water harms a body. For many other tasks they are the same.
- **The historian and philosopher of science Hasok Chang, in 'Is Water H2O?', 2012:** He shows that chemists argued for decades in the 1800s before they settled on the formula. Other ways of describing water also fitted the evidence of the time. He asks us to see the formula as a very successful way of describing water, and to keep other ways in view.
### Mixing happens by itself, but separating always needs work · *defined*
**Definition:** Materials that can mix will spread into each other if left alone. Taking them apart again always needs energy, from the Sun, a fire, a motor or a body.

Let one drop of blue ink fall into a tumbler of still water. Do not stir. In an hour the whole tumbler is pale blue. The ink mixed itself in, because the particles of water and ink never stop moving. Now wait for the ink to gather back into a drop. You can wait for ever. To get it out you must do something, such as evaporating all the water, and that takes energy. Every method in this lesson has such a cost. Your arm shakes the sieve. The Sun dries the salt pan. Getting drinking water from the sea shows the size of the cost. The best plants use about 3 to 4 units of electricity for every 1,000 litres. Physics sets a lowest possible cost of about 1 unit, and no machine can go below it.
- e.g. The Minjur plant near Chennai opened in 2010. It makes about 10 crore litres of drinking water from sea water each day, and it uses a great deal of electricity to do so.
- e.g. The smell of agarbatti spreads through a room without a fan. Nobody has seen the smell gather back into the stick.
- e.g. Your kidneys use about one tenth of the energy your body spends at rest, mostly to take back salts and water after filtering.
- **Engineers:** They treat the cost of separating as a bill to be cut. Much of the energy used by the world's factories goes into separating things: metals from rock, petrol from crude oil, fresh water from salt water.
- **Physicists, since Rudolf Clausius in 1865:** They measure how spread out and mixed things are with a number called entropy. Left alone, the entropy of a closed box of things goes up or stays the same. Separating lowers the entropy in one place, and it always raises it by more somewhere else, usually as waste heat.
### Why does mixing go only one way? Science has a partial answer and then runs out · *idea*
**Definition:** Physicists can explain why mixed arrangements are far more likely than separated ones. They cannot yet explain why the universe started in an orderly state. That orderly start is what gives the world a 'before' and an 'after'.

Here is the puzzle. Film two particles bumping and play the film backwards. Nothing looks wrong. The rules for single particles work the same both ways in time. Now film ink spreading in water and play it backwards. Everyone can tell it is backwards. In 1877 Ludwig Boltzmann gave a reason. There are hugely more ways for ink particles to be spread out than gathered in one drop. Moving at random, they almost surely end up spread out. But that reason needs a start. The ink had to begin as a drop. Follow that back, and the whole universe had to begin very orderly. Nobody knows why it did. Science also has no agreed definition of time itself. What do you think makes 'before' different from 'after'?
- e.g. Shuffle a new pack of cards that is in order. It becomes mixed. Keep shuffling a mixed pack and it almost never returns to order, because the mixed orders hugely outnumber the sorted ones.
- e.g. Hot tea and cold milk make warm tea. Warm tea never sorts itself into a hot half and a cold half.
- **Ludwig Boltzmann, in 1877:** Mixing is a matter of chance and counting. Unmixing by itself is not forbidden. It is so unlikely for a tumbler of water that it would not be seen once in many lifetimes of the universe.
- **Josef Loschmidt, in 1876:** He objected that if every particle's motion were exactly turned round, the ink would gather again. So the rules alone cannot explain the one-way direction. How things start must also be part of the answer.
- **The philosopher David Albert and the physicist Roger Penrose:** They hold that the answer lies at the beginning of the universe, which was in a very special orderly state. Albert named this the past hypothesis. Why the beginning was like that is unknown. Some physicists, such as Carlo Rovelli, add that time may not be a basic part of the world at all.
### Is 'mixed' a fact about the materials, or about what a test can tell apart? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists disagree about whether two materials count as mixed because of what they are, or only because we have a test that can tell them apart.

Take two tumblers of water, one coloured red and one blue. Pour them together. They mix into purple, and you cannot easily get them back. Now take two tumblers of the same plain water and pour them together. Did mixing happen? The molecules from the two tumblers have spread among each other in just the same way. Yet no test can find any change. In the 1870s the American scientist Josiah Willard Gibbs noticed something odd in the sums. When two different gases mix, the entropy, the number that measures mixing, jumps up. The jump is the same size however small the difference between the gases. When the gases are the same, the jump is zero. So mixing seems to depend on whether a difference can be told. What do you think?
- e.g. Mix white rice from two different bags of the same kind. Nobody would call the result a mixture, unless they had a test that could tell the bags apart.
- e.g. Before the heavy kind of hydrogen was found in 1931, a jar of water counted as one substance. Afterwards the same jar could be described as a mixture.
- **The physicist Edwin Jaynes, in 'The Gibbs Paradox', 1992:** He argued that entropy is not a property of the material alone. It depends on which differences we have chosen, or are able, to keep track of. Find a new way to tell two things apart, and what was one material becomes a mixture that can be separated.
- **Many physicists who work with quantum physics:** They answer that in quantum physics two particles of the same kind are exactly alike, with no hidden label at all. So whether two gases are the same or different is a plain fact about the world, and it needs no watcher. Here 'watcher' means any test or instrument, and not a mind.
- **The Jain teaching of anekantavada, set out by Siddhasena Divakara in the Sanmati Tarka, about the 5th century CE:** This teaching holds that a thing has many sides, and each statement about it is true from one standpoint. From the standpoint of what the parts are, the two tumblers of water are one. From the standpoint of where each part came from, they are two.
### Joining and parting in old Indian thought: samyoga and vibhaga · *idea*
**Definition:** In the Vaisheshika school of Indian philosophy, samyoga means the coming together of things and vibhaga means their coming apart. The school taught that all the things we see are made and unmade by these two.

The Vaisheshika thinkers asked what the world is made of. Their answer was tiny particles, called paramanu, that cannot be cut further. On their account, the things we see are made when particles come together, and end when particles come apart. The particles themselves are not made or destroyed. That is close to what this lesson found with rice and dal: the parts stay themselves through mixing and separating. The old thinkers reached their view by reasoning. They did no experiments of the kind chemists do, and their particles are not the atoms of modern science. The old Indian thinkers also disagreed with each other about what a combined thing is. What would you ask them?
- e.g. Tradition says the first teacher of the school was called Kanada, which can mean 'grain eater'. He is said to have lived on grains gathered from the fields after harvest.
- e.g. A clay pot, in the Vaisheshika account, is a new whole that exists while its particles stay joined, and ends when they part.
- **Kanada's Vaisheshika Sutra, 1.1.6, composed between about the 6th and 2nd centuries BCE:** The sutra lists the qualities a thing can have. Among them are separateness, joining (samyoga) and parting (vibhaga), beside colour, taste and smell. So for this school, being joined to another thing is a quality, as real as being red.
- **Umasvati's Tattvartha Sutra, chapter 5 (verse 5.33 in a common numbering), a Jain text of about the 2nd to 5th century CE:** It says that particles of matter bind together because of two opposite qualities, which the text calls smooth and rough. Some modern readers compare this with positive and negative charge. That is a comparison made today, and the text does not describe electric charge.
- **The Milindapanha, Book 2, chapter 1, a Buddhist text of about the 1st century BCE to the 2nd century CE:** The monk Nagasena asks King Milinda if a chariot is the wheels, the axle or the pole. It is none of them. 'Chariot' is a name used when the parts are together. On this Buddhist view, a combined thing is only a name. The Vaisheshika school held the opposite: a whole is a new real thing over and above its parts.

## Mostly agreed
- The Earth itself is sorted by density: the heaviest material is at the centre and the lightest is on top. *How we know:* Surface rocks weigh about 3 grams for each cubic centimetre, but the whole Earth averages about 5.5. So the inside must be much denser. Earthquake waves bend and bounce at layers deep down. In 1936 the Danish scientist Inge Lehmann used such waves to show a solid inner core inside the liquid outer core. Both are mostly iron, which sank when the young Earth was molten.
- Spinning a mixture fast separates parts by density much more quickly than settling does. *How we know:* A machine called a centrifuge spins a tube of blood for a few minutes. The red cells, which are denser, collect at the bottom and take up a little under half the tube. A pale yellow liquid called plasma stays on top. Dairies separate cream from milk in the same way. Churning curd with a wooden mathu to gather butter is an older use of spinning.
- Plastic that has broken into tiny pieces is now mixed into the sea, the soil, the air and living bodies. Nobody has a practical way to take it out. *How we know:* In 2004 Richard Thompson and his team reported tiny plastic fibres and fragments in beach sand and in old plankton samples from around Britain. They named them microplastics. Since then they have been found in deep-sea mud, mountain snow, table salt and human blood. Filters can catch them in a factory pipe, but not across an ocean.
- Crude oil is separated into petrol, kerosene, diesel and tar by boiling, because each part turns to gas at a different temperature. *How we know:* In a refinery, heated oil vapour rises up a tall tower that is cooler towards the top. Each part turns back to liquid at its own height and is piped off. Refineries such as the ones at Jamnagar, Kochi and Digboi work this way. Digboi in Assam has run since 1901.
- The gases of air can be separated by cooling air until it becomes liquid. *How we know:* Air cooled to about 200 degrees below zero Celsius becomes a pale blue liquid. As it warms a little, nitrogen boils off first and oxygen later. Carl von Linde made liquid air in a machine in 1895, and his company later used the method to separate oxygen and nitrogen. The oxygen cylinders in hospitals are filled from such plants.

## How we found out
**Carl Wilhelm Scheele, Joseph Priestley, Antoine Lavoisier, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, Lord Rayleigh (John William Strutt), William Ramsay, About 1771 to 1894.** For about two thousand years, most thinkers in Greece, India and elsewhere counted air as one single element. It fitted what everyone saw. Air has no colour, no smell and no parts that anyone could pick out. In the 1770s three people broke it open. Carl Scheele in Sweden and Joseph Priestley in England each made a gas in which a candle burned brighter. In Paris, Antoine Lavoisier showed that this gas, oxygen, is about one fifth of air, and that the rest does not keep a flame alive. Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier translated the English papers for him, drew the apparatus and kept the records of the tests. So air was a mixture of two gases. For a century that seemed complete. Then in 1892 Lord Rayleigh weighed nitrogen taken from air and nitrogen made from chemicals. The nitrogen from air was heavier by about one part in every 200. He and William Ramsay hunted for the reason. In 1894 they found a third gas hiding in air, about 1 part in every 100, and named it argon. A tiny difference in weight had shown a part that nobody had looked for.  
*What people believed before:* Air is one single pure element, with no parts.

## Challenging perspectives
- **In a sea-water filter, water does not flow through tiny holes. It dissolves into the sheet and moves through it one molecule at a time.** (Harry Lonsdale, Ulrich Merten and Robert Riley in 1965, for the dissolving view. Li Wang, Menachem Elimelech and their team, in Science Advances in 2023, for the tiny-holes view.; *debated*)  
  For: The dissolving view has matched the measured flow of water and salt for nearly sixty years, and engineers have designed working plants with it.  
  Against: The 2023 team ran computer models of the sheet, atom by atom, and did new tests. They report that water moves in connected clusters through passages that open between the plastic chains, pushed by pressure, as it would through holes. Other groups are still checking this.
- **A mixture is nothing more than its parts. If you know the parts fully, you know everything about the whole.** (Many physicists and chemists hold this view, called reductionism. The physicist Philip Anderson argued against it in 'More Is Different', Science, 1972.; *debated*)  
  For: Taking things apart has explained a great deal. The saltiness of sea water, the hardness of steel and the colours of ink were each understood by finding the parts.  
  Against: Anderson pointed out that large groups of particles show behaviour, such as wetness or forming a crystal, that makes no sense for one particle. Each level needs its own ideas. Nobody has yet worked out a living cell's behaviour from a list of its molecules.
- **Crushed moringa seeds can clean drinking water as well as the chemicals used in a water works.** (Samia Al Azharia Jahn recorded the practice of Sudanese village women and tested it from the 1970s. Stephanie Velegol and Darrell Velegol's team, from 2010, studied how the seed protein works.; *debated*)  
  For: Tests show the seeds remove most of the mud and a large share of the bacteria. The tree grows across India and Africa and the seeds cost almost nothing.  
  Against: The seeds leave plant material in the water, and bacteria can grow again on the plant material within a day. The seeds do not remove dissolved poisons such as arsenic. Most water engineers say the treated water must be used the same day and is still safer after boiling.
- **Water keeps a memory of a material once dissolved in it. This is said to hold even after so much thinning with more water that none of the material is left.** (The French scientist Jacques Benveniste and his team, in Nature in 1988. The claim is used to support homeopathy.; *rejected*)  
  For: Benveniste's laboratory reported that a solution thinned until no molecule of the active material should remain still acted on blood cells.  
  Against: A team sent by the journal watched the tests repeated with the tubes secretly relabelled, so that nobody knew which was which. The effect disappeared. Later attempts by other laboratories did not find it. The groups that water molecules form last for less than a billionth of a second.

## Roots
You have taken mixtures apart with a sieve, a magnet, a filter and the Sun. Now we ask what mixing and separating tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You filtered muddy salt water, and it came out clear. To your eyes it was plain water. Then you let it dry, and salt was left on the plate. The filter paper found no second material, and evaporation found one. So 'pure' tells you which tests a thing has passed, and a new test can still find more.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call clear water, white salt or a gold bangle 'pure', as if purity were something the material has, like its weight. That label hides the fact that it only records the tests used so far. It makes each thing look single and closed, when a closer test keeps finding other things in it.  
*Evidence:* In the mud, salt and water test, the filtrate looked as clear as plain water, yet a white crust of salt was left when it dried. Air was called one element for two thousand years, until a difference of one part in every 200 in weight showed argon in it.

- *who am i:* You are a mixture that keeps changing. The water you drank this morning is part of you now, and some of the air in your lungs was outside the window a minute ago. No test finds a hard line where the world stops and you begin. So asking 'who am I?' means asking what stays when every part is being swapped.
- *what is life:* A stone lets the world mix with it as it comes. A living cell keeps sorting: this in, that out. Science has no agreed definition of life, but here is one clue from this lesson. A being stays alive for as long as they keep the mixture inside different from the mixture outside. So life looks like a way of separating that never stops, done by every being from a bacterium to a banyan.
- *what is this world:* The sea becomes cloud, the cloud becomes rain, the rain carries salt back to the sea. Nothing separated ever leaves the world. It only moves to another place in it. Every part you can pull out of a mixture was part of one whole before, and is part of one whole after.

Every method in this lesson worked because the parts were different. So separating is how we come to know differences. But each time, the difference was found and not made: the salt was salty before the plate dried. And each cleaner part turned out, on a closer test, to hold other things again. Follow that down and no part is ever found standing completely alone. Consciousness is the ground that all these parts arise from, and the many materials are that one ground showing itself in different ways. Many scientists do not accept this last step, and their view is given below.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** The teacher Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to put a lump of salt in water and bring it back in the morning. The salt cannot be found. The son sips from the top, the middle and the bottom, and every sip is salty. The teacher says that the finest essence of everything is present like that, unseen and all through, and adds: you are that. (The lesson did the same test. A dissolved solid is in every drop though no eye and no filter finds it, and only tasting or drying shows it. The sage then takes a step that science does not take: from salt in water to one essence in all beings.)
- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes meet at a hub, and the cart is useful because of the empty hole at the centre. Clay is shaped into a pot, and the pot is useful because of the empty space inside. What is there gives a thing its form. What is not there gives it its use. (A sieve and a filter do their work with their holes. The wire only holds the holes in place. The lesson's tools separate by what they leave open.)
- **A doha attributed to Kabir, the 15th-century poet of Varanasi ('Sadhu aisa chahiye, jaisa soop subhay'):** Kabir says a wise person should be like a winnowing basket, which keeps the solid grain and lets the empty husk fly away. (Kabir uses winnowing as a picture for choosing what is worth keeping. The lesson adds a question for him: the husk that flies off feeds cattle and soil, so is anything in the basket only waste?)
- Science (settled): The atoms in your body were mixed into gas and dust between the stars before the Earth formed. Nearly all of them, except hydrogen, were made inside stars. Margaret Burbidge, with Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle, showed in 1957 how stars make them.
- Science (debated): Can everything about a whole, such as a living cell or a mind, be explained from its separated parts? Some scientists say yes in principle. Others, following Philip Anderson, say each level has its own behaviour.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India, and most scientists who study the brain today: The Charvakas taught that only the material elements exist. Awareness, they said, appears when the elements are combined in a body, as the power to make someone drunk appears when certain ingredients ferment together. When the body comes apart, awareness ends. Most brain scientists today hold a similar view: awareness is what a working brain does. On this view matter is the ground, and there is no one essence behind it.
- Practice, One breath, sorted (3 min): Sit comfortably and let your hands rest. Breathe in slowly through your nose. That breath is a mixture of gases. Hold it gently and count to three. Right now your lungs are taking some oxygen out of it. Breathe out slowly. What leaves holds less oxygen and carries extra carbon dioxide. A plant near you may take that in. Do this for five slow breaths. Each time, notice that you are taking a little from the mixture and giving a little back. After the fifth breath, sit still for a moment. Ask yourself: where did the air stop and where did I begin?
- Ask: The filtered water looked pure and was not. What else around you might look like one thing only because nobody has tested it closely?
- Ask: Your body takes in air, water and food and gives them back changed. Which part of that mixture would you point to and say 'this is me'?
- Ask: The salt was in the sea before anyone separated it. Was it 'salt' then, or did separating make it salt?
- Ask: If nothing that is separated ever leaves the world, what does 'throwing something away' mean?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Sugar in tea looks gone, but the tea is still sweet. A material can seem to lose its shape, its crunch and its white colour. How many of its properties can it lose and still be the same material? (hint: List what changed and what came back when the water was evaporated. Then ask which properties you would use to recognise sugar.)
- *deep:* A sieve cannot separate two grains of exactly the same size. Suppose two things had no difference at all that any test could find. Would putting them together make a mixture? (hint: Think about pouring two tumblers of the same water together. Then think about what might be found with a better instrument a hundred years from now.)
- *big:* When you clean rice, the stones are the unwanted part. For someone building a path, the stones are the wanted part. Who decides which part of a mixture is the rubbish? (hint: Think of rice-washing water given to plants, husk fed to cattle, and what a flamingo wants from a salt pan.)
- *deep:* Ink mixes into water without any help, and it never unmixes without help. Yet a film of two single particles bumping looks right played backwards. So where does the one-way direction come from? (hint: Count how many ways the ink could be spread out and how many ways it could be in one drop. Then ask how the ink came to be in a drop to begin with.)
- *big:* Your body is a mixture of water, salts, fats, proteins and air, and the atoms in it are swapped for new ones all the time. If every material in you were separated and laid in jars on a table, where would you be? (hint: Ask what is missing from the jars that was there before. Is it a material, an arrangement, or something else?)
- *big:* Is it fairer to mix waste and pay someone to separate it later, or to keep it apart from the start? Who does the work in each case, and who gets the benefit? (hint: Think about who handles a mixed dustbin after it leaves your home, and what that work is like.)
- *deep:* Is there anything in the world that is not a mixture? If you thought you had found one, how could you ever prove it? (hint: Think about what each test can and cannot find, and whether passing every test known today is the same as being pure.)
- *big:* A child joins a new class, or a family moves to a new city. Do people keep their own properties in a group, as rice does in dal, or do they change, as milk does with lemon? (hint: Think of a habit, a taste or a joke of yours that stays the same everywhere, and one thing that is different with different people.)
- *deep:* A cell stays alive by keeping the mixture inside different from the mixture outside. Is a living being a thing that separates, or is being alive the separating itself? (hint: Think about what a kidney, a root and a cell's skin are doing every second, and what happens to a body when that work stops.)

## Story: What Everyone Took from the Sea
*A made-up story set at the salt pans near Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu. The people are imagined. What is real: sea water is led through a row of ponds as it dries, a chalky mineral called gypsum comes out of the water before the eating salt does, a bitter liquid called bittern is left at the end, and factories take other salts from it. Flamingos do feed in these salt pans on brine shrimp and algae, and a flamingo's pink colour comes from that food.*

Before sunrise the salt pans outside Thoothukudi lay grey and flat, like a hundred slates set side by side. Thenmozhi, who was nine, walked along the mud bank behind her mother, Selvi, carrying the short wooden rake. Behind her came Joseph, the neighbour's son, who was seven and had never been to the pans before.

Joseph looked all round. 'Where is the factory?' he asked. 'Where does the salt get made?'

Thenmozhi laughed. 'Nobody makes it.'

'The sea makes it,' said a voice. It was Pechiammal paatti, the oldest worker on the pans, already bent over her square of white. 'We sow sea water after Pongal, and we reap in the hot months. The salt grows, child, as paddy grows. It is a crop.'

'It does not grow, paatti,' said Thenmozhi. 'We learnt it. The salt was in the sea water all the time. The Sun only takes the water away.'

Paatti did not stop raking. 'Then tell me this. The same sea water stands in that big pond over there. Why is there no salt in it? Why does it come only here, in my pan?'

Thenmozhi opened her mouth and found she did not know.

Selvi answered for neither of them. She led the children to the first pond, wide and deep to the ankle. 'Taste.' It tasted like the sea. 'Here the water can still hold all its salt. Every day the Sun takes a little water. The salt stays, so the same salt is crowded into less and less water.' She walked them along the bank to a middle pond and scraped the floor with her heel. A grey-white crust came up. 'This comes out first. It is not for eating. A man from the plaster works buys it. It lets go of the water before the salt does.' Then she brought them back to paatti's pan, where crystals lay like broken glass. 'Only here is the water so crowded that it cannot hold the eating salt any longer. So here the salt shows itself.'

'And after?' asked Joseph. A little brown water was left in the corner of the pan. He dipped a finger and licked it, and his whole face folded. It was bitter.

'That we drain away,' said Selvi. 'A truck comes for it some months. A factory takes other salts out of it.'

Out in the middle pond stood a line of flamingos, heads upside down in the water, swinging slowly from side to side.

'What are they taking?' Joseph asked.

'Tiny shrimp, and green specks smaller than the shrimp,' said Selvi. 'Their beak is a comb. The water goes out through the comb and the food stays in.'

'Do they want the salt?'

'They cannot stand the salt. They push it out again. To them the salt is the rubbish.'

Joseph counted on his fingers. 'The Sun took the water. The plaster man takes the grey crust. You take the salt. The factory takes the bitter water. The flamingos take the shrimp.' He looked up. 'So what is left that nobody wants?'

Nobody could name anything.

Thenmozhi was quiet. At school the word had been 'impurities': the part you throw away. But here each one who came to the pond called a different part the prize, and called the rest waste. The pond did not seem to have an opinion.

'So who is right, kanna?' said paatti, straightening her back at last. 'Did the sea give me a crop? Or was your salt sitting there all the time?'

Thenmozhi thought about it properly. The salt had been in the sea. That was true. But in the sea it had no whiteness, no edges and no crunch. It had only a taste. And without the ponds, the waiting, and paatti carrying it on her head, not one crystal would ever have reached a plate.

'I think both,' she said slowly. 'It was there. And it was not salt you could hold until you did this.'

Paatti made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

The Sun came over the edge of the pans. The flamingos lifted all at once, and their wings opened pink against the grey. Selvi had once said that the pink came from their food. So the pink had been in the pond too, spread through the water in specks too small to see, until a bird gathered enough of the colour to show.

Thenmozhi looked down at the dull water by her feet. She wondered what else was in there that nobody had found a way to gather yet.

**For the parent:** A separation adds nothing. It only lets one part leave, by a difference that part already had. Which part is the prize and which is the waste is decided by whoever is doing the separating.

- Ask: Paatti says the salt is a crop that grows. Thenmozhi says it was in the sea all the time. What is true in each way of saying it?
- Ask: The flamingos treat salt as rubbish and the salt workers treat it as the prize. Can you think of a mixture at home where your rubbish is someone else's prize?
- Ask: The salt in the sea had a taste but no shape. Was it already salt?
- Ask: What do you think is in a glass of your tap water that nobody has looked for yet? How would you look?

## Experiments
### e1 · The mud, salt and water test (33 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Muddy salt water is filtered until it looks clear. Is there still salt in the clear water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess two things. Will the water look clear after filtering? Will anything be left when a spoonful dries? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Two plates are dried side by side in the same place. Plate A holds the filtered water. Plate B holds plain tap water from the same tap, in the same amount. Only the thing on the plate is different. Plate B shows how much a plain spoonful of your tap water leaves, so you do not blame the salt for what the tap water leaves.
- **safety:** Do not taste the muddy water, the filtered water or the crust. Soil carries germs, and filtering does not remove all of them. Wash your hands afterwards and put the leftover mud in the dustbin or on bare ground outside. It is salty, so keep it away from plants, and keep it out of the kitchen sink where it blocks the pipe.
- **record:** Fill in the three columns of the table for the four liquids. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Liquid | Clear or cloudy? | What is left when a spoonful dries
- **schedule:** day 1: Mix the mud and salt, let it settle, filter it until clear and put two plates in the sun. (28 min); day 2: Look at both plates. If plate A is still wet, check it again the next day. (5 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put two clear tumblers on the table. Fill the first with about one cup of tap water, which is a small steel tumbler three-quarters full. Stick a paper label on it and write A. Fill the second with the same amount of tap water and label it B. Put tumbler B to one side for later.  
  *Parent says:* Tumbler B is our plain water, so we have something to compare with. What do you think we will do with the other one?  
  *Child mode:* Fill two tumblers with the same amount of water. Label one A and one B. Put B to one side.
  - Note: Use the same tap for both tumblers. The plain tumbler B will later show what ordinary tap water looks like and how much it leaves on a plate.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the prediction and the reason before mixing, and write both down. Then stir 2 level tablespoons of fine soil and 2 level tablespoons of salt into tumbler A. Stir for a full minute until the salt has gone and the water is brown. Do not taste it.  
  *Parent says:* We are making muddy salt water. After we filter it, do you think the water will be clear? Will anything of the salt come back when we dry it? What makes you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think and why. Then stir the soil and salt into tumbler A for one minute. Do not taste it.
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. The test will answer it. If a few salt grains stay at the bottom, stir for another minute. Two level tablespoons of salt dissolve in this much water at room temperature.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave tumbler A still for 5 minutes. Look at the layers. Then pour the top liquid slowly into the steel bowl, stopping before the mud at the bottom moves. Scrape the thick mud at the bottom into the dustbin, or onto bare ground outside, and rinse the tumbler. The mud is salty, so do not put it on a plant.  
  *Parent says:* What has sunk? Is the water on top clear now? You have just done settling and pouring off.  
  *Child mode:* Wait 5 minutes. Then pour the top liquid into a bowl and keep the mud behind.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the water on top, after the mud has settled? (good: It is paler, but still cloudy.; The big bits sank and the finest bits are still floating.)
  - Note: The top liquid will still be brown and cloudy. The finest bits of soil sink slowest, which is why settling alone is not enough. Use a bowl you can rinse well afterwards.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fold a coffee filter into a cone and rest it in a tea strainer over a clean tumbler. Pour the cloudy water from the bowl into the cone, a little at a time. Catch the drips. If the liquid is still cloudy, pour it back through the same paper. Do this up to three times. If the paper clogs and nothing drips, use a fresh paper.  
  *Parent says:* The paper has holes too small for the mud to pass. What do you think comes through, and what stays on the paper? Look at the colour of what is dripping.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the cloudy water slowly through the filter paper. Pour it back through again until it looks clear.
  - Ask: What is left on the paper, and what came through? (good: The mud stayed on the paper.; Water, and whatever dissolved in it, came through.)
  - Note: A coffee filter drips slowly, so this takes 5 to 10 minutes. For a faster start, pour first through a clean folded handkerchief and then through the paper. If you have no strainer, fix the paper over the tumbler's mouth with a rubber band and let it sag a little. The clear liquid that comes through is the filtrate, and the mud left on the paper is the residue.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the filtered water beside tumbler B on a sheet of white paper. Look at both from the side and from above. Hold them up to the window. Fill in the 'Clear or cloudy?' column for each liquid.  
  *Parent says:* Can you tell which tumbler holds the filtered water and which holds the plain water? Could we tell if there is salt in the filtered one, just by looking?  
  *Child mode:* Put the two tumblers side by side. Look hard. Can you tell which is which?
  - Note: Do not taste either tumbler. The filtered water may look very slightly yellow or tea-coloured. That colour is something dissolved from the soil that went through the paper, and it is a result too. If it is still cloudy, filter it one more time with fresh paper.
  - Check: The filtered water looks as clear as the plain water. What does that tell you? → **The mud pieces are held back**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 2 tablespoons of the filtered water on one steel plate. Pour 2 tablespoons of plain water from tumbler B on the other plate. Tilt each plate so the liquid spreads in a thin film. Mark the plates A and B with paper labels. Put both on a sunny windowsill or in a warm breezy spot, lying level.  
  *Parent says:* Plate A holds the filtered water. Plate B holds plain tap water. What do you think we will find on each plate when the water has gone?  
  *Child mode:* Spread two spoonfuls of filtered water on plate A. Spread plain water on plate B. Put both in the sun.
  - Note: Two tablespoons spread thin on a wide plate usually dry in one day in sun. On a humid, cloudy day they take two or three days. If the plates are still wet after three days, put them in front of a table fan on a low setting. Keep both plates in the same place.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Next day, look at both plates. Look at them with a magnifying glass. Plate A should show a white crust or a ring of tiny white crystals. Plate B should show nothing, or only a faint ghost ring. Write three sentences: what I think, what I saw and why. Do not taste the crust.  
  *Parent says:* What is on plate A? What is on plate B? What does the difference tell you about the filtered water?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both plates with a magnifying glass. Write what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: If your tap water is hard, plate B leaves a faint ring of dissolved minerals. Plate A should leave far more, because the filtered water also holds the added salt. This test cannot tell us if the crust is only salt. Some soil colour may also have dried on the plate. It also cannot tell what else is in the plain water, and plate B shows only what a spoon of it leaves.
  - Think: The filter paper found no second material in the water, and drying found one. What does that say about the word 'clear'? What else would you test?
### e2 · The black pen test: is black one colour? (30 min)
- **question:** A black sketch pen makes one black line. Is the black ink a single colour, or does it hold other colours?
- **predict:** Guess what will happen to the black dot when water climbs over it. Will it stay black or change? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The paper strips are the same paper, the same width and the same length. Each dot is made near the bottom, the same size and the same height above the water. Each strip is left in water until the water reaches the same line near the top. Only the pen is different.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Stick the dried strips on a page and write a label under each.
- **results table:** Pen | Colours I saw, bottom to top | Did it split? Yes or no
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Tear or cut four paper strips about 2 centimetres wide and 12 centimetres long. If you use scissors, an adult should hold them. Draw a pencil line across each strip 2 centimetres from one end. Write the name of a pen in pencil at the top of each strip.  
  *Parent says:* Each strip will test a different pen. Why do we draw the line in pencil and not in ink?  
  *Child mode:* Make four strips. Draw a pencil line near the bottom of each. Write a pen's name at the top of each.
  - Note: Pencil is not carried by water, but pen ink might be. The strip names are written at the top so they stay dry. The adult should cut the strips if the child cannot yet use scissors safely.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the prediction and the reason, and write both down. Then make a thick round dot of about 5 millimetres on the pencil line of each strip, one pen to a strip. Press and go over the dot twice so it is dark. Let the dots dry for a minute.  
  *Parent says:* I will make a black dot with each pen. Do you think the black will stay black when water goes over it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think and why. Then make a thick dark dot on each strip with a different pen.
  - Note: A small thin dot gives pale, hard-to-see bands. A big dark dot gives clear bands. Use a washable sketch pen in black, and a green or brown water-based pen as a second choice. Include the ballpoint and the permanent marker if you have them, because they may not move at all.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour water about one finger-width deep into the tumbler. Lay a pencil across the top. Hang the strips from the pencil with tape. The bottom edges should just touch the water, and the dots should stay above it. Check that the dots are not under the water.  
  *Parent says:* The water must touch the paper below the dot. What do you think will happen when the water reaches the dots?  
  *Child mode:* Pour a little water in the tumbler. Hang the strips so only their bottom edges are in the water. The dots must stay dry.
  - Note: If the dot goes under the water, the ink washes into the tumbler and nothing climbs. Start again with fresh strips. If the strips curl and touch, put them in separate tumblers.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wait and watch. The water moves up the paper slowly and passes through each dot. When the water is a thumb-width from the top of a strip, lift it out. Mark where the water stopped with a pencil. Lay the strips on newspaper to dry. Write the colours you see in the table, from the bottom up.  
  *Parent says:* What is the water doing to the black dot? Do all the dots do the same thing? What is happening to the colours?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the water move up the paper. Take each strip out when the water is a thumb-width from the top. Write the colours you see.
  - Ask: What did the black pen do, and did the other pens do the same? (good: Some black pens split into several colours.; The ballpoint and permanent marker stayed where they were.)
  - Note: On a coffee filter the water climbs about 8 to 10 centimetres in 10 minutes. Many washable black pens split into blue, purple, pink or yellow bands. Some black pens stay one dark band, and that is a result too. If your black does not split, try the green or brown pen, which nearly always does.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Dry the strips and look at them. Write which pens split and which did not. Stick the strips onto a page. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw and why. Add one sentence on what this test cannot tell you.  
  *Parent says:* Which pens held more than one colour? Why would some ink travel higher than other ink in the same water?

### e3 · Sand, soil and salt in water: which one leaves first? (31 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Sand, soil and salt are each stirred into water. Which one leaves the water clear first, and which never leaves?
- **predict:** Guess the order in which the three tumblers will clear. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The three tumblers hold the same amount of water, the same level tablespoon of each solid and are stirred the same number of times. They are left in the same place. Only the solid is different. The test looks at the same times for all three tumblers.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each time. Draw the three tumblers as they look next morning.
- **results table:** Solid | After 30 seconds | After 5 minutes | After 15 minutes | Next morning
- **schedule:** day 1: Stir the three solids into water, then look at fixed times up to 15 minutes. (26 min); day 2: Look at the tumblers next morning and pour off the top of the soil tumbler. (5 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set three clear tumblers in a row on white paper. Pour about 200 millilitres of tap water into each. Label them sand, soil and salt. Wash the sand in a bowl first. Swirl it with water and pour off the cloudy water twice, so that it holds less dust.  
  *Parent says:* We will put three different solids in three tumblers of the same water. What do you think we will see in each one?  
  *Child mode:* Fill three tumblers with the same amount of water. Label them sand, soil and salt.
  - Note: Washing the sand removes most of its dust, so the test shows the sand itself. Use the same tap and the same amount of water in each tumbler.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the prediction and the reason, and write both down. Ask which tumbler will clear first, which will clear last and which will never settle. Do not correct the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Which tumbler will clear first? Which will take the longest? What makes you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Say which tumbler will clear first and which will last. Say why you think so.
  - Note: Write the guess in a corner of the page, so the child can look back at it later.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 1 level tablespoon of each solid into its own tumbler. Stir each tumbler 10 times with a clean spoon. Start the timer as soon as you put the spoon down.  
  *Parent says:* Stir the same number of times each time. Why do we stir each one the same number of times?  
  *Child mode:* Add a spoonful to each tumbler. Stir ten times. Start the timer.
  - Note: Rinse the spoon between tumblers, or use three spoons. One tablespoon of salt dissolves fully in 200 millilitres of water, and the salt tumbler should be clear in under a minute.
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the tumblers after 30 seconds, 5 minutes and 15 minutes, and write what you see each time. Look from the side, against the white paper. Note the layers, the cloudiness and anything on the bottom. Look at the salt tumbler closely.  
  *Parent says:* Which tumbler clears first? What is in the bottom of each one? Is the salt tumbler clear because the salt has gone?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the tumblers at 30 seconds, 5 minutes and 15 minutes. Write what you see each time.
  - Ask: Why does the salt tumbler look clear, if the salt is still there? (good: The salt has dissolved into pieces too small to see.; The salt is still in the water, and you could taste it.)
  - Note: The sand usually sinks in under a minute and leaves clear water. Some fine dust may take a few minutes. The soil tumbler is still cloudy at 15 minutes, because the finest bits sink very slowly. The salt tumbler looks clear within a minute, and it is clear because the salt is dissolved. Do not taste the sand or soil tumblers. Tasting the salt tumbler is fine if you used clean drinking water and a clean tumbler.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The next morning, look at the tumblers again. Draw the three of them. Pour the top of the soil tumbler slowly into a bowl, leaving the mud behind. Write three sentences: what I think, what I saw and why. Add one sentence on how sinking speed and dissolving differ.

  - Note: The soil water may be clearer at the top but still slightly cloudy. Clay bits are among the smallest and sink slowest. The sand will be a clean layer, and the salt water stays clear. This test cannot tell us how long the soil takes to clear completely. A filter holds back sand and soil, and cannot take out the dissolved salt.
  - Think: Sand sinks quickly, soil sinks slowly and salt does not sink at all. What does this tell you about the pieces of each?

### a3 · Sieve, winnow and pour: which tool for which mess? (30 min)
- **record:** Draw a table with the three mixtures. Write the tool you used, the difference it used and how well it worked.
- **a3s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Spread a large tray or sheet on the floor. Mix 1 cup of rice with a handful of small paper bits on a plate. Put a table fan on its lowest speed on a chair beside the tray, or stand at a window with a breeze. Check that the fan is steady.

  *Parent does:* Hold the plate at shoulder height beside the fan. Tip the mixture slowly in a thin stream so that it falls past the fan onto the tray. Look at where the rice falls and where the paper bits land. Do it again with what is left, until there is no paper in the rice.  
  *Parent says:* Which falls straight down, and which blows away? Why would a farmer use this at harvest?  
  *Child mode:* Pour the mixture slowly past the fan. Look at where the rice and the paper land.

  *Parent does:* Put the rice in a bowl and cover it with water. Swirl it for 10 seconds and wait 10 seconds. Look at the water. Pour the cloudy top slowly into another bowl, keeping the rice back. Look at the water that you poured away and at the rice.

  *Child mode:* Swirl the rice in water and wait. Pour off the cloudy top and keep the rice.

  - Note: The colander's holes are far bigger than the atta pieces, so everything falls through and nothing is separated. A tool works only if it uses a difference the parts really have.
  - Think: Every tool uses one difference. What would you do for two things with no difference at all, such as two grains of the same size, colour and weight?

  *Child mode:* Think of where else water comes out of the air. Say what you notice.

  - Think: Fog on a plate, dew on a leaf and rain from a cloud all catch water out of air. What do they have in common?

### ch1 · Four things in one bowl: plan the steps and get them all back (40 min)
- **problem:** Steel pins, chana, clean fine sand and salt are mixed in a dry bowl. Plan and carry out a way to get each one back on its own plate, with as few steps as you can. There is more than one good answer.
- **your turn:** Make a new mixture of four things from your kitchen, plan how to separate it, and try it. Which of your steps used a difference you had not thought of at first? Is there a mixture that you could not separate at all?
- **constraints:** Use only things you have at home. Do not taste anything.; Every part must be returned, and the salt must come back as dry salt, which takes a day on a plate in the sun.; Write the plan before you start, and say which difference each step uses.; Try the plan once, and then change one step to see if it gets better.
- **general:** Dry first: pass a magnet through the bowl to take out the pins. Then sieve to take out the chana. Then add water, stir, filter out the sand and dry the salt water on a plate.; Pick the pins and the chana by hand, which is slow but needs no tools. Then use water, a filter and evaporation for the sand and salt.; Use the sieve first, then the magnet. Both orders work, because the pins and the chana are different in different ways.
- **creative:** Dissolve the salt first by pouring water through the whole bowl in a cloth bag. Catch the salt water in a plate below, and the pins, chana and sand stay in the cloth. Then use a magnet and a sieve on the dry cloth contents.; Rinse the sand over the filter with a little extra water, so no salt stays on the sand. Add the rinse water to the plate you dry in the sun.; Pass the magnet through twice, once before the sieve and once after, in case a pin hid under a chana.
- **ch1s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write each of the four parts on paper, and next to each, write one way it is different from the other three. Use words like size, being pulled by a magnet and dissolving. Do not start yet.  
  *Parent says:* Four things are in the bowl. For each, what is it that the other three do not do?  
  *Child mode:* List the four parts. For each one, write one difference from the others.
  - Ask: What difference does each part have? (good: Steel is attracted by a magnet.; Chana is much bigger than sand and salt.; Salt dissolves and sand does not.)
  - Note: Steel is the only magnetic one. Chana is the biggest. Salt is the only one that dissolves. Sand is the one left over, which is also a difference.
- **ch1s2 (8 min)**

  *Parent says:* Which step comes first? Why does the order matter? Could two different orders both work?  
  *Child mode:* Draw boxes and arrows for your plan. Say why each step is in its place.
  - Note: A good plan does the dry steps before any water is added. Both a magnet first and a sieve first work, since the pins are not hidden by the chana. The order that fails is adding water first, because then every dry method stops working.
- **ch1s3 (15 min)**

  *Child mode:* Follow your plan. Put each part on its own plate.
  - Note: A usual order is a magnet for the pins and a sieve for the chana. Then water dissolves the salt, a filter catches the sand and evaporation gets the salt.   *Child mode:* Pick one step to change. Say why it would help.

### ch2 · Clear the muddy water: build a filter and test how clear it gets (40 min)
- **problem:** Muddy water must be made as clear as you can with a filter you build, using only home things. Measure how clear it gets with a black cross, and improve your filter. There is more than one good answer.
- **your turn:** Design your own filter from things at home and test it with the cross. What did you change that made the water clearer? What would you need to test to know the water is safe?
- **constraints:** Do not drink the water. Clear water can still carry germs.; Use only things you can find at home, and keep every layer clean.; Test the water with the cross before and after, and use the same amount of muddy water each time.; Change only one thing in the filter between tests.
- **general:** Layers of cloth, sand and small pebbles inside a cut bottle, with the coarse layer at the top and the finest at the bottom.; Let the water settle for an hour, pour off the top with care and then filter only the top part.; Use a coffee filter paper in a tea strainer, which has the smallest holes, and pour in a little at a time.
- **creative:** Fold a clean cotton cloth four times and pour water through it, as the village women in Bangladesh were taught.; Use two filters in a row.   *Child mode:* Pour muddy water slowly until the cross disappears. Measure the depth.

- **ch2s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* A filter works by letting water through and holding the mud back. What do you think should go in first at the bottom, and what at the top?

  - Note: An adult should cut the plastic, because the edge can be sharp. Make sure the bottle neck points down into the tumbler.
- **ch2s3 (12 min)**

  *Parent says:* What is happening to the mud as the water goes down? Is the water that comes out clear?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bottle in layers. Pour muddy water in slowly. Catch what comes out.
  - Note: The first water that comes through may be cloudy, because the sand still holds dust. Pour the first batch back through, or wash the sand first. A filter may take 5 to 10 minutes.
- **ch2s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the filtered water with the cross, and measure the depth when the cross disappears. Compare with your first measurement. Write both and the difference.  
  *Parent says:* How much deeper did the water need to be? Is that clear enough? Is it safe to drink?  
  *Child mode:* Test the filtered water with the cross. Measure the depth. Compare with before.
  - Note: A deeper measurement means clearer water. Even when the cross shows clearly, the water must not be drunk. The holes in sand and cloth are far bigger than germs. It must be boiled or treated first. Pour the water on plants when you finish.

## Worksheets
### Mixtures and the difference that separates them (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which one is a mixture of two materials?  
   Options: One small stone / A cup of rice with a few small stones / A cup of only rice  
   **Answer:** A cup of rice with a few small stones. *Why:* Rice and stones are two materials, each still itself. A single stone or a cup of one grain is one material. If you picked another, you looked for two different materials together.
2. (truefalse) Salt stirred into water has stopped being salt, because you cannot see it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The water tastes salty and evaporation brings the salt back. The pieces are too small to see, but they are still salt.
3. (fill) Iron pins mixed into sand are still attracted by a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['magnet']. *Why:* Iron keeps its own property of being attracted, even when it is mixed with sand. The sand does not change it.
4. (mcq) A sieve separates the parts of a mixture by a difference in what?  
   Options: Colour / Size / Taste  
   **Answer:** Size. *Why:* A sieve lets pieces smaller than its holes through and holds back bigger ones. Colour and taste play no part, so if you picked them you were thinking of picking by eye or tongue.
5. (sort) Sort each mixture by the method that fits best.  
   **Answer:** steel pins in rice → Magnet; stones bigger than rice grains, in rice → Sieve; salt dissolved in water, when you want the salt → Evaporation; soil mud floating in water → Filter; bran flakes in atta → Sieve. *Why:* Each method uses one difference. A magnet uses being attracted and a sieve uses size. Evaporation leaves a dissolved solid behind, and a filter holds back a solid that has not dissolved.
6. (match) Match each tool to the difference it uses.  
   **Answer:** a sieve → the size of the pieces; a magnet → being attracted; a winnowing tray in the breeze → how moving air carries pieces; a coffee filter → a solid that has not dissolved. *Why:* A tool works because the parts of the mixture differ in the one way it uses. Each tool treats the parts differently for just one reason.
7. (order) Put these steps in order to get salt out of muddy salt water.  
   **Answer:** ['Stir the mud and salt into water', 'Pour the muddy water through filter paper', 'Leave the clear water on a plate in the sun', 'Look at the white crust left on the plate']. *Why:* The filter takes out the mud first, because the mud would spoil the crust. Evaporation comes last, because the water must leave for the dissolved salt to be left.
8. (mcq) You pour tea through a strainer. What stays behind in the strainer?  
   Options: The filtrate / The water vapour / The residue  
   **Answer:** The residue. *Why:* The solid that stays behind is the residue. The tea that passes through is the filtrate. If you chose the filtrate, you named the wrong part.
9. (write) Rice and dal are mixed in a cup. Explain why this is a mixture and not a new material.  
   **Answer:** Each stays itself. A grain of rice is still rice and a grain of dal is still dal. You can pick them apart again and get the same rice and the same dal. Nothing new was made.. *Why:* A strong answer says that each material keeps its properties and can be taken out again unchanged.
10. (mcq) Iron pins, sand and salt are all dry. What is the best first step?  
   Options: Pass a magnet through them / Stir them into water / Heat all of it on a stove  
   **Answer:** Pass a magnet through them. *Why:* A magnet works best on dry pieces. It takes out the pins cleanly while the sand and salt are loose. Adding water first would dissolve the salt and make wet sand cling to the pins. Heating would not separate anything.
11. (truefalse) Evaporation takes the salt out of salt water and leaves the water behind.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Evaporation takes the water away as vapour and leaves the dissolved salt behind.
12. (draw) Draw boxes and arrows showing how to separate steel pins, rice and salt. Write the method in each box.  
   **Answer:** Four boxes in order. A magnet takes the pins out of the dry mix. Water and stirring dissolve the salt. A cloth holds the rice back. Evaporation leaves the salt.. *Why:* A good plan does the dry method first and uses a different difference for each step.
13. (mcq) Your lungs take oxygen from the air you breathe in. What is that job?  
   Options: Separating a mixture / Making new oxygen / Mixing air  
   **Answer:** Separating a mixture. *Why:* Air is a mixture of gases. Lungs take some oxygen out and leave the rest. Lungs do not make oxygen and do not mix the air.
14. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Milk with lemon, salt in water, sand in water, pins in rice.  
   **Answer:** Milk with lemon, because a new material is made and the milk cannot come back. The other three are mixtures whose parts can be taken out again.. *Why:* A mixture keeps its parts. When materials join to form a new material, a chemical change has happened.
15. (perspective) Is a glass of clear tap water pure?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer sees that both are useful for different jobs. Safe is what matters for drinking, and one substance only is what matters in a chemistry test.. *Why:* Both meanings are true in their own place. Words like pure depend on which test or purpose is in mind.
16. (write) A friend says 'clear water is just water'. What could you do to find out if it holds anything else?  
   **Answer:** Dry a spoonful on a clean plate and look for a ring or crust left. Compare with a second plate of water that is known to be clean. Taste is not safe for every water.. *Why:* A strong answer uses a test that can find a dissolved solid, such as evaporation, since the eye cannot see it.
### Choosing, testing and explaining (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A sieve has 3 millimetre holes. Which one is stopped by it?  
   Options: A bean 8 millimetres wide / A grain 2 millimetres wide / Flour dust  
   **Answer:** A bean 8 millimetres wide. *Why:* Only pieces bigger than the holes stay on top. The bean is bigger than 3 millimetres and the grain and dust are smaller.
2. (mcq) Which of these can a magnet NOT separate from sand?  
   Options: Iron filings / Steel pins / Salt grains  
   **Answer:** Salt grains. *Why:* A magnet attracts iron and steel and does not attract salt, so it cannot take salt out of sand. If you picked a metal, remember that metals like iron are attracted.
3. (truefalse) If filtered water looks clear, it is safe to drink.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The holes in cloth and paper are bigger than germs, and dissolved things pass straight through. Clear only means that no solid pieces big enough to see are left.
4. (mcq) Lemon juice is stirred into hot milk and the milk curdles. Why is this not a separation?  
   Options: A new material formed / The lemon dissolved / The milk evaporated  
   **Answer:** A new material formed. *Why:* Curdling is a chemical change, so the parts cannot be taken out as they were. If you picked dissolving, remember that dissolved things can be recovered by evaporation.
5. (sort) Sort each separation by the difference it uses.  
   **Answer:** atta sieve holding back bran → Size; scrap dealer lifting iron with a big magnet → Attracted by a magnet; adding water so salt dissolves and sand does not → Dissolving; rice sinking while dust stays cloudy in the wash water → How fast it sinks. *Why:* Each tool or step uses one difference. Naming the difference tells you whether it can work on a new mixture.
6. (order) Put these in order of hole size, smallest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Laboratory filter paper', 'Cotton cloth', 'Atta sieve', 'Kitchen colander']. *Why:* Filter paper holes are about one hundredth of a millimetre. Cloth threads leave gaps of about a tenth of a millimetre. An atta sieve has holes under 1 millimetre and a colander has holes of a few millimetres.
7. (match) Match each one to the separating it does.  
   **Answer:** A flamingo's beak → Takes small animals and algae from muddy water; A kidney → Takes waste out of blood; A mangrove root → Stops most salt from sea water; Rain → Leaves salt behind in the sea. *Why:* Living beings and the Earth use the same kinds of differences that people use, such as size and which materials pass through.
8. (write) A mixture holds iron nails, sugar and sand. Plan the steps and say what difference each one uses.  
   **Answer:** Magnet first, which uses being attracted, to take out the nails. Then add water, which uses dissolving, so the sugar dissolves and the sand does not. Then filter, which holds back the sand. Then evaporate the water to get the sugar.. *Why:* A good plan does dry methods first and names one difference for each step.
9. (mcq) Why wait before pouring off the clear water from muddy water?  
   Options: Mud turns into water / Mud sinks in time and the top clears / Mud dissolves slowly  
   **Answer:** Mud sinks in time and the top clears. *Why:* Heavier pieces sink if you wait, so the top becomes clearer. Mud does not dissolve or become water, so do not expect it to vanish.
10. (draw) Draw the Sun, the sea and a cloud. Show where the salt stays when rain forms.  
   **Answer:** The Sun shines on the sea. Arrows rise from the sea to the cloud for water vapour. Salt is shown staying in the sea, with none in the cloud.. *Why:* Evaporation lifts water but not salt, so rain from sea water is fresh.
11. (perspective) Should household waste be mixed and sorted later, or kept apart at home?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says who does the work and who is paid in each view, and notes that a plant needs workers too.. *Why:* Both views have trade-offs. Mixing is easy for the person who throws, and separating always costs work for someone.
12. (truefalse) Air is one material, because we cannot see its parts.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Air is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide and other gases. Parts too small to see can still be there.
13. (mcq) A test finds nothing else in a water sample. What can the scientist say?  
   Options: It passed the test she used / It is pure forever / All water is pure  
   **Answer:** It passed the test she used. *Why:* A test only finds what it can catch. A new test may still find more, so 'pure' tells you which tests the sample has passed.
14. (fill) Winnowing works because moving air carries ____ pieces farther than heavy ones.  
   **Answer:** ['light']. *Why:* Husk is light and grain is heavy, so a breeze blows the husk farther while the grain falls in a heap.
15. (write) Sugar stirred into tea looks gone. Which of its properties stayed, and which seem lost?  
   **Answer:** The sweet taste stayed. The white colour, the crunch and the shape seem lost, because the pieces are too small to see. The sugar is still there and can be got back by evaporation.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the sugar keeps its properties, even if some are hidden at the scale of the eye.
16. (mcq) A river slows down. Which pieces does it drop first?  
   Options: The finest clay / The dissolved salt / The biggest, heaviest stones  
   **Answer:** The biggest, heaviest stones. *Why:* Heavy pieces sink first as the water slows. Fine clay sinks last and dissolved salt does not sink at all.
### Faulty sums and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Sand and sugar are left. Water is added and stirred. What next?

7. (order) Put these in order to clean rice with stones, husk and fine dust.

8. (truefalse) A 22-carat gold bangle is a mixture.

9. (mcq) Argon was found when nitrogen from air weighed a bit more than nitrogen made in a lab. What does this show?  
   Options: Air is one element / A small difference can reveal a hidden part / Nitrogen changes weight in air  
   **Answer:** A small difference can reveal a hidden part. *Why:* The air sample held a heavier gas mixed in, which turned out to be argon. A careful test finds what an earlier one missed.
10. (perspective) Is mixing a fact about materials, or about what our tests can tell apart?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary, and scientists still disagree. A strong answer gives a reason from the two tumbler example.. *Why:* Neither view is marked right, because the question is still debated.
11. (write) Name one way a living being separates a mixture, and say what difference it uses.  
   **Answer:** A kidney filters blood and keeps the useful materials while letting waste go, using the size and kind of each material. A flamingo's beak strains small animals out of muddy water, using size.. *Why:* A strong answer names the being, the mixture and the difference.
12. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Sieving, winnowing, evaporating, picking.

13. (truefalse) A magnet can lift every metal out of a mixture.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A magnet attracts iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Aluminium, copper and gold are not pulled.

   **Answer:** You cannot get the smooth milk back. *Why:* A mixture can be taken apart and give back each part unchanged. Colour and taste do not tell you that.
3. (mcq) A magnet lifts steel pins out of sand. Why did the sand not change the pins?  
   Options: The sand is also magnetic / Pins are lighter than sand / Materials keep their properties in a mixture  
   **Answer:** Materials keep their properties in a mixture. *Why:* The pins are still attracted, because mixing does not change what a material is. Sand is not magnetic, and weight is not what the magnet uses.
4. (mcq) Salt water freezes at a lower temperature than plain water. What does this show?  
   Options: Salt melts water into ice / Salt turns into water / A mixture can have a property its parts lack alone  
   **Answer:** A mixture can have a property its parts lack alone. *Why:* The whole mixture can have a new property, even though each part keeps its own. Freezing point is one of them.
5. (mcq) Which difference is easiest to use to separate chalk powder from iron filings?  
   Options: Colour / Taste / Being attracted by a magnet  
   **Answer:** Being attracted by a magnet. *Why:* Iron is attracted by a magnet and chalk is not. The magnet leaves the chalk behind. Colour and taste are poor tools here.
6. (mcq) A filter clears muddy water, but the water is still salty. Why?  
   Options: Dissolved salt is too small to be held back / The filter added salt / Mud makes water salty  
   **Answer:** Dissolved salt is too small to be held back. *Why:* Dissolved salt is in pieces far smaller than the filter's holes, so it passes through with the water.
7. (mcq) You dry a spoon of salt water on a plate. Why also dry a spoon of plain tap water?  
   Options: To see what the tap water alone leaves / To make the salt water dry faster / To dilute the salt  
   **Answer:** To see what the tap water alone leaves. *Why:* The tap water may leave a faint ring, and the second plate shows how much. Then you do not give the salt credit for it.
8. (mcq) Which order works for gravel, sugar and iron nails?  
   Options: Magnet, then water and filter, then evaporate / Evaporate, then magnet, then filter / Filter, then water, then magnet  
   **Answer:** Magnet, then water and filter, then evaporate. *Why:* The dry magnet comes first. Then water dissolves the sugar, so gravel is caught by the filter, and evaporation gives the sugar back.
9. (mcq) Why does mixed rubbish cost a waste picker more work than rubbish kept apart?  
   Options: Mixed waste unmixes itself / Separating needs no effort / Mixing is easy but separating always needs work  
   **Answer:** Mixing is easy but separating always needs work. *Why:* Mixing happens by itself, but taking things apart needs effort from a hand, a machine or the Sun.
10. (mcq) Sweat dries on a shirt and leaves a white mark. What does this show?  
   Options: Water changed into salt / The cloth made salt / Salt stayed when the water evaporated  
   **Answer:** Salt stayed when the water evaporated. *Why:* The water left as vapour and the salt could not follow, the same as in a salt pan.
11. (mcq) Bees make honey from nectar that is more than half water. Which separation is that?  
   Options: Evaporation of water / A magnet / Sieving  
   **Answer:** Evaporation of water. *Why:* The water leaves the nectar, and the sugars stay behind, so the honey is thick.
12. (mcq) A bottle says '99.9 per cent pure'. Why does it not say 100?  
   Options: The bottle is old / 100 is not a number / No test can show that nothing else is there  
   **Answer:** No test can show that nothing else is there. *Why:* A test only shows what it can find. A finer test may find more, so purity is given as a number.
13. (mcq) Which question about mixing has no agreed answer yet?  
   Options: Whether salt dissolves in water / Why the universe began in such an orderly state / Whether sand sinks in water  
   **Answer:** Why the universe began in such an orderly state. *Why:* Physicists can explain why mixing is likely, but not yet why the universe began so orderly.
14. (mcq) Black ink splits into blue, pink and yellow on wet paper. What made the colours spread apart?  
   Options: They dissolve at different times / They climb the paper at different speeds / They sink at different speeds  
   **Answer:** They climb the paper at different speeds. *Why:* Each colour is carried along by the water and clings to the paper by a different amount, so it travels at its own speed.

## Applications
- Salt pans at Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu and in the Little Rann of Kutch in Gujarat use evaporation on a huge scale. Sea water or brine from underground is led through a row of shallow ponds. The Sun and wind take the water, and white salt is raked from the last pond. Gujarat gives about three quarters of the salt made in India.
- Waste pickers in Indian cities sort the rubbish that other people mix. Groups such as SWaCH in Pune and Hasiru Dala in Bengaluru work with households who keep wet and dry waste apart. This makes picking faster and safer. The same picking you did with stones in rice is done here by hand, all day.
- Beach sand at Chavara in Kerala and Manavalakurichi in Tamil Nadu holds dark grains of a mineral called ilmenite. A strong magnet pulls it out, and it is used to make titanium. Factories pass the sand over magnets and over heavy-grain tables to part the minerals.
- Water works in cities clear river water in steps. First the water stands in large tanks so that mud settles. Then it passes through beds of sand, which are filters. Last, it is treated to kill germs, because settling and filtering do not stop the smallest germs.
- A sari folded several times was used as a filter for drinking water in villages in Bangladesh. The microbiologist Rita Colwell and her team tested it, and cholera fell by about half. Cloth did not make the water pure. It held back the larger bits that carry the germs.
- Rice mills use sieves to sort whole grains from broken ones. Some use machines that look at the colour of each grain and blow out the discoloured ones with a jet of air. This is picking, done by a camera and fast air instead of fingers.
- In your kidneys, the filters pass roughly 180 litres of liquid a day from your blood, and nearly all of it goes back to the blood. About 1.5 litres leave your body as urine, carrying waste. Your body separates mixtures all day without you noticing.
- Home water purifiers use cloth, ceramic or carbon filters. Some also use a thin sheet that lets water through but not salt. This is called reverse osmosis. The sea-water plant at Minjur near Chennai uses the same idea on a much bigger scale.

## Spotted around us
- When you wash rice or dal, swirl it and wait a few seconds. Notice that the grains sink and the cloudy water sits on top. Pour the cloud away and see how the water clears.
- Watch someone make South Indian filter coffee. Hot water drips through the powder in the metal filter, and the dark decoction comes out below. Notice that the powder stays behind.
- Look at how a cook uses a flat winnowing tray, called a soop in Hindi or a muram in Tamil. The cook tosses dal gently, and the light husk flies off while the dal falls back. Notice that the cook does not touch each grain.
- When paneer is made, milk is curdled with lemon or vinegar and then poured through a muslin cloth. Notice that the lumps stay in the cloth and the thin liquid runs through. The lemon changed the milk into something new, and the cloth only separates what the lemon made.
- After a hot day or a game, look at your school shirt or your forehead. A white salty mark can be left where sweat dried. Notice that the water left and the salt stayed.
- Look inside a steel kettle, a water bottle or a tap. A chalky white ring or crust is left where drops of hard water evaporated. Notice that the water vanished and the minerals stayed.
- In the monsoon, look at how muddy puddle water sits still for an hour. Notice that it slowly clears at the top and a thin layer of mud lies at the bottom. Nobody added a filter.
- Next time someone pours a cup of tea, look at the strainer. Notice which part stays and which part goes through, and think about which difference between them the strainer uses.

## Open questions
- **Why did the universe begin in such an orderly state that things have been mixing ever since?** Physicists can explain why mixing is likely once you start from order. No accepted theory explains the orderly start. It may need a theory of the beginning of the universe, or of what time is, and neither exists yet.
- **How does water really pass through the thin plastic sheet that takes salt out of sea water?** The passages in the sheet are about the size of a few water molecules and keep changing shape. No microscope can watch water move through them. Computer models and flow measurements point to two different pictures, and tests to decide between them are still being done.
- **Can carbon dioxide be taken back out of the air at a cost the world can pay?** Carbon dioxide is only about 4 parts in every 10,000 of air, so a machine must handle a huge amount of air to catch a little. Physics sets a lowest energy cost, and today's machines use several times that. Trees, soils and seas already do this work, and how best to help them is also argued over.
- **How did the first cells come to keep their inside different from their outside?** The first cells had to hold a small private mixture apart from the sea around them. Fatty molecules can form such bubbles by themselves in water, as laboratory tests show. How a bubble first came to hold the right mixture is not known. How it came to control what crossed its skin is not known either, because no trace of those first cells is left.
- **Can the behaviour of a whole mixture, such as a living cell, be worked out from its parts alone?** For a simple mixture such as salt water, chemists can now predict much from the parts. For a cell, with thousands of kinds of molecules acting on each other, nobody can. Scientists disagree on whether this is only a matter of bigger computers or whether wholes need ideas of their own.
- **How can the tiny pieces of plastic now mixed into the sea and the soil be removed, or will they stay?** The pieces are spread thinly through enormous amounts of water and soil. Many are the same size as living plankton, so a filter that catches the plastic also catches living beings. Some bacteria and fungi can break down a few plastics slowly. Whether that can work outside a laboratory is being tested.

## Questions to ask kids
- Think of your walk to school or the shop today. Name one mixture you breathed, touched or walked through, and say how you could separate its parts.
- Think of something you use every day that is a mixture and looks like one thing. What would you test to show it has more than one part?
- If you had to get salt back from a bucket of muddy sea water, what would you do first, and why not last?
- Which is easier at home, mixing two things or separating them again? What makes the difference?
- A cook, a farmer and a scientist each separate something. What is the same about what they do?
- When something looks clear, like water or glass, what could still be hidden inside it?

- What is the mixture you are made of? Name three materials in your body that you could not see from outside.

## Connected maths
- Sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in each litre. How many litres of sea water must dry up to leave 1 kilogram of salt? **About 29 litres.**. 1 kilogram is 1,000 grams. 1,000 ÷ 35 is about 28.6, which rounds up to 29 litres. A salt pan makes tonnes of salt, so it must dry up millions of litres. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A long thin rice grain is 6 millimetres long and 1.5 millimetres wide and thick. Three sieves have round holes of 1, 2.5 and 7 millimetres. Which sieves stop the grain, and which let it pass? **The 1 millimetre sieve stops it. The 2.5 and 7 millimetre sieves let it pass.**. A grain can go through a hole if its smallest cross-section fits. The grain is 1.5 millimetres across when it goes end-first. That is smaller than 2.5, so it passes. It is bigger than 1, so it is stopped. The 7 millimetre hole is bigger than the longest side, so it passes at any angle. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L4`)
- Filter paper has holes about 0.01 millimetres wide. A sand grain is about 0.5 millimetres wide. A bacterium is about 0.001 millimetres long. How many times wider is the sand grain than a hole, and how many bacteria in a line fit across one hole? **The sand grain is 50 times wider than a hole. About 10 bacteria fit across one hole.**. 0.5 ÷ 0.01 = 50. 0.01 ÷ 0.001 = 10. The paper stops sand easily and lets a line of 10 bacteria through, so clear filtered water can still carry germs. (queues `math.multiplication.scaling.L4`)
- A girl mixes 10 grams of sand with 10 grams of salt in water. She filters, dries the sand and gets 10 grams. She dries the filtrate and gets 9 grams of salt. Where did 1 gram go? Give two likely reasons. **It was not destroyed. Likely reasons are salt left stuck to the filter or wet sand, and some salt left on the tumbler or the spoon.**. Nothing that is separated is lost. The 1 gram is somewhere in the apparatus. Rinsing the sand and the tumbler into the filtrate would bring most of it back. A total of 19 grams for 20 grams put in is a clue to look for the missing gram, not for an error in nature. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Stir sugar into tea. Is the sugar still in the tea? How could you tell? → Yes. You can taste that the tea is sweet, even though you cannot see the sugar.
- Name one thing in your kitchen that is a mixture. → Any real mixture, such as rice and dal in a bowl, salt water, tea, or bhel puri.
- How do you get the stones out of a plate of rice? → Spread the rice and pick the stones out by hand. A sieve also works if the stones are bigger than the grains.
- Why does a tea strainer keep the leaves but let the tea through? → Its holes are bigger than the tea but smaller than the leaves.
- What happens to a plate of salt water left in the sun for two days? → The water goes into the air and a white crust of salt is left on the plate.
- What does a magnet pick up from a bowl of rice and steel pins? → The steel pins. Iron and steel are attracted by a magnet and rice is not.
- When you wash rice, why does the water turn cloudy? → Fine dust and loose starch spread in the water. The rice grains are heavy and sink.
- Where does the water go when wet clothes dry on a line? → It evaporates into the air as water vapour.
