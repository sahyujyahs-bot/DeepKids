# Germs, medicines and vaccines: how the body and medicine meet an infection
`sci.microorganisms.germs.L4`

**Summary.** Two children both have a sore throat. The doctor gives one of them tablets and sends the other home with warm water and rest. The doctor is not being unfair. Tablets that kill bacteria do nothing to viruses, and most sore throats are caused by viruses. Your child finds out what a medicine really does by testing which kitchen things stop a living microbe, and by playing a game in which a medicine leaves a few tough germs behind. Then they learn how a vaccine shows the body a safe picture of a germ, so that the body is ready, and why one vaccinated child protects the child sitting next to her.

**Intro.** *A boy in your class had measles last year. You have never had measles, and you did not catch it, even though you sat in the same room. Something in your body was ready for the germ before the germ arrived. What was it, and who put it there?* In this unit you find out what happens inside a body when germs get in, and what medicines can and cannot do. You test which kitchen things slow down a living microbe. You play a game with rice and dal in which a medicine leaves a few tough germs behind, and see what those survivors do next. And you learn, in plain words, how a vaccine works and why it protects people who never had it.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can sort illnesses by their cause, which may be bacteria, a virus, a fungus, a parasite or no germ at all, and say which ones pass from person to person.
1. I can describe what happens in a body from the time germs get in to the time the person feels ill, and say what a fever is for.
2. I can say what the body's defenders do, and why the body is faster the second time it meets a germ.
3. I can explain why an antibiotic works on bacteria but not on viruses, and say what other kinds of medicine are for.
4. I can explain, from the rice and dal game, how germs that survive a medicine become common.
5. I can say when a medicine is needed and when it is not, and name the danger signs that need a doctor at once.
6. I can explain in simple words how a vaccine works, and why it holds no working germ.
7. I can explain why a vaccinated person also protects people who cannot be vaccinated.

## Terms
- **Infectious disease**: An illness caused by a germ that has got into the body. Bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites can all cause one. *e.g.* Typhoid is an infectious disease caused by bacteria. A broken arm is not infectious, because no germ causes a broken arm.
- **Incubation period**: The time between germs getting in and the first signs of illness. The germs are multiplying quietly in that time. *e.g.* For a cold the incubation period is about one to three days. For measles it is about ten to fourteen days.
- **Fever**: A body temperature higher than usual, about 38 degrees Celsius or more. The brain raises the temperature while the body fights an infection. *e.g.* A child with flu may have a fever and feel shivery as the temperature goes up.
- **Immune system**: The parts of the body that find and remove germs. They include skin, tears, stomach acid and many kinds of white blood cell. *e.g.* Your immune system clears most colds in about a week without any medicine.
- **Antibody**: A tiny protein that the body makes to fit one particular germ, the way a key fits one lock. It sticks to the germ and marks them to be removed. *e.g.* After measles, your blood holds antibodies that fit the measles virus and no other germ.
- **Antibiotic**: A medicine that kills bacteria or stops them multiplying. It does nothing to viruses. *e.g.* A doctor may give an antibiotic for a bacterial throat infection but not for a cold.
- **Antiviral**: A medicine that slows down one kind of virus from making copies. Each antiviral works on only a few viruses. *e.g.* Some doctors give an antiviral to people who are at risk of getting very ill from flu.
- **Antiseptic**: A liquid or cream put on skin to kill germs on the surface, for example on a cut. It is not safe to swallow. *e.g.* A grown-up cleans a scrape with an antiseptic or with soap and water.
- **Resistant**: A germ is resistant to a medicine when the medicine no longer kills them. The germ is resistant, not the person. *e.g.* Resistant bacteria can carry on multiplying in a body that is taking an antibiotic.
- **Dose**: The amount of a medicine to take at one time. A child's dose usually depends on how much the child weighs. *e.g.* A label may say 5 millilitres, three times a day. Too little may not work and too much can harm.
- **Prescription**: A paper or message from a doctor that says which medicine to take, how much and for how long. *e.g.* The pharmacist gave the antibiotic only after seeing the doctor's prescription.
- **Side effect**: An effect of a medicine that you did not want, such as a rash or a loose tummy. Most are mild. *e.g.* An antibiotic can upset the helpful bacteria in the gut and cause loose motions.
- **Oral rehydration solution (ORS)**: A drink of clean water with the right amounts of salt and sugar. It replaces the water and salts the body loses in loose motions. *e.g.* A packet of ORS mixed in clean water is given in small sips to a child with loose motions.
- **Vaccine**: A safe version of a germ, or a piece of one, given so the body learns to recognise the real germ. A vaccine does not give you the illness. *e.g.* The measles vaccine teaches the body to make antibodies against the measles virus.
- **Immunity**: Being protected from a germ because the body is ready for them. Immunity can come from a vaccine or from having had the illness. *e.g.* After chickenpox, most people have immunity for life.
- **Booster**: An extra dose of a vaccine given later, to remind the body and keep the protection strong. *e.g.* A booster against tetanus and diphtheria is given at school age in India.
- **Herd protection**: When enough people in a group are protected, a germ cannot find enough people to spread to, so even people who are not protected are safer. *e.g.* A baby too young for the measles vaccine is safer when everyone around the baby is vaccinated.
- **Eradicate**: To remove a disease from the whole world, so that nobody gets it anywhere. Smallpox is the only human disease eradicated so far. *e.g.* Smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980.
- **Pandemic**: An outbreak of an illness that spreads across many countries or the whole world. *e.g.* Covid-19 was a pandemic that began in 2019 and reached every continent.

## Explanations
### Which illnesses come from germs, and which do not · *defined*
**Definition:** An infectious disease is an illness caused by a germ that has got into the body. Some infectious diseases pass from person to person, and some do not.

Different germs cause different illnesses. Bacteria cause typhoid, tuberculosis and many throat infections. Viruses cause colds, flu, measles, dengue and chickenpox. A fungus causes ringworm. A parasite causes malaria. All of these are infectious diseases, because a germ gets in. Some illnesses have no germ at all. A broken bone comes from a fall. Diabetes comes from the way the body handles sugar. Anaemia often comes from too little iron in food. Nobody can catch these from a friend. Contagious is a smaller group, the infectious diseases that pass from person to person. Tetanus is infectious, because bacteria from soil get into a deep cut, but nobody catches tetanus from another person. Knowing the cause tells you what to do. Bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites each need a different kind of medicine, and an illness with no germ needs none of them.
- e.g. Measles is infectious and very contagious, because the virus travels in the air.
- e.g. Tetanus is infectious but not contagious. The bacteria live in soil and get into deep cuts.
- e.g. Diabetes is not infectious. No germ causes diabetes, so nobody catches the illness from a friend.
### Inside you: from landing to feeling ill · *defined*
**Definition:** The time between germs getting in and the first sign of illness is the incubation period. During it the germs are multiplying, and the body is getting ready.

Think of a cold virus that lands in your nose on Monday. For the first day or so you feel perfectly well. Inside, the virus is entering cells and making copies, and each new copy enters another cell. Your body notices. Cells that are being attacked send out chemical alarm signals, and defenders hurry to the nose. Those signals are also what make you feel unwell. They tell the brain to raise the body's temperature by a degree or two, which is a fever, and they make you tired. Many of the signs of illness are signs of the fight, not of the germ. The incubation period is different for each germ. For a cold it is about one to three days. For flu it is about one to four days. For measles it is about ten to fourteen days, and for chickenpox about two weeks. After the fight, most people get better because the defenders have cleared the germs away.
- e.g. A runny nose is partly your body washing germs out of the nose with extra mucus.
- e.g. A child with measles may feel well for a week and a half after catching the virus, and can pass it on in the last days of that time.
- e.g. Feeling very tired during an infection is the body slowing you down while it uses energy for the fight.
### The body's defenders: walls, patrols and memory · *defined*
**Definition:** The immune system is the set of parts that find and remove germs. Its first part is a set of walls. Its second part is a set of defenders that learn.

The walls come first. Skin, tears, mucus in the nose, and the acid in the stomach stop most germs. A germ that gets past the walls meets patrol cells, which are kinds of white blood cell. Some patrol cells swallow germs whole. Other white blood cells make antibodies. An antibody is a tiny protein with a shape that fits one particular germ, the way a key fits one lock. The antibody sticks to the germ and marks them, so that the patrol cells can find and remove them. The first time the body meets a germ, making enough of the right antibodies takes about a week or two. That is why you can be ill for days. After the fight, some of the white blood cells stay on for years, ready to make the same antibody quickly. These are memory cells. The second time the same germ arrives, the body may clear them in a day or two, often before you feel anything.
- e.g. After chickenpox, memory cells stay in your blood for many years, so most people never get chickenpox twice.
- e.g. A cut that gets dirty still heals, because patrol cells swallow the bacteria that got in.
- e.g. An antibody for the measles virus does nothing against the mumps virus, because the shape does not fit.
### What medicines do, and what they do not do · *defined*
**Definition:** A medicine is a substance taken to prevent, treat or ease an illness. Some medicines act on the germ. Others act on the body.

There are four jobs a medicine can do. Some medicines kill germs or stop them multiplying. Antibiotics do this for bacteria. Antivirals do it for a few viruses. Antifungal medicines do it for fungi. Other medicines ease the signs, and do nothing to the germ. Paracetamol, for example, lowers a fever and eases pain, so a child can rest. A third kind replaces what the body has lost. In loose motions the body loses water and salts, and the drink called ORS puts them back. A fourth kind prevents illness, and that is a vaccine. A doctor chooses by cause. For a throat infection caused by bacteria, an antibiotic may help. For most colds, which are caused by viruses, no medicine kills the virus, and the best treatment is rest and drinks while the defenders do the work. For most loose motions in children, ORS is the main treatment, and a doctor may add zinc tablets.
- e.g. An antibiotic helps with a bacterial throat infection but has no effect on a cold.
- e.g. Paracetamol lowers a fever. The fever was a sign of the fight, so the infection is still there.
- e.g. ORS does not kill the germ that caused the loose motions. It keeps the body from drying out until the body clears the germ.
### Why an antibiotic hits bacteria and leaves you alone · *defined*
**Definition:** An antibiotic works by stopping something that bacteria need and that human cells do not have.

A bacterium is a single cell with a tough wall around the outside. To grow and split, she must keep building that wall. Penicillin stops the building. The wall weakens, and the bacterium bursts. Human cells have no wall, so penicillin has nothing to hit in us. Other antibiotics block the tiny machines inside a bacterium that build proteins. Those machines have a different shape from ours, so the medicine fits theirs and not ours. A virus is different again. A virus is not a cell, has no wall and no machines of their own. They make copies only by taking over a cell that belongs to someone else. So an antibiotic has nothing to attack in a virus. That is also why an antiviral is hard to make. It must stop a virus without stopping the cell the virus is using. Antibiotics are not perfect. They also kill some of the helpful bacteria in the gut, which is why they sometimes cause loose motions.
- e.g. Penicillin makes the wall of a bacterium weak, so the bacterium bursts as she grows.
- e.g. A cold virus has no wall, so penicillin has nothing to attack.
- e.g. Taking an antibiotic can cause loose motions because helpful gut bacteria are killed along with the harmful ones.
### Where medicines come from · *defined*
**Definition:** Many medicines were found in living beings, such as trees, moulds and soil bacteria, and then made in factories.

Living beings have been fighting each other with chemicals for a very long time. Moulds and soil bacteria make substances that kill other microbes near them, and some of those became our antibiotics. In 1928, Alexander Fleming saw a clear ring around a mould on a dish of bacteria, where no bacteria grew. That mould made penicillin. It took more than ten years, and the work of Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley, before penicillin was made in enough quantity for patients. Dorothy Hodgkin worked out the shape of the penicillin molecule in the 1940s. In 1943, a mouldy cantaloupe from a market in Peoria, in the United States, gave scientists a mould that, grown in big tanks, made far more penicillin than Fleming's mould. The story says a lab worker called Mary Hunt brought the fruit in, though the records are not certain. Streptomycin, the first medicine for tuberculosis, came from a soil bacterium, and a graduate student named Albert Schatz was the one who found it. Quinine, a medicine for malaria, came from the bark of the cinchona tree. In 1972, Tu Youyou, a Chinese scientist, found artemisinin in a plant called sweet wormwood, after reading a fourth century book of remedies. She won a Nobel Prize in 2015. In 1950, two American scientists made nystatin, the first safe medicine against fungus infections. They were Elizabeth Lee Hazen and Rachel Fuller Brown, and they worked in different cities and posted samples to each other.
- e.g. Penicillin comes from a mould that is closely related to the green mould on old bread.
- e.g. Quinine comes from cinchona bark, and cinchona trees were once grown in the Nilgiri hills to make it.
- e.g. Artemisinin comes from sweet wormwood, a plant used in China for fever for many centuries.
### How germs become resistant · *defined*
**Definition:** Resistance appears when a few germs survive a medicine, multiply, and become the new crowd. The germ becomes resistant. The person does not.

Bacteria multiply by splitting in two, and each time they copy their genes, a tiny mistake can creep in. Most mistakes change nothing. A very few help the bacterium to survive a medicine, for example by making a chemical that breaks the medicine down. So in a crowd of billions of bacteria, a few may already be tough before any medicine arrives. When a person takes an antibiotic, the ordinary bacteria die. The tough few are left, with plenty of food and room, and they multiply. After a few days, nearly all the bacteria in the body are tough. Bacteria can also hand pieces of their genes to each other, even to bacteria of other kinds, so a tough gene can spread fast. This is natural selection, the same process that shaped every living being. The medicine does not teach the bacteria anything. It only chooses which ones carry on. The more an antibiotic is used, the more chances tough bacteria have. Doctors call this a world-wide problem. A study published in 2022 estimated that more than a million people died in 2019 as a direct result of infections that medicines could not treat.
- e.g. A person does not become resistant. The bacteria in their body become resistant.
- e.g. In the rice and dal game, the medicine removed most of the rice, and the dal grains multiplied in the space that was left.
- e.g. A bacterium can pass a tough gene to a different kind of bacterium, so resistance can jump between kinds.
### When to use a medicine, and when not · *defined*
**Definition:** A medicine helps only when it is the right one for the cause, in the right dose, for the right time. A medicine taken for the wrong reason can do harm and no good.

Here are the rules that doctors give families. Give a medicine only when a doctor or a health worker has said so, and follow the dose and times on the label. A child's dose depends on weight, so never guess. Do not give a child a medicine that was bought for someone else, and do not save left-over tablets for next time. Check the date on the packet, and keep all medicines out of reach of small children. Do not press a doctor for an antibiotic for a cold. For most colds and coughs, rest, warm drinks and time are the treatment. Ask the doctor when to stop a medicine, because the right length depends on the illness. Some signs mean a child needs a doctor at once. The signs are these. A child cannot drink, vomits everything, has a fit, is very sleepy and hard to wake, or has trouble breathing. A fever that lasts more than two or three days also needs a doctor. This card is general advice. A doctor who knows the child knows best.
- e.g. A child with a cold and a runny nose needs rest and drinks, not an antibiotic.
- e.g. A child who cannot keep any drink down needs a doctor the same day.
- e.g. Left-over tablets from last month's illness should not be given for a new illness without asking a doctor.
### Vaccines in simple words · *defined*
**Definition:** A vaccine shows the body a safe version of a germ, so the body learns to fight the real germ before the real germ arrives.

A vaccine does not contain a working, full-strength germ that can make you ill. It contains something that looks like the germ to the defenders. There are several kinds. Some contain a germ that has been killed. Some contain a germ that has been weakened so much that the germ can barely multiply. Some contain just one piece of the germ, such as a part of the coat. For tetanus, the vaccine holds a harmless form of the poison the bacteria make. A few newer vaccines hold a set of instructions for the body's own cells to make one harmless piece of the germ for a short time. In every kind, the defenders see the piece, make antibodies, and keep memory cells. If the real germ arrives months or years later, the body is ready, and often the germ is cleared before there is any illness. Some vaccines are given once, some need two or three doses, and some need a booster. Common side effects are a sore arm and a mild fever for a day. Serious reactions are very rare, and health services watch for them. Katalin Karikó, a biochemist born in Hungary, found how to change a set of instructions so that the body does not attack them, which made the newer vaccines possible. She and Drew Weissman shared the Nobel Prize in 2023. Rabies is a special case. The virus takes weeks to travel from a bite to the brain, so the vaccine can be given after a bite and still teach the body in time.
- e.g. The tetanus vaccine holds a harmless form of the poison that tetanus bacteria make.
- e.g. The measles vaccine holds a weakened measles virus that barely multiplies.
- e.g. A person bitten by a dog that might have rabies is given the vaccine at once, as the virus is still on the way.
### Herd protection: how vaccinated people protect others · *defined*
**Definition:** Herd protection means that when most people in a group are protected against a germ, the germ rarely finds anyone to pass to, so people who are not protected are safer too.

Every germ chain needs a next person. A vaccinated person is a broken link. If most of the people around a germ are vaccinated, the germ keeps meeting broken links, and the chain dies out. This protects babies who are too young for a vaccine, and people whose bodies cannot take it, for example those having cancer treatment. How many people need to be protected depends on the germ. Measles is one of the most contagious illnesses known. One ill child can pass it to twelve to eighteen others who are not protected. So about 95 out of every 100 people need protection to stop measles spreading. Herd protection is not a promise, because it works only while enough people stay vaccinated. When vaccination falls in a place, measles comes back there. In the Ring the Germ game, you saw this. When most houses were vaccinated, the illness stayed small. When only a third were, it spread far.
- e.g. A baby of five months cannot yet have the measles vaccine, but she is safer when her brothers, cousins and neighbours have had it.
- e.g. A school where almost every child is vaccinated rarely has a measles outbreak, even if one ill child comes in.
- e.g. A health worker who finds one case vaccinates the people around the ill person, which cuts the chain.
### A short history of vaccines · *defined*
**Definition:** People learned to protect each other from illness long before anyone knew what a germ was.

Smallpox was an illness that killed about three in every ten people who caught it. In several countries, people noticed that someone who had survived smallpox never caught it again. So healers scratched a little material from a smallpox sore into the skin of a healthy person, who then usually had a mild illness and was protected. This was called variolation. It sometimes killed the person. Lady Mary Wortley Montagu saw it done in Istanbul, and in 1721 she had her young daughter protected in London, and she told other people. In England in 1774 a farmer called Benjamin Jesty protected his family with matter from cowpox, a mild illness of cows. In 1796, Edward Jenner tested the same idea carefully. A milkmaid called Sarah Nelmes had cowpox sores, and Jenner used matter from one sore on a boy named James Phipps. Later he showed that James did not catch smallpox. In the 1880s, Louis Pasteur made a vaccine for rabies. In 1897, Waldemar Haffkine made a vaccine for plague in Bombay and tested it first on himself. In the 1960s and 1970s, a world campaign found every case of smallpox and vaccinated everyone around it. The last natural case was in 1977, and in 1980 the world was declared free of smallpox. India reported its last case in 1975. India's last case of polio was reported in January 2011, in a girl in West Bengal, and in 2014 the region was declared free of polio. In India, Gagandeep Kang, a doctor and scientist who worked in Vellore, helped test Rotavac. Rotavac is a rotavirus vaccine made in India, and it protects babies from a common cause of dangerous loose motions.
- e.g. Jesty, Jenner and the milkmaids who knew cowpox all played a part. Jenner's careful test is the one the world remembers.
- e.g. Haffkine's plague vaccine was tested first on himself, and the institute in Mumbai now carries his name.
- e.g. The smallpox vaccine protected people so well that smallpox is the only human disease the world has wiped out.
### Boosters and yearly shots: why some protection fades · *defined*
**Definition:** A vaccine can stop working as well over time, or because the germ has changed. A booster or a new vaccine fixes the problem.

Memory cells last longer for some germs than for others. After two doses of the measles vaccine, protection usually lasts a lifetime. Protection against tetanus and diphtheria fades over some years, so a booster is given, as it is to ten-year-olds in India. Another reason is that some germs change. Flu viruses change a little every year, so last year's antibodies may not fit this year's virus, as an old key may not fit a new lock. So scientists study the flu viruses going around every year, and each year's flu vaccine is made to match. Some germs are hard to make a vaccine for, because they change fast or hide from defenders. There is no vaccine yet for the common cold, which is caused by hundreds of kinds of virus. In 2021 and 2023, the World Health Organization recommended the first two vaccines against malaria, for children in places where malaria is common.
- e.g. A tetanus booster tops up the memory cells that fade over the years.
- e.g. A flu vaccine is updated each year to match the viruses that are going around.
- e.g. There is no single vaccine for the common cold, because hundreds of different viruses can cause one.
### How long should a course of antibiotics be? · *debated*
For many years, doctors told patients to finish every tablet in the packet, even after they felt well. The reason given was that stopping early lets the tough bacteria survive and multiply. In 2017, a group of doctors and scientists wrote that there was little evidence for this rule. They said that the longer a person takes an antibiotic, the more chances there are for resistant bacteria to appear in the body. Other doctors replied that for some infections, such as tuberculosis, stopping early is dangerous. Both sides agree on one thing: families should not decide the length of a course themselves, and the doctor who prescribed it should say when to stop.
- e.g. For tuberculosis, a course of many months is needed, and stopping early is dangerous.
- e.g. For some urinary and chest infections, studies have found that shorter courses worked as well as longer ones.
- **Martin Llewelyn and colleagues, who wrote in the British Medical Journal in 2017:** The idea that you must always finish the packet has little evidence behind it. Taking a medicine for longer than needed gives resistant bacteria more chances to appear. Courses should be as short as works, and doctors should say when to stop.
- **Many infectious disease doctors:** For some illnesses, like tuberculosis and some bone infections, a long course is needed to kill every bacterium, and stopping early is dangerous. The right length depends on the illness.
- **Health services in many countries:** Follow the doctor's advice exactly, and ask when to stop. Never keep tablets for next time and never stop or extend a course on your own.
### Germs that eat germs: bacteriophages · *idea*
**Definition:** A bacteriophage, or phage, is a virus that attacks only bacteria. Phages are the most common living things on Earth.

A phage lands on one kind of bacterium, pushes in their genes, and makes the bacterium build many new phages. Then the bacterium bursts. In 1896, Ernest Hankin, a British scientist working in Agra, reported that the water of the Ganga and the Yamuna killed cholera bacteria. He also reported that boiling the water removed this power. That is an early sign of phages. In 1915 Frederick Twort in England and in 1917 Felix d'Herelle in France both described them. D'Herelle named them. A phage is very choosy and attacks only one kind of bacterium, and does not hurt people. Doctors in Georgia have used phages to treat infections for about a hundred years, and scientists elsewhere are testing them now for infections that no antibiotic can cure. Nobody yet knows whether phages will work widely, because bacteria can become resistant to phages too, and testing them takes a long time.
- e.g. A phage that attacks the bacteria of cholera does not touch the bacteria of tuberculosis.
- e.g. Phage treatment is already used in some hospitals for infections that no antibiotic can cure, under special rules.

## Mostly agreed
- Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them multiplying. They do nothing to viruses. *How we know:* Antibiotics attack parts that bacteria have and viruses do not, such as a cell wall. Careful trials found that antibiotics do not shorten colds or flu.
- Germs that survive a medicine multiply, so the more a medicine is used, the more common resistant germs become. *How we know:* Laboratory tests, including the replica plating of Esther and Joshua Lederberg in 1952, show that a few resistant bacteria are already in a crowd. Hospital records show resistance rising in places where antibiotics are used a lot.
- Vaccines teach the body to recognise a germ, without giving the illness. *How we know:* Blood tests show antibodies and memory cells after a vaccine, and vaccinated people get far fewer cases of the illness than unvaccinated people in the same outbreak.
- Smallpox has been wiped out by vaccination. Polio has been wiped out in India and most of the world. *How we know:* A world-wide search found no natural case of smallpox after 1977, and the World Health Assembly declared the world free of smallpox in 1980. India reported no wild polio case from 2011, and the region was certified in 2014.
- When enough people in a group are vaccinated, people who are not vaccinated are safer too. *How we know:* Measles cases rise in places where fewer than about 95 out of 100 children have both doses, and fall again when vaccination rises.
- Oral rehydration solution saves the lives of children with loose motions. *How we know:* Trials and field use since the 1960s show that salt and sugar in clean water replace what the body loses. During a cholera outbreak among refugees in 1971, a team led by Dr Dilip Mahalanabis used it when drips ran short, and far fewer people died.
- Side effects of vaccines are usually mild, such as a sore arm or a mild fever for a day. Serious reactions are very rare. *How we know:* Health services track every vaccine given and every report of a reaction, and the risks of the illnesses are much larger than the risks of the vaccines.

## How we found out
**Esther Lederberg and Joshua Lederberg, with the earlier work of Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück, 1943 (the numbers test) and 1952 (the velvet stamp).** In the 1940s, scientists argued about a puzzle. When a medicine or a virus attacked a dish of bacteria, a few survived, and their children were resistant. Did the attack change those bacteria, as if they had learned to resist? Or were a few already different, by chance, before the attack came? In 1943, Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück ran a test with numbers that pointed to chance. In 1952, Esther Lederberg showed it with her own eyes. She invented a stamp made from a round piece of velvet. She pressed it on a dish where bacteria had grown as dots, so the velvet picked up a copy of the pattern. Then she pressed the velvet on new dishes with a medicine in them. Dots grew in the same places on every copy. She went back to the first dish, which had never touched the medicine, and tested the dots in those same places. They were resistant already. Joshua Lederberg shared a Nobel Prize in 1958 for other work on bacteria. Esther did not share it.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought that bacteria become resistant because of the medicine, as if the medicine changed them. Esther Lederberg's velvet stamp showed that the resistant bacteria were already there, on a dish that had never met the medicine.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Keep the left-over tablets from last time, because they will work for the next illness.** (A common habit in many homes; *rejected*)  
  For: The packet looks the same and the symptoms seem the same, and tablets cost money.  
  Against: A cold and a bacterial infection feel alike, but only one needs an antibiotic. A part-used packet is the wrong dose and the wrong length. Medicines also go out of date. Taking antibiotics without need gives tough germs more chances.
- **A vaccine can give you the illness it is meant to stop.** (A worry many people have; *rejected*)  
  For: A vaccine holds a germ or a piece of one. A few vaccines hold a weakened live germ, and in very rare cases that germ can cause a mild form of the illness.  
  Against: Most vaccines hold killed germs or just one piece, which cannot multiply at all. Even a weakened germ barely multiplies, and the risk of the real illness is far larger than any risk from the vaccine.
- **It is better to catch the illness than to have a vaccine, because the illness gives stronger protection.** (Some parents, and old ideas in many places; *rejected*)  
  For: For some illnesses, catching the real germ does give long protection, as with chickenpox.  
  Against: The real illness can cause deafness, blindness, damage to the brain or death. Measles, for example, can harm a child's brain and can also weaken the defences against other germs for a while. A vaccine gives protection without that risk.
- **A fever is dangerous in itself and must always be brought down quickly.** (A worry many parents have; *debated*)  
  For: A very high fever can be frightening and uncomfortable, and in small babies a fever can be a sign of serious illness.  
  Against: A fever is part of how the body fights an infection, and it is usually not harmful in itself in a healthy child. Doctors often treat the child's comfort, not the number. Scientists are still studying how much a fever helps.
- **Home remedies like kadha, turmeric milk, tulsi or neem can replace medicine for a serious infection.** (Many families, and some traditional healers; *rejected*)  
  For: Warm drinks, honey for an older child's cough, and rest comfort a child with a cold. Some plants contain substances that do act on microbes, and some medicines, such as quinine, come from plants.  
  Against: Tests of a plant in a dish are not the same as testing a medicine in a person. For serious infections such as typhoid, tuberculosis or malaria, delay can be dangerous, and tested medicines are needed. Comfort and cure are different jobs.
- **Once you have had a vaccine, you can never catch that illness.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Vaccines work very well, and most vaccinated people never catch the illness.  
  Against: No vaccine works for every person, and protection can fade, so a few vaccinated people still catch the illness. They usually have a milder illness. That is one reason boosters are given and why herd protection matters.
- **Killing as many germs as possible with strong cleaners and antibacterial products keeps a home healthiest.** (Advertisements, and many families; *rejected*)  
  For: Germs cause illness, so fewer germs seem better.  
  Against: Most microbes are harmless or helpful, and strong cleaners everywhere can help tough germs to take over. Plain soap and water, clean water and clean food break most germ chains. Disinfectants are for particular jobs, such as a toilet or a wound.

## Roots
We have watched a medicine leave a few tough germs behind, and a vaccine teach a body before the germ arrives. Now we ask what these tell us about the fight we call health, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the rice and dal game, the medicine did not make any germ tougher. It removed the ordinary ones, and the few tough ones carried on. So the germs of tomorrow are the survivors of what we do today. We are part of the fight, not outside it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture illness as a war between us and a fixed enemy called germs. But germs are never all the same, and the ones that survive our medicines become the next crowd.  
*Evidence:* In the Rice and Dal game, the plate with the medicine ended with mostly tough germs, and the plate with no medicine kept its ordinary crowd. Esther Lederberg's velvet stamp showed that real resistant bacteria were already on a dish that had never met the medicine.

- *who am i:* You are a body that remembers. Your defenders keep a record of the germs you have met, and a vaccine can add to that record without making you ill.
- *what is life:* A crowd of bacteria is never all the same. A few differ by chance, and when the world changes, those few carry on. Life goes on because it is varied.
- *what is this world:* People, medicines and germs shape each other. Our medicines decide which germs live, and the germs that live decide which medicines we must make next.

In the game, a crowd that looked all the same held a few that were different. When the medicine came, those few became the future. A world that holds many kinds can survive a change that would end a world of one kind. Your own defenders work this way too: your body makes millions of kinds of antibody before any germ arrives, and the germ finds the one that fits. Nobody plans the variety. It is how life keeps going when conditions change.

- **Tao Te Ching 64:** Act on things before they appear. Put things in order before there is confusion. A tree that fills your arms grew from a tiny shoot. (A vaccine acts before the germ arrives. A small step early prevents a large trouble later.)
- **Louis Pasteur, opening lecture at the University of Lille, 7 December 1854:** In the fields of observation, chance favours only the prepared mind. (Fleming saw a clear ring round a mould because he was ready to notice the ring. A body that has met a vaccine is prepared in the same way, and the real germ finds defenders ready.)
- **Thirukkural 948:** Find out the illness, find out its cause, find out the cure, and then treat with care. (A doctor chooses a medicine by the cause. A cold and a bacterial throat infection feel alike, but they have different causes and need different help.)
- Science (settled): Resistant bacteria are already present in a crowd before a medicine arrives. The medicine selects them, and they multiply. This is natural selection.
- Science (open): How the body keeps a memory of a germ for decades, and how long that memory lasts for each germ, is still being studied.
- Other view, Some biologists: Saying that germs and medicines 'shape each other' is a picture. A bacterium copies her genes with small mistakes, and a medicine sorts the copies. That is all the evidence needs. Calling it a fight, or a conversation, adds a story that the evidence does not require.
- Practice, Feel your pulse: the blood that carries your defenders (3 min): Sit still. Press two fingers on the inside of your wrist, below the thumb, until you feel your pulse. Each beat pushes blood past defenders that are patrolling right now. Some of them hold the memory of germs you met as a baby. Breathe in slowly and out slowly. Count ten beats. Then rest your hand in your lap.
- Ask: If a medicine decides which germs carry on, who is shaping whom? Are we shaping the germs, or are the germs shaping the medicines we make?
- Ask: Your defenders remember germs you met when you were too young to remember anything yourself. Who is the you that remembers?
- Ask: Esther Lederberg's name is often left out of the story of her own discovery. How can we know who did a piece of science, and who should get the credit?

## Story: The Round Scar
*The story is made up, but the facts are true. Smallpox killed about three in ten people who caught it. Health workers in India's last years of smallpox found each case and vaccinated the people around it. A vaccine given in the first days after meeting the germ can still protect.*

Anu was nine, and she lived with her parents and her grandmother in a flat in Kozhikode. Her grandmother was called Muthassi. On Monday, their class teacher, Revathi Miss, gave every child a pink slip.

"The health team is coming on Thursday," said Revathi Miss. "Everyone in Class 4 gets a booster injection. Take the slip home and ask your parents to sign it."

Anu carried the slip home as if it were hot. She did not like needles. She liked even less the idea of a germ being put into her arm on purpose. "Why would anyone put a germ into a healthy girl?" she wondered.

That evening, Muthassi was plaiting Anu's hair on the balcony. Her sari slipped off her shoulder. Anu saw something she had seen a hundred times and never asked about. High on Muthassi's left arm was a round, pale, dented mark, as big as a button.

"Muthassi, what is that?"

"That is my smallpox stamp," said Muthassi. "Have you heard of smallpox?" Anu shook her head. "No child your age has. It was an illness that covered people with sores. About three out of every ten who caught it died. And now it is gone from the whole world."

"Gone? How can an illness be gone?"

"Sit still, and I will tell you. When I was ten, my little brother Gopi woke up hot and shivering, with red spots on his face. In two days the spots were blisters. My father went very quiet, because everyone knew what that meant."

"The next morning, a woman came to our gate on a bicycle, with a cloth bag on the handlebar. She was Thankamma Chechi, the health worker. A neighbour had told her. She did not shout, and she did not run from the house. She sat on our front step with a notebook, and she asked questions."

"Who lives in this house? Who lives next door? Who brings the milk? Who sits beside Gopi at school? She wrote down every name. Then she said, 'By tonight, I need every one of these people in a line.'"

"By evening they were all there. The neighbours, the milkman, Gopi's whole class, and me. Thankamma Chechi dipped a small needle in a drop of liquid and pricked the top of each arm. It stung a little. A week later my arm swelled into a blister, and when it healed, it left this."

"And Gopi?" asked Anu, in a small voice.

"He was ill for many days, and some marks stayed on his face. But he lived. And nobody else in our village got smallpox that year. Nobody. Not the milkman, not the neighbours, not Gopi's classmates. Not even me, and I slept in his room for two weeks to look after him."

Anu frowned. Something did not fit. "But you were right next to him all day, Muthassi. You must have breathed his germs. Why didn't you get ill?"

"What do you think?" said Muthassi.

Anu thought. The drop in the needle must have had something to do with smallpox, or it would not have worked. But Muthassi had only a blister, not smallpox. So the drop must hold something that looks like the germ, but cannot make you really ill.

"The drop was a picture," said Anu slowly. "A picture of the germ, for your body to look at. Your body made antibodies from the picture, and they were ready when Gopi's germs came."

"Yes," said Muthassi. "Mine was made from a mild cousin of the smallpox germ, which cows catch. It is a lesson, not an attack."

"But Muthassi, why did Thankamma Chechi stamp the milkman and the neighbours? They were not Gopi's family."

Muthassi smiled. "Fetch me some rice flour."

Anu came back with a steel plate of rice flour. On the balcony floor, Muthassi made dots in a square, like the start of a kolam. One dot she made red with a pinch of kumkum. "That is Gopi's house. If a germ goes from house to house, which dots will the germ reach first?"

Anu pointed to the dots that touched the red one. "Those. And then the ones touching those."

"Now, if every one of those dots around the red one has a stamp?"

Anu rubbed white flour over the dots around the red one. Then she saw it. The germ had nowhere to go. A ring of protected people around the ill one, and the germ was stuck.

"So the stamp was not only for you," she said. "It was for the milkman and the neighbours, and for everyone in the next village too."

"For all of them," said Muthassi. "That is why Thankamma Chechi cycled from house to house. Every stamp was a gap in the road the germ wanted to travel."

On Thursday, Anu stood in the line outside the school hall. The health worker was a young woman with a steady hand. "It will sting for a moment," she said. Anu looked at the small raised dot on her own arm, from a vaccine she had as a baby. She took a breath, counted to three, and looked away. It stung. Then it was done.

That evening she showed Muthassi the plaster. Then she took the rice flour and drew the dots again, on the doorstep, with a ring of white around one red dot.

Muthassi touched her round scar and smiled. "Our whole village was in that picture," she said. "And now you are in it too."

**For the parent:** A vaccine shows the body a safe picture of a germ, so the body is ready when the real germ arrives. When the people around an ill person are protected, the germ has nowhere to go, and the chain stops.

- Ask: Why did Thankamma Chechi vaccinate the milkman and the neighbours, and not only Gopi's family?
- Ask: How could Muthassi sleep in Gopi's room for two weeks and stay well?
- Ask: What did the ring of white flour show Anu about how a germ spreads?
- Ask: Anu was afraid of the needle at the start. What changed her mind, and was it a fear or a fact that changed?

Add 2 teaspoons of salt to the Salt glass. 5. Add 4 teaspoons of vinegar to the Vinegar glass. 6. Add the crushed garlic to the Garlic glass. 7. Add 2 teaspoons of turmeric to the Turmeric glass. 8. Stir each glass again for 10 seconds. 9. Write down the start time.  
  *Parent says:* The Plain glass gets nothing extra. That is our comparison. Stir each glass for the same time. Now write the time, and start the clock.  
  *Child mode:* Add sugar and yeast to every glass. Add the test things. Write the start time.
  - Note: A grown-up crushes one garlic clove on the board with the flat of the knife until it is a paste, and puts all of it into the Garlic glass. Wash the spoon between glasses. The Salt glass may not dissolve fully, and that is fine.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the glasses every 5 minutes. 2. Draw what you see in each. 3. Wait until 20 minutes have passed since you stirred.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the glasses every five minutes. Which glass starts to foam first? Which stays flat? Draw what you see.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the glasses every five minutes. Draw what you see.
  - Ask: What do you think is making the bubbles and foam? (good: The yeast is making gas.; The yeast is eating the sugar and breathing out bubbles.)
  - Note: Yeast is slow in the first minutes. Bubbles usually start to appear by 5 to 10 minutes in the Plain glass. If nothing at all has happened in the Plain glass after 15 minutes, the water was probably too cold or too hot, or the yeast is old. Start again with fresh yeast and hand-warm water.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 20 minutes, hold the ruler beside each glass. 2. Measure the height of the foam, from the water up to the top of the foam, in millimetres. 3. Write the number in the table. 4. Wave the air above each glass towards your nose and write what you smell.  
  *Parent says:* Measure from the water up to the top of the foam. If there is no foam, write zero. Then wave the air gently towards your nose.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the foam in each glass. Write the number. Wave the smell to your nose.
  - Note: Expected pattern: a thick foam on the Plain glass and a similar one on the Turmeric glass, much less on the Salt glass, and less than Plain on the Vinegar and Garlic glasses. Your own result is the real one. If the glasses behave differently, write it down. Different yeast, water and room temperature can change the numbers.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why. 3. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think happened? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened that way?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What I think, what I saw, and why? (good: What I think: strong salt and vinegar slow microbes. What I saw: less foam in those glasses. Why: they make it hard for yeast to live.)
  - Check: The Salt glass had much less foam than the Plain glass. What does this show? → **Strong salt slowed or stopped the yeast**
- **s7 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour all the glasses down the sink. 2. Wash the glasses and spoons with soap. 3. Wash your hands with soap, because of the garlic. 4. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* A real germ-killer must hurt the germ and not hurt you. Which of our glasses held back the yeast? Would that thing be safe to put into a body?  
  *Child mode:* Pour the glasses out. Wash everything with soap. Wash your hands.
  - Think: A medicine must hurt the germ and not you. Why can't we drink strong salt water or vinegar to cure an infection?
### e2 · Rice and Dal: what is left after the medicine? (35 min)
- **question:** A medicine kills nearly all the ordinary germs in a body, but a few tough germs survive it. What does the crowd of germs look like after three days with the medicine, and after three days without it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After three days, how many of the 40 germs will be tough ones with the medicine, and how many without? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both plates start the same, with 38 ordinary germs and 2 tough germs. Both follow the same rules each night. Only one thing is different. Plate A gets the medicine every day, and Plate B never does. When the germs are picked for the crowding step, you pick without looking, so that nothing is chosen on purpose. If you can, play the game twice, because chance changes the numbers a little.
- **safety:** Keep rice and dal away from babies and small children, who may put grains in their mouth or nose. Do not eat the grains afterwards if they have been handled a lot. Wash your hands when you finish.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the two plates at the end of Day 3, with the rice and dal grains drawn in two colours.
- **results table:** Day | Plate A (medicine): ordinary germs | Plate A (medicine): tough germs | Plate B (no medicine): ordinary germs | Plate B (no medicine): tough germs
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the rice, the dal and the two plates. 2. Say that rice grains are ordinary germs and dal grains are tough germs. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. 4. Write the guesses at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* These grains are pretend germs. Rice is the ordinary germ. Dal is a germ who happens to be tough against a medicine. Plate A will get the medicine every day. Plate B will not. After three days, how many tough germs will each plate have? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Rice is the ordinary germ. Dal is the tough germ. Guess how many tough germs each plate will have. Why?
  - Ask: What makes you think the plate with medicine will have more tough germs? (good: The medicine kills the ordinary ones, so only the tough ones are left.; The medicine makes the tough ones stronger.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Some children think the medicine makes germs tougher. Write that down and let the game answer it.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count 38 rice grains on to Plate A. 2. Add 2 dal grains. 3. Do the same on Plate B. 4. Write the start numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Each plate is a body with 40 germs. Most are ordinary. Two are tough, and they were already a little different before any medicine arrived. Each body has food for only 40 germs.  
  *Child mode:* Put 38 rice grains and 2 dal grains on each plate.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play Day 1 on Plate B, with no medicine. 1. Night: count the rice grains on the plate and add the same number from the supply. 2. Do the same with the dal grains. 3. Crowding: put all the grains in the bowl and mix. 4. Look away and pick 40 grains, one at a time, back on to the plate. 5. Count the rice and the dal on the plate and write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* At night every germ splits in two, so each kind doubles. But the body has food for only 40 germs, so the rest die. We pick the 40 without looking, so the picking is fair.  
  *Child mode:* Add the same number of grains to each kind. Mix. Pick 40 without looking. Count.
  - Note: On Plate B there are 76 rice and 4 dal after the night, which makes 80 grains. You pick 40 of the 80. The dal share usually stays small, around 2 out of 40. Chance changes it, so 1 or 3 is normal.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play Day 1 on Plate A, with the medicine. 1. Night: double each kind. 2. Crowding: if there are more than 40 grains, mix and pick 40 without looking. 3. Medicine: put the rice grains into piles of ten. Keep one grain from each pile and take away all the rest, including any left-over rice. 4. The medicine does not touch the dal. 5. Count the rice and the dal and write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* The medicine kills nearly all the ordinary germs. In each pile of ten, only one survives. But the medicine does not kill the tough germs, so every dal grain stays.  
  *Child mode:* Double each kind. Pick 40. Make piles of ten rice grains. Keep one from each pile.
  - Note: After the medicine, Plate A usually holds about 3 rice grains and about 2 dal grains, in a plate that had 40 grains this morning. Many children are surprised how empty the plate looks.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play Day 2 and Day 3 on both plates, with the same rules. Write the table after each day. Remember, only Plate A gets the medicine.  
  *Parent says:* Same rules each day. Double each kind. Pick 40 only if there are more than 40. Plate A gets the medicine again. Write the numbers each day.  
  *Child mode:* Play Day 2 and Day 3 on both plates. Write the numbers each day.
  - Note: On Plate A, a typical run is 3 rice and 2 dal after Day 1, then 0 rice and 4 dal after Day 2, then 0 rice and 8 dal after Day 3. On Plate B, a typical run keeps about 38 rice and 2 dal each day, though chance may give 1, 3 or 4 dal. About one game in five, the dal on Plate B disappear by chance. That is a real result. Write it down and play again.
  - Check: On Plate A, by Day 2 there are almost no rice grains left. Why do the dal grains keep growing in number? → **Each night the surviving tough germs split in two, and the medicine does not remove them**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the grains on both plates. 2. Look at the table together. 3. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think happened? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened that way? Which plate holds more tough germs, and which has more room for them?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What I think, what I saw, and why? (good: What I think: the medicine does not make germs tough, it removes the ordinary ones. What I saw: Plate A ended with mostly dal, and Plate B stayed mostly rice. Why: with the ordinary germs gone, the tough ones had all the room and food.)
  - Ask: On Plate B, why did the dal stay so few? (good: The rice used up the food and room, so the dal could not take over.; Every night half of the grains were picked away, rice and dal alike.)
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the fact aloud. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Real bacteria work like this. A crowd of billions has a few that are tough before any medicine arrives. In 1952, Esther Lederberg showed this with a velvet stamp. The medicine does not teach them. It only decides which ones carry on. Why might a doctor avoid giving an antibiotic when it is not needed?  
  *Child mode:* Real germs work like this. The medicine only chooses which ones carry on.
  - Check: A family takes an antibiotic for a cold, which is caused by a virus. Why is this a bad idea? → **It cannot help the cold, and it gives tough bacteria in the body a chance to take over**
  - Think: In the game, who made the tough germs: the medicine, or chance? Where were the dal grains before the medicine began?

### a1 · The Medicine Box Detective (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up takes out the medicines and holds them. A child reads the labels but does not open any bottle or tablet strip, and never tastes anything. Keep medicines out of reach of small children. Put expired medicines aside for a grown-up to return to a chemist or health centre, and never flush them or throw them in a drain.
- **record:** A table with columns: name, what it is for, does it kill a germ or ease a sign, and the expiry date.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up brings the medicine box to the table and lays out everything. 2. Ask your child to guess how many of the medicines kill germs. 3. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look at what is in our medicine box. How many of these do you think kill germs? How many only ease a sign, like pain or fever? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up lays out the medicines. Guess how many kill germs. Why?
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the front of each packet: the name, what it is for, and the expiry date. 2. Write each in the table. 3. Mark whether it kills a germ, eases a sign, or is for the skin.  
  *Parent says:* Read the name and what it is for. The line that says what it contains tells you more than the brand name. Does the medicine kill a germ, or only ease a sign like pain, fever or an itch?  
  *Child mode:* Read each label. Write the name and what it is for. Do not open anything.
  - Note: Most home boxes hold medicines that ease signs, such as fever and pain medicine, plus ORS, creams and an antiseptic. Antibiotics should not be in a home box, because they are given for a particular illness. If there are some left over, ask a doctor or pharmacist what to do with them.
  - Check: A tube says it is a cream for itchy skin. Does it kill the germ that causes a cold? → **No, it eases a sign on the skin**
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find any medicine whose expiry date has passed. 2. Put those in a separate bag for the grown-up to return to a chemist. 3. Check that the box is out of reach of small children.  
  *Parent says:* A medicine can lose strength after its date, and some can become harmful. Which of ours have run out of date? Is our box somewhere a little child cannot reach?  
  *Child mode:* Find any medicine past its date. A grown-up puts those in a bag.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the medicines in each group and talk about the result.  
  *Parent says:* How many kill a germ? How many only ease a sign? Was your guess close? Why do you think a doctor chooses very carefully when to use a germ-killing medicine?  
  *Child mode:* Count the piles. Was your guess close?
  - Ask: Which medicines in the box do something about the germ? (good: Perhaps only the antiseptic for the skin.; Few or none. Most medicines at home ease signs.)
### a2 · Memory Cards: how a vaccine saves time (25 min)
- **record:** A table with the four times in seconds: first meeting, second meeting, no vaccine, after a vaccine. Then draw a bar for each time.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the game. 2. Ask your child to guess how many times faster the search is after a vaccine. 3. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Your body has defenders that fit one germ each. You are the defenders now. A germ card will arrive, and you must find the matching antibody card. How much faster will you be if a vaccine showed you the germ first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You are the body's defenders. A germ card arrives. Find the matching antibody. Guess how fast.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write a different three-letter code on each of 30 cards. Use codes like KRT, PAM or LOB, and no two the same. 2. The helper secretly chooses three codes and writes each on a germ card. 3. Spread the 30 antibody cards face up on the table.  
  *Parent says:* Each antibody card has its own code. Each germ card matches exactly one of them. The helper picks three germs in secret, so you will not know which cards to look for.  
  *Child mode:* Write 30 different codes on 30 cards. The helper writes three germ cards.
  - Note: Help your child write the codes quickly. The three germ cards must each match one antibody card exactly, and the helper must keep them hidden. Name the germ cards 1, 2 and 3.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Germ 1 arrives for the first time. The helper shows the card and starts the stopwatch. 2. Find the matching antibody card. 3. Stop the watch and write the time. 4. Put the matching card in a separate memory pile. 5. Germ 1 arrives again. Search the memory pile first. Write that time too.  
  *Parent says:* A germ arrives for the first time. Find the match as fast as you can. Now the same germ arrives again. Where will you look first?  
  *Child mode:* Germ 1 arrives. Find the match. Time it. Now Germ 1 arrives again. Time it.
  - Check: Why is the second meeting with Germ 1 so much faster than the first? → **The matching card is already in the memory pile**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The helper shows Germ 2 as a safe picture, which stands for a vaccine. Find the match with no timing and put it in the memory pile. 2. Germ 3 arrives for the first time, with no vaccine. Time the search. 3. Germ 2 arrives for real. Time the search. 4. Write both times.  
  *Parent says:* The helper shows you a safe picture of Germ 2. That is the vaccine. You find the match and keep it safe. Now two germs arrive for real, Germ 3 with no warning and Germ 2 after the vaccine. Which will you find faster?  
  *Child mode:* A safe picture of Germ 2 is a vaccine. Find the match. Then time Germ 3 and Germ 2.
  - Note: Expect the search for Germ 3 to take as long as for Germ 1, often 15 to 30 seconds. The search for Germ 2 after the vaccine takes a second or two. Your child's times will differ, and that is fine.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a bar for each of the four times. 2. Look at the bars together. 3. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which bar is tallest? Which are shortest? What did the vaccine do? What do you think happened? What did you see? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw four bars. Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What did the safe picture give your search? (good: It let me find the match before the germ came, so I could look in the memory pile.; It saved time when the real germ arrived.)
  - Ask: In real life, why is it better to learn the match from a vaccine than from the real germ? (good: With the real germ, the person is ill while the body searches.; The vaccine is safe, so the body can learn without getting ill.)

### a4 · ORS: make it, taste it, and see why it works (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water and lets it cool. Measure the salt and sugar exactly, because too much salt can harm. Taste only a small sip from each cup. This home recipe is for tasting and learning. For a child who is really ill, use a packet of ORS from a chemist or health centre and ask a doctor. Throw away any ORS that is more than a day old.
- **record:** A table with three rows: plain water, sugar water and ORS. Write what each tastes like.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up boils 1 litre of water and lets it cool. 2. Wash your hands with soap. 3. Ask your child to guess what ORS will taste like.  
  *Parent says:* When someone has loose motions, the body loses water and salts. A drink of water, salt and sugar puts them back. What do you think it will taste like? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Boil water and let it cool. Wash your hands. Guess what ORS tastes like.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the cooled water into the clean jug. 2. Add 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt. 3. Stir until both have dissolved. 4. Pour a little plain water into one cup, and a little of the mixture into another.  
  *Parent says:* Six teaspoons of sugar and half a teaspoon of salt in one litre of clean water. Measure them level, not heaped. Too much salt is not safe.  
  *Child mode:* Mix 6 spoons of sugar and half a spoon of salt in the water. Stir well.
  - Note: Make a third cup of plain sugar water by stirring one level teaspoon of sugar into a cup of the cooled water, with no salt. Keep the jug covered and throw away anything left after a day.
  - Check: Why must the salt and sugar be measured exactly? → **Too much salt can harm, and too little will not replace what the body lost**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take a small sip from each cup. 2. Write how each tastes. 3. If you have a packet of ORS, make a cup of it and compare.  
  *Parent says:* Take a small sip of each. Which tastes sweet? Which tastes a little salty? ORS tastes slightly salty, about as salty as tears.  
  *Child mode:* Take a small sip from each cup. Write how each tastes.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about why ORS has both salt and sugar, and how it is given to a child who is ill.  
  *Parent says:* In the gut, sugar and salt are taken into the body together, and water follows them. Without the sugar, much less salt and water get in. A child with loose motions takes small sips every few minutes, even if she vomits. If she cannot keep it down, she needs a doctor.  
  *Child mode:* Sugar and salt go in together, and water follows. Small sips every few minutes help.
  - Ask: Why does ORS include sugar if the body is losing salt? (good: The sugar helps the gut take in salt and water together.; Salt and sugar carry the water into the body.)
  - Think: ORS does not kill the germ. How does it save a life?
### a5 · A Day of Temperatures: what is a fever? (20 min)
- **safety:** Use a digital thermometer only, never a glass one that can break. Clean the thermometer with soap and water or a cotton ball with a little rubbing alcohol, kept by a grown-up, before and after each use.
- **record:** A table with three times for each person, and a line drawing of the readings across the day.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the thermometer and switch it on. 2. A grown-up cleans the tip. 3. Place the tip in the middle of the armpit and hold the arm down against the body until the beep. 4. Read the number and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* A body's temperature is about 37 degrees Celsius. Is it exactly 37 all day? Guess. Then we will measure the same two people in the morning, afternoon and evening.  
  *Child mode:* Put the thermometer in the armpit. Wait for the beep. Read the number.
  - Ask: Do you think a well person's temperature stays exactly the same all day? (good: Yes, it is always 37.; No, it changes a little.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the temperature of two well people in the morning. 2. Take it again in the afternoon. 3. Take it again in the evening. 4. Write every reading in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Take each person's temperature three times today. The armpit gives a slightly lower number than the mouth. Use the same place every time.  
  *Child mode:* Measure two people three times today. Write each number.
  - Note: Most well people read between about 36 and 37.5 degrees under the arm. The numbers are usually lowest in the early morning and highest in the late afternoon or evening. The difference is often half a degree or more. Do not take the temperature right after a hot meal, a bath or running.
  - Check: A well person reads 36.4 in the morning and 37.0 in the evening. What does this show? → **Normal temperature goes up and down a little during the day**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a line through the three readings for each person. 2. Look at the pattern. 3. Talk about the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Your readings go up and down a little in a day. A fever is a temperature of about 38 degrees or more. Why do you think the brain raises the body's temperature when germs get in?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line through the three readings. Does it go up and down?
  - Ask: Why might the body raise its temperature when germs get in? (good: It may help the defenders work better.; Some germs grow worse when it is warmer.)
  - Think: Scientists are still studying how much a fever helps. What would you test to find out?

### c1 · The Vaccine Carrier: keep an ice cube cold for two hours (40 min)
- **problem:** Many vaccines must be kept cold, between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, all the way from the factory to the village. Design a box from things at home that keeps one ice cube from melting for two hours in a warm room.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts any cardboard or thermocol with a knife or scissors. Ice can hurt bare skin if held for long, so pick it up with a spoon or a cloth. Do not put the boxes in the sun or near the stove.
- **your turn:** Sketch your design. Build it and test it with an ice cube for two hours. Measure the melted water. Change one thing, such as the thickness of the wrapping, and test again. Which design kept the ice longest, and why?
- **constraints:** Use only things from home, such as a tiffin box, a thermocol box, newspaper, foil, cloth or a cardboard box.; Use an ice cube of the same size in every box, from the same ice tray.; The ice cube must sit in a small steel katori or a plastic cup so the melted water stays in one place.; Keep the lid closed for the whole two hours, and do not put the box in the sun.
- **general:** Wrap the box in thick layers of newspaper and cloth, which trap air and slow the heat coming in.; Fill the space around the katori with crumpled newspaper so there is no big air gap.; Use a thermocol box, which is a good insulator and light to carry.
- **creative:** Put the katori in a small box inside a bigger box, with newspaper between, for two walls of insulation.; Line the inside with foil with the shiny side in, and wrap the outside in a thick towel.; Add a second ice cube beside the first as an ice pack, and measure only the first.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Look at the constraints. Ask your child to sketch two or three designs.  
  *Parent says:* A vaccine can spoil if it gets warm. Health workers carry it in a special cold box. How could you keep one ice cube from melting for two hours? Draw some ideas first.  
  *Child mode:* Draw two or three ideas for a cold box.
  - Ask: What makes ice melt faster? (good: Heat coming in from the warm air.; Heat coming in through the sides of the box.)
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build each box with the things you chose. Put one ice cube in a katori, put the katori inside the box, and close the lid. Write the start time. Make one box a plain tiffin with no wrapping as the comparison.  
  *Parent says:* Put an ice cube of the same size in every box. Close the lid. Write the time. The plain box with no wrapping shows us how much the wrapping helps.  
  *Child mode:* Build your boxes. Put an ice cube in each. Close the lids. Write the time.
  - Note: Take the ice cubes out of the tray at the last moment. Use cubes of the same size. Keep the boxes in the same shady place in the room.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After two hours, take each katori out. Pour the melted water into a measuring spoon or a small cup. Write how much melted in each box.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the melted water from each katori into the same spoon. The box with the least water kept the ice the best.  
  *Child mode:* Pour out the melted water from each katori. Measure it. Write the numbers.
  - Check: One box kept the ice cube from melting better than the others. What probably made the difference? → **It slowed the heat from the room getting in**
  - Wait: Wait two hours before measuring.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what to change and test again if you have time.  
  *Parent says:* Which box was best? Which part of the design helped? What would you change? Why does a cold box matter for a vaccine?  
  *Child mode:* Which box was best? What would you change?
  - Ask: Why do vaccines need to be kept cold on the way to a village? (good: Heat can spoil the vaccine so it no longer teaches the body.; The vaccine is made of fragile pieces of germs.)
### c2 · The Medicine Reminder Chart (35 min)
- **problem:** Design a chart that helps a grandparent take the right medicine at the right time, using only what the doctor's label says. The chart must be easy to read from one metre away and must be impossible to mix up.
- **your turn:** Now design your own medicine reminder chart. Draw it, make it, and ask an elder to read it from one metre away. What did they find hard? What did you change?
- **constraints:** Copy only what the doctor's label and the pharmacist say, and never change a dose or a time.; The chart must be readable from one metre away by someone who does not read small print.; A grown-up checks the finished chart against every label.; Use things from home such as chart paper, pens and tape.
- **general:** A table with the medicine name down the side and morning, afternoon and night across the top, with a tick box in each cell.; Different colours for morning, afternoon and night.; Big, bold letters, with a picture of a sun, a plate or a moon for each time.
- **creative:** A row of pockets, one for each time of day, with the tablets for that time put in by a grown-up.; A chart with a sticker for each day, so the person can see at a glance what has been taken.; A chart with a picture of what each medicine is for, in a few words, so it is not just a name.
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up reads a real medicine label with your child. Look for the name, the dose, the times and how long to take it. Write the times in a list.  
  *Parent says:* A medicine label tells us the name, how much, how often and for how long. A mix-up can be dangerous. Why do you think a chart helps?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up reads the label with you. Write down the times.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sketch two chart designs on paper.  
  *Parent says:* Draw two ways to show which medicine goes at which time. How will a person who reads slowly know at a glance?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two ideas. Use colours and pictures.
- **s3 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Make the best chart on the chart paper. A grown-up checks it against every label. Then ask an elder to read it from one metre away.  
  *Parent says:* Make your chart. Then a grown-up checks it, line by line, with the label. Last, ask an elder to read it from a metre away. What do they find hard?  
  *Child mode:* Make the chart. A grown-up checks it. An elder reads it.
  - Check: Why must a grown-up check the chart against the doctor's label? → **A small copying mistake in a dose or time could harm the person**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what to change.  
  *Parent says:* What did the elder find hard to read? What would you change? What other things at home could use a chart like this?  
  *Child mode:* What was hard to read? What would you change?

## Worksheets
### Germs, Medicines and Vaccines (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these illnesses is NOT caused by a germ?  
   Options: Measles / Diabetes / Ringworm / Typhoid  
   **Answer:** Diabetes. *Why:* Measles is caused by a virus, ringworm by a fungus and typhoid by bacteria. Diabetes comes from the way the body handles sugar, so nobody can catch it.
2. (sort) Sort each illness by its cause.  
   **Answer:** typhoid → Bacteria; a common cold → Virus; ringworm → Fungus; a broken arm → No germ; measles → Virus; tuberculosis → Bacteria; anaemia from too little iron → No germ; tetanus → Bacteria. *Why:* Each cause needs a different medicine. Typhoid, tuberculosis and tetanus come from bacteria. Colds and measles come from viruses. Ringworm comes from a fungus. A broken arm and anaemia have no germ at all.
3. (truefalse) A medicine that kills bacteria also kills viruses.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Antibiotics attack parts that bacteria have, such as a cell wall, and viruses do not have those parts.
4. (fill) The time between germs getting in and the first sign of illness is called the ________ period.  
   **Answer:** ['incubation']. *Why:* During the incubation period the germs are multiplying, and the person usually feels well.
5. (match) Match each medicine to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Antibiotic → Kills bacteria or stops them multiplying; ORS → Replaces water and salts lost in loose motions; Fever medicine such as paracetamol → Eases fever and pain but does not kill the germ; Vaccine → Teaches the body before the germ arrives; Antifungal cream → Works on a fungus. *Why:* Medicines do different jobs. Only some of them act on the germ. Others replace what the body has lost, ease signs or prevent illness.
6. (mcq) A child has a runny nose and a mild cough for two days. What is the best help?  
   Options: Rest and warm drinks / An antibiotic, to be safe / Tablets left over from last time  
   **Answer:** Rest and warm drinks. *Why:* A cold is caused by a virus, and the defenders clear it in about a week. An antibiotic does nothing to a virus, and left-over tablets may be the wrong medicine.
7. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, to show what happens in a body with an infection.  
   **Answer:** ['Germs get in and begin to multiply', 'The person feels well for a day or two', 'Defenders send alarm signals and the temperature rises', 'The person feels hot and tired', 'Antibodies clear the germs and the person gets better']. *Why:* The germs multiply quietly first. Then the alarm signals raise the temperature and make the person tired. Last, the antibodies clear the germs.
8. (truefalse) A person who takes many antibiotics becomes resistant to them.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is the bacteria that become resistant, not the person. Tough bacteria survive and multiply.
9. (write) Why is the body usually faster the second time it meets the same germ?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that after the first meeting some white blood cells stay on as memory cells. They remember the shape of the germ and make the right antibody quickly, often before the person feels ill.. *Why:* Memory cells are why most people do not get chickenpox twice. A vaccine uses the same trick, so the first meeting is safe.
10. (mcq) A vaccine is made from a killed germ. Why does it not make you ill?  
   Options: A killed germ cannot multiply, but the body still learns its shape / The vaccine is only water / The body is too strong to be ill  
   **Answer:** A killed germ cannot multiply, but the body still learns its shape. *Why:* A killed germ is only a shape for the defenders to look at. It cannot multiply or make a poison. The vaccine is not water, and the body learns because the shape is real.
11. (fill) A tiny protein that fits one germ, like a key fits a lock, is called an ________.  
   **Answer:** ['antibody']. *Why:* An antibody fits one particular germ, sticks to them and marks them to be removed.
12. (draw) Draw nine houses in a square, three rows of three. Colour one house red for an ill house. Then colour the neighbours of that house to show vaccinated houses, and show where the illness is stopped.  
   **Answer:** A drawing in which the vaccinated houses surround the ill house, and arrows from the ill house are stopped at them.. *Why:* This is a ring. A germ spreads only to the neighbours within reach, so a ring of protected houses leaves it nowhere to go.
13. (mcq) Which of these is a danger sign that needs a doctor at once?  
   Options: A child cannot drink and is very sleepy / A runny nose for two days / A child sneezing in the morning  
   **Answer:** A child cannot drink and is very sleepy. *Why:* Not being able to drink and being very sleepy can mean the body is in trouble. A runny nose and a few sneezes are common in a cold.
14. (truefalse) Left-over tablets from last month are safe to give for a new illness.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A new illness may have a different cause. The packet may be the wrong dose or the wrong medicine, and it may be out of date.
15. (perspective) Meera has a cold and a sore throat. Her doctor says no antibiotic is needed.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with the doctor and Farhan: antibiotics do not work on viruses, and they can leave tough bacteria behind. It also says that Meera is right to want to get better, and that rest and drinks are the real help. If the throat gets worse, the doctor can test for bacteria.. *Why:* Wanting to get better is reasonable. The best help is the one that matches the cause.
16. (write) ORS has no germ-killer in it. How does it still save the lives of children with loose motions?  
   **Answer:** Loose motions make the body lose water and salts. ORS puts them back, so the body does not dry out while the defenders clear the germs.. *Why:* Many children die of losing water, not from the germ. Keeping the body topped up gives the defenders time.
17. (mcq) Almost everyone in a school is vaccinated against measles. A baby in the neighbourhood is too young for the vaccine. Why is the baby safer?  
   Options: The germ keeps meeting protected people and rarely reaches the baby / Babies have a stronger body than children / Measles cannot infect babies  
   **Answer:** The germ keeps meeting protected people and rarely reaches the baby. *Why:* Each vaccinated person is a broken link in the chain, so the germ has a hard time reaching anyone. Babies can catch measles, and they can become very ill.
### Resistance, Vaccines and Evidence (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Penicillin weakens the wall of a bacterium so that she bursts. Why does penicillin not harm the cells of a human?  
   Options: Human cells have no wall for the medicine to weaken / Human cells are bigger / The medicine is washed out by the stomach  
   **Answer:** Human cells have no wall for the medicine to weaken. *Why:* A medicine that attacks a wall has nothing to attack in a human cell, which has no wall. This is how an antibiotic can hurt a germ and leave you alone.
2. (truefalse) Taking an antibiotic for a cold can make the cold virus resistant.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An antibiotic does nothing to a virus, so the virus cannot become resistant to the antibiotic. But the antibiotic can still leave tough bacteria behind in the body.
3. (mcq) Two plates start the same, with 38 ordinary germs and 2 tough germs. Plate A gets a medicine every day that removes 9 out of every 10 ordinary germs. Plate B gets none. After two days, which describes Plate A best?  
   Options: Almost no ordinary germs are left, and the tough germs have multiplied / There are more ordinary germs than at the start / The germs are the same as Plate B  
   **Answer:** Almost no ordinary germs are left, and the tough germs have multiplied. *Why:* The medicine removes most ordinary germs each day, and the tough ones survive and double each night. Plate B keeps its crowd of ordinary germs, which holds the tough ones back.
4. (fill) When the germs that survive a medicine are the ones that carry on, this is called natural ________.  
   **Answer:** ['selection']. *Why:* Natural selection means that the living beings who survive and multiply are the ones whose kind carries on. It shaped every living being, and it shapes germs too.
5. (write) A child adds salt, vinegar, garlic and turmeric to separate glasses of yeast and measures the foam after 20 minutes. Why must she also set up a glass with nothing added?  
   **Answer:** The glass with nothing added shows how much foam yeast makes normally. Without it, she would not know whether the other glasses made more or less foam than usual.. *Why:* A comparison glass is a control. Scientists use one so that any change can be put down to the thing they added.
6. (sort) Which kind of medicine would a doctor choose? Sort each illness.  
   **Answer:** a throat infection confirmed as bacterial → Antibiotic; ringworm → Antifungal; an ordinary cold → No germ-killer: rest and drinks; flu in a person at high risk → Antiviral; a chest infection caused by bacteria → Antibiotic; a fungus infection of the foot → Antifungal. *Why:* A doctor matches the medicine to the cause. Antibiotics are for bacteria, antifungal medicines for fungi, and an antiviral for a few viruses. For an ordinary cold, no germ-killer helps.
7. (mcq) A flu vaccine is changed every year. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Flu viruses change a little, so last year's antibodies may not fit this year's virus / The vaccine goes off after a year in the fridge / Flu is a bacterium  
   **Answer:** Flu viruses change a little, so last year's antibodies may not fit this year's virus. *Why:* Antibodies fit one shape, like a key in a lock. If the virus changes their shape, the old key may not fit. So each year's vaccine is matched to the viruses that are going around.
8. (write) A neighbour says, "Nobody around here gets measles, so my child does not need the vaccine." Using herd protection, explain what she may be missing.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that measles is rare around there because most children are vaccinated. If many stop being vaccinated, the germ will find enough unprotected children to spread to, and measles will come back.. *Why:* Herd protection works only while enough people stay vaccinated. The reason nobody sees measles is the vaccine.
9. (truefalse) Herd protection means that people who are not vaccinated can never catch the illness.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Herd protection makes it less likely, because the germ meets fewer people to pass to. It is not a guarantee.
10. (mcq) About 95 out of every 100 people need to be protected to stop measles spreading. In a class of 40, about how many children is that?  
   Options: 38 / 30 / 20  
   **Answer:** 38. *Why:* 95 out of 100 is the same as 19 out of 20. A class of 40 is two lots of 20, so 2 times 19 is 38.
11. (fill) The rabies vaccine can be given after a dog bite and still protect, because the virus travels ________ to the brain.  
   **Answer:** ['slowly']. *Why:* The virus takes weeks to reach the brain, so the vaccine has time to teach the body. Even so, a person must get to a doctor at once.
12. (perspective) Doctors disagree about how long a course of antibiotics should be.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both can be true for different illnesses, and that families should follow the doctor who knows the illness and ask when to stop. Families should not set the length themselves.. *Why:* There is no single answer for all illnesses. A good response uses the evidence and respects that the doctor has the details of the case.
13. (order) Put these in order to show how tough germs take over a body that takes an antibiotic.  
   **Answer:** ['A few germs in the crowd are already tough by chance', 'A person takes an antibiotic', 'The ordinary germs die', 'The tough germs multiply with plenty of room and food', 'Most of the germs in the body are now tough']. *Why:* The tough germs are there first. The medicine removes their competitors. Then they multiply into the space.
14. (mcq) Why can taking an antibiotic cause loose motions?  
   Options: It kills some of the helpful bacteria in the gut as well / It is made of salt / It makes the body drink too much  
   **Answer:** It kills some of the helpful bacteria in the gut as well. *Why:* The gut is home to trillions of helpful bacteria. An antibiotic cannot always tell the helpful ones from the harmful ones, so a change in the gut can cause loose motions.
15. (write) A bacteriophage attacks only one kind of bacterium. Give one reason this could be good for a medicine and one reason it could be hard.  
   **Answer:** Good: it leaves the helpful bacteria and the person's cells alone. Hard: a doctor must find out exactly which bacterium causes the infection, and must have the right phage ready.. *Why:* A narrow tool does less harm but needs more knowledge to use. That is one reason phages are still being tested.
16. (draw) Draw how a velvet stamp copies a pattern of dots from one dish of bacteria to two new dishes. Show which dish has the medicine in it.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of a dish with dots, a round velvet pad pressed on it, then pressed on two new dishes where the same pattern of dots appears, one with a label for the medicine.. *Why:* The stamp copies the position of each colony, so scientists can compare the same colony on a dish with and without the medicine.
17. (mcq) A doctor says a throat infection needs an antibiotic only after a test. Why wait for the test?  
   Options: A sore throat looks the same whether bacteria or a virus cause the pain / Tests make the medicine stronger / The doctor wants the child to wait  
   **Answer:** A sore throat looks the same whether bacteria or a virus cause the pain. *Why:* The cause decides the medicine, and you cannot tell the cause by looking. A test avoids giving an antibiotic that cannot help.
### Thinking Like a Scientist About Medicine (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A scientist stamps a dish of bacteria on to new dishes with a medicine in them. Resistant dots grow in the same places on every copy, and those same places on the first dish also hold resistant bacteria. Which finding would have shown that the medicine made the bacteria resistant?  
   Options: Resistant dots appearing in different places on each copy, and none on the first dish / Resistant dots in the same places on every copy / Every dot on the first dish being resistant  
   **Answer:** Resistant dots appearing in different places on each copy, and none on the first dish. *Why:* If the medicine caused the change, the resistant dots would appear at random on each copy and would not be found in the first dish. The same places each time show that they were there already.
2. (write) A trial gives a new vaccine to 1,000 children and a dummy injection to another 1,000. In the next year, 5 vaccinated children and 50 dummy children catch the illness. What does this show, and how big is the effect?  
   **Answer:** The vaccinated group had far fewer cases, 5 against 50. That is 10 times fewer, or 9 out of 10 fewer cases (90 percent fewer).. *Why:* Comparing a group that gets the vaccine with one that does not shows what the vaccine does. 50 minus 5 is 45, and 45 out of 50 is 9 out of 10.
3. (mcq) Why does a trial use a dummy injection for the comparison group?  
   Options: So that both groups expect the same thing, and any difference comes from the vaccine / Because the vaccine is too expensive / To make the trial longer  
   **Answer:** So that both groups expect the same thing, and any difference comes from the vaccine. *Why:* If one group knew they had nothing, they might behave differently, and that could change the result. A dummy keeps everything the same except the vaccine.
4. (perspective) A new vaccine is made, but there is only enough for a few thousand people.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a plan and gives a reason, such as protecting the most vulnerable or breaking the most chains. It names evidence that would decide, such as how much each group spreads the germ and how likely each is to become very ill.. *Why:* Both plans protect people in different ways. This is a question about fairness as well as science, and a good answer says what it values.
5. (write) A friend says, "My grandmother took antibiotics for years, so her body is resistant now." What is right and what is wrong?  
   **Answer:** Wrong: the person does not become resistant. The bacteria in her body can become resistant. Right: taking antibiotics often does make resistant bacteria more likely.. *Why:* The word resistant belongs to the germ. Careful words help us think clearly about what is happening.
6. (truefalse) Bacteria can pass genes for resistance to other kinds of bacteria.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Bacteria can hand pieces of their genes to each other, even across kinds, so resistance can spread fast.
7. (mcq) In one town, measles cases fall every year for ten years. In the last year, the number of vaccinated children falls too. What should a careful scientist say?  
   Options: Cases may rise soon, because fewer children are now protected / Measles has gone for good / The vaccine has stopped working  
   **Answer:** Cases may rise soon, because fewer children are now protected. *Why:* The fall in cases came from past vaccination. If protection drops now, the chains will have more links again. A careful scientist watches the trend and the cause.
8. (write) Alexander Fleming saw a clear ring around a mould on a dish of bacteria. What could a scientist do next to find out whether the mould caused the ring?  
   **Answer:** Grow the mould alone, then put some liquid from the mould on fresh dishes of the bacteria and see if rings appear. Also use dishes with no mould liquid as a comparison, and repeat the test.. *Why:* A ring next to a mould is a clue, not a proof. A test with the mould's liquid alone, and a comparison dish, tells whether the mould is the cause.
9. (sort) Which of these protects mainly the person, and which also protects others?  
   **Answer:** taking a painkiller for your own headache → Protects mainly the person; a measles vaccine → Also protects other people; covering your cough with your elbow → Also protects other people; a plaster on your own cut → Protects mainly the person; staying at home with flu → Also protects other people; wearing sunglasses on a bright day → Protects mainly the person. *Why:* Some actions protect only you. Actions that stop a germ passing on, like a vaccine, an elbow cough or staying at home, protect everyone down the chain.
10. (draw) Draw a germ chain for a bacterial illness that spreads through water. Mark three places where clean water, a toilet or a vaccine could break the chain.  
   **Answer:** A chain of pictures from an ill person to dirty water to a drinker, with three marked breaks such as a toilet that keeps waste away from the water, boiling or filtering the water, and a vaccine for the next person.. *Why:* A chain can be broken in many places. Putting the break far from the first case, as with clean water, protects a whole town.
11. (mcq) Smallpox was wiped out, but the common cold cannot be. What is the main reason?  
   Options: Smallpox was one virus that lived only in people, and there was a strong vaccine, while colds come from hundreds of viruses / Smallpox was a bacterium / Colds are not caused by germs  
   **Answer:** Smallpox was one virus that lived only in people, and there was a strong vaccine, while colds come from hundreds of viruses. *Why:* To wipe out a disease you need one germ, a way to find every case and a vaccine that works. Colds have hundreds of different viruses that change, so no single vaccine can stop them.
12. (fill) In 1980, the world was declared free of ________.  
   **Answer:** ['smallpox']. *Why:* Smallpox was the first human disease to be wiped out. A world campaign found every case and vaccinated the people around it.
13. (write) A hospital finds a bacterium that none of the hospital's antibiotics can kill. Give two things the hospital could do to stop it spreading.  
   **Answer:** For example: keep the patients who carry it apart from others, make everyone clean their hands carefully between patients, clean the equipment, and test to find the right medicine and use antibiotics only when needed.. *Why:* The germ chain can be broken at many links even when no medicine works. Cleanliness and separation work against resistant germs too.
14. (mcq) A grown-up wants to make ORS stronger for a sick child, so adds extra salt. Why is this a bad idea?  
   Options: The balance of salt and sugar matters, and too much salt can make things worse / Salt makes the sugar disappear / Salt kills the germs  
   **Answer:** The balance of salt and sugar matters, and too much salt can make things worse. *Why:* ORS works only in the tested balance. Too much salt can pull more water out of the body and strain the child. It is safer to measure exactly or use a packet.
15. (write) Is 'fighting germs' a good way to think about health? Give one reason for and one reason against.  
   **Answer:** For: some germs do cause serious illness, and medicines and vaccines really do stop them. Against: most microbes are harmless or helpful, including the bacteria in our gut, and strong medicines can harm them too.. *Why:* A good answer holds two ideas together. We need protection from a few germs, and we live with many microbes that help us.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which illness is infectious but NOT contagious?  
   Options: Tetanus / Measles / Chickenpox  
   **Answer:** Tetanus. *Why:* Tetanus is caused by bacteria from soil that get into deep cuts, and nobody catches it from another person. Measles and chickenpox pass from person to person.
2. (mcq) During the incubation period, what are the germs doing?  
   Options: Multiplying quietly while the person feels well / Waiting for a medicine / Leaving the body  
   **Answer:** Multiplying quietly while the person feels well. *Why:* In the first days, the germs multiply before the defenders raise the alarm. That is why a person can pass germs on before they feel ill.
3. (mcq) What does an antibody do?  
   Options: Fits one germ like a key in a lock, sticks to them and marks them to be removed / Kills every germ at once / Carries oxygen in the blood  
   **Answer:** Fits one germ like a key in a lock, sticks to them and marks them to be removed. *Why:* An antibody has a shape that fits one particular germ. It does not kill every germ, and oxygen is carried by red blood cells.
4. (mcq) Why does an antibiotic not help against a cold?  
   Options: A cold is caused by a virus, which has no cell wall for the antibiotic to attack / A cold is too small to feel / Antibiotics only work in the morning  
   **Answer:** A cold is caused by a virus, which has no cell wall for the antibiotic to attack. *Why:* Antibiotics attack parts that bacteria have. A virus is not a cell and has none of those parts.
5. (mcq) In a game, a medicine removes 9 out of 10 ordinary germs from a crowd but cannot touch the few tough ones. After several days, what will most of the germs be?  
   Options: Tough ones / Ordinary ones / The same mix as at the start  
   **Answer:** Tough ones. *Why:* The medicine removes the ordinary germs, and the tough ones survive and multiply into the space. The medicine did not make any germ tougher. It only chose which ones carry on.
6. (mcq) Who or what becomes resistant to an antibiotic?  
   Options: The bacteria / The person who takes it / The tablet  
   **Answer:** The bacteria. *Why:* Resistance belongs to the germs. A person who takes many antibiotics can carry resistant bacteria, but the person does not become resistant.
7. (mcq) Which sign means a child should see a doctor at once?  
   Options: A child who vomits everything and cannot keep a drink down / A child with a runny nose / A child who feels warm after running  
   **Answer:** A child who vomits everything and cannot keep a drink down. *Why:* A child who cannot keep any drink down can quickly dry out. A runny nose and feeling warm after exercise are common and not dangerous in themselves.
8. (mcq) A doctor says a child's cold needs no antibiotic. What is the best next step?  
   Options: Rest, drinks, and watching for danger signs / Asking another doctor for an antibiotic / Using tablets left over from an earlier illness  
   **Answer:** Rest, drinks, and watching for danger signs. *Why:* A cold is caused by a virus, and the body clears it with time. An antibiotic cannot help, and old tablets are the wrong medicine.
9. (mcq) What does a vaccine hold?  
   Options: A safe form or piece of a germ, or a harmless copy of the germ's poison / A full-strength germ / Only vitamins  
   **Answer:** A safe form or piece of a germ, or a harmless copy of the germ's poison. *Why:* A vaccine shows the defenders what the germ looks like, without being able to make you ill.
10. (mcq) Why is the body faster the second time the same germ arrives?  
   Options: Memory cells stay behind and make the right antibody quickly / The germ gets weaker with age / The body forgets it was ill  
   **Answer:** Memory cells stay behind and make the right antibody quickly. *Why:* After the first meeting, some white blood cells stay on as memory cells. A vaccine uses this by giving the first meeting safely.
11. (mcq) Why is a baby too young for the measles vaccine safer when everyone around her is vaccinated?  
   Options: The germ keeps meeting protected people and has a hard time reaching her / The vaccine goes through the air to her / Babies cannot catch measles  
   **Answer:** The germ keeps meeting protected people and has a hard time reaching her. *Why:* Every protected person is a broken link in the chain. Babies can catch measles, and the vaccine does not travel through the air.
12. (mcq) About how many out of 100 people need to be protected to stop measles spreading?  
   Options: 95 / 50 / 75  
   **Answer:** 95. *Why:* Measles spreads so easily that one ill child can pass it to 12 to 18 unprotected others. So almost everyone, about 95 out of 100, needs protection.
13. (mcq) A glass of yeast with strong salt makes much less foam than a glass with nothing added. What does this show?  
   Options: Strong salt slows a living microbe / Yeast prefers salt / Salt turns into foam  
   **Answer:** Strong salt slows a living microbe. *Why:* The only difference between the glasses was the salt, so the salt held the yeast back. Strong salt would also harm our own cells, so it is not a medicine.

## Applications
- A hospital lab grows bacteria from a patient on a dish and puts small paper discs of different antibiotics on top. A clear ring round a disc shows which medicine works, the same clue that Fleming noticed round a mould.
- Doctors choose an antibiotic only after finding out the cause, and hospitals keep rules to use antibiotics only when they are needed, so that they keep working.
- Health workers carry vaccines to villages in insulated boxes with ice packs, and in cold rooms and fridges at health centres, so that the vaccine stays between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius all the way.
- When a case of measles, polio or Ebola is found, health workers find the people around the ill person and vaccinate them, which makes a ring that the germ cannot cross.
- ORS packets are kept in anganwadis, health centres and chemists, and given with zinc tablets to children with loose motions.
- Scientists in many countries study the flu viruses that are going around each year, and the flu vaccine is made to match them.
- Vets and farmers vaccinate cattle, poultry and dogs against illnesses such as rabies, and are asked to use antibiotics on animals only when they are needed.
- India is one of the biggest makers of vaccines in the world, and factories in cities such as Pune, Hyderabad and Chennai supply vaccines to many countries.
- New medicines and vaccines are tested in stages on volunteers, with a comparison group, before they are approved.
- Campaigns such as Pulse Polio and Mission Indradhanush visit homes and schools to reach children who missed their doses.
- After a deep cut or a dog bite, doctors decide about a tetanus booster or a rabies vaccine, because each can protect even after the germ has got in.

## Open questions
- **Why is there still no vaccine for the common cold, or for some illnesses such as HIV?** A cold is caused by hundreds of kinds of virus, and some germs change quickly or hide from the defenders. Scientists are still looking for ways round this.
- **Will we find new antibiotics fast enough to keep ahead of resistant bacteria?** Bacteria multiply quickly and change, and very few new kinds of antibiotic have been found in recent years. Nobody knows who will keep ahead.
- **How does the body keep a memory of a germ for decades?** Some memory cells last for years, and the body also makes new ones. Scientists have not fully worked out how the memory stays and how long it lasts for each germ.
- **Which illness will be the next to be wiped out from the world?** Polio and a few other illnesses are close, but the last cases are the hardest to find, and nobody knows when they will go.
- **Can viruses that kill bacteria, called phages, become a normal medicine?** Phages have been used in a few places for about a hundred years, and are being tested again. Nobody knows yet how widely they will work.
- **How much does a fever help the body fight an infection?** Many studies suggest that a higher temperature helps some defenders and slows some germs. Other studies find little difference, and fever is hard to test fairly in people.

## Connected maths
- In the Rice and Dal game, the tough germs on a plate double every night. Starting with 2 tough germs and no limit on room, how many are there after 3 nights? After 6 nights? **16 tough germs after 3 nights, and 128 after 6 nights.**. Doubling 3 times: 2, 4, 8, 16. Doubling 6 times: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. After 3 more nights the number is 8 times larger, because 2 times 2 times 2 is 8. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In the Rice and Dal game, a body holds 998 ordinary germs and 2 tough germs. A medicine keeps 1 in every 10 ordinary germs and all the tough ones. After the medicine, what share of the germs left are tough? After one night of doubling and another dose of medicine, is that share bigger or smaller? **After the medicine, about 100 ordinary germs and 2 tough ones are left, so about 1 in 50 is tough. After one night and another dose, about 20 ordinary and 4 tough are left, so about 1 in 6 is tough. The share is bigger.**. 998 divided by 10 is about 100. 2 tough out of 102 germs is about 1 in 50. After doubling there are about 200 ordinary and 4 tough. The medicine keeps 1 in 10 ordinary, which is 20. So 4 tough out of 24 germs is 1 in 6. Each dose raises the share of tough germs. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In Memory Cards, Kavya took 24 seconds to find the matching antibody the first time, and 3 seconds after a safe picture from a vaccine. How many times faster was she, and how many seconds did the vaccine save? **8 times faster, and 21 seconds saved.**. 24 divided by 3 is 8, so she was 8 times faster. 24 minus 3 is 21 seconds saved. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In the Yeast Test, the Plain glass had a foam of 18 millimetres. The Salt glass had 1, the Vinegar glass 7, the Garlic glass 10 and the Turmeric glass 16. Which glasses had less than half as much foam as the Plain glass? What fraction of the Plain foam did the Vinegar glass keep? **Salt and Vinegar had less than half. The Vinegar glass kept 7 out of 18 of the foam, which is a little less than half.**. Half of 18 is 9. Salt (1) and Vinegar (7) are below 9. Garlic (10) is just above 9. Vinegar kept 7 out of 18 of the foam, and 7 is less than 9. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In Ring the Germ, 24 of the 36 houses were vaccinated. What fraction and what percentage of the houses is that? Measles needs about 95 out of 100 protected. About how many of the 36 houses would that be? **24 out of 36 is two thirds, about 67 out of 100. About 34 houses would be needed for 95 out of 100.**. 24 divided by 12 is 2, and 36 divided by 12 is 3, so 24 out of 36 is 2 out of 3. 2 out of 3 is about 67 percent. 95 percent of 36 is a little over 34, because 36 times 95 divided by 100 is 34.2. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A made-up syrup label says: 5 millilitres, three times a day, for 5 days. How many millilitres of syrup are needed in all? A bottle holds 100 millilitres. Is it enough, and how much is left? **75 millilitres are needed. A 100 millilitre bottle is enough, with 25 millilitres left.**. 5 millilitres three times a day is 15 millilitres a day. 15 times 5 days is 75 millilitres. 100 minus 75 is 25 millilitres. This is a made-up label for practice. Always follow the real label and the doctor. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L4`)
- ORS needs 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt in 1 litre of clean water. How much sugar and salt for 500 millilitres? For 3 litres? **For 500 millilitres: 3 teaspoons of sugar and a quarter of a teaspoon of salt. For 3 litres: 18 teaspoons of sugar and 1 and a half teaspoons of salt.**. 500 millilitres is half a litre, so half of everything: half of 6 is 3, and half of half a teaspoon is a quarter. 3 litres is 3 times as much: 3 times 6 is 18, and 3 times half a teaspoon is 1 and a half. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- On Meera's chart, her temperature was 36.4 in the morning, 36.8 in the afternoon and 37.0 in the evening. At night it was 38.4. By how much did it rise from the evening to the night? Is the night reading a fever? **It rose by 1.4 degrees. Yes, 38.4 is above 38, so it is a fever.**. 38.4 minus 37.0 is 1.4. A fever is about 38 degrees or more. The three day readings all fall in the normal range for a well person. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- A child is exposed to measles on 3 March. Measles usually shows after 10 to 14 days. Between which dates would she usually first feel ill? On what date at the latest should a doctor have vaccinated her contacts, if the vaccine must be given within 3 days of meeting the germ? **She would usually first feel ill between 13 March and 17 March. The contacts' vaccines should be given by 6 March at the latest.**. 3 March plus 10 days is 13 March. 3 March plus 14 days is 17 March. For the vaccine, 3 March plus 3 days is 6 March. (queues `math.time.calendar.L4`)
- In a class of 40 children, 36 are vaccinated. A new child who is not vaccinated joins. If one child in the class has measles, would herd protection be strong enough to keep her safe? Use the idea that about 95 out of 100 need protection. **No. 36 out of 40 is 9 out of 10, or 90 out of 100. That is below 95, so measles could still spread.**. 36 divided by 4 is 9, and 40 divided by 4 is 10, so 36 out of 40 is 9 out of 10, which is 90 percent. 95 percent of 40 is 38. 36 is less than 38, so two or more children would still need protection. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
