# Seasons: how plants, animals and people change through the year
`sci.day-night-seasons.seasons.L3`

**Summary.** Every year in India, the hot months, the rains and the cool months come round again in the same order. These parts of the year are called seasons. Summer is hotter because the midday Sun is higher, so its light falls more straight down and is packed into a smaller patch of ground. Plants, animals and people all change with the seasons. Many trees drop their leaves in the dry months, frogs breed in monsoon puddles, ducks fly in from Central Asia for the winter, and families switch to cotton, buttermilk and mangoes in summer. In this unit you pull a hidden yellow colour out of a green leaf, and find out why leaves turn yellow before they fall.

**Intro.** *A neem tree is dropping yellow leaves in March, the hottest, driest time of year. A week later, the same tree is covered in soft new leaves. Is the tree sick, or does she know something?* The neem tree is not sick. She is changing with the season, the way you change into cotton clothes in summer. In this unit you look out of your window for signs of the season. You find a colour hiding inside a green leaf. You use a torch to see why summer sunlight is stronger. And you meet the koel, the frog, the pintail duck, the farmer and the fisher, who all change what they do as the year turns.

## Terms
- **Season**: A part of the year with its own kind of weather, which comes back at about the same time every year. *e.g.* In Chennai, the hot season comes every year from about March to June.
- **Summer**: The hottest season. In most of India it comes from about March to June. *e.g.* In May in Hyderabad, the afternoon can be hotter than 40 degrees Celsius.
- **Monsoon**: A wind that changes direction with the seasons and brings the rains. People also call the rainy season itself the monsoon. *e.g.* The south-west monsoon usually reaches Kerala around the first of June.
- **North-east monsoon**: The rains that come to Tamil Nadu and nearby places from about October to December, when the winds blow from the north-east. *e.g.* Chennai gets most of its rain in October, November and December.
- **Winter**: The coolest season. In most of India it comes from about December to February. *e.g.* On a January night in Delhi it can be colder than 5 degrees Celsius, while in Chennai it stays near 20.
- **Ritu**: The old Sanskrit word for a season. The old Indian calendar splits the year into six ritus of about two months each. *e.g.* Vasanta ritu is spring, and Varsha ritu is the rainy season.
- **Deciduous**: A deciduous tree drops all or most of their leaves for part of the year, and then grows new ones. *e.g.* A teak tree drops their big leaves in the dry months and grows new ones before the rains.
- **Evergreen**: An evergreen plant keeps green leaves all year. Old leaves fall a few at a time, and new ones keep growing. *e.g.* A coconut palm is green all year round.
- **Chlorophyll**: The green colouring in leaves. Plants use it to catch sunlight and make their food. *e.g.* A palak leaf is dark green because she has a lot of chlorophyll.
- **Pigment**: A substance that gives something its colour. *e.g.* Turmeric has a yellow pigment that stains your fingers.
- **Nutrients**: The things a living body needs to grow and stay healthy, apart from water and air. *e.g.* Plants take nutrients such as nitrogen from the soil, through their roots.
- **Migration**: A long journey that animals make every year, at the same season, to find food, warmth or a safe place for their young. *e.g.* Northern pintail ducks fly from Central Asia to the lakes of India every winter.
- **Hibernation**: A long, deep sleep through the cold months, when an animal's body slows right down and they live on stored fat. *e.g.* Some Himalayan black bears sleep in a den through the snowy winter.
- **Day length**: How many hours of daylight there are, from sunrise to sunset. It changes through the year. *e.g.* In Delhi, the longest day in June has about 14 hours of daylight, and the shortest in December has about 10.
- **Harvest**: Gathering a crop when it is ready. *e.g.* Rice is harvested in many parts of Tamil Nadu in January, and Pongal celebrates it.
- **Axis**: An imaginary line through the Earth from the North Pole to the South Pole. The Earth spins round it. The Earth's axis is tilted, and this tilt is what gives us seasons. *e.g.* A spinning top that leans a little to one side spins round a tilted axis.

## Explanations
### What a season is · *defined*
**Definition:** A season is a part of the year with its own kind of weather. The seasons come round in the same order every year.

The year goes round like a wheel. In most of India, it gets hotter from March, and May is often the hottest month. Then the rains come, from about June to September. After the rains, the air gets cooler and drier, and December and January are the coolest months. Then it warms up again. The order is the same every year. The exact dates change a little from year to year, and they change a lot from place to place.
- e.g. Every year, mangoes come to the market in about April and May.
- e.g. Every year, the rains reach Kerala first, around the first of June, and spread north over the next five or six weeks.
- e.g. Every year, the mornings in December are cooler than the mornings in May.
### India's seasons · *defined*
**Definition:** The India Meteorological Department, which watches India's weather, counts four seasons: winter (January and February), summer (March to May), the south-west monsoon (June to September) and the post-monsoon season (October to December).

People split the year in different ways. The weather department uses four seasons. The old Indian calendar uses six ritus of about two months each: Vasanta (spring), Grishma (summer), Varsha (the rains), Sharad (early autumn), Hemanta (the cool time before winter) and Shishira (winter). Old Tamil poems also count six seasons, such as kaar, the rainy season. Many people simply say summer, rainy season and winter. None of these is the one true count. They are different ways of cutting up the same year. The seasons are also different in different places. Most of India gets most of its rain from June to September, from the south-west monsoon. But Chennai gets most of its rain from October to December, from the north-east monsoon. Delhi has a cold winter, with foggy mornings. Kochi and Chennai stay warm all year, so there the rains mark the seasons more than the cold does.
- e.g. In Kochi, the big rains start in June. In Chennai, the biggest rains come in November.
- e.g. Vasanta ritu is the time of Holi in the north and Ugadi in the south.
- e.g. In Srinagar it can snow in January. In Thiruvananthapuram it never does.
### Why summer is hotter than winter · *defined*
**Definition:** In summer, the midday Sun is higher in the sky, so its light falls more straight down and is packed into a smaller patch of ground. The days are also longer. Both make the ground warmer.

Remember the torch test. When the torch shone straight down, its light made a small, bright patch. When it shone at a slant, the same light spread over a much bigger patch, so each square got less. Sunlight works the same way. In summer, the midday Sun in India is high, nearly overhead, and your shadow at noon is very short. Each patch of ground gets a lot of light. In winter, the midday Sun is lower in the sky, and your noon shadow is longer. The same sunlight is spread over more ground, so each patch warms less. The days are shorter in winter too, so there are fewer hours of sunshine. Why does the Sun climb higher in some months? Because the Earth's axis is tilted. For part of the year, our half of the Earth leans towards the Sun. Six months later, it leans away. You will learn more about the tilt in Class 5. Many people think summer comes when the Earth is closest to the Sun. But the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during India's winter.
- e.g. At noon in Chennai in May, your shadow is so short it hides under your feet.
- e.g. At noon in Delhi in December, your shadow is about as long as you are tall, or a little longer.
- e.g. Kerala and Tamil Nadu are close to the equator, where the Sun is high all year. That is why their winters are mild.
### Why many trees drop their leaves in the dry months · *defined*
**Definition:** A deciduous tree drops their leaves for part of the year. In most of India, they do this in the hot, dry months before the rains. Before a leaf falls, the tree breaks down the green chlorophyll and takes useful nutrients back into the branches. The yellow colour that was hidden under the green then shows.

Leaves lose water all the time, through tiny holes underneath. In the rains, the soil is wet, and the roots easily replace that water. From about February to May, many places get almost no rain, and the soil dries out. A big tree with thousands of leaves could lose more water than her roots can find. So trees such as teak, peepal, neem, gulmohar and the Indian almond drop many or all of their leaves. A leaf is full of useful nutrients, such as nitrogen, that the tree took from the soil. Throwing them away would be a waste. So before the leaf falls, the tree breaks down the green chlorophyll and moves much of the nitrogen back into the branches, to use for new leaves. Your strip test showed that a green leaf also holds yellow pigments. They stay in the leaf longer than the green does. So as the green goes, the leaf turns yellow. Some trees, such as the Indian almond, also make new red colours in their leaves before they drop them. Then, often before the rains, new leaves come out, soft and pale green or coppery pink.
- e.g. The peepal tree drops their leaves for a short time in the dry season. Their new leaves come out coppery pink.
- e.g. Neem trees drop many old leaves around February and March. By Ugadi, they have new leaves and tiny white flowers, used in Ugadi pachadi.
- e.g. Coconut palms and mango trees are evergreen. They never stand bare.
### How plants know the season · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants sense the length of the night, and use it to know the time of year. When the nights reach the right length, they start to flower. This is called photoperiodism.

A plant cannot read a calendar, but the length of the night is a very good clock. It changes the same way every year, whatever the weather does. Plants sense light with special substances in their leaves. They notice how long the dark lasts each night. Some plants, such as the chrysanthemum, or sevanthi, flower when the nights get long, in the cool months. That is why sevanthi garlands are cheap in November and December. Other plants flower when the nights get short. Scientists found this in the 1920s, as the story of Garner and Allard tells. Plants also notice warmth, cold and rain. Many seeds wait in the dry soil and sprout only after the first rains. Plants feel, remember and decide, and choosing when to flower is one of their biggest decisions. Some scientists call this an automatic response, built into the plant by their genes.
- e.g. Sevanthi, the chrysanthemum, flowers in the long nights of the cool months.
- e.g. Some old rice varieties flower at the same time every year, in October or November, even when farmers sow them at different times.
- e.g. After the first rains, grass sprouts on bare ground within a few days.
- **Many plant scientists today:** Plants sense the night and make a choice about when to flower. Their senses and signals are real, and their choice is theirs.
- **Other plant scientists:** The flowering is set off by a chain of chemical changes, and it is best called an automatic response, not a choice.
### How animals change with the seasons · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals change what they do, where they live and sometimes how their bodies look as the seasons change. Each change helps them with a problem that season brings, such as heat, cold, dry food or a safe time for their young.

Each season brings its own problems and chances. In summer, water is short and the afternoons are very hot. Buffaloes have fewer sweat glands than cows, so they lie in mud or water to cool off. The koel calls loudly from March to June. This is her breeding time, and she lays her eggs in crows' nests, which are busy then too. The monsoon fills puddles and ponds. Frogs need water for their eggs and tadpoles, so they call and lay eggs after the first heavy rains. Winged termites fly out of the ground after the first rains, looking for a place to start a new colony. In winter, lakes in Central Asia and Siberia freeze, and the food under the water is locked under ice. So ducks such as the northern pintail fly thousands of kilometres to India's lakes, and fly back in spring. In the Himalayas, some black bears sleep in a den through the snowy months, living on their fat. Olive ridley turtles come ashore on Chennai and Odisha beaches from about January to March to lay their eggs.
- e.g. Openbill storks, egrets and other water birds fill Vedanthangal Bird Sanctuary near Chennai from about November to March.
- e.g. After the first big rain of the monsoon, winged termites fly round street lights at night.
- e.g. On hot afternoons, crows sit with their beaks open to lose heat.
### How people change with the seasons · *defined*
**Definition:** People change their clothes, food, homes, work and festivals with the seasons, to stay comfortable and to fit in with the plants and animals they depend on.

In summer, families wear loose cotton clothes and drink buttermilk, tender coconut water and panakam. They eat mangoes, watermelon and nungu, the ice apple of the palmyra palm. Many families dry vadams and make mango pickle on the terrace, because the strong sun dries food fast. In the rains, farmers sow rice and other crops, because the fields finally have enough water. Umbrellas and raincoats come out. In the cool months, people in the north wear sweaters, monkey caps and shawls, and sit in the sun at midday. Many festivals mark a harvest: Onam in Kerala in August or September, Pongal in Tamil Nadu and Makar Sankranti in January, and Baisakhi in Punjab in April. Even fishing changes. Every year the Government of India stops most fishing at sea for 61 days, while many fish breed. On the east coast this is from 15 April to 14 June. On the west coast it is from 1 June to 31 July.
- e.g. Jasmine flowers are plentiful in Madurai in summer, and costly in the cool months, when the plants make fewer.
- e.g. Pongal is cooked with the new rice of the January harvest.
- e.g. In Delhi in winter, people sell roasted sweet potatoes and peanuts on the street.
### Are the seasons changing? · *idea*
Scientists agree that the Earth as a whole is getting warmer, mostly because people burn coal, oil and gas. In many countries, records kept for a hundred years or more show some plants flowering earlier in spring than they used to. In India, fewer such records exist, so scientists are still working out how much each season has shifted. Many farmers say the rains have become less regular, with long dry gaps and then very heavy downpours. Weather scientists are studying whether this is a lasting change. Nobody can yet say exactly how India's seasons will be in 50 years.
- e.g. Japan has kept records of when cherry trees flower in Kyoto for more than a thousand years. In recent decades, the flowering has come earlier.
- e.g. Grandparents often remember cooler summers. Their memories are useful clues, but memory can be wrong, so scientists check them against written records.

## Mostly agreed
- The seasons come round in the same order every year. *How we know:* Weather records kept in India since the 1800s show the same pattern every year: hot months, the rains, then cool months.
- Seasons are caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis, not by the Earth's distance from the Sun. *How we know:* The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during India's winter. And when it is winter in India, it is summer in Australia, under the same Sun.
- Sunlight falling straight down warms the ground more than sunlight falling at a slant. *How we know:* In the torch test, slanting light spread the same light over many more squares. Measurements of sunshine on the ground show the same thing.
- Green leaves hold yellow and orange pigments as well as green chlorophyll. Before a leaf falls, the chlorophyll breaks down first. *How we know:* Paper strip tests separate the pigments. Scientists have also measured the pigments in leaves as they age.
- Many plants use the length of the night to tell the season. *How we know:* Garner and Allard made tobacco plants flower in summer by shutting them in the dark for part of each day. Many experiments since have shown the same with other plants.

## How we found out
**Wightman Garner and Harry Allard, scientists in the United States Department of Agriculture, and later Karl Hamner and James Bonner, 1918 to 1920, with the night-length experiments in 1938.** About 1906, farmers in Maryland, in the United States, found a new kind of tobacco plant. It grew huge, taller than a man, but it would not flower in the summer, so it gave no seeds. People thought plants flowered when they were old enough or big enough, or when the weather was warm. This plant broke that idea. It was big, warm and well fed, and still did not flower. Then farmers noticed it did flower in winter, in a glasshouse. Wightman Garner and Harry Allard, two scientists in the United States government's farm department, wondered if the plant could tell the length of the day. In 1918 they tested it. Every afternoon they pushed some plants into a dark hut, so their days were short, like winter days. Other plants stayed outside. In the middle of summer, the plants with short days flowered, and the others did not. They published their results in 1920 and called this photoperiodism. In 1938, Karl Hamner and James Bonner showed that a short flash of light in the middle of the night could stop such plants from flowering. So what plants really measure is the length of the night.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a plant flowers when they are old enough, big enough or warm enough. Garner and Allard showed that many plants flower when the days reach a certain length, by shortening the days of tobacco plants with a dark hut. Later work showed that the plants are really measuring the length of the night.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Summer comes when the Earth is closest to the Sun.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Being near a fire makes you warmer, so it seems the same should be true of the Sun.  
  Against: The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, in India's winter. And Australia has summer while India has winter.
- **A tree that drops their leaves in the dry season is dying or sick.** (Many people who see a bare tree in March; *rejected*)  
  For: Yellow leaves can be a sign of trouble, for example in a potted plant that gets too much water.  
  Against: Deciduous trees drop their leaves every year and grow new ones. Before the leaves fall, the tree takes back useful nutrients from them.
- **India really has six seasons, and the four-season count is wrong.** (Some people who follow the old six-ritu calendar; *debated*)  
  For: The six ritus fit North India's year well, and people have used them for over two thousand years.  
  Against: Seasons are a way people choose to split the year. The weather department's four seasons fit India's rainfall well. In Chennai or Kochi, three seasons may fit even better.
- **The pied cuckoo, called the chataka in old Sanskrit poems, brings the monsoon to North India.** (An old belief in many parts of India; *debated*)  
  For: Pied cuckoos fly in from Africa and are usually first heard in North India around the time the monsoon arrives.  
  Against: The bird cannot make rain. Both the bird and the rain come with the same seasonal winds. Bird watchers are still studying how closely the bird's arrival matches the rain each year.
- **Plants do not really know the season. They only react to light.** (Some plant scientists; *debated*)  
  For: The steps by which a plant senses the night can be described as chemical changes.  
  Against: Plants feel the length of the night, remember it over many nights, and choose when to flower. Sensing, remembering and choosing are what knowing is.

## Roots
We have pulled hidden colours out of a leaf, watched light spread at a slant, and met the animals and people who change with the year. Now we ask what the seasons tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your strip drew a yellow band out of a leaf that looked only green. The yellow was there all along, hidden under the green. Before a tree drops leaves in the dry season, they break down the green and take back what is useful. Then the hidden yellow shows, and the leaf falls.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a leaf 'turns' yellow, as if a new colour is painted on as the leaf dies. And we read yellow leaves as a sign of a sick tree. In the dry season, the yellow is the colour that was always there, showing once the tree has taken back the green.  
*Evidence:* In the Hidden Colours test, the green palak leaf gave a green band and a yellow band. The yellowing palak leaf gave a strong yellow band and much less green. Scientists have measured that trees take back about half of the nitrogen from their leaves before the leaves fall.

- *who am i:* You change with the seasons too: your clothes, your food, even how long you sleep in the light evenings.
- *what is life:* A tree feels the dry months coming, takes back what she needs from her leaves, and lets them go to grow new ones.
- *what is this world:* One tilted Earth going round one Sun gives every place its own pattern of seasons, and every living thing fits into it.

There is one Sun, and one Earth going round it, tilted the same way all year. From that one simple tilt come summer, the rains and the cool months, different in every place. And from each season come many answers: the neem drops her leaves, the koel calls, the frog sings in the puddle, the farmer sows rice, the duck flies south. Each living thing meets the same turning year in their own way.

## Story: The Neem Tree Outside Our Window
*The story is made up. Everything about the neem tree, the koel and the season is true: many neem trees drop old leaves in February and March, grow new leaves and flowers by Ugadi, and the koel starts calling in the hot months.*

Our flat in Hyderabad is on the third floor, and our balcony looks straight into a neem tree. My name is Lakshmi. I am nine. My brother Arjun is six, and he thinks the neem tree belongs to him.

One morning in February, Arjun came running in from the balcony with his hands full of leaves. They were yellow, with brown spots.

"Akka, the tree is dying!" he said. "The leaves are falling off. We have to give her water."

He filled a steel tumbler at the kitchen tap and leaned over the balcony railing. The water splashed down onto the scooter parked below. Our Amamma, our grandmother, came out of the kitchen with a ladle in her hand.

"Arjun, the tree's roots go deeper than our building's water tank," she said. "Your tumbler will not help her. And Ravi garu's scooter is now very clean."

Arjun did not laugh. He looked at the leaves in his hand. "But why is she dropping them?"

I did not know. So I looked carefully at the tree. Some branches were still green. Others had a lot of yellow leaves, and some were nearly bare. Under the tree, the ground was covered in yellow leaves, and the sweeper aunty had made a pile of them by the gate.

"It hasn't rained since November," I said slowly. "The soil must be very dry."

"That is part of it," said Amamma. "Leaves drink up water all day long. In the dry months, a big tree cannot keep all of them full. So she lets the old ones go."

"But why are they yellow first?" asked Arjun. "Green leaves are good. Why do they turn yellow?"

Amamma smiled at me. "Lakshmi, you did that experiment last week. The one with the palak and the paper strip."

I remembered. Amma had helped me rub a palak leaf onto a strip of blotting paper with a coin, until there was a dark green line. Then we stood the strip in a little nail polish remover, in a tumbler with a plate on top. The liquid had climbed the paper, and the green line had spread out. Above it, near the top, there was a thin yellow stripe. A yellow colour from a green leaf.

"The yellow was inside the green leaf all along," I told Arjun. "You just can't see it, because the green is so strong. When a leaf gets old, the green goes away first. Then the yellow shows."

"Where does the green go?" Arjun asked.

This time Amamma answered. "The tree breaks the green down and takes the good things back into her branches. She keeps them for her new leaves. A tree does not waste."

Arjun thought about it. Then he picked out the yellowest leaf from his pile and held it up to the light. "So this leaf has already given her food back?"

"A lot of it," said Amamma.

We decided to watch the tree every morning. I drew her in my notebook on the first of each week, and Arjun counted the bare branches he could see from the balcony.

In the second week of March, the tree looked thin and tired. Arjun was worried again. But I had noticed something. At the tips of the bare twigs, there were tiny buds, reddish and soft.

By the third week, the buds had opened into new leaves. They were pale green and shiny, so soft that they hung down like little wet feathers. Then came the flowers, tiny and white, in bunches. The whole balcony smelled sweet and a little bitter.

On Ugadi morning, Amamma reached out from the balcony with a long stick and pulled a branch close. She picked a handful of neem flowers. She put them in the Ugadi pachadi, with new jaggery, raw mango, tamarind, salt and chilli.

"Sweet, sour, salty, bitter, hot and tangy," she said, giving us each a spoonful. "All the tastes of the year, for the new year."

The bitter came from the neem flowers. Arjun made a face. Then he went out onto the balcony and looked up at the tree for a long time.

"She wasn't dying," he said. "She was getting ready."

That evening, from somewhere high in the neem tree, a bird called. Ku-oo. Ku-oo. Louder each time, until it seemed to fill the whole street.

"The koel is back," said Amamma. "Now summer has really started."

I wrote it in my notebook, under the drawing of the tree with her new leaves. Week 6: new leaves, flowers, koel calling. Then I thought about the yellow stripe on the blotting paper. It had been hiding in a green leaf all along, waiting for the green to go.

**For the parent:** A tree that drops yellow leaves in the dry months is not dying. She takes back what is useful from her leaves, lets them fall, and grows new ones for the new season. The yellow was in the leaves all along.

## Experiments
### e1 · Hidden Colours: is there yellow inside a green leaf? (35 min)
- **question:** A green leaf and a yellowing leaf: what colours does each one hold?
- **predict:** Look at the green palak leaf and the yellowing palak leaf. When the liquid climbs the paper, what colours do you think each one will show? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same kind of paper strip for both leaves, the same coin and the same number of rubs, 10 for each. Draw both lines 2 cm from the bottom. Stand both strips in the same liquid, 1 cm deep, for the same time. Only the leaf changes.
- **safety:** Nail polish remover catches fire very easily. Keep it far from any flame: no diya, stove, mosquito coil or agarbatti in the room. Open a window. A grown-up pours it, and nobody sniffs it. Keep the tumblers covered. Wash hands afterwards. Pour what is left down the sink with plenty of running water.
- **record:** Tape both dry strips into your notebook side by side. Label each colour band. Fill in the table.
- **results table:** Leaf | My guess: what colours? | Colours I saw, from bottom to top | How high did the top colour go (cm)? | Which colour was strongest?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut two strips of blotting paper, 2 cm wide and 12 cm long. 2. Draw a pencil line across each strip, 2 cm from the bottom. 3. Write 'green' at the top of one strip and 'yellow' at the top of the other, in pencil.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to find out if a leaf has colours we cannot see. First we make two paper strips, one for each leaf.  
  *Child mode:* Cut two paper strips. Draw a line 2 cm from the bottom. Label them 'green' and 'yellow'.
  - Note: Use pencil, not pen. Pen ink would run up the strip too and mix with the leaf colours.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show both leaves. 2. Ask your child to guess what colours each will show on the strip, and why. 3. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* This leaf is green, and this one has gone yellow. When we let a liquid climb the strip, it can carry a leaf's colours up and spread them apart. What colours do you think each leaf will show? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both leaves. What colours will each show? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What colours do you think the green leaf will show? (good: Only green, because it looks green.; Maybe some other colour hiding, because leaves go yellow later.)
  - Note: Don't say what will happen. Most children guess 'only green' for the green leaf and 'only yellow' for the yellow one. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the green palak leaf over the pencil line on the 'green' strip. 2. Roll the edge of the coin firmly along the line, pressing hard, so the leaf leaves a dark green line. 3. Move to a fresh part of the leaf and roll again. Do 10 rolls in all. 4. Do the same with the yellowing leaf on the other strip, also 10 rolls.  
  *Parent says:* Press the coin's edge down hard and roll it along the line. Each roll squeezes a little colour out of the leaf onto the paper. We do 10 rolls on each strip, so it's fair.  
  *Child mode:* Put the leaf on the line. Roll the coin's edge hard along it. Do 10 rolls.
  - Note: The green line should be dark and thin, just on the pencil line. A thick, dark line gives clearer colours. The yellowing leaf gives a paler line, and that is fine. Count the rolls out loud together.
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open a window. 2. Pour nail polish remover into each tumbler, just 1 cm deep. 3. Stand one strip in each tumbler, with the leaf line above the liquid. 4. Tape the top of the strip to the rim. 5. Cover each tumbler with a small plate.  
  *Parent says:* The leaf line must stay above the liquid, or the colours would wash off into it. The plate on top stops the liquid drying up.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours the liquid. Keep the green line above it. Put a plate on top.
  - Note: If the liquid is deeper than 2 cm, it will touch the leaf line and the colours will just dissolve into the tumbler. Pour less. Keep the bottle closed and away from any flame.
- **s5 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Watch the liquid climb the paper. 2. Every few minutes, look at the colours through the side of the tumbler. 3. After about 15 to 20 minutes, or when the liquid is 1 cm from the top, lift the strips out. 4. Mark the top of the liquid with a pencil. 5. Lay them on a plate to dry.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the line. What is happening to the green? Is any other colour moving away from it?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the strip. Does the green move? Can you see another colour?
  - Note: On the green leaf's strip, the green spreads up into a band, and a thin yellow or yellow-orange band runs ahead of it, near the top. On the yellowing leaf's strip, the yellow band is usually clear, and there is much less green, or none. The colours fade as the strip dries, so look at them straight away. If you see only green, the line was too faint. Make a new strip with 20 rolls, or let it run longer. Hand sanitiser works more slowly than nail polish remover.
  - Check: On the green leaf's strip, a yellow band appeared above the green. Where did the yellow come from? → **The leaf had a yellow colour hidden under the green**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two strips side by side. 2. Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw, and why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Put the strips side by side. What do you think now about the colours in a leaf? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think the yellowing leaf looked different?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the strips. What do you think now? What did you see? Why?
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A strong answer sounds like this. What I think: a green leaf has yellow inside it too. What I saw: the green leaf made a green band and a yellow band. The yellowing leaf made a yellow band and hardly any green. Why: when a leaf gets old, the green goes first, and the yellow that was there is left. Now tell your child that trees do this before dropping leaves in the dry season, and take the useful parts of the green back.
  - Check: The yellowing leaf showed a strong yellow band but little green. What does this tell you? → **The leaf lost the green, and the yellow stayed**
### e2 · Straight or Slanting: how far does the same torchlight spread? (25 min)
- **question:** Does the same torch light up more squares when it shines straight down, or at a slant?
- **predict:** Will the patch of light be bigger when the torch shines straight down, or when it shines at a slant from the side? Will it be brighter or dimmer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same torch and the same squared paper every time. Keep the torch 20 cm from the middle of the patch, measured with a ruler. Only the angle changes. Do each angle twice, because a shaky hand can change the patch.
- **safety:** Never point the torch into anyone's eyes.
- **record:** Keep the paper with the three outlines drawn on it. Fill in the table, and draw a bar for each angle.
- **results table:** How the torch shines | My guess: squares lit | Try 1: squares lit | Try 2: squares lit | Bright or dim?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the squared paper flat on the floor or a table. 2. Mark a small dot in the middle. 3. Make the room dark.  
  *Parent says:* This paper is the ground, and the torch is the Sun. We will shine the same torch at the same dot, from different angles.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the paper down. Mark a dot in the middle. Turn off the lights.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, and write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* First the torch will shine straight down. Then at a slant, like the Sun in the afternoon. Then very low, like the Sun near sunset. Which patch will be biggest? Which will be brightest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Straight down, a slant, or very low. Which patch is biggest? Why?
  - Note: Don't give the answer. Some children think the light will be the same size every time, because it is the same torch.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the torch straight above the dot, with the glass 20 cm from the paper. Use the ruler to check. 2. Your child draws round the edge of the bright patch in pencil. 3. Count the squares inside. Count a square if more than half of it is lit. 4. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Hold still. Draw round the edge of the bright part. Now count the squares inside.  
  *Child mode:* Shine straight down. Draw round the bright patch. Count the squares.
  - Note: Rest your elbow on the table to keep the torch steady. The edge of the patch may be fuzzy. Draw round the edge of the brightest part each time, so the counts are fair.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now hold the torch at a slant, half-way between straight down and flat, still 20 cm from the dot. Rest it on a pile of books to keep it still. 2. Draw round the patch and count. 3. Then hold the torch very low, nearly flat, still 20 cm from the dot. 4. Draw round the patch and count. Do each twice.  
  *Parent says:* The torch is the same distance from the dot. Only the angle has changed. Is the patch bigger or smaller? Is it brighter or dimmer?  
  *Child mode:* Now shine at a slant. Then very low. Draw and count each patch.
  - Note: The slanting patch is an oval, bigger than the straight one, and dimmer. The very low patch is a long oval, often two to three times the squares of the straight patch, and clearly dim. Your numbers depend on your torch, so they will not match anyone else's exactly.
  - Check: The torch gave out the same light each time. When it shone at a slant, the patch was bigger. What happened to the light in each square? → **Each square got less light, because the same light was spread over more squares**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the three outlines together. 2. Ask: what I think, what I saw, and why. 3. Then link it to the Sun.  
  *Parent says:* In summer, the Sun at noon is high, nearly straight above us. In winter, it is lower in the sky. Which torch was like the summer Sun? Which ground would get warmer?  
  *Child mode:* Which torch was like the summer Sun? Which ground warms more?
  - Note: A strong answer: what I think is that straight light warms the ground more. What I saw: the straight patch was smallest and brightest, and the low patch was biggest and dimmest. Why: the same light is packed into fewer squares when it falls straight down. The high summer Sun is like the straight torch. The days are also longer in summer, so the ground gets more hours of sunshine.

### c1 · Water for the Birds in May (30 min)
- **problem:** In the hottest weeks of summer, birds in the city struggle to find water. Design a water bowl for your balcony or window sill that birds can use safely, that stays cool and clean, and that does not tip over.

- **creative:** Put the clay saucer inside a slightly bigger one with a little water between them, so it stays cool for longer.; Hang a second bowl under the edge of a sunshade, so it is in shade all afternoon.; Keep a diary of which birds come and at what time.
- **s1 (8 min)**

  *Parent says:* Birds need water most in the hottest weeks, when puddles have dried up. What would make a bowl safe, cool and steady? Let's each draw two ideas.

- Builders put deep sunshades over windows, so the high summer Sun is kept out and the low winter Sun comes in.
- Solar panels are tilted to face the Sun, so its light falls on them as straight as possible.
- The 61-day fishing ban protects fish while many of them breed.
- Bird sanctuaries such as Vedanthangal and Pulicat are protected most carefully in the winter, when the visiting birds are there.
- Families plan holidays, weddings, house painting and pickle-making around the seasons.
- Doctors watch for mosquito-borne illnesses such as dengue in and after the rains, when mosquitoes breed fast.

## Open questions
- **How exactly does a plant combine the length of the night, warmth and other signals into one choice to flower?** Scientists know many of the substances involved, but how they all work together is still being studied.
- **How are India's seasons changing as the Earth warms?** There are fewer long records in India than in some countries. Scientists are still working out how much the rains and flowering times have shifted.
- **How do migrating birds find the same lake in India every winter, after flying thousands of kilometres?** Birds seem to use the Sun, the stars, the Earth's magnetism and landmarks. How they put these together is not fully known.
- **Exactly when, and why, does the monsoon start each year?** Forecasts of the monsoon's arrival have become much better, but they can still be off by days, and the reasons for each year's timing are still studied.
- **Do young trees and old trees of the same kind feel the dry season coming in the same way?** Some studies find young and old trees drop and grow leaves at slightly different times. The reasons are not well understood.

## Connected maths
- In the torch test, Divya's torch lit 40 squares when it shone straight down. When it shone very low, the same light lit 120 squares. How many times as many squares did the low torch light? What share of the light did each square get? **3 times as many squares. Each square got about a third of the light.**. 120 divided by 40 is 3. The same light was shared among 3 times as many squares, so each square got a third as much. That is why the low light looked dim. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In Delhi, the longest day in June has about 14 hours of daylight, and the shortest day in December has about 10 hours. In Chennai, the longest day has about 12 hours 50 minutes, and the shortest about 11 hours 20 minutes. How much longer is the longest day than the shortest, in each city? **Delhi: about 4 hours longer. Chennai: about 1 hour 30 minutes longer.**. Delhi: 14 hours take away 10 hours is 4 hours. Chennai: from 11 hours 20 minutes, add 40 minutes to reach 12 hours, then 50 more minutes to reach 12 hours 50 minutes. 40 + 50 = 90 minutes, which is 1 hour 30 minutes. So the seasons change the day length much more in Delhi, which is further from the equator. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Every year, fishing at sea on the east coast stops from 15 April to 14 June, both days included. How many days is that? **61 days.**. April: from 15 to 30 is 16 days. May has 31 days. June: from 1 to 14 is 14 days. 16 + 31 + 14 = 61 days. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- The weather department splits the year into four seasons: winter is 2 months, summer is 3 months, the south-west monsoon is 4 months and the post-monsoon season is 3 months. What fraction of the year is the south-west monsoon? Which two seasons together make half the year? **The monsoon is 4 out of 12 months, which is one third of the year. Summer and the post-monsoon season together make 6 months, half the year.**. 4 out of 12 is the same as 1 out of 3, because 12 is 3 lots of 4. Half of 12 months is 6. Summer (3) and post-monsoon (3) make 6. Winter (2) and the monsoon (4) also make 6. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- Olive ridley turtle eggs take about 50 days to hatch in warm sand. Lakshmi's family sees a turtle lay eggs on a Chennai beach on 20 February. About when should the babies come out? February has 28 days this year. **About 11 April.**. From 20 February to the end of February is 8 days. March has 31 days, so that makes 39. 50 take away 39 is 11, so 11 days into April: about 11 April. The real time can be a week or more longer or shorter, depending on how warm the sand is. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
