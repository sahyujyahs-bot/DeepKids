# Springs that keep time: stiffness, bouncing and the favourite beat
`sci.forces.stretching-springs.L4`

**Summary.** Hang a bottle on rubber bands and make it bounce. A big bounce takes exactly as long as a small bounce, yet four times the weight makes every bounce twice as slow. Then shake the top end of the bands at just the right beat, and tiny movements of your hand make the bottle swing wildly. You will measure how stiff springs are, time their bounces, find a spring's favourite beat, and learn why a rubber band warms up when you stretch it and why a paper clip tires out.

**Intro.** *Hang a bottle on a rubber band and pull it down a little. Let go and count ten bounces. Now pull it down much further and count ten bounces again. Which takes longer, the small bounces or the big bounces?* Most people guess that the big bounces take longer, because the bottle has further to go. You will find out for yourself. This is a clue to something strange about springs. They keep time. Each spring, with its load, has its own beat, and nothing you do to start it changes that beat. In this unit, you will time bounces, learn what does change the beat, and find out what happens when you push a spring in time with its beat, and out of time with it.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can measure how stiff a spring is, in newtons for each centimetre, and compare springs by their stiffness.
1. I can explain why two springs side by side are stiffer and two springs in a row are softer.
2. I can time bounces fairly, by timing ten, repeating and finding the time of one bounce.
3. I can explain that the bounce time depends on the load and the stiffness, and not on the size of the bounce.
4. I can predict that four times the load makes the bounce time twice as long.
5. I can explain resonance: small pushes timed with a spring's own beat build up into a big movement.
6. I can explain why a stretched rubber band warms up and why a paper clip bent back and forth gets tired and breaks.
7. I can say where springs keep time in the world: watches, atoms and the Sun.

## Terms
- **Stiffness**: How much pull a spring needs for each centimetre it stretches. A stiff spring needs a big pull for a small stretch. Scientists also call it the spring constant. *e.g.* A band that stretches 2 centimetres under a 1 newton pull has a stiffness of 0.5 newtons for each centimetre.
- **Extension**: How much longer a spring or band gets when you pull it. It is the stretched length minus the relaxed length. *e.g.* A band that is 8 centimetres long relaxed and 11 centimetres long under a load has an extension of 3 centimetres.
- **Load**: The weight hung on a spring or band, or any pull put on it. *e.g.* A 500 gram packet hung on a band is a load of about 5 newtons.
- **Oscillation**: A movement that goes back and forth again and again, along the same path. *e.g.* A bottle bouncing up and down on a band, and a child on a swing, are both oscillating.
- **Cycle**: One complete go of an oscillation, from a starting place, out to one side, back through the middle and to the other side, and back to the start. *e.g.* One bounce down and up, back to where you let go, is one cycle.
- **Bounce time**: The time for one cycle of the bounce. Scientists call it the period. *e.g.* If ten bounces take 8 seconds, one bounce takes 0.8 seconds.
- **Frequency**: How many cycles happen in one second. It is counted in hertz, written Hz. *e.g.* A bounce time of 0.5 seconds means 2 cycles each second, which is a frequency of 2 hertz.
- **Amplitude**: How far an oscillation goes from the middle, the size of the bounce. *e.g.* If you pull the bottle down 4 centimetres and let go, the amplitude is about 4 centimetres.
- **Natural beat**: The frequency at which a spring and its load bounce on their own, after a small start. It depends on the stiffness and the load. *e.g.* A bottle on a band might have a natural beat of about 1 bounce each second.
- **Resonance**: What happens when you push a spring in time with its natural beat. Each push adds to the last one, so the movement grows big. *e.g.* Pushing a swing at the end of each swing makes it go higher with small pushes.
- **Damping**: The slow dying away of a bounce, because some of its energy turns into heat and sound. *e.g.* A bottle bouncing on a band bounces smaller and smaller until it hangs still.
- **Fatigue**: The slow wearing out of a material that is bent or stretched again and again, even when each bend is small. Tiny cracks grow until the material breaks. *e.g.* A paper clip bent back and forth ten times snaps in half, though one bend would not break it.
- **Elastic energy**: The energy stored in something that has been stretched, squashed or bent and will spring back. *e.g.* A stretched catapult band has elastic energy, which turns into the movement of the stone.
- **In a row**: Springs joined end to end, one after the other, so the same pull goes through each. Scientists say they are in series. *e.g.* Two bands linked like a chain are two springs in a row.
- **Side by side**: Springs placed next to each other, so that they share the pull. Scientists say they are in parallel. *e.g.* Two bands hanging next to each other, both holding one packet, are two springs side by side.
- **Buckling**: The sudden bending or folding of a long thin thing that is pushed from its ends. It stays straight until the push passes a limit, then it folds. *e.g.* A tape measure stands straight when it is pulled out, and then folds suddenly if you press the end.
- **Balance spring**: A very fine coiled spring in a mechanical watch, which makes a small wheel swing back and forth at a steady beat. *e.g.* A mechanical watch ticks each time its balance spring swings the small wheel once.
- **Atomic bond**: The pull between two atoms that holds them together. Atoms joined by a bond can vibrate, like two balls joined by a tiny spring. *e.g.* In a water molecule, each hydrogen atom is joined to the oxygen atom by a bond that vibrates.

## Explanations
### How stiff is a spring? · *defined*
**Definition:** Stiffness tells you how much pull a spring needs for each centimetre it stretches. A stiff spring needs a big pull for a small stretch. We find it by dividing the pull by the stretch.

Two bands can both be elastic, and still feel very different. One feels soft, and the other feels hard to stretch. To put a number on this, hang a known load on the band and measure how much longer it gets. Suppose a 2 newton load stretches a soft band by 6 centimetres and a stiff band by 2 centimetres. The soft band has a stiffness of 2 divided by 6, which is about 0.33 newtons for each centimetre. The stiff band has a stiffness of 2 divided by 2, which is 1 newton for each centimetre. If you draw a graph of the pull against the stretch, a stiff spring gives a steep line, and a soft spring gives a gentle line. The steepness of the line is the stiffness. Scientists call this number the spring constant. It is only a constant while the spring stays inside its elastic limit. The spring in a pen is stiff, with about 1 to 2 newtons for each centimetre. The springs in a car are very stiff, with hundreds of newtons for each centimetre. A rubber band is soft by comparison.
- e.g. A pen spring may need 1 to 2 newtons to stretch 1 centimetre. A wide rubber band may need 0.5 newtons for the same stretch.
- e.g. The springs under a bus seat are stiffer than the springs under a bicycle saddle.
- e.g. A hair tie is much stiffer than a thin rubber band of the same length.
### Two springs in a row, and two side by side · *defined*
**Definition:** Two springs in a row are softer than one, because each spring stretches and the stretches add up. Two springs side by side are stiffer than one, because they share the pull.

Link two identical bands end to end, and hang a 5 newton packet on the bottom. The same 5 newtons goes through both bands, so each band stretches as far as a single band would. The total stretch is double. For the same pull, the stretch doubles, so the pair is half as stiff. Now hang the two bands side by side, both holding the same packet. Each band now carries only half the pull, 2.5 newtons, so each stretches only half as far. The pair is twice as stiff. Take a band of stiffness 0.5 newtons for each centimetre. Alone, it stretches 10 centimetres under 5 newtons. In a row, two bands stretch 20 centimetres. Side by side, they stretch 5 centimetres. A rule hides in this: stiffnesses add when springs sit side by side. Cars use this rule. Each wheel has a spring, and the four springs work side by side to hold up the car. A softer car needs softer springs.
- e.g. One band stretches 10 centimetres under a packet. Two in a row stretch 20 centimetres. Two side by side stretch 5 centimetres.
- e.g. A bed has many springs side by side, so it is stiff enough to hold a person.
- e.g. A very long spring is softer than a short one of the same kind, because it is like many short springs in a row.
### What a bounce is, and how to time it · *defined*
**Definition:** A bounce is an oscillation. The spring pulls the load back to the middle, the load's speed carries it past the middle, and the spring pulls it back again. One full go is a cycle, and the time for one cycle is the bounce time.

Hang a bottle on a band and pull it down. When you let go, the stretched band pulls the bottle up. The bottle speeds up as it rises. When it reaches the middle place, the band is no longer pulling, but the bottle is moving fast, so it carries on upwards. Now the band is squashed or slack, and the bottle slows. It stops, and falls. It passes the middle place again, goes down past it, and stops at the bottom. That is one cycle. Then it all repeats. The time for one cycle is the bounce time. Scientists call it the period. The number of cycles in a second is the frequency, counted in hertz. To time a bounce fairly, do not time just one. A stopwatch cannot catch a fraction of a second well. Time ten bounces, then divide by ten. Repeat three times and use the middle time. Start the clock on a clear moment, such as the lowest point, and count the first one as nought. If the bounces die away fast, count eight instead of ten.
- e.g. Ten bounces take 8 seconds, so one bounce takes 0.8 seconds. The frequency is about 1.25 hertz.
- e.g. A child on a swing completes one cycle each time she returns to where she started.
- e.g. A guitar string's frequency is how many times it vibrates in a second. A lower note has a lower frequency.
### What sets the bounce time: the load and the stiffness · *defined*
**Definition:** The bounce time depends on the load and on the stiffness of the spring. A load four times bigger makes the bounce time twice as long. A spring four times stiffer makes it half as long.

A heavy load is harder to speed up, so it takes longer to go through each cycle. A stiff spring pulls harder for each centimetre, so it speeds the load up faster, and each cycle is quicker. The two ideas combine into one rule. The bounce time grows with the square root of the load, and shrinks with the square root of the stiffness. A square root is the number that, multiplied by itself, gives your number. The square root of 4 is 2, and the square root of 9 is 3. So four times the load makes the bounce time twice as long, and nine times the load makes it three times as long. For a spring with a stiffness of 0.5 newtons for each centimetre, a 0.25 kilogram load bounces in about 0.44 seconds, a 0.5 kilogram load in about 0.63 seconds, a 1 kilogram load in about 0.89 seconds and a 2 kilogram load in about 1.26 seconds. Notice that 0.89 is twice 0.44, because the load went up four times. Real rubber bands follow the rule only roughly, because rubber is not a perfect spring. The rule also does not depend on gravity. A load on a spring bounces at the same beat on the Moon, in space and on Earth.
- e.g. A schoolbag on a spring bounces slowly. A pencil box on the same spring bounces fast.
- e.g. A car with a full load of passengers bounces more slowly on its springs than an empty car.
- e.g. A ruler clamped at the table's edge wobbles faster when only a short part sticks out, because the short part is stiffer.
### Why a bigger bounce does not take longer · *defined*
**Definition:** A bigger bounce has further to go, but a spring pulls harder when it is stretched further. So the load goes faster, and the two effects cancel. The bounce time stays the same.

Most people expect a bigger bounce to take longer. Think about it from the spring's side. Pull the bottle down 2 centimetres, and the band pulls back with a small force. Pull it down 6 centimetres, and the band pulls with three times as much. A bigger pull speeds the bottle up more. So the bottle travels three times as far, but it also goes about three times as fast. The two effects cancel, and the time for one bounce stays the same. This works as long as the spring keeps pulling in even steps, that is, as long as the pull is proportional to the stretch. Springs that do this are the reason a pendulum clock and a mass on a spring keep steady time, however big the swing. Real bounces die away slowly, but the time of each one hardly changes. When the stretch gets very large, the pull stops being proportional, and then the bounce time does change a little. Rubber bands show this a bit more than steel springs do.
- e.g. A swing of a pendulum clock takes the same time whether the swing is large or small, as long as it is not too large.
- e.g. A bottle pulled down by 2 centimetres and a bottle pulled down by 6 centimetres bounce at about the same beat.
- e.g. A guitar string vibrates at the same note, whether it is plucked hard or softly. Only the loudness changes.
### The favourite beat: resonance · *defined*
**Definition:** Every spring and load has a natural beat. If you push or shake it in time with that beat, each push adds to the movement, and the bounce grows big. This is called resonance.

Think of pushing a child on a swing. A swing has a natural beat. If you push at the right moment, as the swing comes back to you, each push adds to the swing, and it goes higher with only small pushes. If you push at the wrong moment, while the swing is coming towards you, you work against its movement and slow it down. A bottle on a band is the same. Move the top end of the band up and down in time with the bottle's natural beat, and tiny movements of your hand build a big bounce. Move your hand much slower or much faster than the natural beat, and the bottle hardly moves at all. A heavier bottle has a slower natural beat, so it needs a slower shake to build up. Resonance is everywhere. A radio picks one station out of thousands, because its circuit has a favourite beat that matches that station. Musical instruments ring because their strings, skins or air columns have natural beats. The same idea helps a tuning fork ring loudly in a hall. A pushed child, a tuned radio and a ringing bell are using the same idea.
- e.g. A parent pushes a swing in time with its beat, and a tiny push builds a high swing.
- e.g. A radio tunes to one station by matching its own beat to the station's.
- e.g. Soldiers marching over a bridge are told to break step, in case their steps match the bridge's natural beat. Records of some incidents of this kind exist, though they are hard to confirm.
### Why bounces die away: damping · *defined*
**Definition:** A bounce gets smaller and smaller because friction inside the spring, and with the air, turns the movement's energy into a little heat and sound. This dying away is called damping.

A real bounce never goes on for ever. The bottle bounces smaller and smaller, and at last hangs still. Where does the movement go? Some of the energy is turned into heat inside the rubber, because the rubber chains rub against each other as they stretch. Some goes into the air as sound, and some is lost as the air rubs the bottle. Damping is often useful. A car has springs, to soak up bumps, and also dampers, called shock absorbers, to stop the springs from bouncing again and again. A shock absorber is a piston pushing oil through a narrow hole. It turns the bounce into heat. Without it, a car would keep bouncing after every pothole. Engineers choose just enough damping for each job. Too little, and the thing bounces on. Too much, and it feels hard and slow to return. A door closer, a bicycle fork and a cushion all use damping in different ways.
- e.g. A rubber band bounces about eight times before it hangs still. A steel spring bounces for much longer.
- e.g. A car's shock absorbers stop it from bouncing along the road after a pothole.
- e.g. A foam cushion absorbs a fall without bouncing the person back up.
### A rubber band warms up when you stretch it · *defined*
**Definition:** A stretched rubber band gives out a little heat, and it cools down again when it is let go. This is the opposite of what happens in a metal spring, and it comes from the way rubber is built.

Take a thick rubber band, stretch it quickly and touch it to your lip. It feels slightly warm. Hold it stretched for a few seconds, then let it go and touch it to your lip again. It feels cool. Rubber is made of long chains of molecules, tangled up like a bowl of noodles. When you stretch it, the chains straighten and line up. This is a more orderly arrangement, and as the chains line up they give out some heat. When you let go, the chains tangle up again and take heat in from their surroundings, so the band feels cool. John Gough, an English natural philosopher, noticed this around 1805. James Joule measured it carefully in 1859. The change is small, usually a few degrees at most. It is very fast, so you must stretch the band quickly, and touch it right away. The warming is one reason rubber bands do not bounce for ever. Each time they are stretched and relaxed, a small part of the movement's energy turns into heat.
- e.g. A rubber band feels warm on your lip right after a quick stretch, and cool right after it is released.
- e.g. A car tyre warms up as it flexes on the road, partly for the same reason.
- e.g. A rubber band that is stretched and let go many times in a row gets warm, like a paper clip bent back and forth.
### Tired metal: fatigue · *defined*
**Definition:** A material that is bent or stretched again and again can break, even if each bend is small. Tiny cracks start at the weak spots and grow a little with every bend, until the material snaps. This is called fatigue.

Bend a paper clip once by a small angle, and it springs back. Bend it back and forth by a big angle, and after ten or so bends it snaps. The bend spot also gets warm. Each bend leaves a tiny, invisible change in the metal. After enough bends, a crack starts, and each bend makes the crack grow. This is fatigue, the tiredness of materials. It is a danger for anything that moves again and again. A bridge has thousands of lorries crossing it. A railway line takes the weight of trains. An aeroplane's body flexes with every flight. In 1954, two de Havilland Comet jet airliners broke up in the air. Investigators traced the cause to fatigue cracks that started at the corner of a window in the cabin roof. Since then, aeroplane windows have rounded corners, and engineers test and inspect for cracks. A small crack can be found early, before it grows. Fatigue is also why a spring in a pen, after years of clicking, can break.
- e.g. A paper clip bent back and forth snaps in about ten to twenty bends.
- e.g. Two Comet jet airliners of the 1950s broke up because of fatigue cracks at a window corner, and aeroplane windows have had rounded corners ever since.
- e.g. A plastic ruler bent back and forth many times gets a white line and cracks.
### Snap-through: how a flytrap closes · *defined*
**Definition:** Some things store elastic energy by being held in a curved shape. When a small push makes them flip to the opposite curve, they snap through suddenly. The Venus flytrap uses this to close her trap in about a tenth of a second.

Press the middle of a ruler from its ends, and it bends. Push a little more, and it suddenly flips to bend the other way. Between the two bent shapes is an unstable shape, and a small push is enough to flip it over. Engineers call this snap-through or buckling. A tape measure stays straight when you pull it out, because its curved cross-section makes it stiff. Press on the end, and it suddenly kinks. A Venus flytrap uses snap-through. Her open trap is curved outwards, held in tension like a loaded spring. When an insect touches two of the small hairs inside, she closes. She closes only if the hairs are touched twice within about twenty seconds. This is a kind of counting and remembering. The two touches release a sudden change that flips the curve of the leaves from outward to inward. The trap snaps shut. Some scientists call this an automatic response. Others point out that the plant is sensing, remembering and deciding. Researchers published their study of the snap in 2005.
- e.g. A tape measure stands straight when pulled out, then folds suddenly when pressed.
- e.g. A Venus flytrap snaps shut on a fly in about a tenth of a second.
- e.g. A popper toy, a bendy plastic cup that jumps when turned inside out, uses snap-through.
### Springs that keep time · *defined*
**Definition:** Because a spring's bounce time does not change with the size of the bounce, springs make good clocks. Mechanical watches use a balance spring. Quartz watches use a crystal that vibrates. The second itself is now defined by the vibrations of atoms.

A pendulum clock does not work on a rocking ship, which is why people needed something else. In the 1670s, Christiaan Huygens and Robert Hooke both worked on small coiled springs for watches. Such a spring pulls a tiny wheel back and forth at a steady beat. This balance spring is the reason a watch can go in a pocket and still keep time. Quartz watches use a tiny crystal of quartz, cut like a small tuning fork. It vibrates 32,768 times each second. A chip counts the beats and halves the count fifteen times to get one tick each second. Atomic clocks use something even steadier: the vibrations of caesium atoms. Since 1967, the second has been defined as exactly 9,192,631,770 of these vibrations. In each case, a springy thing with a steady natural beat is counting time for us. The steadier the beat, the better the clock. A good quartz watch is wrong by less than half a minute in a month. Atomic clocks are wrong by less than a second in millions of years.
- e.g. A mechanical watch has a balance spring that swings a small wheel back and forth, ticking about five times each second.
- e.g. A quartz watch counts the 32,768 vibrations each second of a small crystal.
- e.g. The clocks in satellites that give your phone its place on the map are atomic clocks.
### Weighing without gravity · *defined*
**Definition:** A spring and a load bounce at a beat that depends on the load and the stiffness, not on gravity. So the bounce time can show how much mass a thing has, even where nothing weighs anything.

In the unit on measuring forces, we saw that a bag of rice weighs less on the Moon, but is still the same bag of rice. In space, an astronaut floats, and a bathroom scale cannot weigh her, because she does not press on it. But a spring can still weigh her mass. NASA has used springy seats for this. In the 1970s, the space station Skylab had a Body Mass Measurement Device. The astronaut strapped to a seat on springs and was set bouncing. A heavier astronaut bounces more slowly, and the time of the bounce shows her mass. You can do the same on a table at home. Clamp a ruler to a table edge. Fix a lump of clay to the free end. Flick it, and count the wobbles in ten seconds. A heavier lump wobbles more slowly. If you lay the ruler flat along the table, it works the same way. Gravity is not needed, because the beat depends on the mass and the stiffness. Weighing by wobbling measures the amount of stuff, the mass, rather than the pull of a planet.
- e.g. An astronaut's mass is found on a spring seat by timing the bounces.
- e.g. A ruler with a lump of clay wobbles more slowly than the same ruler with a small lump.
- e.g. A bathroom scale gives a different number on the Moon. A wobble scale gives the same number on the Moon.
### Springs all the way down: atoms and the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** Atoms joined by a bond can vibrate like balls on a tiny spring, billions of times in a second. Heat is the random shaking of all these tiny springs. At the other end of size, the whole Sun rings like a bell.

Zoom into a spring, and you meet atoms. Each atom is held in place by pulls from its neighbours. If you push an atom a little, its neighbours push it back, just like a spring. Atoms joined by a bond vibrate in and out like balls on a tiny spring. In a water molecule, the bonds vibrate about a hundred million million times each second. The warmth of a thing is the random shaking of all of these tiny springs. Light of the right colour can set the springs shaking more. Carbon dioxide, a gas in air, has bonds whose natural beat matches infrared light, the kind of light that carries heat. So the gas absorbs the heat that leaves the Earth. This is the resonance behind the greenhouse effect. At the huge end of size, the Sun is a ball of gas that oscillates. Its surface rises and falls about every five minutes, as sound waves ring through it. Scientists study these oscillations to look inside the Sun, a science called helioseismology. So the same idea, a springy thing with a natural beat, appears in a pen, in an atom and in a star.
- e.g. A water molecule's bonds vibrate about a hundred million million times each second.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide absorbs infrared light, because the light's beat matches the beat of its bonds.
- e.g. The surface of the Sun rises and falls once about every five minutes.
### Singing plates and Sophie Germain · *defined*
**Definition:** A thin springy plate has natural beats too. Sand sprinkled on a plate that is vibrating at one of these beats gathers in lines where the plate stays still. Sophie Germain worked out the mathematics of this in 1816.

In the early 1800s, a German scientist named Ernst Chladni travelled around Europe showing something strange. He scattered sand on a thin metal plate and drew a violin bow along its edge. The plate sang, and the sand jumped into beautiful patterns of lines. The sand collects where the plate does not move. A different note gives a different pattern. In Paris, the French Academy of Sciences offered a prize for a theory that could explain the patterns. It was a hard problem, since it needed new mathematics. Sophie Germain, who had taught herself mathematics from books in her father's library, entered the contest. Women were not allowed to study at the university or to join the Academy. She entered anonymously, and her early tries did not win. After a third try, she won the prize in 1816. Her work helped found the study of how elastic bodies bend and vibrate. Her result was not perfect, and others later improved on it. But the ideas she opened up are used today to design bridges, buildings and instruments.
- e.g. Sand on a vibrating plate gathers into lines that shift when the note changes.
- e.g. The patterns look like the ones you see on the bottom of a drum or a guitar body when they ring.
- e.g. Engineers use the mathematics of vibrating plates to design the floors of bridges and the walls of aeroplanes.
### Are the smallest things tiny vibrating strings? · *idea*
Everything we know is made of particles such as electrons and quarks. Some physicists have an idea, called string theory, that these particles are not points at all. They suggest that each one is a tiny thread of energy, and that the different particles are different notes of vibration, like the different notes of a guitar string. An electron might be one note, and a photon another. The idea is beautiful, and the mathematics holds together. But nobody has found a way to test it by experiment, because the strings would be unimaginably small. Many physicists are excited by it. Others say that a theory that cannot be tested is not yet science. It is an open question.
- e.g. A guitar string makes many different notes by vibrating in different ways.
- e.g. String theory suggests that different particles may be different vibrations of the same kind of thread.
- **Many string theorists:** The idea joins gravity and the other forces in one picture, and its mathematics is rich and consistent.
- **Critics of string theory:** No experiment has been able to test it, and a theory that cannot be tested may be mathematics rather than a description of nature.
- **Other physicists:** There are other ideas, such as loop quantum gravity, for the very small and the very early universe. It is too soon to say which, if any, is right.

## Mostly agreed
- A spring's stiffness is the pull it needs for each centimetre it stretches, and stiffnesses add when springs sit side by side. *How we know:* Hang the same packet on one band, on two bands in a row and on two bands side by side. The stretch is 10, 20 and 5 centimetres for bands of 0.5 newtons for each centimetre, which matches the rule.
- The bounce time of a load on a spring depends on the load and the stiffness, and not on the size of the bounce. *How we know:* Ten bounces from a 2 centimetre pull and ten bounces from a 6 centimetre pull take about the same time. Four times the load makes the bounce time about twice as long. Pendulum clocks and balance springs depend on this.
- A spring and its load bounce at the same beat on Earth, on the Moon and in space. *How we know:* The beat depends on the load and the stiffness, and gravity does not come into it. Astronauts on Skylab found their mass by bouncing on a spring seat.

## How we found out
**John Harrison (1693 to 1776), an English clockmaker, He finished his fourth sea timekeeper in 1759, and it was tested at sea in 1761 and 1762.** For centuries, sailors could find how far north or south they were, but not how far east or west. To find that, they needed to know the exact time at a faraway port, and compare it with the Sun's time on their ship. Pendulum clocks failed at sea, because the rocking of the waves threw off the swing. In 1714, the British Parliament asked experts, including Isaac Newton, how to solve the problem. Newton told them that a clock accurate enough for use at sea had not yet been made. Many astronomers believed that the answer would come from the Moon and the stars. John Harrison, an English clockmaker, thought otherwise. He built clocks and watches with springs and balanced wheels in place of pendulums, so that rocking could not stop them. His fourth, a large watch finished in 1759, was tested on a voyage to Jamaica in 1761. It was found to be only about five seconds slow after the voyage. A balance spring keeps its beat however the ship tilts.  
*What people believed before:* Many experts believed that no clock could keep accurate time on a rocking ship, with changes in heat, damp and gravity. Harrison showed it could be done, with a clock that used a spring, not a pendulum, and the sea trial proved it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A bigger bounce takes longer than a small one.** (Most people, who guess that a longer path takes more time.; *rejected*)  
  For: A bigger bounce has further to go, and it looks slower.  
  Against: A bigger stretch makes the spring pull harder, so the load goes faster. The two effects cancel. Ten bounces of 2 centimetres and ten bounces of 6 centimetres take about the same time.
- **A heavier load makes a spring bounce faster.** (Some people, who think a heavier thing has more push.; *rejected*)  
  For: A heavier load stretches the spring further, and the spring pulls harder.  
  Against: The spring pulls harder, but the load is also harder to speed up. The load wins. A heavier load bounces more slowly, and four times the load makes the bounce time twice as long.
- **A stiffer spring is a stronger spring.** (Many people, who use 'stiff' and 'strong' for the same thing.; *rejected*)  
  For: A stiff spring is hard to stretch, which feels like strength.  
  Against: Stiff means a big pull for a small stretch. Strong means able to take a big load without breaking. A stiff rubber band can snap at a small stretch, and a soft steel spring can take a huge load. A glass rod is stiff but breaks easily.
- **The Tacoma Narrows Bridge fell in 1940 because of resonance with the wind.** (Many textbooks and videos.; *debated*)  
  For: The bridge twisted in a wind of about 65 kilometres an hour, and the movement grew until the deck broke.  
  Against: Engineers who studied the bridge later found that the wind did not push it at its natural beat. They found that the bridge's own movement drew energy from the steady wind, an effect called flutter. This is a different effect from simple resonance.
- **A spring only stores energy. It never loses any.** (Many students who learn about ideal springs.; *rejected*)  
  For: A good steel spring bounces many times and seems to give back almost all the energy.  
  Against: No real spring is perfect. A rubber band warms up and bounces only a few times. A steel spring bounces for longer, but it too loses a little each time, to heat and sound.
- **Only coiled metal is a spring.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: The word spring brings to mind a coil of wire.  
  Against: Anything elastic acts like a spring: a ruler, a diving board, a tree branch, a tendon and even the bond between two atoms. A spring is anything that pulls back in proportion to how far it is pushed.

## Roots
We have bounced bags, shaken bands at the right beat and watched a clip tire out. Now we ask what the beat of a spring tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Favourite Beat test, small hand movements made the bag bounce high when they matched its own beat. At other beats, the same movements made it bounce hardly at all. A push adds to the last push only if it comes at the right moment. So what a push does depends on its timing as well as its strength.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think the result of a push depends on how hard it is. That leaves out timing, which can make a tiny push build into a huge movement.  
*Evidence:* In the Favourite Beat test, a hand movement of about 2 centimetres made the bag bounce much further at its own beat than at other beats. If your own numbers differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your arm swings like a pendulum when you walk. Each person has an easy walking pace, and it is the pace that takes the least effort.
- *what is life:* Many living beings keep a steady beat. A hummingbird's wings, a cricket's chirp and a human heart each repeat at a rhythm that suits the body.
- *what is this world:* A bond between two atoms, a swing, a bridge and the surface of the Sun all have a natural beat. The world is full of springy things, each with its own favourite rhythm.

A swing, a band, a watch, a bond between two atoms and the whole Sun all behave as springs with a natural beat. They are very different things, and one rule describes all of them. Atoms hold on to each other with the electric force, and that pull-back is what makes anything springy. Whether there is a still deeper layer, such as tiny vibrating strings, is an idea that nobody has yet been able to test.

- **Ecclesiastes 3:1:** To everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven. (A push builds a swing only at the right moment. The same push at another moment adds nothing.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.50:** Yoga is skill in action. (A parent who pushes a swing well does not push harder. She pushes at the right moment, and the swing does the rest.)
- Science (settled): Every springy thing has a natural beat set by its stiffness and its load, and pushes at that beat build a large movement. This is resonance.
- Science (idea): Whether the smallest particles are tiny vibrating strings is an idea that has not been tested.
- Other view, A hard-nosed engineer: Resonance is simple mathematics that holds for anything that can be described by a spring and a mass. Talk of a world full of favourite beats adds nothing to the equations.
- Practice, Find your own easy beat (3 min): Stand up and let one arm hang loose. Swing it forwards and backwards gently, without pushing hard. Notice how it settles into a steady beat. Now try to swing it faster, and then slower. Feel how much harder that is. Go back to the easy beat and breathe in slowly three times.
- Ask: Is the strength of a push the only thing that decides what it does?
- Ask: Your arm has an easy beat that takes the least effort. Does every part of you have one?
- Ask: If a bond between two atoms, a swing and the Sun all have a natural beat, is having a beat a fact about the thing, or about how we describe it?

## Story: Push When It Comes Back
*A made-up story. The lane, Saroja, Paati, Karuppasamy and the others are imagined. The science is real. Small pushes timed with the natural rocking of a vehicle on its springs and soft tyres build up a big rocking, which is how stuck vehicles are often rocked out of mud. Pushing a swing at the right moment works in the same way.*

Saroja lived in a narrow lane near the big temple in Madurai, where the evening rain turned the road into brown soup. One Tuesday, the milk van came down the lane as the sun was setting. Its back wheel dropped into a hole where the road had been dug up for a new pipe. The van stopped with a jolt, tilted like a tired buffalo.

Karuppasamy, the driver, pressed the pedal until the engine screamed and mud flew up the wall. The wheel spun, and the hole got deeper.

People came out of their houses. Mani Anna, who sold tea at the corner, came with a cloth over his shoulder. Two college boys came, and the watchman, and Fatima Akka from the tailoring shop, her hands still dusty with chalk. 'Everyone push!' shouted the driver. They stood behind the van in a line and shoved with all their strength.

The van moved a finger's width, and then rolled back into the hole. They shoved again, groaning. It moved a finger's width, and rolled back. After ten minutes they were hot and muddy and tired, and the hole was still full of van.

Saroja watched from her verandah steps, where her little brother Kannan was swinging on the old wooden unjal. Its chains creaked softly. Every evening she pushed him, and every evening Paati, her grandmother, said the same thing from her chair: 'Don't push the swing when you feel like it. Push when it comes back to you.'

Saroja looked at the van again. Something about it looked familiar. Each time the people shoved, the van moved forward a little, and then it moved back. It was not just stuck. It was rocking, very slightly, forwards and backwards on its springs, and on its soft tyres in the soft mud. It was rocking like a slow swing.

She ran down the steps and into the lane. 'Stop!' she shouted. 'Don't push all together!' Some of the men turned and looked at her. A girl of ten, with muddy feet, in the middle of the road.

'The van is rocking,' Saroja said. 'Look! It goes forward, and then it comes back. It has its own beat, like Kannan's swing. When you all push at once, some of you push while it is coming back, and you stop each other. Push only when it comes back to you. Then push it forward.'

The college boys laughed. But Fatima Akka wiped her hands on her sari. 'Let the child try,' she said. 'Nothing else is working.'

So Saroja stood on a stone by the wall where everyone could see her. 'Wait,' she said. They waited. The van rocked forward a tiny bit, stopped, and rocked back. 'Now!' she called, just as it began to roll forward again. They all pushed together. The van lurched forward a little more than before.

'Wait.' It rolled back. 'Now!' They pushed. It went a little further. 'Wait.' 'Now!' With every push, the rocking grew. A finger's width. Two fingers. A hand. A forearm. Nobody pushed harder than before. They only pushed at the right moment.

On the eighth push, the van's back wheel climbed the lip of the hole with a loud squelch and rolled out onto the road. The lane filled with cheering. Karuppasamy jumped out of the cab, shaking his head. 'How did you know?' he asked.

Saroja shrugged. 'It's like a swing,' she said. 'Paati says you can't hurry it. You can only join it.'

From her chair on the verandah, Paati lifted one eyebrow, the way she did when she was pleased and trying not to show it.

That night, Saroja pushed Kannan on the unjal and counted slowly. One swing took about three seconds, whether he was swinging high or low. She pushed him a little at the wrong moment, and he slowed down. She pushed him a little at the right moment, and he went higher. Every time, a small push at the right moment did more than a big push at the wrong one.

**For the parent:** For the parent: every springy thing, including a swing, a vehicle on its springs and a bottle on a band, has a natural beat. A push adds to the movement only if it comes at the right moment, so that it goes with the movement and not against it. Many small pushes in time build a big movement. This is called resonance. Saroja noticed that the van was rocking, worked out its beat, and called the pushes at the right moment, while the grown-ups pushed hard but at random.

- Ask: Why do many small pushes at the right moments move a swing higher than a few big pushes at the wrong moments?
- Ask: What is a natural beat, and what two things set the natural beat of a bottle on a band?
- Ask: When you push a swing as it is coming towards you, what happens to the swing? Why?
- Ask: Why does a heavier swing, with a heavier rider, have a slower natural beat?
- Ask: Name another springy thing, other than a swing or a vehicle, where timing the pushes could make a small push build a big movement.

  *Parent says:* Move your hand up and down once for each tick. Keep the movement small, about 2 centimetres. After ten ups and downs, the helper tells us how big the bag's bounce got. Then we speed up.  
  *Child mode:* Move your hand up and down to the ticks. Ten times at each speed. How big does the bag bounce?
  - Ask: At which speed did the bag bounce the most? (good: At the speed close to the bag's own beat.; Not the fastest, and not the slowest.; At about the speed we measured in the last step.)
  - Note: The bag should bounce more than the hand moves when the beat is close to its own, perhaps 4 to 10 centimetres for a 2 centimetre hand movement, though rubber bands vary. At a much slower beat, the bag just follows the hand. At a much faster beat, it hardly moves at all. Rubber damps the bounce, so the best speed is a range rather than a single value. Read the biggest bounce as the distance between the highest and lowest place of the bottom of the bag. Hang the top loop over a pencil that you hold, so that the band cannot slip off your finger. No metronome? Use the stopwatch: the helper says go and stop, and you must finish ten ups and downs in 20 seconds (30 a minute), 15 seconds (40), 10 seconds (60), 7.5 seconds (75-80), 6 seconds (100) or 4 seconds (150).
  - Check: The bag's own beat is 80 beats a minute. At which hand speed do you expect the biggest bounce? → **About 80 beats a minute**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add the second packet, so that the bag holds 1000 grams. Ask your child to guess whether the best speed is faster or slower. Test the same six speeds. Write the biggest bounce for each.  
  *Parent says:* Now the bag is twice as heavy. Will its favourite beat be faster or slower? Test the same six speeds and find out.  
  *Child mode:* Make the bag heavier. Will its favourite beat be faster or slower? Test it.
  - Note: A heavier load has a slower natural beat, about 0.7 times the beat of the 500 gram load, since the square root of 2 is about 1.4. So the best speed should drop from about 80 beats a minute to about 57. The nearest speeds to test are 60 and 40, and 60 will probably give the biggest bounce, though with soft bands the two may be close. If so, say that the best speed is between them.
  - Think: Why would a heavier bag want a slower shake?
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at your table. Circle the biggest bounce in each column. Write a claim, evidence and reasoning about the favourite beat and the load.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? Which speed made the biggest bounce for each bag? Why does a push at the right beat build a big bounce, and a push at the wrong beat do very little?  
  *Child mode:* Circle the biggest bounce in each column. What did you find? Why?
  - Ask: Why did the heavier bag have a different favourite beat? (good: A heavier bag bounces more slowly on its own, so it wants a slower shake.; The bag's own beat changed.; The push must match the bag's own beat.)
  - Think: If you shook much harder at the wrong beat, would the bounce grow? Try it.

### a1 · The Stiffness League: which stretchy thing is stiffest? (28 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up takes the spring out of the pen, because it can spring out and the small parts are easy to lose. Count the pieces back afterwards, and keep them from the youngest child's mouth. Keep the load low over a cushion, and stop if a band looks thin and pale.
- **record:** A table with each thing, its relaxed length, its length with the load, its extension and its stiffness. A bar chart of the stiffness of the four things. The extension of the spring and the wide band with 250 grams and with 500 grams.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Line up the four things. Ask your child to guess the order, from softest to stiffest, and to say why. Write the guess down. With one child, the parent measures while the child hangs and writes. With 2 to 4 children, each child tests a different thing, then everyone shares the numbers and draws the chart.  
  *Parent says:* Pull each thing gently with your fingers. Which feels softest and which feels stiffest? Put them in order and say why.  
  *Child mode:* Pull each thing gently. Put them in order, softest to stiffest. Why?
  - Ask: How can you tell which thing is stiffest? (good: It is the hardest to stretch.; It needs the biggest pull for the same stretch.; It stretches the least under the same load.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Children often say the biggest thing is the stiffest. Write the guess down.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Measure the relaxed length of the first thing. Hang the 250 gram bag from it and wait for it to stop moving. Measure the new length. Take away the first to get the extension. Do this for all four.  
  *Parent says:* Measure the relaxed length. Hang the bag and wait. Measure again. The extension is the second length minus the first. Do this for each thing.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the length. Hang the bag. Measure again. Take away the first number.
  - Note: The load is the same for every thing, which is what makes this a fair test. Measure each thing from the same two places, for example from the hook to the bottom of the knot. A grown-up should take the spring out of the pen. A pen spring may stretch very little, perhaps 1 or 2 centimetres, so measure it carefully. Hang the load gently, do not drop it. Wait about 10 seconds, because a band creeps a little longer. Measure a band or a hair tie as a loop, from end to end, lying relaxed.
  - Check: Why must the load be the same for all four things? → **So that the differences in stretch come from the things, not from the load**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out each stiffness. Divide 2.5 newtons by the extension in centimetres. Draw a bar chart with four bars. Then hang a second 250 gram packet too (500 grams in all) on the pen spring and on the wide band. Does the stretch double?  
  *Parent says:* The load is 2.5 newtons. Divide it by the extension in centimetres. A bigger answer means a stiffer thing. Draw a bar for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Divide 2.5 by each extension. A bigger answer is stiffer. Draw a bar for each.
  - Note: For example, an extension of 5 centimetres gives 2.5 divided by 5, which is 0.5 newtons for each centimetre. Typical values, which will differ with your things: a pen spring 1 to 3, a wide band 0.3 to 1. A thin band is usually softer than a wide band of the same length. With 500 grams, a pen spring stretches about twice as far as with 250 grams, because a spring follows the rule that double the pull gives double the stretch. A rubber band may stretch more or less than double, because it is not so regular. Say what you saw, whatever it is.
  - Check: A thing stretches 5 cm under 2.5 N. A second thing stretches 1 cm under 2.5 N. Which is stiffer, and how many times? → **The second, 5 times as stiff**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the chart. Compare it with your first guess. Ask your child which thing surprised them most.  
  *Parent says:* Was the order what you guessed? Which thing surprised you most? What would you use the stiffest thing for, and the softest?  
  *Child mode:* Was your order right? Which thing surprised you most?
  - Think: If you hang the same load on two bands side by side, how stiff is the pair?
### a2 · Hot Lip: the band that warms when you stretch it (15 min)
- **safety:** Do not do this if you are allergic to rubber. Each child uses their own band, because it touches the lip. Hold the band in both hands, stretch it only to about three times its length, point it away from the eyes, and never let go of it near your face. Old or sun-dried bands snap more easily, so stop if a band looks thin or cracked.
- **record:** A table with three tests: a quick stretch, a held stretch then a release, and a slow stretch. For each, write warm, cool or no change. A blind-test score out of 6.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the thick band. Ask whether it will feel warm, cool or the same on the lip after a quick stretch. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* I will stretch the band and touch it to my lip. Will it feel warm, cool or the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Stretch the band quickly. Touch it to your lip. Warm, cool or the same? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the band might change its temperature? (good: Stretching it is hard work, so it gets warm.; Rubbing inside it makes heat.; It will feel the same, because I am only stretching it.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the band against your upper lip. Stretch it quickly to about three times its length. Feel it. Hold it stretched for 5 seconds, and then relax it quickly against the lip and feel it again.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the band to your lip. Stretch it fast, to three times its length. Is it warm? Hold it stretched for five seconds. Now let it relax and touch your lip again. Is it cool?  
  *Child mode:* Stretch the band fast. Feel it on your lip. Hold, then relax it. Feel again.
  - Note: The change is small, about a degree or a few degrees, but a lip is sensitive. Stretch the band as fast as you can, and touch it within a second. The relaxed band should feel cool. If you feel nothing, try a thicker band or stretch it further. In hot weather the band is already near body warmth, so the warm feel is small and the cool feel after release is easier to notice. Work in the coolest room.
  - Check: A band feels warm right after it is stretched and cool right after it is let go. What is the best explanation? → **Stretching lines up the rubber chains and gives out heat. Relaxing lets them tangle, which takes heat in**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stretch another band very slowly, over about 30 seconds, and touch it to your lip. Compare this with the quick stretch. Write what you find.  
  *Parent says:* Now stretch the band very slowly, over half a minute. Touch it to your lip. Is it as warm as before?  
  *Child mode:* Stretch a band very slowly. Touch it to your lip. Is it as warm?
  - Note: A slow stretch gives the heat time to spread into the air and your fingers, so it hardly feels warm. This is one reason you must be quick.
  - Think: Why would a slow stretch feel less warm than a fast stretch?
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Blind test. One child shuts their eyes. Their partner tosses a coin six times in secret. On heads, the partner stretches the band quickly and touches the child's lip. On tails, the partner touches it with the unstretched band. The child says warm or no change each time. Count how many are right out of 6. With one child, the parent is the partner. With 2 to 4 children, take turns, with one as the coin-tosser and one as the writer.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. I will touch the band to your lip, sometimes stretched and sometimes not. Tell me warm or no change. Let us see how many you get right out of 6.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Say warm or no change. How many are right out of 6?
  - Note: Pure guessing gets about 3 right out of 6. A score of 5 or 6 shows that the warmth is real and not imagined. If the score is low, stretch further and faster, touch within a second, and say honestly that the warming is small.
  - Think: Why do we use a coin and closed eyes?
### a3 · Tired Clip: how many bends before it breaks? (25 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up bends the clips, or watches closely if an older child does it. The broken ends are sharp and can fly. Hold the clip low, over the tray, and keep your face away. The bend gets warm, so touch it gently. Count the pieces and throw them away safely, out of reach of small children.
- **record:** A table with the angle of bend, the number of bends before the clip broke, and whether the bend felt warm.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up bends one clip a little and lets go, and then bends another a lot and lets go. Ask your child what happened in each case. Ask them to guess how many back and forth bends it takes to break a clip. First open each clip out into a straight wire, gently. Then every clip is bent at a fresh spot in the middle. With one child, the parent bends and the child counts. With 2 to 4 children, one counts, one touches the bend, one writes.  
  *Parent says:* The first clip sprang back. The second stayed bent. Now I will bend a clip back and forth. How many bends do you think it will take to break? Why?  
  *Child mode:* One bend: the clip springs back or stays bent. How many bends to break it?
  - Ask: Why do you think the clip will break after that many bends? (good: Each bend wears it out a little.; It needs a lot of bends, because a clip is tough.; It will break at the first big bend.)
  - Note: A small bend stays inside the elastic limit and the clip springs back. A big bend goes past the limit and the clip stays bent. Do not correct the guess. Write it down.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up bends a clip wire back and forth by about 90 degrees against the paper guide. One bend each way counts as one. The child counts aloud. Touch the bend lightly between bends. Stop when the clip breaks. Do this with 3 clips.  
  *Parent says:* I bend the clip back, and then forward. That is one bend. You count out loud. Touch the bend lightly. Is it warm? Stop when it breaks.  
  *Child mode:* Count each bend aloud. Touch the bend lightly. Is it warm? Stop when it breaks.
  - Note: Typical: a plain steel clip often breaks after about 5 to 20 bends of 90 degrees, but clips differ a lot, so use the middle of your three. The bend feels warm. The break usually starts at the outside of the bend, where the metal is stretched most. Keep the clip low over a tray. Wear glasses if you have them.
  - Check: A clip bends fine once, but snaps after 12 bends back and forth. What is the best explanation? → **Each bend leaves a tiny change, and tiny cracks grow until it snaps**
- **s3 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Bend three more clip wires back and forth by about 45 degrees, using the guide. Count the bends to break. Compare the middle count with the 90 degree clips. Write what you find.  
  *Parent says:* Now bend a clip by only half as far, about 45 degrees, three times. Count the bends until each breaks. Does it take more bends or fewer?  
  *Child mode:* Bend clips by a smaller angle. Count the bends. More bends or fewer?
  - Note: A smaller bend usually takes many more bends, often three times as many or more, but the numbers vary a lot. If a clip does not break after 50 bends, stop and write 50 or more. This shows why engineers keep bends small in parts that flex many times.
  - Think: Why might a smaller bend take more bends to break the clip?
### a4 · The Tape Measure Snap: why a thin strip folds suddenly (19 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up holds the tape measure. The tape edge is sharp and can cut, and it can snap back fast. Keep your fingers away from the tape's edge, keep faces away from above the tape, and never let it spring back. Press the end gently.
- **record:** A drawing of the tape standing out, the tape kinked, and a note about what flattening the curve did. How far the curved tape, the card strip and the pinched tape stand out before they droop.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up stands the tape measure on the table with the case flat. The grown-up pulls 30 centimetres of tape straight up and locks it. With one child, the parent holds and the child presses. With 2 to 4 children, one presses, one watches the middle, one writes.  
  *Parent says:* The tape is sticking out straight. If I press the end gently, what will happen? Will it bend slowly or suddenly?  
  *Child mode:* The tape stands out straight. What happens when the end is pressed?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen when you press the end? (good: It will bend slowly.; It will fold suddenly.; It will stay straight.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A curved tape stays surprisingly straight until the push reaches a limit, then folds all at once.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Press down gently on the end of the tape with one finger, along the line of the tape. Notice that nothing happens until a certain push, and then the tape kinks suddenly. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Press gently down on the end. Watch the middle of the tape. Does it bend a little at a time, or all at once?  
  *Child mode:* Press the end gently. Does the tape bend slowly or all at once?
  - Note: Press gently along the tape, not sideways. Keep your fingers off the edge. The tape's curved cross-section makes it stiff, and it stays straight until the push passes a limit. Then it flips into a fold. This is buckling. A flat strip of card bends smoothly, with no sudden snap, and you can try it for comparison.
  - Check: The tape stays straight as you press harder, and then folds suddenly. What does this tell you? → **There is a limit to the push, and passing it makes the tape fold all at once**
- **s3 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Squeeze the tape flat between your finger and thumb, and press the end again. Compare it with the first time. Now lay the case on the table edge and slowly pull the tape out sideways. Measure how far it stands out before it droops. Do the same with the card strip held at one end, and then pinch the tape flat just outside the case.  
  *Parent says:* Squeeze the tape flat for a moment. Now it is floppy. Why is the curved tape stiff, and the flat tape floppy?  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze the tape flat. Is it still stiff? Try a flat card strip.
  - Note: The curve makes the tape like a very shallow tube, and a tube resists bending much more than a flat strip of the same material. Engineers use curved and tubular shapes for the same reason, in bicycle frames, bamboo, drinking straws and aeroplane bodies. Typical: a curved tape stands out about half a metre to over a metre, depending on its width; a flat card strip droops at about 10 to 15 centimetres; a tape pinched flat folds at once. No tape measure? Roll a sheet of paper into a tube and tape it: stood on end, it holds a few books, while the same flat sheet folds at once.
  - Think: Why does a curve make a thin strip stiff?
### a5 · The One-Second Bouncer: tune a bag to bounce once each second (25 min)
- **safety:** Stand to the side and keep your face away from the bands. Never use more than 1 kilogram of load, and keep the cushion under the bag.
- **record:** A table with each load, the time for ten bounces, the time for one bounce, and a note of whether it is too fast or too slow.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Time ten bounces with 500 grams, three times. Find the middle time and divide by ten. Write the bounce time for one bounce. With one child, the parent times and the child counts and writes. With 2 to 4 children, one pulls, one times, one calculates the load, one writes. Swap after each try.  
  *Parent says:* Time ten bounces with half a kilogram, three times. Find the middle time. Divide by ten. That is the time of one bounce. Is it more or less than one second?  
  *Child mode:* Time ten bounces with 500 g. Find one bounce. More or less than one second?
  - Note: A bounce time under one second with 500 grams is usual. For example, 7.5 seconds for ten bounces is 0.75 seconds for one bounce. The task is to make it exactly one second.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out the load needed. The bounce time must grow from your time to 1 second. Divide 1 by your time, and multiply the answer by itself. Multiply that by 500 grams. Write the load.  
  *Parent says:* We need a bigger bounce time, so we need a heavier load. Divide one second by your time. Multiply the answer by itself. That is how many times heavier. Multiply by 500 grams.  
  *Child mode:* Divide 1 by your time. Multiply the answer by itself. Multiply by 500 g.
  - Note: Bounce time grows with the square root of the load, so the load grows with the square of the time. For example, a bounce time of 0.75 seconds needs a time of 1 second. 1 divided by 0.75 is 1.33. 1.33 times 1.33 is 1.78. 1.78 times 500 grams is about 890 grams. Use your own numbers. If the answer is over 1000 grams, stop at 1000 grams, add a third band to the chain (that makes it softer and slower), and start again from the first step. If your time with 500 grams is already over one second, you need a lighter load.
  - Check: A load of 500 g bounces in 0.5 seconds. About what load will bounce in 1 second? → **About 2000 g**
  - Think: Why do you multiply the answer by itself?
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Weigh out the new load on the kitchen scale. Hang it. Time ten bounces three times. Find the middle time. Write how close it is to ten seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Weigh out the load you worked out. Hang it and time ten bounces, three times. Is the middle time close to ten seconds?  
  *Child mode:* Weigh the new load. Hang it. Time ten bounces. Is it near 10 seconds?
  - Note: Rubber bands follow the rule only roughly, so you may land within about 10 percent of ten seconds. That is a good result for this activity. Timing by hand is only good to about 0.3 seconds, so a result of 9.5 to 10.5 is a fine place to stop.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If the time is too short, add a little rice. If it is too long, take a little out. Test again. Stop when ten bounces take between 9.5 and 10.5 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Too fast, add some rice. Too slow, take some out. Try again. How close can you get to exactly one second a bounce?  
  *Child mode:* Too fast? Add rice. Too slow? Take some out. Test again.
  - Ask: How did your calculation compare with the real answer? (good: It was close but not exact, because rubber is not perfect.; I had to add a bit of rice.; The rule worked well.)

### p1 · The Wobble Scale: weigh a lump of clay without gravity (50 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** A ruler can snap if it is bent too far, and plastic can crack into sharp edges. Flick it gently, keep your face away from it, and stop if it cracks. Clay is not food: wash hands afterwards.

With 2 to 4 children, one flicks, one times, one counts, one writes.

  *Child mode:* Stick clay on the ruler's end. Flick it. Will a heavier lump wobble faster or slower?
  - Ask: Why do you think a heavier lump will change the wobble? (good: It is harder to move, so it wobbles slower.; The ruler pulls harder, so it wobbles faster.; A heavier lump pulls the end down more.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Press the book down hard, so that the ruler does not slip. If the ruler wobbles too fast to count with a 20 gram lump, slide more of it off the table, so that 25 centimetres stick out.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stick the 20 gram lump on the end of the ruler. Flick the end down about 1 centimetre and let go. Time ten wobbles. Do it three times and use the middle time. Do the same with 40 grams and 80 grams.  
  *Parent says:* Stick the lump to the end. Flick the end down a little and let go. Count ten wobbles and stop the clock. Do it three times. Then do the same with the 40 gram and 80 gram lumps.  
  *Child mode:* Stick the clay on the end. Flick. Time ten wobbles. Do three times. Then try heavier clay.
  - Note: Typical: about 3 to 8 seconds for ten wobbles with a thin, bendy plastic ruler. The 80 gram time should be about 1.7 to 2 times the 20 gram time, not exactly 2, because the ruler has a little mass of its own. A stiff or thick ruler wobbles faster: if ten wobbles take under 3 seconds, slide more of the ruler out, or time 20 wobbles and halve. If the ruler droops more than about 5 centimetres under the 80 gram lump, slide less out. Use the same flick size each time. If the wobbles die away before ten, count eight and divide.
  - Check: Ten wobbles take 4 seconds with 20 grams. About how long do you expect for 80 grams? → **About 8 seconds**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a graph with the mass in grams along the bottom and the time for ten wobbles up the side. Plot the three points. Draw a smooth curve through them, and carry it from 0 to 100 grams.  
  *Parent says:* Plot the three points. Draw a smooth curve through them. It will curve a little, because the time grows with the square root of the mass. This graph is your scale.  
  *Child mode:* Plot three points. Draw a smooth curve through them.
  - Note: The curve should bend over, rising quickly at first and then more slowly. Do not draw a straight line. Do not extend the curve far past 80 grams.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, stick a mystery lump on the ruler. Time ten wobbles three times. Find the time on your graph, go across to the curve, and then down to the mass. Do the same for the second lump. Weigh both on the kitchen scale and compare. With 2 or more children, each shapes a mystery lump in secret for another to weigh by wobble.  
  *Parent says:* Day two. Time ten wobbles of a mystery lump. Find the time up the side of the graph, go across to the curve and down to read the mass. Then check it on the kitchen scale.  
  *Child mode:* Time a mystery lump. Use the graph to read its mass. Check it on the kitchen scale.
  - Ask: How close was your wobble scale to the kitchen scale? (good: It was within a few grams.; It was close for one lump and not so close for the other.; It worked, which is surprising for a ruler.)
  - Note: A difference of 10 to 15 percent is a good result for a ruler. Use mystery lumps that weigh between 20 and 80 grams, so that the graph can be trusted. Lumps that are heavier or lighter than your known ones are out of range. Wobble times of the same lump can differ by a few tenths of a second, so use the middle of three.
- **s5 (13 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep the 40 gram lump on the end and change only how much ruler sticks out. Try 15, 20 and 25 centimetres. Time ten wobbles three times at each. Compare the three middle times.  
  *Parent says:* Now we change the ruler, not the clay. A short ruler is stiff and a long ruler is soft. Which will wobble faster? Time ten wobbles at 15, 20 and 25 centimetres with the 40 gram lump.

Gravity only bends the ruler down a little and then the wobble goes on around that place, so the same scale works in space.
  - Check: With the same clay, a ruler sticking out 15 centimetres wobbles faster than the same ruler sticking out 25 centimetres. What does this show? → **The beat depends on the stiffness as well as the mass**
  - Think: The clay is pulled down by gravity, but does the wobble need gravity? What sets the beat?

### c1 · Soft Landing: keep a stack of coins standing (40 min)

### Stiffness, bounces and beats (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A band stretches 4 cm under a 2 N load. What is its stiffness?  
   Options: 0.5 N for each cm / 2 N for each cm / 8 N for each cm  
   **Answer:** 0.5 N for each cm. *Why:* Stiffness is the pull divided by the stretch. 2 divided by 4 is 0.5. The answer 8 multiplies the numbers instead of dividing them.
2. (truefalse) A bottle bounces on a band. If you pull it down twice as far before letting go, ten bounces take twice as long.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A bigger bounce goes further, but the band pulls harder and the bottle goes faster. The two effects cancel, so the time stays the same.
3. (fill) To find the time of one bounce, you time ten bounces and then ____ the time by ten.  
   **Answer:** ['divide']. *Why:* Ten bounces take ten times as long as one, so you divide by ten. Timing ten shrinks the error of the stopwatch.
4. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Natural beat → the beat a spring and its load bounce at on their own; Resonance → small pushes in time with the beat build a big movement; Damping → the bounce slowly dying away; Fatigue → a material wearing out from being bent again and again. *Why:* The natural beat is set by the load and the stiffness. Resonance needs pushes at that beat. Damping is where the movement goes as heat and sound. Fatigue is the slow cracking of a material that flexes over and over.
5. (mcq) A bag of rice on a band bounces so that ten bounces take 6 seconds. You hang a bag that is four times heavier. About how long do ten bounces take?  
   Options: About 12 seconds / About 24 seconds / About 6 seconds  
   **Answer:** About 12 seconds. *Why:* Four times the load makes the bounce time twice as long, because the square root of 4 is 2. Twice 6 is 12. The answer 24 seconds multiplies by 4. The answer 6 seconds forgets that the load matters.
6. (sort) How does each change affect the bounce time of a load on a band?  
   **Answer:** a heavier load → Longer; a stiffer spring → Shorter; a bigger bounce → The same; two bands in a row instead of one → Longer; two bands side by side instead of one → Shorter; taking the bag and bands to the Moon → The same. *Why:* A heavier load and a softer spring make the bounce slower. A stiffer spring makes it faster. The size of the bounce and the strength of gravity do not change the beat.
7. (write) Why does a small push at the right moment make a swing go higher, when a bigger push at the wrong moment does not?  
   **Answer:** A swing has its own natural beat. A push at the right moment goes with the swing's movement and adds to it. Pushes at the right moment build up a big swing. A push at the wrong moment works against the movement or does not add, so the swing does not go higher.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the swing has a natural beat, that a push in time adds to the movement, and that a push out of time does not.
8. (mcq) One band stretches 10 cm under a packet. How far do two identical bands side by side stretch under the same packet?  
   Options: 5 cm / 20 cm / 10 cm  
   **Answer:** 5 cm. *Why:* Two bands side by side share the pull, so each carries half and stretches half as far. The pair is twice as stiff. The answer 20 cm is for two bands in a row.
9. (order) Put these steps for timing a bounce fairly in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Pull the load down by a measured amount', 'Let go and start the clock', 'Count ten bounces and stop the clock', 'Repeat the timing three times', 'Take the middle time and divide it by ten']. *Why:* You set a fair start first, then time ten bounces, repeat to see the spread, and finally find the time of one bounce from the middle time.
10. (count) A load bounces once every 0.8 seconds. How many bounces does it make in 20 seconds?  
   **Answer:** 25. *Why:* 20 divided by 0.8 is 25. A bounce time of 0.8 seconds means 1.25 bounces in every second, and 1.25 times 20 is 25.
11. (truefalse) A thick rubber band feels warm on your lip right after you stretch it quickly.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The long chains in the rubber line up as it stretches and give out a little heat. The change is small, so you need a quick stretch and a sensitive place like the lip.
12. (draw) Draw a graph with the pull along the bottom and the stretch up the side. Draw one line for a stiff spring and one for a soft spring. Label each line.  
   **Answer:** Two straight lines from the corner. The soft spring's line climbs steeply upward, since it stretches a lot for each newton. The stiff spring's line climbs gently, since it stretches only a little for each newton.. *Why:* On a graph of stretch against pull, a soft spring has a steep line and a stiff spring has a gentle line. If the axes are the other way, the stiff one is steep.
13. (mcq) What does a quartz watch count to keep time?  
   Options: The vibrations of a tiny crystal / The swings of a pendulum / The movement of the Sun  
   **Answer:** The vibrations of a tiny crystal. *Why:* A quartz watch uses a tiny crystal that vibrates 32,768 times each second. A chip counts the beats. A pendulum clock is a different kind of clock, and the Sun is used by a sundial.
14. (count) Two bands in a row stretch 20 cm under a packet. How far will the same packet stretch two such bands side by side?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* Two bands in a row stretch 20 cm, so each band alone stretches 10 cm. Two bands side by side stretch half as far as one, which is 5 cm.
15. (write) A paper clip survives one big bend, but snaps after bending back and forth ten times. Explain what is happening.  
   **Answer:** Each bend leaves a tiny change in the metal at the bend. Tiny cracks start and grow a little with every bend. After enough bends, the crack goes right through and the clip snaps. This is called fatigue.. *Why:* A strong answer names fatigue, and says that small changes add up with each bend until a crack goes through.
16. (mcq) A bottle bounces on a band and the bounces get smaller and smaller. What stays about the same?  
   Options: The time for one bounce / The size of the bounce / The speed of the bottle  
   **Answer:** The time for one bounce. *Why:* The size of the bounce falls, but the bounce time depends on the load and the stiffness, so it stays about the same. The speed of the bottle also falls, since smaller bounces are slower.
### Square roots, resonance and clocks (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)

   Options: Four times as stiff / A quarter as stiff / The same  
   **Answer:** Four times as stiff. *Why:* Bands side by side share the pull and their stiffnesses add. Four bands are four times as stiff. A quarter as stiff would be for four bands in a row.

   **Answer:** ['3']. *Why:* 6 divided by 2 is 3. The spring needs 3 newtons for each centimetre.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The bounce time depends on the load and the stiffness, and gravity does not come into it. The bottle would bounce at the same beat on the Moon.
5. (sort) Is each of these an example of resonance?  
   **Answer:** pushing a swing at the end of each swing → Resonance; a radio picking out one station → Resonance; a ball bouncing lower and lower on the floor → Not resonance; one tuning fork making a second fork ring → Resonance; a paper clip snapping after many bends → Not resonance; pushing a swing at random moments → Not resonance. *Why:* Resonance is when pushes or signals at a thing's own natural beat build up a big response. A ball getting lower is damping, and a snapped paper clip is fatigue.
6. (count) A load of 1 kg bounces in 1 second. What load on the same spring would bounce in 3 seconds, in kilograms?  
   **Answer:** 9. *Why:* To triple the bounce time, the load must be 3 times 3, which is 9 times as heavy. So the load is 9 kilograms.

9. (write) Soldiers are told to break step when they cross a bridge. Explain why, using the idea of a natural beat.

## Applications
- Choosing the springs for a car, a bus or a motorbike, with dampers to stop the bouncing.
- Building mechanical watches and quartz watches that keep time with a steady beat.
- Defining the second with atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of atoms.
- Tuning musical instruments, by changing the stiffness, length or load of strings and skins.
- Tuning a radio to a station, using a circuit with a natural beat.
- Designing buildings and bridges so that wind, traffic and earthquakes do not push them in time with their natural beat.

## Open questions
- **Are the smallest particles tiny vibrating strings?** String theory is mathematically rich, but nobody has found an experiment that can test it. Some physicists think it is the most promising idea, and others doubt it.
- **How does a Venus flytrap count touches, and where does she keep the memory?** Scientists know the trap needs two touches within about twenty seconds. They are still working out the chemistry and the electrical signals that store the first touch.
- **Can we predict exactly when a metal part will fail from fatigue?** Engineers can estimate the number of bends a part will take, but the exact moment depends on tiny flaws that are hard to see. They use safety margins and inspections.
- **Why does the Sun oscillate in the way it does, and what does it tell us about its inside?** Scientists learn a great deal from the Sun's ringing. They are still working out a few puzzles about the Sun's centre and its magnetic field.
- **How steady can a clock be?** Atomic clocks keep getting steadier, and new kinds are being built. Nobody knows how far this can go, or whether there is a limit.
- **Why do the same simple rules of springs and beats appear in swings, atoms and stars?** Physicists can explain how this works with mathematics. Why nature repeats the same patterns at such different sizes is a question people are still asking.

## Connected maths
- Look at the table of three made-up springs, all pulled with 2 N. They stretched 6 cm, 2 cm and 1 cm. Which spring is the stiffest? How many times stiffer than the 6 cm spring is the 1 cm spring? **The 1 cm spring is the stiffest. It is 6 times as stiff as the 6 cm spring.**. The stiffnesses are 2 divided by 6, which is about 0.33, 2 divided by 2, which is 1, and 2 divided by 1, which is 2. 2 divided by 0.33 is about 6. You can also see it directly: the same pull stretches the first spring 6 times as far as the third. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In the Bounce Time test, ten bounces took 8.4 seconds. How long does one bounce take? How many bounces happen in one second, to one decimal place? **One bounce takes 0.84 seconds. About 1.2 bounces happen in each second.**. 8.4 divided by 10 is 0.84. One divided by 0.84 is about 1.19, which rounds to 1.2. A bounce time of 0.84 seconds is a frequency of about 1.2 hertz. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A 250 gram load bounces ten times in 5 seconds. Use the square-root rule to predict the time for ten bounces with 1000 grams, and with 2250 grams. **10 seconds with 1000 grams, and 15 seconds with 2250 grams.**. 1000 grams is 4 times 250 grams, and the square root of 4 is 2, so 2 times 5 is 10 seconds. 2250 grams is 9 times 250 grams, and the square root of 9 is 3, so 3 times 5 is 15 seconds. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- A band has a stiffness of 0.5 N for each cm. A 6 N packet hangs on it. How far does it stretch? How far do two such bands stretch in a row, and how far side by side? **12 cm for one band, 24 cm for two in a row, and 6 cm for two side by side.**. 6 divided by 0.5 is 12 cm for one band. Two in a row are half as stiff, with 0.25, and 6 divided by 0.25 is 24 cm. Two side by side are twice as stiff, with 1, and 6 divided by 1 is 6 cm. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Look at the line graph of made-up results from the Favourite Beat test. At which hand speed did the bag bounce the most? How many times bigger was that bounce than the bounce at 30 beats a minute? **The biggest bounce was at 80 beats a minute. It was 9 times bigger than at 30 beats a minute.**. The tallest point is at 80 beats a minute, with a bounce of 9 cm. At 30 beats a minute, the bounce was 1 cm. 9 divided by 1 is 9. The peak is at the bag's natural beat. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- A metronome ticks 80 times each minute. How many seconds are there between ticks? The bag's natural bounce time is 0.8 seconds. Is the metronome a little faster or a little slower than the bag? **0.75 seconds between ticks. The metronome is a little faster than the bag.**. 60 seconds divided by 80 ticks is 0.75 seconds. The bag takes 0.8 seconds for one bounce. 0.75 is shorter than 0.8, so the metronome is a little faster. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- A quartz watch crystal vibrates 32,768 times each second. A chip halves the count again and again until it gets 1. How many times does it halve? **15 times.**. 32,768 halves to 16,384, then 8,192, then 4,096, then 2,048, then 1,024, then 512, then 256, then 128, then 64, then 32, then 16, then 8, then 4, then 2, then 1. That is 15 halvings. It is because 2 multiplied by itself 15 times is 32,768. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- The Sun's surface rises and falls once about every 5 minutes. About how many times does that happen in an hour, and in a day? **About 12 times in an hour and about 288 times in a day.**. An hour has 60 minutes, and 60 divided by 5 is 12. A day has 24 hours, and 24 times 12 is 288. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What does the stiffness of a spring tell you? → How much pull it needs for each centimetre it stretches.
- Two bands side by side hold a load. Are they stiffer or softer than one band? → Stiffer, because they share the pull.
- How do you find the time of one bounce from ten bounces? → Divide the time for ten bounces by ten.
- Does a bigger bounce take longer than a small one? → No. The time stays about the same.
- What happens to the bounce time if the load is four times heavier? → It becomes twice as long.
- What is resonance? → Pushing a spring in time with its natural beat, so that small pushes build a big movement.
- Why does a stretched rubber band feel warm? → The rubber chains line up and give out a little heat.
- What is fatigue? → Cracks growing in a material that is bent or stretched again and again.
- What does an atomic clock count? → The vibrations of atoms.
