# Plant parts: how each part's shape suits its job
`sci.plants.plant-parts.L3`

**Summary.** A potato grows under the ground, but it is a stem, not a root. Its eyes are buds, and buds grow on stems. Every part of a plant has a shape that suits its job. Thin roots reach between soil grains to soak up water, and fat roots and stems store food. Climbing stems are thin and hold on to a support, and flat leaves spread out to catch sunlight. In deserts, ponds and salty mud, the parts take new shapes to fit.

**Intro.** *Look at a carrot and a potato. Both grow under the ground. Both are fat and full of food. But only one of them is a root. Which one? And how could you ever tell?* A carrot and a potato look alike, but they are different parts of a plant. The carrot is a root. The potato is a stem that grows under the ground. Its 'eyes' show this, because only stems have buds like that. In this unit you will learn to tell each part by its clues, not by where it grows. You will also find out why each part of a plant has the shape it has.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the parts of a plant and say the main job of each one.
1. I can explain how a part's shape suits its job, with examples from roots, stems and leaves.
2. I can tell a root from a stem, even under the ground, using clues like eyes, rings and buds.
3. I can give examples of roots and stems that store food, stems that climb and leaves that catch light, and say how each one helps the plant.
4. I can run a fair test, measure carefully, and show my results in a table and a bar chart.
5. I can explain how plants in hard places, like deserts, ponds and salty mud, have parts with special shapes.
6. I can say what scientists agree on about plant shapes and what they still argue about. I can link plant shapes to light, atoms, the history of the Earth and even other planets.

## Terms
- **Root**: The part of a plant that is usually under the ground. It holds the plant in the soil and soaks up water. *e.g.* Pull up a weed from a crack in the wall. The thin white threads at the bottom are their roots.
- **Stem**: The part that holds the plant up and joins the roots to the leaves. Water travels up it from the roots, and food from the leaves travels through it to the rest of the plant. *e.g.* A tree trunk is a stem. So is the soft green stalk of a coriander plant.
- **Leaf**: The usually flat, green part of a plant. Leaves catch sunlight and use it to make the plant's food. *e.g.* A mango leaf, a tulsi leaf and a blade of grass are all leaves.
- **Job**: What a part does for the plant. Scientists call it the part's function. *e.g.* The job of a root is to hold the plant in the soil and soak up water. The job of a leaf is to catch light.
- **Shape suits the job**: Each part of a plant has a shape that helps it do its job well. Scientists say form fits function. *e.g.* Leaves are thin and flat to catch lots of light. Roots are long and thin to reach far into the soil.
- **Adaptation**: A shape or habit that helps a living thing survive where they live. *e.g.* A cactus has spines instead of wide leaves. That helps them keep water in the hot desert.
- **Tap root**: One thick main root that grows straight down, with thinner roots growing out of its sides. *e.g.* Mustard, neem and carrot plants have tap roots.
- **Fibrous roots**: A bunch of many thin roots, all about the same size, spreading out like a beard. *e.g.* Grass, wheat, rice and onions have fibrous roots.
- **Root hair**: A tiny hair on a young root. Root hairs push between soil grains and soak up water. *e.g.* Look at a moong sprout's root against black paper. The white fuzz is root hairs.
- **Storage root**: A root that grows fat because it is full of stored food. *e.g.* Carrot, radish, beetroot and shakarkand, which is sweet potato, are storage roots.
- **Bud**: A tiny folded-up shoot, waiting to grow into new leaves, a branch or a flower. Buds grow on stems, not usually on roots. *e.g.* The 'eyes' of a potato are buds. Leave an old potato in a dark basket and they grow shoots.
- **Node**: The place on a stem where a leaf or a bud grows. Nodes often look like rings or bumps. *e.g.* The rings on sugarcane and on a piece of ginger are nodes.
- **Tuber**: A fat underground stem that stores food. It has buds, called eyes, that can grow into new plants. *e.g.* A potato is a tuber.
- **Rhizome**: A stem that grows sideways under the ground. It stores food and has rings and buds. *e.g.* Ginger and turmeric (manjal, or haldi) are rhizomes.
- **Bulb**: A tiny flat stem with thick, juicy leaves wrapped round it. The leaves store food. *e.g.* An onion is a bulb. Each layer you peel is the swollen bottom of a leaf.
- **Climber**: A plant with a thin stem that grows upwards by holding on to something else. *e.g.* Money plant, bean, grape and bottle gourd are climbers.
- **Twiner**: A climber whose stem winds round and round a support, like a spiral staircase. *e.g.* A bean plant twines round a stick.
- **Tendril**: A thin, curly thread that a climbing plant uses to grab hold of things. It coils when it touches something. *e.g.* Pea and bottle gourd plants have tendrils.
- **Aerial root**: A root that grows in the air, out of a stem or branch, instead of under the ground. *e.g.* A banyan tree's hanging roots, and the roots a money plant uses to climb, are aerial roots.
- **Prop root**: A root that grows down from the stem or a branch into the ground, and holds the plant up like a pillar. *e.g.* Old banyan trees stand on many pillar roots. Maize and sugarcane have short prop roots near the ground.
- **Breathing root**: A root that sticks up out of wet mud into the air, so the plant can get air to their roots. *e.g.* Mangrove trees in the Sundarbans have breathing roots poking up like pencils.
- **Leaf blade**: The wide, flat part of a leaf. *e.g.* A banana leaf has a huge blade. A pine needle has a very thin one.
- **Vein**: The thin lines in a leaf. They carry water in and food out, and they hold the leaf open like the spokes of an umbrella. *e.g.* Hold a leaf up to the window. You can see its veins.
- **Stoma (many are called stomata)**: A tiny hole in a leaf's skin, too small to see without a microscope. Air goes in and water vapour comes out through it. *e.g.* One leaf can have many thousands of stomata.
- **Chlorophyll**: The green substance in leaves. It catches sunlight so the leaf can make food. *e.g.* Chlorophyll is why leaves, grass and peas are green.
- **Spine**: A hard, sharp point on a plant. A cactus's spines are leaves that have changed shape. *e.g.* Touch a cactus gently and you feel its spines.
- **Cell**: The tiny building block that every living thing is made of. A leaf is built from millions of cells. *e.g.* Onion skin under a microscope looks like a wall of tiny bricks. Each brick is a cell.
- **Botanist**: A scientist who studies plants. *e.g.* Janaki Ammal was an Indian botanist who studied sugarcane.

## Explanations
### Every part has a job, and the parts need each other · *defined*
**Definition:** Most plants have roots, a stem and leaves. Many also have flowers, which make fruits and seeds. Each part has its own job, and the parts need each other. A cut-off part lives on only if it can grow the parts it is missing, as a money plant cutting grows new roots in a glass of water.

The roots hold on and soak up water. The stem holds everything up and carries water and food between the roots and the leaves. The leaves catch sunlight and make food. The flowers make seeds, and the fruits protect the seeds and help spread them. Botanists, the scientists who study plants, split the plant into two halves. Everything under the ground is the root system. Everything above the ground is the shoot system. The roots need food from the leaves. The leaves need water from the roots. So each part depends on the others.
- e.g. Cut a rose and put it in a vase. Without roots it lasts only a few days.
- e.g. Cover every leaf of a plant and they slowly starve, even with plenty of water.
- e.g. In a coriander bunch from the market, you can see roots, stems and leaves on one small plant.
### Shape suits the job · *defined*
**Definition:** The shape of each plant part helps it do its job. Scientists say form fits function. Form means shape, and function means job.

Look at the parts of any plant and ask, 'Why this shape?' A root is long and thin and branches again and again, so it can reach water far away and grip lots of soil. A leaf is thin and flat, so it can spread out to catch light. A tree trunk is thick and round, so it can stand up to wind from any side. Here is the surprise. Plants that are not related at all often end up with the same shapes when they live in the same kind of place. Cactuses in America and some euphorbias in Africa look almost the same. Both are fat, green and spiny. But they come from different plant families. Each family came to this shape on its own, because the same shape works in the same kind of place.
- e.g. A lotus leaf is round and flat, so it floats like a plate.
- e.g. A pine needle is thin and tough, so it loses little water in cold, dry winds.
- e.g. A bean stem is thin and bendy, so it can wind round a stick.
### Roots: holding on and soaking up water · *defined*
**Definition:** Roots hold the plant in the ground and soak up water and minerals from the soil. Some plants have one big tap root. Others have a bunch of thin fibrous roots.

A tap root is one thick main root that grows straight down, like a tent peg. It can reach deep water and hold a plant firm. Fibrous roots are a beard of thin roots near the top of the soil. They grip the soil like a net and catch rain quickly. Just behind the tip of each young root are root hairs, far thinner than the hairs on your head. They push between the soil grains and soak up water. Roots are thin and many because a thin root can squeeze between soil grains, and many roots touch far more soil than one fat root could. In 1937 a scientist called Howard Dittmer grew one rye plant in a box of soil for four months. Then he washed out and measured their roots. Together they were about 600 kilometres long. The root hairs added more than 10,000 kilometres. The unit on how water travels through a plant follows the water from these hairs up to the leaves.
- e.g. Mustard, neem and carrot have tap roots.
- e.g. Grass, wheat, rice and onion have fibrous roots.
- e.g. In the Kalahari Desert in Africa, roots of a shepherd's tree have been found about 68 metres down. That is deeper than a 20-storey building is tall.
### Roots that store food · *defined*
**Definition:** Some roots grow fat because they are full of stored food. The food was made in the leaves and sent down the stem to be kept for later.

A carrot, a radish, a beetroot and a sweet potato are all storage roots. They taste sweet or starchy because they are packed with food. But the plant did not store that food for us. They stored it for their own use. A carrot's life has two parts. First they grow leaves and fill their root with sugar. Then, after the cold weather, they use that stored food very fast. They send up a tall stalk with white umbrella-shaped flowers, and makes seeds. In cold countries this takes two years. In north India it happens between autumn and the next spring. Farmers in north India use this. They dig up their carrots in winter, choose the best ones and plant them again. By spring those carrots have flowered and made seed for next year. If you dig up a carrot after they have flowered, the root is woody and not sweet. Their food has gone up into the flowers and seeds.
- e.g. A carrot top left in a saucer of water grows new leaves, using food stored at its top.
- e.g. Sweet potato, shakarkand, is a root, even though a potato is a stem.
- e.g. Beetroot is so full of sugar that a kind of beet, called sugar beet, is grown in many cold countries to make sugar.
### Roots with surprising jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Roots can do more than hold on and drink. Some roots prop the plant up like pillars. Some breathe. Some climb. Some even grow into living bridges.

A banyan tree sends down thin roots from their branches. When they reach the ground, they grow thick and woody, like pillars. These prop roots hold up the huge branches, so the tree can spread wider and wider. Mangrove trees grow in salty mud by the sea, where there is almost no air in the soil. Their breathing roots poke up out of the mud like pencils, and air gets in through tiny holes. A money plant and a paan creeper climb walls with short roots that grow out of their stems and grip like fingers. Orchids hang on trees with spongy roots that soak up rain from the air. In Meghalaya, Khasi and Jaintia people guide the aerial roots of rubber fig trees across streams. Over many years the roots grow thick, join together and become bridges strong enough for people to walk on.
- e.g. The Great Banyan in the botanic garden at Howrah, near Kolkata, stands on more than 3,000 pillar roots.
- e.g. Maize and sugarcane grow short prop roots near the ground, like tent pegs.
- e.g. In the Sundarbans, mangrove breathing roots stick up out of the mud at low tide.
### Stems: holding the plant up and carrying water and food · *defined*
**Definition:** A stem holds the leaves up to the light and the flowers up to the insects. Inside, tiny tubes carry water up from the roots and food from the leaves to the rest of the plant.

A stem's shape depends on how much it has to hold up. A herb, like coriander, has a soft green stem because it holds up only a few leaves. A tree has a thick woody trunk, because they must hold up a huge crown against wind and rain. Many stems are tubes. Bamboo and wheat stems are hollow tubes. At each ring, called a node, a solid wall runs across the inside of the tube, and this stops the tube from squashing flat when it bends. A tube is very strong for its weight. People make poles and bicycle frames from tubes for the same reason. Not every stem is hollow. Sugarcane is filled with soft, juicy tissue that stores sugar. Stems also have nodes, the places where leaves and buds grow. That is how botanists tell a stem from a root, even under the ground. Stems have nodes and buds. Roots usually do not.
- e.g. Bamboo is a giant grass with a hollow stem, so strong that it is used for scaffolding.
- e.g. The rings on sugarcane are nodes, where leaves once grew.
- e.g. A tree trunk grows thicker every year, so it can hold up more branches.
### Stems that store food · *defined*
**Definition:** Some stems grow fat and store food, often under the ground. Potato, ginger, turmeric, chembu and sugarcane all store food in their stems.

A potato looks like a root, but it is a stem. You can tell from its eyes. Each eye is a bud, and the eyes sit in a spiral round the potato, just as leaves sit in a spiral round a stem. Ginger and turmeric are stems that grow sideways under the ground. Look for the rings on them. Those are nodes. Chembu is a short, fat underground stem. Sugarcane stores sugar in their tall stem above the ground. An onion is a little different. Its bottom is a tiny flat stem, and its juicy layers are swollen leaves full of stored food. So the onion stores food in its leaves. Plants store food in underground stems so they can survive a dry season or a cold winter, and grow again quickly when the rain comes.
- e.g. Farmers cut seed potatoes into pieces, making sure each piece has at least one eye. Each eye can grow a new plant.
- e.g. A piece of ginger left in a damp place grows a green shoot from one of its buds.
- e.g. Sugarcane is crushed to get its sweet juice, which is made into jaggery and sugar.
### Stems that climb · *defined*
**Definition:** A climber is a plant with a thin stem that reaches the light by holding on to something else. Some climbers twine round a support. Others grab with tendrils, hook on with thorns or cling with roots.

Plants need light to make their food. In a crowded garden or forest, the tallest plants get the most light. A tree reaches the light by building a thick trunk, which takes years. A climber grows a thin stem that needs much less food to build, and they lean on something else. A twiner, like a bean, winds their whole stem round a stick. A pea or a gourd sends out tendrils, curly threads that coil round anything they touch. Bougainvillea hooks on with thorns. A money plant clings with little roots. A young climbing shoot does not grow straight. Its tip slowly sweeps round in circles, feeling for something to hold. Charles Darwin timed a hop shoot. One full circle took about two hours. He also found that some tendrils start to curl within a minute or two of a touch.
- e.g. Scientists counted the twining plants of the world. About 9 out of every 10 kinds wind the same way.
- e.g. Hops and honeysuckle are famous for twisting the other way.
- e.g. Amarbel, called dodder, is a twining plant with no proper leaves. They feed on other plants, and as a seedling they find them by smell.
### The tendril's double twist · *defined*
**Definition:** When a tendril grabs a support, it coils into a spring that twists one way at one end and the other way at the other end. In the middle there is a switch point.

Once the tip of a tendril has caught hold, both of its ends are fixed. One end is on the plant and one end is on the support. A thread fixed at both ends cannot coil all one way without twisting itself up. So it coils one way for part of its length and the other way for the rest, and the two twists cancel out. Darwin saw this switch point and described it. In 2012, a team of scientists at Harvard University worked out exactly how it happens. One side of the tendril shrinks more than the other, so it curls. The spring shape pulls the plant closer to their support, and lets them stretch a little in the wind without snapping.
- e.g. An old curly telephone cord often has a switch point where the twist changes direction.
- e.g. A coiled gourd tendril pulls the vine up towards the string it caught.
### Leaves: shaped to catch light · *defined*
**Definition:** Most leaves are thin, flat and wide. This shape spreads them out to catch as much sunlight as possible.

A leaf works like a solar panel. A wide, flat shape catches lots of light. A thin leaf lets the light and air reach every cell inside it. The veins hold the leaf open, like the spokes of an umbrella. Leaves are also arranged so they do not shade each other. Look down on a plant from above. The leaves fit together like a jigsaw, with few gaps and few overlaps. Many plants grow their leaves in a spiral round the stem, each one turned a little from the last, so every leaf gets its own patch of sky. Leaves even change shape with the light. Leaves in the shade grow bigger and thinner, to catch every bit of weak light. Leaves in full sun are smaller and thicker. The unit on photosynthesis explains what the leaf does with the light it catches.
- e.g. Shade plants, like the money plant on a dim landing, often have big, thin leaves.
- e.g. In a field of grown sunflowers, almost every flower head faces east, towards the morning Sun.
- e.g. Look down on a tulsi plant. Each pair of leaves sits at right angles to the pair below.
### Leaves in hard places · *defined*
**Definition:** Where light, water or food is hard to get, leaves change shape. Some become spines, some float, some store water and some even catch insects.

In a desert, a wide leaf would lose too much water. So a cactus has no wide leaves. Their leaves have become spines that guard them, and their fat green stem does the leaf's job. Aloe stores water in thick, juicy leaves. On a pond, a lotus leaf is round and flat, so it floats. Its tiny holes, the stomata, are on the top side, facing the air, not underneath in the water. Its skin is covered with tiny waxy bumps, so water rolls off in balls and carries the dirt away. Scientists call this the lotus effect. In the Amazon, a giant water lily grows leaves wider than a car, held up by thick ribs underneath. A garden designer called Joseph Paxton said those ribs gave him the idea for a huge glass building in London, the Crystal Palace, in 1851. In Meghalaya, a pitcher plant grows in poor soil. Their leaves end in jugs that trap insects, which feed them.
- e.g. Pine trees in cold mountains have needle leaves that lose little water.
- e.g. Chembu (arbi) leaves, like lotus leaves, make raindrops roll off in shiny balls.
- e.g. Jalkumbhi, water hyacinth, has puffy leaf stalks full of air, so they float on ponds.
### Is every part secretly a leaf? · *debated*
About 230 years ago, a German poet and thinker had a bold idea. He said that petals, tendrils, spines and many other parts above the ground are all leaves that have changed shape. Botanists have argued about how far this idea goes ever since. Some parts really are changed leaves. Others are not. And for some, like a gourd's tendril, botanists still disagree.
- e.g. A pea's tendrils are the tips of their leaves. You can see them growing where the last leaflets should be.
- e.g. A cactus spine is a changed leaf.
- e.g. Bougainvillea's thorns are changed branches, not leaves. So not every part is a leaf.
- **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, a German poet and thinker, in 1790:** In a book called The Metamorphosis of Plants, he said every part above the ground, even the parts of a flower, is the same basic leaf in a different form.
- **Agnes Arber, an English botanist, in 1950:** She studied plant shapes for fifty years, much of it in a small laboratory in her own home. She argued that a leaf is really a 'part of a shoot' that stopped growing early. She was the first woman botanist chosen as a member of Britain's top science society, the Royal Society, in 1946.
- **Scientists studying plant genes, from 1991:** Enrico Coen and Elliot Meyerowitz found the genes that turn a leaf into each part of a flower. When those genes are switched off, a flower grows only leaf-like parts. So Goethe was partly right about flowers. But other parts, like many thorns, come from stems.
### Inside a leaf: cells, chlorophyll and packets of light · *debated*
Zoom into a leaf and you find millions of cells, packed like bricks. Inside each cell are tiny green blobs full of chlorophyll. Keep zooming. Each chlorophyll molecule is a ring of atoms with one magnesium atom at its centre. Light is made of tiny packets of energy called photons. When a photon hits a chlorophyll molecule, its energy is caught and passed along, from molecule to molecule, to the place where food is made. All of that is agreed. Scientists argue about one part. How does the energy find its way so fast, and lose so little on the way?
- e.g. A leaf is only a few cells thick, so light can reach the green blobs in almost every cell.
- e.g. Chlorophyll catches red and blue light best. It catches less green light, so more green bounces back to our eyes.
- **Gregory Engel, Graham Fleming and colleagues, in America, in 2007:** In the light-catching parts of some bacteria, the energy seemed to behave like a wave. It seemed to spread along many paths at once, instead of hopping along one path. This is an idea from quantum physics, the science of the very tiny.
- **Later scientists, from about 2017:** Repeating the tests in conditions more like real sunlight, some found the wave effect fades far too quickly to matter much. The question is still argued about.
### Why are leaves green and not black? · *idea*
This sounds like a silly question, but it is a real puzzle. A black leaf would soak up every colour of light. A green leaf still soaks up most of the green light, but it lets more green bounce off than red or blue. Green is one of the strongest parts of sunlight. So why are almost all leaves green? Nobody is completely sure. In 2020, Nathaniel Gabor and his team in California suggested an idea. Sunlight flickers up and down as clouds and leaves move. A leaf may be tuned to catch light steadily, without being flooded when the Sun bursts out. Letting some green go may keep the leaf's light-catcher running smoothly, like a car that avoids sudden speeding up and braking. Other scientists think plants are green simply because of the history of life. Their ancestors in the sea used this kind of chlorophyll, and plants never switched.
- e.g. Some leaves look red or purple, like red amaranth, lal saag. They still have chlorophyll, hidden by red colours.
- e.g. Sunlight is strongest in the blue-green and green part of the rainbow.
- **Nathaniel Gabor and colleagues, 2020:** Leaves let some green light go to keep their energy supply steady when the light keeps changing.
- **Other scientists:** Leaves are green because their ancient ancestors in the sea were built that way, and changing would be too hard. Both ideas may be partly true.
### How plants grew their parts: a story 470 million years long · *defined*
**Definition:** The first plants on land had no roots and no leaves. Over many millions of years, plants grew roots, then woody stems, then wide leaves, and much later flowers.

For about three billion years, almost all life lived in water. Then, about 470 million years ago, small green plants began to live on land. Fossils show that some of the earliest, like Cooksonia, were just thin forked stems, shorter than your finger, with no roots and no leaves. In rocks at Rhynie in Scotland, about 407 million years old, scientists found whole little plants turned to stone. They had no true roots. By about 400 million years ago, plants had roots and woody stems. By about 385 million years ago there were the first forests of real trees. Wide flat leaves only became common later, about 360 million years ago. Flowering plants came last, about 130 million years ago. Why did wide leaves take so long? One idea, from David Beerling and colleagues, is about the air. Early air had much more carbon dioxide, so leaves needed only a few stomata to take it in. Leaves cool down by letting water out through their stomata. With so few stomata, a wide leaf in the sun would have overheated. That idea is still being tested.
- e.g. Roots changed the whole Earth. They cracked rocks and helped make the first deep soils.
- e.g. Much of India's coal formed from forests of plants that grew about 250 to 300 million years ago.
### Plants on other worlds · *idea*
If there are plants on planets around other stars, what would they look like? Their leaves would be shaped by the light they get, just like leaves on Earth. Nancy Kiang, a scientist at NASA, worked on this with her team. Many stars are smaller and redder than our Sun. On a planet circling a red star, the light is dim and reddish. Plants there might be dark, even black, to catch every bit of it. On a planet with a brighter, bluer star, plants might reflect more light and look yellow or orange. Nobody has found plants on another planet. This is a careful guess, based on the rules that shape leaves here.
- e.g. Our Sun is a yellow-white star. A lot of its light reaches the ground as the colours we can see.
- e.g. Most stars in our galaxy are small red stars, and many planets have been found circling them.

## Mostly agreed
- Each part of a plant has a job, and its shape helps it do that job. *How we know:* Botanists have compared thousands of plants. Plants that live in the same kind of place often grow the same shapes, even when they are not related.
- Some roots and stems store food for the plant to use later. *How we know:* Povidone-iodine, a brown liquid from the chemist, turns blue-black on a cut potato. That shows stored starch. A carrot top in water grows new leaves with no soil, using its stored food.
- You can tell a stem from a root by its nodes and buds. *How we know:* A potato's eyes grow into shoots. Ginger and sugarcane have rings where leaves once grew. Most roots have no buds like these.
- Climbing plants sense touch and move to hold on. *How we know:* Charles Darwin timed tendrils curling after a touch, and drew the circles made by growing shoot tips. Anyone can repeat these tests with a gourd or pea plant.
- Leaves are arranged and shaped to catch light. *How we know:* Photos taken from above plants show leaves fitting together like a jigsaw. Leaves grown in shade are bigger and thinner than leaves grown in sun on the same plant.
- The first land plants had no roots or leaves. *How we know:* Fossils of the oldest land plants, such as those in the 407-million-year-old rocks at Rhynie in Scotland, show small stems without true roots or leaves.

## How we found out
**Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1790); Enrico Coen and Rosemary Carpenter in England, and Elliot Meyerowitz and his team in America (1980s to 1991); Takashi Honma and Koji Goto in Japan (2001), Goethe's idea in 1790; the flower genes explained in 1991; leaves turned into petals in 2001.** In 1790 the German poet and thinker Johann Wolfgang von Goethe wrote a short book about plants. He had noticed parts that were halfway between a leaf and a petal. He guessed that every part of a flower is a leaf that has changed shape. Many botanists treated petals, stamens and leaves as completely different kinds of part. For about 200 years nobody could test Goethe's guess properly. Then, in the 1980s, scientists studied flowers that had grown wrongly. Enrico Coen and Rosemary Carpenter in England worked on snapdragons. Elliot Meyerowitz and his team in America worked on a small weed called thale cress. In some odd flowers, petals grew where the stamens should be. In 1991, Coen and Meyerowitz explained it. A few genes, working together, decide what each part of a flower becomes. When scientists switched off three of these genes, the flower grew only green, leaf-like parts. In 2001, Takashi Honma and Koji Goto in Japan did the reverse. They switched these genes on in ordinary leaves, and the leaves grew like petals.  
*What people believed before:* That petals, stamens and leaves are completely different kinds of part. Flowers with changed genes showed that they are all built on the plan of a leaf, and a few genes decide which shape each one takes. Goethe also went too far: some sharp points, like bougainvillea thorns, are changed branches, not leaves.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Potatoes and carrots are both roots, because they both grow under the ground.** (Many people, and some picture books; *rejected*)  
  For: Both grow in the soil, both are fat, and both are dug up.  
  Against: A potato has eyes, which are buds, and buds grow on stems. Put an old potato in a dark basket and its eyes sprout shoots. A carrot has no eyes along its sides. Where a part grows does not tell you what it is. Its clues do.
- **Plants are simple, because they cannot move.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants have no muscles and no brain. They stay in one place all their lives.  
  Against: Plants move, just slowly. Shoot tips sweep round in circles. Tendrils coil round a stick within hours, sometimes minutes. Leaves turn to face the light. Speed up a film of a growing bean and they look like they are searching.
- **Fat roots grow big so that people and animals can eat them.** (A way of thinking many of us have without noticing; *rejected*)  
  For: We grow carrots and radishes for food, and farmers choose the biggest ones.  
  Against: The root stores food for the plant's own future. A carrot left in the ground uses their stored sugar to grow a tall flower stalk and seeds. People did breed carrots to be bigger and sweeter, but the store was there first, for the plant.
- **Roses have thorns.** (Almost everyone, in songs and sayings; *rejected*)  
  For: They are sharp and pointed, like thorns.  
  Against: Botanists say a true thorn is a changed branch, like bougainvillea's. A rose's sharp points grow only from their skin. You can snap one off cleanly, and there is no bud near it. Botanists call them prickles. In everyday speech, though, nobody minds if you call them thorns.
- **Charles Darwin was the first person to study how plants climb.** (Many popular books; *rejected*)  
  For: Darwin wrote a famous book on climbing plants, in 1875, and did many clever experiments.  
  Against: In 1827, two German botanists, Hugo von Mohl and Ludwig Palm, each wrote books about how climbing plants twine and grab. Darwin read them and named them in his own work. Famous names often stand on the work of people we forget.
- **Leaves are green because green is the best colour for catching sunlight.** (A natural guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Leaves are so good at catching light that their colour must be the best one.  
  Against: A leaf looks green because it lets more green light bounce off than red or blue. Black would catch more light. Why leaves let some green go is still being studied.

## Roots
We have been learning how roots, stems and leaves are each shaped for their job. Now we ask what a plant's shape tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A potato is a stem and a sweet potato is a root. Yet both are fat, both grow underground, and both are packed with stored food. The same job gave two different parts the same shape. So to tell a stem from a root, look for buds, like the potato's eyes.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things by how they look and where we find them. Fat and underground seems to mean root. But what a part is depends on what it grows from, and its buds show that. Its shape only tells us its job.  
*Evidence:* In Vegetable Detectives, the potato has eyes in a spiral and the sweet potato has none. In the Blue-Black Test, both turn blue-black, because both are packed with starch. In Wake the Store, the potato's eyes grow shoots, because they are buds on a stem.

- *who am i:* Your arm and a bat's wing have the same bones inside, shaped for different jobs. So the same kind of part can take very different shapes.
- *what is life:* A plant grows each part to suit its job and its place. A shade leaf grows bigger than a sun leaf on the same plant.
- *what is this world:* In the world, things that do the same job often end up looking alike, even when they started out different.

Every plant begins as one single cell. That cell divides again and again, first into the tiny plant inside a seed, then into the whole plant. Their cells become roots, stems, leaves, tendrils, spines and petals, each shaped for its job. One life takes many forms, and a single plant shows this inside one body. You also grew from one cell, into hands, eyes and ears.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son to split a banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing.' From that unseen nothing, a great banyan tree grows. (Inside a seed you cannot see a root, a stem or a leaf. Yet all of them grow from the seed, each with its own shape.)
- **Udana 6.4, a Buddhist text. Jain teachers tell the same story to explain anekantavada, many-sidedness:** Blind men each touch one part of an elephant. One feels an ear and says an elephant is like a winnowing basket, a soop. Another feels a leg and says an elephant is like a pillar. Each is partly right, and each is wrong about the whole. (One clue can fool us. Underground and fat makes a potato seem like a root. Botanists look at many clues together, like buds, rings and what the part grows from.)
- **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, The Metamorphosis of Plants (1790):** Goethe, a German poet, believed every part of a flower is a changed leaf. One basic form takes many shapes. (Petals and pea tendrils really are changed leaves. Here very different shapes turn out to be the same kind of part.)
- Science (settled): Cactuses in America and spiny euphorbias in Africa are not related, but both grew fat, green, spiny stems in dry places. Biologists call this convergent evolution: unrelated living things grow alike because they live in a similar way.
- Science (settled): A few genes decide whether a part grows as a leaf, a petal or a stamen. Switch them off, and a flower grows only leaf-like parts (Coen and Meyerowitz, 1991).
- Other view, Rolf Sattler, a Canadian botanist, and others who study plant shape: They say root, stem and leaf are boxes people made. Many real plant parts sit between the boxes, partly stem and partly leaf. So asking 'is it really a stem?' may not always have one answer.
- Practice, Two look-alikes (3 min): Close your eyes. I will put a potato in one hand and a sweet potato in the other. Feel them slowly. Feel how heavy they are, and how smooth. Now open your eyes and look for eyes on each one. They felt alike. One is a root and one is a stem. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly.
- Ask: A potato and a sweet potato look alike. Is a thing what it looks like, or what it grew from?
- Ask: A cactus spine is a leaf that no longer catches light. Is it still a leaf?
- Ask: Does a tendril feel it when it touches something?

## Story: Ten Carrots for Next Year
*Jasmeet, her family and their village are made up. The farming is real. In north India, farmers grow carrot seed by digging up the best carrots in winter and planting them again. This is called the root-to-seed method.*

In the village of Kheri, near Karnal, the winter fog sat on the fields like a grey quilt. Under the fog, under the soil, the carrots were growing fat and red.

Jasmeet was nine, and she loved carrots more than anything. She loved them raw, with a pinch of salt. She loved them in gajar halwa, glossy with ghee. Most of all, she loved pulling one out of the cold ground with both hands and hearing the soft pop they made as they let go.

This year, her father had gone to work in a dhaba in Chandigarh for the winter. So it was Jasmeet and her mother, Harpreet, who would harvest the field.

In the corner of the kitchen stood the old steel tin where the family kept its carrot seed. Every year, a handful of seed from that tin became a whole field. But in January, a heavy winter rain had dripped through a crack in the roof, straight into the tin. When Harpreet opened it, the seeds were black and furry with mould. She closed the lid very quietly.

'We will buy seed from the shop next year,' she said. But Jasmeet had seen her face. Shop seed came in shiny packets, and it cost a great deal of money.

Harvest day came. Mother and daughter worked down the rows, pulling, shaking off soil, twisting off the leafy tops. The baskets filled with red. Some carrots were thin and forked, like a man with two legs. Some were fat and straight and as long as Jasmeet's forearm.

'The fat ones fetch the best price at the mandi,' said Harpreet. 'Put those on top.'

Jasmeet held a fat one up to the pale Sun. It was so heavy for its size. So sweet. Why does a carrot make themselves so sweet? she wondered. It is not as if they want to be eaten.

Then she remembered something.

Last spring, at the far edge of the field, behind the tube well, one carrot had been missed. Nobody had pulled them. In February their feathery leaves had grown thick. In March a stalk had shot up out of the middle, taller than Jasmeet herself, and at the top it had opened into white flowers. They were like umbrellas made of tiny stars. Bees had crawled over them all day long, humming. Later the umbrellas had dried and curled shut like fists. When Jasmeet shook one, little brown seeds had fallen into her palm.

She had pulled up that carrot, out of curiosity, in April. It was no longer red and sweet. It was pale and hard, like a stick of wood. She had wondered where all its sweetness had gone.

Now, standing in the field with the fat carrot in her hand, she knew.

The sweetness had gone up. Up the stalk, into the umbrellas, into the seeds. The carrot had not been storing their sugar for her. They had been packing their own lunch, for their own journey. They needed that food to grow a stalk taller than a girl, and a hundred flowers, and a thousand seeds, all in one spring.

'Mummy,' she said. 'Don't sell all of them.'

Harpreet looked up, tired. 'Jassi, every fat carrot is money.'

'Ten,' said Jasmeet. 'Just the ten best. We plant them again, at the edge of the field. They will flower. And then we will have seed. Our own seed, like before.'

Her mother frowned. 'Plant carrots that are already grown? Who does that?' But Jasmeet told her about the carrot behind the tube well, the stalk, the bees, the seeds and the woody root. She told it all in one breath.

Harpreet was quiet for a while. Then she picked up the fattest carrot and turned it over in her hands, as if she was seeing it for the first time.

'Your nani used to do something like this,' she said slowly. 'I was too small to understand.' Then she gave Jasmeet a small knife and showed her how to trim the leaves, leaving a crown of green at the top. 'Not too close,' she said. 'The new shoot grows from there.'

They chose ten. Straight ones, red to the core, with no cracks. Jasmeet planted them in a row by the tube well, each one in their own hole, the green crown just peeping out of the soil. She pressed the earth round each one as gently as if she was tucking them into bed. The rest of the carrots went to the mandi.

For two weeks, nothing seemed to happen. Then the crowns pushed out new leaves, fresh and feathery. By March, ten stalks were rising, one from each carrot. They grew so fast that Jasmeet measured them against her own body every morning. Knee. Waist. Shoulder.

Then the umbrellas opened. Ten plants, and on each one a hundred little white umbrellas, and on each umbrella a hundred tiny stars. The bees came from everywhere. The edge of the field hummed like a temple bell long after it has been struck.

In April the umbrellas dried and curled up into brown nests. Jasmeet and her mother cut them carefully into a cloth, and rubbed them between their palms. The seeds fell out, small and brown and smelling faintly of carrot. There were so many that they filled the old steel tin almost to the top.

That evening, Jasmeet dug up one of the ten carrots. It was pale and woody, exactly like the one from last year. She held it next to the full tin of seed.

When her father came home for Baisakhi, she showed him both. She did not explain. She just put the woody carrot in one of his hands and the tin of seed in the other, and watched him understand.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a carrot is a storage root. The plant packs it with sugar made by their leaves, not for us but for their own next stage, when they use that food to grow a tall flower stalk and seeds. Jasmeet's turn comes when she connects the sweet root with the woody root she found after the forgotten carrot flowered. The sweetness had moved up into the flowers and seeds. Shape and job go together: the root is fat because its job is to store. The story also quietly asks who seeds belong to, and what we keep back for the future.

- Ask: Why do you think a carrot makes themselves so sweet, if they do not want to be eaten?
- Ask: Where did the sweetness of the forgotten carrot go?
- Ask: Why did Harpreet leave a crown of green at the top of each carrot?
- Ask: Jasmeet's mother wanted to sell every fat carrot. Jasmeet wanted to keep ten. Who was right, and why?
- Ask: Jasmeet chose straight carrots with no cracks. Why choose the best carrots to make seed?

### e3 · Sun Leaf, Shade Leaf (30 min)

- **safety:** Pick only 10 leaves, and ask the owner of the plant first. Wash hands after handling leaves.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Put each group's numbers in order and circle the middle one. Draw two bars, one for the middle sun leaf and one for the middle shade leaf.
- **results table:** Leaf | Sunny outside: squares covered | Shady inside: squares covered

  *Child mode:* Lay a leaf on the squared paper. Draw round it. Count the squares inside. Half a square or more counts as one.
  - Note: Counting squares measures area, how much flat space a leaf covers. Children often miscount the edges. That is fine, as long as they use the same rule every time.

- **problem:** Using only one sheet of paper and a little tape, make a stem that stands up by itself and holds up the most books. A stem's job is to hold the plant up. What shape works best?

### Plant Part Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (match) Match each part of a plant to its main job.  
   **Answer:** Root → holds the plant in the soil and soaks up water; Stem → holds the plant up and carries water and food; Leaf → catches sunlight to make food; Flower → makes seeds. *Why:* Each part has its own job. The plant needs all of them to stay alive.
2. (mcq) Why are most leaves thin and flat?  
   Options: To spread out and catch lots of sunlight / To be light enough to blow away / To hold the plant in the ground / To store water  
   **Answer:** To spread out and catch lots of sunlight. *Why:* A thin, flat leaf works like a solar panel. It spreads out to catch light, and light can reach every cell inside it. Picking 'to store water' mixes up ordinary leaves with thick, juicy leaves like aloe, which are a special case.
3. (mcq) A potato has eyes that sprout shoots. What is a potato?  
   Options: A root / A stem / A fruit / A seed  
   **Answer:** A stem. *Why:* The eyes are buds, and buds grow on stems. So a potato is a stem that grows underground, called a tuber. Picking 'root' usually means deciding by where it grows. Many stems grow underground.
4. (mcq) Which of these is a root that stores food?  
   Options: Carrot / Ginger / Sugarcane / Onion  
   **Answer:** Carrot. *Why:* A carrot is a storage root. Ginger and sugarcane are stems, with rings and buds. An onion stores food in its leaves. Picking ginger is a common mix-up, because ginger grows underground. Look for its rings: they show it is a stem.
5. (fill) A plant with a thin stem that grows up by holding on to something else is called a ____.

   Options: No, it is still an open question / Yes, green catches the most light / Yes, plants just like green / Leaves are not really green  
   **Answer:** No, it is still an open question. *Why:* It is still argued about. Picking 'green catches the most light' is a common idea. But a leaf lets more green light bounce off than red or blue, and a black leaf would catch more.
15. (mcq) What did some of the earliest land plants, about 430 million years ago, look like?  
   Options: Tiny forked stems with no roots and no leaves / Huge trees with wide leaves / Flowers like roses / Cactuses  
   **Answer:** Tiny forked stems with no roots and no leaves. *Why:* Fossils show the earliest land plants were small stems without roots or leaves. Roots, wide leaves and flowers came much later. Picking 'huge trees' imagines the past like today's forests.
16. (mcq) Agnes Arber studied plant shapes for fifty years, much of it in a lab in her own home. What was she the first to become?  
   Options: The first woman botanist chosen for Britain's Royal Society / The first person to find roots / The first person to grow a carrot / The first queen of England  
   **Answer:** The first woman botanist chosen for Britain's Royal Society. *Why:* In 1946 she became the first woman botanist chosen as a Fellow of the Royal Society, a top science society. She did much of her work without a university lab. Picking 'first to find roots' does not make sense: people have always known about roots.

## Applications
- Farmers grow carrot and radish seed by replanting the best roots in winter. This is called the root-to-seed method.
- Farmers plant pieces of potato, ginger and sugarcane, each with a bud, to grow new crops without seeds.
- Grass and vetiver are planted on slopes and river banks, because their roots hold the soil against rain.
- Mangroves are planted along coasts, because their roots break the force of waves and storms.
- Khasi and Jaintia people in Meghalaya grow living bridges from the aerial roots of rubber fig trees.
- Bamboo stems, strong tubes, are used for scaffolding, ladders, houses and furniture.
- Self-cleaning paints and glass copy the bumpy, waxy surface of the lotus leaf.
- Gardeners give climbers a support, like a trellis or a string, so they can reach the light.
- Sugar is made from sugarcane stems and sugar beet roots, where those plants store their food.
- Cooks use turmeric and ginger, two underground stems, in almost every Indian kitchen.
- The Crystal Palace, a giant glass building in London in 1851, was designed by a gardener who said the ribs of a giant water lily leaf gave him the idea.

## Open questions
- **Why are leaves green and not black?** Black would catch more light. Some scientists think green keeps the energy supply steady. Others think plants simply kept the chlorophyll of their ancestors.
- **How does a tendril feel a touch, with no nerves?** Scientists know touch sets off changes inside the tendril's cells within minutes. Exactly how the cells sense it is still being studied.
- **Is a gourd's tendril a changed stem or a changed leaf?** Botanists have argued about this for over a hundred years. Recent studies of genes point one way, but not everyone agrees.
- **Why do about 9 out of 10 twining plants twist the same way?** It does not depend on which half of the world they grow in. Nobody knows for sure why one direction became so common.
- **Why did wide leaves take about 40 million years to become common after plants grew roots?** One idea is that early air had so much carbon dioxide that wide leaves would have overheated. It is still being tested.
- **Does light energy travel through a leaf in a strange 'quantum' way?** Some experiments suggested it does, for a very short time. Others say the effect is too short to matter. Scientists still argue.
- **How deep can roots really go?** Roots are hidden, so they are hard to measure. The deepest ones are found by chance, in caves, mines and wells.
- **What would plants look like on other planets?** We have not found any. Scientists can only make careful guesses from the light of other stars.

## Connected maths
- In the Sun Leaf, Shade Leaf test, the middle sun leaf covered 18 squares of 1 cm squared paper. The middle shade leaf covered 27 squares. How many more squares did the shade leaf cover? **9 more squares.**. Find the difference between the two numbers. 27 take away 18 is 9. You can check by counting on: from 18 to 20 is 2, and from 20 to 27 is 7. 2 + 7 = 9. So the shade leaf covered 9 more squares than the sun leaf. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L3`)
- In the Wake the Store test, the onion's tallest shoot was 12 mm on day 2 and 60 mm on day 8. How much did it grow in those 6 days? About how much is that each day? **It grew 48 mm, which is 8 mm each day.**. First find how much it grew. 60 take away 12 is 48 mm. From day 2 to day 8 is 6 days. Share 48 mm equally between 6 days. 48 divided by 6 is 8. So it grew about 8 mm each day. Check: 6 lots of 8 is 48. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Here are the Wake the Store results after 8 days: onion 60 mm, radish top 55 mm, carrot top 40 mm, potato 15 mm, carrot middle 0 mm. Draw a bar chart. Which piece grew second tallest? **The radish top grew second tallest. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each piece, as tall as its number. Put the numbers in order from tallest to shortest: 60, 55, 40, 15, 0. The tallest is the onion, at 60 mm. The next is the radish top, at 55 mm. So the radish top is second. The carrot middle gets no bar at all, because it grew 0 mm. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- On a pineapple you counted 8 spirals one way and 13 the other. These are in the Fibonacci pattern: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. What are the next two numbers? **21 and 34.**. Each number in the pattern is the two before it added together. 1 + 1 = 2. 1 + 2 = 3. 2 + 3 = 5. 3 + 5 = 8. 5 + 8 = 13. So the next is 8 + 13 = 21. The one after is 13 + 21 = 34. Big sunflower heads often have 34 spirals one way and 55 the other. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- A farmer cuts a seed potato into pieces, so that each piece has one eye. The potato has 6 eyes. Each piece grows into a plant that makes about 5 new potatoes. About how many potatoes will the farmer dig up? **About 30 potatoes.**. There are 6 pieces, one for each eye. Each piece makes about 5 potatoes. That is 6 groups of 5. 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 30. Or 6 times 5 is 30. So one potato can become about 30. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Climbing Bean Diary, you and your friends looked down on 10 twining plants. 9 wound anticlockwise and 1 wound clockwise. Make a tally chart. How many more wound anticlockwise than clockwise? **8 more.**. Make a tally mark for each plant. Anticlockwise gets 9 marks: a bundle of five and four more. Clockwise gets 1 mark. 9 take away 1 is 8. So 8 more plants wound anticlockwise. Scientists who counted hundreds of kinds of twining plant found a similar split: about 9 out of every 10 twist the same way. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
