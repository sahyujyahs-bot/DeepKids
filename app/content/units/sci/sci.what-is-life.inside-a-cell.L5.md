# Inside a cell: why your cells need a transport system and a bacterium does not
`sci.what-is-life.inside-a-cell.L5`

**Summary.** Your cells need a transport system inside them, but a tiny bacterium does not. Why? In this lesson you will time how far colour drifts into a potato over 1, 4 and 16 hours, walk a coin to see wandering against walking straight, and send proteins to the right place in a cell.

**Intro.** *A bacterium is alive. She eats, grows and makes copies of herself. Yet she has no nucleus, no Golgi body, no lysosomes and no tracks. Your cells have all of them. So what are these parts for, if a living cell can do without them? Say what you think, and why.* Here is one clue. A bacterium is about 2 micrometres long. A micrometre is one thousandth of a millimetre. A cell from your skin is about 20 micrometres wide, so she is ten times as wide as the bacterium. In the Potato Colour Test you will find out what that extra size does to a cell. You will measure how far colour drifts in 1 hour, 4 hours and 16 hours. The answer explains why your cells have tracks and motors, and it is a clue to the other parts too.

## Terms
- **Micrometre**: A micrometre is one thousandth of a millimetre. Scientists measure cells in micrometres. *e.g.* A hair from your head is about 70 micrometres thick. A bacterium is about 2 micrometres long.
- **Diffusion**: Diffusion is the spreading of particles from a place with many of them to a place with few. It happens because particles are always being knocked about by their neighbours. *e.g.* The smell of sambar reaches the next room by diffusion and by moving air.
- **Prokaryote**: A prokaryote is a living being whose cell has no nucleus. Bacteria are prokaryotes. Almost all of them are single cells. *e.g.* The bacteria who turn milk into curd are prokaryotes.
- **Eukaryote**: A eukaryote is a living being whose cells have a nucleus and other parts wrapped in membranes. Animals, plants, fungi and amoebas are eukaryotes. *e.g.* You, a coconut palm, a mushroom and the yeast in bread dough are all eukaryotes.
- **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)**: The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of flat bags and tubes of membrane, joined to the skin of the nucleus. Rough ER carries ribosomes and makes proteins. Smooth ER has no ribosomes and makes fats. *e.g.* Cells of the pancreas, which make digestive enzymes all day, are packed with rough ER.
- **Golgi body**: The Golgi body is a stack of about four to eight flat bags of membrane. It finishes proteins that come from the ER, adds sugar tags and sorts them by where they must go. *e.g.* A cell that makes mucus in your nose has a large Golgi body, because mucus proteins need many sugars added.
- **Vesicle**: A vesicle is a tiny bubble of membrane that carries cargo inside a cell. It pinches off from one membrane and joins another. *e.g.* Insulin waits inside vesicles in a pancreas cell until your blood sugar rises.
- **Signal label**: A signal label is a short stretch of a protein's own chain, or a tag added to it, that says where in the cell the protein belongs. Scientists call it a signal sequence. *e.g.* A protein with a nucleus label is carried through a pore into the nucleus. A protein with no label stays where it was made.
- **Secretion**: Secretion is the sending of a substance out of a cell. A vesicle joins the cell membrane and empties its cargo outside. *e.g.* Your salivary glands secrete saliva when you smell lemon rice.
- **Enzyme**: An enzyme is a protein that speeds up one chemical change. Each enzyme works on one kind of molecule and comes out unchanged, ready to work again. *e.g.* An enzyme in your saliva cuts starch from rice into sugar.
- **Lysosome**: A lysosome is a small bag of membrane that holds about fifty kinds of digesting enzymes in acid. It takes apart worn-out cell parts and things the cell has swallowed. *e.g.* A white blood cell swallows a bacterium and joins the bag to a lysosome, where the bacterium is digested.
- **Autophagy**: Autophagy is a cell's way of wrapping her own worn-out parts in a membrane and sending them to a lysosome. The pieces are used again. The word means self-eating. *e.g.* When you have not eaten for many hours, your liver cells recycle more of their own parts by autophagy.
- **Peroxisome**: A peroxisome is a small bag of enzymes that breaks down fats and destroys hydrogen peroxide. Hydrogen peroxide is a harmful chemical that cells make as they work. *e.g.* Potato cells and liver cells both have many peroxisomes, so both make hydrogen peroxide fizz.
- **Cytoskeleton**: The cytoskeleton is the network of protein fibres inside a cell. It gives the cell her shape, lets her move and gives motor proteins tracks to walk on. *e.g.* A white blood cell crawls after a bacterium by building new fibres at her front edge.
- **Microtubule**: A microtubule is a hollow tube built from a protein called tubulin. It is the thickest fibre of the cytoskeleton, and it is the main track for long-distance cargo. *e.g.* Microtubules run the whole length of the nerve cell that reaches from your lower back to your toes.
- **Actin filament**: An actin filament is a thin fibre built from a protein called actin. Actin filaments lie mostly under the cell membrane, and they push and pull it. *e.g.* Your muscles shorten when the motor protein myosin pulls on actin filaments.
- **Motor protein**: A motor protein is a protein that walks along a fibre of the cytoskeleton, carrying cargo. Each step uses the energy of one ATP. ATP is the small molecule that carries energy in every cell. *e.g.* The motor protein kinesin takes about a hundred steps a second along a microtubule.
- **Endosymbiosis**: Endosymbiosis is one living being living inside the cells of another, with both gaining from it. Mitochondria and chloroplasts began this way. *e.g.* The bacteria in the root swellings of a groundnut plant live inside her cells and make nitrogen food for her.

## Explanations
### Diffusion: drifting is quick over a short distance and slow over a long one · *defined*
**Definition:** Diffusion is the spreading of particles from where there are many to where there are few. To drift twice as far takes about four times as long.

Every molecule in water is moving, and neighbours bump it billions of times each second. Each bump sends it a new way, so the molecule wanders. Wandering covers a tiny distance quickly. But the time grows much faster than the distance. Twice as far takes about four times as long. Ten times as far takes about a hundred times as long. You saw this in the Potato Colour Test: after sixteen times the time, the colour was only about four times as deep. Now think about cells. A small protein drifts across a bacterium in a small fraction of a second. Across one of your skin cells, ten times as wide, it needs a few seconds. That is still quick enough. But a vesicle full of proteins is far bigger than one protein, and the crowded inside of a cell holds it back. Drifting could take it hours. And a nerve cell can be a metre long. Drifting from one end to the other would take hundreds of years. So a large cell does not rely on drifting for her large cargo. She carries it.
- e.g. In the Potato Colour Test, the colour went about twice as deep in 4 hours as in 1 hour, not four times as deep.
- e.g. In the Coin Walk, a token that wanders needs about 9 tosses to get 3 squares away and about 36 tosses to get 6 squares away.
- e.g. A drop of ink in a still tumbler reaches the water next to it in seconds. Without stirring or currents, it would take weeks to colour the whole tumbler evenly.
### The route of a protein: rough ER, Golgi body, vesicle · *defined*
**Definition:** A protein that will leave the cell is made on the rough ER, finished and sorted in the Golgi body, and carried to the cell membrane in a vesicle.

A ribosome is the small machine that builds a protein by joining amino acids into a chain. If the protein is for export, the ribosome sits on the rough ER and feeds the growing chain inside. There, helper proteins fold the chain into its working shape. A chain that folds wrongly is held back, and if it cannot be mended it is taken apart. This checking matters, because a wrongly folded protein can stick to others and harm the cell. A vesicle then pinches off the ER and carries the protein to the Golgi body. The Golgi body has a receiving side and a sending side. As the protein passes through, enzymes add and trim sugars. On the sending side, proteins are sorted into different vesicles by their labels. The vesicle joins the cell membrane and the protein is outside. The smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes the fats for new membrane, and in your liver it changes medicines and poisons so the body can remove them.
- e.g. Collagen is the most plentiful protein in your body. It is the strong rope between cells in skin, bone and tendon. In 1954, G. N. Ramachandran and Gopinath Kartha at the University of Madras worked out that collagen is three chains wound together. Cells fold those three chains inside the rough ER.
- e.g. Insulin is made in the pancreas, packed into vesicles and kept ready. When your blood sugar rises after a meal, the vesicles join the membrane and let the insulin out.
- e.g. One cell that makes antibodies can send out thousands of antibody molecules every second. She is almost filled with rough ER.
### How a protein finds its place: the label is part of the protein · *defined*
**Definition:** A protein carries its own address. The address is a short stretch of its chain, or a tag added to it, and machines in the cell read it.

A cell makes thousands of kinds of protein, and each kind belongs in one place. No part of the cell holds a list of where everything goes. Each protein carries its own label. In the 1970s, Günter Blobel and his colleagues showed that a protein for export begins with a short signal stretch. A particle in the cell holds on to that stretch and brings the ribosome to the ER. Other labels send a protein into the nucleus or into a mitochondrion. A protein with no label stays in the watery part of the cell where it was made. Enzymes for the lysosome get a sugar tag in the Golgi body. In 1972, Elizabeth Neufeld and Scot Hickman studied children with a rare illness called I-cell disease. Their cells made the lysosome enzymes correctly, but sent them out of the cell. Neufeld and Hickman proposed that the enzymes were missing a tag. Later work found the tag and proved them right. Vesicles carry labels too. Matching proteins on a vesicle and on its target membrane fit together, and only then do the two membranes join.
- e.g. Haemoglobin has no label, so it stays in the watery part of the young red blood cell that makes it.
- e.g. The proteins that DNA winds around carry a nucleus label, so they are taken in through the pores of the nucleus.
- e.g. James Rothman, Randy Schekman and Thomas Südhof shared a Nobel Prize in 2013 for showing how vesicles find the right membrane and join it.
### Lysosomes: bags of enzymes that work only in acid · *defined*
**Definition:** A lysosome is a bag of digesting enzymes kept in acid. It breaks large molecules into small ones, and the cell uses those again.

Every part of a cell wears out. A mitochondrion lasts a few weeks. Many proteins last only hours or days. A cell that only built and never took apart would fill up with broken parts. Lysosomes do the taking apart. Each holds about fifty kinds of enzyme, and each enzyme cuts one kind of large molecule: proteins, fats, starch-like chains or DNA. Why do these enzymes not digest the cell herself? The enzymes work well only in acid. The cell pumps acid into the lysosome, so the inside is about as sour as tomato juice. The rest of the cell is not sour. If a little enzyme leaks out, it almost stops working. The bag's own membrane is protected on the inside by a coat of sugars. Worn parts reach the lysosome by autophagy. The cell wraps the old part in a membrane, and that parcel joins a lysosome. The small molecules that come out are used to build new parts. Yoshinori Ohsumi worked out the steps of autophagy in yeast cells in the 1990s.
- e.g. When a tadpole becomes a frog, lysosomes help to digest the cells of the tail. The tadpole uses the material to build legs.
- e.g. Some children are born without one working lysosome enzyme. The molecule it should cut piles up in their cells and makes them ill. Doctors can now treat a few of these illnesses by giving the missing enzyme.
- e.g. A white blood cell that has swallowed a bacterium joins the bag to a lysosome, and the enzymes digest the bacterium.
### Peroxisomes: small bags that deal with a harmful chemical · *defined*
**Definition:** A peroxisome is a small bag of enzymes that breaks down fats and destroys hydrogen peroxide.

When a cell breaks down fats, one of the products is hydrogen peroxide. Hydrogen peroxide damages proteins and DNA, so the cell must not let it spread. The cell does this work inside peroxisomes. A peroxisome holds an enzyme called catalase. Catalase turns hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen gas as fast as it is made. One catalase molecule can deal with millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules every second. In the Fizzing Potato test, the bubbles are oxygen made by catalase from potato cells. Why keep this work in a separate bag? The harmful chemical is made and destroyed in the same small space, so very little of it reaches the rest of the cell.
- e.g. Raw potato, raw liver and even a drop of blood make hydrogen peroxide fizz, because all of them contain catalase.
- e.g. Boiled potato does not fizz. Heat changes the shape of an enzyme, and an enzyme with the wrong shape no longer works.
- e.g. The foam on a cut cleaned with hydrogen peroxide is oxygen, made by catalase from your own cells.
### The cytoskeleton: three kinds of fibre that are always being rebuilt · *defined*
**Definition:** The cytoskeleton is a network of three kinds of protein fibre: actin filaments, intermediate filaments and microtubules. A cell builds and takes apart most of these fibres all the time.

Your bones were built once and stay for years. A cell's skeleton is different. Actin filaments are the thinnest fibres. They lie under the cell membrane and push it out or pull it in. A crawling white blood cell builds actin at her front and takes it apart at her back. Microtubules are hollow tubes and the thickest fibres. Most of them grow outward from a spot near the nucleus. In 1984, Tim Mitchison and Marc Kirschner found that a microtubule grows steadily and then suddenly shrinks. Most microtubules last only a few minutes. Why would a cell keep building tracks and breaking them? Because she can then change shape, move or divide within minutes, using the same building blocks again. Intermediate filaments are the third kind. They are the lasting ones, like ropes, and they stop the cell being torn when she is stretched. Your hair and nails are made of an intermediate filament protein called keratin, left behind by cells that have died.
- e.g. When a cell divides, she takes apart her microtubules and rebuilds them into the frame that pulls the two sets of chromosomes apart.
- e.g. The tiny hairs that sweep dust out of your windpipe are bundles of microtubules, bent to and fro by motor proteins.
- e.g. Skin cells are tied to each other by keratin ropes. That is why skin can stretch over a bent knee without tearing.
### Motor proteins: carrying cargo along the tracks · *defined*
**Definition:** A motor protein walks along a fibre of the cytoskeleton and carries cargo. Each step uses one ATP.

In 1985, Ronald Vale, Thomas Reese and Michael Sheetz found a motor protein in the giant nerve cells of squid. They named it kinesin. Kinesin has two feet. One foot holds the microtubule while the other swings forward, and each step is 8 millionths of a millimetre. Kinesin can take about a hundred steps a second. A microtubule has two different ends, so a motor knows which way to walk. Most kinesins walk outward, towards the edge of the cell. Another motor, dynein, walks inward. A vesicle often carries both kinds, and it moves in the direction of the side that is pulling harder. At about one micrometre a second, a motor crosses an ordinary cell in under a minute. At that speed, the trip down a nerve cell one metre long takes about twelve days. Drifting would take hundreds of years. In Mumbai, Roop Mallik and his group have studied how several motors share one load, like people carrying a heavy cot together.
- e.g. A pigment cell in the skin of a fish or a frog moves her grains of colour outward or inward along microtubules. This changes how dark the skin looks within minutes.
- e.g. In your muscles, the motor protein myosin pulls on actin filaments. Millions of these small pulls add up to lifting your school bag.
- e.g. Some viruses fix themselves to a cell's motor proteins and are carried to the nucleus.
- e.g. Every step of a motor protein is paid for with ATP. ATP was found in muscle in 1929 by Cyrus Fiske and Yellapragada Subbarow, who was born in Bhimavaram in Andhra Pradesh.
### A bacterial cell compared with one of your cells · *defined*
**Definition:** A bacterium is a prokaryote: a small cell with no nucleus and no organelles wrapped in membrane. Your cells are eukaryote cells: about ten times as wide, with a nucleus and many organelles.

A bacterium and one of your cells share the basic equipment of life. Both have a cell membrane, DNA, ribosomes and ATP, and both read DNA with the same code. The differences follow from size and from history. A bacterium is about 1 to 2 micrometres long. Her DNA is usually one ring that lies in the watery inside of the cell. Her ribosomes are a little smaller than yours and have a different shape. Most bacteria have a tough cell wall outside the membrane. She makes ATP on her own cell membrane. Because she is so small, molecules reach every part of her by drifting. So she lives well without a transport system. With plenty of food she can copy herself in 20 minutes. Your cells keep their DNA in a nucleus, make ATP inside mitochondria, and move large cargo by vesicle and motor. Medicines use the differences. Penicillin stops bacteria building their wall, and your cells have no wall. Other antibiotics jam bacterial ribosomes and leave yours alone. In 2000, Venkatraman Ramakrishnan, who was born in Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu, and his group showed exactly where such antibiotics sit on the bacterial ribosome.
- e.g. The bacteria in curd, the bacteria on your skin and the bacteria in soil are all prokaryotes.
- e.g. Yeast is a single cell, but she is a eukaryote. She has a nucleus and mitochondria, as your cells do.
- e.g. Bacteria do some chemistry that no animal or plant cell can do. Some take nitrogen from the air. Some live on iron or sulphur.
### Mitochondria were once free-living bacteria: the evidence · *defined*
**Definition:** Mitochondria come from bacteria that began to live inside a larger cell about two billion years ago. Chloroplasts come from other bacteria that could use sunlight.

This idea sounds unlikely, so look at the evidence. First, a mitochondrion has her own DNA, and it is a ring, as in bacteria. Second, she has her own ribosomes, and they are like bacterial ribosomes. A few antibiotics that jam bacterial ribosomes also disturb mitochondria. Third, a cell cannot make a mitochondrion from nothing. New mitochondria come only from old ones, which grow and split in two, as bacteria do. Fourth, she has two membranes, and she makes ATP on the inner one, as a bacterium does on her own membrane. Fifth, she is the size of a bacterium. Sixth, when scientists read the DNA of mitochondria, it matched one group of living bacteria. But a mitochondrion can no longer live alone. Over time, most of her genes moved into the cell's nucleus. Your mitochondria hold only 37 genes. The cell makes more than a thousand kinds of protein for them and sends these in with a mitochondrion label. Lynn Margulis argued for this history in 1967, against most biologists of her time.
- e.g. In 1966, Kwang Jeon's laboratory amoebas were infected by bacteria. Most died. After many generations of amoebas, the survivors could not live without those bacteria. A harmful guest had become a needed partner while scientists watched.
- e.g. An amoeba called Paulinella took in a sunlight-using bacterium about a hundred million years ago. This happened separately from the plants.
- e.g. In 2024, scientists reported a sea alga whose nitrogen-catching bacterium has become a true part of the cell. It is the first known organelle that takes nitrogen from the air.
### Which came first: the large cell or the mitochondrion? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that mitochondria were once bacteria. They do not agree on what the host cell was like when the two met.

Nobody watched it happen, and the first such cells left almost no fossils. So scientists reason from living cells and their DNA. The host belonged to a group of microbes called archaea. They are small cells with no nucleus, but they are not bacteria. The argument is about the order of events.
- e.g. If the large cell came first, there should once have been large cells with a skeleton and no mitochondria. No such cell has been found alive today.
- e.g. In 2020, Hiroyuki Imachi and his team reported that they had grown a close relative of the host. It took them twelve years. She is a tiny cell with long arms, and she lives closely beside partner microbes.
- **Large cell first:** The host had already become large, with a skeleton that let her swallow other cells. One day she swallowed a bacterium and did not digest her. In this view the skeleton made the mitochondrion possible.
- **Mitochondrion first (Nick Lane and William Martin, among others):** The host was still small. A bacterium came to live inside her without being swallowed. The extra ATP from the new partners let the cell grow large and build a skeleton and a transport system. In this view the mitochondrion made the large cell possible.
- **In between:** The relatives of the host already have a few skeleton proteins and long arms. Perhaps the host slowly wrapped her arms round her partners. Then both changes happened together.

## Mostly agreed
- To drift twice as far by diffusion takes about four times as long. *How we know:* Albert Einstein worked out this rule in 1905, and Jean Perrin confirmed it in 1908 by tracking tiny grains under a microscope. Your potato pieces show the same rule.
- Proteins for export are made on the rough ER, pass through the Golgi body and leave the cell in vesicles. *How we know:* In 1967, James Jamieson and George Palade gave pancreas cells radioactive amino acids for a few minutes. They then found the new proteins first over the rough ER, later in the Golgi body and last in vesicles at the cell's edge.
- A protein carries its own address as part of itself. *How we know:* Cut the signal stretch off a protein and it stays in the watery part of the cell. Join that stretch to a different protein and the cell sends the different protein to the ER.
- The cytoskeleton is built and taken apart all the time. *How we know:* Films of living cells with glowing tubulin show single microtubules growing and then shrinking within minutes.
- Bacteria and our cells share the same basic chemistry and one ancestor. *How we know:* Both use DNA, the same code for reading it, ribosomes and ATP. Hundreds of genes are similar in all living cells.
- Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria. *How we know:* They have their own ring of DNA and their own ribosomes of the bacterial kind. They form only by splitting, and their genes match living groups of bacteria.

## How we found out
**Camillo Golgi, who first saw it. Albert Dalton and Marie Felix, who showed it with the electron microscope. James Jamieson and George Palade, who traced proteins through it., 1898 (Golgi's silver stain), 1954 (electron microscope pictures) and 1967 (the tracing experiment).** In 1898, in the Italian town of Pavia, Camillo Golgi stained nerve cells from a barn owl with silver. Inside each cell he saw a dark net that nobody had described. Other scientists saw it too, but many did not believe it. For fifty years, some of the best cell scientists argued that the net was not a real part of the cell. They said the silver stain itself made it, the way a spilt drink leaves a mark on a table. Light microscopes could not settle the question. In the 1950s the electron microscope could. It showed the net as a neat stack of flat bags in cell after cell, even with no silver stain. Then people asked what it was for. In 1967, James Jamieson and George Palade fed pancreas cells radioactive amino acids for three minutes. They looked for the radioactive proteins at later times. The proteins were first in the rough ER, then in the Golgi body, then in vesicles at the cell's edge. The Golgi body was a station on the route out of the cell.  
*What people believed before:* That the Golgi body was not a real part of the cell, only a mark made by the silver stain. Electron microscope pictures showed the same stack of bags in cells that had never been stained with silver.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Bacteria have no skeleton and no compartments inside.** (Most school books until about 2000; *rejected*)  
  For: Under an ordinary microscope a bacterium looks like a plain bag. She has no nucleus and no membrane-wrapped organelles like ours.  
  Against: In 1991, Erfei Bi and Joe Lutkenhaus found a ring of protein fibre that pinches a bacterium in two. That protein is a relative of our tubulin. In 2001, relatives of actin were found too. Some bacteria also build small compartments with protein walls.
- **Bacteria are simple, early cells, and our cells are the advanced kind.** (A common way of speaking; *rejected*)  
  For: Our cells have many more parts, and they can build large bodies. Bacteria appeared on Earth first.  
  Against: Today's bacteria have been changing for just as long as we have. They do chemistry no animal can do, and they live in boiling springs and deep rock. At her size, a bacterium is not missing a transport system, because drifting does that work. Each kind of cell fits a different way of living.
- **A bacterium is always tiny and never has compartments for her DNA.** (The usual rule about prokaryotes; *accepted*)  
  For: It is true for almost every bacterium studied.  
  Against: In 2022, Jean-Marie Volland and colleagues described a bacterium from mangrove swamps in Guadeloupe who is about one centimetre long. You can see her without a microscope. She keeps her DNA in many small membrane bags. So a bacterium who is very large also has compartments.
- **The Golgi body is a row of fixed bags, and vesicles carry proteins from one bag to the next.** (Many cell biologists, from the 1970s onward; *debated*)  
  For: Vesicles are seen budding from the edges of every bag in the stack.  
  Against: Films of living yeast cells made in 2006 showed single bags changing over a few minutes from the receiving kind to the sending kind. So perhaps the bags themselves move through the stack, with the proteins inside them, and the vesicles carry enzymes backward. Both may happen.
- **A mitochondrion is still a bacterium, living inside us as a separate being.** (Some writers and a few biologists; *debated*)  
  For: She has her own DNA and ribosomes, and she multiplies by splitting. She comes only from another mitochondrion.  
  Against: She has passed almost all her genes to the nucleus and cannot live outside the cell for long. The cell decides when she divides and when she is recycled. Most biologists call her an organelle, a part of the cell.

## Roots
We have learned how a large cell ships, recycles and holds her shape, and how a bacterium lives without those parts. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Potato Colour Test, the colour needed four times as long to go twice as deep. Drifting molecules cross a tiny distance quickly and a long distance very slowly. A bacterium is so small that drifting reaches every part of her almost at once. Your cells are about ten times as wide, so they carry large cargo along tracks. Tracks, motors and vesicles are what a large cell needs, and a bacterium lives well without them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call a bacterium a simple cell and our own cells advanced, as if she were an unfinished version of us. The potato result shows a different reason for the difference. At her size, drifting does the work that vesicles, tracks and motors do in a large cell.  
*Evidence:* In the Potato Colour Test, sixteen times the time gave only about four times the depth. In the Coin Walk, a wandering token needed about four times as many tosses to get twice as far. A giant bacterium found in 2022, one centimetre long, keeps her DNA in membrane bags as large cells do.

- *who am i:* Each of your cells is large, and she stays alive by carrying cargo along tracks every second. Your mitochondria descend from bacteria who moved into another cell about two billion years ago.
- *what is life:* Life has more than one design. A bacterium lives by being small, and your cells live by being large and organised inside. Bacteria have lived their way for more than three billion years, and cells like yours for about two billion.
- *what is this world:* The same water and the same restless molecules are inside a bacterium and inside you. Size changes what those molecules can do in the time there is.

A bacterium and one of your cells share one ancestor and the same chemistry: DNA, ribosomes, a membrane and ATP. From that one beginning, life found different ways to live at different sizes. Then two different kinds of cell met, and one came to live inside the other. Every animal, plant and fungus has grown from that joining. So the many kinds of life are one family, and some of its branches have joined again.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 17, 'Autumn Floods' (paraphrased):** “莊子曰：「鯈魚出游從容，是魚樂也。」 / 惠子曰：「子非魚，安知魚之樂？」 / 莊子曰：「子非我，安知我不知魚之樂？」” Seen from the Way, nothing is noble and nothing is lowly. Seen from each thing, each one calls itself noble and the others lowly. (We call our own cells advanced and bacteria simple. The science in this lesson shows two designs, each fitted to a size. The ranking comes from us.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra 5.21, a Jain text by Umasvati (paraphrased):** “परस्परोपग्रहो जीवानाम्” Living beings exist by helping one another. (A mitochondrion gives the cell ATP. The cell gives her food, shelter and most of her proteins. Neither can now live without the other.)
- Science (settled): Mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from free-living bacteria that came to live inside larger cells.
- Science (settled): The time needed to drift a distance grows with the square of the distance. This limits how large a cell can be without a transport system.
- Science (debated): Whether the extra energy from mitochondria is what first allowed cells to become large is still argued.
- Other view, Many cell biologists: A cell has no aims and needs nothing. A motor protein steps because ATP changes its shape, and a label is only a patch that sticks to a matching patch. Words like 'needs', 'carries' and 'reads' are a short way of describing chemistry. Nobody inside is doing anything.
- Practice, The longest cell in you (3 min): Sit with your legs stretched out. Put one hand on your lower back and look at your big toe. A single nerve cell runs all the way from your back to that toe. Wiggle the toe once. The message went down that one cell. Now close your eyes and breathe slowly five times. Inside that long cell, motor proteins are carrying vesicles along microtubules right now. A vesicle that sets off today needs many days to reach your toe. Notice that you do not have to do anything for this to happen.
- Ask: If you were as small as a bacterium, which parts of your cells would you still need?
- Ask: Nobody in a cell directs the traffic, and each protein carries its own label. Who, or what, is organising the cell?
- Ask: Your mitochondria and your cells cannot live apart any more. When did they stop being two and become one? Did they?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* A bacterium lives well with few parts, and your cells live well with many. Is one of these a better way to be alive? (hint: What would 'better' mean here? Count how many bacteria there are, and how long they have lived on Earth.)
- *deep:* No part of a cell knows where every protein should go. Each protein carries its own label. Can something be well organised with nobody organising it? (hint: Think of how a letter reaches a house. Does any one person know the whole route?)

## Story: Six Digits
*A made-up story. Thenmozhi, her mother and the night in the sorting hall are imagined, and children are not normally allowed into a sorting hall. The postal facts are real. India's six-digit PIN code began on 15 August 1972. The first digit names a large zone of the country, the first three together name a sorting district, and the last three name one post office. Madurai's head post office is 625001 and Shillong's is 793001. A letter that cannot be delivered or sent back goes to a Returned Letter Office.*

The sorting hall behind Madurai railway station never slept. At ten at night its tube lights were all on, and the rain was loud on the tin roof.

Thenmozhi sat on an upturned crate with her school bag on her knees. She was ten. Her mother, Amudha, sorted letters here on the night shift. Tonight the neighbour who usually kept Thenmozhi had gone to a wedding in Dindigul, so Thenmozhi had come along. The supervisor had looked at her over his glasses and said nothing, which meant yes.

In her bag was a letter. She had written it three times before she liked it. It was for Ibadahun, who had sat beside her for two years and who had moved in June, with her whole family, back to Shillong. Thenmozhi had found Shillong on the map in her atlas. It was almost as far from Madurai as India went.

She looked round the hall. Sacks came in through one door and went out through another. Forty people stood at wooden frames full of small open boxes and flicked letters into them, fast, without seeming to look.

'Amma,' she said. 'Who here knows where Ibadahun's house is?'

Her mother did not stop sorting. 'Nobody.'

'Then who knows the way to Shillong?'

'Nobody here has been to Shillong, kanna.'

Thenmozhi held her bag tighter. She had imagined one person, somewhere at the back, with a great book of every lane in India. She had imagined giving the letter to that person. Now she looked at forty tired people and a leaking roof, and she wanted to take her letter home again.

Her mother glanced at her face. Then she pulled a tray of letters across the table. 'Come. Do this tray for me. You see my frame? Nine boxes on the top row, numbered one to nine. Look at the first digit of the PIN code on each letter. Only the first. Put the letter in the box with that number.'

'Only the first digit?'

'Only the first.'

The first letter said 600 028. Thenmozhi put it in box six. The next said 110 001, and went in box one. Then 682 011, box six again. 400 050, box four. It was easy. It was so easy that she became suspicious.

'But Amma, 600 028 and 682 011 are in the same box. One is Chennai and one is Kochi. Appa's sister lives in Kochi. They are not the same place.'

'They are the same zone. Six. The six bag gets opened again at another table, and there somebody reads the next two digits. Then it goes to a town, and somebody there reads the last three. And then a postman reads the street and the door number.' Her mother flicked four letters into four boxes. 'Each of us reads our own part. Nobody reads the whole thing.'

Thenmozhi sorted. A letter came up that had been out in the rain. The name had run into a blue cloud, and the street was gone. But the six digits stood in their six printed squares, a little blurred: 5 6 0 0 0 3. She showed her mother.

'Box five,' said her mother. 'It will get to Bengaluru. The last post office will work out the rest. They know their own streets.'

A few letters later, Thenmozhi stopped. This envelope was dry and clean. The handwriting was large and very careful, a child's. It said: To Paati, The Blue House, Near the Temple Tank. There was a stamp. There was no town. The six squares were empty.

She turned it over. Nothing on the back. She held it over box six, then box five, then put her hand down.

'Amma. Where does this one go?'

Her mother took it and read it, and her face went soft. 'Nowhere,' she said. 'Every town has a temple tank. This one cannot leave the hall.' She put it gently on a small pile at the end of the table. 'It goes to the Returned Letter Office. They will open it and look for a name inside. Sometimes they find one.'

'But the address is so neat.'

'It is very neat.'

Thenmozhi looked at the careful letters for a while. Whoever wrote them knew exactly where the blue house was. They had walked there. They had thought that was enough.

Then she opened her school bag very fast.

Her own envelope said Ibadahun's name, and the lane, and the big word SHILLONG, underlined twice. The six squares at the bottom were empty. She had not known what to put in them. She had thought the post office would know.

Her mother had already taken down a thick book with a soft, torn cover. She turned the pages with her thumb, the way she turned letters. 'Meghalaya. Shillong. Read it out.'

'Seven, nine, three, zero, zero, one.'

Thenmozhi wrote the digits in the squares, one in each, as large as they would go. Then she walked to her mother's frame by herself, and found the box with seven on it, and put the letter in.

**For the parent:** For the parent: nobody in the sorting hall knew the way to Shillong, and nobody needed to. The letter carried its own address, and each sorter read only one part of it. A cell works the same way. No part of a cell holds a list of where each protein belongs. Each protein carries a signal label as part of itself, and each machine along the route reads only that label. One difference is worth saying: a protein with no label is not sent back. It simply stays in the watery part of the cell where it was made.

- Ask: A letter with a smudged name but a clear PIN code can still travel. Why?
- Ask: Nobody in the hall knew the whole route. How did letters still reach the right house?
- Ask: A cell sends thousands of kinds of protein to the right places. What does each protein need to have?
- Ask: In a cell, what happens to a protein that has no label at all?
- Ask: Where else do things find their way with nobody directing them?

### e1 · The Potato Colour Test: how deep does colour drift in 1 hour, 4 hours and 16 hours? (50 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Colour drifts into a piece of potato with nobody pushing it. If you wait four times as long, does the colour go four times as deep?
- **predict:** Guess first. After 1 hour you will measure how deep the colour has gone. After 4 hours, will it be the same depth, about twice as deep or about four times as deep? What about after 16 hours? Write your guesses and the reason for each.
- **fair test:** Change only the time. Cut all three pieces from one potato, as near the same size as you can. Keep them in the same tumbler of colour, fully covered, in the same place. Measure every piece the same way: on a fresh cut through the middle, at the middle of each of the four sides, away from the corners. At a corner, colour comes in from two sides at once.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the cutting. Food colour and ink stain cloth and wooden boards, so work on a steel plate and wear old clothes.
- **record:** Fill in the table with all four depths and the mean for each time. Draw a line graph of mean depth against time. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Time in the colour | Depth on side 1 (mm) | Depth on side 2 (mm) | Depth on side 3 (mm) | Depth on side 4 (mm) | Mean depth (mm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up the three potato pieces in the colour and write your guesses. Cut and measure one piece after 1 hour and one after 4 hours. (32 min); day 2: Cut and measure the last piece after about 16 hours. Draw the graph and explain the result. (18 min)

  - Ask: A bacterium is about 2 micrometres long, and one of your cells is about 20 micrometres wide. Which one can rely on drifting alone to carry things across? Keeping DNA and the things that work with it close together in many small bags makes every distance short again.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the rule from the potato test and then says how compartments keep distances short. Nobody is yet sure of the full reason, so 'suggest' is the right word.
2. (perspective) Three people talk about what a mitochondrion is.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence from both sides: her own DNA, ribosomes and splitting, and also the genes she has passed to the nucleus and the fact that she cannot live alone. It chooses one view and gives a fair point from another.. *Why:* Scientists agree on the facts and differ on the word. The question of when two beings count as one has no agreed answer.
3. (mcq) A scientist cuts the signal stretch off a protein that normally leaves the cell. Where does the protein end up?  
   Options: In the watery inside of the cell / Outside the cell, as before / In the Golgi body / In a lysosome  
   **Answer:** In the watery inside of the cell. *Why:* Without its signal stretch the protein is never brought to the ER, so it stays where it was made. This experiment is part of the evidence that the address is in the protein itself.
4. (count) A motor protein takes 100 steps a second, each 8 millionths of a millimetre. How many millionths in one second?  
   **Answer:** 800. *Why:* 100 steps × 8 = 800 millionths of a millimetre each second. That is a little less than one micrometre a second.
5. (write) Many scientists doubted the Golgi body, seen only with silver stain. What evidence settled it, and why was it stronger?  
   **Answer:** The electron microscope showed the same stack of flat bags in cells that had not been stained with silver. This was stronger because it was a second, different way of looking. If the stain had made the shape, a method without the stain would not have shown it.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a result seen by two different methods is unlikely to be made by either method.
6. (sort) Sort each statement: shown by strong evidence, or still argued about.  
   **Answer:** Mitochondria came from free-living bacteria → Shown by strong evidence; A protein carries its own address label → Shown by strong evidence; The host cell was already large when mitochondria arrived → Still argued about; The bags of the Golgi body themselves move through the stack → Still argued about; Microtubules grow and shrink within minutes → Shown by strong evidence; A mitochondrion should be called a living being of her own → Still argued about. *Why:* The first group rests on many experiments that agree. In the second group, scientists accept the same facts but explain them differently, or the evidence is not yet enough.
7. (mcq) A new medicine stops bacteria building their cell wall. Why should it leave your cells unharmed?  
   Options: Your cells have no cell wall / Your cells are too big for it / Your nucleus protects the cell / Your cell walls are thicker  
   **Answer:** Your cells have no cell wall. *Why:* A medicine that blocks wall-building has nothing to block in a cell with no wall. Choosing 'thicker walls' assumes that animal cells have walls. They have only a membrane.
8. (draw) Two vesicles reach the cell membrane. Only one has surface proteins that match the membrane. Draw what happens to each.  
   **Answer:** One vesicle joined to the membrane and open to the outside, with its matching proteins fitted to the membrane's proteins. The other vesicle still closed and still inside the cell.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that only the vesicle with matching proteins joins. The other does not join, because joining needs the two sets of proteins to fit.
9. (truefalse) True or false: with the right genes in her nucleus, a cell can build a new mitochondrion from nothing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A new mitochondrion comes only from an older one that grows and splits. The nucleus holds most of the genes, but a mitochondrion must already be there to grow.
10. (write) One mitochondrion has a folded inner membrane and another, the same size, a smooth one. Which makes more ATP, and why?  
   **Answer:** The one with the folded inner membrane. The machines that make ATP sit in the inner membrane. Folding fits much more membrane into the same space, so there is room for more machines.. *Why:* A strong answer says where ATP is made and that folding gives more of that surface. The newspaper in the tumbler showed the same idea.
11. (fill) A boiled enzyme stops working because heat changes its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['shape']. *Why:* An enzyme works by fitting the molecule it changes. Heat changes the enzyme's shape, so the fit is lost.
12. (order) Put these in order of size, smallest first.  
   **Answer:** ['A ribosome', 'A vesicle', 'A lysosome', 'A mitochondrion', 'A nucleus', 'A whole skin cell']. *Why:* In the true-scale model these were a grain of salt, a khus khus seed, a peppercorn, a rajma bean, a lemon and a circle 20 centimetres wide.
13. (mcq) In the Thread Line, one push did not carry the parcel 2 metres. What does a cell do about the same problem?  
   Options: Her motors use ATP at every step of the way / She pushes cargo once, from the nucleus / She waits for cargo to drift / She makes her cargo lighter  
   **Answer:** Her motors use ATP at every step of the way. *Why:* A push at the start runs out. A motor protein takes in new energy at each step, so the cargo keeps going for the whole distance.
14. (write) Laboratory amoebas came to need bacteria that had infected them. What does this show about mitochondria, and what not?  
   **Answer:** It shows that a guest living inside a cell can become a needed partner over many generations. So the first step towards a mitochondrion is possible. It does not show that mitochondria began this way two billion years ago. That evidence comes from their DNA and ribosomes.. *Why:* A strong answer separates 'this can happen' from 'this is what happened'. An experiment today can show only the first.
15. (match) Match each thing you saw to its explanation.  
   **Answer:** Boiled potato does not fizz → heat changed the shape of an enzyme; Colour goes only twice as deep in four times the time → drifting molecules wander forward and back; A wet pencil passes through a soap film → a liquid sheet closes round a gap; A whole newspaper fits in a tumbler → folding fits a large area into a small space; One bean stands for a bacterium and a mitochondrion → the two are the same size. *Why:* Each result in this lesson has a reason at the level of molecules. A strong answer could explain any pair in a full sentence.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Colour drifts 1 millimetre into a potato in 10 minutes. About how long does it need to drift 2 millimetres?  
   Options: 20 minutes / 40 minutes / 10 minutes / 100 minutes  
   **Answer:** 40 minutes. *Why:* Twice as far takes about four times as long, and 4 × 10 = 40 minutes. Choosing 20 minutes means thinking that drifting has a steady speed.
2. (mcq) Where in a cell are sugars added to proteins, and proteins sorted into different vesicles?  
   Options: In the Golgi body / In the nucleus / In a mitochondrion / On the cytoskeleton  
   **Answer:** In the Golgi body. *Why:* The Golgi body finishes proteins from the ER and sorts them by their labels. The cytoskeleton carries the vesicles afterwards, but it does not sort them.
3. (mcq) Which of these proteins must pass through the rough ER and the Golgi body?  
   Options: Insulin, which leaves the cell / Haemoglobin, which stays in a red blood cell / Actin, which builds fibres in the cell / Tubulin, which builds microtubules  
   **Answer:** Insulin, which leaves the cell. *Why:* Insulin does its work outside the cell that makes it, so it takes the export route. The other three stay in the watery inside of the cell.
4. (mcq) How does a new protein get to the right place in a cell?  
   Options: It carries a label that is part of itself / The nucleus sends directions after it / The Golgi body keeps a list of all proteins / It drifts until it finds its place  
   **Answer:** It carries a label that is part of itself. *Why:* The address is a short stretch of the protein's own chain, or a tag added to it. Choosing 'the nucleus' or 'a list' imagines a manager, and a cell has none.
5. (mcq) A little enzyme leaks out of a lysosome. Why does it do little harm to the cell?  
   Options: It works well only in acid, and the rest of the cell is not acid / It is too big to move / It freezes outside the lysosome / The rest of the cell has no proteins to cut  
   **Answer:** It works well only in acid, and the rest of the cell is not acid. *Why:* Lysosome enzymes need acid. The cell pumps acid into the lysosome, and the rest of the cell is not acid, so a leaked enzyme almost stops working.
6. (truefalse) True or false: peroxisomes turn hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The enzyme catalase in peroxisomes does this. The oxygen is the fizz you see when raw potato meets hydrogen peroxide.
7. (mcq) Which fibre is the main track for carrying vesicles a long way across a cell?  
   Options: A microtubule / A keratin rope / A strand of DNA / The cell wall  
   **Answer:** A microtubule. *Why:* Kinesin and dynein walk along microtubules. Keratin ropes give strength, and no motor walks on them.
8. (mcq) Why does a cell keep taking her microtubules apart and building new ones?  
   Options: So she can quickly change shape, move or divide / Because they are badly made / To warm herself up / Because tubulin is harmful if it is left  
   **Answer:** So she can quickly change shape, move or divide. *Why:* A skeleton that is rebuilt every few minutes can be rebuilt in a new arrangement. That lets a cell crawl, change shape and divide.
9. (mcq) Which of these does a bacterium not have?  
   Options: Mitochondria / Ribosomes / DNA / A cell membrane  
   **Answer:** Mitochondria. *Why:* A bacterium is about the size of one mitochondrion. She makes ATP on her own cell membrane. She has the other three, as every living cell does.
10. (mcq) A growing cell needs more mitochondria. Where do the new ones come from?  
   Options: Old mitochondria grow and split in two / The nucleus builds them / The Golgi body packs them / They come in with food  
   **Answer:** Old mitochondria grow and split in two. *Why:* New mitochondria come only from older ones, as new bacteria come only from older bacteria. This is one piece of evidence for where mitochondria came from.
11. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Whether the first host cell was already large / Whether mitochondria have their own DNA / Whether bacteria have ribosomes / Whether proteins carry address labels  
   **Answer:** Whether the first host cell was already large. *Why:* Nobody knows whether the host cell became large before or after mitochondria came to live in her. The other three have been shown many times.

## Applications
- Antibiotics work on the differences between bacterial cells and ours. Penicillin stops bacteria building their wall. Others jam bacterial ribosomes and leave ours working.
- Insulin for people with diabetes is made by yeast or bacteria that have been given the human insulin gene. Companies in Bengaluru and Hyderabad make medicines and vaccines this way.
- Vincristine, a cancer medicine, comes from the nithyakalyani plant, the pink periwinkle that grows by many Indian roadsides. It stops cells building microtubules, so cells that divide fast can no longer divide.
- A few illnesses caused by a missing lysosome enzyme can be treated by giving the enzyme. The medicine carries the lysosome tag, so cells take it in and send it to their lysosomes.
- Some vaccines are packed in tiny bubbles of fat, much like vesicles. The bubble joins the cell's own membranes and lets the cargo into the cell.
- Doctors clean some wounds with hydrogen peroxide. It foams because catalase from your cells turns it into water and oxygen.
- In some nerve illnesses the feet are harmed first. One reason is that the longest nerve cells have the furthest to carry their cargo.

## Spotted around us
- A vessel of idli batter rising overnight. Bacteria and yeast work in it side by side: cells without a nucleus and cells with one.
- Milk set into curd by morning. A spoon of bacteria has become billions, because one of these bacteria can copy herself in about an hour.
- The pink and white nithyakalyani flowers by a roadside, the plant that gave a medicine which stops microtubules.
- The swellings on the roots of a groundnut plant pulled from a field. Bacteria live inside the plant's cells there.
- A frog who looks darker on dark mud than on a pale stone. Her skin cells have moved their grains of pigment along microtubules.
- Hair on the floor of a barber's shop and the nail clippings at home. Both are keratin, the rope protein of the cytoskeleton.
- Corals in an aquarium or a video. Tiny algae live inside the coral animal's cells and share the food they make from sunlight.
- Your mouth watering at the smell of lemon rice. Gland cells are letting out vesicles of saliva proteins.

## Open questions
- **What was the host cell like when the first mitochondria came to live in her?** It happened about two billion years ago and left almost no fossils. Her living relatives were first grown in a laboratory only in 2020.
- **How do proteins move through the Golgi body?** Either vesicles carry them from bag to bag, or the bags themselves move through the stack. Films of living cells support both in different cells.
- **How does a cell set the size and the number of her organelles?** A cell keeps roughly the right amount of ER, Golgi body and mitochondria for her size. Nobody has found the full set of signals that does this.
- **When a vesicle carries motors that walk in opposite directions, what settles which way it goes?** Some results look like a tug of war. Others suggest the motors take turns. It may differ from cargo to cargo.
- **How many partnerships between cells are turning into new organelles today?** The nitrogen-catching organelle in a sea alga was recognised only in 2024. Most single-celled life has never been studied closely.

## Connected maths
- In the Potato Colour Test, the coloured rim on the four sides of one block was 3, 4, 5 and 4 millimetres deep. What is the mean depth? **4 millimetres.**. Add the four depths: 3 + 4 + 5 + 4 = 16. Divide by the number of sides: 16 ÷ 4 = 4. So the mean depth is 4 millimetres. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the Potato Colour Test, the mean depth was 2 millimetres after 1 hour, 4 millimetres after 4 hours and 8 millimetres after 16 hours. What depth do you expect after 64 hours? **About 16 millimetres.**. Each time the hours are multiplied by 4, the depth is multiplied by 2. From 16 hours to 64 hours is 4 times as long. So the depth doubles from 8 to 16 millimetres. A real potato will be close to this, not exact. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the Coin Walk, a wandering token needs about 9 tosses to get 3 squares away, and about 36 tosses to get 6 squares away. About how many tosses to get 12 squares away? **About 144 tosses.**. The number of tosses is about the distance multiplied by itself. 3 × 3 = 9, and 6 × 6 = 36. So for 12 squares it is 12 × 12 = 144. A token that steps forward every time needs only 12. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- A motor protein carries a vesicle about 9 centimetres in one day. A nerve cell runs 90 centimetres from a girl's lower back to her toe. How many days does the vesicle take? Drifting would take hundreds of years. Why is the motor so much quicker? **10 days. The motor steps forward every time, and a drifting vesicle wanders forward and back.**. 90 ÷ 9 = 10, so the trip takes 10 days. A motor protein moves about one micrometre each second, always in the same direction. That adds up to about 9 centimetres in a day. A drifting vesicle changes direction all the time, so most of its movement is undone. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- In the true-scale cell model, 1 micrometre becomes 1 centimetre. That makes everything 10,000 times as big. A child is 130 centimetres tall. How tall would she be at the same scale, in kilometres? **13 kilometres.**. 130 × 10,000 = 1,300,000 centimetres. There are 100 centimetres in a metre, so that is 13,000 metres. There are 1,000 metres in a kilometre, so that is 13 kilometres. One of her cells would be 20 centimetres wide inside a body 13 kilometres tall. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- A cell is 10 times as wide, 10 times as long and 10 times as high as a bacterium. How many bacteria would fill the same space? The cell's outer surface is only 100 times as big. Why is that a problem for the cell? **1,000 bacteria. The cell has 1,000 times as much inside to supply, through only 100 times as much surface.**. 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. Surface grows as 10 × 10 = 100. Everything a cell takes in must cross her surface. So each bit of surface of the large cell must serve ten times as much inside. Folded membranes inside the cell, and mitochondria spread all through her, help to solve this. (queues `math.area-perimeter.volume.L5`)
