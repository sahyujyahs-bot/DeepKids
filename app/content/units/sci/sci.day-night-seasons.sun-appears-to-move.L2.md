# The Sun appears to move: sunlight and shadows from morning to evening
`sci.day-night-seasons.sun-appears-to-move.L2`

**Summary.** A patch of sunlight on the floor creeps slowly across the room, even when nobody touches the window. In this unit your child marks a sun patch every 5 minutes and watches it move. They count their own shadow in footsteps in the morning, at noon and in the evening, and find it long, then short, then long again. They learn that the Sun rises in the east, is highest around noon and sets in the west. And they learn that the Sun only appears to move, because the Earth, with our home on it, is turning.

**Intro.** *In the morning, your shadow on the road is longer than you are. At lunchtime it hides near your feet. Why does it change?* Your body stayed the same size all day. Your shadow did not. A shadow is the dark patch where your body blocks the sunlight. So if the shadow changed, something about the sunlight changed. In this unit we watch the sunlight and the shadows from morning to evening. We find out how the Sun seems to move across the sky, and why.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say how the Sun appears to move across the sky: it rises in the east, is highest around noon, and sets in the west.
1. By the end I can say that the Sun only seems to move round us, because the Earth turns.
2. By the end I can say that shadows are long in the morning and in the evening, and shortest around noon.
3. By the end I can say that a shadow points away from the Sun, so morning and evening shadows point opposite ways.
4. By the end I can mark how a sun patch or a shadow moves, and count how far it went.
5. By the end I can find which side of the sky the Sun is on from a shadow, without ever looking at the Sun.

## Terms
- **Sunrise**: Sunrise is the time in the morning when the Sun first appears at the edge of the sky. *e.g.* At sunrise, the sky near the ground turns pink and orange, and the birds start calling.
- **Sunset**: Sunset is the time in the evening when the Sun goes down below the edge of the sky. *e.g.* After sunset, the street lights come on and the sky slowly gets dark.
- **Noon**: Noon, or midday, is the middle of the day, when the Sun is highest in the sky. *e.g.* Around noon, your shadow is shorter than at any other time of the day.
- **Shadow**: A shadow is the darker patch where light cannot reach, because something is in the way. *e.g.* Under a neem tree on a sunny day, there is a cool shadow on the ground.
- **East**: East is the side of the sky where the Sun rises in the morning. *e.g.* If the morning sunlight comes in through your bedroom window, that window looks east.
- **West**: West is the side of the sky where the Sun sets in the evening. It is opposite east. *e.g.* If the evening sunlight comes into your kitchen, the kitchen window looks west.
- **Appears**: Something appears to happen when it looks as if it happens, even if the real reason is different. *e.g.* From a moving train, the trees appear to rush backwards. Really, the train is moving.
- **Rotate**: To rotate means to spin or turn round. The Earth rotates once every day. *e.g.* A top rotates on the floor. The Earth rotates too, but so smoothly that we do not notice it.
- **Sun patch**: A sun patch is the bright shape that sunlight makes on a floor or wall when it comes in through a window or a door. *e.g.* In the morning, a cat often lies in the sun patch by the balcony door.
- **Sundial**: A sundial is a clock that tells the time from the shadow made by the Sun. *e.g.* At Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, a huge stone sundial shows the time with the shadow of a tall wall.

## Explanations
### The Sun appears to move across the sky · *defined*
**Definition:** Each day the Sun appears to rise in the east, climb higher until about noon, then sink lower and set in the west.

In the early morning, the Sun is low in the east. Through the morning it seems to climb, and around noon it is highest. In the afternoon it seems to sink, and in the evening it sets in the west. We can see all this from sunlight and shadows, without looking at the Sun.
- e.g. Early morning: the Sun is low, and the light comes in sideways through east windows.
- e.g. Noon: the Sun is high, and the light falls almost straight down.
- e.g. Evening: the Sun is low again, and the light comes in sideways through west windows.
### Why the Sun seems to move: the Earth turns · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun seems to move across our sky because the Earth rotates, or turns, once every day.

The Earth is a huge ball, and it is always turning. Our home, our street and we ourselves turn with it. As we turn, the Sun seems to rise, cross the sky and set. It is like sitting in a train: the trees seem to rush past, but really the train is moving. The turning is so smooth that we cannot feel it.
- e.g. From a moving train, still trees appear to move backwards.
- e.g. On a turning merry-go-round, the people standing outside seem to slide past you.
- e.g. The sun patch on your floor moved, though nobody touched the window.
### Morning, noon and evening shadows · *defined*
**Definition:** When the Sun is low, shadows are long. When the Sun is high, shadows are short. So shadows are long in the morning and evening, and shortest around noon.

Sunlight travels in straight lines. When the Sun is low, its light comes in sideways, so your body blocks a long stretch of ground and your shadow is long. When the Sun is high, the light comes from above, so your shadow is short. Around noon it is shortest.
- e.g. At 8 in the morning, a child's shadow can be 10 or more of her own footsteps long.
- e.g. Around noon, the same child's shadow may be only 1 or 2 footsteps long.
- e.g. In the evening, the shadow is long again.
### A shadow points away from the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** A shadow always falls on the side of a thing away from the light. So a shadow points away from the Sun.

A shadow falls on the side away from the Sun. In the morning the Sun is in the east, so shadows point west. In the evening the Sun is in the west, so shadows point east. So a shadow tells you where the Sun is, without looking at it. In most of India, for most of the year, the noon shadow is short and points a little to the north.
- e.g. Morning: the shadow of a lamp post points west.
- e.g. Evening: the same shadow points east.
- e.g. If your shadow is in front of you, the Sun is behind you.
### Noon with no shadow: Zero Shadow Day · *defined*
**Definition:** On Zero Shadow Day, the noon Sun is straight overhead, so an upright thing makes almost no shadow at its side.

At noon the Sun is usually a little to one side, so even then there is a small shadow. But south of a line called the Tropic of Cancer, the Sun is straight overhead at noon on two days each year. Then a standing pole or tumbler has almost no shadow beside it. Bengaluru has these days in late April and mid-August. Delhi is north of the line, so it never has one.
- e.g. In Bengaluru, people stand bottles and glasses on the ground at noon in late April to see their shadows vanish.
- e.g. Chennai, Hyderabad, Mumbai and Kochi have Zero Shadow Days too, each on its own dates.
- e.g. Delhi and Jaipur never have one.
### Other beings follow the Sun's movement too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals and plants keep track of the Sun's movement across the sky and use it.

Honeybees use the Sun to find their way back to flowers, and change direction as it moves. Young sunflower plants turn to face the Sun in the morning and follow it west. At night they turn back to face east. Cats move from patch to patch to stay in the warm sunshine. These beings sense the Sun, remember where it was, and get ready for where it will be.
- e.g. A honeybee finds her way home by the Sun, even as it moves.
- e.g. A young sunflower plant faces east before sunrise.
- e.g. A cat moves to the next sun patch as the first one slides away.
- **Many plant and animal scientists:** Bees and sunflowers keep track of the Sun with an inner clock, and they sense, remember and act on what they sense.
- **Some other scientists:** Some scientists call these automatic responses, and prefer not to use words like remember and plan for plants.

## Mostly agreed
- The Sun appears to rise in the east, cross the sky and set in the west every day. *How we know:* People all over the world, in every age, have seen this. You can see it from your own sun patches and shadows.
- The Sun seems to move because the Earth rotates once a day. *How we know:* Scientists have shown the Earth's turning with swinging pendulums, with spacecraft photos and with the way the stars all circle round the sky at night.
- Shadows are longest when the Sun is low, and shortest around noon, when the Sun is highest. *How we know:* You can count your own shadow in footsteps at three times of day. Sundials have worked this way for thousands of years.
- Never look straight at the Sun, because its light can hurt the back of your eyes. *How we know:* Eye doctors see damage from looking at the Sun, often after people watch an eclipse without safe glasses.

## How we found out
**Aristarchus of Samos, a Greek astronomer; Seleucus of Seleucia, on the river Tigris; Aryabhata in India; later Nicolaus Copernicus in Poland, About 2,300 years ago for Aristarchus, about 2,150 years ago for Seleucus, about 1,500 years ago for Aryabhata, and 1543 for Copernicus.** For a very long time, most people thought the Earth stood still and the Sun travelled round it once a day. It really does look that way. About 2,300 years ago, a Greek astronomer called Aristarchus of Samos said that the Earth turns, and also goes round the Sun. Seleucus, who lived in Seleucia on the river Tigris, in what is now Iraq, agreed with him. Almost nobody else did. About 1,500 years ago in India, Aryabhata wrote that the Earth turns. He said the stars only seem to move west, like still trees that seem to move backwards when you sit in a boat. In 1543, Nicolaus Copernicus in Poland wrote a book with the Sun in the middle. Later, careful tests with telescopes and pendulums showed that the Earth really does turn. These thinkers were right, though most people around them did not believe it.  
*What people believed before:* That the Earth stands still and the Sun travels round it every day. It looks that way from the ground, but later evidence from telescopes, pendulums and photos from space showed that the Earth turns.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Sun travels round the Earth once every day.** (Most people for thousands of years, and the first idea of most children; *rejected*)  
  For: It really does look that way. The Sun rises, crosses the sky and sets, and the ground does not seem to move.  
  Against: The Earth turns once a day, and that makes the Sun seem to move. Swinging pendulums, photos from space, and the way all the stars wheel across the night sky together show it.
- **At noon, there is no shadow at all.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: At noon, shadows are very short and hide close to your feet.  
  Against: In most places, the noon Sun is a little to one side, so there is still a small shadow. Only on Zero Shadow Day, in places south of the Tropic of Cancer, is it straight overhead.
- **The Sun rises in exactly the same spot every day.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Every morning, the Sun rises on the east side of the sky.  
  Against: Over the months, the place where the Sun rises slides slowly north and then back south along the edge of the sky. It rises exactly in the east only around 21 March and 23 September.

## Roots
We have watched a sun patch creep across the floor, and our own shadows change from morning to evening. Now we ask what the moving patch tells us about ourselves and our home.

**Deep truth.** The sun patch crept across your floor while the window stayed where it was. The Sun does not travel round the Earth each day. The Earth turns, and it carries your home round with it. So even sitting still on the floor, you were moving the whole time.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call a thing still when it does not move compared with the things near it. The room, the floor and you were all moving together, so none of you could see it. Only the sunlight, coming from far outside, showed the movement.  
*Evidence:* In the Moving Sun Patch test, the corner of the patch moved a few finger widths from the first coin in 20 minutes. Nobody had touched the window, the curtain or the coins.

- *who am i:* You are someone riding on a turning Earth. Even asleep in bed, you travel all the way round with it every day.
- *what is life:* Cats, honeybees and young sunflower plants all keep track of where the Sun is going. Living beings watch the sky and plan by it, just as you can.
- *what is this world:* The ground under your feet is moving all the time. The Sun's daily journey across the sky is the Earth's own turn, seen from the ground.

One turning of the Earth makes the whole day. It brings the sunrise to Kohima first, and to Kochi a little later. It makes long morning shadows, short noon shadows and long evening shadows from the same Sun. Morning, noon and evening are not three different things. They are one movement, seen at different moments.

- **Aitareya Brahmana 3.44:** The Aitareya Brahmana, an old Sanskrit text, says the Sun never really rises or sets. It says that when people think the Sun is setting, it is only making night on one side and day on the other. (Your sun patch test agrees that rising and setting are not what they seem. The old text gave a different reason. We now know it is the Earth's turning that makes the Sun seem to rise and set.)
- **Jain teaching of anekantavada, many-sidedness:** Jain teachers say that a thing can be truly described in more than one way, depending on where you stand. They tell of blind men who each touch one part of an elephant and each describe the elephant differently. (From the ground, the Sun moves across the sky. From space, the Earth turns. Both describe the same day, from two places.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 91 (Diels-Kranz numbering):** Heraclitus, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,500 years ago, said you cannot step into the same river twice, because new water is always flowing past. (Your floor looked as still as a calm pond. Yet it was always moving with the Earth. Even things that look still are on the move.)
- Science (settled): The Earth turns once a day. In most of India, the ground carries you east at about 1,500 kilometres an hour, faster than a jet plane.
- Science (settled): Young sunflower plants follow the Sun across the sky by day and turn back east at night. An inner clock helps them get ready for the sunrise.
- Other view, Some physicists and philosophers: Movement is always measured against something else. So from the ground, it is fair to say the Sun moves across the sky. Saying the Earth turns is chosen because it explains the Sun, the stars and the seasons together, most simply.
- Practice, Sit in the Sun Patch (3 min): Sit at the edge of a sun patch, with your toes just touching its corner. Close your eyes. Feel the warmth on your toes. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly, ten times. Now open your eyes. Is the corner still touching your toes? You sat still. What moved?
- Ask: You sat still, yet the patch moved. What else around you might be moving that you do not notice?
- Ask: From the ground the Sun moves, and from space the Earth turns. Which way of saying it feels true to you, and why?
- Ask: Kutti the cat moves to stay in the sunshine. How do you think she knows where it will go next?

- *big:* From the ground, the Sun moves. From space, the Earth turns. Is one of these more true than the other? (hint: Both describe the same day. Which one explains more things at once?)

## Story: Kutti Follows the Sunshine
*A made-up story. It is true that a sun patch moves across a floor during the day, that families in India dry chillies, vadam and mango slices in the sun and move them as the sunshine moves, and that cats seek out warm sunny spots.*

Meenakshi lived on the third floor of a building in Madurai. She lived with her Amma, her little brother Hari and her grandmother, Ammamma. They also had a ginger cat called Kutti. Kutti loved one thing even more than fish. She loved lying in the sunshine.

On Saturday morning, a bright patch of sunlight lay on the floor by the balcony door. It had the shape of the door, with dark stripes from the grill. Kutti was curled up right in the middle of it, warm and sleepy.

Ammamma was out on the balcony, spreading red chillies on an old cotton saree to dry. "The sunshine is on this end now," she said. "I will move them after lunch."

Meenakshi sat down to draw. Hari played with his cars. After a while, Meenakshi looked up. Kutti had moved. She was lying a little closer to the door now, still right in the sunshine.

"Kutti moved the sunshine," said Hari. "She pulled it with her."

Meenakshi was not sure. "Let us find out," she said. She took a one-rupee coin and put it on the floor, right at one corner of the sun patch.

Then she lifted Kutti gently and carried her to the sofa. Kutti gave a cross little mew, turned round twice and went back to sleep.

Meenakshi and Hari watched the coin. At first, nothing happened. Hari counted to a hundred, twice. Then he shouted, "Look! The corner has moved away from the coin!"

It had. The bright corner was now a finger's width away from the coin. Kutti was on the sofa, far away. Nobody had touched the door or the curtain.

"So Kutti did not move it," said Meenakshi. "The sunshine moves by itself. Kutti just follows it to stay warm."

"Then what moves it?" asked Hari.

Ammamma came in from the balcony. "Come and see," she said. "But never look at the Sun. Look at the shadow of the water tank on the next building." Early that morning, the tank's shadow had stretched right across the terrace. Now it was much shorter.

"When I was your age," said Ammamma, "my mother dried mango slices on our roof. She moved them three times a day. In the morning, the sunshine came from one side. At noon, it was everywhere, and the shadows were tiny. In the evening, it came from the other side."

"Because the Sun moves across the sky," said Hari.

"It looks that way," said Ammamma. "The Sun seems to move. But really, the Earth is turning, slowly, all day long. Our building turns with it, and so do we. So the sunlight comes in through our door at a new slant, and the patch slides across the floor."

Hari stood very still in the middle of the room. "I am not moving," he said.

Meenakshi laughed. "You are," she said. "The coin showed it. The whole room is turning, and you are in it."

After lunch, Ammamma moved her chillies to the other end of the balcony. By the evening, the patch by the balcony door was gone. A new, long patch of orange light lay across the kitchen floor. It came in through the window on the other side of the flat.

Kutti was already lying in the new patch.

"How did she know it would be there?" asked Hari.

"She has been watching the sunshine move every day of her life," said Meenakshi. "She remembers where it goes."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Hari first thinks the cat pulls the sunshine with her. Meenakshi's coin test shows the patch moves even when the cat is far away. Ammamma explains that the Sun only seems to move, because the Earth, with the building on it, is turning.

- Ask: How did Meenakshi find out that Kutti was not moving the sunshine?
- Ask: Why did Ammamma move her chillies after lunch?
- Ask: Why was the water tank's shadow shorter later in the morning?
- Ask: Why was the evening sunshine in the kitchen, and not by the balcony door?

### e2 · Shadow Footsteps: how long is your shadow in the morning, at noon and in the evening? (on a sunny terrace or in the building compound) (20 min)
- **question:** Is your shadow the same length all day, or does it change between morning, noon and evening?
- **predict:** Guess first. When will your shadow be longest: in the morning, at noon or in the evening? When will it be shortest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We stand on the same spot each time, with our back straight and our arms down. We count in the same child's footsteps, heel touching toe.
- **safety:** Never look straight at the Sun. A grown-up comes along to the terrace or the compound. At noon in summer, keep each visit short, wear a cap and drink water.
- **record:** Fill in the table, and draw yourself three times with your shadow, the right length and pointing the right way.
- **results table:** Time | My guess: long or short? | My shadow in footsteps | It points towards
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason for each time of day.  
  *Parent says:* We will count your shadow three times today: morning, noon and evening. When will it be longest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* When will your shadow be longest? Morning, noon or evening? Why?
  - Note: This test needs an open sunny place. A grown-up fills in the table. If you have no open sunny place, skip this test or do it at a park or on a relative's terrace.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At about 8 in the morning, mark a spot with chalk. 2. Your child stands on it. 3. Put a stone where the shadow of the head ends. 4. Your child walks heel to toe from the cross to the stone and counts. 5. Note a building or tree the shadow points at.  
  *Parent says:* Stand on the cross and keep still. I will put a stone where your shadow ends. Now walk from the cross to the stone, heel to toe, and count. What is the shadow pointing at?  
  *Child mode:* Stand on the cross. Keep still. Then walk to the stone, heel to toe, and count your steps.
  - Note: A child's shadow at 8 in the morning is often 8 to 15 of the child's own footsteps. The exact number depends on the month and the city.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go back around noon, when your shadow looks shortest (about 12 to 12:30 in most of India, earlier in the east). 2. Your child stands on the same cross. 3. Mark the end of the shadow with a stone and count again.  
  *Parent says:* It is around noon now. Before we count, guess: longer or shorter than this morning?  
  *Child mode:* It is noon. Guess first. Then count your shadow again.
  - Note: Around noon the shadow is only 0 to 3 footsteps, close round the feet. In the summer months in South India, it can almost vanish.
  - Wait: Come back around noon
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go back at about 5 in the evening. 2. Your child stands on the same cross. 3. Mark the end of the shadow with a stone, count again and see which way the shadow points.  
  *Parent says:* It is evening. Count your shadow. Which way does it point now? Is that the same way as this morning?  
  *Child mode:* It is evening. Count your shadow. Which way does it point now?
  - Ask: What is different about the evening shadow? (good: It is long again.; It points the other way from the morning shadow.; It points towards the east now.)
  - Note: The evening shadow points nearly the opposite way from the morning one, because the Sun is now on the other side of the sky.
  - Wait: Come back at about 5 in the evening
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask what your child thinks, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Your body was the same height all day. What changed your shadow?  
  *Child mode:* You did not grow or shrink. So why did your shadow change?
  - Check: Rani, a girl in Class 2, finds her shadow is 12 footsteps long in the morning. What is it likely to be at noon? → **About 2 footsteps**

### p1 · The Floor Clock: make a sun patch tell the time (30 min, 2 days)
- **record:** A photo or drawing of the floor clock with its time strips, and a table: my guess from the floor clock, the real time.
- **schedule:** day 1: Every half hour for two hours, stick a strip at the same corner of the sun patch and write the time on it. Each mark takes about 2 minutes. (15 min); day 2: Without looking at a clock, guess the time from where the patch corner is. Then check the clock. Do it three or four times. (15 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick a sun patch that lasts at least two hours. 2. Choose one sharp corner. 3. Cut 5 strips.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to turn this sun patch into a clock. Which corner has the sharpest edge?  
  *Child mode:* Pick the sharpest corner of the sun patch.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stick the first strip at the corner and write the time. 2. Every half hour, stick the next strip at the corner and write the time. 3. Leave the strips in place.  
  *Parent says:* It is half past nine. Stick a strip at the corner and write the time.  
  *Child mode:* Stick a strip at the corner. Write the time on it.
  - Wait: Every half hour for two hours
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The next day, ask your child to read the time from the patch, without a clock. 2. Then check the clock together. 3. Do this three or four times.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the patch. Which strip is it touching? So what time do you think it is? Now let us check the clock.  
  *Child mode:* Which strip is the corner touching? What time is it, then? Check the clock.

  - Wait: The next sunny day

  *Child mode:* Was your floor clock right? Why does it work?
  - Note: Over a few weeks, the patch drifts a little, because the Sun's path changes with the seasons. Over two days, the floor clock is usually right to within a few minutes.
  - Check: Leela, a girl in Class 2, made a floor clock. It worked on Monday and on Tuesday. Why can a sun patch tell the time? → **The Sun seems to move across the sky the same way every day**

## Challenges
### c1 · Shade for a Toy, All Day Long (20 min)
- **problem:** Can you build a shade from card and paper that keeps a toy in shadow in the morning, at noon and in the evening?
- **your turn:** Build your own shade. Test it at all three torch places. Where does light still sneak in? Change one thing and test again.
- **constraints:** Use only paper, card from old boxes, tape and ice-cream sticks or pencils.; The shade must not be a closed box. The toy must still be seen from the front.; Test it with a torch held low on the left, high above, and low on the right.
- **general:** A flat roof on four sticks shades the toy at noon, when the torch is high.; A roof alone fails in the morning and evening, because the low light comes in sideways under it.; Add walls on the two sides where the low morning and evening light comes from.
- **creative:** Make the roof wide and slope it down at both sides, like the roof of a Kerala house.; Hang paper flaps from the roof edges that you can lower in the evening.; Use a tall tree made of card on one side, the way a real tree shades a house.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the challenge with your child. Show the materials.  
  *Parent says:* The torch is the Sun. It will shine from low on this side, then from high up, then from low on the other side. How will you keep the toy in the shade every time?  
  *Child mode:* The torch is the Sun. Keep the toy in the shade all day.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child build. 2. Test the shade at all three torch places. 3. Help with tape.  
  *Parent says:* Test it now. Torch low on the left. Is the toy in shadow? Now high above. Now low on the right.  
  *Child mode:* Test it. Low on the left. High above. Low on the right. Is the toy shaded?
  - Note: Most first designs are a flat roof, which works only at noon. Let your child find this out by testing, before you give any hints.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child which torch place was hardest, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which Sun was hardest to block: morning, noon or evening? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Which Sun was hardest to block? Why?
  - Check: Divya, a girl in Class 2, puts a flat roof over her toy. When does the torch Sun still shine on the toy? → **When the torch is low at one side**

## Worksheets
### The Sun's path and our shadows (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) On which side of the sky does the Sun rise?  
   Options: East / West / Under the ground  
   **Answer:** East. *Why:* The Sun rises in the east every morning. A strong answer can point to the east side of the home.
2. (truefalse) True or false: at noon, shadows are at their longest.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. At noon the Sun is high, so shadows are at their shortest. They are longest in the early morning and the evening.
3. (match) Draw a line from each time to the shadow it makes.  
   **Answer:** Early morning → a long shadow pointing west; Noon → a short shadow near the feet; Evening → a long shadow pointing east. *Why:* A low Sun makes a long shadow, and a high Sun a short one. The shadow points away from the Sun.
4. (draw) Draw yourself standing in the sunshine at noon. Draw your shadow too.  
   **Answer:** A child with a small dark shadow close round the feet, and the Sun high above.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the Sun high up and the shadow short and close to the feet.
5. (fill) When the Sun is low in the sky, a shadow is ___.  
   **Answer:** long. *Why:* A low Sun shines sideways, so the shadow stretches a long way.
6. (order) Put the Sun's places in order, from morning to evening.  
   **Answer:** ['low in the east', 'highest in the sky', 'low in the west']. *Why:* The Sun seems to rise in the east, climb to its highest around noon, then sink and set in the west.
7. (truefalse) True or false: the shadow of a tree points towards the Sun.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A shadow falls on the side away from the Sun, so it points away from the Sun.
8. (write) Remember the Moving Sun Patch test. Say it, and a grown-up writes it down: what happened to the corner of the patch?  
   **Answer:** The corner moved a little further from the first coin every 5 minutes, though nobody touched the window.. *Why:* A strong answer says the patch moved by itself and steadily, and names the way it went.
9. (label) Label the picture: sunrise, noon, sunset, east, west.  
   **Answer:** ['sunrise: the low Sun on the left', 'noon: the high Sun in the middle', 'sunset: the low Sun on the right', 'east: the left edge of the sky', 'west: the right edge of the sky']. *Why:* These are the main places on the Sun's daily path.
10. (sort) Sort these into: safe ways to find the Sun, and not safe.  
   **Answer:** looking at a shadow → Safe; looking at the Sun through sunglasses → Not safe; feeling which cheek is warm, with eyes closed → Safe; staring at the Sun for one second → Not safe; seeing which wall is brightly lit → Safe. *Why:* Sunlight is strong enough to hurt the back of the eye, even through sunglasses. Shadows, warmth and lit walls show where the Sun is without looking at it.

### The Sun's path and our shadows (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (write) Many people think the Sun goes round the Earth, because that is what it looks like. What would you show a friend to help them think again? You can say it and a grown-up can write it down.  
   **Answer:** For example: a train ride, where still trees seem to move because we are moving. Or the sun patch, which moved though nothing in the room moved.. *Why:* A strong answer uses something the friend can see or try, and explains that something moving can make still things seem to move.

   Options: The Sun is straight overhead / The Sun has gone behind a cloud / Shadows stop working in April  
   **Answer:** The Sun is straight overhead. *Why:* On Zero Shadow Day, the noon Sun is straight overhead. The light falls straight down, so the shadow is right under her.
3. (mcq) Sneha, a girl in Class 2, wants to dry her clothes in the sun all afternoon. Her balcony faces east. Is it a good place?  
   Options: No, an east balcony gets the morning sun, not the afternoon sun / Yes, an east balcony is sunny all day / Yes, because the Sun sets in the east  
   **Answer:** No, an east balcony gets the morning sun, not the afternoon sun. *Why:* The Sun is in the east in the morning. In the afternoon it is in the west, so a west-facing place gets the afternoon sun.
4. (order) Put these shadows of the same girl in order, from shortest to longest: at 7 in the morning, at 10 in the morning, at noon.  
   **Answer:** ['at noon', 'at 10 in the morning', 'at 7 in the morning']. *Why:* The higher the Sun, the shorter the shadow. The Sun is highest at noon, a bit lower at 10, and lowest at 7.
5. (truefalse) True or false: the Sun rises at the same moment everywhere in India.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Earth turns towards the east, so places in the east see the Sun first. In Arunachal Pradesh, the Sun rises almost two hours before it does in the far west of Gujarat.
6. (draw) Design a shade for a bench on a terrace that keeps it shady in the morning and in the evening, not only at noon. Draw it, with the Sun.  
   **Answer:** A roof with walls or a screen on the east and west sides, or a very wide roof that comes down low on both sides.. *Why:* A strong design blocks the low morning Sun from the east and the low evening Sun from the west, not only the high noon Sun.
7. (perspective) Two friends, Sunita and Imran, disagree about when noon is.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. Sunita is right that clocks call 12 o'clock noon, so everyone shares one time. Imran is right that the Sun's noon is when shadows are shortest. In many Indian cities that is a little after or a little before 12.. *Why:* Clock noon and Sun noon are not exactly the same. All of India uses one clock time, but the Sun is highest at slightly different times in different cities.
8. (match) Draw a line from each being to how they use the Sun's movement.  
   **Answer:** a honeybee → finds her way back to the flowers by the Sun; a young sunflower plant → turns to follow the Sun, and faces east before sunrise; a cat → moves to the next sun patch to stay warm. *Why:* Many beings keep track of where the Sun is going and act on it, each in their own way.
9. (count) Bengaluru has 2 Zero Shadow Days every year. How many Zero Shadow Days is that in 5 years?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* Count in twos: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. That is 10 Zero Shadow Days.
10. (truefalse) True or false: the Sun sets exactly in the west every day of the year.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Sun sets exactly in the west only around 21 March and 23 September. On other days it sets a little north or south of west.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) On which side of the sky does the Sun set?  
   Options: West / East / Straight overhead  
   **Answer:** West. *Why:* The Sun sets in the west every evening. It rises in the east, on the opposite side.
2. (mcq) When is the Sun highest in the sky?  
   Options: Around noon / Just after sunrise / Just before sunset  
   **Answer:** Around noon. *Why:* The Sun seems to climb through the morning, is highest around noon, and sinks in the afternoon.

   Options: The Earth turns once a day / The Sun runs round the Earth / The clouds push the Sun along  
   **Answer:** The Earth turns once a day. *Why:* The Earth rotates, carrying us with it. From the ground, that makes the Sun seem to move. The Sun is not going round us.
4. (mcq) Nila is on a train. The trees outside seem to rush backwards. What is really moving?  
   Options: The train / The trees / Nothing  
   **Answer:** The train. *Why:* The train moves, so still things outside appear to move the other way. The turning Earth makes the Sun appear to move in the same way.
5. (mcq) At what time of day is your shadow shortest?  
   Options: Around noon / At 8 in the morning / At 5 in the evening  
   **Answer:** Around noon. *Why:* Around noon the Sun is high, so its light comes from above and your body blocks only a small patch near your feet.

   Options: The Sun is low, so its light comes in sideways / You are taller in the morning / The Sun is closer in the morning  
   **Answer:** The Sun is low, so its light comes in sideways. *Why:* A low Sun shines sideways, so your body blocks the light over a long stretch of ground.
7. (mcq) In the morning, the Sun is in the east. Which way do shadows point?  
   Options: West / East / Up  
   **Answer:** West. *Why:* A shadow falls on the side away from the light. With the Sun in the east, shadows point west.
8. (mcq) Your shadow is in front of you as you walk. Where is the Sun?  
   Options: Behind you / In front of you / Under your feet  
   **Answer:** Behind you. *Why:* A shadow points away from the Sun. If your shadow is in front, the Sun must be behind you.
9. (mcq) Pooja, a girl in Class 2, marked a sun patch corner every 5 minutes. The marks went further and further from the first one. What did she find?  
   Options: The sun patch moves slowly and steadily / The sun patch stays still / The sun patch moves only when she looks away  
   **Answer:** The sun patch moves slowly and steadily. *Why:* Her marks show that the patch creeps a little way every few minutes, because the Sun seems to move across the sky.
10. (mcq) What is the safe way to find where the Sun is?  
   Options: Look at a shadow. The Sun is on the opposite side / Look at the Sun for just a second / Look at the Sun through sunglasses  
   **Answer:** Look at a shadow. The Sun is on the opposite side. *Why:* Looking at the Sun, even briefly or with sunglasses, can hurt the back of your eyes. A shadow tells you where the Sun is without any danger.
11. (mcq) The shadow of a lamp post points towards the temple. Where is the Sun?  
   Options: On the side of the lamp post away from the temple / Above the temple / Under the lamp post  
   **Answer:** On the side of the lamp post away from the temple. *Why:* The Sun is always on the opposite side from the shadow. So it is on the side away from the temple.

## Applications
- Sundials, like the giant Samrat Yantra at Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, tell the time from the shadow of the Sun.
- Families across India dry chillies, papad, vadam, mango slices and washing in the sun, and move them as the sunshine moves.
- Architects place windows, balconies and sunshades so rooms get gentle morning light and stay cooler in the hot afternoon.
- Solar panels are tilted to face the part of the sky where the Sun spends most of the day. Some big ones turn slowly to follow the Sun.
- Hikers and sailors have used the Sun's place in the sky to find east and west.

## Spotted around us
- In the morning, a cat or a dog lies in the sun patch by a window, and moves as the patch moves.
- On a terrace in Hyderabad, papad and vadam drying in the sun are moved to the sunny side after lunch.
- In Kolkata, during an evening walk, the long shadows of buildings stretch across the road towards the east.
- At noon in a Kochi school yard, children's shadows shrink to small dark puddles round their feet.
- At Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, the shadow of a giant stone wall creeps along a curved scale to show the time.
- On a sunny morning in Mumbai, the auto-rickshaws parked along one side of the road are in shade, and by evening those on the other side are.

## Open questions
- **Why does the Earth's turning speed up and slow down by tiny amounts?** Clocks show that some days are a few thousandths of a second shorter or longer than others. Scientists think the Moon, the oceans, the air and the Earth's melted core all play a part, but they still argue about how much each one matters.
- **Were the great stone wheels of the Konark Sun Temple in Odisha made to tell the time?** Guides show visitors how to read the time from the shadows on the wheels' spokes. But no old record says the builders meant them as sundials, so historians are not sure.
- **How exactly does a young sunflower plant know when to turn back east at night?** Scientists have shown that an inner clock helps, and that the two sides of the stem grow at different times. How the clock and the growing work together is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the Moving Sun Patch test, Leela, a girl in Class 2, found that her patch corner moved 3 finger widths in 15 minutes. If it keeps going at the same speed, how far will it move in 30 minutes? **6 finger widths.**. 30 minutes is two lots of 15 minutes. Each lot moves the corner 3 finger widths. 3 and 3 make 6. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- In the Shadow Footsteps test, Rani, a girl in Class 2, counted her shadow. It was 11 footsteps in the morning, 2 at noon and 14 in the evening. Which shadow was longest? How many footsteps longer was it than the morning shadow? **The evening shadow was longest. It was 3 footsteps longer than the morning shadow.**. 14 is more than 11, and both are more than 2. Count on from 11 to 14: 12, 13, 14. That is 3 more. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the story, Meenakshi's grandmother Ammamma moves her drying chillies at 8 o'clock in the morning, at 12 o'clock, and at 4 o'clock in the afternoon. How many hours are there between each move? **4 hours.**. From 8 o'clock to 12 o'clock: 9, 10, 11, 12. That is 4 hours. From 12 o'clock to 4 o'clock: 1, 2, 3, 4. That is 4 hours too. (queues `math.time.telling-time.L2`)
- Meenakshi, the girl in the story, marks a sun patch every 5 minutes. Her first mark is at 9:00, the next at 9:05, then 9:10. What time will her fifth mark be? **9:20.**. Count on in fives: 9:00, 9:05, 9:10, 9:15, 9:20. The fifth mark is at 9:20. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
