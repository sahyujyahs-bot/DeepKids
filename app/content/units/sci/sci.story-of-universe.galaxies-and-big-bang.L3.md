# Galaxies and the Big Bang: the universe is growing, and it had a beginning
`sci.story-of-universe.galaxies-and-big-bang.L3`

**Summary.** Almost every galaxy is moving away from us, and the further away a galaxy is, the faster it moves away. This happens because the space between the galaxies is stretching. Run the stretching backwards, and everything was once packed close together and very hot, about 13.8 billion years ago. Scientists call that beginning the Big Bang. Galaxies come in different shapes, from flat spirals to smooth ovals, and their light takes so long to reach us that we see far galaxies as they were long ago.

**Intro.** *Astronomers have found that almost every galaxy in the sky is moving away from us. Does that mean we are at the centre of the universe?* In this unit you stick bindis on a balloon and blow it bigger, to see how things move when the space between them grows. You meet the shapes that galaxies come in, and test how one galaxy can look like different shapes. You find out how Henrietta Leavitt and Edwin Hubble showed that our Milky Way is one galaxy among very many. And you learn why looking far away means looking back in time.

## Terms
- **Galaxy**: A huge group of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity. A galaxy can have billions of stars. *e.g.* Our Sun is one of the stars in a galaxy called the Milky Way.
- **Milky Way**: The galaxy we live in. From a dark place, we see part of it as a faint glowing band across the night sky. *e.g.* The Milky Way has between 100 and 400 billion stars, and it is about 100,000 light-years across.
- **Spiral galaxy**: A galaxy shaped like a flat disc, with arms that curl out from a bright middle. New stars are being made in the arms. *e.g.* The Andromeda galaxy and the Whirlpool galaxy are spiral galaxies.
- **Barred spiral galaxy**: A spiral galaxy with a straight bar of stars across its middle. The arms start from the ends of the bar. *e.g.* Our Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy.
- **Elliptical galaxy**: A galaxy shaped like a smooth oval or ball of stars, with no arms. Most of its stars are old, and few new ones are being made. *e.g.* M87 is a giant elliptical galaxy, about 55 million light-years away.
- **Irregular galaxy**: A galaxy with no clear shape. Many irregular galaxies are small. *e.g.* The Large Magellanic Cloud is a small irregular galaxy close to the Milky Way.
- **Light-year**: How far light travels in one year, about 9.5 million million kilometres. It is a distance, not a length of time. *e.g.* The Andromeda galaxy is about 2.5 million light-years away, so its light takes about 2.5 million years to reach us.
- **Expanding universe**: The space between the galaxies is growing all the time, so galaxies far apart get further and further apart. *e.g.* A galaxy twice as far away from us is moving away about twice as fast.
- **Big Bang**: The name for the start of the universe as we know it. About 13.8 billion years ago, everything was packed close together and very hot, and it has been stretching and cooling ever since. *e.g.* The hydrogen in the water you drink was made in the first few minutes after the Big Bang.
- **Cosmic microwave background**: A very faint glow of microwaves that reaches us from every direction in the sky. It is light left over from when the whole universe was hot. *e.g.* Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found it by accident in 1965 with a big radio antenna.
- **Redshift**: When light travels across stretching space, its waves are stretched too. This shifts the light towards red. Astronomers measure it to find how fast a galaxy is moving away. *e.g.* Light from very far galaxies is stretched so much that it arrives as infrared light, which our eyes cannot see.
- **Cepheid**: A kind of star that gets brighter and dimmer again and again, in a steady rhythm. The slower it blinks, the brighter it really is. *e.g.* Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid in the Andromeda galaxy, and used it to show how far away Andromeda is.
- **Nebula**: A cloud of gas and dust in space. Before 1923, astronomers also used this word for the faint spiral smudges that turned out to be other galaxies. *e.g.* The Orion Nebula is a real cloud of gas inside our galaxy, where new stars are being made.

## Explanations
### What a galaxy is · *defined*
**Definition:** A galaxy is a huge group of stars, gas and dust, held together by its own gravity.

Stars are not spread evenly through space. They gather in galaxies, with huge empty gaps between the galaxies. Our Sun is one star in the Milky Way. The Milky Way has between 100 and 400 billion stars. Light takes about 100,000 years to cross it. There are hundreds of billions of galaxies in the part of the universe we can see, and perhaps many more. In 2022, the Webb space telescope photographed a patch of sky as small as a grain of sand held at arm's length. It held thousands of galaxies.
- e.g. The Andromeda galaxy, our nearest big neighbour, has about a trillion stars.
- e.g. The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are small galaxies close to the Milky Way. They can be seen by eye from the far south of the Earth.
### Galaxies come in different shapes · *defined*
**Definition:** Astronomers sort galaxies by shape: spiral, barred spiral, elliptical and irregular. Edwin Hubble made this sorting in 1926.

A spiral galaxy is a flat, spinning disc with a bright bulge in the middle and arms that curl out. A barred spiral has a straight bar of stars across its middle. An elliptical galaxy is a smooth oval or ball of stars with no arms. An irregular galaxy has no clear shape. Spirals still have lots of gas, so new stars keep being made in their arms. The youngest, hottest stars are blue, so spiral arms look blue. Elliptical galaxies have little gas left, so few new stars are made, and their older stars make them look yellow or red. A disc looks different from different sides. Seen from the top, it is round. Seen from the side, it is a thin line.
- e.g. The Pinwheel galaxy is a spiral we see from the top, so its arms show clearly.
- e.g. The Sombrero galaxy is a spiral we see almost from the side. It looks like a hat with a dark band of dust across it.
- e.g. M87 is a giant elliptical galaxy. The first photo of a black hole, in 2019, was of the black hole at its centre.
### Why spiral galaxies have arms · *debated*
A spiral galaxy spins, but not like a wheel. Stars near the middle go round in less time than stars further out. So you might expect the arms to wind up tighter and tighter, and vanish. They don't. Astronomers have more than one explanation, and different galaxies may have different reasons.
- e.g. The Sun takes about 230 million years to go once round the Milky Way.
- e.g. The Whirlpool galaxy has very clear arms, and a smaller galaxy is passing close to it.
- **The traffic-jam idea (Chia-Chiao Lin and Frank Shu, 1964):** The arms are like a slow traffic jam that goes round the galaxy. Stars and gas move into an arm, crowd together for a while, and move out again. The crowding squeezes gas clouds, so new stars are made there.
- **The come-and-go idea:** In many galaxies, arms form, stretch, fade and form again, as the galaxy spins. Computer models of spinning galaxies show arms like this.
- **The passing-galaxy idea:** A nearby galaxy can pull on a spiral as it passes, and stir up strong arms. The Whirlpool galaxy may have got its arms this way.
### The universe is expanding · *defined*
**Definition:** The space between galaxies is growing all the time. So distant galaxies are moving away from each other, and the further apart two galaxies are, the faster they move apart.

In 1929, Edwin Hubble showed that far galaxies are moving away from us, and the further away they are, the faster they go. Georges Lemaître had worked this out two years earlier. It does not mean we are at the centre. The galaxies are not flying through space away from one spot. The space between them is stretching, everywhere at once. So from any galaxy, all the others seem to move away. This happens only across the huge gaps between groups of galaxies. Galaxies themselves do not grow, because gravity holds their stars together. The Earth, your house and your body do not stretch either. Our nearest big neighbour, Andromeda, is even moving towards us, because gravity pulls the two galaxies together.
- e.g. On a balloon, every bindi moves away from every other bindi as the balloon grows, and the far ones move the most.
- e.g. A galaxy twice as far away from us moves away about twice as fast.
- e.g. Andromeda is coming towards us at about 110 kilometres every second.
### The Big Bang: the universe had a beginning · *defined*
**Definition:** About 13.8 billion years ago, everything in the universe we can see was packed close together and very hot. It has been stretching and cooling ever since. This is called the Big Bang.

If galaxies are moving apart now, they were closer together in the past. Run the film backwards, and everything was once packed together. Packed things get hot, so the early universe was extremely hot. As it stretched, it cooled. Atoms formed, then stars and galaxies, and much later the Sun, the Earth and us. The Big Bang was not a bomb going off at one spot. It happened everywhere at once, including the place where you are now. Three clues tell us it really happened. Galaxies are moving apart. A faint glow, left over from the hot early universe, comes from every direction in the sky. And the universe is mostly hydrogen and helium, in just the amounts that a hot beginning would make.
- e.g. Fred Hoyle, an astronomer who did not believe in a beginning, made up the name 'Big Bang' on BBC radio in 1949.
- e.g. The leftover glow was found in 1965 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson. At first they thought the hiss came from pigeon droppings inside their antenna. They cleaned the antenna, and the hiss stayed.
### Looking far is looking back in time · *defined*
**Definition:** Light takes time to travel. So we see every faraway thing as it was when its light set off, not as it is now.

Light is the fastest thing there is. It travels about 300,000 kilometres every second. Even so, space is so big that light from far away takes a very long time to reach us. Light from the Moon takes just over 1 second. Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes. Light from the Andromeda galaxy takes about 2.5 million years. So when you see Andromeda, you see it as it was before there were any people like us on Earth. The further away a galaxy is, the older the picture we see. This lets astronomers look back at the young universe. The Webb space telescope has seen galaxies whose light set off more than 13 billion years ago, when the universe was very young.
- e.g. India's AstroSat space telescope picked up ultraviolet light from a galaxy called AUDFs01, about 9.3 billion light-years away. The team was led by Kanak Saha in Pune, and they reported it in 2020.
- e.g. Light from the middle of the Milky Way set off about 26,000 years ago.
### How astronomers measure how far a galaxy is · *defined*
**Definition:** A star that really shines brightly looks faint when it is far away. If you know how bright a star really is, how faint it looks tells you how far away it is.

A torch close to you looks bright. The same torch at the end of a long road looks dim. If you know it is the same torch, you can tell how far away it is from how dim it looks. Henrietta Leavitt found a kind of star that works like this. Cepheid stars get brighter and dimmer in a steady rhythm. In 1912 she showed that the slower a Cepheid blinks, the brighter it really is. So astronomers time a Cepheid's blinks, work out how bright it really is, and compare that with how bright it looks.
- e.g. Edwin Hubble timed a Cepheid in Andromeda in 1923. It blinked every 31 days but looked very faint, so Andromeda had to be far outside the Milky Way.
- e.g. For galaxies too far to see a single Cepheid, astronomers use exploding stars called supernovae, which are much brighter.
### What came before the Big Bang? · *idea*
Nobody knows. Science can follow the universe back to when it was extremely hot and packed together. Before that, our best theories stop working, so we only have ideas. Some scientists think time itself began with the Big Bang, so there was no 'before'. Others think there was something earlier, perhaps another universe that came before ours. None of these ideas has been tested yet.
- e.g. Stephen Hawking said asking what came before the Big Bang may be like asking what is north of the North Pole.
- e.g. Roger Penrose thinks our universe may be one in a long chain of universes, one after another.

## Mostly agreed
- The universe is expanding. Distant galaxies are moving apart, and the further away they are, the faster they move away. *How we know:* Astronomers measure the redshift of galaxies, how much their light is stretched. Since Hubble's work in 1929, thousands of galaxies have been measured, and the further away they are, the more their light is stretched.
- About 13.8 billion years ago, the universe was extremely hot and packed together. This is the Big Bang. *How we know:* The expansion, the faint leftover glow found in 1965, and the amounts of hydrogen and helium in the universe all point to the same hot beginning. The Planck spacecraft measured the glow in detail and gives the age as 13.8 billion years.
- The Milky Way is one galaxy among hundreds of billions. *How we know:* In 1923, Hubble measured the distance to Andromeda using a Cepheid star, and showed it is far outside the Milky Way. Telescopes such as Hubble and Webb have since photographed tiny patches of sky holding thousands of galaxies.
- We see far galaxies as they were in the past. *How we know:* Light travels at a fixed speed, about 300,000 kilometres a second, so it takes time to cross space. The most distant galaxies look smaller, younger and more broken up than nearby ones, which is what we would expect if we see them when the universe was young.

## How we found out
**Georges Lemaître, a Belgian physicist and priest, Edwin Hubble, an American astronomer, and Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, American radio astronomers. Albert Einstein and Fred Hoyle held the older idea., 1917 (Einstein's still universe), 1927 and 1929 (the growing universe), 1948 (the steady state idea and the predicted glow), 1965 (the glow is found).** In 1917, Albert Einstein used his new theory of gravity to describe the whole universe. Like almost everyone then, he thought the universe had always been the same size. His equations said it should grow or shrink, so he added an extra part to hold it still. In 1927, Georges Lemaître, a Belgian physicist and priest, showed that the equations allowed a growing universe, and that measured galaxy speeds suggested it really was growing. In 1929, Edwin Hubble showed that far galaxies move away faster than near ones. Lemaître said that a growing universe must once have been tiny and hot. Many astronomers disliked the idea of a beginning. In 1948, Fred Hoyle, Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold said new matter keeps appearing as galaxies move apart, so the universe never really changes. The same year, Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman worked out that a hot beginning would leave a faint glow everywhere. In 1965, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found a hiss in their radio antenna that came from every direction. They even cleaned pigeon droppings out of the antenna, but the hiss stayed. It was the leftover glow, and the never-changing universe could not explain it.  
*What people believed before:* Most scientists, Einstein among them at first, thought the universe had always existed and stayed the same. The steady state idea kept this even after galaxies were found to move apart. The leftover glow, found in 1965, showed the universe really had a hot beginning.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If the universe is getting bigger, it must be growing into some empty space outside it.** (Many people, when they first hear that the universe is expanding; *rejected*)  
  For: Everything we see growing, like a balloon or a rising idli, grows into the room around it.  
  Against: The expanding universe means that the distances between galaxies grow. It does not need anything outside. On the balloon model, only the rubber skin stands for space, and the bindis can never leave it. Nobody has found any sign of an outside, and the main theory of gravity describes stretching without one.
- **The universe has always existed and looks the same at all times. As galaxies move apart, new matter quietly appears to fill the gaps. This is called the steady state theory.** (Fred Hoyle, Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold, from 1948; *rejected*)  
  For: It explained why galaxies move apart without needing a beginning. Many people found a universe with no beginning easier to accept.  
  Against: In 1965 Penzias and Wilson found the faint glow left from a hot early universe. A steady state universe would have no such glow. Far galaxies, seen as they were long ago, also look different from nearby ones.
- **The Big Bang was an explosion at one spot in space, and the galaxies are flying away from that spot.** (Many people, because of the name 'Big Bang'; *rejected*)  
  For: The name sounds like an explosion, and galaxies are moving apart, like pieces after a blast.  
  Against: If galaxies were flying from one spot, there would be a centre, and galaxies there would see things differently from galaxies at the edge. Astronomers see the same pattern in every direction. The stretching happens everywhere at once.
- **The two main ways of measuring how fast the universe expands give different answers, so something may be missing from our picture of the universe.** (Many astronomers, including Adam Riess and his team; *debated*)  
  For: Measuring nearby galaxies with Cepheids and exploding stars gives a faster speed than working it out from the leftover glow. Both sets of measurements have been checked many times.  
  Against: Some astronomers think a small mistake in one of the methods will explain the difference, and newer measurements sometimes come out closer together.

## Roots
We have stretched a balloon, met the shapes of galaxies and learned that far light is old light. Now we ask what this says about where we are, and where everything came from.

**Deep truth.** On your balloon, every bindi moved away from every other, and far ones moved furthest. No bindi was the centre. Galaxies move apart the same way, because the space between them stretches. So the Big Bang happened everywhere at once, including right where you sit.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture the Big Bang as a bomb going off at one spot, with galaxies flying out like sparks. That picture puts a centre somewhere, and puts us off to one side. But the stretching happens everywhere at once, so there is no centre, and our place was part of the beginning like every other.  
*Evidence:* On the balloon, the bindi three times as far from home moved about three times as much, and the same was true from a second bindi. Hubble and Lemaître found this pattern in real galaxies: twice as far away means moving away about twice as fast, in every direction.

- *who am i:* From every galaxy, the others seem to move away, just as from every bindi. So no place is the centre of the universe, and your place is as central as any other.
- *what is life:* Living things are made of carbon, oxygen and other atoms that only stars can make. The first stars had to shine and explode before there were any. So the universe was already hundreds of millions of years old before life could begin anywhere.
- *what is this world:* The world is not standing still. The space between the galaxies grows every moment, and the light reaching us carries pictures of the past. The sky you see tonight shows many different times at once.

On the balloon there was no special bindi. Every one saw the same thing: the others moving away. Science found that every place in the universe is like this. No galaxy is the middle and none is the edge, and every place was part of the same hot beginning. People are one kind of life on one planet, in one galaxy among hundreds of billions, and that galaxy is in the middle exactly as much as every other.

- **Nicholas of Cusa, On Learned Ignorance (De docta ignorantia), Book 2, chapter 12, 1440:** The world has its centre everywhere and its edge nowhere. (Nicholas of Cusa, a German thinker, wrote this nearly 500 years before Hubble. On the balloon, every bindi could be called the centre, because from each one the others moved away.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta (the hymn of creation):** Then there was neither being nor non-being. Who really knows where this world came from? The one who watches over it from the highest heaven perhaps knows, or perhaps does not know. (Science has traced the universe back 13.8 billion years, to a hot, packed beginning. Before that, it does not know either. The hymn asks the same question, and is just as honest about not knowing.)
- Science (settled): The universe is expanding, and about 13.8 billion years ago it was extremely hot and packed together.
- Science (settled): No place in the universe is the centre. Astronomers see the same pattern of galaxies moving apart in every direction.
- Science (open): What came before the Big Bang, or whether there was any 'before', nobody knows.
- Science (idea): Some physicists think the total energy of the universe may be exactly zero (Edward Tryon, 1973).
- Other view, Many cosmologists: The Big Bang theory describes how the universe changed from a hot, packed state. It does not explain why there is a universe at all, and many scientists think that question lies outside science. Some, like Roger Penrose, think our Big Bang was not the start, and an earlier universe came before it.

Think of a light that set off from Andromeda before there were any people. It is still travelling, and some of it is arriving now. Open your eyes slowly.

- Ask: When you see Andromeda, are you seeing Andromeda, or a message from its past?
- Ask: Can something begin from nothing? What would 'nothing' be?

## Story: The Letter in Ammamma's Trunk
*Kavya, Arjun, Periamma Meenakshi and their family are made up. Bhogi is the first day of Pongal, when families in Tamil Nadu clear out old things. The Vainu Bappu Observatory at Kavalur is real. The light times in the story are real: just over 1 second for the Moon, about 8 minutes for the Sun, about 2.5 million years for the Andromeda galaxy, and more than 13 billion years for the furthest galaxies seen. For the parent: Kavya learns that a letter, like light from a star, shows how things were when it was sent, not how they are now. Then she uses a balloon to show her father that galaxies moving away from us does not put us at the centre.*

On the morning of Bhogi, the first day of Pongal, Kavya's family in Madurai cleared out the old things in the house. Kavya and her little brother Arjun were given Ammamma's tin trunk to sort. It was full of old saris, school certificates and bundles of letters tied with string.

One envelope had a picture of a telescope drawn on the back. Kavya opened it. 'Dear Amma,' it said. 'Today I used the big telescope at Kavalur for the first time. I saw a galaxy so far away that its light left it before there were any people on Earth. I am nineteen, and I have never been so happy. Your Meenakshi.'

'This is from Periamma!' said Kavya. Periamma Meenakshi was her mother's elder sister, and she was an astronomer. She had come for Pongal, and she was in the kitchen, stirring the payasam.

Kavya ran in with the letter. Periamma laughed when she saw it. 'I wrote that twenty years ago,' she said. 'Look at my handwriting. I was so young.'

'But in the letter you're nineteen,' said Arjun, who was six. 'You're not nineteen now.'

'That's right,' said Periamma. 'A letter tells you how things were when it was written. By the time you read it, things have changed.' She stopped stirring and looked at Kavya. 'Light from the sky is like that too.'

That evening, the family went up to the terrace. The city lights were bright, so only a few stars showed. The Moon was rising over the temple towers.

'Light is the fastest thing there is,' said Periamma. 'But it still takes time to travel. The moonlight on your face left the Moon just over one second ago. Sunlight takes about eight minutes to get here. So you always see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago.'

'Like a letter that took eight minutes to arrive,' said Kavya.

'Exactly. And the further away something is, the older its letter. The Andromeda galaxy is so far away that its light takes two and a half million years to reach us. The galaxy I saw at Kavalur, in my letter, was further still. The furthest galaxies that big telescopes see today sent their light more than thirteen billion years ago, when the universe was very young.'

Kavya thought about this. 'So if you look far enough, you could see the beginning?'

'Almost,' said Periamma. 'The oldest light of all is a very faint glow. It comes from every direction in the sky, left over from when the whole universe was packed together and hot. Our eyes can't see it, but radio telescopes can.'

'Where did the beginning happen?' asked Kavya. 'Over there?' She pointed at the sky beyond the temple towers.

'Everywhere,' said Periamma. 'Including right here on this terrace.' Kavya frowned. That did not make sense at all.

Appa came up with a tray of coffee. 'I read that all the galaxies are rushing away from us,' he said. 'So we must be right in the middle of everything. That makes us quite special.'

Periamma smiled at Kavya. 'Is your Appa right? Are we in the middle?' Kavya did not know. She lay awake that night, thinking about it.

The next day was Pongal. While the rice boiled over in the new pot, and everyone shouted 'Pongalo Pongal!', Arjun came back from the market with a red balloon. Kavya had an idea. She borrowed Amma's packet of bindis and stuck six of them on the balloon while it was still small. She called one of them 'Milky Way'.

'Appa, blow it up, please,' she said. Appa blew. The balloon grew, and the bindis spread apart. 'Look at Milky Way,' said Kavya. 'All the other bindis moved away from it. The far ones moved most.'

'So Milky Way is the centre,' said Appa.

'Now look from this one,' said Kavya, and put her finger on a different bindi. 'All the others moved away from this one too. And from this one. Every bindi sees the others go away. None of them is the centre.'

Appa turned the balloon round and round in his hands. 'So if I lived on any of these bindis, I would think I was in the middle,' he said. 'And so would everyone else.'

'And the bindis didn't get bigger,' said Kavya. 'Only the rubber between them stretched. Periamma says galaxies don't stretch either. It's the space between them.'

Then she understood what Periamma had said on the terrace. If she let the air out, all the bindis would come back together, every one of them. None of them had started somewhere else. 'When the universe was small, every place was squeezed together,' she said slowly. 'So the beginning happened everywhere. Here too.'

Periamma had been listening from the doorway. 'That is exactly what astronomers found,' she said. 'You have just explained it better than my letter did.'

**For the parent:** Kavya realises that light from far away is like an old letter: it shows how things were when it was sent. Then, with the balloon, she sees that galaxies moving away from us does not put us at the centre, and that when the universe was small, every place was squeezed together, so the beginning happened everywhere, here too.

- Ask: Arjun said Periamma was not nineteen any more. Why was the letter still about a nineteen-year-old?
- Ask: How is sunlight like a letter that takes eight minutes to arrive?
- Ask: Appa thought we must be in the middle of everything. How did Kavya show him what the balloon says?
- Ask: Why didn't the bindis get bigger when the balloon grew?
- Ask: What did Kavya mean when she said the beginning happened 'here too'?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bindi Balloon: do far bindis move away faster than near ones? (35 min)
- **question:** When a balloon grows, do the bindis on it all move apart by the same amount? Is there any bindi that the others do not move away from?
- **predict:** Before you blow the balloon bigger, guess. Will a near bindi and a far bindi move away from your home bindi by the same amount, or different amounts? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use one balloon and do not move the bindis. Measure every gap the same way, from the middle of one bindi to the middle of the other, with the thread lying flat on the rubber. Measure all the gaps when the balloon is small, then all of them again when it is big.
- **safety:** A grown-up blows up the balloon. Stop before the rubber gets tight, so it does not burst near anyone's face. If it bursts, pick up every piece at once. Balloon pieces can choke a child, so they never go in the mouth.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then write one sentence: the bindi that was furthest away moved ____ cm, and the nearest one moved ____ cm.
- **results table:** Pair of bindis | Gap on the small balloon (cm) | Gap on the big balloon (cm) | How much further apart (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the balloon and the bindis. 2. Ask the guess question below. 3. Wait for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Each bindi will be a galaxy, and the balloon's rubber will be space. When I blow the balloon bigger, will a near bindi and a far bindi move away by the same amount? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Each bindi is a galaxy. The rubber is space. When the balloon grows, will near and far bindis move the same? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Which bindi do you think will move furthest from home? Why? (good: They will all move the same, because the balloon grows the same everywhere.; The far one, because there is more rubber between them to stretch.)
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Do not give the answer. The measuring will show it.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Blow the balloon up to about the size of a big mango, around 12 cm across. 2. Twist the neck and clip it with the peg. 3. Your child sticks the home bindi in the middle of the fat part. 4. They stick three more in a rough line from it, about 3 cm, 6 cm and 9 cm away. 5. They stick the last two anywhere else.  
  *Parent says:* This first bindi is our home, the Milky Way. Now stick three in a line going away from it, a little further each time. Put the last two anywhere.  
  *Child mode:* Stick the home bindi in the middle. Stick three more in a line, each one further away. Put two more anywhere.
  - Note: Keep the bindis on the fat middle of the balloon, away from the neck, because the rubber near the neck stretches less. Give each bindi a letter on the results table: H for home, then A, B and C along the line, and D and E for the other two.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold one end of the thread on the middle of H. 2. Lay the thread flat along the rubber to the middle of A. 3. Pinch the thread there, lift it off, and hold it against the ruler. 4. Write the gap in the table. 5. Do the same for H to B, H to C, C to A and C to B.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the thread on the home bindi. Lay it along the rubber to the next one, and pinch it there. Now let's read it on the ruler.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the thread on H. Lay it to A. Pinch it. Read it on the ruler. Write it down.
  - Note: This needs two pairs of hands: one person holds the thread end on the bindi, the other lays it and pinches. Round each gap to the nearest half centimetre. The gaps from C let you look at the balloon from a second bindi later.
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Unclip the neck, holding it tight. 2. Blow the balloon bigger, to about twice as wide, around 24 cm across. 3. Clip it shut again.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the bindis while I blow. What do you see them doing?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the bindis while the balloon grows. What are they doing?
  - Ask: What did you see while the balloon grew? (good: All the bindis spread apart.; The far ones seemed to move faster.)
  - Note: Stop before the rubber feels tight. A balloon that is nearly full is more likely to burst.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure the same five gaps again, in the same way. 2. Write them in the table. 3. For each pair, take the small gap from the big gap and write how much further apart they are.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure the same gaps again. Then we can work out how much further apart each pair has gone.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the same gaps again. Write them down. How much further apart is each pair?
  - Check: From home, which bindi moved furthest away? → **The furthest one, C**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the gaps measured from C. 2. Ask: did the other bindis move away from C as well? 3. Measure one bindi across its middle with the ruler, and compare it with how big it was before.  
  *Parent says:* Now pretend C is home. Did the other bindis move away from C too? And did the bindis themselves get any bigger?  
  *Child mode:* Pretend C is home. Did the others move away from C too? Did the bindis get bigger?
  - Ask: Was there any bindi that the others did not move away from? (good: No. From every bindi, the others moved away.; None of them is the middle.)
  - Note: The bindis stay the same size because they are stuck on, and only the rubber between them stretches. Galaxies are like this: gravity holds them together, and only the space between them grows. Only the balloon's skin stands for space. The air inside and the room outside are not part of the model.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why does it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: when space stretches, far things move apart faster than near things, and there is no centre. What I saw: the bindi 9 cm away moved about three times as far as the one 3 cm away, and the bindis moved away from C as well as from H. Why: there is more rubber between far bindis, so more of it stretches. Now explain: galaxies are moving away from us in the same way. Hubble found that a galaxy twice as far away moves away about twice as fast. Every galaxy would see the same thing, so we are not at the centre.

## Worksheets
### Galaxies and the Growing Universe (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a galaxy?  
   Options: A huge group of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity / One very bright star / A cloud in the Earth's air / Another name for the Solar System  
   **Answer:** A huge group of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity. *Why:* A galaxy holds billions of stars. Picking 'a cloud in the Earth's air' is what a galaxy looks like through a small telescope, which is why people once called them nebulae, the Latin word for clouds.
2. (truefalse) The Milky Way is the only galaxy there is.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In 1923 Edwin Hubble showed that Andromeda is a separate galaxy far outside ours. There are hundreds of billions of galaxies in the part of the universe we can see.
3. (match) Match each galaxy shape to what it looks like.  
   **Answer:** Spiral → a flat disc with arms that curl out from a bright middle; Barred spiral → a disc with a straight bar of stars across the middle, and arms from its ends; Elliptical → a smooth oval or ball of stars, with no arms; Irregular → no clear shape at all. *Why:* These are the four main shapes that Edwin Hubble sorted galaxies into in 1926. Our own Milky Way is a barred spiral.
4. (mcq) In the Bindi Balloon test, Divya, a girl in Class 3, stuck three bindis 3 cm, 6 cm and 9 cm from her home bindi. Then she blew the balloon bigger. Which bindi moved furthest from home?  
   Options: The one that started 3 cm away / The one that started 6 cm away / The one that started 9 cm away / They all moved the same amount  
   **Answer:** The one that started 9 cm away. *Why:* The bindi that started furthest away moved furthest, because there was the most rubber between it and home to stretch. Picking 'all the same' is the most common first guess, and the measuring shows why it is wrong.

## Applications
- Astronomers use Henrietta Leavitt's blinking stars as measuring sticks, to find how far away galaxies are.
- India's AstroSat space telescope and the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope near Pune study far galaxies and the early universe.
- The Planck spacecraft mapped the faint leftover glow, and from it scientists worked out that the universe is 13.8 billion years old.
- Computer programs that sort galaxies by shape were trained using the sorting done by Galaxy Zoo volunteers.
- Because far light is old light, astronomers can study how galaxies grew, by looking at galaxies at different distances.
- In 2017, a team from Pune found a huge group of galaxies about 4 billion light-years away and named it the Saraswati supercluster.

## Spotted around us
- The Milky Way, as a faint glowing band across the sky, on a clear night far from city lights, for example in a village or during a long power cut.
- The Andromeda galaxy, a faint grey smudge high in the north-east on November evenings, from a dark place only.
- A swirl of milk in a tumbler of filter coffee, which looks like a spiral galaxy. The coffee's spiral forms in a different way.
- The 30 big dishes of the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope near Narayangaon in Pune district, which pick up radio waves from far galaxies.
- Galaxy photos in the newspaper when a telescope such as AstroSat or Webb makes a new find.

## Open questions
- **What came before the Big Bang, if anything?** Our best theories stop working at the very start. Some scientists think time began then, and others think an earlier universe came first. None of the ideas has been tested.
- **Why is the stretching of the universe speeding up?** In 1998, astronomers found that the expansion is getting faster. They call the unknown cause 'dark energy', but nobody knows what it is.
- **How did the first galaxies grow so big so quickly?** The Webb telescope has found more bright galaxies in the young universe than many astronomers expected. They are working out whether their ideas about how galaxies grow need to change.
- **Does the universe go on for ever, or does it have an edge?** We can only see as far as light has had time to travel. Beyond that, nobody can look, so nobody knows how big the whole universe is.
- **Will Andromeda and the Milky Way crash together?** Andromeda is coming towards us. A study in 2025 found it is uncertain whether the two will merge in the next 10 billion years, because the pulls of other nearby galaxies are hard to measure.

## Warm-up
- What is a galaxy? → A huge group of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity.
- What is the name of our galaxy? → The Milky Way.
- Name two shapes of galaxy. → Any two of spiral, barred spiral, elliptical and irregular.
- About how old is the universe? → About 13.8 billion years.
- Far galaxies move away from us. Do far ones move faster or slower than near ones? → Faster.
- Is there a centre of the universe? → No. From every galaxy, the others seem to move away.
- When we look at Andromeda, do we see it as it is now? → No, as it was about 2.5 million years ago.
- Who found how to measure star distances with blinking stars? → Henrietta Leavitt.
