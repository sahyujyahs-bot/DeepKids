# Atoms: how small they are and what is inside them
`sci.the-very-small.atoms.L3`

**Summary.** Cut a strip of paper in half again and again, and your scissors stop after about 8 cuts. To get a sliver only one atom wide, you would need about 31. Everything you can touch is made of atoms, and an atom is almost all empty space. At its centre is a tiny, heavy nucleus, made of protons and neutrons. Electrons are found around it, not on tracks but in a cloud of places where they are likely to be.

**Intro.** *Take a piece of paper. Tear it in half. Tear one half in half again. Keep going. Can you go on for ever? Or will you reach a piece so small it cannot be cut?* More than 2,000 years ago, a thinker in India asked this same question. His name was Kanada. A story says he lived on the grains of rice left behind in fields after the harvest. His name may even mean 'grain-eater'. Kanada taught that everything is made of tiny particles, far too small to see. He called them paramanu. Far away in Greece, a thinker called Democritus had a similar idea. He called them atoms. Neither of them had a microscope. They worked it out by thinking. In this unit you test their idea yourself, with a strip of paper and a pair of scissors.

## Terms
- **Matter**: Anything that has weight and takes up space. Solids, liquids and gases are all matter. *e.g.* A stone, water and the air in a balloon are all matter. A shadow is not.
- **Particle**: A very small piece of matter, too small to see. Atoms and molecules are particles. So are the protons, neutrons and electrons inside atoms. *e.g.* Sugar stirred into water breaks up into particles too small to see. You can still taste them.
- **Atom**: The tiny particle that everything you can touch is built from. An atom has a nucleus at its centre, with electrons around it. About 5 million atoms in a row would be just 1 millimetre long. *e.g.* Your hand, the floor and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Element**: A substance whose atoms are all of one kind. Atoms are the same kind when they have the same number of protons. About 90 elements are found in nature. *e.g.* Gold, iron, oxygen and carbon are elements. The graphite in a pencil is made only of carbon atoms.
- **Molecule**: Two or more atoms joined together into one particle. *e.g.* A water molecule is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.
- **Nucleus**: The tiny, heavy centre of an atom. Nearly all of an atom's weight is in it. *e.g.* If an atom were as big as a cricket ground, its nucleus would be about the size of a peppercorn on the middle stump.
- **Proton**: One of the two kinds of particle in the nucleus. A proton has a positive electric charge, shown as plus. The number of protons decides which element an atom is. *e.g.* Every carbon atom has 6 protons. Every gold atom has 79.
- **Neutron**: The other kind of particle in the nucleus. A neutron has no electric charge. It weighs about the same as a proton. *e.g.* A carbon atom usually has 6 neutrons next to its 6 protons.
- **Electron**: A very light particle with a negative electric charge, shown as minus. Electrons are found around the nucleus. When atoms meet, it is their electrons that push on each other or join the atoms together. *e.g.* When you rub a balloon on dry hair, some electrons move from your hair onto the balloon.
- **Quark**: An even smaller particle. Each proton and each neutron is made mainly of three quarks. *e.g.* A proton is made of two 'up' quarks and one 'down' quark.
- **Electric charge**: A property of some particles that makes them attract or repel each other. Attract means pull together. Repel means push apart. A plus and a minus attract. Two pluses, or two minuses, repel. *e.g.* Two balloons rubbed on dry hair both get extra electrons, so they repel each other.
- **Empty space**: Space with no matter in it. Most of an atom is empty space. Its nucleus is tiny, and its electrons are tiny too, spread out as a cloud. *e.g.* Nearly all of your body is empty space, because nearly all of every atom is.
- **Electron cloud**: A picture that shows where an electron is likely to be found. It is thick where the electron is often found and thin where it is rarely found. *e.g.* Drop 50 pencil dots at a target with your eyes closed. They make a cloud that is thick in the middle.
- **Quantum physics**: The science of the very tiniest things, like atoms, electrons and light. It has rules that are very different from the rules for balls and bicycles. *e.g.* Quantum physics explains why salt sprinkled on a gas flame turns it bright yellow.
- **Measurement**: Finding out something, such as where a thing is, by letting it bump into something else, like light or a detector. *e.g.* To find an electron, scientists let it hit a screen. The screen makes a tiny flash where it hit.
- **Observer (in physics)**: Anything that measures, such as a detector, a screen or even a single particle of light. It does not have to be a person. *e.g.* A camera chip or a speck of dust can 'observe' an electron by being hit by it.
- **Probability**: How likely something is to happen. It is the maths of chance. *e.g.* With two dice, a total of 7 is more likely than a total of 12.
- **Energy level**: One of the fixed amounts of energy an electron in an atom can have. Electrons can only be on these levels, like standing on the steps of a staircase, never in between. *e.g.* When an electron drops down one step, the atom gives out light of one exact colour.
- **Diffusion**: Particles spreading out by themselves, from where there are many to where there are few. *e.g.* The edges of a drop of food colour in still water slowly turn fuzzy, with nobody stirring it.
- **Scanning tunnelling microscope**: A special machine that 'feels' a surface with a needle tip only one atom wide. It can make pictures of single atoms. *e.g.* In 1989, scientists used one to spell the letters IBM with 35 single atoms.
- **Paramanu**: The word used by Kanada, a thinker in ancient India, for the smallest particles that everything is made of. *e.g.* Kanada taught that paramanu join in pairs and threes to build bigger things.

## Explanations
### Everything is made of atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** Everything you can touch is made of tiny particles called atoms. About 90 kinds of atom are found in nature, and they join in different ways to make everything.

Look around you. A steel tumbler, a mango, your skin and the air look nothing alike. But zoom in far enough and they are all made of atoms. Different things are made of different kinds of atom, joined in different patterns. It is a bit like the alphabet. Just 26 letters make every word in English. About 90 kinds of atom make a stone, a tree and you.
- e.g. Water is made of hydrogen and oxygen atoms, joined in little groups called molecules.
- e.g. The grey tip of a pencil is mostly graphite, which is made only of carbon atoms. A diamond is made only of carbon atoms too. The atoms are just arranged differently.
- e.g. Your body is made mostly of four kinds of atom: oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen.
### How small is an atom? · *defined*
**Definition:** The smallest atom, hydrogen, is about one ten-millionth of a millimetre across. Most atoms are two or three times wider. That is still far too small to see with any ordinary microscope.

Small numbers are hard to imagine, so try halving. Take a strip of paper 24 centimetres long and cut it in half. Then cut one half in half. Keep going. With home scissors, most people can make about 8 cuts. The piece is then a sliver about 1 millimetre wide. To make it just one atom wide, you would need about 31 cuts. Even then, the sliver would still be 2 centimetres long and hundreds of thousands of atoms thick. To reach the width of an atom's centre, you would need about 45 cuts. The chart shows how far each thing is down this halving ladder.
- e.g. About 5 million atoms side by side would fit across the 1 millimetre gap between two lines on a ruler.
- e.g. A single hair is a few hundred thousand atoms thick.
- e.g. One grain of salt holds about a million million million atoms.
### How we know atoms are real · *defined*
**Definition:** We know atoms are real because of what they do. Moving molecules bump tiny specks in water and make them jiggle. Special microscopes can now make pictures of single atoms.

For more than 2,000 years, atoms were only an idea. Some famous scientists, as late as the year 1900, said atoms were just a handy way of thinking. Then came proof. In 1827, Robert Brown saw tiny specks from pollen jiggling in water, never still. In 1905, Albert Einstein showed that invisible water molecules bumping the specks would make exactly that jiggle. In 1908, Jean Perrin in France measured it carefully, and Einstein was right. In 1981, Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer built the scanning tunnelling microscope. Its needle tip feels a surface like a finger reading bumps. At last we could make pictures of single atoms.
- e.g. The edges of a drop of food colour in still water slowly turn fuzzy. Water molecules keep bumping the colour's molecules.
- e.g. In 1989, Don Eigler and Erhard Schweizer moved 35 single atoms to spell the letters IBM.
- e.g. In 2013, IBM scientists made a tiny cartoon film called 'A Boy and His Atom' by moving single molecules.
### Who first thought of atoms? · *debated*
Long before experiments, thinkers in India and in Greece imagined that everything is made of tiny particles. They had no microscopes. They worked it out by thinking and arguing. Who was first is not known for sure.
- e.g. Kanada called the tiny particles paramanu. Democritus called them atomos, which means 'uncuttable'.
- e.g. In 1808, John Dalton in England gave the first evidence from chemistry: elements always join in fixed amounts.
- **Kanada, founder of the Vaisheshika school of thought in ancient India:** Everything is made of paramanu: tiny, invisible particles that last for ever. They join in pairs and threes to build bigger things. Scholars do not know when Kanada lived. Guesses range from about 600 to 200 years before the Common Era.
- **Democritus, a thinker in ancient Greece, about 2,400 years ago, with his teacher Leucippus:** Everything is made of atoms moving in empty space. Atoms differ in shape and size, and that is why things differ.
- **Historians today:** Both traditions seem to have had the idea on their own. Claims that one was clearly first go beyond the evidence. Both were brilliant guesses, and both were partly wrong. Atoms can be cut: they have smaller parts inside.
### What an atom is made of · *defined*
**Definition:** An atom has a tiny centre, the nucleus, made of protons and neutrons. Electrons are found around it. Protons and neutrons are made of even smaller particles called quarks.

The number of protons decides what an atom is. One proton makes hydrogen. Six make carbon. Seventy-nine make gold. Protons are positive and electrons are negative, so they attract each other. That attraction keeps the electrons around the nucleus. Neutrons have no charge. As far as anyone knows, electrons and quarks are not made of anything smaller. But nobody can be sure.
- e.g. J.J. Thomson found the electron in 1897, in England.
- e.g. James Chadwick found the neutron in 1932.
- e.g. Quarks were first imagined in 1964 by Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig. Experiments found signs of them in 1968.
### An atom is almost all empty space · *defined*
**Definition:** Nearly all of an atom is empty space. The nucleus is tens of thousands of times smaller across than the whole atom, yet it holds nearly all the weight.

In 1909, in Manchester in England, Hans Geiger and a 20-year-old student, Ernest Marsden, fired alpha particles at very thin metal foil. Alpha particles are tiny, fast particles shot out by radium, the element found by Marie and Pierre Curie. Almost every alpha particle went straight through the metal. But about 1 in every 8,000 bounced back. In 1911, Ernest Rutherford explained why. Each atom has a tiny, heavy centre with a strong positive charge. The rest is almost empty. Most alpha particles miss the centre. The rare one that comes close is repelled hard and bounces back.
- e.g. If a room were one atom, its nucleus would be a speck in the middle, about as wide as a hair.
- e.g. If all the empty space were squeezed out of every person on Earth, all of us together would fit into about one sugar cube.
### Why things feel solid · *defined*
**Definition:** Things feel solid because the electrons of one thing push away the electrons of another. A quantum rule also stops electrons from crowding into the same place.

If atoms are nearly empty, why doesn't your hand sink into the table? Two reasons. First, all electrons are negative, and two negative charges repel. When your hand comes near the table, its electrons repel the table's electrons very strongly, long before any nuclei meet. Second, quantum physics has a rule, found by Wolfgang Pauli in 1925. Two electrons can never be in exactly the same state. So electrons cannot squeeze together, and atoms keep their size. What you feel as solid is not atoms packed with no gaps. It is electrons repelling electrons.
- e.g. Hold two magnets with the same ends facing. You feel them push apart before they touch. This is a magnetic push, not the push between electrons, but it feels alike.
- e.g. Two rubbed balloons on threads lean away from each other. Their extra electrons repel.
### Electrons are found in clouds, not on tracks · *defined*
**Definition:** An electron in an atom does not go round the nucleus on a track. Quantum physics can only say how likely it is to be found in each place. Scientists draw this as a cloud: thick where the electron is likely to be, thin where it is not.

Many books draw atoms like tiny solar systems, with electrons going round like planets. That picture came from Niels Bohr in 1913. It was a useful step, but it is not right. In 1926, Erwin Schrödinger found the maths that describes electrons. It does not give a track. It gives a cloud of chances. Each time scientists measure an electron, they find it at one spot. Do this many times, and the spots make a cloud, like your pencil dots around the target. Between measurements, the maths does not say the electron is at any one spot.
- e.g. Close your eyes and drop pencil dots at a target 50 times. The dots make a cloud, thickest near the target.
- e.g. The electron cloud of a hydrogen atom is shaped like a fuzzy ball. Other clouds are shaped like dumbbells or rings.
### The very small does not behave like the things we see · *defined*
**Definition:** Atoms, electrons and light follow the rules of quantum physics. Their energy comes in fixed steps, and they can spread out like waves, even though they are always found as single particles.

A ball can roll at any speed you like. An electron in an atom cannot have any energy it likes. It can only sit on certain energy levels, like the steps of a staircase. When it drops down one step, the atom gives out light of one exact colour. That is why salt sprinkled on a gas flame flashes yellow. Electrons can also spread like ripples on water. In 1989, Akira Tonomura's team in Japan sent electrons one at a time past a very thin wire that split their path in two. Each electron landed as a single dot. But thousands of dots built up a pattern of stripes, the pattern ripples make. Nobody knows how to picture this. Scientists only know the maths works, every time it is tested.
- e.g. Old orange street lights glow one exact colour of yellow-orange, from sodium atoms.
- e.g. Fireworks get their colours from different atoms: copper for blue-green, strontium for red.
- e.g. Your phone's chips and screen were designed using quantum physics.
### Looking means bumping: what 'observer' means · *defined*
**Definition:** To measure something tiny, you must let it bump into something, like light or a detector. The bump changes it. In physics, the 'observer' is whatever does the measuring. It does not need to be a person.

When you look at a cricket ball, light bounces off it into your eyes. The light is far too weak to move the ball. But an electron is so tiny that one particle of light can knock it away. So you cannot find an electron without changing it. Quantum physics goes further. In 1927, Werner Heisenberg showed a rule. Nobody can know exactly where an electron is and exactly how fast it is going, both at the same moment. This is not because our tools are poor. It is how nature works. Some people say quantum physics shows that a person's mind makes the world real. Physics does not show that. A detector, a screen or a speck of dust counts as an observer.
- e.g. In the Catch the Electron game, a torch beam or a paper ball counts as a measurement, not only a person.
- e.g. Air molecules bumping an electron count as measuring it. That is one reason big things never look fuzzy.
### What is the electron doing when nothing measures it? · *debated*
Everyone agrees on the maths, and on what experiments show. They disagree about what is really going on between measurements. This is one of the biggest open arguments in all of science.
- e.g. Every experiment gives the same results, whichever idea a scientist believes.
- e.g. Many scientists simply use the maths and leave the meaning to others, because the maths works either way.
- **Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg, in the 1920s, and many physicists today:** Between measurements, the electron has no exact place at all. Only the chances exist. A measurement gives one result.
- **David Bohm, an American physicist, in 1952:** The electron always has an exact place. A hidden wave guides it, and we cannot know the place without measuring.
- **Hugh Everett, an American physicist, in 1957:** Every possible result really happens, each in a different branch of the world. We only see one branch.
- **What all of them agree on:** A measurement is any bump with the wider world, by a detector, light or air. None of these ideas needs a human mind to make it work.
### Every electron is exactly the same · *defined*
**Definition:** Every electron in the universe is exactly the same as every other electron. The same is true of every proton, and of every neutron. There is no way at all to tell two of them apart.

Two mangoes from the same tree always differ a little, if you look closely enough. Two electrons never do. In 1924, Satyendra Nath Bose, a young teacher at Dacca University, now in Bangladesh, worked out how to count particles of light that are perfectly identical. A journal did not accept his paper. He sent it to Albert Einstein, who translated it and had it published. Einstein then took the idea further. Today a whole family of tiny particles is called bosons, after Bose.
- e.g. An electron in your fingertip and an electron in a faraway star are exactly alike.
- e.g. The particles of light are bosons, named after Satyendra Nath Bose.
### Your atoms are very old, and on loan · *defined*
**Definition:** Most of the hydrogen in you was made soon after the Big Bang. Nearly all your other atoms were made inside stars. Atoms last for billions of years and keep moving from one thing to another.

The carbon in your fingernails and the oxygen in your blood were made inside stars that lived and died before the Sun was born. In 1957, Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle showed how stars build atoms. Atoms do not wear out. Every breath you take holds more molecules than there are grains of sand on all the world's beaches. Some of those molecules were breathed before by other people and animals, long ago. A grown-up's body is made of about 7 followed by 27 zeros atoms. You are always swapping some of them with the world around you.
- e.g. The iron in your blood was made inside stars.
- e.g. The water you drink has fallen as rain many times before.
### Particles from space pass through you · *defined*
**Definition:** Fast particles from space, called cosmic rays, hit the air all the time. They make showers of other particles, and some of these pass right through your body.

These particles are far too small to feel. About one or two pass through your hand every second. Scientists catch their tracks with special detectors. In India, Bibha Chowdhuri and D.M. Bose of the Bose Institute in Calcutta, now Kolkata, carried photographic plates up mountains near Darjeeling. Between 1939 and 1942, the plates recorded tracks of a kind of particle called a meson. They published their work in the journal Nature. Later, a team in England led by Cecil Powell used better plates to find one kind of meson, the pion. Powell won the Nobel Prize in 1950. Today, a star is named Bibha after her.
- e.g. A cloud chamber, made with very cold dry ice and alcohol, shows thin trails of mist where these particles pass.
- e.g. Big detectors under mountains count particles that pass through the whole Earth.
### Is there a smallest thing? · *idea*
Nobody knows. As far as anyone can tell, electrons and quarks have no size at all. Many physicists now think particles are not really little balls. They may be ripples in invisible 'fields' that fill all of space, like ripples on a pond. Others wonder if space itself is made of tiny separate pieces. Some think there may be tiny vibrating strings deeper down. This is called string theory. No experiment has tested it yet.
- e.g. Space that looks empty is never quite empty. It fizzes with tiny flickers of energy, which scientists have measured.
- e.g. A ripple on a pond is not a thing you can pick up. It is the pond moving.
- **Many physicists today:** Particles are ripples in fields. The fields are what the world is really made of.
- **String theorists, such as Ashoke Sen, an Indian physicist:** Deep down, particles may be tiny vibrating strings. Experiments have not tested this idea yet.
- **Other physicists:** We may never be able to see deeper. Some questions about the very small may stay open.

## Mostly agreed
- Everything we can touch is made of atoms, and about 90 kinds are found in nature. *How we know:* Chemists have weighed elements joining since John Dalton in 1808. Brownian motion, measured by Jean Perrin in 1908, proved atoms are real. Today special microscopes make pictures of single atoms.
- Atoms are tiny: about one ten-millionth of a millimetre across. *How we know:* Perrin worked out their size from jiggling specks. X-rays bouncing off crystals and atom-picturing microscopes give the same answer.
- Every atom has a tiny, heavy nucleus, and the rest is almost empty space. *How we know:* In 1909, Geiger and Marsden saw about 1 in 8,000 alpha particles bounce back from thin metal foil. Rutherford explained it in 1911.
- Electrons do not move on neat tracks. Quantum physics gives the chances of finding them in each place. *How we know:* Its predictions have been tested millions of times, from the colours of flames to the electron stripe experiments. Every phone chip depends on them being right.
- Nearly all the atoms in your body, except most of the hydrogen, were made inside stars. *How we know:* Astronomers see these atoms being made and thrown out by stars today. Margaret Burbidge and her colleagues worked out how, in 1957.

## How we found out
**Robert Brown, a Scottish botanist, Albert Einstein, a German-born physicist, and Jean Perrin, a French physicist, 1827 to 1909.** In 1827, Robert Brown, a Scottish botanist, looked at pollen grains in water under a microscope. Tiny specks from inside the grains jiggled about and never stopped. At first he wondered if they were alive. Then he saw specks of crushed glass and rock jiggle in the same way. Nobody could explain it. By 1900, many chemists used atoms to explain how elements join. But some famous scientists, such as Ernst Mach and Wilhelm Ostwald, said atoms were not real. Nobody had seen one, so they called atoms only a handy way of thinking. In 1905, Albert Einstein worked out what would happen if water really were made of moving molecules. Each speck would be bumped a little more on one side than the other, again and again, so it would jiggle. His maths said how far a speck should wander in a given time. In 1908, Jean Perrin in Paris watched specks under a microscope and measured how far they moved. The numbers matched Einstein's maths. From them, Perrin even worked out how big molecules are. Ostwald changed his mind and accepted that atoms are real. Perrin won the Nobel Prize in 1926.  
*What people believed before:* Atoms are not real. They are only a handy way of thinking about chemistry.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Atoms are tiny hard balls that cannot be split.** (John Dalton in 1808, and the old Greek word 'atomos', which means uncuttable; *rejected*)  
  For: Elements join in fixed amounts, as if they were made of tiny, unchanging balls. This idea helped chemistry for a hundred years.  
  Against: In 1897 the electron was found inside atoms. In 1911 the nucleus was found. Atoms have parts, and nuclei can even be split.
- **Electrons go round the nucleus on tracks, like planets round the Sun.** (Niels Bohr's model of 1913, still drawn in many books and logos; *rejected*)  
  For: It explained the exact colours that hydrogen gives out, and it is easy to draw.  
  Against: It fails for every atom bigger than hydrogen. Since 1926, the maths of quantum physics has shown that electrons have no tracks, only clouds of chances.
- **Atoms are not real. They are just a handy way of thinking.** (Ernst Mach and Wilhelm Ostwald, two famous scientists, around 1900; *rejected*)  
  For: In 1900 nobody had seen an atom, and good science should rest on what can be measured.  
  Against: Einstein's work on Brownian motion in 1905 and Perrin's measurements in 1908 showed atoms are real. Ostwald changed his mind. Today we picture single atoms.
- **Quantum physics proves that our minds create the world.** (Some films, books and speakers, and some people who sell healing or success courses; *rejected*)  
  For: In quantum physics, measuring does change what you find. And the word 'observer' sounds like it means a person.  
  Against: In physics, an observer is anything that measures, such as a detector or a particle of light. The experiments work the same with no person watching. Physicists still argue about what measurement means, but none of the main ideas need a person watching.
- **Kanada discovered the atom long before anyone else.** (Some books and speakers in India; *debated*)  
  For: Kanada really did teach that everything is made of tiny invisible particles, paramanu, that join to make bigger things. It was a deep and careful idea.  
  Against: Nobody knows when Kanada lived. Democritus in Greece had a similar idea about 2,400 years ago. Neither tested it with experiments. Proof came only in the 1900s.
- **You never really touch anything.** (A popular saying in science books and videos; *debated*)  
  For: When your hand meets a table, the electrons push each other away before the atoms' centres come anywhere near each other. There is always a tiny gap.  
  Against: That electric push is what touching is. There is no other kind of touching to compare it with. So in the everyday meaning of the word, you do touch.
- **The dust you see dancing in a sunbeam is being jiggled by atoms.** (The Roman poet Lucretius, about 2,000 years ago, and many people since; *rejected*)  
  For: Atoms really do bump tiny specks and make them jiggle. This is called Brownian motion.  
  Against: The dust you can see is too big. It swirls mainly because of small air currents. True Brownian motion shows only for much tinier specks, under a microscope.

## Roots
We have been learning that everything you can touch is made of atoms, and what atoms are made of. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Cut a strip of paper in half again and again, and each piece is still paper, for a while. After about 31 halvings, a piece would be only one atom wide. Its atoms are carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, the same kinds as in sugar and in you. None of them is paper, so if you cut anything far enough, the pieces are no longer that thing.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a small piece of a thing is just a smaller copy of it, so a crumb of dosa is still dosa. That is true only down to a point. Below it, the pieces are atoms, and a carbon atom is not dosa or paper.  
*Evidence:* In the paper-cutting experiment, the last sliver was still paper, and the magnifier showed its plant fibres. The halving chart shows that a piece one atom wide is still about 23 halvings further down. Paper is mostly cellulose, which is built from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Sugar is built from the same three kinds of atom.

- *who am i:* You are made mostly of atoms of oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen, the same kinds as in air and food. None of those atoms is 'you' by itself.
- *what is life:* A living thing is built from the same kinds of atom as a stone or a star. Their atoms keep coming and going as they eat and breathe, yet they stay themselves.
- *what is this world:* In this world, about 90 kinds of atom make everything you can touch. All of them are built from just three kinds of particle.

About 90 kinds of atom build everything you can touch. Each atom is built from only three kinds of particle: protons, neutrons and electrons. The same few particles become stars, rivers, mangoes and you, joined in different ways. Consciousness runs through all of it, down to every particle. So a mind is not something that appears only when atoms build a brain.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to break open a banyan fruit, then one of its tiny seeds. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing at all.' Uddalaka says that from this finest part, which you cannot see, the great banyan tree grows. (When you cut the paper smaller and smaller, the pieces soon became too small to see. Yet everything big is built from far smaller particles, which nobody can see with their eyes.)
- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes meet at the hub of a wheel. It is the empty hole in the middle that makes the wheel useful. A pot is shaped from clay, but it is the empty space inside that holds things. (An atom is almost all empty space, and that space gives the atom its size. Because the electrons spread out around the nucleus, atoms take up room, so the things built from them do too.)
- Science (settled): Everything we can touch is made of about 90 kinds of atom, and every atom is built from protons, neutrons and electrons.
- Science (open): What an electron is doing between measurements is still argued about. Quantum physics does not prove that a person watching makes the world real. In physics, an 'observer' means anything that measures, such as a detector.
- Other view, The Charvaka school in ancient India, and most physicists and brain scientists today: Only matter is real. Particles follow the rules of physics, but they have no experience of their own. Awareness appears only when matter is built into a living brain. Many careful thinkers hold this view.
- Practice, One grain of salt (3 min): Put one grain of salt on your fingertip. Look at it closely. It holds about a million million million atoms. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out. Salt is made of sodium and chlorine atoms. Atoms of these same two kinds are in the sea and in your tears. Close your eyes and feel your fingertip. What is it in you that is noticing?
- Ask: Your scissors stopped after about 8 cuts. If you could go on, at which cut would the paper stop being paper?
- Ask: Is paper in its atoms, or in the way its atoms are joined?
- Ask: Your atoms were once in stars and will one day be somewhere else. Are they yours, or only borrowed?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If you had perfect scissors and perfect eyes, could you cut a piece of paper in half for ever? (hint: The paper is made of atoms. What happens when you reach one? And what is inside it?)
- *big:* Can we know something is real if we can never see it? (hint: Nobody saw an atom for 2,000 years. How did scientists become sure? What else do you believe in without seeing it?)
- *big:* Your atoms were made in stars and will go on after you. So what is 'you'? The atoms, or the pattern they make? (hint: Your atoms keep changing as you eat, breathe and grow. Are you still the same you as last year?)
- *deep:* Every electron is exactly the same as every other one. Is anything else in the world exactly the same as something else? (hint: Think of two coins, two leaves, two twins. Are they exactly the same?)
- *deep:* If big things are made of tiny things that follow rules of chance, why do big things seem so sure and solid? (hint: One dice roll is a surprise. What about the total of a million rolls?)

## Story: Counting in the Dark
*Real: Ernest Marsden was 20 and a student in Manchester in 1909. He and Hans Geiger counted tiny flashes made by alpha particles from radium, in a darkened room, through a microscope. They found that a few bounced back from metal foil, about 1 in 8,000 for platinum. Rutherford explained it in 1911 with the tiny nucleus. Rutherford's words about the shell and the tissue paper are from a lecture he gave much later. Marsden later moved to New Zealand. Imagined: Marsden's thoughts, the rain, the exact words of the conversations, and the last scene of Marsden in his old age.*

In the spring of 1909, in the city of Manchester in England, a young man sat alone in a dark room and waited for his eyes to change. His name was Ernest Marsden. He was twenty years old, still a student, and he was fairly sure he had been given the most boring job in the building.

The job came from Professor Ernest Rutherford, a big, loud man from New Zealand, whose laugh shook the windows. Rutherford studied the tiniest things there are. In his laboratory he kept a little tube of radium, the strange element that Marie and Pierre Curie had discovered. Radium shoots out tiny particles, far too small to see. Rutherford called them alpha particles. They fly thousands of times faster than a bullet.

You cannot see an alpha particle. But if one hits a screen coated with a special powder, the powder gives one tiny flash of green light, gone in a moment, like a firefly very far away. To see the flashes, you must first sit in the dark until your eyes are ready. Then you look through a microscope at the screen, and you count.

Marsden's job was to fire alpha particles at a sheet of gold, beaten so thin that light shone through it, soft and green. Hans Geiger, Rutherford's patient German assistant, had shown him how. Almost every alpha particle went straight through the gold, as if it were not there. A few were nudged a little to one side. That was what everyone expected. In those days, scientists pictured an atom as a soft ball of positive charge, with electrons dotted through it like raisins in a pudding. A pudding cannot stop a bullet.

'Now,' Rutherford had said, rubbing his big hands together, 'put your screen on the same side as the radium. See if any of them bounce back.'

'Will they?' Marsden had asked.

'Almost certainly not,' said Rutherford cheerfully. 'But look anyway.'

So Marsden looked. He sat in the dark, and waited for his eyes, and looked, and saw nothing. He rubbed his eyes and looked again. Nothing. Outside, the rain drummed on the roof. He could hear other students laughing in the corridor, on their way to tea. He thought, not for the first time, that this was the job you gave to the youngest person in the building, the one who did not matter.

His eyes ached. The dark seemed to press on them. Once, he almost got up to tell Rutherford that there was nothing to see. But Rutherford had said 'look anyway'. And Marsden was the kind of person who, when he was asked to do something properly, did it properly. So he made himself sit still and keep watching the dark patch of screen, where nothing was supposed to happen.

And then, something did.

A flash. One tiny green spark, on the wrong side.

Marsden held his breath. Tired eyes play tricks, he told himself. He waited. The minutes crawled. Another flash. Then, a long time later, another. They were rare, very rare. But they were there.

For days, Marsden and Geiger counted. They tried gold, then platinum and other metals. They counted until their eyes swam with green ghosts. Nearly all the alpha particles went straight through the metal. But about one in every eight thousand came flying back, as if it had hit a wall.

Marsden went to Rutherford and told him. Rutherford did not laugh. He went very quiet. Years later he said it was the most incredible thing that had ever happened to him. It was as if you fired a giant cannon shell at a sheet of tissue paper, and it came back and hit you.

Rutherford thought about it for almost two years. Then he understood. If nearly all the alpha particles went straight through, the gold must be nearly all empty space. If a few bounced back hard, there must be something hiding in that emptiness. Something tiny and heavy, with a strong positive charge, at the very centre of every atom. He called it the nucleus. Each gold atom was not a pudding at all. It was a vast, empty space, with a speck in the middle and its electrons spread thinly around it. If the atom were a cricket ground, the speck would be a peppercorn on the middle stump. Most alpha particles simply flew past the speck. Only the rare one that came very close was thrown back.

The idea became famous, and it was named after Rutherford. Marsden grew older, crossed the world to New Zealand, and became a scientist there. Few people remember the student who sat in the dark and kept looking at the place where nothing was supposed to happen.

But on rainy evenings, in his old age, Marsden would sometimes sit in a dark room on purpose, just for a moment. He would put his hand flat on a table and press. The table pressed back, firm and solid, as tables do. And he would smile, because he knew what the table really was. Almost all empty space, with a tiny speck in the middle of every atom, and electrons pushing back.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the story turns on the idea that atoms are almost entirely empty space. Most alpha particles pass straight through the gold because there is almost nothing there to hit. The rare ones that bounce back have met the tiny, heavy nucleus. What feels solid is not atoms packed with no gaps. It is electrons repelling electrons. Marsden and Geiger did the counting and Rutherford explained it, so the credit is shared. The last scene with the table is imagined.

- Ask: Why did Rutherford think no alpha particles would bounce back?
- Ask: Most alpha particles went straight through the gold. What did that tell Rutherford about atoms?
- Ask: A few came flying back. What did that tell him?
- Ask: Why do you think Marsden kept looking, when he thought there was nothing to see?
- Ask: The idea was named after Rutherford. Do you think Marsden and Geiger should share the credit? Why?

### g2 · Catch the Electron (20 min)
- **rules:** Put the atom map flat on the floor. You need two dice and 20 dried tamarind seeds or chana in a bowl.; The electron is hidden. Nobody knows where it is until it is measured.; Before each roll, every player guesses where the electron will be found: the inner ring, the middle ring or the outer ring. Write your guess down.; Then 'measure' the electron: roll both dice and add them up. Put one seed on the spot with that number. That is where the electron was found this time.; Everyone who guessed the right ring scores one point.; Play 20 rolls, one seed for each roll. The player with the most points wins. If players are tied, play 5 more rolls with spare seeds. Playing alone, try to score 9 points or more.; At the end, look at the seeds. Together they show the electron cloud.; Extra round for three or more people: one player holds a torch and is the detector. The others creep towards the nucleus while the torch is off. When the detector switches the torch on, anyone the beam lands on is 'measured' and must freeze in that one spot. The detector may also roll a paper ball along the floor, and anyone it touches is measured too. The first player to touch the nucleus without being measured wins.
- **Printable board: 12 parts of the atom map: the nucleus dot and 11 numbered spots in three rings. Cut out or draw each part big enough to hold a seed, and lay them out on the floor with the nucleus in the middle.:** Nucleus: a dot in the middle; Spot 6: inner ring; Spot 7: inner ring; Spot 8: inner ring; Spot 4: middle ring; Spot 5: middle ring; Spot 9: middle ring; Spot 10: middle ring; Spot 2: outer ring; Spot 3: outer ring; Spot 11: outer ring; Spot 12: outer ring
- **Printable score sheet: Guess sheet:** Roll number; My guess: inner, middle or outer ring; Where the electron was found; Point?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the atom map on the floor. 2. Put a bowl of seeds and two dice beside it. 3. Show the nucleus in the middle and the three rings.  
  *Parent says:* This map is an atom. The dot in the middle is the nucleus. Somewhere around it is an electron. We can never know exactly where it will be. We can only measure it and see.  
  *Child mode:* This map is an atom. The middle dot is the nucleus. An electron is somewhere around it. Nobody knows where until we measure.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Before each roll, everyone guesses a ring and writes it down. 2. Roll two dice and add them. 3. Put a seed on that number's spot. 4. Score a point for each right guess. 5. Play 20 rolls.  
  *Parent says:* Guess first: inner, middle or outer ring? Now let's measure. Roll the dice, add them, and put a seed where the electron was found.  
  *Child mode:* Guess a ring. Roll both dice and add them. Put a seed on that number. Did you guess right?
  - Ask: Which ring is the best one to guess? Why? (good: The inner ring, because 6, 7 and 8 come up a lot.; The inner ring, because there are more ways to make 7 than 12.)
  - Note: With two dice, 6, 7 and 8 come up most often, about 4 rolls in every 9. A child who always guesses the inner ring will usually win. That is the lesson: you can know the chances, but never the next result. This is how quantum physics treats electrons.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the seeds on the map together after 20 rolls.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the seeds. Where are they thickest? This is how scientists draw an electron. They call it an electron cloud.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your seeds. Where are there lots? Where are there few? You made an electron cloud!
  - Check: After 20 measurements, what do the seeds show? → **Where the electron is likely to be found: a cloud, thick near the middle**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about who or what can measure. If you have three or more people, play the torch extra round first (about 5 more minutes).  
  *Parent says:* A torch beam or a paper ball can measure you, just like a person. Does a measurement need a person?  
  *Child mode:* Could a torch or a paper ball measure you? Or only a person?
  - Note: Say clearly: in physics, an 'observer' is anything that measures. A detector, a beam of light or even air molecules can measure an electron. No person needs to watch. Some people claim quantum physics proves our minds make the world. It does not.
  - Think: If a speck of dust can 'measure' an electron, what does the word observer really mean?

## Experiments
### e1 · Cut It in Half, Again and Again (25 min)
- **question:** How many times can you cut a strip of paper in half?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many times do you think you can cut the strip in half before the piece is too small to cut? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason down.
- **fair test:** Always cut the piece exactly in half, across its width, and measure it with a ruler. Always keep one half and cut that one next. Count every cut. If you try again, use the same kind of paper and the same scissors.
- **safety:** Hold the paper by its long end, away from the blades. A grown-up does the cuts once the piece is shorter than 2 cm.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a line on a graph for how long the piece was after each cut.
- **results table:** Cut number | Length of the piece (cm or mm) | Could you still hold it easily? Yes or no
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut a strip of paper 24 cm long and 2 cm wide. 2. Put the strip, scissors, ruler and results table on a table. 3. Ask your child to guess how many cuts they will manage.  
  *Parent says:* We'll cut this strip in half, then cut one half in half, again and again. How many cuts do you think we can do before it's too small?  
  *Child mode:* Here is a strip of paper, 24 cm long. You will cut it in half again and again. How many cuts can you do? Write your guess.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the strip in half to find the middle. 2. Cut across the strip at the fold. 3. Measure one half and write the length in the table. 4. Keep one half and cut it in half the same way. 5. Repeat until you have made 4 cuts in all. The piece is now 1 and a half centimetres long.  
  *Parent says:* Each time, we cut across the strip, so the piece stays 2 centimetres wide. That gives you an edge to hold. Measure it and write it down.  
  *Child mode:* Fold the strip to find the middle. Cut across it. Measure one half. Keep it and cut it in half again.
  - Note: The lengths should be 12 cm, 6 cm, 3 cm and then 1 and a half cm. Cutting across the strip keeps one long edge of 2 cm, so small fingers stay away from the blades.
  - Check: The strip was 24 cm long. How long is one piece after the first cut? → **12 cm**
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up now holds the piece by its 2 cm edge and cuts it in half with sharp scissors. 2. Measure with the millimetre marks on the ruler. 3. Keep going until the piece is too thin to cut, usually after 6 to 8 cuts in all.  
  *Parent says:* Now the pieces are tiny, so I'll do the cutting. Count with me. Is it getting hard?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to do the tiny cuts. Count each cut out loud. When does it get too small?
  - Note: After 8 cuts, the piece would be a thin sliver about 1 millimetre long. The last cuts are fiddly, so stopping at 6 or 7 is fine. If the sliver slips, press it under a fingertip on a cutting board and cut beside your finger. Stop when it cannot be cut cleanly.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the cut edge of the last sliver with a magnifying glass, or a zoomed-in phone camera. 2. Look for tiny threads sticking out.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the edge. Can you see tiny threads? Paper is made of plant fibres. Each fibre is made of molecules, and each molecule is made of atoms.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the edge with a magnifier. Can you see tiny threads? Paper is made of them. The threads are made of atoms.
  - Ask: We stopped after about 8 cuts. How many more cuts would it take to make the piece one atom wide? (good: Lots more.; About 23 more, about 31 altogether.)
  - Note: To make the piece just one atom wide, you would need about 31 cuts in all. So everyone is still about 23 cuts away when the scissors stop. Show the halving chart on the 'How small is an atom?' card.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What do you think? What did you see? Why did it happen?
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: paper is made of particles much too small to cut with scissors. What I saw: I cut it 8 times, and the last piece was about 1 millimetre. Under the magnifier I saw tiny threads. Why: the threads are made of molecules, and molecules are made of atoms. A piece one atom wide is still about 23 cuts away.
### e2 · The Hidden Centre: roll marbles like Rutherford (30 min)
- **question:** Can you find out the size of a hidden thing just by rolling marbles at it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If the hidden thing is very small, how many of 20 marbles will come back? If it is big, how many? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Roll every marble the same way: from the same edge, at the same gentle speed, at a different spot along the edge each time. Roll 20 marbles each time. Only the hidden thing changes.
- **safety:** Pick up every marble afterwards, so nobody slips on them. Marbles are a choking risk for children under 3.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each hidden thing. Draw the card from above, with arrows showing where the marbles went.
- **results table:** Hidden thing | Marbles rolled | Went straight through | Turned aside | Came back
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the four tumblers on a smooth floor, about 45 cm apart, in a square. 2. While your child looks away, put the upside-down bowl in the middle. 3. Lay the cardboard on top of the tumblers, so it hides the bowl.  
  *Parent says:* Something is hidden under this card. You may not look or touch it. You can only roll marbles under the card and watch what comes out.  
  *Child mode:* Something is hidden under the card. Don't peek! You can only roll marbles under it. Watch where they come out.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child kneels at one side and rolls a marble gently under the card. 2. Watch where it comes out: straight across, off to one side, or back towards them. 3. Make a tally mark in the right column. 4. Roll 20 marbles, from different spots along the edge.  
  *Parent says:* Roll gently, a different spot each time. Did it go straight through? Turn aside? Or come back to you?  
  *Child mode:* Roll a marble under the card. Where did it come out? Make a tally mark. Roll 20 marbles, a new spot each time.
  - Note: A big bowl usually turns several marbles aside, and sends a few straight back. If marbles stop on a rug, move to a smooth floor. If they roll over the hidden thing, use something taller.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to guess how big the hidden thing is and where it sits. 2. Then lift the card and look.  
  *Parent says:* From what the marbles did, how big do you think the hidden thing is? Where is it? Let's look.  
  *Child mode:* Guess: is the hidden thing big or small? Where is it? Now lift the card and see!
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. While your child looks away, swap the bowl for one AA battery. 2. Press its bottom end into a small ball of chapati dough, so it stands up firmly. 3. Put the card back. 4. Roll 20 marbles again and tally.  
  *Parent says:* Now something much smaller is hidden. Roll 20 marbles again. What happens this time?  
  *Child mode:* Now something smaller is hidden. Roll 20 marbles again. How many come back now?
  - Note: With the battery, only 1 or 2 marbles in 20 usually hit it. Most of those turn aside, and only a head-on hit comes back. If none hit it, roll 20 more.
  - Check: With a tiny battery hidden, what happened to most marbles? → **They went straight through**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child how the real experiment worked, using the 'An atom is almost all empty space' card.  
  *Parent says:* In 1909, scientists fired alpha particles at very thin gold. Almost all went straight through. About 1 in 8,000 came back. What does that tell us about atoms?  
  *Child mode:* Scientists did this with gold in 1909. Almost everything went straight through. Only 1 in 8,000 bounced back. What does that tell you?
  - Think: Your battery was hit by about 1 marble in 20. The real nucleus was hit by about 1 in 8,000. Which is emptier, your card or a gold atom?
### e3 · Colour Spreads by Itself (20 min)
- **question:** Does colour spread through still water by itself? Does it spread faster in hot water or in cold water?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the colour spread if nobody stirs it? In which glass will it spread faster: the hot one or the cold one? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two glasses of the same size, with the same amount of water. Wait one minute so both are still. Add one drop to each at the same moment, from the same height. Do not stir or bump the table. Only the water's warmth changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water. Let the kettle water cool for 2 minutes first, so the glass does not crack. Don't touch the hot glass.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both glasses at 1, 3 and 5 minutes.
- **results table:** Glass | After 1 minute | After 3 minutes | After 5 minutes
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two glasses side by side on the white paper, on a table nobody will bump. 2. Fill one with cold water. 3. A grown-up fills the other with hot water to the same level. 4. Wait one minute until both are still.  
  *Parent says:* This glass is cold and this one is hot. We'll wait one minute so the water stops moving.  
  *Child mode:* Put two clear glasses on white paper. A grown-up fills one with hot water. You fill the other with cold. Wait one minute.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the food colour just above the water. 2. Drop one drop into each glass at the same moment. 3. Do not stir. 4. Watch and draw at 1, 3 and 5 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* One drop in each, at the same time. Now hands off the table. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Drop one drop of colour in each glass at the same time. Don't stir! Watch closely and draw what you see.
  - Ask: Nobody is stirring. So what is moving the colour? (good: The water is moving it.; Water molecules are bumping it.)
  - Note: In the hot glass, the colour usually swirls through most of the water within 2 or 3 minutes. In the cold glass, it sinks in a thin stream and is still patchy after 5 minutes. Most of the hot glass's swirling is warm water rising and cooler water sinking. This is called convection. Molecules bumping alone would take hours to colour the glass, but they do blur the colour's edges.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what made the colour spread, using the 'How we know atoms are real' card.  
  *Parent says:* Nobody stirred, but the water was moving. In the hot glass, warm water rises and cooler water sinks, so the water stirs itself. This is called convection. Water is also made of molecules that never stop moving. They bump the colour and blur its edges. In hot water, they move faster.  
  *Child mode:* Nobody stirred, but the water moved. Hot water stirs itself. Warm water rises and cool water sinks. Water molecules never stop moving, too. Hot ones move faster!
  - Check: Why did the colour spread faster in the hot water? → **Hot water moves more: it swirls as warm water rises, and its molecules move faster**

### a1 · Pencil Dots: draw an electron cloud (15 min)
- **record:** Count the dots in each ring and write the numbers. Draw a bar for each ring.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the paper flat on a table, with the target in the middle. 2. Show your child how to lift the pencil to their ear, close their eyes, and dot.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the target. Lift the pencil up to your ear. Close your eyes. Now bring it down and make a dot where you think the target is.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the target dot. Lift your pencil up to your ear. Close your eyes. Bring the pencil down to make a dot!
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child makes 50 dots, eyes closed every time. 2. Open your eyes between dots only to look at the target again. 3. Count out loud together.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going until we have 50 dots. Look at the target, close your eyes, dot. Count with me.  
  *Child mode:* Make 50 dots. Look, close your eyes, dot. Count out loud as you go.

- **s3 (4 min)**

  *Parent says:* Let's draw rings around the target and count. Which ring has the most dots? Where are the dots packed most tightly?  
  *Child mode:* Draw rings with a bangle and a plate. Count the dots in each ring. Where are they packed most tightly?
- **s4 (3 min)**

### Inside the Atom (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) In the marble test, a girl hides a big steel bowl under the card instead of a small battery. Before she rolls, what should she predict?  
   Options: Many more marbles will turn aside or come back, because the bowl fills much more of the space / All the marbles will go straight through, just like with the battery / None of the marbles will come out

2. (write) In 1909, most scientists pictured an atom as a soft ball, with its plus charge spread evenly all through it. Then Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden saw a few alpha particles bounce straight back from thin metal foil. Why did this prove the soft-ball picture wrong?  
   **Answer:** Alpha particles are fast and heavy. A soft ball with its charge spread out could only nudge them a little. To throw one straight back, the atom needs something tiny, heavy and packed tight. So each atom must have a tiny, heavy centre, the nucleus.. *Why:* A strong answer explains why a soft, spread-out atom could not throw an alpha particle back, and what must be there instead.
3. (truefalse) Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden looked through their microscope and saw single atoms.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They could not see atoms. They saw tiny flashes of light on a screen, each one made when an alpha particle hit it. By counting where the flashes appeared, they worked out what the alpha particles had hit.
4. (mcq) A girl puts one drop of food colour into a glass of hot water and one drop into a glass of ice-cold water, at the same moment. She does not stir. What will she see after five minutes?  
   Options: The colour has spread much further in the hot water / The colour has spread much further in the cold water / The colour has not spread in either glass, because nobody stirred / Both glasses look the same  
   **Answer:** The colour has spread much further in the hot water. *Why:* Water molecules are always moving, and in hot water they move faster. They knock the colour around and spread it. Many people think colour only spreads if you stir, but the test shows it spreads with nobody stirring.
5. (write) In the colour test, the colour spread slowly in the cold glass too, even though nobody stirred it and the water looked still. Why did it spread at all?  
   **Answer:** The water only looks still. Its molecules are moving all the time, even in cold water. They keep bumping into the colour and pushing it about, so it slowly spreads through the glass.. *Why:* A strong answer says that molecules are always moving, even when the water looks still.
6. (mcq) In 1827, Robert Brown, a Scottish scientist, saw tiny specks from pollen jiggling in water under his microscope. At first he wondered if they were alive. Then he saw specks of crushed glass jiggle in just the same way. What did that tell him?  
   Options: The jiggling was not caused by life. Something in the water was knocking the specks about / Glass is alive / His microscope was shaking  
   **Answer:** The jiggling was not caused by life. Something in the water was knocking the specks about. *Why:* Crushed glass is not alive, so life could not explain the jiggling. In 1905, Albert Einstein explained it: invisible water molecules keep bumping the specks from all sides. This jiggling is called Brownian motion.

### Quantum Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (perspective) People argue about what 'observing' means in quantum physics, the science of the very small.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer knows that the film character mixes up the physics word 'observer' with its everyday meaning, because experiments work with no person watching. It may still side with the philosopher, because the question of what happens between measurements really is open. A strong answer gives a reason and is fair to another view.. *Why:* The physicist's point is settled: a measurement does not need a person. The philosopher's point is also true: physicists still disagree about what the electron is like between measurements.
2. (truefalse) Every electron is exactly the same as every other electron, everywhere in the universe.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. There is no way at all to tell two electrons apart. Satyendra Nath Bose's work in 1924 used this kind of perfect sameness to count particles of light.
3. (mcq) If all the empty space were squeezed out of the atoms of every person on Earth, how big would all of humanity be?  
   Options: About the size of a sugar cube / About the size of a house / About the size of India / Exactly the same size  
   **Answer:** About the size of a sugar cube. *Why:* Nearly all of an atom is empty, and nearly all its weight is in the tiny nucleus. Squeeze out the emptiness, and the whole human race would fit in a sugar cube. It would still weigh about 500 million tonnes. Picking 'a house' is already a huge squeeze, but not nearly enough.
4. (fill) Protons and neutrons are each made mainly of three even smaller particles called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['quarks', 'quark']. *Why:* Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig imagined quarks in 1964. Experiments found signs of them in 1968.
5. (mcq) Ritu, a girl in Class 5, says, "One day, better microscopes will show exactly where an electron is and exactly how fast it is going, both at once." What would a physicist tell her?  
   Options: No tool can ever do this. Nature sets a limit: the more exactly anyone knows where an electron is, the less exactly they can know its speed / She is right. Our tools just need to get better / Electrons are invisible, so we can never measure them at all  
   **Answer:** No tool can ever do this. Nature sets a limit: the more exactly anyone knows where an electron is, the less exactly they can know its speed. *Why:* Werner Heisenberg showed this in 1927. It is a rule of nature, not a problem with our tools. Electrons can be measured, but never both their exact place and their exact speed at once. Physicists still argue about what this means the electron is really like.
6. (match) Match each scientist to their idea about electrons between measurements.  
   **Answer:** Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg → it has no exact place until it is measured; David Bohm → it always has an exact place, guided by a hidden wave; Hugh Everett → every possible result happens, in different branches. *Why:* These are three of the main ideas. None of them needs a human mind to do the measuring.
7. (truefalse) Nearly all the atoms in your body, apart from most of the hydrogen, were made inside stars.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Hydrogen was made soon after the Big Bang. Heavier atoms, like the carbon in your nails and the iron in your blood, were made in stars. Margaret Burbidge and her colleagues showed how in 1957.
8. (mcq) Bibha Chowdhuri and D.M. Bose carried photographic plates up mountains near Darjeeling, to catch the tracks of tiny particles coming from space. Why did they go up mountains?

   **Answer:** There is less air above a mountain to stop the particles, so more of them reach the plates. *Why:* The particles come from space and crash into the air. High up, there is less air above, so more particles get through. Their work, from 1939 to 1942, was an early step in finding particles called mesons.
9. (write) The space inside an atom is called empty. Is it truly empty? What do you think?  
   **Answer:** For example: it has no atoms in it, so in that way it is empty. But the electron cloud spreads through it, and scientists say even empty space fizzes with tiny flickers of energy. So perhaps nothing is truly empty.. *Why:* Any thoughtful answer is good. Scientists themselves say empty space is never perfectly empty.

   **Answer:** Democritus lived about 2,400 years ago, and his dates are fairly well known. Nobody knows exactly when Kanada lived. Scholars guess somewhere between about 600 and 200 years before the Common Era. So nobody can say who was first. I think it matters less than the idea itself, because both reached it by careful thinking, on their own.. *Why:* A strong answer explains that Kanada's dates are uncertain, then gives an opinion with a reason.
11. (truefalse) Some of the atoms in your body were once part of other living beings, such as plants and animals.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Atoms are not used up. The carbon in the rice you eat was taken from the air by the rice plant. When you breathe out, some of those atoms go back into the air, and a plant may take them in again.
12. (write) Plan a test to find out whether a smell spreads faster through a warm room or a cool room. Say what you will change, what you will keep the same, and what you will measure.

I will measure the time, in seconds, from opening the peel to the person raising a hand.    **Answer:** Light waves are too big to show the shape of a single atom, so no light microscope can show one. *Why:* To show the shape of something, light waves must be smaller than it. ## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is everything made of?  
   Options: Atoms / Only what we can see / Light / Nothing  
   **Answer:** Atoms. *Why:* All matter, including air and you, is made of atoms. Picking 'only what we can see' is natural, because atoms are invisible. Light is not made of atoms, so it cannot be what everything is made of.
2. (mcq) Can you see one atom with a magnifying glass?  
   Options: Yes, easily / No, atoms are far too small / Only in sunlight  
   **Answer:** No, atoms are far too small. *Why:* An atom is about one ten-millionth of a millimetre across. A magnifying glass only makes things a few times bigger. Picking 'only in sunlight' is a guess that more light helps. It doesn't, because light waves are thousands of times longer than an atom is wide.
3. (mcq) Which is the smallest?  
   Options: A grain of salt / A virus / An atom  
   **Answer:** An atom. *Why:* A virus is about a thousand times bigger across than an atom, and a grain of salt is far bigger again. Picking 'a virus' usually means thinking that germs are the smallest things there are.
4. (mcq) What is in the nucleus of an atom?  
   Options: Protons and neutrons / Only electrons / Air  
   **Answer:** Protons and neutrons. *Why:* The nucleus holds protons and neutrons. Electrons are found around it. Picking 'air' is a mix-up, because air is itself made of atoms.
5. (mcq) Which part of an atom is by far the lightest?  
   Options: The electron / The proton / The neutron  
   **Answer:** The electron. *Why:* A proton or a neutron is about 1,800 times heavier than an electron. So nearly all of an atom's weight is in the nucleus. Many pictures draw electrons big, but they are very light.
6. (mcq) Scientists fired alpha particles at thin metal. Almost all went straight through. What did that show?  
   Options: Atoms are almost all empty space / Metal is soft / The alpha particles were too small to matter  
   **Answer:** Atoms are almost all empty space. *Why:* If almost everything passes through, there is almost nothing there to hit. The rare bounce showed a tiny, heavy centre. Picking 'metal is soft' misses why a few alpha particles came flying back.
7. (mcq) Why did Ernest Rutherford decide that the nucleus must be very heavy?  
   Options: It could throw a fast alpha particle straight back / Gold is a heavy metal / It was big enough to see  
   **Answer:** It could throw a fast alpha particle straight back. *Why:* A fast, heavy alpha particle can only be thrown straight back by something heavier and packed tight. A light thing would simply be knocked out of the way. Nobody could see the nucleus, so the bounce was the evidence.
8. (mcq) How do electrons move in an atom?  
   Options: Round and round on tracks, like planets / We can only say where they are likely to be found, like a cloud / They sit still on the nucleus  
   **Answer:** We can only say where they are likely to be found, like a cloud. *Why:* Quantum physics gives the chances of finding an electron in each place, so scientists draw a cloud. Picking 'like planets' is the old 1913 picture that is still in many books and logos.
9. (mcq) Why do scientists draw an electron as a cloud, and not as a dot?  
   Options: They can only say how likely the electron is to be found in each place / Electrons are made of mist / Electrons are too big to draw as a dot  
   **Answer:** They can only say how likely the electron is to be found in each place. *Why:* An electron does not follow a path that anyone can trace. Scientists can only work out the chances of finding it in each place. The cloud is a picture of those chances: thick where it is often found, thin where it is rarely found.
10. (mcq) In physics, what can be an 'observer'?  
   Options: Anything that measures, like a detector or a beam of light / Only a person / Only a scientist with a notebook  
   **Answer:** Anything that measures, like a detector or a beam of light. *Why:* Anything that a particle bumps into can measure it. No person is needed. Picking 'only a person' comes from the everyday meaning of the word. Experiments work the same with no person in the room.
11. (mcq) Which of these is a way the very small behaves differently from a cricket ball?  
   Options: An electron in an atom can only have certain amounts of energy, never amounts in between / An electron falls when you drop it / An electron can be caught in a hand  
   **Answer:** An electron in an atom can only have certain amounts of energy, never amounts in between. *Why:* A cricket ball can roll at any speed and stop anywhere. An electron in an atom can only have certain amounts of energy, like standing on steps. The very small follows rules that the things we see do not.
12. (mcq) Which of these is evidence that atoms are real?  
   Options: Scientists can make pictures of single atoms with needle-tip microscopes / Atoms are written about in old books / Many people believe in atoms  
   **Answer:** Scientists can make pictures of single atoms with needle-tip microscopes. *Why:* Evidence is something that can be tested or seen. Pictures of single atoms, and the way tiny specks jiggle in water, are evidence. Old books and many believers are not, because people can believe things that are wrong.
13. (mcq) Which of these is still an open question for scientists?  
   Options: What an electron is doing when nothing is measuring it / Whether everything is made of atoms / Whether an atom has a nucleus  
   **Answer:** What an electron is doing when nothing is measuring it. *Why:* That atoms exist, and that they have a nucleus, is settled science. What an electron is like between measurements is still argued over. Bohr, Bohm and Everett each had a different idea.

## Applications
- Phone chips, computers and LED lights were designed using quantum physics, the science of the very small.
- Atomic clocks keep time using the exact energy steps of caesium atoms. The CSIR-National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi keeps Indian Standard Time with them.
- India's NavIC navigation satellites carry atomic clocks to tell your phone where you are.
- Scanning tunnelling microscopes picture and move single atoms, to design new materials.
- Fireworks get their colours from different atoms: sodium for yellow, copper for blue-green, strontium for red.
- Astronomers read the colours in starlight to find out which atoms stars are made of.
- Solar panels turn light into electricity, because light knocks electrons loose.
- Hospitals use MRI scanners, which read the tiny spins of hydrogen nuclei in your body to make pictures.
- Lasers, used in barcode scanners and eye surgery, work because electrons drop down exact energy steps.
- Nuclear power stations, like the ones at Tarapur and Kudankulam, get energy from splitting the nuclei of atoms.
- Medicines are designed by working out the shapes of molecules, atom by atom.
- Electron microscopes use beams of electrons, instead of light, to see things far smaller than a cell.

## Spotted around us
- The dots in a newspaper photo, and the tiny coloured lights in a phone screen. Many small dots make one picture.
- Silver vark on a barfi. It is beaten so thin that it is only around a thousand atoms thick.
- Salt and sugar grains. Salt grows as tiny cubes because its atoms are stacked in neat rows.
- Old orange street lights. They glow one exact colour, from sodium atoms.
- The smell of tadka from the kitchen. Tiny molecules of hot oil and spices travel through the air to your nose.
- Dust dancing in a sunbeam through a window. It swirls in air currents, a reminder that the air is full of things too small to see.
- Deepavali fireworks, coloured by different kinds of atoms.
- A pencil line on paper: a thin smear of graphite, which is flat layers of carbon atoms rubbed off the tip.

## Open questions
- **What is an electron doing between measurements?** The maths predicts every experiment perfectly. But scientists still argue about what is really happening when nothing measures it.
- **Is there a smallest thing?** Electrons and quarks have no size that anyone has found. Nobody knows if there is anything smaller inside them.
- **Are particles really little things, or ripples in invisible fields?** Many physicists think fields are what the world is really made of. What the fields themselves are, nobody knows.
- **Is string theory right?** It is the idea that the smallest particles are tiny vibrating strings. No experiment has been able to test it yet.
- **Why does the universe have more matter than antimatter?** Every particle has an opposite twin. The Big Bang should have made equal amounts, which would destroy each other. Somehow a little matter was left over, and nobody knows why.
- **What is most of the universe made of?** All the atoms we know make up only about a sixth of the matter in the universe. The rest, called dark matter, has never been seen directly.
- **Why do big things look so sure and solid, when tiny things follow rules of chance?** Scientists understand a lot of this: bumps with the world around them hide the chance in big things. But exactly where the tiny world ends and the everyday world begins is still studied.
