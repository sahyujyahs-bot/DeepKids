# Swachh Bharat: small actions for a clean India
`sci.environment.swachh-bharat.L3`

**Summary.** A family sweeps the house spotless and tips the dust outside the gate. Is the house clean now? In this lesson you sort one day of your own waste and find out what it is made of and where each part goes. You learn why wet and dry waste are kept apart. You learn how a toilet and a compost pit make waste safe, and why spitting and littering harm people you never meet. You look at the Swachh Bharat Mission, at what it changed and what people still argue about, and at the workers who clean India every day.

**Intro.** *Think of the last thing you threw away. Where is it right now, this minute? Who has touched it since it left your hand?* Most of us cannot answer. The bin is where our knowing stops. But the thing did not stop. Someone lifted it, someone sorted it, and it is lying somewhere now, or it has become something else. Today you will be a detective of your own dustbin. You will find out what your family throws away in one day and follow each kind to where it goes next. Then you will see why a clean India is made of small actions, and what else it needs.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what 'clean' means in science, and why a place can look clean and still be unsafe.
1. I can sort household waste into wet, dry and harmful, and say why mixing them spoils all three.
2. I can explain how wet waste turns into compost, and what happens to it in a dump with no air.
3. I can explain how germs from toilet waste and spit reach other people, and how toilets and habits stop them.
4. I can describe the Swachh Bharat Mission and give one thing it changed and one thing people still argue about.
5. I can name the people and other living beings who clean India, and say what the workers need to be safe.
6. I can choose three small actions for my home or street and say what each one changes.

## Terms
- **clean**: Free of harmful things, so that nothing dangerous can reach a mouth, water, food or soil. *e.g.* A hand pump with a covered drain around it is clean, even if the ground is muddy.
- **tidy**: Neat and in order, whether or not the place is safe. *e.g.* A shelf of bottles in a straight row is tidy. The shelf can still be dusty.
- **germs**: Living beings so small that you need a microscope to see them. Some make people ill and most do not. *e.g.* Germs ride on unwashed fingers from a toilet door handle to a roti.
- **solid waste**: Everything solid that people throw away. *e.g.* Peels, wrappers, broken pots and old shoes are all solid waste.
- **wet waste**: Waste that came from plants and animals and will rot. *e.g.* A banana peel, tea leaves and leftover rice are wet waste.
- **dry waste**: Waste that does not rot quickly, such as paper, plastic, glass, metal and cloth. *e.g.* A newspaper, a rinsed milk packet and an empty tin are dry waste.
- **harmful waste**: Waste that can hurt the people who handle it or poison water and soil. *e.g.* A used bandage, a battery and a broken tube light are harmful waste.
- **e-waste**: Old electrical things such as phones, chargers, bulbs and batteries. They hold metals that can be reused and poisons that must be kept out of soil. *e.g.* A broken phone charger goes to an e-waste collection point and not to the dustbin.
- **segregation**: Keeping different kinds of waste apart from the moment they are thrown away. *e.g.* Putting a peel in one bin and a clean paper in another is segregation.
- **compost**: Dark, crumbly, soil-like stuff made when tiny living beings eat plant and food waste. *e.g.* Peels left in a pot with dry leaves become compost in two to three months.
- **bacteria**: Tiny living beings, each made of one cell, found in soil, water, air and our bodies. Most are harmless and many do useful work. *e.g.* Bacteria in soil eat a peel and turn the peel into compost.
- **fungi**: Living beings such as mushrooms and moulds. They feed on dead plants and animals and break them down. *e.g.* The white fuzz on an old chapati is a fungus feeding on the chapati.
- **methane**: A gas made when wet waste rots without air. It burns easily and it traps heat around the Earth. *e.g.* A cow-dung gas plant makes methane on purpose and burns the gas for cooking.
- **dump**: A place where mixed rubbish is piled up in the open or buried, with no sorting. *e.g.* A hill of mixed rubbish at the edge of a city is a dump.
- **recycling**: Turning used material into new things. *e.g.* A used plastic bottle can be shredded and spun into thread for a shirt.
- **waste picker**: A person who earns a living by collecting, sorting and selling used material. *e.g.* A waste picker sorts bottles, paper and metal into separate sacks and sells each to a dealer.
- **cooperative**: A group that is owned and run by its own members, who share the work and the earnings. *e.g.* In Pune, waste pickers formed their own cooperative to collect waste from homes.
- **faeces**: The solid waste that a body passes out after food. Many families call it potty or latrine waste. *e.g.* Faeces carries germs, so it must be kept away from water and food.
- **diarrhoea**: An illness in which a person passes loose, watery faeces again and again and loses a lot of water. *e.g.* A child with diarrhoea needs a drink of salt and sugar in water and a visit to a doctor.
- **cholera**: A dangerous illness caused by a germ in water or food dirtied with faeces. It can cause very bad diarrhoea. *e.g.* In 1854, cholera killed hundreds of people near one water pump in London.
- **open defecation**: Passing faeces in fields, roadsides, rivers or other open places instead of a toilet. *e.g.* Where there is no toilet at home, a family may go to a field, and the germs can wash into a well.
- **twin-pit toilet**: A toilet joined to two pits that are used one at a time. The resting pit turns the waste into safe, dry manure. *e.g.* When the first pit is full, the family turns the pipe to the second pit and lets the first rest.
- **manure**: Plant food made from animal dung, rotted plants or safe human waste, which is dug into soil to help crops grow. *e.g.* Cow dung left to rot in a heap becomes manure for the vegetable patch.
- **sewer**: An underground pipe that carries used water and toilet waste away from homes. *e.g.* In a big city, the toilet at home empties into a sewer under the street.
- **sewage**: The used water from toilets, kitchens and bathrooms. *e.g.* The water that runs from a kitchen sink is part of a town's sewage.
- **treatment plant**: A place where sewage is cleaned, mostly by bacteria in big tanks, before the water goes to a river or the sea. *e.g.* A treatment plant makes the water clear enough to let into a river.
- **drain**: A channel or pipe that carries water away from a street or house. *e.g.* When it rains, the street drain carries water to a bigger drain and then to a river.
- **Harappan civilisation**: A group of cities in the north-west of the Indian subcontinent. They were at their height from about 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE. *e.g.* Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal and Dholavira were Harappan cities with covered brick drains.
- **saliva**: The watery liquid in the mouth. *e.g.* Your mouth fills with saliva when you smell hot idlis.
- **sputum**: The thick liquid coughed up from deep in the chest. *e.g.* A person with a chest illness may cough up sputum that carries germs.
- **tuberculosis**: An illness, called TB for short, caused by germs that mostly settle in the lungs and spread through coughs. *e.g.* TB can be treated, and a person who takes the full course of medicine gets well.
- **litter**: Waste left lying in a shared place instead of in a bin. *e.g.* A chips packet on a pavement is litter.
- **shared place**: A place that belongs to everyone, such as a road, a park, a beach or a river. *e.g.* The lane outside your home is a shared place, because every neighbour uses it.
- **Swachh Bharat Mission**: A programme of the Government of India, begun on 2 October 2014, to build toilets and clean up waste. The name means Clean India. *e.g.* The mission's sign is a pair of round spectacles, like Mahatma Gandhi's.
- **open defecation free**: A place where nobody goes to the toilet in the open. It is often written as ODF. *e.g.* A village that has declared itself ODF says that every family uses a toilet.
- **survey**: A way of finding out what is true by asking or counting many people or places. *e.g.* Researchers walked into villages to ask families whether they used their toilets, and wrote down each answer.
- **sanitation worker**: A person whose job is to clean streets, drains, toilets, sewers or septic tanks. *e.g.* The person who sweeps your street at dawn is a sanitation worker.
- **manual scavenging**: Cleaning human waste by hand from toilets, drains or tanks, with no safe equipment. It has been against the law since 1993. *e.g.* A man who goes down a blocked sewer with no mask or harness is doing manual scavenging, which the law forbids.
- **caste**: A group that a person is born into in India. For many centuries it was used to decide a person's work and who could eat or marry with whom. The Constitution forbids unequal treatment because of caste. *e.g.* For centuries, cleaning toilets was forced on certain castes, whatever the children wanted to be.
- **Dalit**: The name that many people from castes once treated as untouchable use for themselves, to stand together against unfair treatment. *e.g.* Dr B. R. Ambedkar was born into a community treated as untouchable, and he chaired the Drafting Committee of India's Constitution.
- **zero-landfill**: A place that sends almost nothing to a dump. Wet waste is composted or turned to gas, and dry waste is sold for recycling. *e.g.* A town that turns its wet waste into gas and sells its dry waste is working towards zero-landfill.
- **scavenger**: An animal who eats dead animals or leftover food, and so cleans up what would rot. *e.g.* A crow that picks scraps from the gutter is a scavenger.
- **vulture**: A large bird who feeds on dead animals. In India, vultures once cleaned away dead cattle within hours. *e.g.* Vultures can strip a dead cow to the bones within about an hour.
- **refuse**: To say no to something you do not need, so that it never becomes waste. *e.g.* When you say no to a plastic straw, you refuse it.
- **reuse**: To use a thing again as it is, without changing it into something else. *e.g.* Refilling the same steel bottle every day is reuse.
- **single-use plastic**: A plastic thing made to be thrown away after one use, such as a straw, a spoon or a thin carry bag. *e.g.* A plastic spoon given with a snack is used for ten minutes and then thrown away.
- **microplastics**: Pieces of plastic smaller than a grain of rice, made when bigger pieces break up. *e.g.* Scientists have found microplastics in sea salt, in fish and in rain.
- **clean-up**: A time when people come together to remove waste from a shared place. *e.g.* A weekend beach clean-up on Versova beach in Mumbai began with two people.
- **system**: A set of rules, services and designs that shape what everyone does, such as waste collection and the packaging that companies make. *e.g.* A city truck that collects sorted waste each morning is part of the waste system.
- **atom**: A tiny piece of matter, far too small to see. Everything you can touch is built from atoms. *e.g.* A peel is made of atoms. When bacteria eat it, the same atoms go into the soil and into the bacteria.

## Explanations
### Clean means harmful things are kept away from mouths, water and soil. It is different from tidy · *defined*
**Definition:** In science, a clean place is one where germs and harmful waste cannot easily reach people, other living beings, water or food. A tidy place is one that looks neat.

A swept room looks clean. A glass of clear water looks clean. Looks can mislead. Germs are living beings too small to see, and some of them cause illness. A spoonful of pond water can look clear and still hold millions of germs. So scientists ask a different question from 'does it look neat?'. They ask whether anything harmful can travel from here to a mouth, to drinking water, to food or to the soil. A tidy lane with an open drain beside the hand pump is not clean. A muddy garden with a covered compost pit is.
- e.g. A shining white plate wiped with a dirty cloth carries more germs than before.
- e.g. A forest floor is covered in fallen leaves and is not dirty. The leaves are food for the soil.
- e.g. A neat row of plastic bottles on a beach is tidy, and the plastic is still in the wrong place.
- e.g. Dust swept from a house onto the road has only moved.
- **Doctors and public health scientists:** Clean means the paths that carry germs and poisons to bodies are blocked. It can be measured by counting germs in water or illness in a neighbourhood.
- **Mary Douglas, a British anthropologist, in her 1966 book 'Purity and Danger':** She wrote that dirt is 'matter out of place'. Soil in the garden is soil. The same soil on the bed is dirt. Every culture has its own rules about what belongs where, and calls the rule-breaking thing dirty.
- **Thiruvalluvar, in Thirukkural, verse 298:** Outer cleanness comes from water. Inner cleanness shows in truthfulness. The verse holds that a person can be washed and still not clean, and it puts the two side by side.
### What is in India's dustbins: about half is food and plant waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Solid waste is everything solid that people throw away. Wet waste is the part that came from plants and animals and will rot. Dry waste is the part that will not rot quickly, such as paper, plastic, glass and metal.

India's towns and cities throw away about 1.6 lakh tonnes of solid waste every day. That is roughly a third of a kilogram for each town-dweller, each day. What is it made of? About half is wet waste: peels, leftover food, tea leaves, flowers, garden clippings. Wet waste rots, and rotting is useful in the right place. The rest is mostly dry waste: paper, cardboard, plastic, glass, metal and cloth. A small part is harmful waste, such as used bandages, nappies, batteries and old medicines. So most of what we call rubbish is either food for soil or material for a factory.
- e.g. A banana peel, an eggshell and used tea leaves are wet waste.
- e.g. A milk packet, a newspaper and a steel-wool scrubber are dry waste.
- e.g. A used plaster, a blade and a tube light are harmful waste.
- e.g. Many town families of four make 1 to 2 kilograms of waste a day. At 2 kilograms a day, that is about 730 kilograms in a year.
### Three bins: wet, dry and harmful. Mixing them spoils all three · *defined*
**Definition:** Segregation means keeping different kinds of waste apart from the moment they are thrown away.

Tip leftover sambar onto a newspaper. Now the paper is soggy and smelly, and no paper mill will take it. The sambar is full of ink and cannot go to compost. Two useful things have become one useless thing. That is why India's rules, made in 2016, ask every home to keep three kinds of waste apart. Many towns use a green bin for wet waste and a blue bin for dry waste, rinsed and dry. Harmful waste, such as nappies, sanitary pads, bandages, blades and batteries, is wrapped and kept in a third bin. The colour of that bin differs from town to town, so ask your collector. Sorting takes a few seconds at home. Un-mixing a truckload later is nearly impossible.
- e.g. A curd cup rinsed with a splash of water can be recycled. A curd cup with curd in it spoils the whole sack.
- e.g. Broken glass and blades are wrapped in paper and marked, because a person will handle the bag.
- e.g. Coconut shells and bones rot very slowly, so many towns ask for them in dry waste.
- e.g. Old phones, chargers and bulbs are 'e-waste' and go to special collection points.
- **Waste workers' groups, such as SWaCH in Pune and Hasiru Dala in Bengaluru:** Sorting at home is the single act that most changes a waste worker's day. Clean, dry material earns them money. Mixed waste cuts their hands and makes them ill.
- **Some city engineers and companies:** People will never sort reliably. So cities should collect mixed waste and use machines to separate it or burn it. Critics reply that machines sort wet Indian waste poorly. They add that burning it gives little electricity and dirty smoke.
### Wet waste becomes compost, with the help of countless small living beings · *defined*
**Definition:** Compost is the dark, crumbly, soil-like material left when living beings have eaten plant and food waste, with air. These beings are bacteria, fungi and earthworms.

A peel left in a pot of soil does not vanish. Someone eats it. Bacteria and fungi, who are living beings mostly too small to see, feed on the peel. Earthworms, ants and beetles join in. They need three things: food, air and a little dampness. As they feed they give off warmth, and a big heap can get hotter inside than a summer afternoon. In two to three months the peels have become compost, dark and crumbly, smelling like earth after rain. Plants grow well in it. Nothing was thrown away. What the peel was made of went from a plant, to you, to the soil, and is ready for a plant again.
- e.g. A home composter can be a clay pot with holes, layered with dry leaves to let air in.
- e.g. A heap that smells bad has too little air. Stirring it or adding dry leaves fixes this.
- e.g. Cooked food, peels, tea leaves, flowers and eggshells can all be composted at home.
- e.g. In Alappuzha, Kerala, neighbourhoods compost their wet waste in shared bins instead of sending it to a dump.
- **Albert Howard and Gabrielle Howard, British plant scientists who worked in Indore from 1924 to 1931:** They studied how Indian farmers returned every scrap to the soil and worked out measured steps for it, known as the Indore method. Albert Howard wrote that the peasants of India were his professors. Gabrielle Howard, a trained plant scientist, did much of the research and is often left out.
- **Soil scientists today:** Compost improves how soil holds water and feeds soil life. It carries less plant food than factory fertiliser, so many say that feeding the world needs both.
- **Vrikshayurveda of Surapala, a Sanskrit book on plant care from about the 10th century CE:** It gives recipes for a liquid manure called kunapajala. This is made by letting animal and plant remains break down in water. The book says which trees to feed with it. Turning waste into plant food is an old, written practice in India.
### The same peel in a dump, with no air, makes a gas that burns and warms the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Methane is a gas made when wet waste rots without air. It burns easily, and in the air it traps heat.

Now bury the same peel deep in a mountain of mixed rubbish. No air reaches it. Different bacteria take over, ones who live without air. They work slowly, and they give off a gas called methane. Methane has no smell and burns easily. Dumps catch fire from inside and smoulder for days. Methane that escapes also traps heat around the Earth, far more strongly than the same amount of the gas we breathe out. So one peel has two futures. With air, in a compost pot, the peel becomes soil in three months. Without air, in a dump, the peel becomes a fire risk and a warming gas.
- e.g. The Ghazipur dump in Delhi, begun in 1984, had grown to about 65 metres high by 2019. That is nearly as tall as the Qutub Minar.
- e.g. Dark, bad-smelling liquid drains out of dumps and can seep into the water under the ground.
- e.g. Some cities now trap dump gas in pipes and burn it for cooking or electricity.
- e.g. A gobar gas plant on a farm uses the same airless rotting on purpose, in a closed tank, to make cooking gas from dung.
### Dry waste is recycled by people: India's waste pickers · *defined*
**Definition:** Recycling is turning used material into new things. A waste picker is a person who earns a living by collecting, sorting and selling used material.

A plastic bottle does not walk to a factory. In India, most recycling begins with a person. Waste pickers collect bottles, paper, metal and cardboard from bins, streets and dumps. They sort them by hand into dozens of kinds and sell them to dealers, who sell them to factories. Estimates of their number run from about 15 lakh to 40 lakh. Many are women, and many are Dalits. They save cities a great deal of money and keep mountains of material out of dumps. For years most had no gloves, no fixed pay and no thanks. Some have now formed their own groups and won identity cards and contracts.
- e.g. In Pune, the waste pickers' own cooperative, SWaCH, has collected sorted waste door to door for the city since 2008.
- e.g. In Bengaluru, waste pickers were given city identity cards from 2011, after a campaign by the group Hasiru Dala.
- e.g. A used drinks bottle can be shredded, melted and spun into thread for cloth.
- e.g. A shiny chips packet is plastic and metal foil stuck together. It is very hard to recycle, so almost nobody buys it.
- **Waste pickers' unions, such as the Kagad Kach Patra Kashtakari Panchayat in Pune, formed in 1993:** Waste picking is skilled work that serves the whole city and the environment. Pickers should be paid by the city, given safety gear and health care, and have first right to the dry waste.
- **Some city planners:** Waste work by hand is unsafe and should be replaced by companies with trucks and machines. Pickers' groups answer that this takes away the living of waste pickers and recycles less.
### Germs from toilet waste reach mouths by a few known paths · *defined*
**Definition:** Faeces is the solid waste a body passes out. Germs in faeces can reach another person's mouth through fingers, flies, fields, water and food.

Faeces, which many families call potty, carries very large numbers of germs. Most are harmless inside the gut, and some cause diarrhoea, cholera and typhoid. If faeces is left in the open, the germs travel. Fingers carry them. Flies land on the faeces and then on food. Rain washes them into wells and rivers. Feet carry them from fields. In 1958 two engineers, Edmund Wagner and Joseph Lanoix, drew all these paths in one picture for the World Health Organization. It shows where to block each path. A toilet blocks the first step. Washing hands with soap blocks fingers. Covering food blocks flies. Safe water blocks the rest.
- e.g. One fly can carry germs on the hairs of the legs from a heap to a plate in seconds.
- e.g. A well downhill from an open field can be spoilt by one heavy shower.
- e.g. Diarrhoea used to kill more than a lakh young children in India every year. The number has fallen as toilets, clean water and medicine spread.
- e.g. A child's faeces carries as many germs as a grown-up's, so nappies need the same care.
### How a twin-pit toilet makes waste safe without a sewer · *defined*
**Definition:** A twin-pit toilet is a toilet joined to two pits in the ground, used one at a time. In the resting pit, the waste slowly turns into safe, dry manure.

Most Indian villages have no sewer pipes. The twin-pit toilet was designed for them. The pan slopes steeply, so one mug of water flushes it. A bend in the pipe holds a little water, which keeps smell and flies from coming up. The pipe leads to one of two pits lined with bricks that have gaps between them. Water soaks away into the soil, and the soil filters it. When the first pit fills, after a few years, the pipe is turned to the second pit. The first is left to rest. In about a year or two, soil bacteria turn the contents into dry, crumbly manure with no smell and no harmful germs.
- e.g. A flush toilet with a tank uses about ten litres each time. A pour-flush pan needs about one and a half to two.
- e.g. The pits are dug away from wells and hand pumps, so that soaked-away water is well filtered first.
- e.g. Bindeshwar Pathak, who founded Sulabh in 1970, improved this design and spread it to lakhs of homes.
- e.g. In cities, toilets drain to sewers and treatment plants, where bacteria in large tanks do the same cleaning work faster.
- **Sanitation engineers:** The twin pit is cheap, saves water and needs no person to handle fresh waste. It suits most soils, though not places where the water under the ground is very near the surface.
- **Diane Coffey and Dean Spears, researchers, in their 2017 book 'Where India Goes':** In villages they surveyed, many families who owned a pit toilet did not use it. People feared the day the pit would need emptying, a job long forced on Dalits and seen as polluting. They argue that toilets alone do not end open defecation unless these beliefs change.
### Covered drains and home toilets were built in the Harappan cities over 4,000 years ago · *defined*
**Definition:** The Harappan civilisation was a group of cities in the north-west of the Indian subcontinent, at its height from about 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE.

Planned sanitation in this land is very old. At Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal and Dholavira, from about 2600 BCE, many houses had a bathing floor of tightly fitted bricks. Some had a toilet. Waste water ran through a pipe in the wall into a drain along the street. The street drains were built of baked brick and covered with slabs. They sloped gently so that water kept moving. At some corners there were pits where solids settled and could be dug out. Few other cities in the world had anything like this at that time. Nobody knows who cleaned the drains, or how those workers were treated.
- e.g. Lothal, in Gujarat, had brick drains leading from houses to the street.
- e.g. Dholavira, in Kutch, had large stone drains to carry storm water to its reservoirs.
- e.g. Kautilya's Arthashastra, book 2, chapter 36, sets fines for throwing dirt on a road. It also fines people who let mud or water collect there, with higher fines on the king's road.
- e.g. Rome's great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima, was begun about 600 BCE, some 2,000 years after the Harappan drains.
- **Archaeologists such as Jonathan Mark Kenoyer:** The drains show shared rules and shared spending across a whole city. Someone planned the slopes, and someone kept the drains clear for centuries.
- **Historians who ask about the workers:** The Harappan script has not been read, so we do not know if cleaning was shared by all, paid work, or forced on one group. Good drains tell us about engineering. They do not tell us whether the system was fair.
### Spitting sends germs and stains into shared places · *defined*
**Definition:** Saliva is the liquid in the mouth. Sputum is the thicker liquid coughed up from the chest. Both can carry germs.

Spit looks like water. It carries whatever germs are living in a person's mouth, nose and chest. When someone with a chest illness coughs or spits, tiny drops fly into the air and can be breathed in by people nearby. Tuberculosis, called TB, spreads this way, mostly through coughing. India has more TB patients than any other country. Spit on a floor is also trodden through and touched by small children and by other animals. Spit mixed with paan or gutkha does one more thing. It is slightly acid and it stains. Red streaks on walls and stairs are hard to remove, and over years they can eat into steel.
- e.g. On Kolkata's Howrah Bridge, the steel bases of the hangers were found to be thinning from years of gutkha spit, and were given protective covers.
- e.g. Indian Railways spends crores of rupees and a great deal of water every year on washing spit stains from stations and coaches.
- e.g. During the 2020 pandemic, spitting in public was made a punishable offence across India.
- e.g. A person who needs to spit can use a wash basin, a toilet or a tissue that goes in a bin.
- **Doctors who study tuberculosis:** TB spreads mainly through the air when a sick person coughs, and much less from spit on the ground. They support no-spitting rules for general hygiene, and say that finding and treating patients early prevents far more TB.
- **The Buddhist monastic rules, in the Vinaya Pitaka, Sekhiya rules 74 and 75:** A monk or nun who is not ill is told not to pass waste or spit onto growing plants or into water. The rule is about 2,400 years old, and it protects water and crops that other beings depend on.
- **Researchers who study habits:** Fines seldom work when nobody enforces them. People spit less where spittoons and basins are provided, where walls are clean to begin with, and where others around them do not spit.
### Litter invites more litter, and one person's small act becomes everyone's problem · *defined*
**Definition:** Litter is waste left in a shared place. A shared place is one that belongs to everyone and so is easily cared for by no one.

Why does a person who would never throw a wrapper on the floor at home throw one on the road? One wrapper seems to make no difference. And a road is nobody's and everybody's. In 1990 the American researcher Robert Cialdini tested this. People found a paper slip on their car. In a car park already covered in litter, far more of them dropped the slip on the ground than in a clean car park. Litter tells the next person 'this is what people do here'. A clean street sends the opposite message. So the first wrapper matters most, and so does the first person who picks one up.
- e.g. A corner where one bag of rubbish is dumped at night often has ten bags by the weekend.
- e.g. In 2008, researchers in the Netherlands tested a lane. People littered about twice as often when the walls were scribbled on as when the same walls were clean.
- e.g. In Mumbai's floods of 26 July 2005, plastic bags blocked storm drains and made the flooding worse.
- e.g. Some towns paint rangoli or plant flowers on old dumping corners, and the dumping stops.
- **Garrett Hardin, an American ecologist, in 1968:** He called this the 'tragedy of the commons'. Each person gains a little by misusing a shared thing, and the cost is spread over everyone. He believed only private ownership or strict government control could prevent ruin.
- **Elinor Ostrom, an American scholar, who won the Nobel Prize in economics in 2009:** She studied forests, fisheries and water channels around the world, including in Nepal. She found many communities that cared for shared things very well for centuries, with their own rules, their own watchers and fair shares. The tragedy is not certain.
### Street drains lead to rivers, and most of India's waste water reaches them untreated · *defined*
**Definition:** Sewage is the used water from toilets, kitchens and bathrooms. A sewage treatment plant cleans the sewage before it is let into a river or the sea.

A wrapper dropped in a gutter does not stay there. The next rain carries it to a bigger drain, then to a stream, then to a river and at last to the sea. The used water from homes takes the same road. In a treatment plant, bacteria in large tanks eat most of the waste in the water before it is let go. India's towns and cities make about 72,000 million litres of sewage a day. Less than one-third of it is treated. The rest flows into rivers and lakes as it is. People downstream bathe in that river, water crops with it and often drink from it. So do fish, birds and dolphins.
- e.g. The Ganga passes about a hundred towns and cities, each adding waste water to what the last one sent down.
- e.g. The Namami Gange programme, begun in 2014, builds treatment plants and stops drains from emptying straight into the river.
- e.g. Flowers offered at temples are now collected in several cities and turned into compost and incense sticks, instead of going into the river.
- e.g. The Ganga river dolphin, India's national water animal, is nearly blind and finds fish by sound. Dirty water and boat noise make this harder for them.
- **River engineers:** A river is a flow of water to be measured and managed. The cure is more treatment plants, enough clean flow kept in the river, and factories made to treat their own waste.
- **The Uttarakhand High Court, in March 2017:** The court declared the Ganga and the Yamuna to be living persons in law, with rights of their own. The Supreme Court put this order on hold a few months later, because it raised hard questions, such as who answers if a river floods.
- **The Māori people of the Whanganui River in New Zealand:** They say: 'I am the river, and the river is me.' In March 2017 New Zealand's parliament recognised the Whanganui as a legal person. That means the law treats the river as someone with rights. Two guardians speak for the river.
- **The Atharva Veda, book 12, hymn 1, verse 35:** The poet asks the Earth that whatever is dug from her may quickly grow again, and asks not to hurt her. The hymn treats the Earth as someone who can be hurt. Its words are given in full in the Roots part.
### The Swachh Bharat Mission: toilets for every home, and cleaner towns · *defined*
**Definition:** The Swachh Bharat Mission is a Government of India programme that began on 2 October 2014. It aims to end defecation in the open and to manage waste in every village and town.

On 2 October 2014, Mahatma Gandhi's birthday, the Government of India began the Swachh Bharat Mission. 'Swachh Bharat' means 'Clean India'. Its first aim was that nobody should have to defecate in the open by 2 October 2019. The government gave families money to build toilets, and reports that more than ten crore home toilets were built in villages. Its second aim is clean towns: door-to-door collection, sorted waste, and no more rubbish mountains. Each year a survey ranks cities on cleanliness. It was not India's first such programme. Earlier ones began in 1986 and 1999. This one was far larger, and it made cleanliness something the whole country talked about.
- e.g. The mission's sign is Gandhi's round spectacles, with 'Swachh' on one lens and 'Bharat' on the other.
- e.g. A village where nobody defecates in the open is called 'open defecation free', or ODF.
- e.g. The second stage, begun in 2020 and 2021, adds compost pits, soak pits, plastic collection and treating waste water.
- e.g. Public toilets were built at bus stands, markets and petrol pumps.
### Did Swachh Bharat work? The counts disagree · *debated*
**Definition:** To judge a programme, people compare what was measured before and after. Different ways of measuring can give different answers.

In October 2019 the government declared rural India open defecation free. It had counted toilets, village by village. Independent researchers counted something else: what people actually do. In 2018 a team revisited villages in Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh. Open defecation there had fallen a great deal, from about 70 percent of people to about 44 percent. That is a big change in four years, and it is not zero. About one in four people who owned a toilet still went in the open. A national health survey in 2019 to 2021 found about one household in five without a toilet in use. So both things are true. Very much changed, and the work is unfinished.
- e.g. A toilet with no water nearby, or with a pit the family fears to empty, often becomes a store room.
- e.g. A 2024 study linked the mission to tens of thousands fewer deaths of babies each year.
- e.g. Some reports described officials shaming or fining people to push up the numbers.
- e.g. Women and girls gain most from a home toilet: safety at night, and no long wait for darkness.
- **The Government of India:** More than ten crore toilets were built, and every village declared itself open defecation free, as checked by a national survey. Toilet coverage rose from under 40 percent of homes to nearly all in five years.
- **Aashish Gupta, Diane Coffey, Dean Spears and colleagues at the research group r.i.c.e., in 2018 and 2019:** Their repeat survey found a large fall in open defecation. It also found that open defecation stayed common in the four states they studied. They found that some poor families were pushed or threatened, and argued that counting toilets overstates success.
- **Suman Chakrabarti and colleagues, in 2024:** Comparing districts before and after the mission, they found that districts where many toilets were built under the mission had clearly fewer deaths of babies and young children. They estimated 60,000 to 70,000 fewer infant deaths a year.
### Who cleans India? Sanitation workers, an old injustice, and two views of it · *debated*
**Definition:** A sanitation worker is a person whose job is to clean streets, drains, toilets, sewers or septic tanks. Manual scavenging means cleaning human waste by hand.

Every clean street was cleaned by someone. Every sewer was unblocked by someone. In India, for many centuries, this work was forced on certain castes, the people now called Dalits, and their children were given no other choice. Cleaning human waste by hand was banned by law in 1993 and again in 2013. It still happens. Workers still go down into sewers and septic tanks without safety gear. By the government's own count, more than 300 died doing so in five recent years. A clean India has to mean safe machines, fair pay and respect for these workers. It also means the work is no longer tied to birth.
- e.g. Bezwada Wilson, born into a family of manual scavengers in Karnataka, leads the Safai Karmachari Andolan and won the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 2016.
- e.g. In 2018 engineers in Kerala built 'Bandicoot', a robot that goes down a manhole and clears blocked sewers, so that no person has to.
- e.g. In Kerala, women of the Haritha Karma Sena collect sorted waste from homes as paid, uniformed workers.
- e.g. Wrapping a blade and rinsing a bottle are small acts that protect a worker's hands.
- **Mahatma Gandhi:** He cleaned toilets himself, at his ashrams and at the 1901 Congress meeting in Calcutta, and asked every person to clean up their own waste. He called the work noble, like a mother's care, and wanted all castes to share it.
- **B. R. Ambedkar, in his 1945 book 'What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables':** He wrote that in India a person is a scavenger because of birth and not because of the work. Calling the work noble, he argued, keeps the same people doing it. What is needed is an end to caste, and other work, schooling and equal rights for those families.
- **Bezwada Wilson and the Safai Karmachari Andolan:** Building crores of toilets without sewers or machines means more pits and tanks for people to clean by hand. They ask that the money and attention given to toilets also go to ending manual scavenging.
### Places that did it: Indore, Ambikapur, Alappuzha and Mawlynnong · *defined*
**Definition:** A zero-landfill place sends almost nothing to a dump. Its wet waste is composted or turned to gas, and its dry waste is sold for recycling.

Four places in India show that it can be done, each in a different way. Indore, in Madhya Pradesh, has been ranked India's cleanest city every year from 2017 to 2023. Every home hands over sorted waste to a truck each morning. A plant turns the city's wet waste into gas that runs its buses. Ambikapur, in Chhattisgarh, is cleaned by women's groups who sort waste into many kinds and sell it. Alappuzha, in Kerala, closed its dump and composts in each neighbourhood. Mawlynnong, a Khasi village in Meghalaya, has had bamboo dustbins on every path for years, and every family sweeps the lanes.
- e.g. Indore's gas plant, opened in 2022, takes in roughly 550 tonnes of wet waste a day.
- e.g. Ambikapur opened a 'Garbage Café' in 2019, where a kilogram of plastic waste buys a full meal.
- e.g. In 2017 the United Nations Environment Programme named Alappuzha among five cities in the world tackling waste well.
- e.g. A travel magazine called Mawlynnong the cleanest village in Asia in 2003.
- **City officials in Indore:** Success came from strict rules. Every door gets a daily collection. Littering and mixing are fined. Thousands of workers and vehicles are paid for by a user fee.
- **People's groups in Alappuzha and Mawlynnong:** Success came from neighbours agreeing among themselves, with little machinery and little cost. They say a big city's plants and trucks are costly to run and that small, local ways last longer.
### Other living beings clean India too, and losing them costs lives · *defined*
**Definition:** A scavenger is an animal who eats dead animals or leftover food. Scavengers and decomposers together clear away what would otherwise pile up and rot.

Long before any mission, India had cleaners. Vultures stripped a dead cow to the bones in under an hour. Crows, kites, ants, dung beetles and bacteria took what was left. In the 1990s India's vultures began to die. The scientist Vibhu Prakash, of the Bombay Natural History Society, recorded the crash. More than 95 in every 100 were lost in about fifteen years. In 2004 a team led by Lindsay Oaks found the cause. A pain medicine given to cattle, called diclofenac, poisoned vultures who ate the dead cattle. With the vultures gone, dead animals lay rotting, stray dogs grew in number, and rabies spread. India banned the medicine for animals in 2006.
- e.g. A 2024 study estimated that the loss of vultures led to about one lakh extra human deaths a year in India between 2000 and 2005.
- e.g. Black kites circle above markets and dumps in most Indian cities and pick up scraps.
- e.g. Ants clear a dropped crumb of food from a floor within hours.
- e.g. Vulture breeding centres at Pinjore in Haryana and elsewhere are now raising and releasing young vultures.
- **Ecologists:** Scavengers do public health work for free. The vulture crash is one of the clearest cases anywhere of people's health depending on another animal.
- **The Parsi community of India:** For centuries Parsis have laid their dead in open towers for vultures, so that the body neither dirties the earth nor wastes fire. With vultures gone, the community has had to rethink an ancient practice.
### The cleanest waste is the waste never made: refuse and reuse come before recycle · *debated*
**Definition:** To refuse is to not take a thing you do not need. To reuse is to use a thing again as it is. Both come before recycling, which needs fuel or electricity, water and work.

Sorting helps. Making less helps more. Every wrapper not taken is one that nobody has to collect, sort or bury. India has old habits that do this. Food is served on a banana leaf or a plate of stitched sal leaves, and the plate goes to a cow or the compost heap. Tea comes in a clay kulhad. Lunch travels in a steel tiffin for thirty years. Milk came in a returned bottle. On 1 July 2022 India banned a list of throw-away plastic items, such as straws, plastic cutlery and thin carry bags. People disagree on how well bans work, and on what should replace plastic.
- e.g. A steel bottle refilled every day for a year replaces hundreds of plastic bottles.
- e.g. At many weddings and temple feasts in South India, meals are still served on banana leaves.
- e.g. A cloth bag saves waste only if it is used many times, because making the bag took water, land and fuel.
- e.g. Mending a strap, a zip or a chappal keeps a whole thing out of the bin.
- **Zero-waste campaigners, such as Bea Johnson, and the makers of India's 2022 ban:** Recycling cannot keep up with what is made. The only sure answer is to stop making throw-away things and to bring back refill and reuse.
- **Researchers who measure a product's whole life, such as a Danish government study in 2018:** A cotton or paper bag takes more water, land and fuel to make than a thin plastic one. It is better only if it is used a great many times. Swapping plastic for another throw-away thing can make matters worse.
- **Street vendors and small shopkeepers:** Plastic is cheap, light and keeps food dry. Leaf plates and paper cost more and leak. They ask that affordable replacements come before fines.
### Small actions and big systems: a clean-up helps, and it is not enough alone · *debated*
**Definition:** A small action is something one person or family can do. A system is the set of rules, services and designs that shape what everyone does.

In October 2015 a lawyer named Afroz Shah and his 84-year-old neighbour began picking rubbish off Versova beach in Mumbai. People joined them, weekend after weekend. In under two years, volunteers removed more than 50 lakh kilograms of waste. In 2018 olive ridley turtle hatchlings were seen on Versova for the first time in about twenty years. That is what small actions can do. But the sea brought new plastic with every tide, because factories kept making it and drains kept carrying it. So a clean India needs both. People must carry their wrapper to a bin, sort, compost and not spit. Cities must collect, and companies must make less throw-away packing.
- e.g. Small actions a child can take: keep a wrapper in your pocket until you find a bin, rinse and sort, and carry your own bottle.
- e.g. A street that sweeps together once a month also learns who its neighbours are.
- e.g. Counting litter by brand name during a clean-up shows which companies' packing ends up on the ground.
- e.g. India's rules now require companies to collect back as much plastic packing as they sell.
- **Afroz Shah and other clean-up leaders:** Picking up waste with your own hands changes how you see it. People who have cleaned a beach stop littering and start asking their city and their shops for change.
- **Groups such as Break Free From Plastic, and researchers on plastic pollution:** Clean-ups treat the result and leave the cause. For decades, packaging companies paid for anti-litter campaigns that blamed the public while they made more throw-away packing. Real change comes from rules on what is made.
- **Researchers who study habits, such as Robert Cialdini:** Both matter, because each feeds the other. People follow what they see others doing, and governments act when enough people care.

## Mostly agreed
- Where people gain toilets, clean water and handwashing, fewer children fall ill and children grow taller. *How we know:* Dean Spears and colleagues compared many countries in 2013. Where more people went in the open, children were shorter on average for their age. Repeated gut infections stop the body from using its food well.
- Cholera and typhoid spread through water and food dirtied by faeces, and not through bad smells. *How we know:* John Snow mapped cholera deaths around one water pump in London in 1854. In 1884 Robert Koch found the cholera germ in Calcutta. In 1959 Sambhu Nath De showed how its poison causes the illness.
- Plastic in the sea breaks into smaller and smaller pieces and does not go away. *How we know:* Since Richard Thompson's team named 'microplastics' in 2004, they have been found in sea salt, in fish, in rain and in human blood. Their effect on health is still being studied.
- Burning rubbish in the open makes harmful smoke. *How we know:* Measurements of the air beside burning waste find fine dust and poisonous gases, especially when plastic burns. Indian law bans open burning of waste.

## How we found out
**John Snow, Filippo Pacini, Robert Koch, Sambhu Nath De, 1854 to 1959.** For centuries, people in many countries believed that illness came from bad air. The idea was called miasma. It fitted what they saw: the smelliest, dirtiest lanes had the most sickness. In 1854 cholera struck Soho, in London. A doctor named John Snow marked every death on a street map. The marks crowded round one water pump in Broad Street. Workers at a nearby brewery, who drank beer and not pump water, stayed well. Snow had the pump handle taken off. The leaders of the city did not accept his idea for years. In the same year, in Florence, Filippo Pacini saw the cholera germ under his microscope, and was ignored. In 1884, in Calcutta, Robert Koch found the same germ in a water tank used by sick families. The idea of bad air began to give way. In 1959, also in Calcutta, Sambhu Nath De showed how the germ's poison makes the gut pour out water. His work led to the simple salt-and-sugar drink that now saves lakhs of lives. He was never given a major prize.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that bad smells themselves caused disease. It was believable because dirty, smelly places really did have more illness. Snow's map and the later finding of the germ showed that the cause was in the water, carried from human waste.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Rural India became open defecation free in 2019.** (The Government of India; *debated*)  
  For: Records show more than ten crore toilets built, and every village declared itself open defecation free, backed by a national survey of toilet use.  
  Against: The r.i.c.e. survey of 2018 and the National Family Health Survey of 2019 to 2021 both found a real share of people still without a toilet in use. Counting toilets is easier than counting habits.
- **Burning waste to make electricity is the answer to India's rubbish mountains.** (Some city governments and waste-to-energy companies; *debated*)  
  For: Burning shrinks waste to a small amount of ash, needs little land and makes some electricity. Countries such as Japan and Sweden burn much of their waste.  
  Against: Indian waste is about half wet, so it burns poorly. Several Indian plants have shut or been fined for dirty smoke. Burning also destroys the paper and plastic that waste pickers earn from.
- **A river should have the rights of a person.** (The Uttarakhand High Court in 2017, the Māori of the Whanganui River, and many environmental lawyers; *debated*)  
  For: A river with rights can be defended in court by guardians, as a child can. It matches how many peoples have long thought of rivers.  
  Against: India's Supreme Court put the order on hold. Critics ask who pays when a river with legal personhood floods a town, and say strong pollution laws, properly enforced, would do more.
- **Litter is caused by careless people, so teaching the public is the cure.** (Anti-litter campaigns in many countries since the 1950s; *minority*)  
  For: Experiments show that reminders, clean surroundings and good examples do reduce littering.  
  Against: Historians such as Finis Dunaway have shown that packaging companies paid for many of these campaigns. The campaigns turned attention away from throw-away packing itself. Where there are no bins and no collection, careful people have nowhere to put waste.

## Roots
You have followed your family's waste out of the door and seen where it goes. Now we ask where 'my place' ends, and what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You sorted one day of your family's waste and asked where each thing goes next. Every single thing went to a place where somebody lives: a sorter's hands, a cow's mouth, a drain, a river. A fly from the heap at the corner can land on your plate. So one home cannot be clean alone. Clean is something a whole place does together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We speak of 'my house' and 'outside' as two separate places, and feel that pushing dirt over the line makes it gone. That line is drawn by us. Germs, water, flies and wrappers cross it without noticing.  
*Evidence:* In the dustbin count, not one item ended at the bin. Each had a next place and a next someone. The paths of germs from open waste to a mouth, by fingers, flies, fields, water and food, all cross from 'outside' to 'inside'.

- *who am i:* Your body takes in food and water and gives back peels, breath, sweat and waste every day. What leaves you becomes part of soil, water and other bodies. So you are not a closed box. You are a place that things pass through. Knowing this, you can ask where 'you' end.
- *what is life:* In a forest, one being's waste is always another being's food. A leaf feeds a fungus, a fungus feeds a beetle, a beetle feeds a bird. Life keeps going by passing things on. Composting a peel puts you back inside that passing-on.
- *what is this world:* The Earth has no outside bin. Everything ever thrown away is still here, changed or unchanged. A world where nothing can leave is a world where everything is shared, whether we mean to share it or not.

The lesson began with a house swept clean and dust outside the gate. It ends with the gate looking less like a border. The water in your tap passed through other homes upstream. The person who sorts your bag is touched by what you put in it. The crow, the cow and the river meet your waste before any machine does. Seeing these links plainly is the science of public health. It also points further. Some traditions go one step further. They say that everything, the house, the street and the river, arises from one awareness, and none of it stands apart. Science cannot test this step, and many people do not accept it. One strong objection is given below.

- **Thirukkural, verse 298, by Thiruvalluvar:** Outer cleanness is made by water. Inner cleanness is seen in truthfulness. (The lesson shows how water, soap and toilets make the outside clean. The verse adds that a person can do all this and still deceive. Telling ourselves the dust is 'gone' when it is only outside the gate is a small untruth of this kind.)
- **Atharva Veda, book 12, hymn 1 (the Hymn to the Earth), verse 35:** Whatever I dig from you, O Earth, may it quickly grow again. May I not pierce your vital parts or your heart. (The poet takes from the Earth and promises not to harm her. The lesson's science shows what that takes: returning wet waste to soil and keeping poisons out of water. The hymn speaks to the Earth as a someone. Science describes the Earth's soils and waters, and has no test for whether the Earth is a someone.)
- **The saying of the Whanganui Māori people of New Zealand, written into their country's law in 2017:** 'I am the river, and the river is me.' (The water in a child's body was river and rain not long ago, and will be again. That is measured science. The Māori saying takes it as a statement of who they are.)
- Science (settled): Everything is made of atoms, tiny pieces of matter far too small to see. The atoms in a peel, in soil, in a plant and in a child are the same atoms passed around. Matter is never used up. It only moves and changes form.
- Science (open): A few scientists and philosophers take seriously the question of whether rivers, forests or the Earth as a whole have awareness. Most do not.
- Other view, B. R. Ambedkar: Ambedkar warned that fine words about unity, and about the nobility of cleaning, have long been used while one group of people was made to do all the cleaning. On his view, what matters is not a feeling of oneness. What matters is law, schooling, machines and equal rights, so that no person is born into cleaning up after others.
- Practice, Follow one thing (4 min): Give your child one thing from today's waste, such as a peel or a clean wrapper. Say: 'Hold this in your hand. Take three slow breaths. Now, with your eyes closed, go backwards. Where was this before it reached our home? Who touched it? Go as far back as you can.' Wait. Then say: 'Now go forwards. Where will it be tomorrow? In a month? In a hundred years? Who will meet it?' Then let your child put it in the right bin.
- Ask: Where does your home end? At the door, the gate, the drain or the river?
- Ask: In a forest nothing is waste. What would have to change for that to be true in your home?
- Ask: If the person who sorts your waste could send your family one message, what do you think it would be?
- Ask: Is the Earth a thing we live on, or someone we live with? How would you decide?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Soil in a garden is soil, and the same soil on a bed is dirt. So is 'dirty' a property of the thing, or does it depend on where the thing is? (hint: Try it with a banana peel, a fallen leaf and a drop of water in three different places.)
- *deep:* A peel turns to soil, and the soil grows a plant. At what moment does the peel stop being waste? (hint: Think about who is eating the peel at each stage.)
- *deep:* If you sweep your house clean and push the dust onto the road, have you made the world cleaner, dirtier or the same? (hint: Ask where the edge of 'your place' really is, and who drew it.)
- *deep:* A forest has no dustbins and no sweepers, and is not buried in waste. What does a forest do that a city does not? (hint: Follow a fallen leaf in a forest and a wrapper in a city, step by step.)
- *big:* Is it fair that the people who make the least waste often do the most cleaning? What would a fair way look like? (hint: Think about who cleans in your home, your school and your street.)
- *big:* A government counts toilets built. Researchers count toilets used. Which count tells you more, and why might the two differ? (hint: Think of something in your home that is owned and not used.)
- *big:* Can a river be a person? What would the Ganga ask for, if a guardian could speak for her? (hint: List what a river needs to stay a river.)
- *big:* Nobody saw you drop it, and nobody will know. Is there still a reason not to litter? (hint: Think about who meets the wrapper next, and what the next passer-by learns from seeing it.)
- *big:* Who should do more for a clean India: each person, the city or the companies that make the packing? (hint: Try taking away each one in turn, and see what stops working.)

## Story: Mariyamma's Table
*A made-up story set in Bengaluru. Sahana, her father and Mariyamma are imagined. What is real: Bengaluru has dry waste collection centres where waste pickers sort material into many kinds, waste pickers there have held city identity cards since 2011, clear drinks bottles are recycled into polyester thread, shiny multi-layer wrappers have almost no buyers, and mixed waste injures the people who sort it.*

Sahana's teacher had given the class a strange task. 'Choose one thing you throw away this week,' she said. 'Find out where it goes.'

Sahana, who was eight, chose two things, because she could not decide. A juice bottle and a chips packet. On Saturday morning she and her father, Prakash, walked behind the collection auto to a shed at the end of the main road. A board said 'Dry Waste Collection Centre'.

Inside, a woman in a green apron stood at a long table. Sacks hung open all around her like the pockets of a huge coat. Her hands moved faster than Sahana could follow. A bottle flew into one sack. A milk packet into another. A flattened carton into a third.

'Mariyamma,' said Sahana's father, 'my daughter has homework. She wants to see where our garbage goes.'

Mariyamma did not stop. 'I do not have any garbage here,' she said. 'Come and look.'

She held up a clear bottle. 'This is PET. It goes to a factory that cuts it into flakes and spins the flakes into thread. In a few months somebody will wear it as a shirt.' She held up a milk packet. 'This is a different plastic, soft. It becomes pipes and sheets.' She tapped the cartons, the white paper, the newspapers, the tins. 'Each one has a buyer. Each one has a name. Twenty-eight kinds on this table. You call all of it garbage because you have finished with it. I have only begun.'

Sahana found her juice bottle in the pile. She knew it by the torn label. Mariyamma took it, twisted off the cap, dropped the cap in one sack and the bottle in another. 'Shirt,' she said.

'And this?' Sahana held out the chips packet.

Mariyamma turned it over. It was shiny silver inside and bright red outside. 'This one is two things stuck together, plastic and metal, so thin that nobody can pull them apart. No factory wants it. I pick up hundreds of these every day and nobody pays me one rupee for them.' She dropped it into a bin by itself. 'It will be burnt in a cement factory, or it will sit in a dump longer than you will live.'

Sahana looked at the packet in the bin. It had held chips for about ten minutes.

The auto tipped out a new load. One bag split open, and Sahana smelt it before she saw it. Rice and sambar, soaked into newspapers, wrapped round a broken tube light.

Mariyamma's hands stopped for the first time. She pulled on a second pair of gloves. 'This bag is from a house that does not sort,' she said. 'The paper is ruined. The food is ruined. And the glass is waiting for my fingers.' She showed Sahana a pale scar across her palm.

Sahana's father shifted his feet. 'We pay the collection fee every month,' he said. 'Is sorting not the centre's job?'

'Sorting is my job, sir,' said Mariyamma. 'I am good at it. But it is your waste. When a bag comes rinsed and dry, it is like a letter from that house. It says: I know a person will open this. When a bag comes like this one...' She lifted the dripping paper. 'This letter says: I do not know you are there.'

Outside, a crow was pulling a piece of roti from the gutter. A cow nosed at a plastic bag for the peels inside.

'For years the police chased us from the bins as thieves,' Mariyamma said. She touched the card pinned to her apron. It had her photo and the city's stamp. 'Now this says I work for the city. I would like gloves that last, and a pension. But I will tell you what I would like most.' She looked at Sahana. 'I would like that packet not to be made.'

On the way home Sahana was quiet. At the gate she stopped. 'Appa, when does a thing become garbage?'

Her father thought about it. 'When we throw it, I suppose.'

'No,' said Sahana slowly. 'The bottle is not garbage. Mariyamma has a name for it. The peel is not garbage to the cow. I think a thing is only garbage when nobody anywhere knows what to do with it next.' She frowned. 'Like the chips packet. So who made it garbage? Me, or the people who made the packet?'

Her father opened his mouth, and found that he did not know.

**For the parent:** Mariyamma has a name and a buyer for almost everything on her table. A thing becomes waste only when nobody knows its next use, and that is decided partly at home and partly where the thing is designed.

- Ask: Mariyamma said 'I do not have any garbage here.' What did she mean?
- Ask: She called a sorted bag 'a letter' from a house. What does your family's bag say?
- Ask: Sahana's father said sorting is the centre's job because he pays a fee. Mariyamma said it is his waste. Who do you agree with, and why?
- Ask: Who made the chips packet into garbage: Sahana, the company, or someone else?

### g1 · Three Bins: a sorting card game with arguments (25 min)
- **rules:** There are 36 cards. Each has an item on the front and the answer on the back. Keep the answer side hidden.; On your turn, take the top card and read the front aloud. ## Experiments
### e1 · The dustbin count (50 min, 2 days)
- **question:** What is one day of our family's waste made of, and where does each kind go next?
- **predict:** Before you open anything, guess. Out of all the waste, how much will be wet, how much dry and how much harmful? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The family collects one whole day of waste in the same bucket, from waking to bedtime. Every item is counted by the same rule. Anything bigger than a thumb counts as one, and a pile of tiny things such as tea leaves counts as one. One day can be unusual, so if you can, repeat the count on a second ordinary day and compare.
- **safety:** Wear gloves or use kitchen tongs and shoes. Do the sorting outdoors, on a balcony or on a bathroom floor over newspaper, never in the kitchen. A grown-up alone handles glass, blades, medicines, batteries, nappies, pads and bandages, and the child counts them from the closed bag without touching. Wash hands and forearms with soap for 20 seconds afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then write three sentences: what I thought, what I counted, and why my guess was right or wrong. Draw a path for each kind of waste from the bin to its next place, with a small picture of who is there.
- **results table:** Kind of waste | My guess (big, medium or small heap) | Items counted | Weight in grams, if you have a scale | Where it goes next | Who meets it there
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess the shares, then start the covered bucket and fill it for one whole day. (10 min); day 2: Sort the waste into three heaps with gloves, count each heap, find where each kind goes next and compare with your guess. (40 min)
- **e1s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Give your child three circles of the same size drawn on paper. Ask your child to colour each circle to show how much of the day's waste will be wet, dry and harmful.  
  *Parent says:* Think about everything we throw away today. How much do you think will be food and peels, how much paper and plastic, and how much dangerous stuff? Colour in your guess, and tell me why.  
  *Child mode:* Colour in your guess for wet, dry and harmful. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: Which kind do you think will be biggest, and why? (good: Wet, because we cook and eat every day and peel things.; Dry, because of packets, bottles and paper.; Any answer with a reason from the family's own day is a good answer.)
  - Note: Do not give the real shares. A wrong guess is the best start. Keep the drawing to compare at the end.
- **e1s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Set a bucket with a lid or a plate over it by the kitchen. Tell the whole family that today everything goes into it as usual. Anything sharp, medical or dirty goes into the small marked bag, handled by a grown-up only.  
  *Parent says:* From now till bedtime, everything we throw away goes in this bucket. Sharp, medical and nappy waste goes in the small bag, and a grown-up puts it there.  
  *Child mode:* Put your waste in the bucket today. Never put sharp things in it yourself.
  - Note: In hot weather, cover the bucket and keep it away from the food shelf, because wet waste smells and brings flies within hours. If the family makes very little waste, keep the bucket for a second day. A small heap is a real result.
  - Wait: Leave the bucket covered for one day, from waking to bedtime.
- **e1s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* The next morning, spread 4 or 5 sheets of newspaper on a balcony, terrace or bathroom floor. Put on gloves, or pick up tongs. Open the marked bag only outdoors and count the items from the closed top without opening the sealed packets.  
  *Parent says:* Today we find out the truth about our bin. Gloves on first, and nothing is touched with bare hands.  
  *Child mode:* Put on gloves or pick up tongs. Wait for the grown-up before opening anything.
  - Note: If anyone has dust allergy or asthma, a cloth mask helps. Do not sort in the kitchen. If the bucket smells strongly, sort quickly and cover it again.
- **e1s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tip the bucket onto the newspaper. Using gloves or tongs, lift each item to one of three plates or sheets: wet, dry and harmful. Some items are stuck together, such as a leaf plate with food on it. Put these in the kind that most of the item is. Leave glass, blades, medicine, nappies, pads and bandages to the grown-up.  
  *Parent says:* Pick up one thing at a time. Say what it is, say where it goes, and put it down. If you are not sure, say both bins and why.  
  *Child mode:* Lift one thing at a time with tongs. Say the bin before you put it down.
  - Ask: Which item was hardest to place, and why? (good: Items made of two things stuck together, such as a shiny chips packet or a leaf plate with food on it.; Items that were wet and dry at once, such as a curd cup with curd in it.)
  - Note: Mixed items are the real finding. Do not hurry the child to the right answer. Say 'Both are possible. What would each choice cause?' Allow only items you can see clearly. If glass or something sharp comes up, stop and let a grown-up pick it up.
- **e1s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the items in each heap and write the numbers in the table. If you have a scale, weigh each heap on its plate, then take away the plate's weight. Wrap up the harmful heap again. Compare the heaps by eye: which one is biggest, and by how much?  
  *Parent says:* Count each heap by tens, and write the number. Is it what you guessed?  
  *Child mode:* Count the items in each heap. Write each number in the table.
  - Note: Do not worry if your counts are very different from the national picture of about half wet. Cities and homes are different, and so are the days. If you use a scale, weigh each empty plate first, then subtract it.
  - Check: A bucket holds 20 wet items, 12 dry items and 3 harmful items. What share is wet? → **About half**
- **e1s6 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each heap, find out where it goes next and who meets it there. Ask the family, the person who collects waste, or the housing society. If nobody knows, use the usual route and write that nobody here knew.  
  *Parent says:* Where does this heap go when it leaves our door? Who touches it next? Draw a line to each place.  
  *Child mode:* Draw an arrow from each heap to the next place. Draw who is waiting there.
  - Ask: Who meets your wet waste next, and who meets your dry waste? (good: Wet: a compost pit, a gas plant, a cow or crow, or a dump. Dry: a waste picker, a dealer and a factory.; If a heap goes to a dump, say that nobody here separates it, and ask why.)
  - Note: The usual routes are these. Wet waste goes to a compost pit or a gas plant, or to a dump. Dry waste goes to a waste picker, a dealer and a factory. Harmful waste goes to a sealed bag and a special collection point. Your own town may send it all to one place. That is also a real finding.
- **e1s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put everything back in the right bins. Wash your hands and forearms with soap for 20 seconds. Then compare the real counts with the guess drawing from the beginning and write three sentences in the record.  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess close? What would you change in how our family throws things away tomorrow?  
  *Child mode:* Wash your hands with soap. Write what you thought, what you counted and why.
  - Note: What the test cannot tell us: one day can be an odd day, such as a festival or a guest. It also does not show what happens once the truck takes the waste away. Ask the child how they would find out.
  - Think: If we change one thing, which heap will shrink?
### e2 · Water or soap? The turmeric hand test (25 min)
- **question:** Does washing hands with plain water get as much off as washing with soap?
- **predict:** Guess before you wash. Will plain water or soap leave less yellow on your hands, or will they be the same? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Both palms get the same amount of turmeric and oil. Each hand is washed for the same 20 seconds, with the same amount of water. Only the soap changes. Because one hand may rub harder than the other, the experiment is done twice, with the hands swapped the second time. Each wash is checked by wiping on a clean white tissue.
- **record:** Fill in the table for both tries. Then write what you thought, what you saw, and why you think soap did what it did. Add one sentence about what turmeric cannot tell you about real germs.
- **results table:** Try | Which hand | Washed with | Yellow left on the tissue (none, a little, a lot) | Yellow left under the nails or in the lines of the palm (none, a little, a lot)
- **e2s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put out a pinch of turmeric, a few drops of oil, soap, tissues, water and a clock. Wear old clothes, because turmeric stains. Keep turmeric away from eyes.  
  *Parent says:* Today the turmeric is pretending to be germs. Our job is to find which way of washing takes the most away.  
  *Child mode:* Put on old clothes. Lay everything out on the table.
  - Note: If you have no turmeric, use a drop of washable poster paint instead. Do the test over a sink or a bowl so that spills are easy to clean.
- **e2s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mix a pinch of turmeric (about a quarter of a teaspoon) with 3 or 4 drops of oil to make a paste. Rub half on each palm, spreading it into the lines of the hand and a little under the nails.  
  *Parent says:* Rub the yellow paste on both hands. It is the same on each hand.  
  *Child mode:* Rub the same yellow paste on both palms.
  - Ask: Which will take off more yellow, plain water or soap? Why? (good: Soap, because it grips the oil and lifts it away.; Plain water, because water washes things off. Both are fair guesses. We will test them.)
  - Note: Do not say which is right. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write them down.
- **e2s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wash the left hand with plain water only for 20 seconds, rubbing like normal. Wash the right hand with soap for 20 seconds, rubbing like normal, then rinse it with the same amount of water. Wipe each hand on its own white tissue and mark the tissues Water and Soap.  
  *Parent says:* Rub for 20 seconds on each hand. Count slowly. Then wipe each hand on its own tissue.  
  *Child mode:* Wash one hand with water and one with soap. Count to 20. Wipe each hand on a tissue.
  - Note: A 20-second wash is about the time it takes to sing a short song twice. Use a timer so that both times match.
- **e2s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the two tissues and at both hands, especially the nails and the lines of the palms. Write the results in the table. Then repeat with the hands swapped: soap on the left hand and water on the right.  
  *Parent says:* Which tissue is more yellow? Which hand still has yellow in the lines? Now swap hands and do it again, so that it is a fair test.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the tissues. Which one is more yellow? Swap hands and try again.
  - Note: Normally the soap tissue is clearly cleaner. If both tissues look the same, make the paste thicker with a little more oil. Turmeric on a dry hand simply falls off.
  - Check: Why do we swap hands in the second try? → **One hand might rub harder than the other.**
- **e2s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Extra step. Sprinkle black pepper on a plate of water so that the pepper floats. Touch the middle with a clean finger and watch. Then touch the same place with a finger that has a little soap on it.  
  *Parent says:* Pepper floats like tiny germs on water. First touch with a dry finger. Now touch with a soapy finger. What happened?  
  *Child mode:* Touch the pepper water with a plain finger. Then touch it with a soapy finger. What do you see?
  - Ask: What did the soapy finger do to the pepper? (good: The pepper rushed to the edges of the plate.; The soap changed how the water holds together, and the pepper was pushed away.)
  - Note: A plain finger moves the pepper only a little. The soapy finger sends the pepper skating to the edge. Soap works on the film of the water and on oil. It is why soap lifts germs and oil off skin, while plain water only rinses some of them. The turmeric is not a germ, so this test cannot show how many real germs are left. It shows only that soap helps. Ask the child what else they would test.
- **e2s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wash the hands properly with soap and clean the table. Put the tissues in the dry bin and wash the turmeric from any clothes soon.  
  *Parent says:* Now wash with soap, like you would after the toilet, and in the way you would before eating.  
  *Child mode:* Wash your hands with soap for 20 seconds.
  - Note: Remind the child of the moments when washing counts most: after the toilet, before eating, and after touching waste.

### a1 · Swept clean? Follow the dust (15 min)

  *Child mode:* Draw two paths. Write which way is cleaner.

### a4 · A ten-minute clean-up of one corner, and a count by kind (30 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up goes along and chooses the place. Wear gloves and closed shoes. Pick up only dry wrappers, paper, plastic and cans. Never pick up glass, needles, syringes, blades, medicine, nappies, pads, bandages, dead animals, anything wet from a toilet or anything you are unsure of. Show these to the grown-up and leave them. Stay away from roads, water and slopes.
- **record:** Draw the corner before and after. Make a tally by kind: bottle, wrapper, cup, bag, paper and other. Write the three kinds you found most of, and the brand names that came up most often.
- **a4s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a small corner, about the size of a classroom, in a place where you have permission. It could be your lane, the edge of your school, a park or a corridor. Draw how it looks before you start. Show the child the safety rules and the things never to touch.  
  *Parent says:* We will pick up a small corner for ten minutes. Gloves on, shoes on, and ask me before you pick up anything you are not sure of.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the corner. Put on gloves. Listen to the rules.
  - Note: Ask the housing society, school or neighbours first if it is not your own space. If you have no corner nearby, use a school corridor or your building's compound. Check the corner yourself first for glass and sharp things.
- **a4s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Set a timer for ten minutes. Ask your child to pick up safe dry litter with tongs or gloves. Hold the bag together. If the child finds glass, a needle, a syringe or anything wet, leave it and tell a grown-up.  
  *Parent says:* Use the tongs. Put each thing in the bag. Stop and call me if it is sharp, wet, medical or you are not sure.  
  *Child mode:* Pick up dry litter with tongs. Stop and call a grown-up if it is sharp or wet.
  - Note: Stay close to the child. Do not take away the child's wish to help, but keep the rules firm. Put sharp or dirty things aside yourself with a stick or a pair of tongs.
- **a4s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tip the bag onto newspaper. Sort by kind into piles, and make a tally of each kind. Read the brand names on the labels and make a tally of the most common ones. Then sort what can be recycled into the dry bin.  
  *Parent says:* What did we find the most of? Which names did we see the most? Whose packet is it?  
  *Child mode:* Sort the litter into kinds. Make a tally of each.
  - Ask: Which kind did you find the most of, and why do you think so? (good: Wrappers and bottles, because most things come in them and people drop them while eating or walking.; A kind that came from one brand or one shop nearby.)
  - Note: Counting brand names shows which packaging ends up on the ground. It can lead to a letter to the company or the shop.
- **a4s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the corner again. Wash hands and forearms with soap for 20 seconds. Ask your child whether the corner will stay clean, and what would help it stay clean.  
  *Parent says:* Will this corner stay clean? What would help? A bin? A sign? A neighbour?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the corner after the clean-up. Wash your hands with soap.
  - Note: Cleaning one corner is useful. Keeping it clean needs a bin, regular collection or neighbours who look after it. The lesson explains why.
  - Think: One person cleaned this corner today. What would it take to keep it clean tomorrow?
### a5 · An old T-shirt becomes a carry bag, with no sewing (30 min)
- **safety:** The grown-up does all the cutting with scissors. The child marks, ties and tests.
- **record:** Draw your bag. Write how many knots you tied, how much weight it held, and what you would change.
- **a5s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the T-shirt flat. Use chalk to draw a wide curve across each side of the neck so that it becomes a scoop. Mark a line across the bottom, 8 centimetres from the hem, and strips 3 centimetres wide along it from side to side.  
  *Parent says:* This T-shirt is going to be a bag. We mark where the grown-up will cut.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the T-shirt flat. Draw the lines with chalk.
  - Note: Any cotton T-shirt works. Knit cloth does not fray, so no hem is needed.
- **a5s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts off the sleeves along the seams and along the neck curve. Then the grown-up cuts the bottom strips, through both layers, up to the 8 centimetre line.  
  *Parent says:* The grown-up cuts. You hold the cloth flat.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the shirt flat while the grown-up cuts.
  - Note: Cut through both layers at once so that front and back strips match. Cut the strips up to, and not past, the line.
- **a5s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tie each front strip to the strip behind it in a double knot, working from one side to the other. Pull each knot tight. Then tie each strip to the nearest strip of the next pair, to close the small holes. Pull the bottom of the bag a little wide and check that no gap is left.  
  *Parent says:* Tie a front strip to the one behind it. Tie it twice. Pull it tight. Go along the whole bottom.  
  *Child mode:* Tie each pair of strips twice. Pull tight. Go from side to side.
  - Ask: Why do we tie each pair twice? (good: One knot can slip under weight. A double knot holds.; Any answer about a knot coming loose is a good answer.)
  - Note: Check for gaps. A gap smaller than a rice grain holds, but bigger gaps spill lentils.
- **a5s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the T-shirt inside out so the knots are inside, and test the bag with a kilogram of rice, dal or books. Lift it by the straps.  
  *Parent says:* Put in the rice. Lift the bag. Does it hold?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bag with rice. Lift it. Does it hold?
  - Note: If the bag leaks, tie another knot over the gap. A small gap is easy to mend.
  - Check: The bag leaks rice at the corners. What is the best fix? → **Tie each corner strip a second time.**
- **a5s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to hang the bag by the door. Then ask how many thin carry bags it could replace in a year.  
  *Parent says:* Where will we keep it so that it goes with us every time?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the bag by the door.
  - Think: A cloth bag only helps if it goes to the market every time. What would make you remember?

### p1 · Two peels, two futures: a jar with air and a jar without (75 min, 21 days)
- **safety:** Do not close either lid tightly, because gas can build up. Do not put your nose close to the jars. Wave the smell towards you with your hand. If you see black or green mould, do not open the jar. Ask a grown-up to empty it outdoors. Wear gloves to empty the jars and wash hands with soap afterwards.
- **record:** Keep a table with the day, what each jar looks like, how it smells and the length of the biggest peel in centimetres. Draw each jar on days 1, 7 and 21. Then write three sentences: what I thought, what I saw and why.
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up jar A with soil, peels and dry paper under a cloth, and jar B with the same peels pressed under water. (30 min); day 3: Look at both jars from the outside. Write the changes. (5 min); day 7: Look at both jars, waft the smell gently, and write what you notice. (10 min); day 14: Look, smell and measure the largest piece in each jar. (10 min); day 21: Compare the jars, then empty them safely and wash your hands. (20 min)
- **p1s1 (30 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Label two jars A and B. Cut 12 pieces of peel into thumb-sized pieces and put 6 in each jar. For jar A, put in a layer of soil, then 6 peels, then a handful of torn dry paper. Sprinkle a little water so that the jar is damp but not wet. Cover with cloth and a rubber band. For jar B, press the other 6 peels to the bottom and fill the jar with water to the top. Rest the lid loosely on top. Put both jars together on a shelf out of the sun.  
  *Parent says:* These two jars hold the same peels. One has air and soil. One has no air. What do you think will happen to each peel?  
  *Child mode:* Put the same peels in both jars. Add soil and paper to A. Add water to B.
  - Ask: What do you think will happen in jar A and jar B, and why? (good: In A, the peels will shrink, go dark and turn to soil, because tiny living beings eat them with air.; In B, the water will smell and look cloudy, because different bacteria work without air.)
  - Note: Use a warm shelf, about 25 to 35 degrees. In cold weather, things go slower, and it may take 10 extra days. Keep both jars in the same place so the test is fair. It is not a perfect test, because jar A also has soil and paper. It is a good first look at air against no air.
  - Wait: Leave the jars for two days.
- **p1s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at both jars from the outside. Draw them and write what you see. Do not open them.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely. What has changed in A? What has changed in B? How is the water?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both jars. Draw what you see. Do not open them.
  - Note: By day 3, jar B often has small bubbles, cloudy water and some froth. Jar A may have some white threads. Both are normal.
  - Wait: Leave the jars for four days, until day 7.
- **p1s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look and measure again. Wave the smell towards you with a hand from about an arm's length from each jar. Write one word for each smell, such as earthy, sour, sweet or none. Measure the largest peel in jar A without opening, through the plastic.  
  *Parent says:* Smell it from far away, with your hand like a fan. Which smells like soil? Which smells sour?  
  *Child mode:* Wave the smell towards you. Choose one word for each smell.
  - Note: A white fuzz on the peels in A is fungus, a normal part of rotting. Black or green mould in large patches means the damp is too high. Ask a grown-up to empty it.
  - Wait: Leave the jars for another week.
- **p1s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look, smell and measure the largest piece in each jar. Write what is different from day 7.  
  *Parent says:* What is smaller? What changed colour? Which jar smells stronger, and why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look, smell and measure. Write what changed.
  - Ask: Which jar smells stronger now, and why? (good: Jar B smells stronger, sour or like rotten eggs, because bacteria that live without air make smelly gases.; Jar A smells more like soil, because bacteria with air make little smell.)
  - Wait: Leave the jars for one more week.
- **p1s5 (20 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* With gloves on, empty jar A onto soil in a pot or into the wet waste bin. A grown-up pours jar B into a toilet or onto soil far from the house. The peels go in the wet bin. Wash the jars. Compare the two drawings and write three sentences.  
  *Parent says:* Now compare jar A and jar B. Which one turned into soil? Which one was a smelly mess? What is the difference?  
  *Child mode:* Wash your hands with soap. Write three sentences about what you found.
  - Ask: What do jars A and B tell you about a dump with no air? (good: In a dump with no air, wet waste rots in a smelly way and gives off gases such as methane. Jar B showed this in a small way.; A heap with air becomes soil, as in jar A.)
  - Note: What the test cannot tell us: a jar is not a real dump or a real compost heap. Real heaps are hotter, bigger and have worms. Ask the child how they would change the test to be closer to the real thing.

## Worksheets
### Sorting, germs and clean (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (truefalse) A place that looks neat is always clean.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A neat lane can have an open drain beside the hand pump. Clean means harmful things cannot reach mouths, water and food.
2. (sort) Sort each item into the right bin.  
   **Answer:** leftover idli → Wet; a cardboard box → Dry; a wrapped razor blade → Harmful; dried marigolds → Wet; a rinsed glass bottle → Dry; old tablets → Harmful. *Why:* Food and flowers rot, so they are wet. Cardboard and glass can be recycled, so they are dry. Blades and medicine can hurt people or poison soil, so they are kept apart.
3. (mcq) Why is a banana peel in the dry bin a problem?  
   Options: It wets and spoils the paper and plastic / It makes the bin too heavy / It turns the plastic yellow  
   **Answer:** It wets and spoils the paper and plastic. *Why:* A wet peel soaks paper, and dirty plastic is hard to sell. If you chose heavy, think about what the buyer sees: a sack that cannot be used.
4. (fill) Wet waste soaks and spoils dry waste, so homes keep wet, dry and ____ waste in three separate bins.  
   **Answer:** ['harmful']. *Why:* Wet, dry and harmful. Each is handled in a different way.
5. (match) Match each helper or thing to what it does for a compost heap.  
   **Answer:** bacteria and fungi → break the peels into compost; earthworms → mix air into the soil; methane → a gas that burns and traps heat; dry leaves in the heap → let air in. *Why:* Compost is made by living beings eating peels with air. Methane is what you get instead when there is no air.
6. (order) Put these in order, first to last: what happens to a peel in a compost pot.  
   **Answer:** ['A chopped peel is put in a pot with dry leaves', 'Bacteria and fungi start to eat the peel', 'The heap becomes warm inside', 'Dark, crumbly compost is left']. *Why:* The peel is food first, then the eaters arrive, they make warmth as they work, and what is left is compost.
7. (truefalse) Cholera spreads because of bad smells.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. People once blamed smells because dirty lanes both smelled and had illness. John Snow showed that the cause was in the water, carried from human waste.
8. (draw) Draw how germs get from a field to a child's mouth. Mark three places to block them.  
   **Answer:** A field, then arrows along two or more paths. For example flies to food, rain to a well to a glass, or feet and fingers to a mouth. Blocks are marked with a toilet, a covered plate, safe water or soap.. *Why:* There are several paths and each can be blocked. A toilet stops the first step, covering food stops flies, and soap stops fingers.
9. (mcq) A fly lands on an open heap and then on food. What stops this?  
   Options: Covering food and keeping heaps closed / Washing the floor / Opening a window  
   **Answer:** Covering food and keeping heaps closed. *Why:* The germs ride on the fly's legs from the heap to the food. Covering food blocks that path. A clean floor or an open window does not.
10. (write) Why does a twin-pit toilet have two pits and not one?  
   **Answer:** While one pit fills and is used, the other rests for a year or two. In the resting pit, bacteria turn the waste into safe, dry manure. Then the pipe is turned back.. *Why:* A strong answer says that one pit is used while the other rests, and that resting is what makes the waste safe.
11. (truefalse) A programme that builds toilets has also made sure that people use them.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A toilet can be built and still not used. That is why researchers count who uses toilets, as well as how many were built.
12. (count) A family of four makes about 2 kilograms of waste a day. About how many kilograms in 30 days?  
   **Answer:** 60. *Why:* 2 kilograms × 30 days = 60 kilograms.
13. (write) Name two living beings who are not humans and who help clear waste. Say what each one does.  
   **Answer:** Examples: bacteria and fungi rot peels into compost, vultures clear dead animals, crows pick up scraps, ants carry away crumbs, dung beetles bury dung.. *Why:* Many beings do the cleaning. Their work is also part of keeping places clean.
14. (perspective) A rinsed, sorted bag takes five minutes more each day. Two views on who should do that work.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says that sorting only helps if the truck keeps the bags apart. So the resident and the city both have a part.. *Why:* Both views are right in part. The resident is right that mixing in the truck cancels the sorting. The cooperative is right that sorting at home is what makes recycling possible.
15. (mcq) Which small action keeps the most waste from ever being made?  
   Options: Refusing a thing you do not need / Recycling it after one use / Burning it in the street  
   **Answer:** Refusing a thing you do not need. *Why:* If you refuse a thing, it never has to be made, carried, sorted or buried. Recycling needs fuel, water and work. Burning plastic makes harmful smoke.
16. (draw) Draw one corner of your street. Mark where you would put a bin, and say why you chose that place.  
   **Answer:** A street corner with a bin near where people wait or eat. A reason, such as people drop wrappers while they wait. The bin is clear of the drain.. *Why:* People use a bin if it is close to where waste is made. A bin in a place where nobody passes does not help.
### Counts, paths and who cleans (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A glass of clear-looking water is tested and found to hold cholera germs. What does that show?  
   Options: Clear water can still be unsafe to drink / Germs are always visible / All germs cause illness  
   **Answer:** Clear water can still be unsafe to drink. *Why:* Looking clean is not being clean. Cholera germs are too small to see. Not all germs cause illness, because most are harmless, but this test found one that does.
2. (truefalse) A forest floor covered in fallen leaves is dirty, because it is untidy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The leaves feed the soil, and nothing harmful travels from them to a mouth. Untidy and unclean are different things.
3. (fill) India's towns and cities make 1.6 lakh tonnes of waste a day. Half is wet, which is ____ thousand tonnes.  
   **Answer:** ['80']. *Why:* 1.6 lakh is 160 thousand. Half of 160 thousand is 80 thousand tonnes.
4. (match) Match each mix-up to what it spoils.  
   **Answer:** leftover food in a bag of paper → the paper cannot be recycled; broken glass in a mixed bag → a sorter's hands are cut; a battery in the wet bin → poison reaches the compost; curd in a plastic cup → the whole sack of plastic. *Why:* Mixing waste moves a problem from one heap to all of them, and the next person pays for it.
5. (mcq) Two heaps get the same peels. One is turned every week. One is packed tight. Which composts faster?  
   Options: The turned heap, because it gets more air / The packed heap, because it is warmer / Both take the same time  
   **Answer:** The turned heap, because it gets more air. *Why:* Bacteria and fungi that eat with air work faster. Turning lets air in. A packed heap runs short of air and turns sour.
6. (write) A gobar gas plant also rots dung without air. Why is that useful there, and a problem in a dump?  
   **Answer:** In a closed tank, the methane is caught and burned for cooking. In a dump, the methane escapes, traps heat and can start fires.. *Why:* The same airless rotting gives fuel when it is captured and harm when it is not. The difference is whether someone catches the gas.
7. (order) Put these in order, first to last: a path from open faeces to an ill child.  
   **Answer:** ['Faeces is left in the open', 'A fly lands on the faeces', 'The fly lands on food', 'A child eats the food', 'The child gets diarrhoea']. *Why:* Each step hands the germs on. If you remove any step, such as a toilet at the start or a cover on the food, the chain breaks.
8. (truefalse) Soap blocks the path from fingers to mouth, and it does not block the path through unsafe water.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Soap works on the germs on hands. Safe water, a toilet and covered food block the other paths.
9. (count) A flush toilet uses 10 litres a flush. A pour-flush pan uses 2 litres. How many litres do 5 pour-flushes save?  
   **Answer:** 40. *Why:* Each flush saves 10 − 2 = 8 litres. 8 × 5 = 40 litres.
10. (odd) Three of these are ways germs reach a mouth. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Fingers / Flies / Rain washing into a well / A closed twin-pit toilet  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A closed twin-pit toilet keeps the waste in a pit. Fingers, flies and well water are paths that carry germs.
11. (mcq) A government counts toilets built, and researchers count toilets used. Why might the numbers differ?  
   Options: A toilet can exist and still not be used / One of the counts must be lying / Toilets vanish when counted  
   **Answer:** A toilet can exist and still not be used. *Why:* Both can be right. A toilet with no water, or with a pit the family fears to empty, may be built and not used.
12. (write) Give one change the Swachh Bharat Mission made, and one thing people still argue about.  
   **Answer:** Changes: many more home toilets, and cleanliness became a talked-about subject. Arguments: whether the toilets are used, whether the numbers were counted fairly, and what happens to the waste.. *Why:* A good answer gives one real change and one real argument.
13. (perspective) Two views of a village that has declared itself open defecation free.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer asks for both counts. It also asks why some families do not use their toilet, for example no water, a full pit or old habits.. *Why:* Counting toilets is easy and counting use is harder. Both give true information, and only use tells you whether germs still reach the open.
14. (write) Who cleaned your street or building today? Name two people and one other being, and say what each did.  
   **Answer:** A sweeper, a waste collector, a cleaner, and a crow, a cow, ants or bacteria. Each with the work they did, such as sweeping, lifting bags, eating scraps or breaking down peels.. *Why:* Cleaning is done by many hands and by other beings. Noticing them is the first step to thanking them.
15. (mcq) A shop stops giving thin bags. Which result shows that this works?  
   Options: Fewer bags found in drains and at clean-ups / More shops selling thin bags / More bags in the dry bin  
   **Answer:** Fewer bags found in drains and at clean-ups. *Why:* A good test counts what we care about: bags that end up in drains and on the ground. Counting bags in a bin only shows where they went.
16. (draw) For one litter problem, draw a small action by a person and a bigger change by a city.  
   **Answer:** A child carrying a wrapper to a bin. A city adding bins and a daily collection. A company making packaging that can be recycled.. *Why:* A clean place needs both. Small actions change habits, and bigger changes change what is easy.
### Faulty reasoning and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (truefalse) Tipping dust outside the gate makes the whole neighbourhood cleaner.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The dust has only moved. A neighbour's house, a drain or a person's lungs now meets it.
2. (mcq) A town's truck tips sorted wet and dry bags together into one pile. What is most likely?  
   Options: Mixing in the truck spoils both heaps / Nothing, because trucks sort by themselves / The wet waste becomes dry  
   **Answer:** Mixing in the truck spoils both heaps. *Why:* Sorting at home only helps if the heaps stay apart. A mixed pile has the same problem as a mixed bin. Wet waste soaks the dry waste, so both are spoilt.
3. (truefalse) A peel buried deep in a dump rots in the same way as in a compost pit.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In a pit with air, bacteria and fungi make compost. In a dump with no air, other bacteria make methane.
4. (write) Methane is both a danger and a fuel. How can it be both?  
   **Answer:** It burns easily, so it can start fires in a dump and can run a stove when it is caught. It also traps heat in the air, which harms the climate when it escapes.. *Why:* The same gas is harmful when it escapes and useful when it is captured, so what matters is whether anyone captures it.
5. (fill) India makes about 72,000 million litres of sewage a day. If one-third is treated, ____ million litres are not.  
   **Answer:** ['48000', '48,000']. *Why:* One-third of 72,000 is 24,000. 72,000 − 24,000 = 48,000 million litres. The real figure is less than a third treated, so the untreated amount is even larger.
6. (mcq) Why does heavy rain make open defecation uphill of a well more dangerous?  
   Options: Rain washes germs along the ground into the well / Rain makes germs grow wings / Rain kills the germs  
   **Answer:** Rain washes germs along the ground into the well. *Why:* Water carries things downhill. A well downhill of a field can be spoilt by one heavy shower. Rain does not kill germs.
7. (sort) Sort each way of checking as a count of toilets built or toilets used.  
   **Answer:** a list of toilets in each village → Toilets built; asking each person where they went this morning → Toilets used; watching at dawn who walks to a field → Toilets used; counting concrete rings that were delivered → Toilets built. *Why:* A count of toilets built tells us about money and work. A count of use tells us about what people really do.
8. (match) Match each cleaner to what they clear.  
   **Answer:** vultures → dead cattle; bacteria → peels and dung; waste pickers → bottles and cardboard; Kerala's Haritha Karma Sena → sorted waste from homes. *Why:* People and other living beings do the cleaning, and each works on a different kind of waste.
9. (write) Manual scavenging has been against the law since 1993, yet it still happens. What else is needed to end it?  
   **Answer:** Safe machines, so that nobody has to go down a sewer. Fair pay and safety gear for sanitation workers. Other jobs open to everyone. Respect, so that the work is no longer tied to birth.. *Why:* A law says what must not happen. Ending it also needs machines, fair pay and an end to the idea that some people must do dirty work because of their birth.
10. (perspective) Two views on cleaning toilets, from the early 1900s and 1940s.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer gives a reason from one view, or uses both. For example, share cleaning in schools and homes, and also build machines and open other jobs.. *Why:* Both men wanted to end the shame. Gandhi stressed sharing the work. Ambedkar stressed ending the link between work and birth.
11. (mcq) A neighbour says: 'One wrapper makes no difference.' Which finding answers this?  
   Options: A littered place leads more people to litter / Wrappers rot in a day / Wind carries every wrapper away  
   **Answer:** A littered place leads more people to litter. *Why:* Researchers found that people dropped more litter where there was already litter. One wrapper changes what the next person does.
12. (truefalse) Recycling is always better than reusing a thing as it is.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Recycling needs fuel or electricity, water and work. Using a thing again as it is, or refusing it, avoids all of that.
13. (draw) Draw the path of a wrapper from a lane to the sea. Mark two places where it could be stopped.  
   **Answer:** Lane, then drain, then bigger drain, stream, river, sea. Stops marked, for example a bin at the lane, a grill or trap at the drain, a treatment plant before the river.. *Why:* A wrapper follows the water. The earlier it is stopped, the fewer beings meet it.
14. (write) Soil in a garden is soil. On a bed, it is dirt. Is 'dirty' in the thing or in the place?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says that dirt is about where a thing is and what it can do there. Soil in a garden feeds plants. Soil on a bed gets onto skin and food. It may also say that germs are harmful in most places.. *Why:* This is an open question. Scientists ask whether harmful things can travel to mouths, water and food. That depends on place and on the thing.
15. (count) A family makes about 730 kilograms of waste a year, and half is wet. How many kilograms can be composted?  
   **Answer:** 365. *Why:* Half of 730 is 365 kilograms. If this is composted, that much stays out of the dump.
16. (perspective) Two views on where to start, for a street that is covered in litter.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a start, gives a reason, and notes that the other view's part is also needed.. *Why:* Habits and systems depend on each other. A good plan does both, and a good question is which comes first in your street.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A lane is swept and neat, but an open drain runs beside its hand pump. Is it clean?  
   Options: No, because germs can reach the water / Yes, because it looks neat / Yes, because the pump still works  
   **Answer:** No, because germs can reach the water. *Why:* Clean means harmful things are kept away from mouths, water and food. A neat lane can still let germs from the drain reach the pump. If you chose yes, you judged by looks and not by where things can travel.
2. (mcq) Which place is cleanest in the scientific sense?  
   Options: A shiny plate wiped with a dirty cloth / A swept house with dust tipped outside / A muddy garden with a covered compost pit  
   **Answer:** A muddy garden with a covered compost pit. *Why:* A plate wiped with a dirty cloth gets more germs, not fewer, and dust tipped outside has only moved. A covered pit keeps harmful things away from people even though the garden is muddy.
3. (mcq) Leftover sambar is tipped onto a pile of newspapers. What is the main problem?  
   Options: The paper becomes too heavy to lift / Two useful things become one that nobody can use / The sambar will never rot  
   **Answer:** Two useful things become one that nobody can use. *Why:* Soaked paper cannot go to a paper mill, and food mixed with paper and ink cannot go to compost. If you chose the weight, you saw a small problem and missed that both heaps are spoilt.
4. (mcq) Which of these belongs in the harmful bin?  
   Options: A used bandage / A banana peel / A rinsed milk packet / A cotton rag  
   **Answer:** A used bandage. *Why:* A bandage can carry germs and can hurt the person who sorts it, so it is wrapped and kept apart. A peel is wet waste, a clean packet is dry waste, and a rag is dry waste.
5. (mcq) Why must a curd cup be rinsed before it goes into dry waste?  
   Options: Curd melts the plastic / A rinsed cup weighs less to carry / Curd left in it spoils the whole sack  
   **Answer:** Curd left in it spoils the whole sack. *Why:* Food left in one cup soaks the paper and plastic around it so a buyer will not take the sack. If you chose melting, remember that curd does not harm plastic, it only makes the sack dirty.
6. (mcq) A peel is buried deep in a dump with no air. What do its bacteria give off?  
   Options: Methane, a gas that burns and traps heat / Compost and warmth, as in a pot / Nothing, because the peel stays whole  
   **Answer:** Methane, a gas that burns and traps heat. *Why:* Without air, different bacteria take over and make methane. If you chose compost, you described the same peel with air. If you chose nothing, remember that a buried peel still rots.
7. (mcq) A compost heap smells bad and rots very slowly. What is the most likely cause?  
   Options: Too much sunlight / Too little air reaches it / Too many dry leaves  
   **Answer:** Too little air reaches it. *Why:* A bad smell means that bacteria without air have taken over. Stirring the heap or adding dry leaves lets air in. Adding more leaves is part of the fix, and not the cause.
8. (mcq) Many cholera cases cluster round one water pump. What does that suggest?  
   Options: Bad smells in the air cause cholera / Cholera germs live only in pumps / Something in the pump's water spreads the illness  
   **Answer:** Something in the pump's water spreads the illness. *Why:* A cluster around one source points to the water. Smells were once blamed, because dirty lanes smelled and had illness, but the smell was not the cause.
9. (mcq) Which step blocks germs on fingers from reaching a meal?  
   Options: Washing hands with soap before eating / Spraying scent in the toilet / Closing the window  
   **Answer:** Washing hands with soap before eating. *Why:* Germs travel on fingers, and soap lifts them off. Scent hides a smell and does nothing to germs. A closed window does not stop fingers.
10. (mcq) Why can spit on a floor harm people who never met the person who spat?  
   Options: It dries and floats off as steam / Its germs get on shoes, hands and into the air / It makes the floor slippery  
   **Answer:** Its germs get on shoes, hands and into the air. *Why:* Spit can carry germs from the chest and mouth, and shoes, hands and drops spread them. A slippery floor is a different problem. If you chose steam, remember that germs do not vanish when spit dries.
11. (mcq) A village has built a toilet for every home. Does that prove nobody goes in the open?  
   Options: Yes, toilets are always used / Yes, if the toilets are new / Only if the toilets are painted / No, you must also count who uses them  
   **Answer:** No, you must also count who uses them. *Why:* A toilet that is built but not used still leaves the open field. Researchers visited villages to see what people really do, and found that some toilets were not used.
12. (mcq) Two surveys of the same villages give different numbers. What is the best first step?  
   Options: Ask what each survey counted and how / Believe the bigger number / Throw both surveys away  
   **Answer:** Ask what each survey counted and how. *Why:* Different counts can both be true. One may count toilets built and the other people using them. Throwing both away loses what each one tells us.
13. (mcq) If a town's scavenging birds disappear, what is most likely to happen to dead animals?  
   Options: They turn into compost at once / Nothing changes at all / They rot longer, and dogs gather to feed  
   **Answer:** They rot longer, and dogs gather to feed. *Why:* Scavengers remove dead animals fast. Without them, the bodies lie longer, and dogs and flies gather. If you chose nothing, you may have forgotten that scavengers are part of the cleaning.
14. (mcq) A sorter is cut by glass hidden in a bag of mixed waste. What would have prevented it?  
   Options: A heavier bag / Wrapping the glass and marking the bag / Throwing the bag out at night  
   **Answer:** Wrapping the glass and marking the bag. *Why:* The sorter cannot see the glass in a mixed bag. Wrapping and marking it tells the next person what is inside. Weight and timing do not make a bag safer.
15. (mcq) Which small action cuts the most waste over a whole year?  
   Options: Putting each thin bag in the blue bin / Using each thin bag twice / Asking the shop for two thin bags / Carrying your own bag and refusing the thin bag  
   **Answer:** Carrying your own bag and refusing the thin bag. *Why:* Refusing a thing means that nobody has to collect, sort or bury it. Recycling and reusing a bag twice still use up the bag.
16. (mcq) A beach clean-up removed tonnes of waste, but plastic kept arriving. What does this show?  
   Options: Clean-ups help, and the sources must change too / Clean-ups are pointless / The beach was never really cleaned  
   **Answer:** Clean-ups help, and the sources must change too. *Why:* The people who clean up do real good, and new plastic arrives while drains and factories keep sending it. Both small actions and bigger changes are needed.

## Applications
- Indore sends its sorted wet waste to a plant that makes gas for the city's buses. If your town collects wet waste separately, find out where it goes. Ask whether a plant, a compost yard or a dump is at the end.
- Many housing societies in cities have a composter in the compound. Ask the person who looks after the garden how the peels become compost. Look at the heap and smell it.
- Waste pickers in Pune work through their own cooperative, SWaCH. Pickers in Bengaluru carry city identity cards. If you see someone collecting sorted waste, look for the gloves, the apron or the card that shows the work is recognised.
- Banana leaves and sal leaf plates are used at feasts and temples in South India and in the East. After the next feast, see where the leaves go, such as a cow, a compost pit or a heap.
- A village twin-pit toilet uses about two mugs of water to flush. Compare that with a flush tank of ten litres. Find out how much water your own toilet uses by filling a bucket after one flush.
- Hospitals and clinics keep used bandages and sharp things in sealed, coloured bags. Next time you see one, notice the colour and the sign that tells workers to be careful.
- A steel tiffin, a cloth bag and a refilled bottle are used in thousands of homes in India. Count how many of these your family uses in a day, and how many thin bags and wrappers they replace.

## Spotted around us
- Look at the bins in your building, school or market. Notice the colours. Green is for wet waste and blue is for dry waste. Look inside and see whether people really sort.
- Watch the person who collects waste from your lane. Notice whether the truck or the cart has one space or two, and whether the sorted bags are tipped together at the back.
- Next time it rains, watch the drain in your lane for a minute. Notice what floats down and where the water goes. If a drain is blocked with bags, see where the water stands.
- At a tea stall, look at what the tea is served in. A clay kulhad, a paper cup and a plastic cup all end up on the ground sometimes. Notice which one is still there after the next rain.
- At a railway station or bus stand, look for red stains on walls and stairs, and for places where people wash hands. Notice whether there is soap beside the tap.
- At the vegetable market near closing time, see where the peels, stems and wilted leaves go. Notice if a cow, a crow, a heap or a truck gets them.
- Look at a packet of something you eat. Notice whether it says what to do with it, and whether it is one plastic or a shiny layer that is stuck together.

## Open questions
- **What do tiny pieces of plastic do inside living bodies?** Microplastics have been found in fish, in birds and in human blood. Studies on health effects are new and small, and scientists cannot yet say how much harm they cause.
- **What makes a whole village or city change a habit and keep the change?** Indore and Mawlynnong changed in very different ways. Researchers have ideas about rules, examples and pride, and cannot yet predict which places will hold their gains.
- **How many people in India still defecate in the open?** Government records and independent surveys give different numbers, because they count different things, and asking people about a private habit is hard to do accurately.
- **Can any living being eat plastic fast enough to matter?** In 2016 Japanese scientists found bacteria who slowly digest one kind of bottle plastic. Whether such bacteria, or the tools they make to break plastic, can ever clear real waste at useful speed is not known.
- **Should rivers, forests and animals have rights in law?** Courts and laws in India, New Zealand, Ecuador and Colombia have answered in different ways. It is a question of values as well as science, and countries are still working it out.

## Connected maths
- In a dustbin count there were 20 wet items, 12 dry items and 3 harmful items. What fraction of all the items is wet? About what fraction is dry? **Wet: 20 out of 35, which is a little more than half. Dry: 12 out of 35, which is a little more than one-third.**. Total = 20 + 12 + 3 = 35. Wet is 20 ÷ 35, a bit more than 17.5 ÷ 35 = half. Dry is 12 ÷ 35, a bit more than 11.67 ÷ 35 = one-third. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- India's towns and cities make about 1.6 lakh tonnes of waste a day, or roughly one-third of a kilogram for each person. About how many people is that? (1 tonne is 1,000 kilograms.) **About 48 crore people.**. 1.6 lakh tonnes = 160,000 × 1,000 = 160,000,000 kilograms. Dividing by one-third of a kilogram is the same as multiplying by 3: 160,000,000 × 3 = 480,000,000, which is 48 crore. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L3`)
- A family of four makes about 2 kilograms of waste a day. How much waste does each person make in a year of 365 days? **About 182 kilograms.**. 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5 kilograms for each person each day. 0.5 × 365 = 182.5 kilograms in a year. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L3`)
- A tippy-tap uses 250 millilitres for one hand wash. A running tap uses about 1 litre. A bucket holds 10 litres. How many washes does the bucket give with each method? **With the tippy-tap, 40 washes. With the running tap, 10 washes.**. 10 litres = 10,000 millilitres. 10,000 ÷ 250 = 40. 10,000 ÷ 1,000 = 10. The tippy-tap gives four times as many washes. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L3`)
- A family counted 24 wrappers and bottles on day 1 and 18 on day 7. By what fraction did the count fall? If it kept falling by the same amount each day, after how many more days would it reach zero? **It fell by one quarter. It fell by 6 in 6 days, which is 1 a day, so it would reach zero after 18 more days. It cannot really keep falling like that.**. 24 − 18 = 6, and 6 out of 24 is one quarter. 6 fewer in 6 days is 1 fewer each day. 18 ÷ 1 = 18 more days. In real life, some waste always remains, so the pattern has to slow down. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What are the three kinds of waste that India's rules ask homes to keep apart? → Wet, dry and harmful.
- Is a swept room always clean? Why or why not? → No. A room can look neat and still have germs or harmful waste that can reach people.
- What does a peel become when bacteria and fungi eat the peel with air? → Compost, a dark, crumbly, soil-like material.
- What gas forms when wet waste rots with no air? → Methane. It burns easily and traps heat.
- Name two paths by which germs from faeces can reach a mouth. → Fingers, flies, fields, water or food. Any two.
- Who sorts most of India's dry waste by hand? → Waste pickers.
- Which comes first, recycling, reusing or refusing? → Refusing. If a thing is never made, nobody has to collect, sort or bury it.
- What did people once believe caused cholera? → Bad smells. The cause was germs in water dirtied by faeces.
