# The immune system: how your body keeps germs out and fights them
`sci.body.immune-system.L3`

**Summary.** Germs land on your skin all day, yet most of them never get inside you. Germs are tiny living things that can make you ill. The immune system is everything your body does to keep germs out, and to fight the ones that get in. Your first walls are your skin, your tears, the sticky mucus in your nose, your saliva and the acid in your stomach. Germs that get past the walls meet white blood cells, which find them and eat them. A fever is your brain turning up your body's heat to help the fight.

**Intro.** *Look at the skin on the back of your hand. Germs are landing on it right now, thousands of them. Most of them never get inside you. What is stopping them?* Think of the last time you scraped your knee. First it hurt. Then, a few hours later, the skin around the scrape went pink. It felt warm and a bit puffy. Maybe a little yellow goo came out. Was the scrape getting worse? Or was something inside you rushing to that spot, on purpose? In this unit you will find out. You will meet the defenders that guard your body every second, even while you sleep.

## Learning outcomes

1. I can name the body's first walls (skin, tears, mucus and nose hairs, spit and stomach acid) and say how each one stops germs.
2. I can explain that white blood cells travel in the blood, find germs and eat them, and that redness, warmth, swelling and pus around a cut are signs of that fight.
3. I can explain that a fever is the body turning up its own heat to fight germs. I can read a thermometer and say when a grown-up should call a doctor.
4. I can run a fair test, like the apple skin test, keep a results table and say what I think, what I saw and why.
5. I can explain that most microbes are harmless or helpful, and that the body keeps deciding what belongs and what does not.
6. I can give deep and wide examples: a tear chemical that breaks germs open, plants and bacteria that defend themselves, sick lizards that warm up on purpose, and things scientists still do not know.

## Terms
- **Immune system**: Everything your body does to keep germs out and to fight the germs that get in. It is not one organ. It is skin, tears, mucus, blood cells and much more, working together. *e.g.* When you have a cold and then get better, your immune system has won the fight.
- **Germ**: A tiny living thing, too small to see, that can make you ill. Most germs are bacteria or viruses. A few are tiny fungi, or other one-celled living things like the malaria germ. *e.g.* The germs of a cold spread when someone sneezes near you.
- **Microbe**: Any living thing too small to see without a microscope. Most microbes are harmless, and many help us. Only a few are germs. *e.g.* Microbes in curd turn milk sour and thick. They are friendly microbes.
- **Bacteria**: One kind of microbe. Each one is a single tiny living cell. Bacteria sense what is around them and decide where to go. Many swim towards food. One drop of curd can hold millions of them. *e.g.* The bacteria in curd are friendly. The bacteria that cause typhoid are germs.
- **Virus**: A tiny germ, much smaller than bacteria. A virus can only make copies inside a living cell. *e.g.* Colds and chickenpox are caused by viruses.
- **Skin**: The body's outer covering. Its top layer is made of flat, dead cells packed tight, like tiles on a roof. Most germs cannot get through skin that has no cuts. *e.g.* Germs land on your hand all day, but unbroken skin keeps them outside.
- **Mucus**: The wet, sticky stuff inside your nose and throat. It traps dust and germs before they reach your lungs. *e.g.* When you blow your nose into a hanky, you blow out mucus full of trapped dust and germs.
- **Tears**: Salty water your eyes make all the time, not just when you cry. Every blink spreads tears and washes the eye. Tears also hold a chemical that breaks some germs apart. *e.g.* When a speck of dust lands in your eye, tears wash it out.
- **Saliva**: Spit: the watery juice in your mouth. It washes your teeth and holds chemicals that slow germs down. *e.g.* Your mouth makes more saliva when you smell food cooking.
- **Stomach acid**: A very strong, sour juice inside your stomach. It breaks down food and kills many germs that get swallowed. *e.g.* Many of the germs on the food you swallow are killed in your stomach.
- **White blood cell**: A tiny cell that travels in your blood and hunts germs. Some kinds eat germs. Other kinds make chemicals that stick to germs, or remember them for next time. *e.g.* When germs get into a cut, white blood cells rush there from the blood.
- **Phagocyte**: A white blood cell that eats germs. The word comes from Greek words meaning 'eating cell'. *e.g.* Phagocytes swallow germs whole, then break them down inside.
- **Inflammation**: When part of the body goes red, warm, swollen and sore because the immune system is fighting there. Extra blood brings more defenders to that spot. *e.g.* The pink, puffy skin around a new mosquito bite or scrape is inflammation.
- **Pus**: The thick, yellowish goo that can come out of an infected cut. It is mostly white blood cells that died fighting, mixed with dead germs. *e.g.* A pimple's white tip is pus.
- **Scab**: The hard, dark crust that forms on a scrape. Dried blood and a sticky mesh seal the hole, so new skin can grow underneath. *e.g.* A scab on your knee falls off by itself when the new skin is ready.
- **Fever**: When the body turns its own heat up higher than usual, often to fight germs. Doctors usually call 38 degrees Celsius or more a fever. *e.g.* With the flu, your temperature might go up to 38.5 degrees Celsius for a day or two.
- **Thermometer**: A tool that measures how hot something is. A body thermometer measures your temperature in degrees Celsius or degrees Fahrenheit. *e.g.* A digital thermometer under your arm beeps when it has finished measuring.
- **Microbiome**: All the microbes that live on your skin, in your mouth and in your gut. Most of them are harmless or helpful. *e.g.* Friendly bacteria on your skin take up the space that germs would like to use.
- **Vaccine**: A medicine, usually an injection or drops, that shows your immune system a harmless form or a small part of a germ. Your white blood cells learn the germ's shape. If the real germ ever comes, they fight them quickly. *e.g.* Polio drops and the measles injection protect children all over India.
- **Antibiotic**: A medicine that kills bacteria or stops them growing. Antibiotics do not work on viruses, so they do not cure a cold. Only a doctor should decide when someone needs one. *e.g.* A cold is caused by a virus, so an antibiotic will not help it get better. Rest, water and time do.
- **Self and not-self**: How the immune system sorts things. 'Self' means your own cells. 'Not-self' means things from outside, like germs. Scientists are still working out exactly how it decides. *e.g.* Your white blood cells leave your own cells alone, but they attack a germ.

## Explanations
### What the immune system is · *defined*
**Definition:** The immune system is everything the body does to keep germs out and to fight the germs that get in.

Germs are everywhere: on door handles, in the air, on your food. Most of the time they cannot hurt you. That is because your body defends itself in layers, like a fort. First come the outer walls, which stop germs getting in at all. Then come the guards inside, the white blood cells, which hunt any germs that slip through. Last, some white blood cells remember a germ, so the next fight is quicker. Scientists call all of this together the immune system.
- e.g. You touch a dirty railing, and nothing happens, because your skin keeps the germs out.
- e.g. You catch a cold, feel ill for a few days, then get better. Your immune system fought and won.
- e.g. Most children get chickenpox only once. The body remembers the germ.
### Skin: the outer wall · *defined*
**Definition:** Skin is the body's outer covering. Its tight, dead top layer is a wall that germs cannot get through unless there is a cut.

Skin is the biggest organ of your body, and it is your first wall. Its top layer is made of flat, dead cells packed tightly together, like tiles on a roof. Germs cannot push through. New cells grow underneath and slowly move up. The old ones flake off, taking germs with them. Skin is also a little oily and a little sour, which many germs dislike. And it is covered in friendly microbes, which leave no room for germs. A cut is a hole in the wall. That is why we wash a cut with clean water and cover it.
- e.g. Germs land on your hands all day. They stay outside unless you have a cut, or you rub your eyes, nose or mouth.
- e.g. A mosquito bite is a tiny hole. That is how the malaria germ gets past the skin.
- e.g. Your skin makes a whole new top layer about every month or two. Some of the dust in a house is old skin flakes.
### Wet walls: tears, mucus, spit and stomach acid · *defined*
**Definition:** Tears, mucus, saliva and stomach acid are wet walls. They wash germs away, trap them, or kill them before they can get inside the body.

Your eyes, nose and mouth are openings, so skin cannot cover them. Wet walls guard them instead. Tears wash your eyes every time you blink. The hairs just inside your nose catch bigger bits of dust. Sticky mucus traps smaller dust and germs. Further in, even tinier hairs called cilia sweep the mucus up to your throat, where you swallow it or cough it out. Saliva washes your mouth. Anything you swallow lands in stomach acid, a juice so sour that it kills many germs. Tears, mucus and saliva also hold a chemical called lysozyme. It cuts open the outer coat of some bacteria, so they burst.
- e.g. When you chop onions, your eyes stream with tears. They are washing out the stinging gas from the onion.
- e.g. A sneeze blasts trapped dust and germs out of your nose.
- e.g. In 1921 Alexander Fleming, a Scottish doctor, had a cold. A drop from his runny nose fell on a dish of bacteria, and the bacteria near it dissolved. That is how lysozyme was found.
### White blood cells: the defenders inside · *defined*
**Definition:** White blood cells are cells that travel in the blood, find germs and destroy them. Some eat germs whole. Others make chemicals that stick to germs, or remember them.

Your blood is mostly red blood cells, which carry oxygen, the part of the air your body needs. Mixed in with them are a few white blood cells. There is only about one white cell for every 700 red ones. White blood cells are made inside your bones, in a soft middle part called bone marrow. They ride around in the blood. When germs get in, the hurt cells send out chemical signals, like a smell. White blood cells follow the smell. A blood vessel is a tube that blood flows in. White blood cells squeeze out through its wall and crawl to the germs. Some kinds, called phagocytes, wrap around a germ and eat them. Other kinds make sticky chemicals that grab germs, or remember a germ for next time. You will learn about those in a later unit.
- e.g. In a cut finger, white blood cells arrive within an hour or so.
- e.g. White blood cells are not really white. Each one is almost see-through. A big pile of them looks pale and creamy.
- e.g. Your body makes billions of new white blood cells every day, because many of them only live for a few days.
### Red, warm, swollen and sore: inflammation · *defined*
**Definition:** Inflammation is when part of the body goes red, warm, swollen and sore because extra blood is bringing white blood cells there to fight.

When germs get into a cut, the cells nearby call for help. The tiny blood vessels there get wider and a little leaky. More blood comes, so the skin goes red and warm. Some water leaks out, so it swells up. The swelling presses on nerves, so it feels sore. All of this lets white blood cells pour into the spot. This fight is called inflammation. About two thousand years ago, a Roman writer called Celsus listed its four signs: redness, heat, swelling and pain. When white blood cells die fighting, they pile up with dead germs. That pale goo is pus. A scab forms on top, like a lid, while new skin grows underneath.
- e.g. A splinter in your finger: the skin round it goes pink and puffy within a day.
- e.g. A mosquito bite goes red and itchy. That is your body reacting to the mosquito's spit.
- e.g. A pimple's white tip is pus, left over from a small fight against germs.
### Fever: the body turns up the heat · *defined*
**Definition:** A fever is when the brain sets the body's heat higher than usual, often because the immune system is fighting germs.

Your body keeps itself at about 37 degrees Celsius, whether the day is hot or cold. A part of your brain works like the knob on an iron. It sets how hot you should be. When white blood cells meet germs, they send chemicals to the brain. The brain turns the knob up. Now 37 degrees feels too cold, so you shiver and want a blanket, until you warm up. That is a fever. Many germs grow more slowly when it is hotter. And some white blood cells work faster. When the fight is over, the brain turns the knob back down. Then you feel too hot, and you sweat until you cool off.
- e.g. Doctors usually call 38 degrees Celsius or more a fever. That is 100.4 degrees Fahrenheit.
- e.g. At the start of a fever you may shiver, even though your forehead feels hot.
- e.g. When a fever breaks, you often wake up sweaty.
### When a fever needs a doctor · *defined*
**Definition:** Most fevers need rest, water and a watchful grown-up. Some warning signs mean a doctor should see the child quickly.

Most fevers in children are the body doing its job, and they pass in two or three days. A grown-up gives plenty of water and lets the child rest. If the child feels very uncomfortable, a grown-up can ask a doctor what to do. Children never take medicine by themselves. Some fevers need a doctor quickly. A grown-up should call a doctor for any fever in a baby under 3 months old. They should also call if a fever reaches 40 degrees Celsius or lasts more than 3 days. Call at once if the fever comes with a stiff neck, a new rash, trouble breathing or a fit. Call at once, too, if the child is very hard to wake.
- e.g. A child has a fever of 38.4 degrees and a runny nose, but still plays a little. Rest, water and watching are enough.
- e.g. A child has a fever with a stiff neck and a rash. A doctor must see them at once.
- e.g. Feeling hot is not enough to know. A thermometer tells you the real number.
### Should we bring a fever down? · *debated*
Since fever helps fight germs, should we stop it? People have argued about this for a long time. Today doctors mostly agree on what to do, but scientists still study why.
- e.g. Some families give a fever medicine so a child can sleep. Others wait. Doctors say a fever medicine is for comfort, and a doctor should say which one and how much.
- e.g. Scientists have tested fever medicines in many sick children. Most studies found the medicines did not make illnesses last much longer.
- **Many doctors long ago, and many families today:** The fever is the danger, so it must be brought down fast. Some people even wake a sleeping child to give medicine, or sponge them with cold water.
- **Scientists who study fever in animals, such as Matthew Kluger in the 1970s:** Fever is an old, useful defence. Sick lizards that were kept cool were more likely to die than lizards allowed to warm up. So fever helps, and we should not be afraid of it.
- **Children's doctors today, for example the American Academy of Pediatrics and the UK's NHS:** Treat the child, not the number. A fever medicine is for comfort, not to reach a 'normal' number. Watch the child, and see a doctor if there are warning signs.
### How does the body tell 'me' from 'not me'? · *debated*
White blood cells leave your own cells alone, but they attack germs. How do they know the difference? This is one of the biggest questions in the study of the immune system. Scientists have given different answers, and the story is still being worked out.
- e.g. Your immune system does not attack the food you eat, though food is from outside.
- e.g. It leaves alone the billions of friendly microbes in your gut.
- e.g. A mother's body does not attack the baby growing inside her, though half of the baby's genes come from the father.
- **Frank Macfarlane Burnet, an Australian scientist, around 1949 to 1959:** While a baby is growing, the body learns what 'self' is. Later, anything that is not self gets attacked. He shared a Nobel Prize for this work in 1960.
- **Polly Matzinger, an American scientist, in 1994:** The body does not attack things just because they are new. It attacks when cells send 'danger' signals, like hurt cells do. That is why it leaves food and friendly microbes alone. She came to science late, after working as a jazz musician, a carpenter and a dog trainer. At first many scientists disagreed with her.
- **Most scientists today:** Both ideas are partly right. The body does learn its own self. It also listens for danger signals. Exactly how these work together is still being studied.
### Friendly microbes are part of the defence · *defined*
**Definition:** The microbiome is all the microbes that live on and in you. Many of them help protect you by leaving no room or food for germs.

You are never alone. About as many bacteria live on and in your body as there are cells of your own. Scientists counted in 2016 and found roughly 38 trillion bacteria and 30 trillion human cells in a grown-up. Almost all of them are harmless or helpful. On your skin and in your gut they eat the food that germs want, and take up the space that germs want. Some even make chemicals that keep germs away. So washing with soap is good, but scrubbing your skin raw is not. Your friendly microbes are part of your wall.
- e.g. A healthy gut is packed with friendly bacteria, so germs struggle to settle there.
- e.g. Curd and idli batter are full of friendly microbes.
- e.g. Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria. After taking them, some people get tummy trouble, because friendly gut bacteria were killed too.
### All living things defend themselves · *defined*
**Definition:** All living things, from bacteria to plants to people, have ways to keep out or fight what harms them.

The immune system is not only a human thing. Every living thing has some way to keep out what harms them. Plants have no blood and no white blood cells. But their leaves have waxy coats, and they can close the tiny holes in their leaves when germs land. A hurt plant makes sticky sap and sends chemical alarms to their other leaves. Even bacteria, which are single cells, have a way to cut up viruses that attack them, and to remember them. Scientists found this in bacteria used to make curd and yoghurt. Insects, fish and lizards fight germs too. Some of them warm up on purpose when they are sick.
- e.g. A neem or mango tree oozes sticky gum where their bark is hurt. The gum seals the wound.
- e.g. Sick lizards in a lab moved to the warmest spot they could find, giving themselves a fever.
- e.g. Honeybees heat up their hive when a harmful fungus attacks the baby bees.
### Down to the molecules: shapes that fit · *defined*
**Definition:** Immune cells and chemicals recognise germs by touch. Their molecules fit the molecules on the germ, and tiny electric forces of attraction hold them together.

A white blood cell has no eyes. The cell finds out what a germ is by touch. The outside of every cell, and every germ, is covered in molecules. A molecule is a tiny group of atoms joined together, and each kind has its own shape. A white blood cell has their own molecules on their outside, called receptors. When a receptor fits a molecule on a germ, like a key in a lock, the cell grabs on. Tiny electric forces between the atoms attract each other and hold the two together. That is how the cell knows to attack. Lysozyme, the chemical in your tears, works the same way. Its shape fits a part of a germ's coat, and it snips it.
- e.g. A white blood cell bumps into thousands of things. They grab only those whose shapes fit their receptors.
- e.g. Lysozyme is also found in egg white. An enzyme is a chemical that does a job in living things. In 1965, lysozyme became the first enzyme whose full shape scientists worked out, using X-rays.
### Sleep, food, play and the immune system · *defined*
**Definition:** Good sleep, varied food, daily play, handwashing and vaccines all help the immune system. No single food or drink 'boosts' it.

There is no magic food or drink that 'boosts' your immune system. But the whole body works better when it is looked after, and the immune system is part of the body. Doctors agree on a few simple things. Sleep well, because the body does a lot of repair at night. Eat many kinds of food, including vegetables, fruit, dal and grains. Play and move every day. Wash your hands with soap before eating and after the toilet, so fewer germs reach your mouth. Get the vaccines your doctor offers. A vaccine shows your immune system a harmless form of a germ, so it can fight the real germ quickly.
- e.g. Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night.
- e.g. Washing hands with soap and water for about 20 seconds removes many more germs than water alone.
- e.g. Adverts often say a drink 'boosts immunity'. That is usually not what scientists have found.
### What nobody knows yet · *idea*
Scientists have learnt a huge amount about the immune system. But some big puzzles are still open. Why do two people catch the same germ, and one gets very ill while the other hardly notices? When we are ill, we feel tired, grumpy and sleepy. How exactly does the immune system send these signals to the brain? Why do some immune systems attack harmless things like pollen or peanuts? There are ideas about all of these, but no full answers yet.
- e.g. Feeling sleepy and sad when ill seems to be partly caused by chemicals from white blood cells reaching the brain. Scientists think this makes us rest.
- e.g. The same cold virus can give one child a sniffle and another child a fever.

## Mostly agreed
- Germs cause many illnesses, and most of them must get past the skin or the wet walls to make us ill. *How we know:* In the 1870s and 1880s, Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch showed that particular germs cause particular diseases. Koch grew the germs of anthrax and tuberculosis from sick animals and people. Washing hands and cleaning wounds cuts infections a lot.
- White blood cells find and destroy germs. *How we know:* Under a microscope, scientists have filmed white blood cells chasing and swallowing bacteria. In 1882, Élie Metchnikoff saw cells like these gather round a thorn.
- Redness, warmth, swelling and pus are signs that the body is fighting, not just signs of damage. *How we know:* When scientists look at swollen tissue under a microscope, it is full of white blood cells that have come out of the blood.
- A fever is controlled by the brain, and it usually helps the body fight germs. *How we know:* Chemicals made by white blood cells reach the brain and raise the body's set heat. In many animals, sick ones that can warm up survive better.
- Most microbes are harmless or helpful, and many live on and in us. *How we know:* When scientists count and name the microbes on skin and in the gut, almost all are harmless. Some help digest food or keep germs away.
- All living things, even a single bacterium, have some way to defend themselves. *How we know:* Plants close leaf pores and send chemical alarms. In 2007, scientists showed that bacteria cut up viruses with a system called CRISPR.
- Vaccines protect people from serious illnesses caused by germs. *How we know:* After years of vaccination all over the world, smallpox disappeared. Its last natural case was in 1977. India has had no new case of wild polio since 2011, after millions of children were given polio drops.

## How we found out
**Élie Metchnikoff, George Nuttall, Almroth Wright and Stewart Douglas, 1882 to 1908.** In the 1880s, many scientists thought that germs inside the body were killed only by chemicals in the blood. The body's cells, they said, did nothing useful. In 1882, Élie Metchnikoff pushed rose thorns into see-through baby starfish. By the next morning, wandering cells had crowded round every thorn. In 1884 he watched tiny water fleas that had swallowed the spores of a fungus. When the fleas' cells ate the spores, the flea lived. When the spores grew faster than the cells could eat them, the flea died. Other scientists, such as George Nuttall in 1888, showed that blood with no cells in it could still kill some bacteria. For years, each side said the other was wrong. Then, in 1903, Almroth Wright and Stewart Douglas in London showed that chemicals in the blood coat germs and make them easier for the eating cells to swallow. The chemicals and the cells work together. In 1908, Metchnikoff and Paul Ehrlich, who studied the chemicals, shared the Nobel Prize.  
*What people believed before:* Germs inside the body are killed only by chemicals in the blood, and the body's cells play no useful part. Metchnikoff watched cells eat germs, and Wright and Douglas showed that the chemicals and the cells work together.

## Challenging perspectives
- **All germs are bad, and the cleaner everything is, the better.** (Many adverts for cleaning products, and many of us without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some germs really do cause serious illness. Washing hands with soap really does stop many of them spreading.  
  Against: Only a few microbes are germs. Friendly microbes on our skin and in our gut help guard us. Scientists think that meeting many harmless microbes when young, outdoors and with animals, may help the immune system learn. Scrubbing everything with strong chemicals is not needed.
- **Illness comes from bad air, or 'miasma', not from tiny living things.** (Many doctors in Europe and India until the late 1800s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Diseases like cholera were common near stinking drains and dirty water. Cleaning up drains really did reduce illness.  
  Against: Germs were found in the 1800s. Cholera spreads through dirty water carrying a germ, not through the smell itself. Cleaning drains helped because it removed the germs.
- **A fever is dangerous in itself, so it must always be brought down quickly.** (Many families, and many doctors in the past.; *rejected*)  
  For: A very high fever can be a sign of serious illness, and fever makes children feel awful.  
  Against: In most children a fever is the body fighting, and it does no harm by itself. Doctors today say to treat the child's comfort and watch for warning signs, rather than chase a number.
- **The immune system attacks only things that send danger signals, not simply anything foreign.** (Polly Matzinger, an American immunologist, in 1994. Her 'danger model'.; *debated*)  
  For: The body leaves food, friendly microbes and a growing baby alone, though they are all 'not self'.  
  Against: The body really does learn its own self, and some foreign things are attacked even without much harm. Most scientists now think both self and danger matter.
- **Germs inside the body are killed only by chemicals in the blood, not by cells that eat them.** (Paul Ehrlich and many other scientists, from the 1880s. Élie Metchnikoff said cells did the work. They argued for about 25 years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Each side had strong experiments. Metchnikoff watched cells eat. Ehrlich and others showed blood without cells could still kill some germs.  
  Against: Both were right. In 1908 they shared the Nobel Prize. The immune system uses both eating cells and germ-grabbing chemicals.
- **You catch a cold just from being cold or getting your hair wet.** (Many grandparents, and many of us.; *debated*)  
  For: Colds are more common in the cold months. Some studies suggest that cold noses may fight viruses a little less well.  
  Against: A cold is caused by a virus. Without the virus, you cannot catch a cold however cold you get. In winter people also crowd indoors, which helps viruses spread.

## Roots
We have been learning how the body keeps germs out and fights the ones that get in. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Every apple in the test had microbes on its skin. The ones that rotted all had cuts, and the rot began at the cut. Unbroken skin kept the microbes out just by being whole. Most of your body's protection works this way, with walls that stop germs before any fight begins.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture the immune system as soldiers fighting germs. Most of the work is done before any fight, by skin, tears and mucus that are always there.  
*Evidence:* In the apple skin test, the apple with whole skin stayed fresh, while cut apples went soft, starting at the cut. In the sticky nose test, sticky tape held far more dust than smooth tape.

- *who am i:* Your skin, your tears and the mucus in your nose keep you well every minute, and you hardly notice them.
- *what is life:* Every living thing, from a bacterium to a neem tree to you, has an outer layer that keeps their inside apart from the outside.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of microbes, on every apple, door handle and breath of air. Most of them never get in, and most would do you no harm.

The first living things were single cells, each wrapped in a thin skin called a membrane. That skin let food in and kept harm out. Your own body began as one cell, and grew into trillions, each still wrapped in their own membrane. Your skin, your tears and your mucus do the same job for the whole body. Life began with one kind of wall, and it has grown into countless kinds, in bacteria, trees and you.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 17:** The best rulers are hardly noticed by their people. When the work is done, the people say, 'It happened by itself.' (Your skin and tears protect you so quietly that you forget them. In the apple test, the whole skin kept the apple fresh, and nobody saw it doing anything.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, 'interbeing', in his book The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in it. Nothing exists all by itself. (Look deeply at your skin and you can see the friendly microbes that live on it and help guard it. Your wall is made partly of other living things.)
- **Jain anekantavada, the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Each blind man touched one part of the elephant and was sure he knew the whole. Each was partly right. (Metchnikoff said cells fight germs. Ehrlich said chemicals do. They argued for twenty-five years, and they were both partly right.)
- Science (settled): Skin, tears, mucus and stomach acid stop most germs before white blood cells are needed. Most germs get in only where a wall is broken, such as a cut or a mosquito bite.
- Science (debated): How the immune system decides what is 'self' and what to attack, by learning self early or by listening for danger, is still argued about.
- Other view, Many immunologists: The walls are only half the story. Some germs get past them every day, through the gut, the lungs and tiny scratches. The white blood cells inside, and the way they remember germs, matter just as much. People whose inside defenders do not work well need special care from doctors, even with healthy skin.
- Practice, Notice your walls (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Blink slowly three times. Feel your tears spread across your eyes. Now breathe in slowly through your nose. The hairs and mucus inside are cleaning that air right now. Now feel the skin on your arms and face. It is keeping germs out this very moment, and you never asked it to. Sit for one more slow breath.
- Ask: Your skin keeps germs out all day, and you hardly notice it working. What else in your body works without you noticing?
- Ask: If a wall stopped everything, what would your body miss out on?
- Ask: The microbes in the apple test were living things looking for food. Were they doing anything wrong?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* If more than half the living cells on and in you are microbes, which part is really 'you'? (hint: Your friendly microbes help guard you and digest your food. Are they visitors, or part of you?)
- *deep:* Your white blood cells fight for you every second, but you never notice them. What else is your body doing right now without asking you? (hint: Think about your heart, your breathing and your blinking.)
- *big:* A white blood cell dies fighting a germ, and becomes pus. Is that sad, brave, or just what cells do? (hint: Think of people who protect others, like firefighters or nurses.)
- *deep:* Why might it be good, not bad, that a cut goes red and sore? (hint: What would happen if you never felt it, and never noticed the cut?)
- *deep:* Your skin keeps germs out, but it lets sweat out. What else must the body's walls let through? (hint: Think about your nose and mouth. What must come in through them every minute?)
- *deep:* Why does a fever make you feel cold at first, when your body is getting hotter? (hint: Think of the brain's heat knob being turned up. What does 37 degrees feel like then?)
- *big:* Plants and even bacteria defend themselves. Does that mean they, in some way, know what they are? (hint: A bacterium has no brain. But they can tell their own self from a virus.)
- *big:* If germs are living things too, is it fair that we kill them? (hint: Germs are not bad on purpose. They are trying to live, like us.)

## Story: The Thorns of Messina
*Élie Metchnikoff, the starfish larvae, the rose thorns and the family's trip to the circus in Messina, Sicily, in 1882 are real. He told the story himself, and his wife Olga wrote it down in her book about his life. Olga telling the story, her little brother Sasha, his splinter and all the talk are made up.*

My name is Olga. In the winter of 1882 I lived in a white house above the sea, in a town called Messina, on the island of Sicily. My husband was Élie Metchnikoff. He studied animals so small and so clear that you could see right through them.

Our sitting room was not like other sitting rooms. There were glass dishes on every table. In every dish swam tiny baby starfish, each one smaller than a grain of rice. They were not star-shaped yet. They looked like little bells made of water. Élie would sit for hours with his eye pressed to a microscope, a tube of glass that makes small things look big.

"Come and look, Olga," he would say. And I would look. Inside each baby starfish, you could see everything. Their tiny stomach. Its thin walls. And something else. Small round blobs that drifted about inside them, all by themselves, like boats with no oars.

"What are they?" I asked.

"I do not know," said Élie. "They wander. Nobody has told them where to go. And yet they go."

My little brother Sasha was staying with us that winter. He was eight. He did not care for starfish. He cared for oranges, and for climbing the low wall at the end of the garden, and for the circus.

The circus had come to Messina, with performing monkeys and a man who ate fire. On a bright December afternoon, the whole family put on our good coats to go and see it. Everyone except Élie.

"Go," he said, waving his hand without looking up. "I will stay with my bells."

So we went. Sasha laughed until he hiccuped. But when we came home, Élie was standing in the middle of the sitting room. His hair was wild. His eyes were shining.

"Olga," he said. "I have had an idea. It may be the most important idea of my life."

This is what had happened. Alone in the quiet house, he had been watching the wandering blobs. And a thought had struck him, the way lightning strikes a tree. What if the blobs were guards? What if, when something got inside the starfish that should not be there, they came to fight it?

"But how can you know?" I said.

"I can test it," said Élie. "I can put something into a starfish, and see if the blobs come."

A few days before, we had made a Christmas tree for the children out of a small tangerine tree in the garden. Roses grew beside the tree. While we were at the circus, Élie had gone out into the garden and snapped off a few rose thorns.

That evening, very gently, he pushed a single rose thorn into each baby starfish. Then he covered the dishes and went to bed. He did not sleep. I heard him get up twice in the night, and stand by the dishes, and sit down again.

In the morning, Sasha woke with a cry. While climbing the garden wall the day before, he had pushed a splinter of wood into his finger. Now the finger was red and fat and hot. He held it up to me, frightened.

"It is getting worse," he said. "Look! The splinter is eating me."

Before I could answer, Élie called from the sitting room. His voice was strange, high and shaking.

"Olga! Come! Bring the boy!"

We ran in. Élie stood back from the microscope so I could look. There was the baby starfish, clear as a raindrop. There was the rose thorn, dark and sharp inside them. And all around the thorn, crowded together like people around a fire, were the wandering blobs. Hundreds of them. They had come from every part of the tiny body. They were pressing against the thorn. Some had wrapped themselves around its tip.

"They came," Élie whispered. "In the night, they all came."

Sasha pushed in to look. He squinted down the tube for a long time. Then he lifted his head and stared at his own red finger.

"Is that," he said slowly, "what is happening in me?"

I knelt down and took my brother's hand very gently. I looked at the red, fat finger, and then back at the dish. All at once I understood what Élie had found.

"Yes," I said. "I think it is. This redness is not the splinter eating you, Sasha. It is you. Your own blood is rushing here, carrying your own guards. That warmth is the fire they are standing round. The swelling is the crowd. The splinter got through your wall, and your body has sent an army to the spot where it broke in."

Sasha looked at his finger as if he had never seen it before. "So it is not getting worse?"

"It is getting busier," I said. "That is different." Behind me, Élie nodded, and he was smiling.

I took a needle and cleaned it in a flame, and I eased the splinter out. A tiny bead of yellow came after it. Sasha made a face. "What is that?"

"Those are guards that fought," I said quietly. "Some of them did not survive. They gave themselves up to keep you well." Élie was silent for a moment. Then he said, "Every one of us is defended like this. Every second. And nobody notices."

Within two days, Sasha's finger was pink and ordinary again. Over the next few years, Élie watched the same kind of guards in water fleas and in many other animals. He saw them swallow germs whole. The cells were given a name made from old Greek words. Phagocytes. The eating cells.

Many great scientists disagreed with him. Germs are killed by chemicals in the blood, they said. Not by little wandering blobs. The argument went on for twenty-five years. Then, in 1908, Élie was given the Nobel Prize, the biggest prize in science. He shared it with Paul Ehrlich, who had studied the chemicals in the blood. They had both been right. The body uses chemicals and cells together.

But the evening I remember best is not the evening of the prize. It is the evening of the circus, when we came home laughing and found him in the middle of the room with his hair on end. The monkeys and the fire-eater had been wonderful. But the real show had been in a glass dish, in a creature smaller than a grain of rice, where a crowd of tiny guards had gathered, in the dark, around a thorn from a Christmas rose.

**For the parent:** For the parent: redness, warmth and swelling around a cut feel like damage, but they are mostly the body's own response. Blood vessels widen and leak a little so that white blood cells, the eating cells Metchnikoff found, can crowd to the spot. Pus is the remains of that fight. The story's turn is Sasha seeing his own sore finger as a busy battlefield rather than a wound getting worse. It also quietly shows how a new idea can be doubted for years, and how two rivals can both be right.

- Ask: In the story, Sasha thought the splinter was eating him. What was really making his finger red and hot?
- Ask: Why did Élie push thorns into the baby starfish? What did he think would happen?
- Ask: What is the yellow bead that came out after the splinter?
- Ask: Élie stayed home from the circus. Was that a good choice? Why do you think so?
- Ask: Other scientists disagreed with Élie's idea for years. Why do you think new ideas are often doubted at first?

## Games
### g1 · Get Past the Walls! (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the germ cards and the defence cards. Keep them in two piles.; One player is the Germ. The other players are the Body. Each Body player holds 4 defence cards.; The Germ turns over a germ card and reads it out loud.; A Body player must play one defence card that stops this germ, and say how it works. For example, 'Stomach acid kills the germs on the guava.'; If the defence fits, the germ is stopped. Put both cards aside. The Body scores a point, and that player takes a new defence card from the pile.; If no one can stop them with a wall, the germ gets inside. Then only a WHITE BLOOD CELL, INFLAMMATION or FEVER card can stop them.; After 8 germ cards, swap sides. The side that stops the most germs wins.
- **Printable cards: 16 germ cards to cut out:** Cold virus floating in a sneeze; Dust with germs blowing into your eyes; Germs on a dirty hand, about to rub your nose; Germs on an unwashed guava; Bacteria on a rusty nail that pokes your foot; Dirty puddle water splashing on a scraped knee; Germs on a shared spoon; A mosquito carrying a germ; Germs on a door handle; Germs in a cough on the bus; Germs on a dog's paw that touches your arm (no cut); Germs in dirty water from a broken pipe; A splinter carrying bacteria under the skin; Germs in a cut that nobody washed; Germs in dust you breathe in on a road; A germ that got past every wall
- **Printable cards: 12 defence cards to cut out:** SKIN: a tight wall of dead cells; TEARS: wash the eye at every blink; MUCUS and NOSE HAIRS: trap dust and germs; SNEEZE and COUGH: blast them out; SALIVA: washes the mouth; STOMACH ACID: kills germs in food and water; WHITE BLOOD CELL: finds and eats germs; INFLAMMATION: red, warm, swollen, more defenders arrive; FEVER: the brain turns up the heat; WASH IT: clean a cut and cover it; SOAP: wash hands before eating; FRIENDLY MICROBES: no room for germs
- **Printable board: The body board:** OUTSIDE (germs start here); THE WALLS (skin, eyes, nose, mouth); INSIDE (blood and white blood cells)
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Germ player: germs that got inside ____; Defence player: germs stopped ____
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the body board on the floor. 2. Shuffle the germ cards face down. 3. Deal 4 defence cards to each Body player.  
  *Parent says:* In this game, one of us is a germ trying to get into the body. The rest of us are the body's defences. Germs are tiny living things that can make us ill. Our job is to stop them.  
  *Child mode:* Lay out the body board. Mix the germ cards. Take 4 defence cards. You are the body. Stop the germs!
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the practice card 'Germs on an unwashed guava' together.  
  *Parent says:* Practice card: germs on an unwashed guava. Someone eats it. Which defence stops these germs?  
  *Child mode:* Practice card: germs on an unwashed guava. You eat it. Which defence stops the germs? Tap it.
  - Ask: How does it stop them? (good: Stomach acid is very sour and kills most germs in food.; Washing the guava first would also help.)
  - Check: Someone eats a guava without washing it. Which defence kills most of the germs on it? → **Stomach acid**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play 8 germ cards. Each time, the Body player says how their defence works.  
  *Parent says:* Turn over the next germ. Who can stop them? Tell us how your defence works.  
  *Child mode:* Turn over a germ card. Play a defence that stops them. Say how it works.
  - Note: Listen for the reason, not just the card. 'Skin stops it because it is a tight wall with no holes' is a great answer. If a cut or a mosquito is involved, the skin wall is broken, so the germ goes INSIDE.
  - Check: The germ card says 'Germs in a cut that nobody washed.' The skin is broken. Which defence card can stop these germs now? → **White blood cell**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap sides and play the remaining germ cards.  
  *Parent says:* Now swap. The germ becomes the body, and the body becomes the germ.  
  *Child mode:* Swap sides! Now you are the germ. Try to get in.
- **s5 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the stopped germs on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count. Which defence did we use most? Which defences stopped germs that were already inside?  
  *Child mode:* Count the germs you stopped. Which defence did you use most?
  - Think: Your real body plays this game every second, and it usually wins. What do you think happens on the days it doesn't?
### g2 · Germ Tag (15 min)
- **rules:** Put a cushion in the middle. The cushion is the body.; Skin wall players hold hands in a line in front of the cushion. They can only shuffle sideways.; Germ players try to get round the wall and touch the cushion.; The white blood cell runs freely. If they tag a germ, that germ is out.; Round 2: shout 'FEVER!' Now germs must hop instead of run.; Round 3: the white blood cell remembers the germs from round 1 and starts right next to them.
- **Printable cards: Role cards:** GERM; GERM; SKIN WALL; SKIN WALL; WHITE BLOOD CELL; FEVER CALLER (the grown-up who shouts 'FEVER!' in round 2)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hand out role cards. Put the cushion down and line up the skin wall.  
  *Parent says:* The cushion is the body. You two are the skin wall. You are germs. And you are a white blood cell, a hunter in the blood.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a role card. Germs try to touch the cushion. The wall and the white blood cell stop them.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 1 for about 2 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Round one! Germs, go! Wall, shuffle to block. White blood cell, tag any germ that gets past.  
  *Child mode:* Round one! Run, block, tag!
  - Ask: What happened when a germ got round the wall? (good: The white blood cell had to chase them.; They nearly reached the body.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 2, the fever round, with germs hopping.  
  *Parent says:* Round two: FEVER! The body has turned up its heat. Germs, you must hop now. Many germs grow more slowly when the body is hotter.  
  *Child mode:* Round two: FEVER! Germs must hop. Is it easier to catch them now?
  - Check: In Germ Tag, the germ players must hop in the fever round. What does the hopping stand for? → **Many germs grow more slowly when the body is hotter**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 3 with the white blood cell starting next to the germs.  
  *Parent says:* Round three: the white blood cell remembers these germs from before. So they start right next to the germs.  
  *Child mode:* Round three: the white blood cell remembers you. They start right beside you. Run!
- **s5 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit down together and talk.  
  *Parent says:* Which round was hardest for the germs? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Which round was hardest for the germs? Say why.
  - Think: In round three the white blood cell remembered. You will learn how the body remembers germs in a later unit.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Apple Skin Test (35 min, 7 days)
- **question:** Does unbroken skin keep germs out of an apple? What happens if the skin is cut, and what helps a cut?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which apple will go soft and bad first? Which will stay fresh longest? Why do you think so? Write your guess for each apple.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use four apples that are the same kind and look the same. Make each cut the same size, with the same clean knife. Keep all four bags in the same place. Only one thing changes: what happens to the skin.
- **safety:** Only a grown-up uses the knife. Wash hands with soap after touching the soil. Never open the bags after day 1, because mould can grow inside. Throw the closed bags away at the end.
- **record:** Each time you look, write one or two words for each apple: fresh, brown, soft patch, soft and wet, or mouldy. On day 7, draw each apple and show where the soft patch began.
- **results table:** Apple | What we did | Day 1 | Day 2 | Day 3 | Day 5 | Day 7
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Label the apples. A grown-up makes the cuts. Rub soil on two cuts, and rinse one of them. Close the bags. (20 min); day 2: Look at each apple through the bag. Write what you see. (3 min); day 3: Look again and write what you see. (3 min); day 5: Look again and write what you see. (3 min); day 7: Last look. Compare the apples. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (6 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the four apples. Ask them to guess which will go bad first and why.  
  *Parent says:* Here are four apples. An apple's skin is a bit like your skin. It is a wall. We are going to test what happens if the wall is broken. Which apple do you think will go bad first?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the four apples. Their skin is like your skin. Guess which apple will go bad first. Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The cut one with dirt, because germs can get in.; The whole one will stay fresh, because the skin keeps germs out.)
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the apples A, B, C and D. 2. Leave A whole. 3. Make one cut about 2 centimetres long in B, C and D, all the same size.  
  *Parent says:* Apple A keeps its skin whole. I will make a small cut in B, C and D. Watch: I make each cut the same size, so the test is fair.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts apples B, C and D. Apple A stays whole. Each cut is the same size.
  - Note: Use a clean knife and wipe it between apples. Cuts about 2 cm long and shallow are enough.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub a little soil into the cuts on C and D with a finger. 2. Rinse cut D under running tap water for 10 seconds, then pat it dry with a clean cloth. 3. Wash hands with soap. 4. Put each apple in its own bag, close it and label it.  
  *Parent says:* Soil has lots of microbes in it, and some are the kind that make fruit rot. C gets soil and stays dirty. D gets soil, then we wash it, like you would wash a scraped knee. B is cut but clean.  
  *Child mode:* Rub a little soil on cuts C and D. Rinse cut D under the tap. Wash your hands with soap. Put each apple in its own bag.
  - Check: In the apple skin test, why do we wash cut D but not cut C? → **To see if washing a cut helps**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the apples through the bags on days 2, 3, 5 and 7. Each time, write a word or two for each apple in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look at our apples. Don't open the bags. Which ones have changed? Where on the apple did the change start?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each apple through its bag. Don't open it. Write what you see. Where did the change start?
  - Note: Keep the bags somewhere warm and out of direct sun, not in the fridge, because microbes grow slowly in the cold. A cut apple goes a little brown quickly even without microbes, because the air reacts with it. Rotting is different: the apple goes soft, wet and dark around the cut, and mould may appear. That is what microbes do. If nothing has gone soft by day 7, keep looking for a few more days.
  - Wait: Do this step again on days 2, 3, 5 and 7.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 7, compare all four apples together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all four apples. Which stayed freshest? Which went bad first? Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Look at all four. Which one stayed fresh? Which one went bad first? Was your guess right?
  - Check: In the apple skin test, apple A (skin whole) stayed fresh longest. What does that tell us? → **Unbroken skin keeps microbes out**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: skin keeps germs out, and washing a cut helps. What I saw: apple A stayed fresh, apple C went soft first, starting at the cut, and apple D went soft later than C. Why: the cut let microbes in, and washing took many of them away. Throw the closed bags away without opening them.
### e2 · My Temperature Through the Day (25 min)
- **question:** Is my body temperature the same all day? What is normal for me?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will your temperature be the same all day? Will it be higher after running about? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, measure the same way every time: the same thermometer, under the same arm, held for the same time until it beeps. Do not measure straight after a hot bath or a cold drink.
- **safety:** Use only a digital thermometer. Old glass thermometers can break and they hold mercury, which is poisonous. Wipe the tip clean before and after each use.
- **record:** Write each temperature in the table. Then make a line chart: time along the bottom, temperature up the side.
- **results table:** Time | What I had just been doing | Temperature (degrees Celsius)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the thermometer and how to switch it on.  
  *Parent says:* This is a thermometer. It measures how hot your body is, in degrees Celsius. Today we will find out what is normal for you. Then, if you ever have a fever, we will know.  
  *Child mode:* This is a thermometer. It tells how hot your body is. Today you find out your normal number.
  - Ask: Do you think your temperature is the same all day? (good: I think it changes a little.; Maybe it goes up when I run.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wipe the tip. 2. Tuck it deep under one armpit, against the skin. 3. Hold the arm down until it beeps. 4. Read and write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Tuck the tip right into your armpit. Squeeze your arm down. Keep still until it beeps. Now, what does it say?  
  *Child mode:* Tuck the tip under your arm. Squeeze your arm down. Wait for the beep. Read the number and write it down.
  - Note: Under the arm reads a little lower than in the mouth. That's fine, as long as you measure the same way each time.
  - Check: The thermometer reads 36.7 degrees Celsius. Is that a fever? → **No, it is normal**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure again three more times today: after running about, in the afternoon and in the evening.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure again after you have run about or jumped for a few minutes. Then this afternoon, and after dinner. Remember to sit still for a few minutes first, and measure the same way.  
  *Child mode:* Measure again: after playing, in the afternoon and in the evening. Same arm, same way. Write each number.
  - Wait: Come back to this step later in the day.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a line chart of their temperatures.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw our numbers as a line. Time goes along the bottom. Temperature goes up the side. Put a dot for each one and join the dots.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a dot for each temperature. Time along the bottom. Temperature up the side. Join the dots.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think about your temperature now? What did you see? Why might it change?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: my temperature changes a little in a day, but it stays close to 37. What I saw: it was lowest in the morning, 36.3, and highest after running, 36.9. Why: running makes heat, and the body is coolest early in the morning. Write the child's normal range on the thermometer box, so you can compare when they are ill.
### e3 · The Sticky Nose Test (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Does a sticky surface, like the mucus in your nose, trap more dust than a dry one?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which card will catch more dust specks: the sticky one or the smooth one? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use two cards the same size, with the same tape, in the same place, for the same time. Only one thing changes: on one card the sticky side of the tape faces up.
- **safety:** Wash hands after handling the dusty cards. Put the cards where a baby or a pet cannot reach them.
- **record:** Count the dust specks between the lines on each card twice, and write the counts in the table. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each card.
- **results table:** Card | Specks between the lines, count 1 | Count 2 | Halfway between the two counts
- **schedule:** day 1: Make the two cards: sticky tape facing up on one, smooth tape facing up on the other. Lay both flat near an open window. (10 min); day 2: Turn each card over and tap it once. Count the dust specks between the lines on each card with a magnifying glass. Make a bar chart. (15 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to breathe in through their nose and think about where the dust in the air goes.  
  *Parent says:* The air we breathe has dust in it, and germs ride on the dust. Inside your nose are tiny hairs and sticky mucus. Today we'll make a pretend nose and test if sticky catches more.  
  *Child mode:* The air has dust in it. Germs ride on dust. Your nose has hairs and sticky mucus. Guess: does sticky catch more dust?
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stick a strip of clear tape about 8 cm long across each card. On card 1, lay it sticky side up and hold its two ends down with small bits of tape. On card 2, stick it down the normal way, smooth side up. 2. Draw two pencil lines 4 cm apart across each strip. 3. Lay both cards flat by an open window, and tape their corners down.  
  *Parent says:* This sticky tape is like the mucus in your nose. The smooth tape is like a nose with no mucus. Both go in the same place for the same time, so the test is fair.  
  *Child mode:* Stick tape sticky side up on one card. Stick tape smooth side up on the other. Draw two lines on each. Put both by an open window.
  - Note: Count only between the two lines, so both cards are counted over the same area. If the sticky card has fewer than 10 specks after a day, leave both cards one more day.
  - Wait: Leave the cards for one day.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold each card upside down and tap it once, so loose dust falls off. Then count the specks between the lines on each card with the magnifying glass, twice. Write both counts, then the number halfway between them.  
  *Parent says:* First we turn each card over and tap it. Air rushes through your nose with every breath. Only dust that stays stuck has really been trapped. Now let's count the specks between the lines on each card. Count twice, because it's easy to miss some. Then we find the number halfway between the two counts.  
  *Child mode:* Turn each card over and tap it once. Use the magnifying glass. Count the specks between the lines. Count twice. Write the numbers.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart, one bar for each card.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a bar for each card. Make it as tall as the number of specks.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each card. The taller bar caught more dust.
  - Check: In the sticky nose test, the sticky card caught many more specks than the smooth card. What does that show about mucus? → **Sticky mucus traps dust and germs before they go deeper**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: I think sticky mucus traps dust. I saw 42 specks on the sticky card and only 6 on the smooth card. Because dust that touches something sticky stays there. Then ask: so what should we do with what we blow out of our nose? (Blow into a hanky or tissue, throw the tissue away, wash hands.)

## Activities
### a1 · Onion Tears (10 min)
- **safety:** Only a grown-up uses the knife. Do not rub your eyes with onion on your hands. If they sting, step away and blink.
- **record:** Draw your eye before and after the onion. Write one sentence about what your tears did.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Cut an onion in half and then into slices, with your child standing nearby.  
  *Parent says:* I'm going to cut an onion. Stand here and watch. Don't touch your eyes. Just notice what happens to them.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts an onion. Stand near and watch. Don't touch your eyes. What do you notice?
  - Ask: What do you notice? What do you wonder? (good: My eyes are stinging.; My eyes are getting wet.; Why do onions make us cry?)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hand your child the mirror. Then explain what the tears are doing.  
  *Parent says:* Cut onions let out an invisible gas that stings the eyes. Your eyes don't wait for you to decide. They make extra tears straight away and wash the gas out.    **Answer:** Both catch the drops. But my hand then touches food, faces and door handles, and passes the germs on. My elbow hardly touches anything.. *Why:* A strong answer says that hands spread germs after the sneeze, and uses the spots on the paper from the Sneeze Stopper test as evidence that a sleeve catches drops.
12. (draw) Draw three pictures to show a phagocyte eating a germ: touch, wrap, swallow.  
   **Answer:** 1. A blob cell touching a small germ. 2. The cell's edges wrapping around the germ. 3. The germ inside the cell.. *Why:* Phagocytes eat germs by wrapping around them and pulling them inside, where chemicals break them down.
13. (fill) A white blood cell that eats germs is called a ____. The word means 'eating cell'.  
   **Answer:** ['phagocyte']. *Why:* Phagocyte comes from old Greek words for eating and cell. Élie Metchnikoff studied these cells, starting with baby starfish in 1882.
14. (mcq) Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria. Shanthi, a girl in Class 4, has a cold, which is caused by a virus. Will an antibiotic help her get better?  
   Options: Yes, antibiotics kill every kind of germ / No. Antibiotics do not work on viruses. Rest, water and her own immune system will beat the cold. / Only if she takes a double dose  
   **Answer:** No. Antibiotics do not work on viruses. Rest, water and her own immune system will beat the cold.. *Why:* Many people think antibiotics cure colds. They do not, because a cold is caused by a virus, not bacteria. Taking antibiotics when they are not needed also helps bacteria learn to survive them. A strong answer says why: antibiotics work on bacteria only.
15. (match) After a cut, you can see signs of the body's fight. Match each sign to what is causing it.

16. (mcq) A vaccine shows the immune system a harmless form of a germ, such as a dead or weakened one. Why does this help when the real germ comes later?

   **Answer:** The body recognises the germ and fights them much faster. *Why:* The immune system remembers the shapes on a germ. After a vaccine, the body has met those shapes already. When the real germ arrives, the body is ready, and fights before the germ can make you very ill.
### Me, Not-Me and Every Living Thing (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Your immune system does not attack the food you eat, even though food comes from outside. What is one idea scientists have for why?  
   Options: Food has no shapes on it / The stomach hides food from white blood cells forever / It attacks only things that send 'danger' signals, like hurt cells / White blood cells are asleep after meals  
   **Answer:** It attacks only things that send 'danger' signals, like hurt cells. *Why:* Polly Matzinger's 'danger model' says the immune system reacts to danger, not just to anything new. Food, friendly microbes and a growing baby don't send danger signals. Most scientists think danger and 'self' both matter. Food does have shapes, so that answer doesn't work.
2. (mcq) Plants have no blood and no white blood cells. How do they defend themselves?  
   Options: They can't defend themselves at all / Waxy leaves, closing leaf pores, sticky sap and chemical alarms / They run away from germs / They grow white blood cells in their roots  
   **Answer:** Waxy leaves, closing leaf pores, sticky sap and chemical alarms. *Why:* Plants have their own defences. They close the tiny holes in their leaves when germs land, seal wounds with sap, and send chemical warnings to other leaves. Picking 'can't defend' is natural, because we can't see a plant's fight.
3. (mcq) Bacteria are single cells. Can they defend themselves against viruses?  
   Options: No, bacteria have no defences / Only if they live inside people / Yes, some cut up virus pieces and remember them, using a system called CRISPR / Only in hot places  
   **Answer:** Yes, some cut up virus pieces and remember them, using a system called CRISPR. *Why:* In 2007, scientists found that some bacteria, like those used to make yoghurt, store bits of the viruses that attacked them. Next time, they use these bits to recognise the virus and cut them up. Even a single cell has an immune system.
4. (mcq) A body thermometer reads 101 degrees Fahrenheit. What is that in Celsius, roughly, and is it a fever?  
   Options: About 101 degrees Celsius: boiling hot / About 38.3 degrees Celsius: yes, a fever / About 36 degrees Celsius: normal / You can't tell  
   **Answer:** About 38.3 degrees Celsius: yes, a fever. *Why:* Normal is about 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit, which is 37 degrees Celsius. A fever starts at about 100.4 degrees Fahrenheit, which is 38 degrees Celsius. So 101 Fahrenheit is about 38.3 Celsius, a fever. Picking 101 Celsius mixes up the two scales.
5. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 3, took her temperature four times in one day. It read 36.3 degrees Celsius at 7 am and 36.9 after running. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Her body has a fever after running / Body temperature changes a little through the day, and moving muscles make heat / The thermometer is broken  
   **Answer:** Body temperature changes a little through the day, and moving muscles make heat. *Why:* A small change of half a degree or so is normal. Moving muscles make heat, and the body is coolest early in the morning. 36.9 is not a fever, because a fever is 38 or more.
6. (truefalse) White blood cells sometimes attack the body's own cells by mistake.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. This is called an autoimmune illness. It shows how hard the job of telling 'me' from 'not me' is. You will learn more in Class 5.

   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* The signal comes first, then the new setting, then shivering to reach it. When the fight ends, the setting drops, and you sweat to cool down.

The lizards kept cool were more likely to die, so without fever I might get more ill, or stay ill longer.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a prediction and a reason, then uses the lizards as evidence. ## Quiz
1. (mcq) Ravi, a boy in Class 3, says the immune system is one organ, like the heart. Is he right?  
   Options: Yes. It sits in the chest / No. It is many parts working together, like skin, tears, mucus, white blood cells and fever / Yes. It is the stomach  
   **Answer:** No. It is many parts working together, like skin, tears, mucus, white blood cells and fever. *Why:* The immune system is not one organ. It is everything the body does to keep germs out and to fight the ones that get in.
2. (mcq) Germs on an unwashed guava go into your mouth. Saliva washes some of them down. Which wall do the swallowed germs meet next?  
   Options: Tears / Nose hairs / Stomach acid  
   **Answer:** Stomach acid. *Why:* The acid in the stomach kills many germs that come in with food. Tears guard the eyes, and nose hairs guard the nose.
3. (mcq) Why do your eyes fill with tears when someone cuts an onion near you?  
   Options: You feel sad / Tears wash out the stinging gas / Onions are full of water that jumps into your eyes / Your eyes are tired  
   **Answer:** Tears wash out the stinging gas. *Why:* Tears are a defence that washes the eye. Cut onions give off a gas that stings, so the eyes make extra tears. Picking 'sad' shows we often think tears are only for feelings.
4. (mcq) When you have a cold, your nose runs with extra mucus. How might that help you?  
   Options: The extra mucus traps germs and carries them out / The extra mucus feeds the germs / It does not help at all  
   **Answer:** The extra mucus traps germs and carries them out. *Why:* Mucus is a sticky trap. More mucus means more germs are caught and blown out or swallowed. A runny nose is annoying, but it is part of the fight.

   Options: Wraps round the germ and swallows them / Runs away from the germ / Turns into a germ  
   **Answer:** Wraps round the germ and swallows them. *Why:* A phagocyte is a white blood cell that eats germs. They wrap round the germ, pull them inside and break them down with chemicals.

   **Answer:** No. Both are normal, and a fever is 38 or more. *Why:* A small change through the day is normal, and playing makes heat. Doctors usually call 38 degrees Celsius or more a fever.

## Applications
- Doctors wash their hands and wear gloves, so they do not carry germs through a patient's broken skin.
- First aid: washing a scrape with clean water and covering it with a clean plaster helps the skin wall heal.
- Thermometers at home and in clinics tell families when a child has a fever.
- Hospitals count white blood cells in a blood test. Too many or too few can tell a doctor that the body is fighting something.
- Vaccines teach white blood cells to recognise a germ safely. You will learn how in Class 4.
- Masks and covering sneezes stop germs travelling in drops from one person to the next.
- Food safety: cooking, boiling water and keeping food covered help stomach acid by sending fewer germs down.
- Eye drops that are like tears help people whose eyes do not make enough tears.
- Lysozyme from egg white is used to help keep some foods, like some cheeses, from spoiling.
- Plant scientists breed crops with stronger natural defences, so farmers need fewer sprays.
- Scientists use the CRISPR system, borrowed from the defences of bacteria, as a tool to edit genes.
- Doctors grow and study white blood cells in labs to find new treatments for illnesses.

## Open questions
- **Why does the same germ make one person very ill and another hardly ill at all?** Genes, age, sleep, food, earlier illnesses and luck all seem to play a part. Scientists are still working out how they fit together.
- **Exactly how does the immune system decide what is 'me' and what is 'not me'?** There are strong ideas, like learning 'self' early and listening for danger signals. But nobody has the full answer yet.
- **How does the immune system talk to the brain, and make us feel tired and grumpy when ill?** Chemicals from white blood cells reach the brain. Scientists think this makes us rest, but how it all works is still being studied.
- **Is bringing a fever down good, bad or neither for getting better?** Studies give mixed answers. Fever helps animals survive infections, but fever medicines in children do not seem to make illnesses much longer.
- **Why do some immune systems attack harmless things, like pollen or peanuts?** Allergies have become more common in many countries. Scientists have several ideas, including less contact with harmless microbes when young, but it is not settled.
- **How many kinds of white blood cells are there, really?** Every few years scientists find new kinds, or new jobs for old kinds. The list keeps growing.
- **Do our friendly microbes change how our immune system works, and even how we feel?** Experiments in mice suggest gut microbes affect the immune system a lot. How much this is true in people is still being studied.
- **How did the first living things begin to tell themselves apart from the outside world?** Even the oldest cells had a skin, a membrane, keeping inside separate from outside. How the first defences began, billions of years ago, is a mystery.
