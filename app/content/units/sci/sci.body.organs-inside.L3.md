# Organs inside you: where they sit and what they do
`sci.body.organs-inside.L3`

**Summary.** Most people think the stomach is behind the belly button, but it sits higher, under the ribs on the left. An organ is a body part with a special job. Your heart pumps blood, your lungs take in air, your stomach churns food and your brain sends and receives messages. Bone and liquid protect the soft organs, and each organ needs all the others to stay alive. Scientists know where organs are and what they do, but not yet how a brain makes a thought.

**Intro.** *Put your hand flat on your chest and keep very still. You can feel a beat under your hand. It started before you were born, and it goes on while you sleep. What is beating, and where exactly is it?* Your heart, lungs, stomach and brain are hidden under your skin and bones. You cannot see them, but you can feel what they do. You can feel a beat in your chest, air filling you up and a full feeling after dinner. In this unit you find out where each organ sits, what it does and how the organs help each other.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what an organ is: a body part with a special job, made of many living cells.
1. I can point to where my heart, lungs, stomach and brain are, on my own body and on a drawing.
2. I can say the main job of the heart, the lungs, the stomach and the brain in one sentence each.
3. I can explain how the skull and the ribs protect the organs inside them.
4. I can give an example of organs working as a team, like when I run or eat.
5. I can run a fair test with a model organ, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
6. I can say what scientists agree about these organs, and what they still do not know, like how the brain makes thoughts.
7. I can give surprising examples: one cell that uses oxygen, breaks down food and senses on its own, an octopus with three hearts, and mini-organs grown in labs.

## Terms
- **Organ**: A body part with a special job. It is made of many tiny living cells working together. *e.g.* Your heart is an organ. Its job is to pump blood.
- **Heart**: A strong muscle about the size of your fist. It pumps blood all around your body. *e.g.* When you run fast, you can feel your heart thumping in your chest.
- **Lungs**: Two soft, spongy organs in your chest. They take in air and pass oxygen into your blood. *e.g.* When you blow up a balloon, the air comes out of your lungs.
- **Stomach**: A stretchy bag shaped like a letter J. It holds your food, mixes it with juices and churns it into a soup. *e.g.* After a big plate of rice and dal, your stomach stretches to hold it all.
- **Brain**: A soft, wrinkly organ inside your head. It sends and receives messages for the whole body. It helps you think, feel, remember and move. *e.g.* Your brain is working right now, helping you read and understand these words.
- **Cell**: The tiniest living piece of a body. Your body is made of about 30 trillion of them. Each one is far too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Your skin, your blood and your heart are all made of cells.
- **Tissue**: A group of the same kind of cells working together. Different tissues join together to make an organ. *e.g.* Muscle tissue in your heart squeezes. Nerve tissue in your brain carries messages.
- **Blood**: The red liquid that flows all over your body through tubes called blood vessels. It carries oxygen and food to every cell, and takes waste away. *e.g.* When you scrape your knee, the red drops are blood.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air that nearly every cell in your body needs to stay alive. You cannot see it or smell it. *e.g.* Plants give out oxygen. You breathe it in through your nose.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A waste gas your cells make. Your blood carries it to your lungs, and you breathe it out. *e.g.* The air you breathe out has much more carbon dioxide in it than the air you breathe in.
- **Nerve**: A thin cord, like a cable, made of the long threads of nerve cells. It carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body. *e.g.* When you touch a hot tawa, nerves carry the message 'hot!' to your brain very fast.
- **Spinal cord**: A thick bundle of nerves that runs from your brain down the middle of your back. It sits safe inside your backbone. *e.g.* Run your finger down the bumps on your back. Your spinal cord is inside those bones.
- **Skull**: The hard bony case around your brain. It works like a helmet. *e.g.* Knock gently on your head. The hard part you feel is your skull.
- **Rib cage**: The curved bones around your chest. They make a cage that guards your heart and lungs. *e.g.* Press gently on the sides of your chest. The hard curved bars are your ribs.
- **Diaphragm**: A dome-shaped muscle under your lungs. When it pulls down, air rushes into your lungs. *e.g.* When you hiccup, your diaphragm is giving a sudden little jerk.
- **Food pipe**: The tube that carries food from your mouth down to your stomach. Doctors call it the oesophagus. *e.g.* When you swallow a sip of water, it slides down your food pipe in a few seconds.
- **Intestines**: Long coiled tubes after the stomach. They take the useful parts of food into your blood. *e.g.* Most of your 'tummy' below the belly button is intestines, not stomach.
- **Digestive juices**: Liquids your body makes to break food down into tiny pieces. The stomach's juice has a strong acid in it. *e.g.* When you smell hot pakoras, your mouth fills with saliva, or spit. Saliva is a digestive juice too.
- **Organ system**: A team of organs that work together for one big job. *e.g.* Your mouth, food pipe, stomach and intestines are a team for digesting food.
- **X-ray**: A kind of light that our eyes cannot see. It passes through the soft parts of the body, but bones block most of it. Doctors use it to take pictures of your inside. *e.g.* If you break your arm, a doctor takes an X-ray to see the bone.

## Explanations
### What an organ is · *defined*
**Definition:** An organ is a body part with a special job. It is made of different tissues, and each tissue is made of many cells of one kind.

Think of a house. Bricks are like cells. A wall of bricks is like a tissue. A whole room, built from walls, a floor and a door, is like an organ. A kitchen is a room with a special job. Your stomach is an organ with a special job. Your body has many organs. This unit looks at four big ones inside you: the heart, the lungs, the stomach and the brain. Your skin is an organ too, and it is the biggest one you have.
- e.g. The heart is an organ. Its job is pumping blood.
- e.g. The eye is an organ. Its job is seeing.
- e.g. The skin is an organ. Its job is to cover and protect you.
### The body map: where the four organs live · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain is in your head. The heart and lungs are in your chest, behind the ribs. The stomach is in the upper left of your belly, partly tucked under the lowest ribs.

Start at the top. Your brain fills the inside of your head, behind your eyes and above your ears. Go down your neck into your chest. Here your two lungs fill most of the space, one on each side. Your heart is tucked between them, in the middle of your chest, tipped a little to the left. Now go down to where your ribs end. Your stomach sits on the left, partly tucked under the lowest ribs. Many people think the stomach is behind the belly button. It is higher than that. Most of the soft belly below it is filled with your intestines.
- e.g. Put a hand on the middle of your chest. Your heart is just behind your breastbone, the flat bone in the middle.
- e.g. Breathe in deeply with your hands on your ribs. You can feel your lungs filling and your ribs lifting out.
- e.g. Press gently just under your left ribs after a big meal. That full feeling is your stomach.
### The heart: a pump that never rests · *defined*
**Definition:** The heart is a hollow muscle about the size of your fist. It squeezes again and again to pump blood all around the body.

Your heart is a bag of strong muscle with four rooms inside. Each squeeze pushes blood out into tubes that reach every part of you. Blood carries oxygen and food to every cell, and carries waste away. Then the heart relaxes, fills with blood again and squeezes again. In a grown-up, this happens about 70 times every minute. That is about one hundred thousand beats every day. A child's heart beats a little faster. The heart is in the middle of your chest, tipped a little to the left. That is why you feel the beat most on the left. The heart even has its own tiny timekeeper inside it, which starts each beat. It can keep beating for a while even after it is taken out of a body.
- e.g. A heart going to a person who needs a new one is kept cold in a box. Once it is joined up and warmed, it can start beating again in the new body.
- e.g. When you are scared, your heart beats faster. Your brain sends a message to speed it up.
- e.g. A blue whale's heart is as heavy as two grown-up people. A mouse's heart is smaller than a peanut.
### The lungs: two spongy air bags · *defined*
**Definition:** The lungs are two soft, spongy organs in the chest. They bring in oxygen from the air and get rid of carbon dioxide.

You have two lungs, one on each side of your heart. They fill most of your chest, from just above your collarbones to low down inside your ribs. Air comes in through your nose or mouth and goes down your windpipe. The windpipe splits into two tubes, one for each lung. Inside, the tubes branch again and again, like the branches of a tree. At the very ends are hundreds of millions of tiny air bags. Oxygen passes from these bags into the blood. Carbon dioxide passes out of the blood and into the bags, and you breathe it out. Your left lung is a little smaller than your right one. It has a dent in it, to make room for your heart. Lungs cannot pull in air by themselves. A dome-shaped muscle under them, the diaphragm, pulls down to make them fill with air.
- e.g. When you blow out birthday candles, the air comes from your lungs.
- e.g. The tiny air bags in your lungs, all spread out flat, would cover a large part of a badminton court.
- e.g. Lungs are so full of air that a piece of lung floats in water.
### The stomach: a stretchy bag that churns · *defined*
**Definition:** The stomach is a stretchy, muscly bag at the end of the food pipe. It holds food, mixes it with strong juices and churns it into a thick soup.

When you swallow, food slides down your food pipe into your stomach. Your stomach sits under your ribs on the left side, shaped a bit like the letter J. When it is empty, it is small and wrinkly. When you eat, it stretches. A grown-up's stomach can comfortably hold about one litre of food, like a big water bottle. The stomach walls are made of muscle. They squeeze and churn the food, like hands kneading dough. The stomach also makes a juice with a strong acid in it. The acid helps break down food and kills many germs. Why does the stomach not digest itself? It covers its inside with a thick slimy layer, called mucus, and it keeps replacing its lining. After two to four hours, the soupy food moves on into the intestines.
- e.g. After a festival feast you feel full and tight under your ribs. That is your stomach stretched wide.
- e.g. A tummy rumble is mostly gas and liquid being squeezed through your stomach and intestines.
- e.g. Some people have had their whole stomach removed because of an illness. They can still live, by eating small meals often.
### The brain: the body's message centre · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain is a soft organ inside the skull. It receives messages from the body, sends messages back and lets us think, feel, remember and move.

Your brain sits inside your skull, floating in a clear liquid that cushions it. In a grown-up it weighs about 1.3 kilograms, a little more than a 1-kilogram packet of rice. It is made of about 86 billion nerve cells, joined in a huge web. Messages race along nerves between the brain and every part of your body. Your eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin send messages in. Your brain sends messages out to your muscles. Some parts of your brain work without you thinking at all. They keep you breathing and change how fast your heart beats, even while you sleep. The brain uses about a fifth of your body's energy, even though it is a small part of your weight. The brain itself cannot feel pain. That is why doctors can sometimes operate on a brain while the person is awake.
- e.g. When a cricket ball flies towards you, your eyes tell your brain and your brain tells your hands to catch it.
- e.g. When you sleep, your brain keeps you breathing and sorts out what you learned that day.
- e.g. Messages cross over. The left side of your brain moves the right side of your body.
### Bones that guard the soft organs · *defined*
**Definition:** The skull is a hard case around the brain. The rib cage is a cage of curved bones around the heart and lungs.

Your brain, heart and lungs are soft and very important. A hard knock could hurt them. So your body keeps them inside bone. The skull is like a helmet. Inside it, the brain floats in a clear liquid, so it does not bang against the bone when you jump. Your ribs curve round from your backbone to the front of your chest, like the bars of a birdcage. They protect your heart and lungs. But the ribs are not a solid box. They are joined by muscles, and they swing up and out every time you breathe in. This gives your lungs room to get bigger. Your stomach sits partly under the bottom ribs, so it is partly guarded too.
- e.g. A cricket helmet protects your skull, which protects your brain. That is two helmets.
- e.g. When you breathe in deeply, your ribs swing up and out, like the handle of a bucket being lifted.
- e.g. A coconut has a hard shell and liquid inside, a bit like your skull and the liquid around your brain.
### Organs work as a team · *defined*
**Definition:** Every organ needs the others. The body only works because organs pass things and messages to each other all the time.

Follow a breath and a bite. Your lungs take oxygen from the air. Your stomach and intestines take food from your plate. Your blood picks up both. Your heart pumps that blood to every cell, including the cells of the lungs, the stomach and the heart itself. Your brain keeps watch over all of it. When you run, your brain tells your heart to beat faster and your lungs to breathe faster. When you have just eaten, more blood goes to your stomach. If one organ stops, the others soon have trouble. Even the brain needs the heart's blood every second.
- e.g. Running for the school bus: the brain speeds up the heart and breathing, so the leg muscles get more oxygen.
- e.g. After a big lunch, more blood flows to your stomach and intestines, to carry away the food they take in.
- e.g. If the heart stops for even a few minutes, the brain is badly hurt, because no fresh blood reaches it.
### Zoom in: every cell does a little of every job · *defined*
**Definition:** Organs are made of cells. Almost every cell uses oxygen, breaks down food and sends and receives signals, in a tiny way of its own.

Look at a cell in your toe. It is far from your lungs, but it uses oxygen. Tiny parts inside it, called mitochondria, use oxygen and food to make energy. They give out carbon dioxide, which the blood carries back to the lungs. The cell also has tiny bags of strong juice that break down food and worn-out parts, a little like a tiny stomach. And it has sensors on its outside that notice chemicals and pass on signals. Organs are what happens when huge numbers of cells share out the work. Heart cells are very good at squeezing. Nerve cells are very good at carrying messages. But almost every cell still uses oxygen, breaks down food and senses in its own small way. The first living things, long ago, were single cells, and each one did every job by itself.
- e.g. A single amoeba in pond water has no stomach. It wraps itself around food and breaks it down inside a little bubble.
- e.g. Heart muscle cells grown in a dish in a lab start to twitch and beat by themselves.
- e.g. A bacterium has no brain, but it can sense food and swim towards it.
### Does the gut have a mind of its own? · *debated*
Your stomach and intestines are wrapped in a net of about 500 million nerve cells. This net can run the squeezing of the gut by itself, even if the nerves to the brain are cut. Some scientists call it the 'second brain'. The gut also sends many messages up to the brain. Scientists are now studying how the gut, and the germs that live in it, may affect our feelings. But how much it matters is still argued about.
- e.g. People talk about 'butterflies in the tummy' when they are nervous. The brain and the gut are talking to each other.
- e.g. In tests, the gut of an animal kept squeezing food along even after it had been cut off from the brain.
- e.g. Most of the body's serotonin, a chemical the brain also uses for mood, is made in the gut.
- **Michael Gershon, a scientist who wrote a book called The Second Brain in 1998:** The gut's nerve net is so big and so independent that it is fair to call it a second brain. It makes its own decisions about digesting food.
- **Many brain scientists:** The gut's nerve net runs digestion very well. But it is much smaller and simpler than the brain, so calling it a brain can confuse people.
- **Scientists studying gut germs:** The trillions of germs in our gut seem to send signals that reach the brain. In mice, they change behaviour. Whether they change human feelings much is still being tested.
### Is the brain where 'you' are? · *debated*
Scientists agree the brain is needed for thinking and feeling. If part of the brain is hurt, a person can lose words or memories, or even feel differently. But nobody knows how a lump of nerve cells makes the feeling of being you. People have argued about this for thousands of years. Here are the main ideas.
- e.g. People with an artificial heart pump still think, love and laugh as before. That shows the heart is not where thinking happens.
- e.g. When you dream, your brain makes a whole world that is not really there.
- e.g. Brain scans show different parts of the brain lighting up when you read, sing or feel scared.
- **Most brain scientists:** The mind is what the brain does, the way digestion is what the stomach does. When we understand the brain fully, we will understand the mind.
- **David Chalmers, a philosopher, in 1995:** We can explain how the brain sorts messages. But it is much harder to explain why it feels like anything at all to be you. He called this the 'hard problem'.
- **The Upanishads and many sages of India:** Awareness is not made by the body. The body, brain and mind appear in awareness, the way waves appear in the sea.
### Other animals, other plans · *defined*
**Definition:** Most animals have organs for pumping, breathing, digesting and sensing, but they come in many shapes and numbers.

An octopus has three hearts. Two pump blood through its gills and one pumps blood round its body. It also has a brain, and most of its nerve cells are in its eight arms. A cow's stomach has four parts. It brings grass back up to chew again, which is called chewing the cud. A fish has no lungs. It breathes with gills. But many fish have an air bag inside called a swim bladder, and scientists have found that lungs and swim bladders come from the same ancient beginning. A starfish can push its stomach out of its mouth to digest a mussel outside its body. And a sponge, one of the oldest kinds of animals, has no organs at all. Every cell does its own breathing and eating.
- e.g. Cows and buffaloes chewing slowly while resting are chewing the cud.
- e.g. An earthworm has five pairs of pumping tubes near its front end, often called its five hearts.
- e.g. A jellyfish has no brain. It has a net of nerves spread all through its body.
### How we found out what is inside · *defined*
**Definition:** People learned about organs by looking carefully inside bodies, by testing ideas, and later by using X-rays and scanners.

For thousands of years, nobody could see inside a living body. The ancient Egyptians, when they made mummies, took out the brain and threw it away, but kept the heart in the body. They thought the heart was where a person's thinking lived. The Greek thinker Aristotle thought so too. About 2,300 years ago, Herophilus, a Greek doctor in Alexandria, cut open human bodies and traced the nerves back to the brain. About 450 years later, the Greek doctor Galen, working in Rome, cut nerves in living animals and showed that the brain controls the voice and movement. In India, the Sushruta Samhita, an old book of surgery, told students to soak a body in a stream for seven days, then gently brush away the skin and flesh layer by layer. In 1543, Andreas Vesalius printed careful drawings of the inside of the human body, and fixed many old mistakes. In 1895, Wilhelm Röntgen found X-rays, and for the first time doctors could see bones inside a living person. Today, scanners like ultrasound and MRI let doctors see a beating heart and a working brain, without any cutting.
- e.g. A pregnant mother can see her baby's beating heart on an ultrasound screen.
- e.g. The very first X-ray of a hand was of the hand of Röntgen's wife, Anna Bertha, wearing her ring.
- e.g. Doctors use X-rays to find a swallowed coin or marble inside a child.
### The frontier: growing mini-organs · *idea*
Scientists can now grow tiny bits of organs in a dish, from a person's own cells. They are called organoids. In 2013, Madeline Lancaster, working in Jürgen Knoblich's lab in Vienna, grew tiny 'mini-brains' the size of a lentil. Others have grown mini-stomachs and mini-guts. These help scientists test medicines and understand illnesses. Nobody can yet grow a full working heart, lung, stomach or brain to put into a person. Scientists hope this may happen one day, but nobody knows when.
- e.g. Mini-guts grown from a sick child's cells can be used to test which medicine works best for that child.
- e.g. Scientists have built machines that pump blood for people waiting for a new heart.
- e.g. Mini-brains in a dish are far too small and simple to work like a whole brain.

## Mostly agreed
- The heart is in the middle of the chest, tipped a little to the left, and it pumps blood around the body. *How we know:* Doctors can see it beating with ultrasound. In 1628, William Harvey measured how much blood the heart pushes out and showed that blood goes round and round.
- The lungs take oxygen from the air into the blood and send carbon dioxide out. *How we know:* The air you breathe out has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than the air you breathe in. Scientists have measured this many times.
- The stomach holds food and breaks it down with churning muscles and strong juices. *How we know:* In the 1820s, a doctor called William Beaumont watched digestion through a hole that a gunshot left in a man's stomach. Today, doctors look inside with a tiny camera on a tube.
- The brain controls the body and is needed for thinking, feeling and remembering. *How we know:* When a part of the brain is hurt, the job of that part changes or stops. Scanners show parts of the brain becoming busy during different tasks.
- Organs are made of cells, and every organ depends on the others. *How we know:* Microscopes show cells in every organ. When one organ fails, doctors see other organs struggle too.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, Herophilus and Galen, About 350 BCE (Aristotle), about 300 BCE (Herophilus) and about 160 CE (Galen's pig experiment).** About 2,350 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle taught that the heart does our thinking and feeling. He thought the brain was there to cool the blood. Many people agreed, because the heart races when we are scared or excited. Some Greek doctors disagreed. In Alexandria, about 2,300 years ago, Herophilus cut open human bodies and traced the nerves back to the brain. About 1,850 years ago, the Greek doctor Galen, working in Rome, tested the idea in front of an audience. He cut two thin nerves in the neck of a squealing pig. The pig went silent, though its heart kept beating and it kept breathing. Galen showed that these nerves came from the brain, not the heart. So the brain, he said, controls the voice. He also cut the spinal cord of animals at different places, and showed which parts of the body could no longer move. Today we have even stronger proof. People living with a machine that pumps their blood still think, laugh and remember.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle taught that the heart does the thinking and the brain only cools the blood. Galen cut the nerves that control a pig's voice, and showed that they come from the brain. The pig fell silent while its heart kept beating.

## Challenging perspectives
- **We think with our heart, not our brain.** (The ancient Egyptians, and the Greek thinker Aristotle about 2,350 years ago. We still say 'learn by heart'.; *rejected*)  
  For: Your heart races when you are scared, excited or in love. It is warm and always moving. A brain looks like a grey, still lump.  
  Against: People with an artificial heart pump think and feel as before. When the brain is hurt, thinking changes. The heart races because the brain tells it to.
- **We only use ten per cent of our brain.** (A popular saying, found in films and adverts; *rejected*)  
  For: It sounds exciting. It suggests we have hidden powers waiting to be switched on.  
  Against: Brain scans show that over a day almost every part of the brain is busy. Damage to any part causes problems. The brain uses about a fifth of the body's energy, and a body would waste that feeding parts it never used.
- **Stomach ulcers, sore patches in the stomach, are caused by stress and spicy food.** (Most doctors, until the 1980s; *rejected*)  
  For: Many people with ulcers were worried and busy. Spicy food can make a sore stomach feel worse.  
  Against: In Australia, Robin Warren found a germ in the stomachs of people with ulcers. Barry Marshall then drank a cup of these germs to prove it, and his stomach became sore and swollen inside. Medicines that kill the germ cure most ulcers. They won the Nobel Prize in 2005.
- **The heart is on the left side of the chest.** (What most of us learn, from putting a hand on our heart; *rejected*)  
  For: You feel the beat most strongly on the left.  
  Against: The heart is in the middle of the chest, behind the breastbone, with about two-thirds of it left of the middle. Its pointy bottom tips to the left, and that is where the beat feels strongest.
- **The brain is the boss of all the organs.** (A common way of describing the body; *debated*)  
  For: The brain sends messages that speed up the heart, change breathing and control muscles.  
  Against: The heart starts each beat by itself. The gut can run its own squeezing. And the brain dies within minutes without the heart's blood. Some scientists say the body is more like a team than a kingdom.
- **We have found all the organs in the body.** (Many people, and older textbooks; *debated*)  
  For: People have studied bodies closely for hundreds of years.  
  Against: In 2016, a surgeon called J. Calvin Coffey argued the mesentery, a folded sheet holding the intestines, should count as one organ. In 2018, scientists argued that a web of fluid-filled spaces, the interstitium, could be one too. Others say these are new names, not new organs.

## Roots
We have been learning where the heart, lungs, stomach and brain are, and what they do. Now we ask what these organs tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Shake a raw egg or a ripe banana piece in an empty jar, and it cracks or squashes. In a jar filled to the top with water, it stays whole. Your brain floats in liquid inside your skull, and that liquid protects it the same way. Hard bone alone would not keep a soft brain safe.  
*Habit it breaks:* We often think that keeping something safe means making it hard, like armour. But a soft thing inside a hard case can still crash into the walls and break. Your body keeps its softest organ safe with liquid as well as bone.  
*Evidence:* In the egg in the jar test, the egg in the empty jar cracked first, and the egg in the jar full of water stayed whole. Inside your skull, your brain floats in a clear liquid in the same way. A cricket helmet copies the idea, with a hard shell outside and soft foam inside.

- *who am i:* You are a team of organs, from your brain to your stomach, and every one of them is made of living cells.
- *what is life:* A living body keeps going because its organs pass blood, food, air and messages to each other all the time.
- *what is this world:* A fish, a cow and you each have a brain in a skull, a heart and a gut, because all animals with backbones share one ancestor.

Long ago, every living thing was a single cell that did every job by itself. Over a very long time, some cells began to live together as one body and share out the work. Some became heart cells that squeeze, some became lung cells that swap gases, and some became nerve cells that carry messages. The jobs one cell once did alone are now shared among many organs. Those many organs still keep one body alive together.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.1:** The senses and breath once argued about which of them was the greatest. Speech, sight, hearing and mind each left the body in turn, and the body carried on. When breath, prana, began to leave, all the others were pulled up with it. They agreed breath was the one they all depended on. (In this unit, the brain is badly hurt within minutes if the heart's blood stops, and every organ needs the oxygen your lungs bring in. The old story also puts breath at the centre of the body.)
- **Saint Paul, First Letter to the Corinthians 12:12–26:** The body is one, though it has many parts. The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I don't need you.' If one part suffers, every part suffers with it. (Scientists see the same thing. When the heart stops, the brain is hurt within minutes. When the lungs struggle, every organ struggles.)
- **Tao Te Ching 76:** A newborn baby is soft and bendy, and a dead body is hard and stiff. Green plants bend, and dry dead ones snap. So the hard and stiff go with death, and the soft and bending go with life. (Your brain is one of the softest parts of you. In the egg test, the soft water around the egg protected it better than the hard jar alone.)
- Science (settled): All animals with backbones share an ancient ancestor. That is why a fish and a child have a brain in a skull, a heart in front and a gut below, and why lungs and swim bladders began as one thing.
- Science (open): Nobody knows how a brain makes the feeling of being someone. Scientists and philosophers call this the hard problem.
- Other view, Most brain scientists, and the old Indian Charvaka school: You are your brain and body, and nothing more. When the body stops, the self stops too. The teamwork of organs is amazing, but nothing beyond the body is needed to explain it. That is an honest view, and many thoughtful people hold it.
- Practice, Listening to the team (3 min): Let's sit very still and close our eyes. Put one hand on your chest. Feel your heart. You are not telling it to beat. Now feel your breath going in and out. Now feel your tummy, quiet and warm. Notice that your brain is noticing all of this. Stay with them all for five slow breaths. Is there one of you in there, or many?
- Ask: Who is beating your heart? Is it you?
- Ask: If you are made of many organs, are you one thing or many things?
- Ask: A heart from one person can keep another person alive. Whose heart is it then?
- Ask: Which part of you is noticing all your other parts?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your heart has been beating since before you were born. Who is making it beat? Is it you? (hint: Can you stop your heart just by deciding to? Can you hold your breath? What is the difference?)
- *deep:* Your brain is inside a dark, quiet skull. It never sees light. So how do you see colours? (hint: Your eyes send messages along nerves. What is the brain doing with those messages?)
- *deep:* If every cell in your toe uses oxygen, why do we need lungs at all? (hint: How far is your toe from the air outside? How would oxygen get there by itself?)

## Story: The Marble and the Pomfret
*Meenu, Appu, Amma and the doctor are made up. The facts are real: X-rays show glass and bones, the stomach sits under the left ribs, a fish's heart sits low behind its gills, and lungs and the swim bladders of fish come from the same ancient beginning. Small smooth swallowed things usually pass through safely, but a child who swallows anything must always see a doctor. On an X-ray the lungs look dark, because air lets X-rays through, and the heart looks paler.*

Meenu did not like insides. She liked outsides. She liked the shine on a fish's skin, the blue on a crab's claw, the pink of a sunset over the Versova boats. Insides were dark and squishy and secret. When Amma cleaned fish on the back step, Meenu looked at the sky.

"You eat fish," said Amma, her knife quick and sure. "You can look at fish."

"I can eat without looking," said Meenu.

Amma laughed and laid a silver pomfret flat on the board. "Then just listen. Here, low down behind the gills, is its heart. Small as a bean. Here is its stomach, a little bag, with the gut curling on after it. Up here, inside the head, in its own bony box, is its brain. And here," she lifted something thin and shiny, like a balloon with the air let out, "is its air bag. It fills this to float."

"Fish don't have lungs," said Meenu, not looking.

"No," said Amma. "But this is a cousin of lungs, from long, long ago. Your teacher can tell you."

Meenu did not ask her teacher. She did not want to think about it.

That night her little brother Appu was playing with his marbles on the floor. Appu was four. He liked to hold things in his mouth, the way a dog holds a stick. Meenu was doing sums. She heard a small click, then a gulp, then nothing at all.

Appu was looking at her with very round eyes. One marble was missing. The green one, with the twist of white inside, like a little galaxy.

"Appu," said Meenu, "where is the green one?"

Appu pointed at his mouth. Then he pointed at his belly button. Then he began to cry.

Everything after that was fast. Amma's hands on Appu's cheeks. "Can you breathe? Breathe for me." Appu breathing, loud and wet and angry. Appa running for an auto. Meenu squeezed in at the back, holding Appu's foot, because it was the only part of him she could reach.

In the auto, Appu cried, "It's in my tummy! It will stay in my tummy for ever!" He pressed his belly button as if he could push the marble back up.

Meenu thought of the insides of him. Dark and squishy and secret. Where did a marble even go, in there? Was it lost? Could a person get lost inside themselves?

At the hospital, a doctor with tired eyes and a kind voice listened to Appu's chest. "His breathing is clear," she told Amma. "That means the marble went down the food pipe, not the windpipe. Good. Now let us see where it is."

They took Appu into a room with a big grey machine. They let Meenu stand behind a glass window with Amma. There was a buzz. Then a picture lit up on a screen.

It was Appu. But it was Appu from the inside.

Meenu stared. She saw the round white bowl of his skull. She saw a long chain of white beads running down the middle of him, his backbone. And she saw curved white bars, row after row, wrapping round his chest.

"Ribs," Meenu whispered. "Like the pomfret's bones."

In the dark space inside the ribs there were two big dark patches, one on each side. Between them was a paler shape, tipped a little to the left.

"Lungs," said the doctor, pointing. "And his heart, in the middle. Do you see how they fit together? The left lung is a bit smaller, to leave room for the heart."

And there, just below the ribs, a little to the left, was a small, perfect white circle. A tiny full moon.

"The marble," said the doctor. "Glass shows up white, like bone. It is sitting in his stomach."

"Not his tummy?" said Meenu. She had thought the stomach was behind the belly button, where Appu had pointed.

"The stomach is higher than most people think," said the doctor. "Under the ribs, on the left. Shaped a little like a J. After the stomach come the long, curly intestines. That is most of the tummy."

Meenu looked at the white moon under the ribs. A little bag, with the gut curling on after it. She had heard those words before, on the back step, in Amma's voice.

"It's the same," she said slowly. "It's the same as the fish."

The doctor raised her eyebrows. Amma smiled a small, surprised smile.

"A brain in its own bony box, at the top," said Meenu. "A heart near the front. A stomach after the food pipe, and the gut after that. Only the fish has an air bag, and Appu has lungs." She remembered the thin shiny balloon. "Cousins," she said.

"That is right," said the doctor. "Very long ago, they began as the same thing."

The doctor told Amma what would happen next. A small, smooth marble almost always travels on by itself, she said. It moves from the stomach into the intestines, slowly, slowly, along the whole long journey, and comes out at the end. They must watch Appu, and come straight back if his tummy hurt or he was sick.

On the auto ride home, Appu was sleepy and hiccuping. "Is it lost?" he asked.

Meenu put her hand gently on him, just below his ribs, on the left. "No," she said. "It's here. In your stomach. Your stomach is squeezing it, like Amma kneading dough. Tomorrow it goes into the long curly tube. Then it goes all the way along, and then out."

"How do you know?" said Appu.

"I saw the map," said Meenu. "Inside you there is a map. It's the same map as the fish."

Appu thought about that. Then he put his own small hand on top of hers, and they rode like that, through the orange streetlights, past the dark sea.

Two days later, the green marble came out, exactly the way the doctor had said. Appa washed it in hot soapy water three times, and then three times again, and then everyone laughed so much that Appu got hiccups again.

The next Sunday, Amma laid a pomfret on the board. Meenu sat down on the step beside her. She did not look at the sky.

"Show me the heart," she said.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Meenu is frightened of 'insides' because they seem dark, unmapped and secret. The turn comes when the X-ray shows that the inside has a plan: a brain in a bony box at the top, a heart in the middle inside a cage of ribs, lungs on either side, and a stomach under the left ribs, not behind the belly button. Knowing where an organ is turns fear into understanding. Underneath, she sees that the fish and her brother are built on the same plan, because all animals with backbones share an ancestor. Lungs and a fish's swim bladder began as one thing.

- Ask: Where did Appu point when he said the marble was in his tummy? Where was it really?
- Ask: How did the doctor know the marble had not gone into Appu's windpipe?
- Ask: What did Meenu see on the X-ray that reminded her of the fish?
- Ask: Why was Meenu afraid of insides at the start? Why do you think she was not afraid at the end?
- Ask: Meenu said, 'Inside you there is a map.' What would your map show?
- Ask: Is there something you are a bit scared of because you do not know much about it yet?

### e1 · The Egg in the Jar: how liquid protects the brain (25 min)

### a1 · Feel Your Inside (8 min)
- **safety:** No special safety needed. Press gently.
- **record:** Draw a quick outline of yourself. Put a dot where you felt each thing.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit together quietly. 2. Ask your child to put a flat hand on the middle of their chest and keep still.  
  *Parent says:* Put your hand flat on the middle of your chest. Be very still. What do you feel? Now move your hand a little to the left. Is it stronger?  
  *Child mode:* Put your hand on your chest. Be very still. What do you feel? Move it a little left.
  - Ask: What do you notice? What do you wonder? (good: A thump.; It's stronger on the left.; Why does it never stop?)
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to put both hands on their ribs and breathe in slowly and deeply, then out.  
  *Parent says:* Hands on your ribs. Breathe in slowly, as much as you can. What happens to your hands? Now breathe out.  
  *Child mode:* Hands on your ribs. Breathe in slowly. Do your hands move? Breathe out.
  - Note: The ribs lift up and out as the lungs fill. A good wonder is 'What is inside that is getting bigger?'
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to take a sip of water and feel it go down, with one hand on the throat.  
  *Parent says:* Take a sip. Swallow. Feel your throat move. Where do you think the water goes next?  
  *Child mode:* Sip some water. Swallow. Feel your throat move. Where does the water go?
  - Check: When you swallow water, where does it go? → **Down the food pipe to the stomach**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to knock gently on the top of their head, then draw dots on a quick outline.  
  *Parent says:* Knock gently on your head. That hard part is your skull. What do you think is inside? Now draw yourself and put a dot for each thing you felt.  
  *Child mode:* Knock gently on your head. That is your skull. What is inside? Draw yourself. Put a dot for each thing you felt.
  - Think: You felt your heart, your breath, your swallow and your skull. What do you think you could not feel at all?
### a2 · The Life-size Organ Map (30 min)
- **safety:** Use safety scissors. A grown-up helps with long cuts.
- **record:** Take a photo of the map before and after you check it. Keep the map for the project and the challenge.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the paper on the floor. 2. Ask your child to lie on it with arms a little out. 3. Trace round them with a marker.  
  *Parent says:* Lie down on the paper. I'm going to draw round you. This is going to be a map of you, from the inside.  
  *Child mode:* Lie on the paper. Keep still. A grown-up draws round you. This will be a map of your inside.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Before cutting anything, ask your child to draw with a pencil where they think the brain, heart, lungs and stomach go.  
  *Parent says:* Before we look anything up, draw where you think your organs are. Just a pencil shape. It's fine to guess.  
  *Child mode:* Draw where you think your brain, heart, lungs and stomach are. Just guess. Use a pencil.
  - Note: This is your child's first model. Do not correct it yet. Many children draw a small heart on the far left, small lungs and a stomach low behind the belly button.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out organs at these sizes, using your child's hands as a guide. Heart: the size of their closed fist, red. Brain: two fists side by side, pink. Each lung: a tall leaf shape as long as their hand and a half, pink. Make the left lung a bit narrower, with a dent. Stomach: a J shape about as big as their open hand, yellow. Intestines: a long brown strip, coiled.  
  *Parent says:* Your heart is about the size of your fist. Your brain is about two fists. Let's cut them out using your own hands to measure.  
  *Child mode:* Make a fist. Your heart is this big. Cut a red heart this size. Your brain is two fists. Cut that too.
  - Note: These sizes are rough guides. The lungs are bigger than most children expect. They fill most of the chest.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to place each organ on the map. Then read the body map card together and move any that are wrong.  
  *Parent says:* Put each organ where you think it goes. Now let's check with the card. Heart in the middle, tipped left. Lungs on both sides. Stomach under the ribs on the left. Brain in the head.  
  *Child mode:* Put each organ on your map. Now check. Heart in the middle. Lungs on both sides. Stomach under the left ribs. Brain in the head.
  - Check: On your organ map, where do the lungs go? → **Filling most of the chest, one on each side of the heart**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Glue the organs down. Compare the pencil guesses with where the organs are now.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your pencil guesses, and where the organs are now. What did you move? What surprised you?  
  *Child mode:* Glue the organs down. Look at your pencil guesses. What did you move? What surprised you?
  - Ask: What surprised you most? (good: The lungs are so big.; The heart is in the middle.; The stomach is higher up.)
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer for the organ map sounds like this. What I think: my stomach is under my ribs on the left, not behind my belly button. What I saw: the body map card put it there, and I felt full there after lunch. Why: the food pipe goes down the chest and ends there.
### a3 · Kitchen Organ Weigh-in (20 min)
- **safety:** Do not lift heavy bags. A grown-up handles anything breakable.
- **record:** Make a table: organ, what weighs the same, its weight in grams.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Weigh things until you find something close to 300 grams. Let your child hold it.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up's heart weighs about 300 grams. Let's find something that weighs the same. Hold it. Your heart is a bit lighter, because you are smaller.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up's heart is about 300 grams. Find something that weighs that much. Hold it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build up about 1,300 grams with a packet of rice and something small. Let your child hold it with two hands.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up's brain weighs about 1,300 grams. That is one kilogram and three hundred grams. Hold it. This is what sits on top of your neck all day.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up's brain is about 1,300 grams. Put things on the scale until it says that. Hold it.
  - Check: Which is heavier in a grown-up, the brain or the heart? → **The brain**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child hold a 1-litre water bottle, then an empty bottle.  
  *Parent says:* When a grown-up's stomach is comfortably full, it holds about one litre, like this bottle. When it is empty, it is small, like a fist.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the full bottle. A full stomach holds about this much. An empty one is small, like your fist.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the table together: organ, kitchen match, weight in grams.  
  *Parent says:* Let's write it down. Which organ was heaviest? Which surprised you?  
  *Child mode:* Write a table. Organ, kitchen thing, grams. Which was heaviest?
  - Note: The two lungs together weigh about 1 kilogram in a grown-up, but they are mostly air and very light for their size. The liver, under the right ribs, is the heaviest organ inside, about 1,500 grams.
### a4 · The Body Council (act it out) (15 min)
- **safety:** Act in a clear space with nothing to trip on.
- **record:** Draw one scene as a comic strip with four boxes.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the four signs. Your child is the BRAIN, sitting on a chair. You are the HEART. Toys are LUNGS and STOMACH.  
  *Parent says:* You are the Brain Mayor. You get messages and send orders. I am the heart. I drum the beat. Teddy is the lungs. Dolly is the stomach.  
  *Child mode:* You are the brain. You get messages and give orders. Who is the heart? Who are the lungs and stomach?
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Act out 'A dog barks suddenly'. The brain hears it and orders the heart to drum faster and the lungs to breathe faster. The stomach stops for a while.  
  *Parent says:* Woof! A big dog barks! Brain Mayor, what do you tell everyone?  
  *Child mode:* A dog barks! You are the brain. What do you tell the heart and lungs?
  - Note: A good brain says: 'Heart, beat faster! Lungs, breathe faster! Stomach, wait!' This is what really happens when we are scared. Blood moves away from the stomach to the leg muscles.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Act out 'A big lunch' and then 'Falling asleep'.  
  *Parent says:* Now lunch is here. Brain, what do you tell the stomach? Now it is night. What happens to the heart drum?  
  *Child mode:* Lunch time! What does the stomach do? Now it's night. Is the heart drum fast or slow?
  - Check: When you fall asleep, what does the brain do? → **It keeps the heart and breathing going, more slowly**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap roles. Then ask the team question.  
  *Parent says:* Let's swap. Now I am the brain. Here is a puzzle for the council: could the Brain Mayor work if the heart went on holiday?  
  *Child mode:* Swap roles. Could the brain work if the heart stopped?
  - Ask: Why can't the heart ever take a holiday? (good: The brain needs blood every second.; Every cell needs oxygen all the time.)
### a5 · Same Plan, Different Animals (20 min)
- **safety:** At a fish market, stay with a grown-up and keep your hands away from knives.
- **record:** Draw yourself and one other animal side by side, with the four organs in each.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the fish card, or a real fish being cleaned if your family eats fish. Find its heart, stomach, brain and swim bladder.  
  *Parent says:* A fish has a brain in a bony box, a small heart low behind the gills, a stomach and a gut. It breathes with gills. It also has an air bag called a swim bladder. That air bag is a distant cousin of your lungs.  
  *Child mode:* Find the fish's brain, heart and stomach. It breathes with gills. Its air bag is a cousin of your lungs.
  - Note: If your family does not eat fish, use the picture card only. The swim bladder and lungs come from the same ancient beginning in early fish.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the cow and the octopus cards. Count the stomach parts and the hearts.  
  *Parent says:* A cow's stomach has four parts. An octopus has three hearts. Most of its nerve cells are in its arms, not its head.  
  *Child mode:* Count the cow's stomach parts. Count the octopus's hearts.
  - Check: How many hearts does an octopus have? → **Three**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the sponge: an animal with no organs.  
  *Parent says:* A sponge is one of the oldest kinds of animal. It has no heart, no stomach and no brain. So how does it stay alive?  
  *Child mode:* A sponge has no heart, no stomach and no brain. How is it alive?
  - Ask: How does a sponge eat and breathe without organs? (good: Each cell does it by itself.; Water flows through it and every cell takes what it needs.)
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw yourself and one animal side by side, using the same colour for the same organ in each.  
  *Parent says:* Draw yourself and one animal. Use red for both hearts and pink for both brains. What is the same? What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Draw you and one animal. Same colour for the same organ. What is the same?
  - Think: Why do you think a fish and you have the same plan inside?
### a6 · One Breath, One Bite: trace the team (15 min)
- **safety:** No special safety needed.
- **record:** Leave the strings on the map. Write one sentence on each string saying what it carries.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape a blue string from the nose, down the windpipe, into both lungs.  
  *Parent says:* This blue string is a breath of air. It goes in at the nose, down the windpipe and into both lungs.  
  *Child mode:* Tape a blue string from the nose to the lungs. This is air.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape a yellow string from the mouth, down the food pipe, to the stomach, then into the intestines.  
  *Parent says:* This yellow string is a bite of food. Mouth, food pipe, stomach, then the long intestines.  
  *Child mode:* Tape a yellow string from the mouth to the stomach. Then on to the intestines. This is food.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape a red string from the heart to the lungs, then to the intestines, the brain and a toe. Bring it back to the heart.  
  *Parent says:* The red string is blood. The heart pumps it to the lungs to pick up oxygen. It picks up food near the intestines. Then it takes both to the brain and the toes.  
  *Child mode:* Tape a red string from the heart. Take it to the lungs, gut, brain and a toe. This is blood.
  - Check: On the organ map, which string touches the blue air string and the yellow food string? → **The red blood string**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to point to the brain and say what it needs from each string.  
  *Parent says:* Point to the brain. What does it need from the red string?  
  *Child mode:* Point to the brain. What does it need from the blood?
  - Ask: What does the brain get from the blood? (good: Oxygen from the lungs.; Food from the stomach and intestines.)

- **record:** The finished book, and the name of the person you read it to.
- **schedule:** day 1: Draw the cover: a child in a shirt. Cut the shirt into a flap that lifts up. Draw the four organs underneath. (20 min); day 2: Make one page each for the heart, lungs, stomach and brain: where it is, what it does and one amazing fact. (20 min); day 3: Staple the book together. Read it to someone younger, and ask them to point to their own organs. (20 min)
- **s1 (20 min)**

  *Parent says:* Under the shirt, draw what is really there. Brain in the head, lungs and heart in the chest, stomach under the left ribs.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a child in a shirt. Cut the shirt so it opens. Draw the organs underneath, in the right places.
- **s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make four pages. On each, draw one organ and write three lines: 'I live…', 'My job is…', 'Amazing fact…'.  
  *Parent says:* For each organ, write where it lives, what it does and one amazing fact.  
  *Child mode:* One page for each organ. Write where it lives. Write its job. Write one amazing fact.
  - Check: Which is a true amazing fact for your heart page? → **The heart can start a beat by itself, without the brain**
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow for the organ pages.
- **s3 (20 min)**

  - Wait: Come back tomorrow to finish and share the book.

## Challenges
### c1 · Helmet for an Egg Brain (30 min)
- **problem:** Design a helmet for a raw egg, so it can fall from the height of a table onto the floor without cracking.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own helmet. Draw it, build it and test it. Then make it better.
- **constraints:** You can only use things from home: paper, cloth, cotton, a plastic cup, rubber bands, tape.; The helmet must fit in two hands.; The egg must be easy to take out and check.
- **general:** Wrap the egg in something soft, like cotton wool or cloth, so it is cushioned.; Put it in a hard cup on the outside, like a skull.; Make sure the egg cannot rattle around inside.
- **creative:** Put the egg in a small bag of water inside the cup, like the liquid around your brain.; Look at a cricket or cycling helmet. It has a hard outside and soft foam inside. Copy the idea.; Add a paper parachute so the helmet lands more slowly.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Remind your child of the egg in the jar test.  
  *Parent says:* Your skull is hard on the outside. Inside, your brain is cushioned by liquid. How could we copy that for an egg?  
  *Child mode:* Your skull is hard outside. Liquid cushions your brain inside. How can you copy that?
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child draws a design, then builds it from things at home.  
  *Parent says:* Draw your helmet first. What is hard on the outside? What is soft inside?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your helmet. Hard outside, soft inside. Then build it.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Over a tray, a grown-up drops the helmet from table height. Open it and check the egg.  
  *Parent says:* Three, two, one, drop! Let's open it carefully. Is the egg whole?  
  *Child mode:* Fetch a grown-up. Drop it from the table over a tray. Open it. Is the egg whole?
  - Check: Which helmet design is most like your real head? → **A hard cup with something soft or watery inside**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Improve the design and test once more.  
  *Parent says:* What would make it better? Change one thing and test again.  
  *Child mode:* Change one thing to make it better. Test again.
  - Think: Why do people wear helmets on scooters, even though they already have a skull?
### c2 · Pack the Chest (25 min)
- **problem:** Fit a model heart and two model lungs inside a shallow box lid, so they fill the space without squashing each other.
- **your turn:** Now design your own chest model. What else could you add, like a dome-shaped diaphragm at the bottom?
- **constraints:** Use playdough or wheat-flour dough for the organs.; Use the lid of a shoe box or a mithai box as the chest. It is shallow, so the model is flat.; The heart must be as wide as your fist, and the lungs must fill the rest.
- **general:** Make the heart first, as wide as your fist and about one finger thick, and put it in the middle.; Mould the lungs around it, one on each side.; Leave a dent in the left lung so the heart fits.
- **creative:** Make the lungs fluffy by pressing in lots of tiny holes with a pencil tip, like tiny air bags.; Tape paper strips over the box as ribs, with small gaps between them.; Push in a straw for the windpipe and split it into two.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make a dough heart as wide as your child's fist and about one finger thick. Put it in the middle of the lid, tipped a little to the left.  
  *Parent says:* Your heart is about as big as your fist. Put it in the middle of the chest, tipped a little left.  
  *Child mode:* Make a flat heart as wide as your fist. Put it in the middle, tipped left.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Press the two lungs flat, about one finger thick, around the heart until the lid is full.  
  *Parent says:* Now fill all the rest of the space with lungs. What do you have to do to the left lung?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the rest of the lid with two flat lungs. What happens to the left lung?
  - Check: Why is your left lung a little smaller than your right lung? → **It leaves room for the heart**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape paper strips across the top as ribs.  
  *Parent says:* Now add the ribs, like the bars of a cage. Why leave gaps between them?  
  *Child mode:* Tape paper ribs over the top. Leave gaps. Why?
  - Note: Real ribs have muscles between them, and they swing up and out when you breathe in. A solid box would not let the lungs grow.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the model to someone and explain it.  
  *Parent says:* Tell someone what each part is, and why the left lung has a dent.  
  *Child mode:* Show your model. Explain each part.
  - Think: What would happen if the ribs were one solid sheet of bone?

## Worksheets
### Inside Explorer (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) What is an organ?  
   Options: Any bone in the body / A body part with a special job, made of many cells / A kind of musical instrument only / A germ inside the body  
   **Answer:** A body part with a special job, made of many cells. *Why:* An organ is a body part with a special job, like the heart pumping blood. It is made of many living cells working together. Picking 'any bone' mixes up organs with the skeleton. Each bone is in fact counted as an organ too, but most organs are soft. Picking 'musical instrument' is a fun mix-up, because the word organ means that too.
2. (mcq) Where is your heart?  
   Options: On the far left of your chest / In the middle of your chest, tipped a little to the left / In your belly / In your head  
   **Answer:** In the middle of your chest, tipped a little to the left. *Why:* The heart sits in the middle of the chest, behind the breastbone, with its pointy end tipped to the left. Picking 'far left' is very common, because the beat feels strongest there.
3. (mcq) What is the main job of the lungs?  
   Options: To pump blood / To take oxygen from the air into the blood / To digest food / To think  
   **Answer:** To take oxygen from the air into the blood. *Why:* The lungs take in air, pass oxygen into the blood and let out carbon dioxide. Picking 'pump blood' mixes them up with the heart, their next-door neighbour.
4. (mcq) Where is your stomach?  
   Options: Behind your belly button / Under your ribs, on the left / In the middle of your chest / In your back  
   **Answer:** Under your ribs, on the left. *Why:* The stomach is just under the ribs on the left side. Below the belly button are mostly the intestines. Picking 'belly button' happens because we call the whole belly the 'tummy'.
5. (mcq) Which organ sends and receives messages for the whole body?  
   Options: The stomach / The brain / The lungs / The heart  
   **Answer:** The brain. *Why:* The brain sends and receives messages along nerves to every part of the body. Picking 'the heart' is an old idea. Aristotle thought the heart did the thinking. Today we know it is the brain.
6. (fill) Your heart is about the size of your ____.  
   **Answer:** ['fist']. *Why:* Make a fist. A heart is about that size. A grown-up's heart is the size of a grown-up's fist.
7. (fill) The hard bony case that protects your brain is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['skull']. *Why:* The skull works like a helmet around the brain.
8. (fill) The curved bones that guard your heart and lungs are your ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ribs', 'rib cage']. *Why:* The ribs make a cage around the chest. They are joined by muscles and swing up and out, so the lungs have room to get bigger when you breathe in.
9. (match) Match each organ to its job.  
   **Answer:** Heart → pumps blood; Lungs → take in oxygen; Stomach → churns food; Brain → sends and receives messages. *Why:* Each organ has a main job. They all need each other to do it.
10. (match) Match each organ to where it lives.  
   **Answer:** Brain → inside the skull; Heart → middle of the chest; Lungs → both sides of the chest; Stomach → under the left ribs. *Why:* Remember the body map: brain at the top, heart and lungs in the chest, stomach just below the ribs on the left.
11. (truefalse) You have two lungs.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. One lung is on each side of the heart. The left one is a little smaller.
12. (truefalse) Your heart stops beating when you are asleep.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Your heart beats all day and all night, your whole life. It just beats a little more slowly when you sleep.
13. (truefalse) In the egg in the jar test, the egg (or banana) in water was safer than the one in the empty jar.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The water filled the jar, so the egg moved with the jar and did not crash into its sides. The liquid around your brain does the same job.
14. (sort) Sort these into the organ that does them: pumping blood, churning food, breathing in oxygen, remembering a song.  
   **Answer:** pumping blood → Heart; churning food → Stomach; breathing in oxygen → Lungs; remembering a song → Brain. *Why:* Each job belongs to one main organ, though all four need each other.
15. (label) Label the body outline: brain, heart, left lung, right lung, stomach.  
   **Answer:** Brain in the head. Heart in the middle of the chest. One lung on each side of the heart. Stomach under the ribs on the left.. *Why:* This is the body map. Check your life-size organ map if you are not sure.
16. (draw) Draw yourself. Draw your heart, lungs, stomach and brain in the right places.  
   **Answer:** A body with the brain in the head, a fist-sized heart in the middle of the chest, two big lungs on either side, and a J-shaped stomach under the left ribs.. *Why:* Drawing a model helps you remember where things are, even the parts you cannot see.
17. (write) Why does your brain need your heart? Use the word blood in your answer.  
   **Answer:** My heart pumps blood to my brain. The blood carries oxygen and food. Without it, my brain cannot work.. *Why:* Every organ needs the heart's blood. The brain stops working within minutes without it.
### Organ Detective (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why is your left lung a little smaller than your right lung?  
   Options: It is still growing / It has a dent to make room for the heart / It does not need air / It is behind the stomach  
   **Answer:** It has a dent to make room for the heart. *Why:* The heart tips to the left, so the left lung has a dent for it. Picking 'still growing' guesses that size means age. Both lungs grow at the same time.
2. (mcq) In the squish bag test, why did the squished biscuit break down faster?  
   Options: Squishing broke it into smaller pieces and mixed it with water / The bag got hotter / The biscuit was a different kind / It did not break down faster  
   **Answer:** Squishing broke it into smaller pieces and mixed it with water. *Why:* Squeezing breaks food into small bits and mixes it. The stomach's muscle walls do the same. Picking 'different biscuit' would make the test unfair. We used the same biscuit in both bags.
3. (mcq) Why do lungs have hundreds of millions of tiny air bags instead of one big bag?  
   Options: Tiny bags are easier to grow / Many tiny bags give a huge surface for oxygen to pass into the blood / One big bag would be too heavy / To make breathing noisy  
   **Answer:** Many tiny bags give a huge surface for oxygen to pass into the blood. *Why:* Remember the lump test: the crushed mishri disappeared much faster, because much more of it touched the water. In the same way, many tiny air bags give a huge surface where air meets blood. Spread out flat, they would cover a large part of a badminton court. Picking 'too heavy' guesses about weight. Lungs are very light, because they are full of air.
4. (mcq) The stomach makes a strong acid. Why does the stomach not digest itself?  
   Options: The acid is weak / It has a thick slimy layer and keeps replacing its lining / The brain switches the acid off / Food protects it  
   **Answer:** It has a thick slimy layer and keeps replacing its lining. *Why:* Mucus, a slimy layer, coats the inside of the stomach, and the lining is replaced again and again. Picking 'the acid is weak' is a common idea. In fact the acid is strong enough to help break down meat.
5. (mcq) When you are scared, your heart beats faster. Which organ tells it to?  
   Options: The stomach / The lungs / The brain / Nobody, it decides alone  
   **Answer:** The brain. *Why:* The brain sends messages that speed up the heart when you are scared or running. The heart starts each beat itself, but the brain changes the speed. Picking 'it decides alone' is half right, which is why this is a good thing to think about.
6. (order) Put these in order, the way food travels: stomach, mouth, intestines, food pipe.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Food goes in at the mouth, down the food pipe to the stomach, and then on into the intestines.
7. (order) Put these in order, the way air travels: lungs, nose, tiny air bags, windpipe.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Air goes in at the nose, down the windpipe, into the lungs and right to the tiny air bags at the ends of the branches.
8. (fill) The brain floats in a clear ____ that cushions it.  
   **Answer:** ['liquid', 'fluid']. *Why:* Like the water around the egg in the jar, the liquid stops the brain banging on the skull.
9. (fill) The dome-shaped muscle under the lungs is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['diaphragm']. *Why:* Lungs cannot pull in air by themselves. The diaphragm pulls down to make them fill with air.
10. (truefalse) The brain itself cannot feel pain.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The brain has no pain sensors of its own. That is why doctors can sometimes operate on a brain while the person is awake.
11. (truefalse) We only use ten per cent of our brain.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Brain scans show almost every part of the brain is busy over a day. This is a popular myth.
12. (truefalse) An octopus has three hearts.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Two pump blood through its gills and one pumps it round the body.
13. (write) You run to catch the school bus. Say what your brain, heart and lungs each do.  
   **Answer:** My brain notices my legs need more oxygen. It tells my heart to beat faster and my lungs to breathe faster. My lungs take in more oxygen. My heart pumps it to my leg muscles.. *Why:* This shows organs working as a team. The brain sends messages and the heart and lungs answer.
14. (write) In the egg in the jar test, what did the egg stand for? What did the jar stand for? What did the water stand for?  
   **Answer:** The egg stood for the brain. The jar stood for the skull. The water stood for the liquid that cushions the brain.. *Why:* A model stands for the real thing, so we can test an idea safely.
15. (draw) Draw the rib cage around a heart and two lungs. Show the gaps between the ribs.  
   **Answer:** Curved ribs like the bars of a cage, a heart in the middle, two lungs filling the rest, with small gaps between the ribs.. *Why:* The gaps and the moving ribs let the lungs grow bigger when you breathe in.
### Body Puzzles and Arguments (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A sponge is an animal with no heart, stomach or brain. How does it stay alive?  
   Options: It does not, it is not really alive / Water flows through it, and its cells take oxygen and food from the water / It borrows organs from fish / It only lives for one day  
   **Answer:** Water flows through it, and its cells take oxygen and food from the water. *Why:* Water flows through tiny holes in a sponge's body. Each cell takes oxygen straight from the water, and some cells catch bits of food and share them with the others. Organs are what happens when cells share out jobs. Picking 'not really alive' is a common idea, because sponges do not move. But they grow, feed and make baby sponges.
2. (mcq) Heart muscle cells grown in a dish start to beat by themselves. What does this tell us?  
   Options: The heart needs the brain to start each beat / Heart cells have their own beat built in / The dish is shaking / The cells are pretending  
   **Answer:** Heart cells have their own beat built in. *Why:* Heart cells beat on their own. The brain only speeds them up or slows them down. Picking 'needs the brain' is the most common idea, and it is only half true.
3. (mcq) Barry Marshall drank a cup of germs in 1984. Why?  
   Options: By mistake / To prove a germ causes stomach ulcers / To make his stomach stronger / To win a bet  
   **Answer:** To prove a germ causes stomach ulcers. *Why:* Doctors did not believe him that a germ caused ulcers. So he tested it on himself, got sick, and showed the germ was there. He and Robin Warren won the Nobel Prize in 2005. Doing this is not safe, and nobody should copy it.
4. (mcq) The ancient Egyptians kept the heart in a mummy but threw away the brain. What did they believe?  
   Options: The brain was too heavy / The heart was where thinking and feeling lived / The brain would grow back / They did not know the brain existed  
   **Answer:** The heart was where thinking and feeling lived. *Why:* Like Aristotle, they thought the heart was the centre of the person. Evidence later showed the brain does the thinking. Picking 'did not know it existed' is wrong, because they had to remove it.
5. (mcq) Which of these is an organ the size of two fists, weighing about 1,300 grams in a grown-up?  
   Options: Heart / Stomach / Brain / Lung  
   **Answer:** Brain. *Why:* A grown-up's brain weighs about 1,300 grams, about two fists in size. The heart is about one fist and 300 grams.
6. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Skull, ribs, brain, backbone.  
   **Answer:** Brain. *Why:* The brain is a soft organ. The skull, ribs and backbone are bones that protect soft organs.
7. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Heart, lungs, stomach, gills.  
   **Answer:** Gills. *Why:* We have hearts, lungs and stomachs. Gills belong to fish. They are the fish's breathing organs, doing the job our lungs do.
8. (write) Some scientists call the gut 'the second brain'. Give one reason for this name, and one reason some scientists do not like it.  
   **Answer:** For: the gut has about 500 million nerve cells, and it can run its own squeezing without the brain. Against: the gut's nerve net is much smaller and simpler than the brain, so some scientists say the name confuses people.. *Why:* This is a debated idea. A good answer gives both sides.
9. (write) Every cell in your toe uses oxygen. So why do you need lungs at all?  
   **Answer:** My toe is far from the air. Oxygen cannot reach it by itself. The lungs take oxygen into the blood, and the heart pumps the blood all the way to my toe.. *Why:* Big bodies need organs to share out jobs. A tiny sponge cell can get oxygen straight from water.
10. (write) Aristotle thought the heart did our thinking. What evidence shows that the brain does?  
   **Answer:** People with an artificial heart pump still think and feel. When the brain is hurt, thinking and memory change. Brain scans show the brain busy when we think.. *Why:* Scientists change their minds when the evidence is strong. Aristotle had good reasons, but not the evidence we have now.
11. (write) Is the brain the boss of the body? Give one reason for yes and one for no.  
   **Answer:** Yes: it sends messages that change how fast the heart beats and how fast we breathe. No: the heart starts its own beat, and the brain cannot live for long without the heart's blood.. *Why:* This is debated. Some scientists see the body as a team, not a kingdom.
12. (truefalse) Scientists have grown full-sized working hearts in a lab and put them into people.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Scientists can grow tiny mini-organs, called organoids. Nobody has yet grown a full working heart for a person.
13. (truefalse) A fish's swim bladder and our lungs began, long ago, as the same thing.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Scientists have found that lungs and swim bladders come from the same ancient beginning in early fish.
14. (fill) The brain has about 86 ____ nerve cells.  
   **Answer:** ['billion']. *Why:* 86 billion is about ten times the number of people on Earth. The Brazilian scientist Suzana Herculano-Houzel counted them in 2009. She turned brains into a soup, then counted the cell centres in a few drops.
15. (match) Match each animal to its surprising organ fact.  
   **Answer:** Octopus → three hearts; Cow → four-part stomach; Starfish → pushes its stomach out of its mouth; Sponge → no organs at all. *Why:* Animals solve the same problems, pumping, breathing, digesting and sensing, in many different ways.
16. (draw) Draw one cell. Inside it, draw a mitochondrion and a small bag of juice. On its outside, draw a few sensors. Write what each one does.  
   **Answer:** A round cell with a small bean shape (uses oxygen to make energy), a small bubble (breaks down food and old bits) and little feelers on the outside (sense chemicals).. *Why:* Almost every cell does small versions of the jobs that organs do. Organs are teams of cells sharing out jobs.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is an organ?  
   Options: A body part with a special job / A tiny germ / Only a bone / A kind of food  
   **Answer:** A body part with a special job. *Why:* An organ is a body part with a special job, made of many cells. Picking 'only a bone' mixes up organs with the skeleton.
2. (mcq) Where is your heart?  
   Options: Far on the left side / In the middle of the chest, tipped a little left / Behind the belly button / In the neck  
   **Answer:** In the middle of the chest, tipped a little left. *Why:* The heart is in the middle of the chest, tipped left. Picking 'far left' comes from feeling the beat most there.
3. (mcq) Where is your stomach?  
   Options: Behind the belly button / Under the ribs on the left / In the head / In the middle of the chest  
   **Answer:** Under the ribs on the left. *Why:* The stomach is under the ribs on the left. The belly below the belly button is mostly intestines.
4. (mcq) Where are your lungs?  
   Options: Two small balls near the neck / Filling most of the chest, on both sides of the heart / In the belly / Behind the eyes  
   **Answer:** Filling most of the chest, on both sides of the heart. *Why:* The lungs are big. They fill most of the chest. Picking 'small balls' is common, because we picture lungs like balloons.
5. (mcq) What does the heart do?  
   Options: Pumps blood around the body / Thinks and remembers / Churns food / Takes in air  
   **Answer:** Pumps blood around the body. *Why:* The heart pumps blood. Picking 'thinks' is the old idea of Aristotle and the ancient Egyptians.
6. (mcq) What do the lungs do?  
   Options: Pump blood / Take oxygen into the blood and let out carbon dioxide / Break down food / Send messages  
   **Answer:** Take oxygen into the blood and let out carbon dioxide. *Why:* The lungs swap gases with the blood. Picking 'pump blood' mixes up the lungs with the heart.
7. (mcq) What does the stomach do?  
   Options: Holds food and churns it with strong juices / Pumps blood / Makes thoughts / Cleans the air  
   **Answer:** Holds food and churns it with strong juices. *Why:* The stomach holds, churns and breaks down food. Remember the squish bag test.
8. (mcq) What does the brain do?  
   Options: Only thinks about schoolwork / Sends and receives messages, and lets us think, feel, remember and move / Pumps blood / Digests food  
   **Answer:** Sends and receives messages, and lets us think, feel, remember and move. *Why:* The brain does all of these, and also runs breathing and heartbeat speed without us noticing. Picking 'only schoolwork' is too small a picture.
9. (mcq) Which bones protect the heart and lungs?  
   Options: The skull / The ribs / The knee bones / The finger bones  
   **Answer:** The ribs. *Why:* The ribs make a cage around the chest. The skull protects the brain.
10. (mcq) In the egg in the jar test, what did the water stand for?  
   Options: The skull / The liquid that cushions the brain / Blood / The stomach  
   **Answer:** The liquid that cushions the brain. *Why:* The water around the egg stood for the clear liquid around the brain. The jar stood for the skull.
11. (mcq) You run fast. What happens?  
   Options: The brain tells the heart and lungs to work faster / The stomach takes over from the heart / Nothing changes / The lungs stop  
   **Answer:** The brain tells the heart and lungs to work faster. *Why:* Organs work as a team. The brain speeds up the heart and breathing so the muscles get more oxygen.
12. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still NOT know?  
   Options: Where the heart is / How the brain makes a thought or a feeling / What the stomach does / That we have two lungs  
   **Answer:** How the brain makes a thought or a feeling. *Why:* Where organs are and what they do is well known. How a brain makes the feeling of being you is one of the biggest open questions in science.
13. (mcq) A sponge has no organs. How does it live?  
   Options: Water flows through it, and its cells take oxygen and food from the water / It is not alive / It uses a fish's heart / It eats sand  
   **Answer:** Water flows through it, and its cells take oxygen and food from the water. *Why:* Organs are teams of cells sharing out jobs. A sponge has no organs. Its cells take oxygen and food straight from the water flowing through it.
14. (mcq) How many hearts does an octopus have?  
   Options: One / Two / Three / Eight  
   **Answer:** Three. *Why:* Three: two for the gills and one for the body. Picking 'eight' guesses one per arm.

## Applications
- Doctors use a stethoscope to listen to the heart and lungs through the chest.
- X-rays show bones and swallowed objects, like coins and marbles, inside the body.
- Ultrasound scans show a beating heart and a baby growing inside the mother.
- MRI scanners take pictures of the brain without any cutting.
- Cricket, cycling and scooter helmets protect the skull and the brain inside it.
- Heart transplants give a new heart to a person whose own heart is failing. The first one in India was done in Delhi in 1994.
- Organ donation lets one person's organs save several other lives after they die.
- Machines called ventilators help people breathe when their lungs are very ill.
- Pacemakers are small devices that keep a heartbeat steady when the heart's own timekeeper goes wrong.
- Doctors look inside the stomach with a thin tube that has a tiny camera on the end.
- Mini-organs grown in labs help scientists test new medicines.
- Seat belts and airbags in cars protect the chest and head in a crash.
- First aid teaches people how to help someone who is choking, by clearing the windpipe.
- Animal doctors use the same knowledge to treat cows, dogs and cats, which have most of the same organs as us.

## Open questions
- **How does the brain make a thought, a dream or the feeling of being you?** Scientists can see which parts of the brain are busy, but not how busy nerve cells turn into a feeling. This is one of the biggest puzzles in science.
- **Why do we sleep, and what is the brain doing all night?** Sleep helps memory and clears waste from the brain. But scientists still argue about its main job.
- **How much do the germs in our gut change how we feel?** Tests in mice show gut germs can change behaviour. Tests in people are new, and the results are mixed.
- **Can we grow a whole new heart, lung or stomach from a person's own cells?** Scientists can grow tiny mini-organs. Nobody has yet grown a full-sized working one for a person.
- **How many organs does a body have?** It depends on what you count as an organ. Scientists still argue about new ones, like the interstitium.
- **Do animals with very different brains, like octopuses, feel things the way we do?** An octopus solves puzzles and plays. But its brain is built very differently, and we cannot ask it what it feels.
- **Exactly how does the heart's own timekeeper stay so steady for a whole life?** Scientists understand a lot about it. But how it stays steady for billions of beats, and why it sometimes goes wrong, is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the kitchen weigh-in, a grown-up's brain weighed about 1,300 grams and a grown-up's heart about 300 grams. How much heavier is the brain? **1,000 grams heavier, which is 1 kilogram.**. The brain is about 1,300 grams. The heart is about 300 grams. 1,300 take away 300 is 1,000. So the brain is 1,000 grams, or 1 kilogram, heavier. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)
- A grown-up's heart beats about 70 times in one minute. How many times does it beat in 10 minutes? **About 700 times.**. In one minute it beats about 70 times. In 10 minutes, that is 10 groups of 70. 70 times 10 is 700. So about 700 beats in 10 minutes. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- A grown-up's full stomach holds about 1 litre. A steel tumbler holds about 250 millilitres. How many tumblers of food would fill the stomach? **4 tumblers.**. 1 litre is 1,000 millilitres. 250 plus 250 plus 250 plus 250 is 1,000. So 4 tumblers fill 1 litre. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L3`)
- Say that in the lump or bits test, the whole lump of mishri took 8 minutes to disappear and the crushed lump took 2 minutes. How many times as long did the whole lump take? **4 times as long.**. The whole lump took 8 minutes. The crushed lump took 2 minutes. 8 divided by 2 is 4. You can check: 2 plus 2 plus 2 plus 2 is 8. So the whole lump took 4 times as long. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- On the life-size organ map, the heart was 1 fist wide and the brain was 2 fists wide. If one fist is 7 centimetres, how wide was the brain? **14 centimetres.**. The brain was 2 fists wide. Each fist is 7 centimetres. 7 plus 7 is 14. So the brain was about 14 centimetres wide. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
