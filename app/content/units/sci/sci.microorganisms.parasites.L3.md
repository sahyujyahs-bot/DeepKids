# Parasites: how lice, ticks, worms and mosquitoes live on other living beings
`sci.microorganisms.parasites.L3`

**Summary.** A head louse spends their whole life on a human head, holding on to hairs and drinking a little blood. A living being who lives on or inside another living being, and feeds from them, is called a parasite. The one they live on is the host. Lice, fleas and ticks live on the outside of the body. Some worms live inside the gut. The malaria parasite lives part of their life in a mosquito and part in a person. Each parasite reaches us by one path, so we stay safe by closing that path: a fine comb, chappals, clean hands, a mosquito net.

**Intro.** *Hold a khus khus seed on your fingertip. A head louse's egg is smaller than this seed, and is glued to one hair. Your comb at home has gaps much wider than that. So how can anyone comb the eggs out?* In this unit you meet the parasites that live on people: head lice, fleas, ticks and worms. You find out what problem each one has, and how their body solves it. You test which comb can catch something as small as a louse egg. You follow the malaria parasite from a person into a mosquito and back. And you learn the simple habits that keep every one of them off you.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a parasite and a host are, and give examples.
1. I can explain how a head louse holds on to hair, and why a fine-tooth comb removes lice and their eggs.
2. I can describe how fleas and ticks reach a host, and how to keep them off.

## Terms
- **Parasite**: A living being who lives on or inside another living being, and takes food from them. *e.g.* A head louse is a parasite. They live in your hair and drink a little of your blood.
- **Host**: The living being a parasite lives on or in. *e.g.* When a dog has fleas, the dog is the fleas' host.
- **Louse (more than one: lice)**: A tiny insect with no wings who lives in hair and drinks blood. A head louse is about as big as a sesame seed. *e.g.* Head lice spread when two heads touch, for example when friends lean together over one phone.
- **Nit**: A louse's egg, or the empty egg case left after the young louse hatches. The mother glues each egg to one hair, close to the scalp. *e.g.* Nits look like tiny white or brown dots stuck to hairs. They do not shake off like dandruff.
- **Flea**: A tiny insect with no wings who jumps very far and drinks the blood of animals such as cats, dogs and rats. *e.g.* A cat flea can jump about 20 cm, about a hundred times their own length.
- **Tick**: A small animal with eight legs, a cousin of spiders, who holds on to skin and drinks blood for several days. *e.g.* Brown dog ticks are common on dogs in Indian cities. They swell up as they feed.
- **Mosquito**: A small fly with long legs. The female drinks blood to make her eggs. Some mosquitoes carry the parasites and viruses that cause malaria and dengue. *e.g.* A mosquito sits on your arm at dusk, and the bite itches afterwards.
- **Proboscis**: The long, thin mouthpart of a mosquito. Inside its cover are six fine needles. *e.g.* A mosquito pushes her proboscis into your skin to find a tiny blood vessel.
- **Malaria**: An illness that brings fever and shivering. A tiny one-celled parasite causes it, and some mosquitoes carry that parasite. *e.g.* A person with malaria may shiver under a blanket, then feel very hot, then sweat. A doctor's blood test shows the parasite.
- **Plasmodium**: The name scientists give the malaria parasite. Each one is a single cell, far too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Plasmodium lives in a person's liver and then inside their red blood cells.
- **Vector**: A living being who carries a germ or parasite from one host to another. The vector is usually not the one who makes you ill. *e.g.* The Anopheles mosquito is the vector of malaria. The parasite inside her is what causes the illness.
- **Roundworm**: A worm who can live in a person's gut. Their eggs are passed in poo and can get into soil. *e.g.* Roundworm eggs can get into the mouth from unwashed hands or unwashed vegetables grown in soil.
- **Hookworm**: A small worm whose young live in soil and get in through the skin of bare feet. In the gut, the worm holds on and drinks blood. *e.g.* Wearing chappals on soil keeps hookworms out.
- **Deworming**: Taking a medicine that clears worms out of the gut. *e.g.* On National Deworming Day, schools and anganwadis in India give children a worm medicine called albendazole.

## Explanations
### What a parasite is · *defined*
**Definition:** A parasite is a living being who lives on or inside another living being, the host, and takes food from them. The host is usually harmed a little, but most parasites do not kill their host.

Some parasites live on the outside of the body. Head lice, fleas and ticks hold on to hair or skin and drink blood. Others live inside. Roundworms and hookworms live in the gut, and the malaria parasite lives in the liver and the blood. Plants can be parasites too. Dodder is a yellow climbing plant with no green leaves. Dodder winds round another plant and pushes tiny suckers into their stem to take food. Birds can be parasites in another way. The koel lays her eggs in a crow's nest, and the crows raise the koel chicks as their own.
- e.g. A head louse lives in human hair and drinks blood several times a day.
- e.g. Dodder twines round a plant on a roadside fence and feeds from their stem.
- e.g. A koel chick grows up in a crow's nest, fed by the crow parents.
### Head lice: how a louse holds on · *defined*
**Definition:** A head louse is a small insect with no wings, up to 3 mm long. Head lice live only on people's heads, and drink blood from the scalp.

A louse has one big problem: staying on. A head moves, gets washed and gets combed. A louse cannot fly or jump, and if they fall off, they usually die within a day or two. So each of their six legs ends in a curved claw that closes round a single hair, the way your fingers close round a rope. A mother louse also solves a problem for her eggs. Eggs need warmth to hatch. So she glues each egg to a hair right next to the warm scalp. The glue is so strong that shampoo and water do not loosen it. The egg hatches in about 8 days. The young louse grows into an adult in about 9 more days, and a female can then lay up to 6 eggs a day.
- e.g. Lice spread when heads touch, for example in a group photo or when friends lean over one book.
- e.g. Lice live on clean hair just as well as on unwashed hair. Getting lice says nothing about how clean someone is.
- e.g. Dogs and cats do not carry human head lice.
### Getting lice out: the fine-tooth comb · *defined*
**Definition:** A fine-tooth lice comb has teeth so close together that a louse or a louse egg cannot slip between them. Combing wet hair from the roots to the ends pulls lice and eggs out.

In Tamil Nadu this comb is often called an eer kolli, the nit remover. The gaps between its teeth are much narrower than an ordinary comb's. Families wet the hair and add conditioner or a little coconut oil, so the comb slides and the lice move slowly. Then they comb every part of the head from the scalp outwards, and wipe the comb on a white tissue to see what came out. One combing misses a few eggs. So the NHS, the health service in the United Kingdom, says to comb on days 1, 5, 9 and 13. That catches young lice as they hatch, before they are old enough to lay eggs. A doctor or chemist can also give a lotion that kills lice. A grown-up uses it, following the instructions exactly.
- e.g. A 3,700-year-old ivory comb found at Lachish, in Israel, has a line carved on it asking the comb to root out the lice of the hair and the beard.
- e.g. Wiping the comb on a white tissue shows each louse clearly.
### Fleas: how a tiny jumper finds a big host · *defined*
**Definition:** A flea is a small insect with no wings, about 2 to 3 mm long, who drinks the blood of mammals and birds.

A flea's problem is getting on to a host who is huge and keeps moving. Their answer is a jump. A pad in each back leg is made of a springy material called resilin. The flea squeezes the pad, holds it, and lets go all at once, like a catapult. A cat flea can jump about 20 cm, about a hundred times their own length. Once on the animal, a flea's body is flat from side to side, so they slide between hairs. Spines on their body point backwards, so they are hard to pull out. Flea eggs fall off into the animal's bed. So washing a pet's bedding and vacuuming the floor remove the eggs.
- e.g. A street dog scratching hard behind the ears may have fleas.
- e.g. Fleas on rats once spread plague. In 1898 Paul-Louis Simond, working in Karachi and Bombay, found that rat fleas carry the plague germ.
### Ticks: wait, climb on and hold on · *defined*
**Definition:** A tick is a small arachnid, a cousin of spiders. An adult tick has eight legs. Ticks hold on to the skin and drink blood, often for several days.

A tick cannot jump or fly, so they wait. They climb a blade of grass and hold out their front legs. Those front legs have sense organs that detect the breath, warmth and smell of an animal walking past. When a dog or a person brushes by, the tick grabs on. Then they cut into the skin and push in a mouthpart covered with backward-pointing barbs, like a fish hook. Many ticks also make a kind of glue to fix themselves in place. That is why you must not tug a tick or squeeze their body. A grown-up takes hold of the tick with fine tweezers right next to the skin and pulls straight up, slowly and steadily. Then they wash the bite with soap and water.
- e.g. Brown dog ticks are common on dogs in Indian cities, inside homes and kennels.
- e.g. In 1957, monkeys began dying in the Kyasanur forest in Shivamogga district, Karnataka. Scientists found that ticks there spread a virus, now called Kyasanur Forest Disease.
### Worms that live inside us · *defined*
**Definition:** Some worms are parasites who live in the human gut. The commonest in Indian children are roundworms, hookworms and threadworms.

A worm inside the gut has plenty of food. The worm's hard problem is getting from one person to the next. Each kind solves it in their own way. A female roundworm lays thousands of eggs, which leave the body in poo. Where poo gets into soil, the eggs wait there. They get into a new person on dirty hands or unwashed vegetables. Young hookworms wait in damp soil and get in through the skin of bare feet. A threadworm crawls out of the bottom at night to lay eggs, which makes it itch. Scratching puts eggs under the nails, and from there they reach the mouth. So toilets, chappals, washing hands, short nails and washed vegetables close every one of these paths. Twice a year, on National Deworming Day, schools and anganwadis give children a tablet called albendazole that clears worms out.
- e.g. Wearing chappals on wet soil keeps hookworms out.
- e.g. Washing hands with soap after the toilet and before eating keeps roundworm and threadworm eggs out of the mouth.
- e.g. National Deworming Day is on 10 February and 10 August.
### Mosquitoes: why she bites and how she finds you · *defined*
**Definition:** A mosquito is a small fly. Male and female mosquitoes drink nectar and plant juice for energy. Only the female drinks blood, because she needs its protein to make her eggs.

A female mosquito's problem is finding blood in the dark. She follows the carbon dioxide in your breath, the warmth of your body and the smell of your skin and sweat. When she lands, she pushes in her proboscis. Inside its cover are six fine needles. Two saw through the skin, two hold it open, one sucks up blood and one pours in saliva. Her saliva stops your blood from clotting so that it keeps flowing. The itch afterwards is your body reacting to that saliva. If the person she bit before had malaria parasites in their blood, she may now be carrying them. Different mosquitoes bite at different times. Anopheles, who carries malaria, bites mostly from dusk to dawn. Aedes, black with white stripes, bites in the daytime and carries the dengue virus.
- e.g. Mosquitoes find you more easily after you run and breathe hard, because you breathe out more carbon dioxide.
- e.g. An Anopheles mosquito rests with her tail end tilted up, at an angle to the wall.
### Malaria: a parasite with two hosts · *defined*
**Definition:** Malaria is an illness caused by Plasmodium, a parasite made of a single cell. The parasite needs two hosts: a person and an Anopheles mosquito.

Here is the journey. A female Anopheles bites a person who has malaria, and drinks parasites with the blood. In the mosquito's stomach the parasites grow and multiply for about one to two weeks. Then they move to her saliva. When she bites someone else, they enter that person's blood. They travel first to the liver and multiply there. Then they move into the red blood cells, multiply again, and burst out. Each time they burst out, the person shivers and then gets a high fever. Malaria can be cured with medicines from a doctor, after a blood test. A medicine called quinine comes from the bark of the cinchona tree. From the 1860s cinchona trees were grown in the Nilgiri hills to make it. In 2024, malaria killed about 610,000 people around the world, most of them young children in Africa.
- e.g. A fever with shivering, in a place with mosquitoes, is a reason to see a doctor and get a blood test the same day.
- e.g. A mosquito only carries malaria if she has bitten a person with malaria at least one to two weeks earlier.
### Keeping safe: close the path · *defined*
**Definition:** Every parasite needs a path to reach a new host. Keeping safe means closing that path.

Lice move from head to head, so keep heads apart, check hair with a fine comb each week, and do not share combs or hair clips. Fleas and ticks come from animals and grass, so check pets and wash their bedding, and check your legs after walking in long grass. Worms come from soil and dirty hands, so wear chappals outside, wash hands with soap, keep nails short and wash vegetables. Mosquitoes need water to lay eggs and skin to bite. So cover stored water, empty flowerpot saucers and coolers once a week, sleep under a net, and wear long sleeves at dusk. A fan helps too. Studies have found that fewer mosquitoes reach people sitting in a fan's wind. Mosquitoes are weak fliers, and the wind blows away the breath and smells they follow. If anyone gets a fever with shivering, see a doctor quickly.
- e.g. A mosquito net over a bed keeps biting mosquitoes out all night.
- e.g. A lid on a water drum stops mosquitoes from laying eggs in it.
### Do parasites sense and choose? · *debated*
Parasites sense the world and make choices, like every living being. A tick waits for days on a grass blade and lets go of the grass only when the breath and warmth of an animal come close. A young dodder plant smells the air and grows towards the plant they will feed on. Scientists showed this in 2006: dodder seedlings grew towards the smell of a tomato plant and away from wheat. A female mosquito picks out one person in a room by their smell.
- e.g. Dodder seedlings grew towards the smell of a tomato plant in a test by Justin Runyon, Mark Mescher and Consuelo De Moraes in 2006.
- e.g. Some people get many more mosquito bites than others. In tests, mosquitoes chose the smell of their skin again and again.
- **Many plant scientists who study how plants behave:** Sensing a host, telling one host from another and growing towards them is choosing. A dodder seedling feels and decides where to go.
- **Other scientists:** These are automatic responses to chemicals, built up over millions of years. They work without anything like thought, the way a thermostat switches on a heater.

## Mostly agreed
- Head lice spread mostly by head-to-head contact. They do not jump or fly, and they are not caused by dirty hair. *How we know:* Health services such as the NHS in the United Kingdom say so, from studies of how lice move between children in schools and families.
- Malaria is caused by a single-celled parasite, Plasmodium, and is spread by the bite of female Anopheles mosquitoes. *How we know:* Laveran saw the parasite in blood in 1880. Ross found it in mosquitoes in 1897 and 1898, and Grassi's team showed that Anopheles passes it to people in 1898.
- Only female mosquitoes drink blood. They need its protein to make eggs. *How we know:* Scientists who raise mosquitoes find that males have mouthparts that cannot pierce skin, and that females lay eggs only after a blood meal.
- Hookworms get in through the skin of bare feet, and roundworm eggs are swallowed from soil on hands or food. *How we know:* Doctors have traced these paths for over a hundred years. Where people wear shoes and have toilets, these worms become much rarer.
- A tick should be removed with fine tweezers held close to the skin, pulled straight up, slowly and steadily. *How we know:* Health agencies such as the CDC in the United States advise this. Squeezing or burning a tick can leave the mouthparts in the skin.

## How we found out
**Alphonse Laveran, Patrick Manson, Ronald Ross with Muhammad Bux, and Giovanni Battista Grassi, 1880 (Laveran sees the parasite) to 1897 and 1898 (Ross and Grassi find the mosquito path).** For more than two thousand years, people blamed malaria on bad air. The name comes from the Italian 'mala aria', which means bad air. People thought foul air rising from swamps at night made them ill. In 1880, Alphonse Laveran, a French army doctor in Algeria, looked at a sick soldier's blood under a microscope. He saw tiny moving living beings inside it: the malaria parasite. But how did the parasite get from one person to another? Patrick Manson, a British doctor, had found that mosquitoes carry tiny worms from person to person. He guessed they might carry malaria too. Ronald Ross, a doctor born in Almora, tested the idea in India. For two years he let mosquitoes feed on people with malaria, then cut the mosquitoes open and searched. On 20 August 1897, in Secunderabad, next to Hyderabad, he found the parasites growing in the stomach of a mosquito with spotted wings, an Anopheles. In 1898 in Calcutta, with his helper Muhammad Bux, he showed that mosquito bites passed malaria between birds. That year, Giovanni Battista Grassi's team in Italy showed that Anopheles mosquitoes pass malaria to people. The bad air idea was wrong.  
*What people believed before:* People thought malaria came from bad air rising from swamps at night. Ross and Grassi showed that a parasite causes it, and a female Anopheles mosquito carries the parasite from one person to the next. Swamps and night mattered only because that is where and when these mosquitoes breed and bite.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Lice come from dirt, so only unwashed children get them.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Lice are linked in people's minds with being poor or unwashed, and itching feels dirty.  
  Against: Lice live on clean hair just as well. Shampoo does not loosen nits. Lice spread when heads touch, which happens to every child.
- **Malaria comes from bad air rising from swamps at night.** (Doctors and ordinary people for more than two thousand years. The name comes from the Italian 'mala aria', bad air.; *rejected*)  
  For: Malaria was worst near swamps and at night, and people who slept near marshes fell ill.  
  Against: Swamps and night are when and where Anopheles mosquitoes breed and bite. Ross and Grassi showed in 1897 and 1898 that the parasite travels in mosquitoes. Nets stop malaria, though air passes through them.
- **Lice can jump from one head to another.** (A common idea among children; *rejected*)  
  For: Lice seem to appear on a new head suddenly.  
  Against: A louse has no wings and no jumping legs. Their claws are made for holding hair, so they crawl across when hairs touch.
- **Some people really are mosquito magnets.** (Many people say so, and some scientists have tested it; *accepted*)  
  For: In tests in 2022, mosquitoes kept choosing the skin smell of certain people, again and again, over months.  
  Against: How many bites you get also depends on where you sit, your clothes, a fan and how much you breathe out.
- **Some worms may help the body as well as harm it.** (Some doctors and scientists who study the immune system; *debated*)  
  For: People and gut worms lived together for a very long time. Some studies suggest that worms calm the body's defences, and that allergies are more common where worms are rare.  
  Against: Worms cause anaemia and tiredness, and slow children's growth. Other studies found no clear help. Deworming children is still the right thing to do.

## Roots
We have met lice, fleas, ticks, worms and the malaria parasite, and tested which comb catches a louse egg. Now we ask what parasites tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Comb Test, most khus khus seeds slipped between the teeth of the wider combs. A louse egg is smaller still, and glued to one hair near the warm scalp. A head louse lives only on people, and dies within a day or two off a head. So a louse's home is a living person, who moves, washes and combs.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a home as a place, like a house, a nest or a pond. For a parasite, the home is another living being. That home breathes, grows, scratches and fights back, and a parasite's whole body is shaped to stay in it.  
*Evidence:* In the Comb Test, only the fine-tooth lice comb pushed the khus khus seeds along. A real nit is smaller than a khus khus seed and glued to the hair, so a louse family survives everything a head does except a very fine comb. Away from a head, a louse dies within a day or two.

- *who am i:* You are a host. Lice can live in your hair, and tiny mites live in the skin of nearly every grown-up's face.
- *what is life:* The malaria parasite cannot finish their life in one body. They need a person and a mosquito, one after the other.
- *what is this world:* All over the world, living beings live on and inside other living beings: dodder on plants, koel chicks in crows' nests, worms in guts.

A louse, a flea, a tick, a worm and the malaria parasite are very different beings. Yet each one lives on the life of another. The blood that keeps you alive also feeds the louse in your hair and the mosquito at your window. One life, yours, holds up several others at the same time. A host and a parasite have lived together for so long that each one's body has been shaped by the other.

- **Acharanga Sutra, Book 1, Lecture 4, Lesson 1 (Jain):** All breathing, living beings should not be killed, harmed or treated with violence. This is the pure and lasting law. (Jain monks take great care not to harm even tiny beings. A louse is a living being with their own life, and the Jain teaching asks us to notice that, even while we comb lice out to keep ourselves well. Combing gently with a fine comb removes lice without poisons.)
- **Thirukkural 948 (chapter on medicine):** Find the illness, then find its cause, then find what will end it, and then act. (This is how malaria was solved. Laveran found the parasite, Ross found the mosquito that carries them, and now we act by closing that path with nets, covered water and medicine.)
- Science (settled): Human head lice and chimpanzee lice split into separate kinds about 5 to 6 million years ago, around the time people and chimpanzees did. Lice have lived on our ancestors all that time.
- Science (debated): Body lice, who live in clothes, split from head lice when people began wearing clothes, perhaps between about 83,000 and 170,000 years ago.
- Science (open): What a louse or a tick senses and feels from the inside is not known.
- Other view, Many doctors and health workers: A parasite is simply a living being who uses another for food, and the right response is to remove them. Thinking about a louse's home or a louse's life can make people slow to treat lice, worms and malaria, which harm children. Health comes first.
- Practice, Feel the warmth they need (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Put both hands gently on top of your head. Feel how warm your scalp is. That warmth is what a louse egg needs to hatch. Now put your hands on your tummy and feel it rise and fall. Your breath is what a mosquito follows. Take five slow breaths. Your warmth and your breath reach out into the world, and other living beings notice them.
- Ask: A louse spends their whole life on one person. Is that person the louse's home, their food or their world?
- Ask: We comb lice out to stay well. How can we do that and still treat the louse as a living being?
- Ask: The malaria parasite needs two hosts. If every mosquito disappeared, what would happen to the parasite?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A louse lives on one person's head all their life. Is the louse part of that person, or a separate being? (hint: Think about where the louse gets their food and warmth, and what happens if they fall off.)
- *deep:* The malaria parasite cannot finish their life without both a mosquito and a person. Who is the parasite's home? (hint: Think of the journey from a person's blood into a mosquito's stomach and back.)
- *big:* A koel chick is raised by crows. Is the koel mother doing something wrong? (hint: Think about whether a crow or a koel follows rules like ours, and what each one needs.)
- *big:* We kill lice and worms to stay well. Is it fair to kill a living being who is only living their own life? (hint: Think about what happens to the host if nobody stops the parasites, and about how Jain monks try to harm no living being.)
- *big:* Your body is home to lice sometimes, and to tiny mites and bacteria all the time. Where do you end and the others begin? (hint: Think about your skin, your hair and your gut, and who else lives there.)
- *deep:* Why did a parasite as small as Plasmodium kill more people than tigers, snakes and wars? (hint: Think about how many mosquitoes there are, and how easily they reach people.)

## Story: Ajji's Comb
*The story is made up. The facts in it are true: nits are glued to the hair close to the scalp and do not wash off, lice spread when heads touch, and they live on clean hair as well as unwashed hair.*

Kavya was eight, and she lived with her parents and her grandmother in a flat in Mysuru. Her grandmother was called Ajji. Every evening Ajji oiled Kavya's hair with coconut oil and plaited it into one long rope.

One Monday, Kavya came home from school with her head bent low. She dropped her school bag by the door and did not say hello.

"What happened, kanda?" asked Ajji.

Kavya scratched behind her ear. Then she scratched the top of her head. "Divya said I have lice," she whispered. "She said lice are for dirty children. Everyone heard."

Ajji did not laugh, and she did not look shocked. She went to the cupboard and came back with a small comb. It was flat and pale, with teeth so close together that Kavya could hardly see the gaps.

"Come and sit on the step by the window," said Ajji. "We need good light for this."

Kavya sat on the low step with a white towel round her shoulders. Ajji sprinkled water on her hair from a steel tumbler and rubbed in a little oil. Then she parted the hair and began to comb, slowly, from the scalp to the ends.

After each stroke, Ajji wiped the comb on a white tissue. On the fourth stroke there was a tiny brown dot on the tissue. The dot moved.

"There," said Ajji gently. "That is a louse. Look."

Kavya looked. The louse was no bigger than a sesame seed. She had six legs, and she was crawling slowly across the tissue.

"So I am dirty," said Kavya, and her eyes filled with tears.

"Are you?" said Ajji. "You had a head bath on Saturday. I washed your hair myself. Was it dirty?"

Kavya shook her head.

"Then let me show you something." Ajji held up one of Kavya's hairs, close to her eyes. Near the root, almost touching the scalp, there was a tiny pale oval stuck to the hair. "That is a louse egg. We call it a nit. The mother louse glues each egg to a hair, right here, next to your warm scalp. The warmth helps the egg hatch."

Ajji dipped her finger in the tumbler and rubbed the nit with water. It stayed. She rubbed it with a little soap. It still stayed. Only when she pinched the nit between her fingernails and slid it all the way along the hair did it come off.

"You see?" said Ajji. "Soap and water do not move a nit. The glue is too strong. Washing does not keep lice away, and dirt does not bring them. Lice come from other heads."

Kavya thought about that. "But lice cannot jump," she said slowly. "We learned that. They have no wings."

"That's right. So how did they reach your head?"

Kavya remembered. On Friday, the whole class had crowded round Divya's new storybook. They had pressed their heads together to see the pictures. Divya had been in the middle, and Kavya had leaned right against her.

"Our heads touched," said Kavya. "Divya's and mine. And she was scratching all day on Friday."

Ajji nodded. "A louse crawls from one hair to the next when two heads touch. It takes only a moment. It does not matter whether the hair is clean or unwashed."

Kavya sat very still. "Then Divya has lice too. And she said lice are for dirty children."

"Divya probably does not know she has them," said Ajji. "And someone told her that lice mean dirt, the way someone told you. People have said it for a very long time. It is still not true."

Ajji combed for a long time. She combed every part of Kavya's head, front to back and side to side. The tissue collected seven lice and many nits. Then she combed the whole head again, to catch any she had missed.

"Is it finished now?" asked Kavya.

"Not yet. A few eggs always hide. In four days they will hatch, so we will comb again on Friday. Then again, four days after that, and once more after that. By then every louse will be gone before they are old enough to lay eggs."

The next morning, Kavya asked Ajji for two things. The first was the comb, wrapped in a clean handkerchief. The second was a note for Divya's mother.

At school, Kavya found Divya alone by the water tank. Divya was scratching behind her ear.

"I had lice," said Kavya. "I got them from you, I think, when we looked at your book. But it is not because you are dirty. My Ajji showed me. Lice crawl when heads touch. Here, my Ajji wrote how to comb them out."

Divya stared at her. Then she took the note. "I am sorry I said that," she said quietly.

That evening, Kavya told Ajji everything. Ajji smiled and began to oil her hair.

"Ajji," said Kavya, "the louse lives on my head and eats from me, and I never even felt her arrive. Her whole life happens right here." She touched the top of her head. "I am her whole world."

"Yes," said Ajji, "and now you know how to ask her to leave."

**For the parent:** Lice do not come from dirt. A louse crawls across when two heads touch, and her eggs are glued so tightly that washing does not move them. Only a fine comb does.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Comb Test: which comb can catch a louse egg? (30 min)
- **question:** A louse egg is smaller than a khus khus seed. Which comb can catch something that small: a wide-tooth comb, an ordinary comb or a fine-tooth lice comb?
- **predict:** Look at the gaps between the teeth of each comb. Guess which combs will push the sesame seeds along, and which will push the tiny khus khus seeds. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same seeds, the same sheet of paper and the same slow sweep for every comb. Only the comb changes. Do three sweeps with each comb, because one sweep can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Keep the seeds away from babies and small children, who may put them in their nose or mouth. Wash the lice comb before using it on hair.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the three combs, with the gaps between their teeth, and draw a sesame seed and a khus khus seed to the same size beside them.
- **results table:** Comb | Sweep | Sesame seeds pushed to the line (out of 10) | Khus khus seeds pushed to the line (out of 10)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the three combs side by side. 2. Hold each one against the ruler. 3. Count how many teeth fit in 1 cm. 4. Look at the gaps with a magnifying glass if you have one.  
  *Parent says:* These three combs have different gaps between their teeth. Let's count how many teeth fit in one centimetre on each comb.  
  *Child mode:* Hold each comb next to the ruler. Count the teeth in 1 cm. Which comb has the smallest gaps?
  - Note: A wide-tooth comb may have 2 or 3 teeth in 1 cm. An ordinary comb has about 4 to 8. A lice comb has many more, often 10 or more, with gaps too thin to measure with a school ruler. Counting teeth in 1 cm is a simple way to compare gaps.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a guess and a reason. 2. Write the guesses at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which comb will push the sesame seeds? Which will push the tiny khus khus seeds? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which comb will push the sesame? Which will push the khus khus? Why?
  - Ask: What makes you think that comb will catch the small seeds? (good: Its gaps are smaller than the seed.; The seed is too big to slip between its teeth.)
  - Note: Wait for a guess and a reason before going on. Do not explain yet. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a start line across the paper with the pencil. 2. Draw a finish line 10 cm away. 3. Put 10 sesame seeds and 10 khus khus seeds along the start line, mixed up, about 1 cm apart.  
  *Parent says:* The seeds are our pretend lice and nits. A sesame seed is about the size of a grown louse. A khus khus seed is a little bigger than a louse egg.  
  *Child mode:* Draw two lines, 10 cm apart. Put the seeds on the first line. Sesame is like a louse. Khus khus is like an egg.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the wide-tooth comb on the start line, behind the seeds, with its teeth touching the paper. 2. Sweep it slowly to the finish line. 3. Count the sesame and the khus khus seeds that reached the line. 4. Put them all back and do it twice more.  
  *Parent says:* Keep the teeth flat on the paper and move slowly. Then count what reached the finish line.  
  *Child mode:* Put the comb behind the seeds. Teeth touch the paper. Sweep slowly. Count the seeds at the line. Do it three times.
  - Note: Seeds sometimes jump over the comb or roll sideways. If a seed rolls off the paper, put the seed back and redo that sweep. Pressing the teeth lightly on the paper gives steadier results.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same three sweeps with the ordinary comb, and then with the lice comb. Write every count in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now the ordinary comb, three times. Then the lice comb, three times. Same seeds, same lines, same slow sweep.  
  *Child mode:* Now try the ordinary comb three times. Then the lice comb three times. Write each count.
  - Note: Usually the wide comb pushes some sesame seeds and almost no khus khus, because the small seeds slip between its teeth. An ordinary comb pushes most sesame seeds and some khus khus. The lice comb pushes nearly all of both. Results vary with the comb, so what your child sees is the real result.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask for a claim, the evidence and a reason. 3. Then read out the fact about real nits.  
  *Parent says:* Which comb caught the khus khus? What did you see? Why do you think that comb worked? Here is a fact. A real louse egg is smaller than a khus khus seed, and is glued to one hair near the scalp.  
  *Child mode:* Which comb caught the khus khus? Why did it work? A real louse egg is even smaller.
  - Ask: What I think, what I saw, and why? (good: What I think: only a comb with very small gaps catches tiny things. What I saw: the lice comb pushed the khus khus, the wide comb did not. Why: the seeds slipped between the wide teeth.)
  - Check: In the Comb Test, the khus khus seeds slipped past the wide-tooth comb. Why? → **The gaps between the teeth were wider than the seeds**
### e2 · The Fan Test: where does a fan stop mosquitoes? (20 min)
- **question:** Mosquitoes are weak fliers. Where in your bedroom is the fan's wind strong, and where is the air still enough for a mosquito to land?
- **predict:** Look at the room with the fan on. Guess which places get strong wind and which stay still: the pillow, the foot of the bed, the corners, under the bed. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same paper strip, held at the same height as a sleeping person, with the fan at the same speed. Only the place changes. Hold the strip still for 10 seconds at each place.
- **safety:** A grown-up switches the fan on and off. Never put fingers or the paper near a table fan's blades. Do not stand on a bed or chair under a ceiling fan.
- **record:** Draw a map of the room from above. Mark the fan, the bed and each place you tested, with its score. Colour the still places in one colour and the windy places in another.
- **results table:** Place in the room | My guess (still, flutters or blows flat) | What the strip did | Score: 0 still, 1 flutters, 2 blows flat
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the tissue strip to the end of the pencil. 2. Choose eight places to test. For example: the pillow, the middle and foot of the bed, each corner and under the bed.  
  *Parent says:* This flag shows where the fan's wind reaches. Mosquitoes are weak fliers, so strong wind makes it hard for them to land.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the paper to the pencil. It is a wind flag. Pick eight places to test.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason for each place, and write them in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which places will be windy, and which will be still? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess each place. Still, flutters or blows flat? Why?
  - Note: Wait for the guesses. Many children think the whole room is windy when the fan is on.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up switches the fan on at its usual speed. 2. Hold the flag at the height of a person lying in bed, at each place, for 10 seconds. 3. Score it: 0 still, 1 flutters, 2 blows flat.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the flag still and count to ten. Is the paper still, fluttering or blowing flat?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the flag still. Count to ten. Is it still, fluttering or flat? Write the score.
  - Note: Under a ceiling fan, the wind is usually strongest just below and around the blades, and weaker in the corners and under the bed. A table fan blows mostly in one direction. Results depend on your fan and room, so what you find is the real answer for your room.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the wind map. 2. Talk about where the pillow should go.  
  *Parent says:* Where are the still places? Mosquitoes can land there easily. Where should the pillow and your face be at night?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your wind map. Where is the air still? Where should your pillow go?
  - Check: In the Fan Test, the flag stayed still in a corner. Why might a mosquito find it easier to bite someone sleeping there? → **Mosquitoes are weak fliers, and in still air they can fly and land easily**

1. (mcq) Which of these is a parasite?  
   Options: A honeybee / A head louse / A tiger  
   **Answer:** A head louse. *Why:* A head louse lives on a person's head and drinks their blood. A bee helps flowers, and a tiger is a predator who kills and eats.

   **Answer:** ['host']. *Why:* The host gives the parasite a home and food.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Lice need warmth and blood, not dirt. Getting lice says nothing about how clean someone is.

   Options: The gaps were wider than the seeds / The seeds were sticky / The comb was too long  
   **Answer:** The gaps were wider than the seeds. *Why:* A seed smaller than the gap slips between two teeth. Only teeth closer together than the seed can push the seed along.
7. (sort) Sort these parasites. Do they live on the outside of the body or inside it?  
   **Answer:** head louse → Outside; roundworm → Inside; tick → Outside; hookworm → Inside; flea → Outside; malaria parasite → Inside. *Why:* Lice, ticks and fleas hold on to hair or skin. Worms live in the gut, and the malaria parasite lives in the liver and blood.
8. (mcq) How many legs does an adult tick have?  
   Options: 6 / 8 / 10  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Ticks are arachnids, cousins of spiders, and adults have eight legs. Insects such as lice and fleas have six.
9. (truefalse) Only female mosquitoes bite.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Females need protein from blood to make eggs. Males drink only nectar and plant juice.
10. (mcq) Which of these keeps mosquitoes from laying eggs at home?  
   Options: Leaving water in flowerpot saucers / Emptying and scrubbing saucers and coolers once a week / Keeping windows open at dusk  
   **Answer:** Emptying and scrubbing saucers and coolers once a week. *Why:* Mosquitoes lay eggs in still water, and the young take about a week to grow. Emptying the water each week stops them.
11. (match) Match each parasite to how they reach a person.  
   **Answer:** Head louse → crawls across when heads touch; Hookworm → gets in through bare feet; Malaria parasite → comes in a mosquito's bite; Roundworm → eggs are swallowed from dirty hands. *Why:* Each parasite has one main path. Closing that path keeps them out.
12. (order) Put the malaria parasite's journey in order.  
   **Answer:** ['A mosquito bites a person with malaria', "The parasites grow in the mosquito's stomach", "The parasites move to the mosquito's saliva", 'The mosquito bites a new person']. *Why:* The parasite needs a person and a mosquito. They grow in the mosquito for one to two weeks before they can pass on.
13. (perspective) Kavya and her friend Divya both have head lice. Three grown-ups each say what to do.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and gives a reason from the science. For example, combing four times, four days apart, catches the lice that hatch from eggs the comb missed. It then names a fair good point of another choice, such as a lotion killing lice in one go, or short hair being quick to comb.. *Why:* All three can work, and each has a cost. Combing takes time but uses no chemicals. A lotion needs a grown-up and care. Cutting hair short is quick but many children do not want it.
14. (write) Why does a mosquito net stop malaria, even though air passes through it?  
   **Answer:** Malaria is not in the air. The parasite is carried by a mosquito. The net's holes are smaller than a mosquito, so she cannot reach you to bite.. *Why:* A full answer says that the mosquito carries the parasite and the net keeps the mosquito out.
15. (draw) Draw a head louse holding a hair, with an egg glued near the scalp.  
   **Answer:** A small insect with six legs, each ending in a claw round the hair, and a small oval egg on the hair close to the scalp.. *Why:* Claws let the louse stay on. The egg is glued near the scalp because warmth helps the egg hatch.
### How Parasites Live (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A louse falls off a child's head onto a cushion. What usually happens to the louse?  
   Options: She lives on the cushion for months / She dies within a day or two / She jumps back  
   **Answer:** She dies within a day or two. *Why:* A head louse needs blood and warmth from a head. Away from one, she usually dies within a day or two. That is why there is no need for big cleaning of the house.
2. (write) A mother louse glues each egg to a hair right next to the scalp. Why there, and not at the end of the hair?  
   **Answer:** The scalp is warm, and the eggs need warmth to hatch. At the end of the hair it is cooler, and the egg could be cut off or combed out more easily.. *Why:* A strong answer names the louse's problem, keeping the eggs warm and safe, and says how gluing them near the scalp solves it.
3. (truefalse) A fine comb must be used on days 1, 5, 9 and 13, because one combing can miss eggs.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Eggs hatch in about 8 days. Combing every four days catches young lice before they are old enough to lay eggs.
4. (mcq) A flea's body is flat from side to side. How does that help the flea?  
   Options: The flea can slide between an animal's hairs / The flea can fly / The flea stays cool  
   **Answer:** The flea can slide between an animal's hairs. *Why:* A thin, flat body slips between hairs. Backward-pointing spines also make the flea hard to pull out.
5. (mcq) Why should a grown-up never squeeze a tick's body to pull the tick off?

   **Answer:** Squeezing can leave the barbed mouthparts in the skin. *Why:* A tick's mouthpart has backward barbs and is often glued in. Squeezing pulls the body off and leaves the mouthpart behind. Grip close to the skin with fine tweezers and pull straight up.

   Options: Straight away / After about one to two weeks / Never

   **Answer:** The air there is still, so a mosquito can fly and rest there easily. *Why:* Mosquitoes are weak fliers. Still places are where they can fly and rest easily.
11. (fill) The malaria parasite's name is ____.

### Think Like Ronald Ross (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In the Comb Test, Meenakshi's wide comb pushed 9 of 10 sesame seeds but only 1 of 10 khus khus seeds. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: The sesame seeds were bigger than the gaps, and most khus khus seeds were smaller / Khus khus seeds are heavier / She swept faster for the khus khus  
   **Answer:** The sesame seeds were bigger than the gaps, and most khus khus seeds were smaller. *Why:* Seeds bigger than the gap get pushed. Seeds smaller than the gap slip through. The one khus khus seed pushed may have been stuck against a sesame seed or a tooth.
2. (mcq) A louse egg hatches in about 8 days. The young louse becomes an adult who can lay eggs about 9 days later. If you comb only once, what could happen?  
   Options: Eggs the comb missed hatch, grow up and lay new eggs / Nothing, because one combing gets every egg / The lice leave on their own  
   **Answer:** Eggs the comb missed hatch, grow up and lay new eggs. *Why:* One combing always misses a few eggs. Combing every four days catches the young lice before they are old enough to lay eggs.
3. (mcq) Why is it hard for a parasite inside the gut to reach a new host?  
   Options: The gut is too warm / They must leave the body, survive outside, and get into someone else / Worms cannot eat  
   **Answer:** They must leave the body, survive outside, and get into someone else. *Why:* Inside the gut there is plenty of food. The hard part is the journey to a new person, through soil, hands, food or skin.
4. (truefalse) A mosquito net stops malaria because air cannot pass through it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Air passes through a net easily. The net stops the mosquito, and the mosquito is what carries the parasite.
5. (mcq) People once thought malaria came from bad air near swamps at night. Why did the idea seem right?  
   Options: Swamps and night are where and when Anopheles mosquitoes breed and bite / Swamp air is poisonous / People never got malaria in the daytime  
   **Answer:** Swamps and night are where and when Anopheles mosquitoes breed and bite. *Why:* The bad air idea matched what people saw, but the real cause was the mosquitoes who breed in still water and bite at night.
6. (write) A tick has no wings and cannot jump. Explain how a tick still finds a host.  
   **Answer:** The tick climbs a grass blade and holds out their front legs. The legs sense the breath, warmth and smell of an animal coming close. When the animal brushes past, the tick grabs on.. *Why:* A full answer says the tick waits, senses the animal and grabs on.
7. (write) Farhan's family empties their cooler once a month. Mosquitoes take about a week to grow from egg to adult in water. Is once a month enough? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. In a month, about four batches of mosquitoes could grow up and fly away. The cooler must be emptied and scrubbed at least once a week, so no young mosquitoes have time to become adults.. *Why:* A strong answer compares the two times, a week to grow and a month between emptying, and says what follows.
8. (perspective) A koel mother lays her egg in a crow's nest, and the crows raise the koel chick. Three voices talk about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a voice and backs it with facts from the unit: what a host gives, what a parasite takes, and how each has changed the other. Then it answers back fairly from a second voice.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. The crow loses food and work, the koel has no other way to raise chicks, and the scientist describes how both have changed over time.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most parasites harm their host a little and live with them for a long time. A host who dies quickly takes the parasite's home and food away.
10. (write) The malaria parasite needs two hosts. Name them, and say what happens in each.  
   **Answer:** A person and an Anopheles mosquito. In the person, the parasites grow in the liver and then in red blood cells, causing fever. In the mosquito, they grow in the stomach and move to the saliva, ready to pass on.. *Why:* A full answer names both hosts and one thing that happens in each.
11. (mcq) Kavya has a fever with shivering, and there are many mosquitoes where she lives. What should her family do?  
   Options: Wait a week to see if it goes / Take her to a doctor for a blood test the same day / Give her a lice comb  
   **Answer:** Take her to a doctor for a blood test the same day. *Why:* Malaria can be cured, but it is dangerous if left. A blood test shows the parasite, and the right medicine clears them.

   **Answer:** He also needed to show that a mosquito's bite passes the parasite to a new host. *Why:* Finding the parasite in a mosquito showed she could pick the parasite up. In 1898 Ross showed bites passing malaria between birds, and Grassi's team showed Anopheles passing it to people.

A predator kills and eats their prey.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a parasite?  
   Options: A living being who kills and eats another / A living being who lives on or inside another living being and feeds from them / Any very small living being  
   **Answer:** A living being who lives on or inside another living being and feeds from them. *Why:* A parasite lives on or in a host and takes food from them. A being who kills and eats another is a predator. Many small beings, such as ants, are not parasites.
2. (mcq) A dog has fleas. What do we call the dog?

   **Answer:** The host. *Why:* The host is the living being a parasite lives on. A vector carries a parasite from one host to another, as a mosquito carries malaria.

   Options: The wide-tooth comb / The fine-tooth lice comb / All three combs the same  
   **Answer:** The fine-tooth lice comb. *Why:* Only the lice comb's gaps were narrower than the seeds. The small seeds slipped between the teeth of the wider combs. A real louse egg is smaller than a khus khus seed, so only a fine comb lifts the egg out.

   Options: They jump / They fly / They crawl across when two heads touch  
   **Answer:** They crawl across when two heads touch. *Why:* Lice have no wings and cannot jump. Their claws are made for gripping hair, so they crawl from hair to hair when heads touch.

   Options: Nits are hidden inside the hair / The mother louse glues each egg firmly to a hair / Nits are too heavy to wash away  
   **Answer:** The mother louse glues each egg firmly to a hair. *Why:* The glue is very strong and water does not loosen it. A fine comb, or sliding the nit off with fingernails, is needed.

## Applications
- Families use fine-tooth lice combs to clear lice without chemicals.
- Mosquito nets, window mesh and covered water tanks protect homes from malaria and dengue.
- National Deworming Day gives worm medicine to children in schools and anganwadis twice a year.
- Health workers test a drop of blood for malaria parasites under a microscope, or with a quick test kit.
- City health teams check homes and building sites for still water where mosquitoes breed.
- Vets check dogs and cats for fleas and ticks.
- Doctors use what is known about each parasite's path to decide how to stop an outbreak.
- Scientists study louse DNA to learn when people first began to wear clothes.

## Open questions
- **Can malaria ever be wiped out completely?** Many countries, including Sri Lanka in 2016, have ended malaria. But mosquitoes are learning to survive insecticides and parasites to survive medicines, so nobody knows whether the whole world can.
- **Why do some people get many more mosquito bites than others?** Tests show that skin smell matters a lot, but scientists do not yet fully know which smells, or why they differ from person to person.
- **Do gut worms help the body in any way?** Some studies suggest worms calm the body's defences and lower allergies. Others find no clear help. The question is still argued about.
- **What is it like to be a tick, waiting for weeks on a blade of grass?** We can measure what a tick senses, but nobody knows what that waiting is like from the inside.
- **Exactly how many kinds of parasites are there?** Many parasites are tiny and live inside other animals, so most have never been counted or named. Some scientists think there may be more parasite species than free-living ones.

## Connected maths
- A louse egg hatches about 8 days after being laid. The young louse becomes an adult about 9 days after hatching. How many days is it from egg to adult? **About 17 days.**. 8 days as an egg, then 9 days growing. 8 + 9 = 17. So a family that combs every 4 days, on days 1, 5, 9 and 13, catches every young louse long before day 17, when they could start laying eggs. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- A female louse can lay up to 6 eggs a day. What is the most eggs she could lay in one week? **42 eggs.**. A week is 7 days. 7 lots of 6 is 42. So one louse missed by the comb can quickly mean many more, which is why every part of the head is combed. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Comb Test, Meenakshi pushed the khus khus seeds three times with each comb. Out of 10, the wide comb pushed 1, 0 and 2. The ordinary comb pushed 4, 6 and 5. The lice comb pushed 10, 9 and 10. Which comb would you use to remove nits, and how sure are you? **The lice comb. Every sweep it pushed 9 or 10 seeds, while the others never pushed more than 6.**. Add each comb's sweeps. Wide: 1 + 0 + 2 = 3 out of 30. Ordinary: 4 + 6 + 5 = 15 out of 30. Lice comb: 10 + 9 + 10 = 29 out of 30. The lice comb's worst sweep, 9, beats the ordinary comb's best sweep, 6. So we can be sure the result is not a fluke. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- On 3 August, a mosquito bites a person with malaria. The parasites need about 12 days in her body before she can pass them on. On which date could she first pass on malaria? **15 August.**. 3 + 12 = 15, so 15 August. This shows something surprising. A young mosquito cannot pass on malaria. Only a mosquito who lives more than about 12 days after biting a sick person can. That is why stopping mosquitoes from living long, with nets and covered water, works so well. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- A cat flea is about 2 mm long and can jump about 20 cm. How many times their own length is that? If a child 120 cm tall could jump the same number of times their height, how far would that be? **About 100 times. The child would jump about 12,000 cm, which is 120 metres.**. 20 cm is 200 mm. 200 divided by 2 is 100. Then 120 cm times 100 is 12,000 cm. 100 cm make 1 metre, so that is 120 metres, longer than a cricket ground. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
