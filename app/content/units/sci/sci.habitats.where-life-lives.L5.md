# Why life lives where it does: moving continents, islands and invasive species
`sci.habitats.where-life-lives.L5`

**Summary.** Both polar seas are freezing and full of fish, yet penguins live only in the south and polar bears only in the north. Why? In this unit you will place eight animals on a world map, test whether two families of copies that never mix stay alike, and line up fossils on the Gondwana jigsaw.

**Intro.** *Penguins swim in icy seas and eat fish. The Arctic, at the top of the world, is an icy sea full of fish. So why does no penguin live in the Arctic?* The Arctic would probably suit a penguin. But penguins first lived in the far south, and nearly all of them still do. Between the south and the Arctic lie thousands of kilometres of warm sea, and penguins need cold water. Polar bears have the same problem the other way round. Their ancestors were bears of the north, and no bear ever walked to Antarctica. So there are two questions to ask about any living being. Does this place suit her? And could her ancestors get here? The study of where living beings are found, and why, is called biogeography. In this unit you find the barriers that keep living beings apart. You learn that the continents themselves move. And you test what happens when one family is split in two.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can give three reasons why a living being is found in one place and not in another.

2. By the end I can explain how moving continents carried living beings apart, and tell the story of India's journey.
3. By the end I can explain why a shallow sea became a land bridge in the ice age and a deep sea did not.
4. By the end I can explain why groups that are kept apart become different, and why islands have endemic species.
5. By the end I can tell native, introduced and invasive species apart, with examples from India.
6. By the end I can give reasons why some introduced species spread so fast, and compare ways of controlling them.

## Terms
- **Biogeography**: Biogeography is the study of where living beings are found, and why they are found there. *e.g.* A biogeographer asks why tigers live in Asia but not in Africa, though both have forests and deer or antelope.
- **Range**: The range of a kind of living being is the whole area where they live in the wild. *e.g.* The range of the Nilgiri tahr, a mountain goat, is a few high hills in Tamil Nadu and Kerala.
- **Barrier**: A barrier is anything that stops a living being from spreading further, such as a sea, a mountain range, a desert or a change in climate. *e.g.* The salty sea is a barrier for a frog. A strip of dry land is a barrier for a river fish.
- **Dispersal**: Dispersal is the movement of living beings, or their seeds, eggs or young, from the place where they were born to a new place. *e.g.* A coconut that floats from one beach to another has dispersed.
- **Tectonic plate**: A tectonic plate is one of about a dozen huge slabs of rock that together make the hard outer shell of the Earth. The plates carry the continents and the sea floor. *e.g.* India, Sri Lanka and part of the Indian Ocean floor ride on the Indian plate.
- **Plate tectonics**: Plate tectonics is the slow movement of the Earth's plates. They move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as a fingernail grows. *e.g.* The Indian plate moves north-east about 5 centimetres every year, and this pushes the Himalaya up.
- **Gondwana**: Gondwana was a giant southern land that existed long ago. It broke into Africa, South America, Antarctica, Australia, Madagascar and India. *e.g.* Fossils of the same plants are found in India and in Antarctica, because both were once parts of Gondwana.
- **Fossil**: A fossil is what is left of a living being from long ago, kept in rock. It can be a bone, a shell, a leaf print or a footprint. *e.g.* Fossil leaves of a plant called Glossopteris are found in the coal of Jharkhand and Odisha.
- **Land bridge**: A land bridge is a strip of land that joins two larger lands for a time, so that animals and plants can cross on foot or by root. *e.g.* During the last ice age the sea was lower, and dry land joined Sri Lanka to India.
- **Ice age**: An ice age is a long cold time when much of the Earth's water is frozen into thick ice on the land. With so much water held in ice, the sea level is lower. *e.g.* About 20,000 years ago the sea was about 120 metres lower than it is today.
- **Isolated**: A group of living beings is isolated when a barrier keeps them from meeting and having young with others of their kind. *e.g.* Frogs on an island far out at sea are isolated from frogs on the mainland.
- **Ancestor**: An ancestor is a living being from long ago from whom later ones are descended, like a great-great-grandparent many times over. *e.g.* The purple frog of Kerala and some tiny frogs of the Seychelles islands share an ancestor who lived on Gondwana.
- **Endemic**: A kind of living being is endemic to a place when they live there naturally and nowhere else on Earth. *e.g.* The Nicobar megapode, a bird who warms her eggs in a mound of sand and leaves, is endemic to the Nicobar Islands.
- **Native**: A kind of living being is native to a place if they reached it, or arose there, without being carried by people. *e.g.* The neem tree and the tiger are native to India.
- **Introduced**: A kind of living being is introduced to a place if people brought them there, on purpose or by accident. *e.g.* The chilli plant was introduced to India from the Americas about 500 years ago.
- **Invasive**: An introduced kind of living being is invasive if they spread fast without help and harm the living beings who were there before. *e.g.* Lantana, a garden shrub from tropical America, is invasive in India's forests.
- **Natural enemy**: A natural enemy of a living being is another being who eats them, lives on them or makes them ill, and so keeps their numbers down. *e.g.* A tiny weevil who eats only the water fern salvinia is a natural enemy of salvinia.
- **Biological control**: Biological control is the use of a natural enemy to bring down the numbers of an invasive plant or animal. *e.g.* In Kerala in the 1980s, weevils from Brazil were released to control salvinia, a water fern from Brazil.
- **Copying mistake (mutation)**: Every living being carries instructions called DNA and copies them for their young. A mutation is a small mistake in a copy. It is then passed on to the next copies. *e.g.* Every child is born with a few dozen small changes in their DNA that neither parent had.

## Explanations
### Biogeography: three reasons a living being lives where she does · *defined*
**Definition:** A kind of living being is found in a place for three reasons together. The place suits them. Their ancestors could reach it. And the land has a history that took them there or kept them there.

Most people stop at the first reason. They say a camel lives in the desert because the desert suits her. That is true, but it leaves a lot unexplained. The deserts of Australia would suit camels too, and no camel lived there until people brought them in the 1800s. Today hundreds of thousands of camels live wild in Australia. So the Australian desert suited camels all along. Camels were missing only because their ancestors, who lived in Asia and Africa, could not cross the sea. The third reason is the oldest. Land moves, seas rise and fall, and mountains grow. A living being may be where she is because the ground under her ancestors carried them there.
- e.g. Penguins would probably find food in the Arctic. They are not there, because warm seas lie between.
- e.g. Wallabies, who are small relatives of kangaroos, were taken from Australia to New Zealand in the 1870s. Their descendants now live wild over a wide area of hill country in the South Island.
- e.g. Lantana grows very well in India. Yet no lantana grew here until people brought the plant about 200 years ago.
### Barriers: what stops a living being from spreading · *defined*
**Definition:** A barrier is anything that a kind of living being cannot cross in the ordinary course of life.

A barrier is different for each being. The sea stops a frog, because salt water draws the water out through a frog's thin skin. The same sea is a road for a sea turtle. A high, cold mountain range stops a lowland snake and hardly slows an eagle. A desert stops a forest tree, because their seedlings die before they find water. Climate is a barrier too, even where nothing stands in the way. Warm tropical water is a barrier for penguins. So to say what a barrier is, you must also say for whom. This is why two places with the same climate can have quite different animals. The same kinds could live in both. But a barrier lies between, and it has been there for a very long time.
- e.g. The Himalaya keep many animals of the cold lands to the north apart from the animals of India.
- e.g. For a fish in a river, dry land and the salty sea are both barriers. So two rivers side by side can hold different fish.
- e.g. The Palghat Gap is a low, warm, dry break in the cool, wet hills of the Western Ghats. Some hill birds and frogs live on only one side of it.
### Crossing a barrier: flying, floating, rafting and riding · *defined*
**Definition:** Dispersal is movement to a new place. Living beings cross barriers by flying, by floating, by riding on something that floats, or by riding on another animal.

In 1883 the volcano Krakatau in Indonesia exploded and destroyed most of the island. When scientists landed nine months later, the only living being they found was one spider. A young spider can let out a thread of silk and be carried by the wind. Within a few years grasses and ferns were growing, from seeds and spores brought by wind, sea and birds. Larger animals cross water on rafts, which are mats of plants or fallen trees washed out to sea. In 1995, after two hurricanes, at least fifteen iguanas landed on the island of Anguilla in the Caribbean Sea. They had ridden on uprooted trees for about 320 kilometres. A crossing like that is very rare. But a thing that happens once in ten thousand years has happened about a hundred times in a million years. Over a very long time, rare crossings become almost certain.
- e.g. A coconut has a thick husk full of air and can float in the sea for many weeks.
- e.g. The ancestors of Madagascar's lemurs probably crossed from Africa on a raft of plants, between 50 and 60 million years ago.
- e.g. A kind of dragonfly called the globe skimmer flies across the sea between India and East Africa.
### The ground moves: tectonic plates · *defined*
**Definition:** The hard outer shell of the Earth is broken into about a dozen large plates. The plates move a few centimetres a year, and they carry the continents with them.

Under the plates lies the mantle, a thick layer of hot rock. The rock of the mantle is solid, but it is so hot that it flows very slowly, a little like very stiff dough. Hot rock rises and cooler rock sinks. Where two plates move apart, hot rock comes up and makes new sea floor. Where they meet, one plate may slide down under the other, and its sinking weight pulls the rest of the plate along. Why is the inside of the Earth hot? About half the heat is left over from the time the Earth formed. The other half is made today by atoms of uranium, thorium and potassium deep in the rock. These atoms slowly break down, and each one gives out a little heat. Today satellites measure the movement directly. The Indian plate moves north-east at about 5 centimetres a year.
- e.g. The Atlantic Ocean gets a few centimetres wider every year, as new sea floor forms down its middle.
- e.g. India pushing into Asia lifts the Himalaya and causes earthquakes from Kashmir to Arunachal Pradesh.
- e.g. Your fingernails grow about 4 centimetres in a year. That is close to the speed of a plate.
### Gondwana, and India's long journey · *defined*
**Definition:** India was once part of Gondwana, a giant southern land. India broke away, moved north across the sea for tens of millions of years, and joined Asia.

About 180 million years ago Gondwana began to break up. India and Madagascar stayed joined for a long time. Then, roughly 90 million years ago, India split from Madagascar. About 65 million years ago the small piece of land that is now the Seychelles islands broke off from India. India was then an island continent, and it moved north unusually fast for a plate. About 50 million years ago India began to push into Asia, and the Himalaya started to rise. Scientists' dates for these events differ by several million years, because they come from different kinds of rock evidence. The name Gondwana comes from India. In 1872 the geologist Henry Medlicott named a set of rock layers in central India after the land of the Gond people. Later the Austrian geologist Eduard Suess used the name for the whole lost southern land.
- e.g. Fossils of Lystrosaurus, a plant-eating land animal about 1 metre long, are found in India, in South Africa and in Antarctica.
- e.g. Fossil leaves of the plant Glossopteris are found in India, Australia, Africa, South America and Antarctica.
- e.g. Very old rocks in eastern Madagascar match very old rocks in south-west India.
### Who rode on India, and who walked in later? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that India carried living beings from Gondwana and also received many from Asia. They do not agree on how large each share is.

The clearest passenger is the purple frog of the Western Ghats. She lives underground for most of the year. In 2003 S. D. Biju and Franky Bossuyt compared her DNA with that of other frogs. Her closest living relatives are tiny frogs who live only on the Seychelles islands, nearly 3,000 kilometres away across the sea. Frogs do not cross salt water easily. The two families share an ancestor who lived on the land when India and the Seychelles were one. People living in those hills knew the frog long before 2003. What the scientists added was her family tree. But India's journey was hard on its passengers. Around 66 million years ago huge lava flows covered much of the land, and the climate changed as India crossed the equator. Many of India's mammals and birds today, such as tigers, deer and hornbills, have their nearest relatives in South-East Asia.
- e.g. Caecilians are limbless amphibians who burrow in wet soil. Those of the Western Ghats have relatives in the Seychelles and in Africa.
- e.g. Tigers have their nearest relatives in the rest of Asia, so their ancestors probably walked into India after it joined Asia.
- **Franky Bossuyt of Belgium and S. D. Biju of India, who study frogs:** Some old families of frogs and caecilians rode on India from Gondwana. A few of them later spread out of India into the rest of Asia.
- **Praveen Karanth, a biologist at the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru:** Family trees built from DNA suggest that many of India's land animals with backbones arrived from Asia after the two lands met. The old Gondwana passengers are a smaller share.
- **Scientists who study fossils:** Fossils from the time when India was an island are rare. Until more are found, nobody can say how many kinds of passengers there were, or how many died on the way.
### Why kangaroos live only in and near Australia · *defined*
**Definition:** Kangaroos and their close relatives are native only to Australia, New Guinea and nearby islands. Their ancestors were there when deep sea cut that land off from all others.

Kangaroos are marsupials. A marsupial mother gives birth to a very small baby, who finishes growing in a pouch on her belly. Long ago marsupials lived across South America, Antarctica and Australia, which were joined as parts of Gondwana. Antarctica was not yet frozen. Then Australia broke away and moved north by itself, with marsupials on board. For tens of millions of years almost no other land mammals could reach it. Bats flew in, and some rats and mice arrived much later across the island chains. So the marsupials of Australia spread into many ways of living. Some graze like deer, some climb like monkeys, and some dig like moles. Kangaroos are missing from India because of this history. India's grasslands would probably feed them. Their ancestors simply never had a way here.
- e.g. Tree kangaroos live in the forests of New Guinea, which was joined to Australia by land when the sea was lower.
- e.g. Opossums are marsupials who still live in South and North America.
- e.g. Wallabies taken to New Zealand by people in the 1870s now live wild there. So relatives of kangaroos can live outside Australia once they are carried.
### Wallace's Line: shallow seas, deep seas and land bridges · *defined*
**Definition:** Wallace's Line is a boundary between islands with Asian animals and islands with Australian animals. It follows a channel of deep sea that never became dry land.

Alfred Russel Wallace collected animals on the islands of Indonesia for eight years. In 1859 he wrote about a puzzle. Bali and Lombok are islands only about 35 kilometres apart. Bali has woodpeckers, barbets and other birds of Asia. Lombok has cockatoos and honeyeaters, which are birds of Australia. The answer lies under the water. During the last ice age, so much water was frozen on land that the sea stood about 120 metres lower than today. Shallow sea floor became dry land. Sumatra, Java, Borneo and Bali were joined to Asia, and animals walked across. New Guinea was joined to Australia. But the channel between Bali and Lombok is much deeper than 120 metres. It stayed sea, so walking animals stopped there. India has an example too. The sea between India and Sri Lanka is very shallow. Until about 7,000 years ago, dry land joined them.
- e.g. Elephants, leopards and sloth bears live in both South India and Sri Lanka. Their ancestors could walk across.
- e.g. Tigers reached Bali on foot when it was joined to Asia. No tiger ever lived on Lombok.
- e.g. The line is sharper for mammals than for birds, bats and insects, because many of those can fly across.
### Groups that are kept apart become different · *defined*
**Definition:** When a barrier splits one kind of living being into two groups that can no longer have young together, the two groups slowly become different.

Every living being carries instructions, called DNA, in each cell. Parents copy their DNA for their young. The copying is very good, but a few small mistakes are made every time. These copying mistakes are called mutations. Most do nothing that anyone can see. In one large group, parents come from all parts of the group, so each change is spread around or lost. The group changes slowly, and it changes together. Now put a sea between two halves of the group. Each half goes on making its own copying mistakes, and they are not the same mistakes. Chance decides which ones are kept. Natural selection also works on each half, and the two places are not alike. Nothing mixes the two halves any more. After many thousands of generations they can be so different that they are two species. Scientists use this to measure time. They count the differences in DNA between two species. More differences mean a longer time apart.
- e.g. The purple frog and her Seychelles relatives have been apart since their lands separated, and they now look nothing alike.
- e.g. Lions in the Gir forest of Gujarat have been cut off from the lions of Africa for a very long time, and differ from them a little in body and in DNA.
- e.g. Leopards of India and Sri Lanka were one group until about 7,000 years ago. They still look almost the same.
### Islands: few arrive, and those few change · *defined*
**Definition:** An island has fewer kinds of living beings than the same area of mainland. The further the island is from the mainland, the fewer kinds reach it, and the more of them are endemic.

An island gets its living beings from somewhere else. Only those who can cross the water arrive. So far islands have many birds, bats, insects, geckos and plants with floating or flying seeds. They have few frogs and few large mammals. In 1967 Robert MacArthur and Edward Wilson put this into a theory. A near island gets more arrivals than a far one. A large island keeps more kinds alive than a small one. Wilson and Daniel Simberloff tested it on tiny mangrove islands off Florida. They removed all the insects and spiders, and watched them return. The islands nearest the shore ended with the most kinds, as the theory said. The few who do reach a far island are then isolated. Their descendants change for thousands of generations without mixing with the mainland. That is how an island comes to have endemic species. A hilltop, a lake and a cave are islands in this sense too. Each is a small patch cut off by land of a different kind.
- e.g. The Nicobar megapode lives only on the Nicobar Islands. The other megapodes live far away to the east.
- e.g. The Narcondam hornbill lives on one small island in the Andaman Sea and nowhere else.
- e.g. The cool, wet hilltops of the Western Ghats have frogs and birds found on only one group of hills.
### Native, introduced and invasive · *defined*
**Definition:** A native species reached a place without people. An introduced species was brought by people. An invasive species is an introduced one that spreads without help and harms those who were there before.

People are now the greatest carriers of living beings on Earth. A ship or an aeroplane crosses in a day a barrier that held for millions of years. Many introduced species are welcome. Chilli, potato, tomato, groundnut, papaya, cashew and tapioca all came to India from the Americas. They grow where people plant them and do not spread through forests. A smaller number get away from farms and gardens and live wild. Only a few of those become invasive. But those few can change a whole forest or lake. The words depend on the place. The common myna is native to India. People took mynas to Australia in the 1860s to eat insects in vegetable gardens. There the mynas drive native birds out of their nest holes. The chital, India's spotted deer, is native to the mainland. People also took chital to the Andaman Islands, where they eat so many seedlings that the forest is changing.
- e.g. Native to India: neem, banyan, peafowl, tiger, black pepper.
- e.g. Introduced to India and not invasive: chilli, potato, tapioca, cashew, marigold.
- e.g. Invasive in India: lantana, water hyacinth, parthenium, the giant African snail.
### Four invasive species of India · *defined*
**Definition:** Lantana, water hyacinth, parthenium and the giant African snail were all brought to India by people in the last 200 years. All four have spread widely.

Lantana is a shrub from tropical America with small pink, orange and yellow flowers. The British brought lantana to India as a garden plant about 200 years ago. Birds eat the berries and drop the seeds. A study in 2020 found lantana in more than 40 of every 100 parts of India's tiger forests. The leaves are poisonous to cattle, and grass cannot grow in lantana's shade. Water hyacinth is a floating plant from South America with pale purple flowers. People brought water hyacinth to Bengal for the flowers. A mat of these plants can double in size in one to two weeks, and the mat blocks light and boats. Parthenium is a weed from tropical America, also called congress grass. Parthenium seeds probably came mixed in with imported grain. The pollen and leaves give many people skin rashes and breathing trouble. The giant African snail is native to East Africa and was brought to India in 1847. An adult can be longer than your hand, and these snails eat a very wide range of plants.
- e.g. Salvinia, a floating fern from Brazil, once choked the canals and rice fields of Kuttanad in Kerala.
- e.g. A thorny tree from the Americas was planted for firewood in dry parts of India from 1877. In Tamil Nadu these trees are called seemai karuvelam, and they now cover large areas of dry land.
### Why do some newcomers take over? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists have several explanations for why a few introduced species spread so fast. Each explanation fits some cases, and none fits all.

Lantana is an ordinary shrub in tropical America, growing there among many other plants. In India the same plant covers whole hillsides. The plant is the same, so the difference must lie in the new place. Scientists have tested four main ideas. They are given below. It is likely that several work together, and that the mix is different for each invader. This matters for what we do. If missing enemies are the cause, bringing one enemy may help. If broken ground is the cause, the answer is to stop breaking the ground.
- e.g. A study of nearly 500 plants introduced to the United States found far fewer kinds of fungus attacking them there than in their home countries.
- e.g. Another study compared 72 pairs of native and invasive plants. The invaders were eaten just as much as the natives.
- e.g. Lantana spreads fastest in forests that have been cut, burned or heavily grazed.
- **Ryan Keane and Michael Crawley, ecologists (2002): the missing enemies idea:** At home, insects and diseases that feed on a plant keep the numbers down. The plant travels without them. In the new land nobody eats the newcomer, so they can put all their growth into spreading.
- **Ragan Callaway and Wendy Ridenour, American ecologists (2004): the new chemicals idea:** Some plants release chemicals from their roots or leaves. Their old neighbours are used to these chemicals. The plants of the new land are not, and they grow badly beside the newcomer.
- **Many ecologists who study disturbed land:** People clear, burn and graze the land, and that opens bare ground. Fast-growing newcomers fill bare ground first. On this view the invader is a sign of the damage as much as a cause of the damage.
- **Julie Lockwood and other ecologists (2005): the numbers idea:** A species brought many times, in large numbers and to many places has many chances to settle. Garden plants are brought again and again, which is why so many invaders began as garden plants.
### Biological control: bringing the missing enemy · *defined*
**Definition:** Biological control means finding a natural enemy of an invasive species in the invader's homeland, testing that enemy carefully, and releasing them.

Pulling out an invasive plant by hand works on a small patch. It costs a great deal over a whole district, and the plant grows back from what is left. Poison sprays kill other plants and animals too. Biological control tries a third way. Scientists go to the invader's homeland and look for an insect or a fungus who feeds on that plant and on nothing else. Then comes the most important step. For months or years they offer the insect crops and native plants in closed rooms. Only an insect who starves beside every other plant is released. Kerala has a famous success. A tiny weevil from Brazil was released there in the early 1980s against salvinia, the floating fern. Within a few years the canals of Kuttanad were open again. There are famous failures too. In 1935 people took 102 cane toads to Australia to eat beetles in sugarcane fields. Nobody had tested them properly. The toads did not control the beetles. They multiplied into many millions, and their poison kills native animals who try to eat them.
- e.g. A beetle from Mexico was released in India in 1984 to eat parthenium. The beetles help in some places, though the weed remains common.
- e.g. In 1872 nine small Indian mongooses were taken to Jamaica to hunt rats in sugarcane fields. They also ate native birds and lizards.
### Should every newcomer be removed? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists and local people disagree about how much effort should go into removing introduced species, and which ones.

Nobody argues for keeping lantana in a tiger forest if the shrubs could be removed easily. The argument is about harder cases. Removing an invader from a large area costs a great deal and often fails. Some newcomers have been in a place for centuries, and other living beings now depend on them. Birds nest in lantana and eat the berries. In the dry districts of Tamil Nadu, many poor families earn money by making charcoal from seemai karuvelam. Courts have ordered these trees to be removed, because they use scarce water. In the hills of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, people of the Soliga and other forest communities build furniture and life-size elephants from lantana stems. On small islands the case is clearer. Rats, cats and goats carried by ships have wiped out many island birds who had never met a hunting mammal.
- e.g. Lantana has been in India for about 200 years. Chilli has been here for about 500. Only one of them harms forests.
- e.g. On many islands, removing introduced rats has let seabirds come back to nest.
- **Mark Davis and 18 other ecologists, in the journal Nature (2011):** Judge a species by what they do, and not by where they came from. Most introduced species do little harm, and some help. Spend money only on the ones that cause real damage.
- **Daniel Simberloff and 140 other scientists, replying in Nature (2011):** Origin does matter. A newcomer may look harmless for decades and then spread suddenly. By then removal is too late. It is cheaper and safer to stop a newcomer early.
- **A family in a dry district of Tamil Nadu who make charcoal from seemai karuvelam:** These trees grow where nothing else will, and they are our income in a year without rain. If they are removed, something must replace what they give us.
### From atoms deep in the Earth to frogs on islands: is the Earth unusual? · *idea*
**Definition:** The Earth is the only planet known to have moving plates. Some scientists think moving plates are needed for a planet to have many kinds of life. This is an idea, and it cannot yet be tested.

Follow the chain from the smallest thing to the largest. Deep in the Earth, atoms of uranium and thorium break down and give out heat. The heat keeps the mantle slowly moving. The moving mantle helps to shift the plates. A plate carried India away from Madagascar. The frogs on the two lands could no longer meet, and over millions of years they became different families. So one reason there are so many kinds of frogs is the heat of atoms far underground. Mars and Venus are rocky planets like the Earth. As far as anyone can tell, their outer shells are single pieces that do not move as plates. Is that connected with the fact that we have found no life there? Nobody knows. We have only one planet with life to study.
- e.g. Moving plates build mountains, and mountains make rain shadows, cold peaks and isolated valleys. Each is a new kind of place to live.
- e.g. Volcanoes at plate edges return carbon dioxide to the air. Over millions of years this has helped to keep the Earth from freezing over.
- **Peter Ward and Donald Brownlee, American scientists, in the book Rare Earth (2000):** Moving plates keep a planet's climate steady and keep making new, separate places. Without them, animal life might never have become so varied. Such planets may be rare.
- **Other planetary scientists:** We know of one living planet. A planet without moving plates might support life in ways we have not imagined. One example cannot tell us what is needed.

## Mostly agreed
- The continents move a few centimetres a year, and they have been joined and separated in the past. *How we know:* Satellites measure the movement directly today. Matching rocks and fossils on separate continents, and the pattern of young rock along the middle of the oceans, show the past movement.
- India was once joined to Madagascar, Antarctica and Australia, and joined Asia later. *How we know:* The same fossil plants and land animals are found in the rocks of all these lands. The sea floor of the Indian Ocean records India's path north.
- A kind of living being can be absent from a place that suits them, only because they could not reach it. *How we know:* Camels in Australia, wallabies in New Zealand and lantana in India all live well in lands their ancestors never reached. People carried them.
- Groups of one species that are kept apart for long enough become different species. *How we know:* Islands and other isolated places have many endemic species whose nearest relatives live on the nearest mainland. DNA shows how long each pair has been apart.
- Far islands have fewer kinds of living beings than near islands of the same size. *How we know:* Counts on islands all over the world show it. Edward Wilson and Daniel Simberloff also tested it by clearing tiny mangrove islands and watching insects return.
- Invasive species are one of the main causes of extinction, most of all on islands. *How we know:* Records of extinct island birds show that many disappeared soon after rats, cats or mongooses arrived with people.

## How we found out
**Alfred Wegener, who proposed that continents move. Marie Tharp, who mapped the ocean floor and found the rift valley, with Bruce Heezen, who collected the depth readings at sea. Philip Sclater, who proposed the sunken land of Lemuria., 1864 (Sclater's Lemuria), 1912 (Wegener's lecture), 1952 (Tharp finds the rift valley), 1957 (her first map is published) and the 1960s (moving plates accepted).** Fossils of the same land animals lie in India, Africa and Antarctica. For a long time geologists explained this with land bridges: strips of land that once joined the continents and later sank under the sea. In 1864 Philip Sclater even proposed a whole sunken land, which he named Lemuria. In 1912 Alfred Wegener, a German weather scientist, said the continents themselves had been joined and had moved apart. Most geologists rejected this, because he could not say what could move a continent. He died in Greenland in 1930. In 1952 Marie Tharp was drawing the floor of the Atlantic from ships' depth readings. Women were not then allowed on the research ships. She found a deep valley running along an undersea mountain range, as if the sea floor were splitting there. Her colleague Bruce Heezen called it 'girl talk'. Then a map of undersea earthquakes was laid over her drawing, and the earthquakes followed her valley. In the 1960s other measurements showed new sea floor forming along it. No sunken bridges were ever found.  
*What people believed before:* That the continents have always been where they are, and that land bridges once joined them and then sank. Maps of the sea floor found no sunken bridges. They found a crack where new sea floor forms and pushes the continents apart.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Each animal lives in the place that suits them best.** (A common way of speaking, and many school books; *rejected*)  
  For: Animals do fit the places where they live. A camel's body suits a desert and a fish's body suits water.  
  Against: Many places would suit a species who is not there. Camels were absent from Australia's deserts until people brought them, and now live wild there in very large numbers. Fitting a place and being able to reach it are separate things.
- **The continents have always been where they are, and sunken land bridges once joined them.** (Most geologists before the 1960s; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody could feel the ground move, and nobody knew a force that could shift a continent. A sunken bridge explained why the same fossils lie on both sides of an ocean.  
  Against: Maps of the sea floor found no sunken bridges. They found a long crack where new sea floor forms. Satellites now measure the continents moving.
- **Invasive species succeed because they are stronger than native species.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Lantana and water hyacinth do push out native plants wherever they spread.  
  Against: At home in the Americas, lantana and water hyacinth are ordinary plants that take over nothing. They are the same plants in India. What differs is the place: fewer enemies, more disturbed ground, or neighbours who are not used to them.
- **A natural enemy brought from abroad is always safer than poison sprays.** (Some early supporters of biological control; *debated*)  
  For: A well-tested enemy attacks only the invader and goes on working for years at no cost. The salvinia weevil in Kerala did exactly that.  
  Against: A badly tested enemy becomes a new invader. Cane toads in Australia and mongooses in Jamaica harmed native animals and did not solve the first problem.
- **Most of India's animals have been in India since Gondwana.** (A popular idea about India as a raft that carried animals from Gondwana; *debated*)  
  For: Some old families, such as the purple frog and the caecilians, do have their relatives on other pieces of Gondwana.  
  Against: DNA family trees show that many of India's mammals, birds and reptiles have their nearest relatives in the rest of Asia. Their ancestors probably arrived after India joined Asia.

## Roots
In this unit you copied lines, fitted continents together and met plants that crossed an ocean with people. Here we ask what it means that living beings become different when they are kept apart.

**Deep truth.** In the Separated Lines Test, four families of lines began as copies of one 100 millimetre line. You copied every line as carefully as you could. The two families kept apart still ended at different lengths, and the two that mixed stayed close. So living beings who are kept apart become different, and those who keep mixing stay alike. A sea between them is enough, given a very long time.  
*Habit it breaks:* When the animals of an island differ from those of the mainland, we ask what the island did to them. The test points another way. The separated copies became different with nothing done to them. They were only kept apart. So the first thing to look for is the barrier.  
*Evidence:* In the Separated Lines Test the copier, the pencil and the care were the same for all four families, and only the mixing differed. Leopards in India and Sri Lanka have been apart for about 7,000 years and still look alike. The purple frog and her Seychelles relatives have been apart for about 65 million years and look nothing alike.

- *who am i:* You belong to one human species because people have never stopped mixing. Humans spread to every continent, but travellers kept meeting and having children together.
- *what is life:* Life copies itself, and no copy is perfect. So wherever a barrier holds two groups apart, life becomes two kinds.
- *what is this world:* The ground is not fixed. The land you live on has crossed an ocean, and it still moves about 5 centimetres every year.

All living beings on Earth share one ancestor. From that one beginning came millions of kinds. This unit shows one way it happened. Moving plates, rising seas and rare journeys kept splitting groups apart, and each separated group went on changing by itself. So the many kinds of life are one family that has been divided again and again. When people carry a plant across an ocean, they undo in a few weeks a separation that lasted millions of years.

- **Purananuru 192, by Kaniyan Pungundranar (Tamil, about 2,000 years old):** “யாதும் ஊரே; யாவரும் கேளிர்” The poet says that every town is his town and every person is his kin. He says life makes its way like a raft on a great river in flood, carried where the water takes it. (The poet was speaking of people. It meets this unit's science in two plain ways. Many living beings are where they are because a current, a wind or a moving land carried their ancestors there. And all living beings are one family, so each one is kin to the rest.)
- **Dhammapada 277:** “Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā, / yadā paññāya passati; / Atha nibbindati dukkhe, / esa maggo visuddhiyā.” The Buddha says that all things that are put together pass away, and that seeing this clearly leads to peace. (A continent seems the most lasting thing there is. Yet Gondwana broke into pieces, and the sea between India and Sri Lanka was dry land 7,000 years ago. Here the science agrees with the verse: lands, seas and kinds of living beings all change.)
- Science (settled): All living beings on Earth share one ancestor. Every one of them uses the same DNA code.
- Science (settled): The Earth's plates are moving now. Satellites measure the movement, which is a few centimetres a year.
- Science (idea): Some scientists think a planet needs moving plates to have many kinds of life. With only one living planet to study, nobody can test this yet.
- Other view, Daniel Simberloff, an American ecologist, and many conservation scientists: Every species is descended from travellers, and that is true. But a species that has lived in a place for a million years has neighbours who eat them and limits that hold them in check. A species carried there last year has none. So 'native' is a real and useful word. Treating every arrival alike would excuse great harm, most of all on islands.
- Practice, One minute on moving ground (3 min): Sit on the floor and put both palms flat on it. Close your eyes. The floor feels still. But this land is moving north-east, about as fast as your thumbnail grows. Breathe in and out slowly, five times. With each breath, think of the land sliding a tiny way, with you and your home on it. Now open your eyes. Who else is riding on this piece of land with you?
- Ask: Tigers walked into India long ago, and lantana was carried here 200 years ago. Is there a real difference between arriving and being brought?
- Ask: Languages also drift apart when two groups of people stop talking to each other. Is that the same thing that happened to your lines?
- Ask: The land under you has moved thousands of kilometres. So where is 'here'?

## Story: The Leaf With No Holes
*Ammu, Unni, their father, the tea shop man and the visiting scientist are made up. So are Ammu's leaf count and the ducks who would not eat the fern. These facts are real. Kuttanad in Kerala is a region of canals and rice fields that lie lower than the water around them. A floating fern called salvinia, known in Kerala as African payal, was first recorded in the state in the 1950s. By the 1960s these ferns covered canals and rice fields. The fern really comes from south-eastern Brazil. A weevil from Brazil who feeds only on salvinia was brought to India in 1982 or 1983 and released in Kerala. In 1985 P. J. Joy and three colleagues reported that the weevils had controlled the fern in Kuttanad. Such insects are tested on crops in closed cages before they are released.*

In Kuttanad the water is higher than the fields. Mud walls hold the canals up, and the rice grows below them, lower than the fish. Ammu was ten. She had crossed the canal to school in the family's small boat every day since she was six.

That year the crossing took twice as long. A green mat lay over the water from bank to bank. People called the plants African payal. Each one was no bigger than her palm, a little raft of soft, furry leaves folded like the pages of a book. But there were so many that the pole stuck fast in them. Her brother Unni had to lean over the front and push them aside with a coconut frond.

Every Sunday the men of the ward stood in the canal up to their chests and dragged the payal out with rakes. They piled the plants on the bank in heaps as high as the tea shop. By the next Sunday the water was green again.

"They are stronger than we are," said the tea shop man. Everyone agreed.

Ammu did not know whether the payal were strong. She only thought they were strange, and she found out why because of the ducks.

Her job after school was to walk the family's ducks down to the water. Ducks will eat almost anything green. They swam through the payal as if through thick porridge, and ate none.

One evening, while she waited for them, she picked a water lily leaf to fan herself. The leaf had three holes in it, and a thin brown track where something small had tunnelled. She looked at the taro on the bank. The edges of the leaves were chewed. A caterpillar had rolled up inside a banana leaf. As for the rice, her father complained every single year about who was eating the crop.

Then she scooped up a handful of payal and turned the plants over, one after another. Not a hole. Not a bite. She tried twenty. Unni counted fifty more out of the boat. Every leaf was whole.

"Everyone here gets eaten by somebody," she told Unni. "Except these."

"Maybe they taste bad," said Unni.

"Karela tastes bad. Somebody still eats Amma's karela plants."

Unni had no answer, and neither had she. It bothered her for a week.

In the week before Onam, a jeep came along the bund road, which did not happen often. A woman in a cotton sari and rubber boots got out. She carried a glass jar with a cloth tied over the top. She was from the agricultural university at Thrissur, and the ward member had called everyone to the tea shop to hear her. Ammu was sent to fetch her father, and she stayed at the back.

The jar went from hand to hand. Inside were a few wet payal and a scattering of black specks, each one smaller than a grain of rice. The specks had legs.

"Weevils," said the woman. "A kind of beetle. We would like to put them in your canal."

The tea shop man laughed so much that he had to sit down. "Madam, we have pulled out forty lorry-loads. You have brought us a spoonful of insects."

"Do they eat the payal?"

The words were out before Ammu could stop them. People turned round.

"Nobody eats the payal," she said, more quietly. "I looked. The lily has holes, and the taro has holes. The payal have none. Not one."

The woman looked at her for a moment. Then she smiled, as if Ammu had handed her something. "Come to the front and say that again."

Ammu said it again.

"She is right, and that is the whole trouble," the woman said. "You call these plants African, but they come from Brazil, on the other side of the world. In Brazil they grow in quiet corners of rivers, a patch here and a patch there. Nobody there rakes them out. These weevils do the work. The grown ones eat the buds, and their young bore into the stems. When the payal were brought to our country, the weevils were left behind. For thirty years the payal have lived here with nobody to eat them."

"So they are not strong," Ammu said slowly. "They only came without their eaters."

"Yes. So we went back and fetched the eaters."

Ammu's father asked what every farmer there wanted to ask. "And when they have finished the payal? Will they start on my rice?"

"That was tested before anything else," said the woman. "For months these weevils were kept in closed cages with rice, with banana, with taro and with coconut seedlings, and no payal. They starved beside the rice. They eat this one plant. When the payal go, most of the weevils go too."

They tipped the jar out beside the boat steps. The specks crawled down among the furry leaves and were gone.

For a month nothing changed. For two months nothing changed, and the tea shop man said so every day. The Sunday raking went on. Each evening, while the ducks fed, Ammu turned the payal over and found whole leaves, whole leaves, whole leaves.

It was Unni who found the first one, in the hot weeks before the rains. The bud in the middle had gone brown, and in the stem was a hole as fine as a pinprick. After that they found them everywhere near the steps. The brown plants filled with water and grew heavy. One by one they sank.

By the next Onam a strip of open water ran down the middle of the canal, wide enough for two boats. It stayed open without a single rake.

Some payal still floated along the edges. Ammu lifted one out as the boat went by, and looked underneath. There was a small, neat hole.

She showed Unni. "Now somebody eats them," she said. "Like everybody else."

She set the plant back on the water, and Unni pushed off for school.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the ferns were not spreading because they were stronger plants. They were spreading because the insects who eat them had been left behind in Brazil. Ammu's clue was that every local plant had bite marks and the newcomer had none. This is one of the main explanations for invasive species. It is also why scientists sometimes bring a carefully tested natural enemy from the invader's homeland.

- Ask: Raking out forty lorry-loads did not clear the canal. Why not?
- Ask: What did the holes in the lily and taro leaves tell Ammu?
- Ask: The tea shop man said the payal were strong. What was a better explanation?
- Ask: Why were the weevils kept in cages with rice and banana before anyone released them?
- Ask: At the end, some payal still floated at the edges, with holes in them. Is that a failure? Why?

### e1 · The Separated Lines Test: do two families of copies stay alike if they never mix? (37 min)
- **question:** Two families of lines both start from the same 100 millimetre line. Each new line is copied from the line before. If the two families never see each other, will they stay the same length? What if they are mixed at every step?
- **predict:** Guess before you draw. After ten copies, how many millimetres apart will the two separated families be? How far apart will the two mixed families be? Why do you think so? Write both guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** All four families start from a line exactly 100 millimetres long. The same person makes every copy, with the same pencil, after looking for the same 3 seconds. Nobody uses a ruler while copying, and nobody looks back at older lines. Every family gets ten copies. Only one thing differs. Families A and B never see each other. Families C and D are copied from both at once. One run can be a fluke, so the last step tells you how to check your result.
- **record:** Draw a table with seven columns and eleven rows, for generations 0 to 10. Write the length of every line in millimetres, and the two gaps for each generation. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Generation (0 to 10) | Family A (millimetres) | Family B (millimetres) | Gap between A and B | Family C (millimetres) | Family D (millimetres) | Gap between C and D
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label four sheets A, B, C and D. 2. With the ruler, draw a line exactly 100 millimetres long near the top of each sheet. 3. Write 0 beside each line. 4. Draw the results table on a notebook page.  
  *Parent says:* Each sheet is one family. The ruled line is the first parent. Every new line will be a child, copied by eye from the line just before.  
  *Child mode:* Label four sheets A, B, C and D. Rule a 100 millimetre line on each. Write 0 beside it.
  - Note: Hold the sheets the tall way, so that eleven lines fit one under another. Leave about 2 centimetres between lines.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the two rules: A and B are copied apart, and C and D are copied together. 2. Ask for both guesses and the reasons. 3. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* After ten copies, how far apart will families A and B be? How far apart will C and D be? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the gap between A and B after ten copies. Guess the gap between C and D. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: I copy carefully, so they will stay the same.; Small mistakes might add up.; C and D keep seeing each other, so they will stay close.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children expect all four families to stay at 100 millimetres, because they mean to copy carefully.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take sheet A alone. 2. Look at the newest line for 3 seconds. 3. Cover all the lines with the newspaper. 4. Draw a line of the same length just below, with no ruler. 5. Number it. 6. Do this ten times. 7. Turn sheet A face down and do the same on sheet B.  
  *Parent says:* Copy only the newest line each time. Look for three seconds, cover it, then draw. Do not peep at the older lines.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the newest line for 3 seconds. Cover it. Draw the same length below. Do this ten times. Then do sheet B.
  - Note: Count 'one and two and three' aloud for the look. Before each new look, slide the newspaper so that only the newest line shows. Sheet A must be out of sight while sheet B is done.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put sheets C and D side by side. 2. Uncover the newest line on both and look for 3 seconds. 3. Cover both. 4. Draw one new line on C and one on D. 5. Make each about halfway in length between the two lines you saw. 6. Do this ten times.  
  *Parent says:* This time the two families mix. Each child line has two parents, one from each sheet. Draw a length halfway between the two.  
  *Child mode:* Put C and D side by side. Look at both newest lines. Cover them. Draw a halfway line on each. Do this ten times.
  - Note: 'Halfway' is judged by eye. If the two lines look the same, your child simply copies that length. One look serves for both new lines.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure every line with the ruler, to the nearest millimetre. 2. Write the lengths in the table. 3. For each generation, subtract to find the gap between A and B. 4. Do the same for C and D.  
  *Parent says:* Now we use the ruler. Measure each line and write it down. Then find the gap for each pair in every row.  
  *Child mode:* Measure every line in millimetres. Write the lengths. Find the gap between A and B. Find the gap between C and D.
  - Note: The gap is the longer length minus the shorter one, so it is never a minus number.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the last row: which pair ended further apart? 2. Count the rows in which the A and B gap is the bigger one. 3. Ask your child what they thought, what they saw and why it happened. 4. Then read the note below together.  
  *Parent says:* Which pair ended further apart? In how many rows was the A and B gap bigger? Say what you thought, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the last row. Tell your grown-up what you saw and why you think it happened.
  - Ask: You tried to copy every line exactly. So where did the difference between A and B come from? (good: Each copy had a small mistake.; The next copy was made from the mistaken line, so mistakes were passed on.; A and B made different mistakes and nothing brought them back together.)
  - Ask: Why did C and D stay close to each other? (good: Each new line was made from both families.; A mistake in one family was shared with the other at the next step.)
  - Note: In most runs A and B end 10 to 40 millimetres apart, and C and D end less than 10 millimetres apart. All four families may also shrink or grow together, because most people copy a little short or a little long. That is fine: the test is about the gap inside each pair. About one run in five, A and B end close by chance, the way two people taking random steps can end up side by side. Then look at how many rows had the bigger gap between A and B, or run a third separated family on a new sheet. The reason: every copy has a small mistake, and the next copy is made from the mistaken line. In a separated family the mistakes pile up, and no other family corrects them. This is a model of living beings. Parents copy their DNA for their young, and each copy has a few small mistakes. In one large group, young have parents from all over the group, so changes are shared. A barrier such as a sea stops the sharing. The model leaves two things out. In real life many copies are made at once and chance decides which are kept. Natural selection also pushes each group to suit its own place.
  - Check: What kept families C and D close to each other? → **They were mixed at every step**
### e2 · The Far Island Test: do fewer travellers reach a far island than a near one? (30 min)
- **question:** Grains scatter from one spot in all directions. Three bowls of the same size stand at one, two and three steps of distance. How many grains land in each? Does a bowl twice as far away catch half as many? Does a wide plate catch more than a small bowl at the same distance?
- **predict:** Guess before you pour. If the near bowl catches 30 grains, how many will the bowl twice as far away catch: 30, 15, or fewer than 15? Will a wide plate catch more than a small bowl at the same distance? Why do you think so? Write your guesses and reasons.
- **fair test:** Use the same spoon of rice, poured from the same height onto the same spot, for every pour. The three island bowls are the same size, and each has a cloth inside so that grains do not bounce out. Only the distance changes. In the second part, only the size of the island changes. Pour three times and add the counts, because grains bounce differently each time.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three distances. Add two more rows for the small bowl and the wide plate at the same distance. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Island | Distance from the middle bowl (centimetres) | Grains caught: pour 1, pour 2, pour 3 | Total of three pours
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the bedsheet on the floor. 2. Turn one katori upside down in the middle. 3. Line three katoris with cloth. 4. Pour one trial spoon of rice onto the upturned katori from waist height. 5. See how far most grains go.  
  *Parent says:* The upturned bowl is the mainland. The grains are seeds and tiny animals swept out to sea. Let's see how far they scatter.  
  *Child mode:* Spread the sheet. Turn one bowl upside down. Pour one spoon of rice on top. See how far the grains go.
  - Note: Grains usually land within 30 centimetres on a sheet. Put the near bowl where plenty of grains fell, often about 10 centimetres from the edge of the upturned bowl. Put the other two at twice and three times that distance, each in a different direction. Pick up the trial rice first.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the three island bowls. 2. Ask how many grains the further bowls will catch, if the near bowl catches 30. 3. Ask whether a wide plate will catch more. 4. Write the guesses and reasons.  
  *Parent says:* If the near island catches 30 grains, how many will the one twice as far catch? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many grains each bowl will catch. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Fewer grains bounce that far.; Twice as far might mean half as many.; Far away, the grains are spread over more floor.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children guess half as many at twice the distance.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour one level tablespoon of rice slowly onto the top of the upturned katori. 2. Count the grains in each island bowl and write them down. 3. Empty the bowls. 4. Do this three times. 5. Add up each bowl's three counts.  
  *Parent says:* Pour from the same height each time. Then count each island's grains. We pour three times, because the grains bounce differently each time.  
  *Child mode:* Pour one spoon of rice on the bowl. Count the grains on each island. Do it three times. Add them up.
  - Note: If the furthest bowl catches nothing in three pours, that is a real result. Write 0. If even the near bowl catches fewer than 5, move all three bowls closer and start again.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take away the near and furthest bowls. 2. Put the cloth-lined plate at the middle distance, in a new direction. 3. Pour three spoons, one at a time. 4. Count the grains on the plate and in the middle bowl.  
  *Parent says:* Now a small island and a large island are at the same distance. Which will catch more travellers? Let's count.  
  *Child mode:* Put the plate at the middle distance. Pour three spoons. Count the grains on the plate and in the bowl.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the totals together. 2. Ask your child what they thought, what they saw and why it happened. 3. Read the note below together. 4. Wash the rice and cook it.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Was twice as far half as many?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your totals. Say what you saw and why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the far bowl catch far fewer than half as many grains? (good: Fewer grains travel that far.; The grains that do go far are spread round a much bigger circle.; The bowl covers a smaller share of that circle.)
  - Ask: What does this say about an island far out at sea? (good: Very few living beings reach it.; It will have fewer kinds than a near island.)
  - Note: In most homes the near bowl catches several times as many grains as the middle bowl, and the furthest catches almost none. Two things work together. Few grains bounce far. And those that do are spread round a wider ring of floor, so one bowl covers a smaller share of the ring. The wide plate catches several times as many as the small bowl beside it, because it covers more floor. Real islands follow the same two rules. In 1967 Robert MacArthur and Edward Wilson wrote that near islands and large islands get more arrivals. The model leaves out what happens after landing. On a real island a traveller must also find food and a mate.
  - Check: Two islands are the same size. One is near the mainland and one is far. Which is likely to have more kinds of living beings? → **The near island**

## Challenges
### c1 · Clear the Tank: beat a water weed that doubles (25 min)
- **problem:** A village tank is drawn as a grid of 64 squares. A water weed covers 1 square. Each round stands for two weeks, and in each round the weed doubles. After the doubling, your team may clear some squares. Find a plan that clears the whole tank, and uses as little work as possible.
- **your turn:** Now make your own plan. You have 30 squares of clearing work in total. The weed already covers 4 squares when you first notice. In which rounds will you spend your work? Play it with the beans. Did you clear the tank? What would you tell a village that has just found its first water hyacinth?
- **constraints:** In every round the weed doubles first. Then you clear.; In the first two parts your team can clear at most 3 squares in a round.; If the weed covers all 64 squares, the tank is lost.; In your own plan you have 30 squares of clearing work in total, to spend in any rounds you choose.
- **general:** Clear 3 squares in every round, starting in round 1.; Wait until the weed is easy to see, then clear as hard as you can.; Clear only the squares near the bank, where people fetch water.
- **creative:** Pay one person to walk round the tank every week, so that the first plant is seen at once.; Stretch a floating rope across the tank, so that the weed is held in one corner while you clear.; Release a tested natural enemy that slows the doubling, and clear by hand as well.
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the grid of 64 squares. 2. Put 1 bean on the grid. 3. Ask how many rounds it will take to fill the tank if nobody clears, and why. 4. Double the beans each round and write the count.  
  *Parent says:* The weed doubles every round. How many rounds until the tank is full? Why do you think so? Let's play it with no clearing.  
  *Child mode:* Draw 64 squares. Put on 1 bean. Guess how many rounds fill the tank. Double the beans each round.
  - Ask: The tank is full after round 6. In which round was it half full? (good: Round 5, only one round earlier.; A half-full tank is one doubling away from full.)
  - Note: The counts are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64. The tank is full after 6 rounds, which stands for 12 weeks. Many children guess 30 rounds or more.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start again with 1 bean. 2. Each round, double the beans and then remove 3. 3. Play again, but begin clearing only when there are 2 beans. 4. Play again from 3 beans, and then from 4 beans.  
  *Parent says:* Your team clears 3 squares a round. Does it matter when you start? Try starting at 1, 2, 3 and 4 beans.  
  *Child mode:* Double the beans, then take away 3. Try starting at 1, 2, 3 and 4 beans. What happens each time?
  - Ask: Why can 3 squares of clearing never beat 4 squares of weed? (good: 4 doubles to 8, and 8 take away 3 is 5, which is more than before.; The weed adds more each round than the team can remove.)
  - Note: From 1 bean the tank is clear in one round. From 2 beans it takes two rounds. From 3 beans the count stays at 3 for ever. From 4 beans the weed wins. The rule: you win only if you can clear more than is there now.
  - Check: A team clears 3 squares each round, after the weed doubles. From which start can they clear the tank? → **From 2 squares of weed**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the task under 'Your turn' together. 2. Your child plans how to spend 30 squares of work, starting from 4 beans. 3. Play the plan with the beans. 4. Compare an early plan with a late plan.  
  *Parent says:* You have 30 squares of work, and the weed covers 4 squares. When will you spend the work? Why? Play it and see.  
  *Child mode:* Plan when to clear. Play your plan with the beans.
  - Note: Spending 8 at once wins: 4 doubles to 8, and 8 are cleared. Waiting two rounds means 16 double to 32, which is more than 30. The same work that wins early loses late. Real water hyacinth mats can double in one to two weeks.

9. (truefalse) True or false: Sri Lanka was once joined to India by dry land.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The sea between them is very shallow. When the sea was lower, it was dry land, until about 7,000 years ago.
10. (write) In the Separated Lines Test, why did the two mixed families stay close to each other?

Careful copying is not the reason, because the copying was the same for all four.

### Barriers, bridges and newcomers (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Camels brought by people now live wild in Australia. What does that show about Australia before?  
   Options: Camels had died out there / It had no deserts / It already suited camels / It was too cold for camels  
   **Answer:** It already suited camels. *Why:* The land did not change when camels arrived. So it suited camels all along, and they were missing only because they could not get there.
2. (write) A warm sea stops a penguin but not a sea turtle. Why does a barrier depend on who meets it?  
   **Answer:** A barrier is something a living being cannot cross. Penguins need cold water, so warm sea stops them. A sea turtle lives in warm sea, so for her it is a road.. *Why:* A strong answer says that each living being has their own needs and ways of moving, so the same sea is a wall for one and a path for another.
3. (match) Match each piece of evidence to what it shows.  
   **Answer:** The same fossils on two continents → The lands were once joined; Many DNA differences between two species → They have been apart a long time; Satellite measurements of the ground → The plates are moving today; Young rock along the middle of an ocean → New sea floor is forming there; Whole leaves on a newcomer plant → The insects that eat them may be missing. *Why:* Each kind of evidence answers a different question: where the land was, how long ago a split happened, or what is happening now.
4. (truefalse) True or false: some of the heat that moves the plates comes from atoms breaking down deep underground.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. About half of the Earth's inner heat comes from atoms of uranium, thorium and potassium breaking down. The rest is left from when the Earth formed.
5. (mcq) Bali and Lombok are 35 kilometres apart, yet have very different animals. Why?

7. (write) The purple frog of Kerala has close relatives only on the Seychelles islands. Explain how that happened.  
   **Answer:** The land of India and the Seychelles was once joined. The frogs' ancestors lived on it. The land split about 65 million years ago, and the two groups of frogs changed separately ever since.. *Why:* A strong answer has the land moving, and the frogs staying on it. Frogs cannot easily cross salt water, so swimming is not a good explanation.
8. (fill) Small mistakes made when DNA is copied are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['mutations', 'mutation']. *Why:* A mutation is a small copying mistake in DNA. It is passed on to the young, and is one reason separated groups become different.
9. (sort) Sort these travellers. Are they likely or unlikely to reach a far island without people?  
   **Answer:** Bat → Likely to arrive; Coconut → Likely to arrive; Fern spore → Likely to arrive; Frog → Unlikely to arrive; Elephant → Unlikely to arrive; River fish → Unlikely to arrive. *Why:* Fliers, floaters and wind-blown spores cross the sea. Salt water stops frogs and river fish, and an elephant cannot swim that far.
10. (mcq) Why do far islands have many endemic species?  
   Options: Islands are colder than mainlands / Few arrive, and they stay isolated / Island animals live longer / Islands have richer soil  
   **Answer:** Few arrive, and they stay isolated. *Why:* Few living beings reach a far island, and their descendants do not mix with the mainland. Over a long time they become kinds found nowhere else.
11. (perspective) A dry village in Tamil Nadu is covered with seemai karuvelam, an invasive thorn tree.

A good reply to another view says what that view gets right.. *Why:* A strong answer uses a fact, such as the harm to native plants or the family's income, and treats the other view fairly.
12. (truefalse) True or false: every species that people bring to a new country becomes invasive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most introduced species, such as chilli and potato, grow only where people plant them. Only a few spread alone and do harm.

   **Answer:** ["Find the invader's homeland", 'Find an insect there who eats the invader', 'Test the insect on crops and native plants in closed cages', 'Release the insect only if they eat nothing else', 'Watch for years to see what happens']. *Why:* The testing comes before the release, because a released insect cannot be called back.
14. (write) Cane toads were released in Australia to eat beetles in sugarcane. What went wrong, and what test was missing?  
   **Answer:** The toads did not control the beetles. They multiplied into many millions, and their poison kills native animals who eat them. Nobody had first tested what the toads would eat and who would eat them.. *Why:* A strong answer names both failures: the toads did not do the job, and they harmed others. It says the testing should have come before the release.
15. (mcq) A bowl twice as far from scattering grains catches much less than half as many. Why?

   **Answer:** One reason: hummingbirds arose in the Americas, and wide oceans stopped them reaching India. Both are possible, and a good answer says how to tell them apart.
2. (mcq) A sea crossing happens about once in 10,000 years. About how many times in 1 million years?  
   Options: 10 times / Never / 1,000 times / 100 times  
   **Answer:** 100 times. *Why:* 1,000,000 divided by 10,000 is 100. A crossing too rare for anyone to see becomes almost certain over a very long time.

   **Answer:** Wegener could not say what force could move a continent, and nobody could feel the movement. 5. (mcq) Two frog species differ in very many places in their DNA. What does that suggest?

   **Answer:** Two drawings. First: sea between two halves of a land, with the same birds on each side. ## Applications
- Plant quarantine at airports and ports. Officers check plants, seeds, soil and fruit that travellers and ships bring, so that new weeds and insects are stopped at the border.
- Rules for ships. A ship carries sea water in tanks for balance and lets it out at the next port. Ships must now treat this water, because it carries tiny sea animals round the world.
- Biological control. India's bureau for farm insects in Bengaluru tests natural enemies of weeds and pests before they are released.
- Finding coal. Geologists look for coal in India in rocks of the same age as the coal of Australia and South Africa, because those lands were once joined.
- Earthquake maps. Engineers use the measured movement of the Indian plate to decide how strong buildings must be in the Himalaya and the North-East.
- Planning protected areas. A patch of forest surrounded by farms is like an island. Planners join such patches with strips of forest so that animals can keep mixing.
- Tracing a new pest. In 2018 a caterpillar from the Americas who eats maize was first found in India, in Karnataka. Scientists compared the moths' DNA with moths from other countries, to trace where they had come from.

## Spotted around us
- Lantana bushes on a roadside or the edge of a forest, with small pink, orange and yellow flowers in tight bunches.
- A pond, lake or canal covered from bank to bank with water hyacinth, in almost any Indian city.
- Parthenium on an empty plot or beside a railway line: a knee-high weed with tiny white flowers. Look, but do not touch.
- Giant African snails on a garden wall after rain, in Kerala, Bengaluru, Chennai and many other places.
- A gulmohar tree with red flowers in May, or a wide rain tree shading a road. The gulmohar came from Madagascar and the rain tree from tropical America.
- The dark, layered rock of the Western Ghats in Maharashtra, seen from the train between Mumbai and Pune. It is lava that flowed while India was crossing the sea.
- The Himalaya on a clear day from Darjeeling, Shimla or Kausani. They are still rising, because India is still pushing north.
- A house gecko on the wall at night. Geckos and their eggs travel easily in boats and cargo, so the same kinds live in ports all round the warm seas.

## Open questions
- **When exactly did India first touch Asia?** Different kinds of rock evidence give dates from about 55 million to about 35 million years ago. Some scientists think there were two meetings, a soft one and then a hard one.
- **How did the first monkeys cross from Africa to South America?** Most scientists think they rode a raft of plants across an ocean that was narrower then. Nobody has found direct evidence of the crossing, and it must have taken many days without fresh water.
- **Why does lantana spread so much more in India than at home?** Missing enemies, disturbed forests, the plant's own chemicals and many separate plantings have all been suggested. The share of each has not been measured.
- **When did the Earth's plates first begin to move?** Estimates run from more than 3 billion years ago to less than 1 billion. The oldest rocks have been melted and remade, so the evidence is thin.
- **As the climate warms, can living beings move to new places as fast as their climate moves?** Some birds and insects are already shifting their ranges. Slow travellers such as trees and frogs may not keep up, and roads and farms now stand in their way.

## Connected maths
- In a Separated Lines Test, the gap between families A and B was 7, 17, 19, 29 and 31 millimetres at generations 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10. The gap between the mixed families C and D was 2, 3, 3, 3 and 2. Describe each pattern. What would you expect at generation 20? **The gap between A and B grows, by about 3 millimetres a generation. The gap between C and D stays at 2 or 3 millimetres. At generation 20, A and B would probably be about 60 millimetres apart, and C and D still about 3.**. From generation 2 to generation 10 the A and B gap rose from 7 to 31. That is 24 millimetres in 8 generations, or 3 a generation. Ten more generations would add about 30, giving about 60. The C and D gap shows no rise at all. The prediction for A and B is rough, because the gap grows by chance steps and not by a fixed rule. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
- A thumbnail grows about 3.5 millimetres a month. The Indian plate moves about 5 centimetres a year. Which is faster? **The Indian plate, by a little. The nail grows about 4.2 centimetres a year, and India moves 5.**. First put both in the same units. 3.5 millimetres a month times 12 months is 42 millimetres a year. 42 millimetres is 4.2 centimetres. 5 centimetres is more than 4.2 centimetres. So the land under your feet moves a little faster than your nail grows. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L5`)
- The Indian plate moves about 5 centimetres a year. How far does it move in 1 million years? How long does it take to move 1 kilometre? **50 kilometres in 1 million years. 1 kilometre takes 20,000 years.**. 5 centimetres times 1,000,000 is 5,000,000 centimetres. Divide by 100 to get 50,000 metres, and by 1,000 to get 50 kilometres. For 1 kilometre: 1 kilometre is 100,000 centimetres, and 100,000 divided by 5 is 20,000 years. A speed too slow to see covers the width of an ocean in 100 million years. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- In Clear the Tank, a weed covers 1 square of 64 and doubles every round. Nobody clears the weed. After how many rounds is the tank full? In which round was it half full? One round is two weeks. How many weeks of warning does a half-full tank give? **Full after 6 rounds. Half full after 5 rounds. A half-full tank gives only 2 weeks of warning.**. Keep doubling: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. That is 6 doublings, so 6 rounds, which is 12 weeks. Half of 64 is 32, which comes one round before the end. So when the tank is half covered, only one round, or 2 weeks, is left. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In a Far Island Test, 2,100 grains of rice were poured in three pours. The near bowl caught 54, the middle bowl 9 and the far bowl 1. What fraction of the near bowl's catch did the middle bowl get? Was 'twice as far' the same as 'half as many'? **One sixth. No: twice as far gave one sixth as many, not one half.**. 9 divided by 54 is one sixth. If distance worked simply, the middle bowl would have caught half of 54, which is 27. It caught far fewer. Fewer grains bounce that far, and those that do are spread round a wider ring of floor. Notice also that only 64 grains of the 2,100 reached any island. Most travellers land in the sea. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Suppose a storm carries a lizard alive to a certain far island about once in every 10,000 years. How many arrivals would you expect in 1 million years? How many in your own 10 years of life? **About 100 arrivals in 1 million years. In 10 years, about one thousandth of an arrival, which means almost certainly none.**. 1,000,000 divided by 10,000 is 100. For 10 years: 10 divided by 10,000 is one thousandth. So nobody alive is likely to see it happen, and yet over a million years it happens about a hundred times. The figure of once in 10,000 years is made up, to show the reasoning. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What does biogeography study? → Where living beings are found, and why.
- Name two barriers that stop living beings from spreading. → A sea and a mountain range. A desert or a change of climate also counts.
- How fast do the Earth's plates move? → A few centimetres a year, about as fast as a fingernail grows.
- Which giant southern land was India once part of? → Gondwana.
- What happens to two groups of one species who are kept apart for a very long time? → They become different, and may become two species.
- What does endemic mean? → Found naturally in one place and nowhere else.
- What is the difference between introduced and invasive? → Introduced means brought by people. Invasive means brought by people, spreading alone and doing harm.
- Name one invasive plant in India, and the plant's homeland. → Lantana, from tropical America. Water hyacinth, from South America, is another.
