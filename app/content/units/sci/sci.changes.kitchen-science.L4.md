# Science in the kitchen: why food browns, bread rises and onions make us cry
`sci.changes.kitchen-science.L4`

**Summary.** Why does a chopped onion make your eyes sting when a whole one does not? In this lesson you will try an onion whole, halved and chopped, stop a cut apple turning brown, toast bread squares on a tawa, and wash a ball of dough to find the gluten.

**Intro.** *A cook can carry a bag of onions all the way home with dry eyes. Then she chops one onion, and tears run down her face. Where was the gas that stings her eyes before she picked up the knife?* The gas was not in the onion. The onion made the gas after the knife went in. In this lesson you test this with a whole onion, a halved onion and a chopped onion. You stop an apple slice from turning brown with two minutes of heat. Then you use heat to turn bread brown, and find what makes it brown faster. You also wash a ball of wheat dough under water until only a stretchy lump is left. That lump is the reason bread can rise.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain why a cut apple or brinjal turns brown: broken cells, an enzyme and oxygen from the air.
1. I can name three ways to slow or stop this browning, and say why each one works.
2. I can explain why toast and dosa turn brown: heat changes the sugars and proteins on a dry surface.
3. I can explain why idli and boiled rice stay pale: wet food cannot get hotter than boiling water.
4. I can name three gas makers that raise dough and batter: yeast, baking soda with a sour food, and steam.
5. I can explain what gluten is, and why wheat dough holds gas while rice flour dough does not.
6. I can explain why chopping an onion makes eyes water, and give two ways to make fewer tears.
7. I can plan a fair kitchen test, and say how people found out what makes onions sting.

## Terms
- **Cell**: The tiny living unit that every plant and animal is built from. A cell is too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* One thin slice of onion holds many thousands of cells.
- **Enzyme**: A chemical made by living cells that speeds up one kind of change. Each enzyme does one job. *e.g.* An enzyme in an apple turns the cut surface brown.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air. We breathe it in, and many chemical changes need it. *e.g.* A cut apple browns in air, because the air holds oxygen.
- **Enzyme browning**: The browning of a cut or bruised fruit or vegetable. An enzyme from the broken cells makes brown colour, using oxygen from the air. *e.g.* A cut brinjal turns brown in a few minutes.
- **Blanch**: To dip a food in boiling water for a short time and then take it out. *e.g.* Cooks blanch cut vegetables before freezing, so that they keep their colour.
- **Boiling point**: The temperature at which a liquid boils. Water in an open pot boils at about 100 degrees Celsius and gets no hotter. *e.g.* Rice in boiling water stays at about 100 degrees Celsius, however high the flame is.
- **Heat browning**: The browning of food by strong heat. On a dry surface hotter than about 140 degrees Celsius, sugars and proteins change into brown substances with new flavours. *e.g.* A dosa turns golden brown on a hot tawa.
- **Maillard reaction**: The main kind of heat browning. Sugars react with the building units of proteins and make brown colour and new smells. *e.g.* The brown crust of bread comes from the Maillard reaction.
- **Caramelise**: To heat sugar until it turns brown. Sugar can do this alone, without any protein. *e.g.* Sugar heated in a pan for payasam turns into brown caramel.
- **Yeast**: Living beings of one cell each, who belong to the fungi. Yeast eat sugar and give off a gas. *e.g.* Bakers mix yeast into dough to make pav rise.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas with no colour and no smell. Yeast give off this gas. Baking soda also makes this gas when it meets a sour food. *e.g.* The bubbles in dhokla batter are carbon dioxide.
- **Ferment**: To be changed by yeast or bacteria as they feed. Fermenting often makes gas or a sour taste. *e.g.* Idli batter ferments overnight and rises in the vessel.
- **Gluten**: A stretchy substance that forms when wheat flour is wetted and kneaded. It is made of wheat proteins joined into a net. *e.g.* Gluten lets a ball of chapati dough stretch without tearing.
- **Tear gland**: A small organ above each eye that makes tears. *e.g.* When onion gas reaches your eye, the tear gland sends tears to wash it away.

## Explanations
### A cell keeps its chemicals in separate parts · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell is the tiny living unit that plants and animals are built from. Inside a plant cell, different chemicals are stored in separate parts.

An onion, an apple and a tea leaf are all built from cells. Each cell has a wall around it. Inside the cell are smaller parts, each closed off by a thin skin. One part may store a chemical. Another part may store the enzyme that can change that chemical. An enzyme is a chemical made by cells that speeds up one kind of change. While the cell is whole, the two never meet, so nothing happens. A knife, a grater or a set of teeth breaks the cells open. Then the stored chemicals and the enzymes mix. New substances form within seconds or minutes. This one fact explains three things in this lesson. It explains why a cut apple browns and why a chopped onion stings. It also explains why crushed mustard becomes sharp.
- e.g. A whole apple stays white inside for weeks. A cut apple browns within the hour.
- e.g. A whole clove of garlic has little smell. A crushed clove smells strong at once.
- e.g. A bruise on a banana turns brown, because the cells under the peel were crushed.
### Why cut fruit turns brown · *defined*
**Definition:** Enzyme browning is the browning of a cut or bruised fruit or vegetable. An enzyme from the broken cells makes brown colour, using oxygen from the air.

The cells of an apple hold colourless chemicals called phenols. They also hold an enzyme called polyphenol oxidase. In a whole cell the two are kept in separate parts. A cut breaks the cells at the surface and mixes them. The enzyme then joins oxygen from the air to the phenols. The changed phenols link into long chains, and the chains are brown. So this browning needs three things together: broken cells, a working enzyme and oxygen. Take away any one, and the slice stays pale. The brown substance is harmless to eat, though the taste changes a little. Apples, bananas, brinjals, potatoes, pears and guavas all brown this way. Oranges and lemons do not. Their juice is sour, and the enzyme works badly in sour juice.
- e.g. A cut brinjal goes brown within a few minutes.
- e.g. Grated potato for a cutlet turns pink and then grey-brown in the bowl.
- e.g. A banana slice in a lunch box is brown by lunch time.
### How to stop cut fruit browning, and when people want it · *defined*
**Definition:** Cut fruit stays pale if the enzyme is destroyed or slowed, or if oxygen is kept away.

Heat destroys the enzyme. Two minutes in boiling water are enough for a thin slice. Cooks call this blanching. Sour juice slows the enzyme, so a squeeze of lemon keeps a fruit chaat pale. Lemon juice also holds vitamin C, which turns the changed phenols back before they can link up. A bowl of water keeps most of the oxygen away, so cooks drop cut brinjal and potato into water. Cold slows every enzyme, so a cut apple browns more slowly in the fridge. Sometimes people want this browning. Tea makers roll fresh tea leaves to break the cells. Then they leave the leaves in the air for up to a few hours. The leaves turn copper brown and become black tea. For green tea, the makers heat the fresh leaves first, in a hot pan or in steam. The heat destroys the enzyme, so the leaves stay green. Black tea and green tea come from the same plant.
- e.g. Cut potatoes wait in a bowl of water before they are fried for bajji.
- e.g. Lemon juice is squeezed over sliced banana in a fruit salad.
- e.g. Tea leaves from one bush in the Nilgiris can become green tea or black tea.
### Browning by heat · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat browning is the browning of food by strong heat. Sugars and proteins on a hot, dry surface change into brown substances with new smells and tastes.

This browning has nothing to do with the enzyme in cut fruit. Heat destroys that enzyme. Heat browning is made by the heat. There are two kinds. In the first kind, sugars react with the building units of proteins. This is called the Maillard reaction, after the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard. He described it in 1912. It makes hundreds of new substances. Some are brown, and many have strong smells. They give toast, dosa, roasted coffee, roasted groundnuts and fried onions their smell. In the second kind, sugar turns brown without any protein. This is called caramelising. Table sugar caramelises at about 160 to 170 degrees Celsius. Both kinds need a temperature well above that of boiling water. The Maillard reaction becomes fast above about 140 degrees Celsius. Milk holds both a sugar and proteins, so foods brushed with milk brown quickly. If the heating goes on too long, the food turns black and bitter. Then it has started to burn.
- e.g. Milk boiled down for a long time in a wide kadai turns light brown and smells of khoa.
- e.g. Sugar heated in a pan turns golden and then deep brown.
- e.g. Onions fried slowly in oil turn brown and sweet.
### Why wet food stays pale · *defined*
**Definition:** Water in an open pot boils at about 100 degrees Celsius and gets no hotter. Food that is wet stays near that temperature, which is too low for heat browning.

Turn the flame up under a pot of boiling water, and the water only boils away faster. Its temperature stays at about 100 degrees Celsius. So rice in boiling water never browns. Idli cooks in steam at about 100 degrees Celsius and stays white. A dosa is made from much the same batter. But the batter is spread thin on a tawa at about 200 degrees Celsius. The water at the bottom boils away in seconds. The dry surface then gets much hotter than 100 degrees Celsius, and it browns. Hot oil works in the same way. Oil for frying is at about 170 to 190 degrees Celsius. The outside of a vada dries and browns in the oil. The inside stays wet and pale. A loaf of bread is brown outside and white inside for the same reason. The crust dries and gets very hot. The inside stays moist and does not pass 100 degrees Celsius.
- e.g. Idli is white, and a dosa from much the same batter is brown.
- e.g. A boiled potato is pale, and a fried potato chip is golden.
- e.g. A wet patch on a slice of bread stays pale on the tawa until it has dried.
### Three ways to make the gas that raises dough · *defined*
**Definition:** Dough and batter rise when gas forms inside them. Cooks make the gas with yeast, with baking soda and a sour food, or with steam.

The first gas maker is alive. Yeast are living beings of one cell each, and a teaspoon of dry yeast holds billions of them. In warm, wet dough they wake and feed on sugars. They give off carbon dioxide gas and a little alcohol. This is called fermenting. It takes an hour or more. Wild yeast and bacteria do the same work in idli batter overnight. The second gas maker is a chemical reaction. Baking soda meets a sour food, such as curd or lemon juice. Carbon dioxide forms at once, with no waiting. Dhokla, bhatura and most cakes rise this way. The third gas maker is steam. Water turns to steam at about 100 degrees Celsius. The steam takes up about 1,600 times as much room as the water did. A puri has no yeast and no soda. The water in the dough turns to steam in the hot oil, and the steam blows the puri up. Phulka and popcorn puff in the same way.
- e.g. Pav and naan are raised by yeast.
- e.g. Dhokla is raised by baking soda or fruit salt meeting sour curd.
- e.g. A puri and a grain of popcorn are puffed by steam.
### Gluten: the net that holds the gas · *defined*
**Definition:** Gluten is a stretchy substance that forms when wheat flour is wetted and kneaded. It is made of wheat proteins joined into a net.

Making gas is only half of rising. The dough must also hold the gas in. Wheat flour contains two kinds of protein that join together when they are wet. Kneading lines them up and links them into a net. The net stretches like a balloon skin when gas pushes on it. In 1728 the Italian scientist Jacopo Beccari is said to have washed a ball of wheat dough in water. The white starch washed away. A grey, stretchy lump stayed in his hand. That lump was gluten. Rice, ragi, jowar, bajra and maize have proteins too, but they do not form gluten. A dough of rice flour cracks when you stretch it. So a jowar bhakri or an akki rotti is patted flat by hand. It does not puff like a loaf. Idli batter holds its gas in another way. Ground urad dal makes the batter thick and slimy, and the slime traps the bubbles. A few people fall ill when they eat gluten. Their illness is called coeliac disease. For most people gluten is an ordinary food protein.
- e.g. Chapati dough stretches when you roll it. Rice flour dough for kozhukattai cracks at the edges.
- e.g. The holes in a slice of bread are the places where gas bubbles were held.
- e.g. Maida dough is stretchier than atta dough, because bits of bran in atta cut into the net.
### What heat does to risen dough · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat makes the gas bubbles in dough grow. Then the heat sets the dough around the bubbles, so the holes stay when the food cools.

Raw risen dough is soft, and it falls flat if you press it. Heat changes that in several steps. First the gas in each bubble spreads out as it warms, and some water turns to steam. So the dough swells further in the first minutes of cooking. Next, at about 60 degrees Celsius, the yeast die. Then the starch and the proteins set firm, in the way an egg sets when it is boiled. The walls of the bubbles become solid. Now the gas can leave and the shape stays. Last, the outside dries and browns. The same steps happen when dhokla or idli is steamed. Only the browning is missing, because steam is not hot enough. If a cook opens the lid too early, the walls have not yet set. The cold air makes the gas shrink, and the cake sinks.
- e.g. A pav in the oven grows taller in the first few minutes.
- e.g. Steamed dhokla keeps its holes after it cools.
- e.g. A cake sinks in the middle if the oven door is opened too soon.
### Why onions make us cry · *defined*
**Definition:** A cut onion gives off a gas that stings the eyes. The onion's cells make this gas in two steps after they are broken, and each step needs an enzyme.

An onion takes sulphur from the soil and builds it into a chemical that the cells store. An enzyme is stored in another part of the same cell. A knife breaks the cells, and the two mix. The first enzyme changes the stored chemical into a new one that does not last. A second enzyme at once turns part of that into a gas. Scientists call the gas the lachrymatory factor, which means the tear maker. It forms within about half a minute of the cut. The gas floats up and reaches the wet surface of the eye. Nerve endings there sense it and send a signal to the brain. The brain signals the tear glands, and tears pour out to wash the gas away. Three things make fewer tears. A cold onion makes gas more slowly, because cold slows enzymes. A sharp knife breaks fewer cells than a blunt one. Moving air carries the gas away from the face. A cooked onion cannot sting, because heat destroys both enzymes. Garlic lacks the second enzyme. So crushed garlic makes a strong smell and no tear gas.
- e.g. Onions chopped straight from the fridge sting less than onions from the basket.
- e.g. A whole onion boiled in sambar can be cut afterwards with dry eyes.
- e.g. Crushed mustard seeds mixed with water turn sharp after ten minutes, in a similar way.
### Do the sting and the brown protect the plant? · *debated*
**Definition:** Most plant scientists think the onion's gas is a defence against animals who would eat the bulb. They disagree about whether browning protects a wounded fruit or leaf.

An onion bulb is alive. They are a resting plant, and they can sprout again. The tear gas is made only when the bulb is bitten or cut. So most scientists see the gas as the onion's defence. The browning of a wound is harder to explain. The brown substances can harm microbes and insects in a dish in the laboratory. Whether they protect a living plant is still being tested.
- e.g. An onion left in the basket for weeks pushes out a green shoot.
- e.g. Many animals leave onion bulbs alone.
- **Scientists who see a defence, such as Li Li and John Steffens at Cornell University in 2002:** They changed tomato plants so that the plants made much more of the browning enzyme. Far fewer harmful bacteria grew in the leaves of these plants. So the enzyme can help a plant resist disease.
- **Scientists who urge care:** Apple trees whose browning enzyme has been switched off still grow and fruit. The enzyme may do other jobs in the cell that nobody has found yet. One result in tomato does not prove what the enzyme does in every plant.

## Mostly agreed
- A cut fruit browns because an enzyme from its broken cells joins oxygen to chemicals in the juice. *How we know:* Heat destroys enzymes, and a slice dipped in boiling water does not brown. In 2015 the United States approved apples whose browning enzyme had been switched off. Their slices stay pale for days.
- Heat browning comes from sugars reacting with the building units of proteins. *How we know:* In 1912 Louis-Camille Maillard warmed sugars with these building units, which are called amino acids. The clear mixtures turned brown and gave off gas.
- Wet food stays near 100 degrees Celsius, so it does not brown. *How we know:* A thermometer in an open pot of boiling water reads about 100 degrees Celsius however high the flame is. Steamed and boiled foods stay pale in every kitchen.
- The onion makes tear gas only after cutting, and needs two enzymes to do so. *How we know:* In 2002 Shinsuke Imai and his team in Japan found the second enzyme. Onion juice without this enzyme made almost no tear gas.
- Wheat dough holds gas because of gluten. *How we know:* In 1728 Jacopo Beccari is said to have washed wheat dough and been left with a stretchy lump of gluten. Flours that leave no such lump make flat breads.
- Yeast are alive, and they make the gas in bread dough as they feed. *How we know:* In the 1850s Louis Pasteur watched yeast under a microscope. He showed that fermenting happens only where living yeast are growing.

## How we found out
**Shinsuke Imai and his team at House Foods in Japan, and Colin Eady and his team in New Zealand, The tear gas was identified in the early 1970s. Imai's team reported the second enzyme in 2002. Eady's team reported onions without it in 2008..** By the 1970s chemists knew what the onion's tear gas was. They also knew that an onion holds an enzyme called alliinase, which starts work when cells are broken. For about thirty years, books said that this one enzyme did the whole job. The gas was thought to form by itself from what the enzyme made. In Japan, Shinsuke Imai and his team at a food company were studying onions and garlic. They mixed the onion's stored chemical with alliinase. Almost no tear gas formed. Then they added another part of the onion's juice, and the gas appeared. So the juice held a second enzyme that nobody had known about. They found it, and they described it in the journal Nature in 2002. Garlic lacks this second enzyme, which is why garlic does not make us cry. In 2008 Colin Eady's team in New Zealand switched off the second enzyme in onion plants. Those onions made almost no tear gas.  
*What people believed before:* That one enzyme, alliinase, makes the onion's tear gas, and that the gas then forms by itself. Imai's team showed that alliinase alone made almost no gas, and that a second enzyme is needed.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A cut apple that has turned brown has gone bad.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rotten fruit is often brown, and a brown slice looks less fresh.  
  Against: The brown comes from the apple's own enzyme within minutes. Microbes need days to rot a fruit. A browned slice is safe to eat.
- **Food turns brown on the tawa because it is burning.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Food left too long on the heat does go from brown to black.  
  Against: Browning comes first and makes new flavours. Burning comes later, when the food breaks down to black carbon and tastes bitter. Golden brown food is not burnt.
- **Onion gas turns into acid on the eye, and the acid makes us cry.** (Many books and websites.; *debated*)  
  For: The gas does break down in water, and one of the products is an acid.  
  Against: The amount of gas is very small. Nerve endings on the eye have sensors that react to stinging chemicals themselves. Many scientists now think these sensors respond to the gas directly.
- **Holding a piece of bread in your mouth stops onion tears.** (A kitchen tip passed on in many families.; *debated*)  
  For: Some cooks say it works for them. Breathing through the mouth may draw the gas away from the eyes.  
  Against: The gas reaches the eyes through the air, and bread cannot catch it. No careful test has shown that the tip works.
- **Gluten is bad for everyone.** (Some advertisements and diet books.; *rejected*)  
  For: People with coeliac disease become ill from gluten. They must avoid wheat for life.  
  Against: Coeliac disease affects about 1 person in 100. For most people gluten is an ordinary protein, and wheat has been eaten for thousands of years.
- **Very brown fried food can harm us.** (Margareta Törnqvist and her team in Sweden, in 2002.; *debated*)  
  For: They found a chemical called acrylamide in starchy foods fried or baked until dark. In large doses it causes cancer in rats and mice.  
  Against: People eat far smaller doses than the test animals received. Studies of people have not shown clear harm. Many food agencies still advise cooking to golden and not to dark brown.

## Roots
In this lesson you found that an onion's sting and an apple's brown are made after the cut. Here we ask what a thing holds before it is cut, and what that tells us about living beings.

**Deep truth.** In the Onion Sting, a whole onion near your eyes did nothing, and a chopped onion stung. A whole onion holds no tear gas. Each cell stores two chemicals in separate parts, and a knife makes them meet. So the sting is made after the cut, by the onion's own cells.  
*Habit it breaks:* We speak as if the sting, the smell and the colour of a food are stored inside it. Some of them do not exist until the cells are broken. What the plant holds is the means to make them.  
*Evidence:* In the Onion Sting, the whole onion did not sting, and the chopped onion stung within seconds. In Wake the Mustard, the dry crushed seeds were mild, and the wet raw paste turned sharp in ten minutes. In Hot Dip, Cold Dip, the boiled slice could not turn brown. So the brown is made by something that heat destroys.

- *who am i:* Your skin makes its brown colour with an enzyme closely related to the one that browns a cut apple. You and an apple share some of the same tools.
- *what is life:* An onion bulb is alive. They keep two chemicals apart in every cell, ready for the moment an animal bites. Plants sense wounds and answer them.
- *what is this world:* An idli and a dosa are made from much the same batter. What the batter becomes depends on heat, water and time. The same matter can become many things.

A whole onion, a whole apple and a dry mustard seed look finished. Yet each one holds chemicals that have not met. When they meet, something appears that was not there before: a gas, a colour or a sharp taste. Many sages taught that the many things of the world come from one source, which holds them all as possibilities. The science here says something smaller and plain. What a thing can become is part of what that thing is.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** “किमत्र पश्यसीति । न किंचन भगव इति / तं होवाच यं वै सोम्यैतमणिमानं न निभालयस एतस्य वै सोम्यैषोऽणिम्न एवं महान्यग्रोधस्तिष्ठति” Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to split the fruit of a banyan, and then one of its tiny seeds. The boy sees nothing inside. His father says that the great tree grows from that fineness, which he cannot see. (You cannot find the tear gas in a whole onion, because it is not there yet. The onion holds the means to make it, as the seed holds the means to make a tree. The Upanishad uses the seed to point to the self, which science does not test.)
- **Udana 1.1, a saying of the Buddha:** “Iti imasmiṁ sati idaṁ hoti, imassuppādā idaṁ uppajjati” When this is, that comes to be. When this arises, that arises. (The sting arises only when broken cells, two enzymes, moving air and an eye come together. Take one away, and there are no tears. That is how you beat the sting in Tear-Free Chopping.)
- **Jagadish Chandra Bose, Response in the Living and Non-Living (1902):** Bose recorded electrical signals in plants. He showed that a plant answers a touch or a blow with a signal, as an animal's muscle does. (A cut onion answers the knife with chemistry. Bose showed that plants also answer with electricity. He went further and said that metals respond in the same way, and most scientists do not accept that part.)
- Science (settled): When a caterpillar bites one leaf of a thale cress plant, a signal reaches the plant's other leaves within minutes. Those leaves then begin to make defence chemicals.
- Science (settled): The enzyme that browns a cut apple belongs to a family of enzymes. Another enzyme of that family makes melanin, the brown colour of human skin.
- Science (open): Whether a plant feels a wound in a way that is like an animal's pain is not known.
- Other view, Many chemists and plant scientists: The onion's gas is simply what forms when two chemicals meet, as baking soda fizzes when it meets lemon juice. Nothing is chosen at the moment of the cut. Long ago, onions who happened to store these chemicals were eaten less often. So more of them lived to make seeds. On this view, 'defence' is a handy word for that result. It tells us nothing about what an onion senses.
- Practice, One leaf, two breaths (3 min): Take one curry leaf, or a leaf of mint, tulsi or coriander. Hold the whole leaf under your nose and breathe in slowly, twice. Now tear the leaf in half and rub it once between your fingers. Breathe in slowly, twice more. The smell reached you when the cells were broken. Sit quietly for five breaths, and notice how long the smell stays.
- Ask: In the Onion Sting, the whole onion did not sting and the chopped onion did. Where was the sting before the knife came?
- Ask: The onion makes the gas when an animal bites. People cut onions anyway, and then plant more of them. What has the onion gained?
- Ask: Your skin and a cut apple make brown colour with related enzymes. What else might you share with an apple?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A whole onion holds no tear gas. So is the sting a part of the onion, or something the onion does? (hint: Think about the Onion Sting activity. The whole onion did nothing to your eyes, and the chopped onion stung.)
- *deep:* Toast and a cut apple are both brown. What would you test to find out if two browns are the same change? (hint: Heat stops one kind of browning and causes the other.)
- *deep:* A dosa and an idli come from much the same batter. Is a food decided by what goes in or by what is done to it? (hint: The batter is the same. The temperature at the surface is different.)
- *big:* The onion makes a stinging gas when cut, and people cut onions every day. Who has won, the onion or the cook? (hint: Think about it from the onion's side. The sting did not stop people from growing onions on millions of farms, because we like their taste.)
- *big:* Humans are the only animals who cook. How might browned, cooked food have changed our ancestors? (hint: Cooked food is softer and gives more energy for less chewing.)
- *big:* People made bread and black tea for thousands of years before anyone knew about enzymes or yeast. Did they understand what they were doing? (hint: A baker knew that dough must be kept warm. Is knowing how the same as knowing why?)

## Story: The Handful That Stayed Green
*Malar, Chellamma, Kathir, the shed and the letter are made up. These facts are real. Tea grows around Kotagiri in the Nilgiri hills of Tamil Nadu. Black tea and green tea come from the same plant. For black tea the leaf is wilted and then rolled to break the cells. It is left in the air for up to a few hours to turn brown, and then dried. For green tea the fresh leaf is heated first, in a hot pan or in steam. Then it stays green when rolled. The browning is done by an enzyme in the leaf, the same kind that browns a cut apple.*

Every evening in the picking season, Malar's hands smelled of cut grass. Her job was to roll the tea.

Her aunt Chellamma made tea by hand in a tin shed above Kotagiri, in the Nilgiri hills. Four families brought their leaf to her. Malar was nine, and she had rolled leaf beside her aunt for two seasons.

The work never changed. In the morning the pickers brought baskets of fresh leaf. Chellamma spread the leaf on cloth racks until it went limp. In the afternoon Malar and her cousin Kathir rolled it. They pressed a heap of limp leaves under their palms and pushed it round and round on a bamboo tray. The leaves twisted and bruised, and green juice wet their hands. Then the rolled leaf lay on trays in the cool end of the shed. In two hours it turned from green to the colour of a new copper pot. Last, Chellamma dried it in a wide iron pan over the wood fire.

That was black tea. It was the only tea the shed had ever made.

On Tuesday a letter came from a shop in Coimbatore. The shop would pay double for green tea. It wanted one kilo by Saturday, to try.

"We cannot do it," said Kathir. He was twelve, and he read the labels on the packets in the bazaar. "Green tea comes from a green tea bush. Ours are black tea bushes."

"Who told you that?" asked Chellamma.

"Everybody knows it."

Chellamma did not answer. She had picked tea since she was a girl, and she had only ever seen one kind of bush. But she had only ever made one kind of tea. She did not know how the other kind was made, and nobody on the hill made it.

Malar said nothing either. She was thinking about last Thursday.

Last Thursday she had carried a basket of fresh leaf past the fire. Her foot had caught on a root that grew across the floor. A handful of leaves had tipped into the hot iron pan. She had snatched them out with the wooden paddle before her aunt could turn round. They were limp and hot, and they smelled like boiled beans. She had been ashamed. She had pushed them into the middle of her heap and rolled them with the rest.

Two hours later she had seen them again. The tray was copper brown from edge to edge. But in the middle lay a few twists of leaf as green as when they were picked. She had picked them out and thrown them to the hens, so that nobody would ask.

Now she went to the cool end of the shed and looked at today's trays. Every leaf was turning brown.

"Chitti," she said. That was what she called her aunt. "Why does the leaf go brown?"

"Because we roll it. Unrolled leaf only dries up. It stays dull green, like hay."

"So the rolling does it."

"The rolling starts it. Then the air does the rest."

"Last week some leaves fell in the hot pan," said Malar. Her ears were burning. "Only for a moment. I rolled them with the others. They never went brown. I threw them to the hens."

Kathir laughed. "You cooked them. Of course they were spoilt."

"They were not spoilt. They were green. They were rolled, and they lay in the air, and they stayed green." Malar turned to her aunt. "Something in the leaf turns it brown after rolling. The hot pan stopped that from working. If we heat the leaf first, and roll it afterwards, it might stay green."

Chellamma looked at her for some time. Then she put two handfuls of fresh leaf into the iron pan.

She stirred with the paddle while Malar counted aloud. The leaves hissed and went soft. At two hundred, Chellamma lifted them out. Malar rolled them on a clean tray. Beside them she rolled two handfuls that had not touched the pan. They put the two trays side by side at the cool end of the shed.

Nobody did much work for the next two hours.

Malar looked after half an hour. The plain leaf had brown edges. The heated leaf was green.

After an hour the plain leaf was brown all over. The heated leaf was green.

After two hours Chellamma came and stood between the trays. She picked up a twist of the green leaf and smelled it. Then she dried both lots in the pan, one after the other, and made two tumblers of tea.

One tea was red-brown and smelled of malt, as their tea always did. The other was pale gold, and it smelled like the hillside after rain.

"Same bush," said Chellamma. She held out the pale tumbler to Kathir. "Drink."

Kathir drank. He looked into the tumbler for a while.

"Everybody was wrong, then," he said.

"Everybody had not dropped the leaf in the pan," said Chellamma.

By Saturday there was a kilo of green tea in a steel tin, tied in a cloth. Malar carried it down to the bus herself. She stepped carefully over the root.

**For the parent:** Kathir was sure that green tea needs a different bush. Malar had seen that leaves heated in the pan stayed green after rolling. Rolling breaks the cells of the leaf, and an enzyme then turns the leaf brown in the air. Heat destroys the enzyme, so leaf that is heated first stays green.

- Ask: Rolled tea leaves turn brown in the air. What does rolling do to the cells of a leaf?
- Ask: The heated leaves were rolled too, and they lay in the same air. Why did they stay green?
- Ask: How could you stop a cut apple from browning, using the same idea?

## Experiments
### e1 · Hot Dip, Cold Dip: can heat stop a cut apple turning brown? (30 min)
- **question:** Three slices are cut from one apple. One gets no dip. One is dipped in cold water for two minutes. One is dipped in boiling water for two minutes. Which slices will turn brown in the next 40 minutes?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which of the three slices will be brownest after 40 minutes, and which will be palest? Will the boiled slice brown faster or slower than the others? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut all three slices from the same apple, each about half a centimetre thick. Dip the cold slice and the hot slice for the same time, two minutes. Then both have been wet, and only the heat differs. Lay all three slices on one plate in the same place. Look at them at the same times. Only one thing changes: no dip, a cold dip or a hot dip.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the apple and does the dip in boiling water. The child stays an arm's length from the stove.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows: no dip, cold dip and hot dip. At each time, write a brown score from 0 to 3. Write 0 for no brown, 1 for a little, 2 for brown patches and 3 for brown all over.
- **results table:** Slice | At the start | After 10 minutes | After 20 minutes | After 40 minutes
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the apple, the bowl of cold water and the plate on the table. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will cut three slices from this apple. One gets no dip. One sits in cold water for two minutes. One sits in boiling water for two minutes. Which will be brownest after 40 minutes? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three slices: no dip, cold dip, hot dip. Which will go brownest? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think heat will do to the slice? (good: It will cook it, so it may go brown faster.; It may kill something in the apple.; I am not sure, but hot things often go brown.)
  - Note: Many children expect the heated slice to brown most, because cooking often browns food. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Bring a small pan of water to the boil. Cut three slices, each about half a centimetre thick. Call the slices N for no dip, C for cold dip and H for hot dip. Put slice N on the plate. Put slice C in the cold water. Hold slice H under the boiling water in the ladle. After two minutes, lift out C and H and put them on the plate. Note the time.  
  *Parent says:* I will cut the slices and do the hot dip. You time two minutes on the clock. Both dipped slices get wet. Why did we give one slice a cold dip?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts and dips. You time two minutes. Then write down the time.
  - Note: The cold dip is the fair partner of the hot dip. Without it, nobody could tell whether the heat or the wetting made the difference. The boiled slice will look a little glassy and soft.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the plate after 10 minutes and after 20 minutes. Your child writes a brown score from 0 to 3 for each slice. At 20 minutes they scratch slice N and slice H with a fork, four lines each.  
  *Parent says:* Give each slice a brown score from 0 to 3. Zero is not brown and three is brown all over. Now scratch the no-dip slice and the hot-dip slice with the fork. The scratches break fresh cells. What do you think the scratches will look like in 20 minutes?  
  *Child mode:* Score each slice from 0 to 3. Scratch two slices with a fork.
  - Note: You can do something else between the looks. The times need not be exact, but look at all three slices together.
  - Check: In Hot Dip, Cold Dip, why do all three slices come from one apple? → **So that only the dip is different**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look again 40 minutes after the dip. Write the last scores. Look closely at the scratch lines on slice N and slice H.  
  *Parent says:* Write the last scores. Now look at the scratches. On which slice did the scratch lines turn brown?  
  *Child mode:* Write the last scores. Which scratches turned brown?
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. The hot slice and the cold slice were both wet. What did the boiling water do that the cold water did not?  
  *Child mode:* Tell your grown-up what you guessed, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: The scratches on the boiled slice stayed pale, though fresh cells were broken. What does that tell you? (good: Whatever makes the brown was destroyed by the heat.; Breaking cells is not enough. Something in the cells must still work.; The boiled slice cannot make brown any more.)
  - Ask: Name three things that a cut apple slice needs to turn brown. (good: Broken cells.; Something in the apple that still works, the enzyme.; Air, or the oxygen in it.)
  - Note: In most kitchens the no-dip slice is clearly brown by 40 minutes. The cold-dip slice is nearly as brown. The hot-dip slice stays pale, and so do its scratches. Write down what you really saw, even if it differs. The reason: apple cells hold an enzyme and some colourless chemicals in separate parts. Cutting mixes them, and the enzyme joins oxygen from the air to the chemicals. That makes the brown. Boiling water destroys the enzyme. Some apples brown slowly. If your no-dip slice is still pale after 40 minutes, repeat the test with a banana or a brinjal.
  - Think: Tea makers heat fresh leaves in a hot pan to make green tea. What does the heat stop?
### e2 · The Tawa Toast Test: which bread square browns most? (25 min)
- **question:** Four squares of bread go on one tawa. One is dry, one is wet with water, one with sugar water and one with milk. Which square will be brownest after four minutes?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Put the four squares in order, from the one that will brown least to the one that will brown most. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut all four squares from one slice of bread. Put the same amount of liquid, half a teaspoon, on each wet square. Lay all four squares at the same distance from the middle of the tawa. Every minute, move each square one place round the tawa. Then no square sits on a hotter spot for the whole time. Only one thing changes: what is on the bread.
- **safety:** A grown-up works at the stove and handles the tawa and the tongs. The child stands an arm's length away and looks at each square when the grown-up holds it out.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows: dry, water, sugar water and milk. After each minute, write a brown score from 0 to 4. Write 0 for white, 1 for cream and 2 for golden. Write 3 for brown like the crust of the bread, and 4 for dark brown.
- **results table:** Square | After 1 minute | After 2 minutes | After 3 minutes | After 4 minutes
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut one slice of bread into four squares. Put out the three cups. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* One square stays dry. One gets water, one gets sugar water and one gets milk. Then all four go on the tawa. Put them in order, from least brown to most brown. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four squares: dry, water, sugar water, milk. Which will brown most? Why?
  - Ask: Will the wet squares brown faster or slower than the dry one? Why? (good: Slower, because the water has to dry first.; Faster, because sugar burns easily.; Milk goes brown when it boils over, so milk may brown fast.)
  - Note: Any order with a reason is a good start. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stir 1 teaspoon of sugar into 2 tablespoons of water until it dissolves. Your child spreads half a teaspoon of each liquid on one face of a square. Leave the fourth square dry. Write down which square is which.  
  *Parent says:* Spread the liquid over the whole face with the back of the spoon. Every wet square gets the same amount. Why must the amount be the same?  
  *Child mode:* Spread half a teaspoon on each wet square. Leave one square dry.
  - Note: Nip one corner off the water square and two corners off the sugar square. Nip three off the milk square. Then you can tell them apart on the tawa.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat the tawa on a low flame for one minute. Lay the four squares wet side down, each halfway between the middle and the edge. After each minute, lift each square with tongs and show the underside. Your child writes the scores. Put each square back one place further round. Stop after four minutes, or sooner if a square turns dark brown.  
  *Parent says:* I will lift each square after every minute. Give each one a brown score from 0 to 4. Zero is white and four is dark brown. I move each square one place round the tawa after every look.  
  *Child mode:* Watch from an arm's length away. Score each square every minute.
  - Note: Keep the flame low. The sugar square can go from brown to black quickly, so take it off if it starts to smoke. Listen for the hiss from the wet squares in the first minute.
  - Check: In the Tawa Toast Test, why does each square move one place round every minute? → **Some parts of the tawa are hotter**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn off the stove. Lay the four squares in a row, toasted side up. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Compare the water square with the dry square. Then compare the water square with the sugar square and the milk square.  
  *Child mode:* Tell your grown-up what you guessed, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: The water square stayed pale for the first two minutes. What was the heat doing in that time? (good: Drying the water.; The water was boiling away.; The bread could not get hot until the water was gone.)
  - Ask: The water, sugar and milk squares were all wet. Why did two of them end up browner? (good: Sugar browns in heat.; Milk has sugar and protein in it.; They had more of what browning needs.)
  - Note: Usually the dry square browns first. The water square stays pale longest and ends palest. The sugar and milk squares start late and then turn the darkest. Write down what you really saw, even if it differs. The reason: wet bread stays at about 100 degrees Celsius until the water has boiled away. Heat browning needs a dry surface well above that. Browning is a reaction of sugars with proteins. Sugar water adds sugar. Milk adds both a sugar and proteins. So those squares brown most once they have dried.
  - Think: Bakers brush milk on buns before baking. What does the milk do?
### e3 · Wash the Dough: what is left of wheat dough and rice dough? (40 min)
- **question:** A ball of wheat dough and a ball of rice dough are each washed in a bowl of water. What will be left in your hand from each ball?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. When you wash each ball of dough in water, what will be left in your hand? Will the two flours leave the same thing? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same amount of each flour, half a cup. Knead both balls for the same time, five minutes. Rest both under water for the same time, 15 minutes. Wash each ball in the same way, squeezing gently under water. Change the water each time it turns white. Only one thing changes: the kind of flour.
- **safety:** Do not taste the raw dough. Pour the white washing water onto plants or down the sink with plenty of water.
- **record:** Draw a table with two rows: wheat and rice. Write how far each dough stretched before it broke. Write what was left after washing, and how big it was.
- **results table:** Flour | How the dough stretched | What was left after washing | How big it was
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the two flours on the table. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will make a ball of wheat dough and a ball of rice dough. Then we will wash each ball in a bowl of water. What will be left in your hand from each one? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will wash two balls of dough. What will be left? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think flour is made of? (good: White powder that mixes into water.; Starch.; I think all of it will wash away.)
  - Note: Most children expect both balls to wash away completely. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mix half a cup of wheat flour with about 3 tablespoons of water. Your child kneads it for five minutes into a smooth ball. Do the same with the rice flour. Pull each ball slowly apart and see how far it stretches. Write that down.  
  *Parent says:* Knead by pressing, folding and turning the dough. Count five minutes on the clock. Now pull each ball slowly apart. How far does each one stretch before it breaks?  
  *Child mode:* Knead each dough for five minutes. Pull each ball slowly. Which one stretches?
  - Note: Add water a teaspoon at a time until the dough holds together. Rice flour may need a little more. The rice ball will crack when it is pulled.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put each ball in its own bowl and cover it with water. Leave both for 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Both balls rest under water for 15 minutes. The rest makes the wheat ball easier to wash.  
  *Child mode:* Put each ball in a bowl of water. Wait 15 minutes.
  - Wait: Leave the balls under water for 15 minutes
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child squeezes and kneads the wheat ball gently under the water. When the water turns white, pour it away through the strainer. Put any bits from the strainer back on the ball. Add fresh water. Repeat until the water stays nearly clear, about five or six times. Then wash the rice ball in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Squeeze the ball gently under the water. Look at the water. Keep the ball in one piece. Now do the same with the rice ball.  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze the wheat ball under water. Change the white water. Then wash the rice ball.
  - Note: Be gentle at first, or the wheat ball breaks into shreds. The shreds join again if you press them together. With atta, the lump is smaller and has brown specks of bran. The rice ball falls apart into white water, and nothing stays in the hand.
  - Check: In Wash the Dough, the washing water turned white. What was the white substance? → **Starch from the flour**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child stretches the lump that is left, pokes it and lets it spring back. Ask them to say what they thought would happen, what they found, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Stretch the lump. Poke it. Say what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think it happened. Which dough could hold a bubble of gas without tearing?  
  *Child mode:* Stretch the lump. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you found. Say why.
  - Ask: Bread is made from wheat far more often than from rice. Why? (good: Wheat dough has the stretchy part that holds gas.; Rice dough cannot stretch, so the gas escapes.; The lump can stretch like a balloon.)
  - Ask: The wheat dough stretched and the rice dough cracked. What explains that now? (good: The stretchy lump was inside the wheat dough all along.; Rice flour has no gluten.)
  - Note: Half a cup of maida leaves a grey, rubbery lump about as big as an amla. The lump is gluten. It forms when two kinds of wheat protein join into a net as the dough is wetted and kneaded. The white that washed away was starch. Rice flour has starch and protein too, but its proteins do not form this net. So nothing is left. Gas pushes into the gluten net and stretches it, and that is how wheat dough rises. The Italian scientist Jacopo Beccari is credited with first describing gluten, in a talk of 1728.
  - Think: Idli batter is made of rice and urad dal, with no wheat. What might hold the gas in idli batter?

### Brown, risen and stinging (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What three things does a cut apple need in order to turn brown?  
   Options: Broken cells, an enzyme and oxygen / Heat, sugar and salt / Yeast, water and warmth / Cold, dark and time  
   **Answer:** Broken cells, an enzyme and oxygen. *Why:* Cutting breaks the cells. An enzyme from the cells then joins oxygen from the air to chemicals in the juice. That makes the brown.
2. (truefalse) A whole onion is full of tear gas that is waiting to escape.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A whole onion holds no tear gas. The cells make the gas only after a knife breaks them and mixes what was stored apart.
3. (fill) The stretchy substance that forms when wheat flour is wetted and kneaded is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['gluten']. *Why:* Gluten is a net of wheat proteins. It stretches around gas bubbles and holds them in the dough.
4. (match) Match each food to what makes it rise.  
   **Answer:** Pav → Yeast; Dhokla → Baking soda and sour curd; Puri → Steam; Idli → Wild yeast and bacteria. *Why:* Yeast and bacteria make gas slowly as they feed. Baking soda makes gas at once when it meets a sour food. Steam forms from the water in the dough.
5. (sort) Sort these. Is each one enzyme browning or heat browning?  
   **Answer:** a cut brinjal → Enzyme browning; a bruised banana → Enzyme browning; rolled tea leaves → Enzyme browning; toast → Heat browning; a dosa → Heat browning; fried onions → Heat browning. *Why:* Enzyme browning happens to raw food whose cells are broken, without any heating. Heat browning happens on a hot, dry surface.
6. (mcq) Why does an idli stay white while a dosa turns brown?  
   Options: Steam is not hot enough to brown / Idli batter has no sugar / Idli is cooked for longer / Dosa batter has more salt  
   **Answer:** Steam is not hot enough to brown. *Why:* Steam is at about 100 degrees Celsius, and heat browning needs more. The dry surface of a dosa on a tawa gets far hotter.
7. (truefalse) Water in an open pot gets hotter and hotter as it boils.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Boiling water stays at about 100 degrees Celsius. A higher flame only makes it boil away faster.
8. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how a chopped onion makes you cry.  
   **Answer:** ['The knife breaks onion cells', 'A stored chemical and an enzyme mix', 'A gas forms and floats up', 'Nerve endings on the eye sense the gas', 'The tear glands send out tears']. *Why:* The gas cannot form until the cells are broken. The tears come last, after the nerves have sent their signal to the brain.
9. (fill) A chemical made by living cells that speeds up one kind of change is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['enzyme']. *Why:* Each enzyme does one job. One enzyme browns a cut apple, and others make the onion's tear gas.
10. (mcq) What does kneading do to wheat dough?  
   Options: Links proteins into a stretchy net / Kills the yeast / Washes out the starch / Makes the gas  
   **Answer:** Links proteins into a stretchy net. *Why:* Kneading lines up the wheat proteins and joins them into gluten. The gas is made by yeast, soda or steam, not by kneading.
11. (write) An apple slice was held in boiling water for two minutes. It did not turn brown afterwards. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The brown is made by an enzyme from the apple's broken cells. Boiling water destroys the enzyme. With no working enzyme, oxygen is not joined to the chemicals in the juice, so no brown forms.. *Why:* A strong answer names the enzyme and says that heat destroyed it. It does not say that the heat cooked the brown away.
12. (write) Washed wheat dough leaves a stretchy lump, and washed rice dough leaves nothing. What does this mean for bread?  
   **Answer:** The stretchy lump is gluten. Gluten stretches around gas bubbles and holds them, so wheat dough can rise into a loaf. Rice dough has no gluten, so the gas escapes and the dough stays flat.. *Why:* A strong answer links the lump to holding gas. It says more than that wheat is stretchy.
13. (draw) Draw a plant cell with two chemicals stored in separate parts. Then draw the cell after a cut.  
   **Answer:** The first drawing shows a cell with two closed bags inside, each holding a different chemical. The second shows the cell cut open, with the two chemicals mixed and a new substance forming.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the chemicals apart before the cut and mixed after it. That mixing is what starts browning and the onion's gas.
14. (perspective) Plant breeders have bred onions that make very little tear gas. Should farmers grow them?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that the gas is the onion's defence, made only when cells are broken. Another is that chilling or a fan already cuts the tears without changing the onion.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses a fact about how the gas is made or what it does for the onion.
15. (mcq) Which gas do yeast give off as they feed on sugar?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide / Oxygen / Steam / Onion gas  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide. *Why:* Yeast turn sugar into carbon dioxide and a little alcohol. The carbon dioxide makes the bubbles in the dough.

   **Answer:** An enzyme changed a stored chemical. *Why:* Crushing and wetting bring a stored chemical and an enzyme together. The enzyme makes the sharp substance within about ten minutes.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Why does a cut brinjal turn brown?  
   Options: An enzyme uses oxygen from the air / The knife leaves rust on it / Germs rot it within minutes / The brinjal dries out  
   **Answer:** An enzyme uses oxygen from the air. *Why:* Cutting breaks the cells. An enzyme from the cells joins oxygen to chemicals in the juice, and the product is brown. If you chose germs, remember that rotting takes days.
2. (mcq) Which of these stops an apple slice from browning?  
   Options: Two minutes in boiling water / Leaving it in a sunny place / Cutting it into smaller pieces / Blowing air over it  
   **Answer:** Two minutes in boiling water. *Why:* Boiling water destroys the browning enzyme. Smaller pieces and more air both make browning faster, because more cells are broken and more oxygen arrives.
3. (mcq) Lemon juice keeps cut banana pale. Why?  
   Options: Sour juice slows the enzyme / Lemon juice is yellow / It makes the banana colder / It adds more oxygen  
   **Answer:** Sour juice slows the enzyme. *Why:* The browning enzyme works badly in sour juice. The vitamin C in lemon also undoes the first step of the browning.
4. (mcq) What does heat browning need?  
   Options: A dry surface hotter than boiling water / Broken cells and cold air / Yeast and warm water / A lid on the pan  
   **Answer:** A dry surface hotter than boiling water. *Why:* Sugars and proteins react fast only above about 140 degrees Celsius. A wet surface stays near 100 degrees Celsius, so it must dry first.
5. (mcq) Boiled rice never browns in the pot. Why?  
   Options: Boiling water stays at about 100 degrees / Rice has no sugar or protein / The lid keeps the air out / Rice is already cooked  
   **Answer:** Boiling water stays at about 100 degrees. *Why:* Rice has both starch and protein, and it browns well when fried. In boiling water it cannot get hot enough.
6. (mcq) Which food is puffed by steam alone?  
   Options: Puri / Pav / Dhokla / Idli  
   **Answer:** Puri. *Why:* A puri has no yeast and no soda. Water in the dough turns to steam in the hot oil. Pav uses yeast, dhokla uses soda, and idli batter is fermented.
7. (mcq) What do yeast need in order to make gas?  
   Options: Sugar, water and warmth / Salt and cold air / Baking soda and lemon / Very hot oil  
   **Answer:** Sugar, water and warmth. *Why:* Yeast are living beings. They feed on sugar in warm, wet dough and give off carbon dioxide. Cold slows them, and great heat kills them.
8. (mcq) Why can wheat dough hold gas bubbles?  
   Options: Its gluten stretches without tearing / Its starch is very sticky / It is heavier than rice dough / It holds more water  
   **Answer:** Its gluten stretches without tearing. *Why:* Gluten is a stretchy net of wheat proteins. It stretches around each bubble. Starch washes away in water and does not stretch.
9. (mcq) What is left when a ball of wheat dough is washed in water?  
   Options: A stretchy lump of gluten / A heap of white starch / Nothing at all / A lump of yeast  
   **Answer:** A stretchy lump of gluten. *Why:* The starch floats away and turns the water white. The gluten stays in the hand. If you chose nothing, that is what rice dough leaves.
10. (mcq) Where is the tear gas before an onion is cut?  
   Options: It has not been made yet / In the dry outer skin / In the middle of the bulb / In the roots  
   **Answer:** It has not been made yet. *Why:* A whole onion holds the makings of the gas in separate parts of each cell. The gas forms only after a cut mixes them.
11. (mcq) Why does a chilled onion sting less?  
   Options: Cold slows the enzymes / Cold freezes the tears / Cold onions have fewer cells / The knife becomes sharper  
   **Answer:** Cold slows the enzymes. *Why:* Enzymes work more slowly when they are cold, so less gas is made in the first minute. The gas also rises more slowly from a cold onion.
12. (mcq) In a fair test of three apple slices, what should be different?  
   Options: Only the dip each slice gets / The apple each slice came from / The thickness of each slice / The time you look at each  
   **Answer:** Only the dip each slice gets. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same. Then any difference in browning must come from the dip.

## Applications
- Tea factories in Assam, Darjeeling, the Nilgiris and Munnar roll tea leaves and let them brown to make black tea. They heat the leaves first to make green tea.
- Factories that freeze peas, beans and sweetcorn dip them in boiling water first. This destroys the enzymes, so the vegetables keep their colour for months.
- Makers of fruit juice add vitamin C, so that apple and guava juice do not turn brown in the packet.
- Bakers choose flour by its gluten. Flour with more gluten makes tall loaves of bread, and flour with less makes soft cakes and biscuits.
- Coffee roasters heat green coffee beans until they turn brown. The roasting makes the smell of coffee by heat browning.
- Plant breeders have bred onions that make very little tear gas, and apples whose slices stay pale.

## Spotted around us
- A fruit chaat seller squeezes lime over cut guava and banana. The sour juice keeps the fruit pale.
- A dosa turns golden on a huge black tawa at a tiffin stall. The idlis in the steamer beside it stay white.
- Trays of pale pav dough stand in a warm corner of a bakery. The dough puffs up before it goes into the oven.
- Luchi or puri puffs into a ball in hot oil at a sweet shop. Only steam is inside.
- Cooks at a wedding kitchen chop sacks of onions next to a big fan. The fan carries the gas away from their eyes.
- Sugarcane juice boils in a wide pan and turns brown as it thickens into jaggery.

## Open questions
- **Which substances give browned food its smell and taste?** The Maillard reaction makes hundreds of substances in one piece of toast. Chemists have named many of them, but nobody has a full list for any food.
- **Does the acrylamide in dark brown food harm people?** Large doses cause cancer in rats and mice. Studies of people who eat ordinary amounts have not given a clear answer.
- **What does the browning enzyme do for a plant?** It may protect wounds from microbes and insects, and it may have other jobs in the cell. Plants with the enzyme switched off still grow, so its use is not settled.
- **Did cooking change the bodies and brains of our ancestors?** The scientist Richard Wrangham argues that cooked food gave early humans more energy. Nobody yet knows how early our ancestors controlled fire, so the idea is debated.
- **Can a dough without gluten hold gas as well as wheat dough does?** Food scientists mix gums, starches and other proteins to copy gluten. No mixture yet stretches and holds gas quite as well.

## Connected maths
- In Hot Dip, Cold Dip, three apple slices were scored after 40 minutes. The scores were 3 for no dip, 2 for the cold dip and 0 for the hot dip. Which dip made the bigger difference? What does the cold-dip score tell you about wetting? **The hot dip made the bigger difference. It lowered the score by 3, and the cold dip lowered it by only 1. So wetting alone does very little.**. 3 take away 0 is 3 for the hot dip. 3 take away 2 is 1 for the cold dip. Both slices were wet, so the extra 2 points must come from the heat. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In Tear-Free Chopping, plain chopping stung after 15 seconds. With a fan it took 45 seconds, and with a chilled onion 30 seconds. A chilled onion with a fan reached 60 seconds, when the test stopped. How many times as long as plain chopping did the fan and the chilled onion last? Why can the last result not be compared fairly? **The fan lasted 3 times as long, and the chilled onion 2 times as long. The last test was stopped at 60 seconds, so its real time is unknown.**. 45 divided by 15 is 3. 30 divided by 15 is 2. The chilled onion with the fan lasted at least 4 times as long, because 60 divided by 15 is 4. It might have lasted much longer. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In Wash the Dough, 60 grams of maida left a lump of gluten that weighed 15 grams. What fraction of the flour's weight is that? How heavy a lump would 240 grams of the same flour leave? **One quarter. 240 grams of flour would leave about 60 grams.**. 15 out of 60 is the same as 1 out of 4. One quarter of 240 is 60. The lump is wet, so part of its weight is water. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- 1 millilitre of water makes about 1,600 millilitres of steam. A drop of water is about one twentieth of a millilitre. A puffed puri holds about 80 millilitres. Is one drop of water enough to fill the puri with steam? **Yes. One drop makes about 80 millilitres of steam, which just fills the puri.**. One twentieth of 1,600 is 1,600 divided by 20, which is 80. A puri's dough holds far more than one drop of water, so there is steam to spare. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- In Two Katori Dhoklas, both batters were 2 centimetres deep. After steaming, the one with fruit salt was 3.6 centimetres high and the plain one was 2.2 centimetres. How much did each one rise? How many times as much did the first one rise? **The one with fruit salt rose 1.6 centimetres, and the plain one rose 0.2 centimetres. The first rose 8 times as much.**. 3.6 take away 2 is 1.6. 2.2 take away 2 is 0.2. Eight lots of 0.2 make 1.6. The plain batter still rose a little, because air and steam swell in the heat. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In Puff or Flop, a dough has Gas 6 and Hold 2. One card adds 3 to Gas. Another card adds 3 to Hold. Which card gives more beans, and how many more? **The Hold card. It gives 5 beans, and the Gas card gives only 2. That is 3 more.**. The rise is the smaller of the two numbers. With the Gas card the numbers are 9 and 2, so the rise is 2. With the Hold card they are 6 and 5, so the rise is 5. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
