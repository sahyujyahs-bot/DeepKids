# Conductors and insulators: what lets electricity through, what holds it back, and why the answer can change
`sci.electricity.conductors-and-insulators.L4`

**Summary.** A steel spoon lights the bulb and a plastic ruler does not. But is a cup of water a conductor or an insulator? In this lesson you build two testers, a bulb and a small red light called an LED, and you find that they do not always agree. You learn what moves inside a wire and inside salt water, why your own body conducts, and why every plug, wire and switch is made of a conductor wrapped in an insulator. You also meet the things that sit in between, such as pencil lead, the chips in a phone, and the air that lightning breaks through.

**Intro.** *Look at any wire at home, without touching the socket. On the outside there is plastic. Inside there is metal. Every wire in every home in the world is made this way, from Kochi to Kohima. Why two materials? And here is a harder one: water is not a metal, so why does everyone say never to touch a switch with wet hands?* Some materials let electricity through easily. We call them conductors. Some hold it back so well that almost nothing gets through. We call them insulators. In this lesson you test water, salt, sugar, lemon, a pencil, a tomato and your own fingertip, using only AA cells. You find out what is really moving inside each one. You read a plug as a map of conductors and insulators, and you learn the reason behind each safety rule. Then you look again at the two boxes, conductor and insulator, and ask whether the world really comes in two boxes at all.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can build a circuit tester and use it in a fair test to rank materials by how well they conduct.
1. I can explain why metals and graphite conduct, and why plastic, rubber, dry wood and glass insulate, by saying which have charges that are free to move.
2. I can explain why tap water and salt water conduct, why very pure water hardly conducts, and what ions are.
3. I can explain why my body conducts, and why 230 volts with wet hands is dangerous when a 1.5 volt cell is not.
4. I can point to the conductors and the insulators in a plug, a wire, a switch and a torch, and say why each one is where it is.
5. I can explain why conductor and insulator are two ends of one long scale, and give examples that sit in between or change.
6. I can give the safety rules for electricity at home, with the reason for each one.
7. I can say what is still argued about or not yet known, and give two views fairly.

## Terms
- **Conductor**: A material that lets electric charge move through it easily. *e.g.* The copper inside a wire and the steel of a spoon are conductors.
- **Insulator**: A material that holds electric charge back so strongly that almost none gets through. *e.g.* The plastic coat on a wire is an insulator. It keeps the electricity in the copper and away from your hand.
- **Electron**: A very tiny particle with an electric charge. Every atom has electrons. In a metal, some of them are free to move from atom to atom. *e.g.* When you close a torch switch, the free electrons all along the wire start to drift together.
- **Ion**: An atom, or a small group of atoms, that carries an electric charge. Ions can move through water. *e.g.* Salt in water breaks into sodium ions and chloride ions. They carry the electricity through salt water.
- **Electric current**: A flow of electric charge. In a wire the moving charges are electrons. In water they are ions. *e.g.* A bigger current makes a bulb glow brighter.
- **Resistance**: How strongly a thing holds back the current. A good conductor has a small resistance. An insulator has a huge one. *e.g.* A long pencil lead has more resistance than a steel spoon, so the bulb glows dimmer with the pencil.
- **Circuit tester**: A circuit with one gap in it. You put a thing across the gap. If the lamp lights, the thing has let current through. *e.g.* Your bulb tester has two cells, a bulb and two free wire ends.
- **LED**: A small lamp made from a semiconductor. An LED lights with a far smaller current than a torch bulb needs, and only one way round. *e.g.* A red LED glows through a cup of tap water. A torch bulb stays dark.
- **Volt**: Volts measure how hard a cell or a socket pushes the charges. More volts means a harder push. *e.g.* One AA cell pushes with 1.5 volts. A wall socket in India pushes with 230 volts.
- **Mains electricity**: The electricity that comes to a home through wires from a power station. In India it pushes with 230 volts, which can kill. *e.g.* Fans, fridges and wet grinders run on mains electricity. Experiments never do.
- **Electric shock**: What happens when an electric current passes through a body. A big enough current can burn the body and stop the heart. *e.g.* A cracked plug can give a shock, because the insulator no longer covers the metal.
- **Earth wire**: A wire that joins the metal body of an appliance to the ground. If a fault makes the body live, the current takes the earth wire. *e.g.* The thick top pin of a three-pin plug is the earth pin.
- **Semiconductor**: A material that conducts a little, and whose conducting can be changed by heat, light, a push or tiny amounts of other atoms. *e.g.* Silicon is a semiconductor. The chips in a phone and the cells in a solar panel are made of it.
- **Superconductor**: A material that, when made cold enough, has no resistance at all. *e.g.* The magnets inside a hospital MRI scanner are coils of superconducting wire kept very cold.

## Explanations
### Conductors and insulators: are the charges free to move? · *defined*
**Definition:** A conductor is a material in which electric charges can move easily. An insulator is a material in which the charges are held so firmly that almost none can move.

Every material is made of atoms, and every atom holds charged particles. So every material is full of charge. What differs is whether any of that charge is free to move. In copper, some electrons are free and drift along when a cell pushes them. In plastic, every electron is held by its own atom. A push of 3 volts moves almost none of them. That is why a spoon across the gap of a tester lights the bulb and a ruler does not. Here is what we know so far, said honestly: no material is a perfect insulator. With a hard enough push, a little charge moves in anything.
- e.g. Conductors: copper, aluminium, steel, iron, silver, gold, and graphite, which is the dark part of a pencil.
- e.g. Insulators: plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood, dry cloth, dry paper, wax and dry air.
- e.g. Copper lets charge through more than a million million million times more easily than glass does.
- **School books and electricians:** Sort every material into two boxes, conductor or insulator. This is quick and it keeps people safe. An electrician needs to know at once what she may touch.
- **Materials scientists:** There are no two boxes. There is one long scale of resistance, from silver at one end to rubber and glass at the other. Sea water, pencil lead, silicon, wet wood and skin all sit somewhere along the scale. Where we draw the line depends on the job.
- **William Gilbert, a doctor in London, in 1600:** He also made two boxes, but different ones. Things that pulled dust when rubbed, such as amber and glass, he called electrics. Metals did not, so people thought metals had no electricity in them. Today we know metals are full of free charge.
### Why metals conduct: electrons that belong to the whole piece · *defined*
**Definition:** In a metal, each atom lets go of one or more of its outer electrons. These free electrons belong to the whole piece of metal and can move through it.

A copper wire is already full of free electrons before you join it to anything. The cell does not pour electrons into the wire. The cell pushes the ones that are already there, all at once, all round the loop. Each electron drifts slowly, less than a millimetre each second. But all of them start together, so the bulb lights at once. In plastic and rubber the electrons are locked into the bonds between atoms. Graphite is a surprise. Graphite is pure carbon, not a metal, yet each carbon atom has one electron free to move along its flat sheet. Diamond is pure carbon too, with every electron locked, and diamond is an insulator.
- e.g. A line of coins on a table, pushed at one end: the last coin moves at once, though each coin moves only a little.
- e.g. A pencil lead conducts. A diamond made of the same carbon atoms does not.
- e.g. Metals that conduct electricity well also conduct heat well, because the same free electrons carry both.
- **Paul Drude, a physicist in Germany, in 1900:** The free electrons are like a gas of tiny balls flying about between the atoms. A push makes the whole gas drift. This picture is simple, and it gives the right answer for many metals.
- **Felix Bloch in 1928 and Alan Wilson in 1931:** Electrons are not tiny balls. They behave as waves spread through the whole crystal, and only some energies are allowed, in bands. A metal has a band that is partly full, so its electrons can move. An insulator has full bands and a wide gap. This picture also explains semiconductors, which the gas picture cannot.
### Does water conduct? The ions do the carrying · *defined*
**Definition:** Water conducts when it holds ions, which are charged atoms from dissolved salts, minerals or acids. The ions move through the water and carry the current.

Water has no free electrons the way a metal has. Very pure water is a poor conductor. But water is very good at pulling salts apart into ions. Table salt in water splits into sodium ions with a plus charge and chloride ions with a minus charge. Put two wires in, and the plus ions drift one way and the minus ions drift the other. That movement is the current. More ions means more current, so each pinch of salt makes the LED brighter. Tap water has minerals from rock and soil, so it conducts a little. Sugar dissolves just as well as salt, but sugar stays as whole molecules with no charge, so it adds nothing. The water inside your body, in rivers, in the sea and on a wet bathroom floor is full of ions.
- e.g. A red LED glows faintly through tap water and brightly through salt water.
- e.g. Sea water conducts about a million times better than the purest water a laboratory can make.
- e.g. The small meter that a water-filter technician dips in your drinking water is a conductivity tester. It counts dissolved minerals by how well the water conducts.
- **Safety posters and electricity boards:** Water conducts electricity. Treat every drop as a conductor. For the water we meet in daily life this is the right rule, because that water always holds ions, and 230 volts pushes hard.
- **Chemists, after Svante Arrhenius in 1884 and Friedrich Kohlrausch in 1894:** Water itself is almost an insulator. The dissolved ions do the carrying. Kohlrausch made water purer than anyone had before and found that it conducted very little, though never zero. A few water molecules always split into ions by themselves.
- **Sharks, rays and other fishes of the sea:** Sea water is a conductor that carries news. Adrianus Kalmijn showed in 1971 that sharks find a hidden flatfish by the tiny electric signals her body sends through the water. For a shark, conducting water is a sense to hunt with.
### Your body conducts: why 230 volts and wet hands do not mix · *defined*
**Definition:** An electric shock is a current passing through a body. The body conducts because the water in every cell is full of ions.

Your body is more than half water, and that water is salty. Blood, sweat and tears all conduct. Dry skin is the body's own insulator. From hand to hand through dry skin, the resistance is about 100,000 ohms or more. Ohms are how resistance is counted. Wet skin can be 100 times easier to get through. A 1.5 volt cell pushes too gently to send a current you could notice through skin, wet or dry. A wall socket pushes with 230 volts, more than 150 times as hard. Through wet skin that is enough to stop a heart, because the heart beats on small electric signals of its own. So the rule is not that water is dangerous and dry is safe. Mains electricity is dangerous always. Water takes away the little protection that dry skin gives.
- e.g. Dry hands before touching any switch. Never use a hair dryer, phone charger or heater in a wet bathroom.
- e.g. A bird sits safely on one bare power line, because both feet are on the same wire and no current is pushed through her body.
- e.g. A phone screen works because your fingertip conducts. A thick glove on the finger does nothing.
### Conductors and insulators working as a team in every appliance · *defined*
**Definition:** An electrical appliance uses conductors to lead the current where it is wanted, and insulators to keep it away from everywhere else, above all from people.

Look at a plug that is out of the socket. The pins are brass, a conductor, because they must carry current in. The body is hard plastic, an insulator, because fingers hold it. Inside the cord are three copper wires, each in a plastic coat of a different colour, so the three never touch each other. A switch is a small metal bridge inside a plastic box. Press it, and the bridge closes the gap. An electric iron has a metal plate that must get hot, and a plastic handle that must not carry heat or current. The metal body of a mixer, a fridge or a wet grinder is joined to the earth wire. If a wire inside comes loose and touches the body, the current runs down the earth wire into the ground. That is a far easier path than a person. The big current trips a safety switch at the meter board.
- e.g. Power lines across the country are bare aluminium, held away from the steel towers by stacks of ceramic or glass discs. The air around them is the insulator.
- e.g. A line worker wears thick rubber gloves and uses tools with rubber handles.
- e.g. Inside an electric iron, thin sheets of mica, a mineral mined in Jharkhand and Andhra Pradesh, keep the hot wire from touching the metal plate.
### In between, and both: graphite, semiconductors and insulators that give way · *defined*
**Definition:** A semiconductor is a material that conducts a little, and whose conducting changes a great deal with heat, light, the size of the push, or a few added atoms.

Whether a thing conducts depends on more than what it is made of. It depends on the push. Dry air is a very good insulator, yet a push of about 30,000 volts across one centimetre tears electrons off the air's atoms. Then the air conducts, and we see a spark. Lightning is the same thing across a kilometre of sky. It depends on heat. Cold glass insulates. Red-hot glass conducts. It depends on water. Dry wood insulates, and a wet tree carries lightning to the ground. It depends on light. A solar panel conducts better when the Sun shines on it. And it depends on the tester. A torch bulb needs a large current to glow, so the bulb reports salt water as an insulator. An LED needs very little, so the LED reports it as a conductor. Silicon sits in the middle of the scale. By adding a few other atoms, engineers make it conduct exactly when they want. Every chip in a phone is billions of tiny silicon switches.
- e.g. A red LED is itself a semiconductor. It conducts one way round and not the other.
- e.g. In Kolkata in 1901, Jagadish Chandra Bose used a crystal of galena, a semiconductor, to detect radio waves. His American patent was granted in 1904.
- e.g. A pencil line on paper conducts a little. A thicker, darker, shorter line conducts more.
- **A school sorting table:** Each material has one right box. Graphite is a conductor, air is an insulator. This is true for a small push at room temperature, which is the only test most classrooms can do.
- **Physicists who study materials:** Conducting is not a fixed label on a material. It is how the material answers a question we put to it, and the answer changes with the push, the heat, the light, the wetness and the purity. One sheet of graphite, called graphene, is among the best conductors known. A stack of such sheets pressed with clay is a pencil lead.
### Superconductors: when resistance drops to nothing · *debated*
**Definition:** A superconductor is a material that carries a current with no resistance at all, once it is colder than its own special temperature.

Even copper wastes some energy as heat. In 1911 in Leiden, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes and his team cooled mercury to minus 269 degrees Celsius. Its resistance did not just get small. It dropped to nothing. A current started in a ring of superconductor has been watched for months and has not weakened. In 1986 Georg Bednorz and Alex Müller found that some ceramics superconduct at much higher temperatures. Ceramics are usually insulators, so this was a great surprise. Within months, a team led by Maw-Kuen Wu and Paul Chu found one that works at minus 180 degrees, which cheap liquid nitrogen can reach. Scientists agree on how the cold metals do it. They do not agree on how the ceramics do it.
- e.g. The magnets in hospital MRI scanners are superconducting coils cooled with liquid helium.
- e.g. A small magnet floats above a superconductor cooled in liquid nitrogen.
- e.g. If wires could superconduct at room temperature, almost no electricity would be lost between a power station and a home.
- **John Bardeen, Leon Cooper and Robert Schrieffer, in 1957:** In a cold metal the electrons join into pairs, helped by tiny shakes of the atoms around them. The pairs all move as one and nothing can slow just one of them. This theory fits the cold metals very well and won a Nobel Prize.
- **Philip Anderson, a physicist at Princeton, from 1987:** In the ceramics the pairs do not need the shaking atoms. The pairing comes from the electrons' own magnetism, in a new state of matter. He argued this until his death in 2020.
- **Douglas Scalapino, David Pines and others:** The pairs in the ceramics are glued by waves of magnetism that pass between the electrons. This is not the same as Anderson's idea, and experiments have not yet settled between them.
### Living conductors: nerves, electric senses and signalling plants · *debated*
**Definition:** Living cells carry electric signals by moving ions through the salty water inside and around them, and they use fatty layers as insulators.

Every living cell is wrapped in a skin of fat only two molecules thick. Fat is an insulator. In that skin are tiny gates that open and shut to let ions through. So every cell is a small bag of conducting water inside an insulating coat with switches in it. A nerve is a long cell who passes a signal along by opening gate after gate. Many nerves are wrapped in extra layers of a fatty insulator called myelin, much like the plastic on a wire. With that wrap a signal can travel about 100 metres in a second. Plants carry electric signals too. Jagadish Chandra Bose measured them in Kolkata more than a hundred years ago. A touch-me-not folds her leaves after an electric signal runs down the stalk. Plants sense, signal and decide. Some scientists call these automatic responses.
- e.g. Bumblebees can sense the weak electric charge on a flower, and learn from that charge which flowers to visit (Dominic Clarke and others, 2013).
- e.g. An electric eel stacks thousands of flat cells in a row to make a push of up to 860 volts.
- e.g. A Venus flytrap shuts after two touches close together, each touch sending an electric signal.
- **František Baluška, Stefano Mancuso and others, from 2006:** Plants pass electric signals from cell to cell, weigh many signals and choose what to do. This is so like what nerves do that the study deserves the name plant neurobiology, and the activity deserves the name thinking.
- **Amedeo Alpi and 35 other plant scientists, in 2007, and Lincoln Taiz in 2019:** Plants do signal with electricity, and that is wonderful enough. But the signals do not run through nerve cells or meet in one gathering place, so words such as nerve and mind mislead. The signals are better understood as chemistry and growth.
- **Michael Levin, a biologist at Tufts University:** All cells, not only nerves, talk to each other with patterns of voltage. In his laboratory, changing these patterns made flatworms grow a head at each end. He argues that bodies use electricity to remember and decide what shape to be.

## Mostly agreed
- Copper and glass differ in how easily charge moves through them by more than a million million million times. No other everyday property of materials has so wide a range. *How we know:* Laboratories measure the resistance of a bar of each material with meters. The numbers have been checked for more than a century and are printed in standard tables.
- When a current passes through salt water, new substances collect at the two wires. This shows that matter itself, the ions, is moving through the water. *How we know:* Michael Faraday measured this in London in 1834 and gave us the word ion. You can see small bubbles form on the wire ends in the Water Test.
- Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. One is an insulator and the other a conductor, so the way atoms are joined matters as much as which atoms they are. *How we know:* In 1797 Smithson Tennant burned a diamond and got exactly the same gas, in the same amount, as from burning the same weight of charcoal.
- Wet skin lets through about 100 times more current than dry skin does, for the same push. *How we know:* Safety engineers measured the resistance of many people's bodies, dry and wet. The results are the basis of the world standard on electric shock, IEC 60479.
- A current in a ring of superconductor does not fade. *How we know:* In 1963 J. File and R. G. Mills watched such a current closely. From the tiny change they could measure, they worked out that the current would last at least 100,000 years.

## How we found out
**William Gilbert, Stephen Gray and Granville Wheler in England, Charles du Fay in Paris, and John Desaguliers in London, 1600 (Gilbert's list), 1729 (Gray's thread), 1733 (du Fay) and 1742 (the two names).** For a long time people sorted materials by rubbing. In 1600 William Gilbert, a doctor in London, listed the things that pulled dust and straw after rubbing: amber, glass, wax. He called them electrics. Metals did nothing when rubbed, so people believed metals had no electricity in them. In 1729 Stephen Gray, a cloth dyer living on charity in London, rubbed a glass tube and found that the pull travelled along a damp hemp thread. With his friend Granville Wheler he sent it 765 feet across a garden. It worked only when the thread hung from silk. When they hung it from brass wire, the pull was lost. So the metal had carried it away. Metals were the best carriers of all. They had never held a charge when rubbed only because it ran off through the hand. In 1733 Charles du Fay in Paris charged metal, and himself, while hanging from silk cords. By 1742 John Desaguliers had given the two kinds their names: conductors and insulators.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that metals had no electricity in them, because a rubbed metal rod pulled nothing. Gray's thread showed the opposite. Metals carry electricity so well that the charge had been running away through the hand that held them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Electricity always takes the path of least resistance.** (A very common saying, also heard from some electricians.; *rejected*)  
  For: When there are two paths, most of the current does go through the easier one. This is why an earth wire protects a person.  
  Against: Current takes every path open to it at once, and shares itself out. The easier path gets more, but the harder path still gets some. A person touching a live wire gets a shock even when an easier path exists nearby.
- **Pure water does not conduct electricity at all.** (Many books that correct the saying that water is a conductor.; *rejected*)  
  For: Very pure water conducts about a million times worse than sea water. In a classroom test with a bulb, it looks like a perfect insulator.  
  Against: Friedrich Kohlrausch and Adolf Heydweiller measured the purest water they could make in 1894. It still conducted a little. About two water molecules in every thousand million split into ions by themselves. And pure water does not stay pure. It takes in gas from the air and salts from any cup or hand within minutes.
- **Rubber chappals or rubber tyres keep a person safe from electric shock and lightning.** (A belief held by many people in many countries.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rubber is a very good insulator. Line workers do wear rubber gloves and boots, tested for the job.  
  Against: A chappal sole is thin, often wet and dirty, and not made or tested for 230 volts. Lightning has just crossed a kilometre of air, so a centimetre of rubber is nothing to it. A car is fairly safe in a storm because of the metal body, which leads the current round the people inside.
- **A material can be made that superconducts at room temperature and ordinary pressure.** (Many physicists. Vitaly Ginzburg, who won a Nobel Prize in 2003, argued for decades that no law of nature forbids it.; *debated*)  
  For: The record temperature keeps rising. In 2019 two teams, one including Maddury Somayazulu, found a compound of lanthanum and hydrogen that superconducts at about minus 23 degrees Celsius, though only when squeezed harder than the deepest rock inside the Earth.  
  Against: Every claim for room temperature so far has failed when other laboratories checked it. A paper from 2020 was withdrawn in 2022. A material called LK-99, announced in 2023, turned out not to superconduct. Some physicists think the atoms cannot hold electron pairs together when they are that warm, without huge pressure.
- **Electric signals between ordinary cells, not only nerves, store memories and make decisions about the shape of a body.** (Michael Levin and his laboratory at Tufts University.; *debated*)  
  For: Changing the voltage pattern in a cut flatworm made her grow two heads, and the change lasted when she was cut again, with no change to her genes (Durant and others, 2017). Changing voltage in a tadpole's body made an eye grow on the gut (Pai and others, 2012).  
  Against: Many biologists say the voltage is one signal among many, switched on by genes and chemicals, and that words like memory and decision claim too much. The work has been done in a few kinds of animals so far.

## Roots
You have asked water, a pencil, a tomato and your own fingertip the same question with two testers, and the testers did not always agree. Now we ask what that says about the boxes we sort the world into, and about where you end.

**Deep truth.** In the Water Test, the torch bulb stayed dark in salt water and the LED glowed. One tester said insulator and the other said conductor, about the same cup. The water had not changed. So conductor and insulator are not two kinds of matter. They are two answers, and the answer depends on how hard we push and how closely we look.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things into pairs of boxes, conductor or insulator, safe or dangerous, me or not me, and then treat the boxes as if nature had made them. This lesson's testers show the boxes were made by the question we asked.  
*Evidence:* The same salt water left a bulb dark and lit an LED. Each pinch of salt raised the glow by a step, with no jump. Dry paper was dark and the same paper wet was not. Air insulates until the push reaches 30,000 volts for each centimetre.

- *who am i:* You are salty water inside a thin skin. The skin holds back a small push and gives way to a large one. Every nerve in you is a conductor wrapped in a fatty insulator, carrying signals as moving ions. So your edge is not one fixed wall. Whether a thing is outside you or passing through you depends on what is pushing.
- *what is life:* Every living cell is conducting water inside an insulating coat with tiny gates in it. A cell lives by choosing, moment by moment, which ions may cross. A touch-me-not plant, a shark and a bumblebee all send or sense electric signals. Life did not wait for wires. Life is made of conductors and insulators that open and close.
- *what is this world:* The same carbon atoms make diamond, which insulates, and graphite, which conducts. The same air insulates all day and conducts for one moment as lightning. Charge is in everything. Whether the charge moves depends on how the atoms are joined, and on the push, the heat and the wetness. The world is not divided into the stuff that carries and the stuff that blocks.

Every material is full of charge. In some the charge is free, in some it is held, and in most it is held only up to a point. A cell, a socket and a thundercloud are three sizes of the same push, and a material that says no to one may say yes to the next. So the two words in the title of this lesson describe a meeting between a material and a push, not a material alone. Consciousness is the ground from which all of this arises: the copper and the plastic, the push and the one who is pushed. From that ground, the line between what conducts and what does not is one of many lines that thought draws for its own use.

- **Isha Upanishad, verse 5:** “तदेजति तन्नैजति तद्दूरे तद्वन्तिके / तदन्तरस्य सर्वस्य तदु सर्वस्यास्य बाह्यतः” It moves, and it does not move. It is far, and it is near. It is inside all this, and it is outside all this. (The sages who composed this verse refused to put the deepest reality into one box of a pair. The salt water in your cup would not go into one box either: it conducted for the LED and did not conduct for the bulb.)
- **Umasvati, Tattvartha Sutra 1.6:** “प्रमाणनयैरधिगमः” Knowledge is gained through the means of knowing and through standpoints. The Jain teachers built on this to say that every statement about a thing is true from some standpoint, and should be said with the words 'in some respect'. (In some respect salt water is a conductor, and in some respect it is not. The bulb and the LED are two standpoints. A Jain teacher would say that neither tester lied, and neither told the whole of it.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2, 'On Making Things Equal':** Everything can be called 'that', and everything can be called 'this'. 'That' comes out of 'this', and 'this' depends on 'that'. The sage does not go by such divisions, and sees things in the light of the whole. (Zhuangzi, writing in China about 2,300 years ago, taught that the two sides of any division are made together. We could not have named insulators without conductors, and the pencil and the tomato sit on the line between.)
- Science (settled): Materials differ in how easily charge moves through them along one unbroken scale covering more than twenty powers of ten. Where the words conductor and insulator divide that scale is a human choice.
- Science (settled): Whether a material conducts depends on the push, the temperature, light, water and purity. Air, glass and silicon each change from insulating to conducting.
- Science (debated): Cells of every kind, not only nerves, may use patterns of voltage to remember and decide. Michael Levin's experiments support this, and many biologists read them differently.
- Science (open): How moving ions in a brain become a felt experience is not known.
- Other view, The Vaisheshika school of Kanada, and many physicists and engineers today: Qualities really belong to substances. Copper has a conductivity that can be measured to many figures, and it is the same in every laboratory on Earth. The difference between copper and rubber is as real as anything in nature. That our lamps have thresholds says something about lamps, and nothing deep about the world or about the self. The boxes are rough, but what they point to was not made up by us.
- Practice, The edge of the hand (3 min): Sit and rest one hand on a steel tumbler and the other on a wooden table. Close your eyes. Notice that the steel seems cold and the wood seems warm, though both are as warm as the room. The steel is carrying your warmth away, and the wood is not. Breathe slowly five times. With each breath, ask where your hand ends and the tumbler begins.
- Ask: The bulb and the LED gave different answers about the same water. Was one of them wrong, or were they answering different questions?
- Ask: A small push stops at your skin and a large push goes through. So is your skin the edge of you?
- Ask: Can you think of any two boxes that have nothing at all in between them?
- Ask: If the boxes come from our questions, what was there before anyone asked?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The bulb said salt water is an insulator. The LED said it is a conductor. Which tester told the truth? (hint: Think about what each tester needs before it can glow. Is a tester's answer about the water, or about the water and the tester together?)
- *deep:* If no material is a perfect insulator and none is a perfect conductor at room temperature, why do we keep the two words? (hint: Think about who uses the words, and for what job. When is a rough word more useful than an exact number?)
- *deep:* Diamond and pencil lead are made of the same atoms. So where does being a conductor come from? (hint: Think of the same bricks built into a wall and into a bridge. What is different, if not the bricks?)
- *big:* A wire is copper wrapped in plastic. A nerve is salty water wrapped in fat. Did humans invent the wire, or copy it? (hint: Think about which came first, and whether an inventor has to know that she is copying.)
- *big:* Your skin is the line where the conducting inside of you meets the insulating air. Is that line where you end? (hint: Think about a phone screen that senses your finger, a shark who senses a hidden swimmer through water, and a hand held near a hot tawa.)
- *big:* The same rule about water, ions and danger is true in every home on Earth. Yet safe wiring and earthing are not in every home. Why not? (hint: Think about who builds houses, who checks them, what it costs, and who is asked.)
- *big:* Is there anything in the world that belongs fully in one box, with no in-between? (hint: Try some pairs: hot and cold, alive and not alive, tall and short, day and night. Look for where the line would go.)

## Story: The Tingle in the Grinder
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Only a trained electrician tests house wiring. If any appliance gives a tingle, switch off at the main switch and call one.*

It had rained on Thanjavur for three days. In the last house on the lane, the wall behind the kitchen shelf had grown a dark damp patch.

Imran, who was eight, was helping his grandfather grind the batter for tomorrow's idlis. Nana poured the soaked rice into the wet grinder. Imran put his palm on the steel drum to feel it hum.

He pulled his hand back fast. "Nana! It bit me!" It was not quite pain. It was a buzzing tingle, like ants running up his fingers.

Nana dried his hands on his towel and turned the grinder off at the wall switch. "Nobody touches it," he said. "Go and call Vasuki."

Vasuki was the electrician who lived three doors down. Her niece Ilakkiya, who was ten, spent every holiday carrying her tool bag. The two of them came through the rain under one umbrella.

Vasuki did not go to the grinder. She went to the meter board by the front door and pushed the main switch down. The fan above them slowed and stopped. "Now the whole house is asleep," she said. "Now we can look."

She pulled out the grinder's plug and held it up. It had two pins. "Where is the third?"

Nana looked at his feet. "The old plug broke. The shop had only this kind."

Ilakkiya put the plug in Imran's hand. "Three wires should come out of a grinder," she said. "Two carry the current in and out. The third is joined to the steel drum. It goes to the thick top pin, and from there down a wire into the ground under the house. It does nothing at all, every day, for years. It waits."

"Waits for what?"

"For today."

Vasuki opened the base of the grinder. Rainwater from the damp wall had crept along the cord. Where the cord bent, the old plastic coat had cracked, and a hair of copper showed through. The copper touched nothing. But it was wet, and the wet ran on to the steel drum.

"But water is not metal," said Imran.

Ilakkiya had been hoping he would say that. From the tool bag she took two small cells, a red LED no bigger than a lentil, and two wires. "Bring me a tumbler of drinking water and the salt."

She dipped the two wire ends into the water. The LED showed the faintest red. Imran had to cup his hands around it to see. "Now one pinch," she said. He dropped it in. The red grew. "One more." Now it shone like a dot of kumkum.

"The water is not the wire," said Ilakkiya. "The salt is. Bits of salt too small to see, each one carrying a charge, swimming from one wire to the other. Water that has run down a wall has picked up salts from the bricks. And now lick the back of your hand."

Imran licked it. "Salty."

"You are a tumbler of salt water with a skin on."

Imran thought about that. "So the electricity went from the cracked wire to the water, from the water to the drum, from the drum to me. And then where?"

"Through you. Out through your feet, into the damp floor, into the ground, and back along the ground to the big grey transformer at the end of the street. For half a second you were a piece of the circuit."

Imran looked at his hand as if it belonged to somebody else. "Why was it only a tingle?"

Vasuki answered without looking up. "Your feet were on Nana's rubber mat. The wet path was long and thin. Your hand was dry. Each of those held back most of the current. Most, not all. That was luck, and I do not like luck."

She cut away the cracked length of cord and fitted a plug with three pins. She tested the socket to make sure its earth wire truly reached the ground. Only then did she push the main switch up. The fan began to turn.

She held a small screwdriver with a tiny lamp in its handle against the steel drum. The lamp stayed dark. "An hour ago it would have glowed," she said.

Ilakkiya leaned close to Imran. "That tester glows only when a tiny current goes through Chithi herself. In through the tip, out through her thumb on the metal cap. It is far too small to notice. She is part of the circuit on purpose."

"On purpose?"

"Inside the handle is a part that holds back almost all of the current. Almost all. Nothing holds back all of it."

That night the idlis were soft, and Nana gave Ilakkiya four. Imran lay awake afterwards, following the path in his head. The cracked wire, the water, the drum, his hand, his feet, the floor, the ground, the street. He had always thought of his skin as the edge of himself, where he stopped and the world began. Today, for half a second, electricity had not seen an edge there at all. He wondered where else his edge was not really an edge.

**For the parent:** Water carries a current because of the ions dissolved in it, and a body is salty water inside a thin skin. An insulator holds back almost all of the current and never quite all, so safety comes from keeping every insulator whole and dry, and from giving the current a far easier path to the ground.

- Ask: An earth wire can do nothing for years. What is it for, and how would you know if yours was missing?
- Ask: A tiny current is safe and a larger one can kill. Who should decide how small is small enough, and how could they find out?
- Ask: Where does a circuit end: at the plug, at the wall, at the transformer in the street, or somewhere else?

## Games
### g1 · Safe Cable: a card game of conductors, insulators and it depends (20 min)
- **rules:** The aim is to build a safe cable: four conductor cards in a row, with two insulator cards laid over them as the cover.; Shuffle the cards and put the pile on the table with the fronts up. Nobody looks at the backs.; On your turn, take the top card. Say 'conductor', 'insulator' or 'it depends', and give your reason. If you say 'it depends', say what it depends on.; The player on your left turns the card over and reads the back aloud. If your box was right, you keep the card. If not, the card goes to the bottom of the pile.; Add a conductor card to your row and an insulator card to your cover. An 'it depends' card is wild. You may use it as a conductor or as an insulator.; If you win a card your cable does not need, give it to any player who needs it.; The first player to finish a safe cable wins. If the pile runs out first, the player with the most cards in a cable wins. Equal numbers share the win.; Playing alone: count how many turns you need to finish a safe cable. Next time, try to do it in fewer.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a thing on the front, and its box with the reason on the back:** The copper inside an electric cord (back: Conductor. Copper has free electrons, and very little holds them back.); A steel spoon (back: Conductor. Steel is a metal, with electrons free to move through the whole spoon.); Kitchen foil (back: Conductor. The foil is aluminium, the same metal as the big power lines across the country.); A five-rupee coin (back: Conductor. Coins are made of metal.); The lead of a pencil, touched at both ends (back: Conductor. The lead is graphite, a form of carbon with one free electron on each atom. It conducts less well than a metal.); A bucket of sea water (back: Conductor. Sea water is full of ions from dissolved salt.); A gold bangle (back: Conductor. Gold is one of the best. The tiny contacts inside a phone are coated with it.); The brass pins of a plug (back: Conductor. The pins must carry the current from the socket into the cord.); An iron nail (back: Conductor. Iron is a metal.); A tumbler of water with two spoons of salt stirred in (back: Conductor. The salt has broken into ions, which carry the current. A torch bulb may still stay dark, but an LED glows.); The plastic coat on a wire (back: Insulator. Every electron in the plastic is held in place. The coat keeps the current in the copper.); A line worker's thick rubber glove, dry and tested (back: Insulator. Rubber holds its electrons firmly. The glove is made and checked for this job.); A dry wooden spoon (back: Insulator. Dry wood has almost no free charges.); A glass marble at room temperature (back: Insulator. Cold glass holds its charges in place.); A dry cotton towel (back: Insulator. Dry cotton has no free charges.); A ceramic disc on a power-line tower (back: Insulator. The discs hold the bare live wire away from the steel tower.); The plastic cover of a wall switch (back: Insulator. The cover stands between your finger and the live metal behind.); Candle wax (back: Insulator. Wax was one of the first insulators people found.); A tumbler of tap water (back: It depends on the push and on the minerals in the water. A 3 volt bulb tester stays dark. An LED glows faintly. At 230 volts, tap water carries a deadly current.); The air in this room (back: It depends on the push. Air insulates very well, until the push reaches about 30,000 volts across each centimetre. Then the air conducts, as a spark or as lightning.); A branch of a tree (back: It depends on water. A dead dry branch insulates. A living branch full of sap, or any wet branch, conducts.); Your fingertip (back: It depends on the push and on wetness. At 3 volts, dry skin lets almost nothing through. Wet skin at 230 volts lets through enough to kill.); A kite string near a power line (back: It depends on what the string is made of and whether it is wet. Dry cotton insulates. Wet string or string coated with metal conducts. Never fly a kite near power lines.); A piece of silicon from a computer chip (back: It depends on what the engineers ask of it. Silicon is a semiconductor. A tiny push from a neighbouring part switches it between conducting and not conducting.)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and shuffle them. Put the pile front side up. Read the rules together and show what a finished safe cable looks like, using six blank scraps of paper.  
  *Parent says:* A cable needs both kinds. Four conductors carry the current. Two insulators keep it in. You win a card by naming its box and giving a reason.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Shuffle them. Fronts up. A safe cable needs four conductors and two insulators.
  - Note: Cards are printed two-sided. If you cannot print two-sided, write each back by hand, or keep the list of backs with one player as the checker.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first two rounds slowly. After each card, the reader reads the whole back aloud. Talk about the reason, not only the box.  
  *Parent says:* Take the top card. Which box, and why? Now let us hear the back. Did your reason match?  
  *Child mode:* Take a card. Say the box and your reason. Listen to the back.
  - Ask: What do all the conductor cards so far have inside them? (good: Charges that are free to move.; Free electrons, or ions.; Something that can carry the current.)
  - Note: A right box with a weak reason still wins the card. Ask for a better reason next time. If a child argues well that a card on the back is wrong, listen: some of these are worth arguing about.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play on until someone finishes a safe cable or the pile runs out. Each time an 'it depends' card comes up, the player must say what it depends on before the back is read.  
  *Parent says:* Keep playing. An 'it depends' card can be either kind, so it is the most useful card. But first you must say what it depends on.  
  *Child mode:* Keep playing. For 'it depends', say what it depends on. First safe cable wins.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay all 24 cards out in one long line, from the best conductor to the best insulator. Move cards until everyone agrees, or agrees to disagree.  
  *Parent says:* The game used two boxes and a wild card. Now forget the boxes. Put every card in one line, from conducts best to conducts least. Where is it hard to choose?  
  *Child mode:* Lay all the cards in one line. Best conductor to best insulator.
  - Note: There is no exact right order for the 'it depends' cards, and that is the point. Ask where they would move if they got wet, or if the push were 230 volts.
  - Think: Why was the wild card the most useful card in the game?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Water Test: which water lights the LED? (42 min)
- **question:** Does water let electricity through? Does it matter what is dissolved in the water, and does it matter which tester you ask?
- **predict:** Before you build anything, look at the five cups. For each one, guess how brightly the LED will glow, from 0 to 3. Give a reason for each guess. Will sugar water and salt water be the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Only what is in the cup changes. Every cup has the same amount of water. The two wire ends are fixed one finger-width apart on a card, and dip to the same depth each time. The room stays equally dim. Rinse and wipe the wire ends before each new cup, so no salt is carried across. Test from the plainest water to the saltiest. Dip three times in each cup, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Use only AA cells. Never test water with anything that is joined to a wall socket. Do not drink any water that the wires have been in, because a little metal dissolves into it. Pour it down the sink and wash the cups.
- **record:** Write the three glow scores for each cup. Then draw a bar for each cup, as tall as its usual score. Under the bars, write one line: what I thought, what I saw, and why I think it happened.
- **results table:** Cup | What is in the water | My guess (0 to 3) | Glow, dip 1 | Glow, dip 2 | Glow, dip 3
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set out the five empty cups and the water, salt, sugar and lemon. Say what will go in each cup. Ask for a guess for each cup, from 0 for no glow to 3 for bright. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will ask five kinds of water one question. Will you let electricity through? Guess a score for each cup. Tell me your reason for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. How bright will the LED be in each cup? Say why.
  - Ask: Will sugar water and salt water score the same? Why? (good: Yes, both dissolve.; No, salt is different from sugar.; I think water alone is enough.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Many children, and many adults, expect all water to conduct equally. With 2 to 4 children, each writes a guess before anyone speaks.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Join one wire from the plus end of the cell holder to the long leg of the LED. Join a second wire to the short leg. Join a third wire to the minus end. Tape the two free ends to the card, one finger-width apart. Leave two finger-widths of bare metal below the card.  
  *Parent says:* Now we build the tester. The long leg of the LED goes towards the plus end. To check it, touch the two free ends together for one second only. Does the LED light?  
  *Child mode:* Build the tester. Long leg to plus. Touch the two ends together for one second. Does the LED light?
  - Note: If the LED stays dark, turn the LED round, because an LED works one way only. Press every joint firmly. Do not hold the two ends together for longer than a second: with nothing in the gap, too much current flows and the LED can burn out. That is why there is a spare. If the wire ends have less than two finger-widths of bare metal, clip or twist each one onto a straightened steel paper clip. The metal is bare, not pointed, but mind eyes with younger children.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Label the cups A to E. Pour half a small tumbler of drinking water into A, C, D and E. Pour the same amount of tap water into B. Stir one level teaspoon of sugar into C. Squeeze one teaspoon of lemon juice into D. Stir one level teaspoon of salt into E.  
  *Parent says:* Every cup gets the same amount of water. A is plain drinking water. B is tap water. C gets sugar, D gets lemon and E gets salt. Stir until you cannot see the sugar or the salt.  
  *Child mode:* Fill five cups the same. A is plain. B is tap water. C sugar, D lemon, E salt.
  - Note: Half a small tumbler is about 100 millilitres. Filtered or bottled drinking water is best for A, C, D and E, because it holds fewer minerals than tap water. If you have only tap water, use it for all five and leave cup B out. Rinse the spoon between cups.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Dim the room. Rest the card on cup A so both wire ends are in the water. Count to five and score the glow. Lift, and dip twice more. Rinse the ends in the mug and wipe them. Do the same for B, C, D and E, in that order.  
  *Parent says:* Dip, count to five and look closely. Cup your hand round the LED. Zero is no glow, one is faint, two is clear, three is bright. Rinse and wipe before the next cup.  
  *Child mode:* Dip in cup A. Count to five. Score the glow. Rinse and wipe. Now B, C, D and E.
  - Ask: Which cup surprised you most? (good: Sugar did nothing.; Plain water hardly glowed.; Lemon worked without salt.)
  - Note: Typical results: A gives no glow or the faintest glow. B is faint. C is the same as A. D is clear. E is bright. Tap water differs from city to city: hard borewell water glows more than soft river water. If even cup E gives nothing, turn the LED round, press the joints, and move the wire ends closer without letting them touch. If it still fails, tape a third cell into the row and test all five cups again with three cells. Small bubbles on a wire are normal.
  - Check: Sugar water scored the same as plain water. Salt water scored much higher. What does that tell you? → **Dissolving is not enough. The salt adds something sugar does not**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Go back to cup A. Add one pinch of salt, stir ten times, dip and score. Add a second, third and fourth pinch, scoring after each one.  
  *Parent says:* Now change cup A a little at a time. One pinch of salt, stir, dip and score. Then another. What happens to the glow with each pinch?  
  *Child mode:* Add one pinch of salt to cup A. Stir. Dip and score. Do it four times.
  - Ask: What pattern do you see? (good: More salt, more glow.; Each pinch makes it brighter.; It goes up in steps, with no sudden jump.)
  - Note: The glow grows step by step. There is no pinch at which the water suddenly turns from an insulator into a conductor. Let your child notice this and say it in their own words.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the LED out of the tester and put the torch bulb in its place. Dip the ends into cup E, the saltiest. Look at the bulb. Then touch the two ends together to check that the bulb works.  
  *Parent says:* Guess first. The LED was bright in cup E. Will the torch bulb light too? Now try it. Then touch the ends together.  
  *Child mode:* Swap the LED for the torch bulb. Dip in cup E. Does the bulb light?
  - Ask: The LED says yes and the bulb says no. Is salt water a conductor? (good: It conducts a little.; It depends on the tester.; It is in between.)
  - Note: The bulb stays dark in salt water and lights when the ends touch. A torch bulb needs about a hundred times more current than an LED before it glows. Do not settle the question for your child. This disagreement is the heart of the lesson.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to write three lines: what I thought, what I saw, and why. Then take the cells out of the holder. Pour every cup down the sink and wash the cups.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Then we pour the water away, because nobody should drink it.  
  *Child mode:* Write what you thought, what you saw, and why. Pour the water away. Do not drink it.
  - Note: A strong answer says that plain water let very little through, that salt and lemon added something that carries current, and that sugar did not. After your child has said this, give the word ions. Then say what this test cannot tell us: it cannot show the ions themselves, and it cannot say that cup A lets nothing through. A more sensitive tester might still find a tiny current.
  - Think: If a better tester found a tiny current even in the purest water, would any water be an insulator?
### e2 · Two Testers: when the bulb says no, does the LED agree? (30 min)
- **question:** Do a bulb tester and an LED tester sort the same ten things into the same boxes?
- **predict:** Put the ten things into three piles before you test. Pile one: it will light the bulb. Pile two: it will light only the LED. Pile three: it will light neither. Give a reason for where you put the pencil, the tomato and your fingertip.
- **fair test:** Use the same two cells and the same wires for every thing. Press the two wire ends on firmly, one finger-width apart, each time. Test in the same dim room. Test each thing twice. Always use the bulb first. Use the LED only on things that left the bulb dark, because a good conductor lets through enough current to burn out an LED.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw one long line across a page. Write 'conducts best' at one end and 'conducts least' at the other. Place all ten things along the line where you think they belong.
- **results table:** Thing | My pile (1, 2 or 3) | Bulb: bright, dim or dark | LED: bright, faint or dark | What I would call it
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay out the ten things: spoon, coin, foil, pencil, ruler, eraser, dry stick, tomato slice, dry newspaper strip and a fingertip. Ask your child to sort them into three piles by guessing. Write the pile number for each in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Pile one will light the bulb. Pile two will light only the LED, which needs far less current. Pile three will light neither. Where does each thing go? Tell me why.  
  *Child mode:* Sort ten things into three piles. Bulb, only LED, or neither. Say why.
  - Note: Do not correct the piles. The pencil, the tomato and the fingertip are the interesting ones. A fingertip cannot go in a pile, so write its number in the table.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the torch bulb in the tester. Press the two wire ends onto each thing in turn, one finger-width apart. For the pencil, press one end on each tip of the lead. For the tomato, push both ends into the slice. Write bright, dim or dark.  
  *Parent says:* The bulb goes first, every time. Press both ends on firmly. Is the bulb bright, dim or dark? Test each thing twice.  
  *Child mode:* Test each thing with the bulb. Press both wire ends on. Bright, dim or dark?
  - Ask: Is the pencil like the spoon, like the ruler, or like neither? (good: Neither. It is dimmer than the spoon.; It is in between.; It lets some through.)
  - Note: Typical results: spoon, coin and foil are bright. A whole pencil is dim or dark, and a short pencil stub is brighter. Everything else is dark. A 3 volt tester on a fingertip is harmless and gives no sensation. If the spoon gives nothing, the bulb is loose or a joint is not touching.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the LED in place of the bulb, long leg towards plus. Test only the things that left the bulb dark. Never test the spoon, coin or foil with the LED. Write bright, faint or dark.  
  *Parent says:* Now ask again with the LED, but only where the bulb was dark. The spoon, coin and foil would burn the LED out. Dim the room and look closely.  
  *Child mode:* Swap in the LED. Test only the things that left the bulb dark.
  - Note: Typical results: the pencil is bright. The tomato or potato is faint to clear. Ruler, eraser, dry stick and dry paper are dark. A dry fingertip is usually dark, though a laboratory meter would find a tiny current through it. A dark LED does not prove that nothing at all gets through.
  - Check: The bulb stayed dark on the tomato slice. The LED glowed. What is the best conclusion? → **The tomato lets a small current through**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the dry newspaper strip with the LED. Then dip the strip in the saucer of salt water and test it again. Lick a fingertip, press both wire ends on it close together, and look once more.  
  *Parent says:* The paper was dark when dry. Guess what the LED will do when the same paper is wet with salt water. Now try it.  
  *Child mode:* Test dry paper. Wet it with salt water. Test again. What changed?
  - Note: The wet strip usually gives a faint to clear glow. The paper is the same paper. Only the water and the ions in it are new. A licked fingertip may give the faintest glow, or none.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then ask them to place all ten things along one line, from conducts best to conducts least.  
  *Parent says:* You made three piles at the start. Would you still use three piles? Put the ten things in one line, from the best conductor to the worst.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why. Put the ten things in one line.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the two testers disagreed on some things, and that the things fall along a line, not into two boxes. After your child has said this, explain that a bulb needs a large current and an LED a small one. Then say what the test cannot show: where the ruler and the eraser sit on the line, because neither tester is sensitive enough.
  - Think: If you had a tester a million times more sensitive than the LED, which pile would shrink?

### a1 · Coin Train: charges that are free and charges that are held (12 min)

  *Parent does:* Lay two rulers side by side, a coin's width apart. Put ten coins in the track, touching. Ask what will happen at the far end if one more coin is pushed in. Then push it in slowly.

  - Note: Nothing moves. Plastic and rubber are like this: every electron is held by its own atom. A much harder push would tear the tape. That is like a spark breaking through an insulator.
- **s3 (4 min)**

  *Child mode:* Which train is copper? Which is plastic? Where is the model wrong?
  - Note: Good answers: real electrons push each other without touching. A wire is a loop, so nothing falls off the end. In salt water two kinds of charge move in opposite directions.

### a2 · Plug Detectives: find the conductors and the insulators (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up switches off and pulls out the plug before anyone looks at it. Nobody touches a socket. Nobody opens a plug or an appliance that runs from the wall.
- **record:** Keep the two drawings: the plug and the torch, with conductors in orange and insulators in blue.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up switches off one appliance, pulls the plug out and lays the plug on a table. Look at the pins, the body and the cord. Count the pins. Find the pin that is thicker and longer than the others.  
  *Parent says:* I have unplugged it, so it is safe to look at. What is each part made of? Which parts would you be willing to hold, if it were plugged in?  
  *Child mode:* Fetch a grown-up to unplug it. Look at the pins, the body and the cord.
  - Ask: Why are the pins metal and the body plastic? (good: The pins must carry current in.; The body is where fingers go.; Plastic keeps the current away from my hand.)
  - Note: The pins are brass. The body is hard plastic. The thick top pin is the earth pin. Some chargers have two pins and a body made fully of plastic. They need no earth pin, because no metal part can be touched.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the plug and its cord large on paper. Colour every conductor orange and every insulator blue. Then draw what you think is inside the cord.  
  *Parent says:* Colour the parts that carry current orange. Colour the parts that hold it back blue. What do you think is hidden inside the cord? Draw your guess.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the plug. Orange for conductors. Blue for insulators. Guess what is inside the cord.
  - Note: Inside the cord are three copper wires. Each has its own plastic coat, and one more coat goes round all three. The coats keep the three wires from touching each other.
  - Check: The earth pin is longer than the other two pins. What is the best reason? → **It joins first and leaves last, so the body is earthed whenever the others are live**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Open the torch and lay out the parts. With a finger, trace the path from one end of the cells, through the bulb, and back. Find the place where the switch makes a gap. Draw the torch and colour it orange and blue.  
  *Parent says:* Trace the whole loop with your finger. Where does the path go through metal? Where does an insulator stop it? What does the switch do to the path?  
  *Child mode:* Open the torch. Trace the loop with a finger. Find the gap the switch makes.
  - Note: In many torches the path runs through a spring, a metal strip along the case, the switch and the bulb. If your torch has a metal case, the case itself may be part of the loop. A torch is safe to open because it runs on cells.
  - Think: A switch is a small insulating gap that you can close. What is the insulator inside the gap of most switches?
### a3 · Safety Walk with a Why: the insulators that guard our home (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up walks with the child. Look only. Nobody touches a socket, a bare wire or the meter board.
- **record:** Keep the list of what you found, and put the poster of five rules, each with its because, near the kitchen.
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Walk through each room together. Find six places where an insulator covers a conductor: a switch plate, a plug body, a cord, an appliance handle, a bulb holder, the cover of the meter board. Look from an arm's length away and write each one down.  
  *Parent says:* Behind this plastic is metal at 230 volts. What is the insulator here? What would happen if it were not there?  
  *Child mode:* Walk with a grown-up. Find six insulators that cover a conductor. Look only.
  - Ask: Which appliances have a metal body, and what kind of plug do they have? (good: The fridge, the mixer, the iron.; They have three pins.; The third pin is for the earth wire.)
  - Note: Metal-bodied appliances such as a fridge, an iron, a mixer and a wet grinder should all have three-pin plugs. At the meter board, show the small switches from a distance and say that they cut the current when there is a fault.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Walk again and look for trouble. Look for a cracked plug, a worn cord and a joint wrapped in tape. Look for a loose socket, a board near water, and a two-pin plug on a metal appliance. Write each one down. The grown-up decides what to mend and when to call an electrician.  
  *Parent says:* Now look for insulators that are broken, worn or wet. Do not touch. Tell me, and I will write it down. Which one should we mend first?  
  *Child mode:* Look for broken, worn or wet insulators. Do not touch. Tell a grown-up.
  - Note: Children are often better at spotting these than adults who have stopped seeing them. Take each find seriously. A home with none is also a good result.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write five safety rules for your home on the large sheet. After each rule write 'because' and the reason. Draw a small picture for each.  
  *Parent says:* A rule with no reason is easy to forget. Write five rules, and give each one its because. Use what you know about conductors and insulators.  
  *Child mode:* Write five rules. Each needs a because. Draw a picture for each.
  - Note: Rules to choose from: dry hands before any switch. Hold the plug, not the cord. Nothing metal goes into a socket or a toaster. Keep kites and long poles away from overhead lines. Stay away from fallen wires and flooded poles. Tell a grown-up about any tingle. If someone is getting a shock, do not touch them: switch off at the main switch.
  - Think: Which of your rules is about a conductor, and which is about an insulator that might fail?

### c1 · The Full Bucket Signal (35 min)
- **problem:** A bucket under a slow tap overflows when nobody is watching. Make a signal that lights when the water reaches a line you choose, three finger-widths below the rim.

- **creative:** Two squares of foil as big as a matchbox, facing each other with a thin piece of dry sponge between. A large area and a small gap let far more current through tap water.; A floating bottle cap carrying a foil bridge. As the water rises, the bridge lifts and touches two foil pads above. The water never has to conduct. Use the torch bulb for this one, because foil touching foil would burn out an LED.; A third cell in the row, for a harder push.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem and the four rules together. Mark a line inside the mug. Ask your child to sketch a first idea, and to say where the conductors and insulators are.  
  *Parent says:* The water has to switch the light on by itself. What could the water join together? Sketch your first idea.  
  *Child mode:* Read the problem. Mark a line in the mug. Sketch your first idea.
  - Note: Do not give the design. Most children think of two wire ends at the line. That is a good start.
- **s2 (12 min)**

  *Child mode:* Build it. Pour water in slowly. Does the LED light at the line?
  - Ask: The glow is faint. Why? (good: Tap water lets only a little through.; There are few ions.; The wire ends are thin.)
  - Note: With thin wire ends in tap water, expect a faint glow that shows only in a dim room. That is the honest first result. It sets up the real problem, which is to make it brighter.
- **s3 (10 min)**

  - Think: Your signal uses water as a switch. What else in your home is a switch that you never thought of as one?

## Worksheets
### Conductor, Insulator or In Between (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A tester's bulb lights when a key is put across the gap. What does that show?  
   Options: The key lets current through / The key makes electricity / The key is a magnet  
   **Answer:** The key lets current through. *Why:* The bulb lights only when the loop is complete, so the key must have carried the current across the gap. The cells make the push, not the key.
2. (mcq) Which part of an electric wire is the insulator?  
   Options: The copper strands / The bare end / The plastic coat  
   **Answer:** The plastic coat. *Why:* The plastic coat holds its charges still, so current stays in the copper. The bare end is copper too, left uncovered so it can be joined.
3. (truefalse) A plastic ruler has no electric charges inside it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every material is made of atoms full of charges. In plastic the charges are held in place. That is what makes plastic an insulator.
4. (truefalse) Sugar water conducts much better than plain water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sugar dissolves, but it stays as whole molecules with no charge. With no new ions, the water conducts as it did before.
5. (fill) The charged atoms that carry current through salt water are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ions']. *Why:* Salt breaks into sodium ions and chloride ions in water. They drift to the two wires and carry the current.
6. (fill) In a metal, the tiny charged particles that are free to move are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['electrons', 'free electrons']. *Why:* Each metal atom lets go of one or more electrons. These belong to the whole piece and drift when a cell pushes them.
7. (sort) Sort these by what a 3 volt bulb tester would show.  
   **Answer:** an eraser → Bulb stays dark; a steel spoon → Bulb lights; a glass marble → Bulb stays dark; kitchen foil → Bulb lights; a dry wooden spoon → Bulb stays dark; a coin → Bulb lights. *Why:* The spoon, the foil and the coin are metals with free electrons. The eraser, the marble and the dry wood hold their charges too firmly for a 3 volt push.
8. (match) Match each part to its job.  
   **Answer:** the brass pins of a plug → carry current into the cord; the plastic body of a plug → keeps fingers away from live metal; the earth wire → leads a fault current to the ground; a switch → opens and closes a gap in the loop. *Why:* Conductors lead the current where it is wanted. Insulators keep it away from everywhere else. A switch is a conductor that can be moved to close an insulating gap.
9. (order) Put these in order, from conducts best to conducts least.  
   **Answer:** ['a copper wire', 'salt water', 'tap water', 'dry plastic']. *Why:* Copper has the most free charges. Salt water has many ions, tap water has few, and dry plastic has almost no free charge at all.
10. (mcq) Why must hands be dry before touching a switch?  
   Options: Wet skin lets far more current through / Wet hands are slippery / Water makes switches rust at once  
   **Answer:** Wet skin lets far more current through. *Why:* Dry skin holds back most of a current. Wet skin is about 100 times easier to pass, and the water can also creep into the switch to the live metal.
11. (write) Explain why an electric wire is made of two materials, not one.  
   **Answer:** The copper inside is a conductor, so it carries the current where it is needed. The plastic outside is an insulator, so the current cannot leave the copper to reach a hand or another wire.. *Why:* A strong answer names both jobs: leading the current, and keeping it in. It may add that two bare wires touching would make a short path.
12. (draw) Draw a cup of salt water with two wires in it. Show what moves, and which way.  
   **Answer:** Two wires in a cup. Small balls marked plus drift towards one wire and balls marked minus drift towards the other.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows two kinds of ions moving in opposite directions. It shows no electrons swimming alone through the water.
13. (label) Here is a three-pin plug with its cord. Label each part.  
   **Answer:** ['the thick, long top pin: earth pin', 'either lower pin: one of the two current pins', 'the part you hold: plastic body', 'the long covered wires: cord']. *Why:* The top pin is thicker and longer, so it joins first. The two lower pins carry the current in and out. The body and the cord's coat are insulators.
14. (mcq) An LED glows in a cup of water where a torch bulb stays dark. Why?  
   Options: The LED needs far less current to glow / The LED makes its own electricity / Torch bulbs never work in water  
   **Answer:** The LED needs far less current to glow. *Why:* The same small current flows in both tests. It is enough for an LED and far too little for a bulb's thin wire to get hot.
15. (write) Write one safety rule for your home. Then write the reason for it.  
   **Answer:** Any true rule with a correct reason. For example: I dry my hands before touching a switch, because wet skin lets about 100 times more current through.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason that uses conductors, insulators, ions or the size of the push. A rule with no reason is only half an answer.
### Ions, Bodies and Earth Wires (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Salt and sugar both dissolve. Why does only salt make water conduct well?  
   Options: Salt is heavier than sugar / Sugar blocks the wires / Salt breaks into charged ions  
   **Answer:** Salt breaks into charged ions. *Why:* Salt splits into ions with plus and minus charges, which can carry current. Sugar molecules stay whole and have no charge to carry.
2. (mcq) A bird stands with both feet on one bare power line and is unharmed. Why?  
   Options: Nothing pushes current through her body / Birds are too small to conduct / Her feet are made of rubber  
   **Answer:** Nothing pushes current through her body. *Why:* Both feet are on the same wire, so there is no push from one foot to the other. If she touched a second wire or the tower, current would pass through her.
3. (truefalse) A 1.5 volt cell is safe to hold because skin can hold back so gentle a push.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Skin has a large resistance, and 1.5 volts sends almost no current through it. The same skin does not hold back 230 volts.
4. (truefalse) On a normal day, the earth wire of a fridge carries a large current.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. On a normal day it carries nothing. It carries current only when a fault makes the metal body live.
5. (fill) A wall socket in India pushes with ____ volts.  
   **Answer:** ['230']. *Why:* India's mains supply is 230 volts. One AA cell is 1.5 volts.
6. (count) Dry skin has a resistance of 100,000 ohms. Wet skin has 1,000 ohms. How many times easier is wet skin to pass?  
   **Answer:** 100. *Why:* 100,000 divided by 1,000 is 100. The same push sends about 100 times more current through the wet skin.
7. (odd) Three of these carry current using ions. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: sea water / lemon juice / a copper wire / a nerve in your arm  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A copper wire carries current with free electrons. Sea water, lemon juice and a nerve all carry it with ions moving through water.
8. (sort) Sort each one by what carries the current inside it.  
   **Answer:** salt water → Ions; a copper wire → Electrons; the inside of a tomato → Ions; a pencil lead → Electrons; your blood → Ions; kitchen foil → Electrons. *Why:* Metals and graphite have free electrons. Watery things, living or not, carry current with dissolved ions.
9. (order) Put these in order, from the gentlest push to the hardest.  
   **Answer:** ['one AA cell', 'two AA cells in a row', 'a wall socket', 'a lightning flash']. *Why:* One cell is 1.5 volts and two in a row are 3 volts. A wall socket is 230 volts. Lightning is many millions of volts.
10. (write) A wet grinder has a metal body and a plug with only two pins. Explain what could go wrong.  
   **Answer:** A two-pin plug has no earth pin, so the metal body is not joined to the ground. If a wire inside comes loose or gets wet and touches the body, the body becomes live. A person who touches it becomes the path to the ground.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the missing pin was for, and names the person as the path the current takes when there is no easier one.
11. (write) A torch bulb stays dark in salt water. An LED glows. Is salt water a conductor? Give your view and your reason.  
   **Answer:** A good answer takes a view and backs it. For example: it conducts, but far less well than a metal, so it gives enough current for an LED and too little for a bulb. Or: the question needs a number, not a yes or no.. *Why:* There is no single right sentence. A strong answer notices that the answer depends on the tester, and that conducting comes in amounts.
12. (draw) Draw the path of a fault current in a mixer with an earth wire. Then draw it in a mixer without one.  
   **Answer:** First drawing: an arrow from the loose wire to the metal body, along the earth wire, into the ground. Second drawing: an arrow from the body through a person's hand and feet into the ground.. *Why:* A strong pair of drawings shows that the current reaches the ground either way. The earth wire only changes whose path it is.
13. (perspective) Three people disagree about what children should be told about water and electricity.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and gives a reason from this lesson, such as: all everyday water has ions, or knowing about ions explains sweat and wet floors. It also gives one fair point to another view.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. The short rule is safe and slightly untrue. The full truth is exact and takes longer to learn. Each speaker is protecting something real.
14. (mcq) Why may a phone charger have only two pins and no earth pin?  
   Options: Chargers use no current / Phones are too small to be dangerous / Its whole case is plastic, with no metal to touch  
   **Answer:** Its whole case is plastic, with no metal to touch. *Why:* An earth wire protects against a metal body going live. A charger wrapped fully in plastic has no metal body to protect. It still carries 230 volts inside, so a cracked charger is not safe.
15. (label) Here are a wire and a nerve fibre, side by side. Label the conductor and the insulator in each.  
   **Answer:** ['the copper strands: conductor in the wire', 'the plastic coat: insulator on the wire', 'the salty water inside the fibre: conductor in the nerve', 'the fatty wrap called myelin: insulator on the nerve']. *Why:* Both are a conductor inside an insulating wrap. The wire uses free electrons in copper. The nerve uses ions in salty water, wrapped in fat.
### The Long Scale (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Why does only graphite conduct?  
   Options: Each atom in graphite has one electron free to move / Graphite is softer / Diamond is too clear  
   **Answer:** Each atom in graphite has one electron free to move. *Why:* In diamond every outer electron is locked in a bond. In graphite the atoms sit in flat sheets, and one electron from each atom can move along the sheet. The atoms are the same. The joining is different.
2. (mcq) What happens to air when lightning passes through it?  
   Options: A huge push tears electrons free, so the air conducts / The air turns into metal / Nothing. Lightning goes round the air  
   **Answer:** A huge push tears electrons free, so the air conducts. *Why:* Air insulates until the push is about 30,000 volts for each centimetre. Beyond that, electrons are pulled off the air's atoms and the air carries a current for a moment.
3. (truefalse) Electricity takes only the easiest path, and none goes down any other path.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Current shares itself among every path open to it. The easier path gets more, but a harder path, such as a person, still gets some.
4. (truefalse) The purest water that can be made conducts nothing at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Kohlrausch and Heydweiller measured this in 1894. A few water molecules always split into ions by themselves, so even the purest water conducts a very little.
5. (fill) A material that has no resistance at all when it is cold enough is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['superconductor']. *Why:* Heike Kamerlingh Onnes and his team found the first one in 1911, when they cooled mercury to minus 269 degrees Celsius.
6. (count) Air starts to conduct at about 30,000 volts for each centimetre. About how many volts make a 3 centimetre spark?  
   **Answer:** 90000. *Why:* 30,000 times 3 is 90,000 volts. That is why sparks from a socket are short, and why lightning needs many millions of volts.
7. (match) Match each finding to the person who made it.  
   **Answer:** charge travels along a thread, 1729 → Stephen Gray; salts split into ions in water, 1884 → Svante Arrhenius; resistance vanishes in very cold mercury, 1911 → Heike Kamerlingh Onnes; a crystal detector for radio waves, 1901 → Jagadish Chandra Bose. *Why:* Gray was a cloth dyer. Arrhenius's examiners gave his idea of ions the lowest pass mark, and it later won a Nobel Prize. Bose worked in Kolkata with equipment he built himself.
8. (odd) Three of these are made from semiconductors. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: a solar panel / an LED / a copper wire / a computer chip  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Copper is a metal and always conducts well. Solar panels, LEDs and chips are made of semiconductors, whose conducting can be controlled.
9. (order) Put these findings in order, from the earliest to the latest.  
   **Answer:** ["Gilbert's list of 'electrics'", "Gray's thread", 'superconducting mercury', 'ceramics that superconduct']. *Why:* Gilbert wrote in 1600. Gray's thread was 1729. Mercury was found to superconduct in 1911, and the ceramics in 1986.
10. (write) Glass insulates when cold and conducts when red-hot. What does this tell you about the words conductor and insulator?  
   **Answer:** A good answer says that the words describe how a material behaves under certain conditions, not a fixed nature. The same glass gives a different answer when heat frees its charges.. *Why:* A strong answer separates what a thing is made of from how it behaves, and names the condition that changed.
11. (write) Plan a fair test to find out whether rainwater or tap water conducts better. Say what you keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Use the LED tester on equal amounts of each water in equal clean plastic cups. Keep the same wire ends, the same gap and depth, the same cells and the same dim room. Rinse the wires between cups. Dip three times in each and compare the glow.. *Why:* A strong plan changes only the water, repeats each test, and says how the result will be read. Catching the rain in clean plastic matters, because a metal bowl adds ions.
12. (draw) Draw one long scale from best conductor to best insulator. Place eight things on it.  
   **Answer:** A line with metals such as copper at one end, then pencil lead, salt water, tap water, wet wood or skin, and dry wood, plastic, rubber or glass at the far end.. *Why:* A strong scale has metals at one end, insulators at the other, and at least three things placed in between. The exact order of near neighbours matters less than having a middle.
13. (perspective) Scientists disagree about what the electric signals in plants mean.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view, gives a reason, and suggests something to test, such as whether a plant acts differently after the same signal on different days, or whether blocking the signal changes what she does.. *Why:* Scientists have not settled this. Both sides accept the measurements. They differ on what counts as thinking, and that is partly a question about words and partly one for more experiments.
14. (mcq) A tester's lamp stays dark on a wooden block. What can you safely say?  
   Options: No charge can ever move in the block / The block is a perfect insulator / Any current was too small for this lamp  
   **Answer:** Any current was too small for this lamp. *Why:* A dark lamp shows only that the current was below what the lamp needs. A more sensitive tester, a harder push or a wet block could give a different result.
15. (label) Here is a power-line tower. Label the conductor and the three things that keep the current in it.  
   **Answer:** ['the long cable: bare aluminium wire', 'the stack between cable and tower: ceramic discs', 'the space all round the cable: air', 'the tall frame: steel tower']. *Why:* The wire is bare because the air around it is the insulator. The ceramic discs hold the wire away from the steel tower, which is a conductor joined to the ground.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Riya, a girl in your class, tests five cups of water. She does not rinse the wires between cups. What goes wrong?  
   Options: The wires stop working / Nothing goes wrong / Salt is carried into the next cup  
   **Answer:** Salt is carried into the next cup. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing only. Salt left on the wires changes the next cup too, so its score is too high. If you chose 'nothing', think about what the wet wire ends are carrying.
2. (mcq) Which tester can show the smallest current?  
   Options: A tester with a torch bulb / Both show exactly the same / A tester with a red LED  
   **Answer:** A tester with a red LED. *Why:* An LED glows with about a hundredth of the current a torch bulb needs. So it catches currents the bulb misses.
3. (mcq) What is inside a copper wire before it is joined to a cell?  
   Options: Nothing. The cell fills it up / Tiny sparks waiting to jump / Free electrons, all along the wire  
   **Answer:** Free electrons, all along the wire. *Why:* The wire is already full of free electrons. The cell only pushes them. If you chose the first answer, think of the coin train: the coins were there before the push.
4. (mcq) Why does rubber insulate?  
   Options: Its electrons are held firmly by their atoms / Rubber has no electrons in it / Rubber is soft and bends  
   **Answer:** Its electrons are held firmly by their atoms. *Why:* Every material has electrons. In rubber none are free to move, so a push moves almost no charge. Softness has nothing to do with it: hard glass insulates too.
5. (mcq) You add salt to plain water one pinch at a time. What does the LED do?  
   Options: It jumps from dark to full at one pinch / It grows brighter with each pinch / It does not change  
   **Answer:** It grows brighter with each pinch. *Why:* Each pinch adds more ions, and more ions carry more current. There is no pinch where water suddenly becomes a conductor.
6. (mcq) Which of these conducts best?  
   Options: Rainwater caught in a clean plastic bowl / Filtered drinking water / Sea water  
   **Answer:** Sea water. *Why:* Sea water holds the most ions by far. Rainwater holds the fewest of the three. The more ions, the better water conducts.
7. (mcq) Why is 230 volts dangerous when 1.5 volts is not?  
   Options: The harder push drives a large current through a body / Mains is a different kind of electricity / A cell has no electricity in it  
   **Answer:** The harder push drives a large current through a body. *Why:* It is the same electricity. 230 volts pushes more than 150 times as hard, so far more current gets through skin. A large current through the chest can stop the heart.
8. (truefalse) Dry skin is a perfect insulator.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dry skin holds back much more than wet skin does, but never everything. At 230 volts enough current passes through dry skin to do harm.
9. (mcq) Why are the handles of an electrician's pliers covered in rubber?  
   Options: To make the pliers heavier / So current cannot pass from the metal to the hand / Only to give a better grip  
   **Answer:** So current cannot pass from the metal to the hand. *Why:* The metal jaws may touch a live wire. The rubber is an insulator between the metal and the hand. A grip helps too, but it is not the main reason.
10. (mcq) What does the earth wire join the metal body of a fridge to?  
   Options: The switch / The bulb inside the fridge / The ground  
   **Answer:** The ground. *Why:* The earth wire runs from the metal body to the ground. If a loose wire makes the body live, the current takes this easy path, and a safety switch cuts the supply.
11. (mcq) Which sentence about conductors and insulators is the most accurate?  
   Options: Every material is fully one or fully the other / They are two ends of one long scale / Only metals have charges inside them  
   **Answer:** They are two ends of one long scale. *Why:* Materials range from silver to rubber, with pencil lead, salt water, silicon and skin in between. All materials have charges inside. What differs is how free the charges are.
12. (mcq) A mixer gives your hand a tingle. What is the right thing to do?  
   Options: Switch off at the main switch and tell a grown-up / Touch it again to make sure / Wipe it with a wet cloth and carry on  
   **Answer:** Switch off at the main switch and tell a grown-up. *Why:* A tingle means current is reaching the metal body, so an insulator has failed. Cut the supply first, then get an electrician. Touching again, or adding water, gives the current a better path through you.
13. (mcq) Someone is getting a shock from an appliance. What should you do first?  
   Options: Pull them away with your hands / Throw water on the appliance / Switch off at the main switch without touching them  
   **Answer:** Switch off at the main switch without touching them. *Why:* A body conducts, so touching the person can pass the current through you too. Cutting the supply ends the danger for everyone. Water would make every path easier.
14. (mcq) Which of these is still an open question for scientists?  
   Options: How ceramic superconductors work / Whether copper conducts / Whether salt dissolves in water  
   **Answer:** How ceramic superconductors work. *Why:* Ceramic superconductors were found in 1986, and physicists still argue about what pairs up the electrons inside them. The other two were settled long ago.

## Applications
- Covering every copper wire in plastic, so current reaches the fan and not the hand.
- Earthing the metal body of a fridge, an iron or a wet grinder through the third pin of the plug.
- Holding bare power lines away from steel towers with stacks of ceramic or glass discs.
- Giving line workers rubber gloves, rubber mats and tools with insulated handles.
- Checking drinking water with a small meter that measures how well the water conducts.
- Making phone screens that sense the touch of a conducting fingertip.
- Building computer chips and solar panels from silicon, a semiconductor whose conducting can be controlled.
- Running the strong magnets of hospital MRI scanners with superconducting wire.
- Fitting a copper strip from the top of a tall building to the ground, to lead lightning safely down.
- Making tank alarms in which rising water joins two metal points and sounds a buzzer.

## Spotted around us
- The three-pin plug of a mixer or an iron. The thick top pin is the earth pin. Look at it only when a grown-up has unplugged it.
- Stacks of brown or grey discs on the big steel towers that carry power lines across fields. Each disc is an insulator.
- A mynah or a crow sitting calmly on one bare wire in the street.
- The technician who services a water filter, dipping a small pen-shaped meter into a tumbler of water.
- A flat copper or iron strip running down the side of a temple tower, a church spire, a minaret or a tall block of flats, into the ground.
- The switchboard in a home, with rows of small switches that trip when there is a fault. Look, and do not touch.
- An electrician's pliers and screwdrivers, with thick red or yellow rubber on the handles.
- Your own fingertip working a phone screen, and a pencil eraser failing to.

## Open questions
- **How do the ceramic superconductors work?** They were found in 1986. Thousands of experiments later, physicists still argue about what joins the electrons into pairs in them, and no theory has predicted the next material correctly.
- **Can any material superconduct at room temperature without being squeezed?** No law of nature is known to forbid it, but nobody has made one. Each claim so far has failed when other laboratories tried to repeat it.
- **Exactly how do ions push past each other in crowded sea water?** The simple rules work for weak salt water. In strong salt water the ions crowd and pull on each other, and chemists still cannot work out the conducting exactly from first rules.
- **How much do plants, fungi and single cells use electric signals to remember and decide?** The signals are real and measured. What they mean to the plant is hard to test, and scientists disagree on which words are honest to use.
- **How do the electric signals in a brain become a feeling, such as the sting of a small static shock?** Scientists can follow ions through nerve gates in great detail. Nobody has yet shown how any amount of moving charge becomes the feeling itself.

## Connected maths
- In the Water Test, the five cups scored 0, 1, 0, 2 and 3 for glow. Draw a bar chart of the scores. Which two cups have equal bars, and what does that tell you? **Cups A and C both score 0. Plain water and sugar water conduct the same, so sugar added nothing that carries current.**. Draw five bars with heights 0, 1, 0, 2 and 3. A bar of height 0 is still a result. Equal bars for A and C show that dissolving sugar changed nothing. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- You tested 12 things. 4 lit the bulb. 3 lit only the LED. The rest lit neither. What fraction lit neither? What fraction let some current through? **5 out of 12 lit neither. 7 out of 12 let some current through.**. 12 minus 4 minus 3 is 5, so 5 twelfths lit neither. 4 plus 3 is 7, so 7 twelfths let some current through. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- One AA cell pushes with 1.5 volts. How many AA cells in a row would you need to push at least as hard as a 230 volt socket? **154 cells.**. 153 cells give 153 times 1.5, which is 229.5 volts, just short. 154 cells give 231 volts. So you need 154. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Sea water conducts about 1,000,000 times better than the purest water. Tap water conducts about 100 times worse than sea water. About how many times better than the purest water does tap water conduct? **About 10,000 times better.**. 1,000,000 divided by 100 is 10,000. So tap water sits between the two, much nearer to sea water than a bulb tester suggests. (queues `math.multiplication.scaling.L4`)
- Lakshmi says: if the bulb lights, the thing is a conductor. Her bulb did not light on a tomato. She says: so the tomato is an insulator. Is her reasoning sound? **No. Her rule says what follows when the bulb lights. It says nothing about what follows when the bulb stays dark.**. From 'if the bulb lights, it conducts' you cannot turn the rule round. A thing may conduct too little to light a bulb. The LED showed that the tomato does. (queues `math.logic.if-then.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What do we call a material that lets electric charge move through it easily? → A conductor.
- What is the plastic coat on a wire for? → It is an insulator. It keeps the current in the copper and away from you.
- What carries the current through salt water? → Ions, which are charged atoms from the salt.
- Does sugar help water conduct? → No. Sugar dissolves without making ions.
- How many volts does a wall socket in India push with? → 230 volts.
- What does the thick top pin of a three-pin plug join to? → The earth wire, which leads to the ground.
- Which needs less current to glow, a torch bulb or an LED? → An LED.
- Name one thing that is neither a good conductor nor a good insulator. → Pencil lead, tap water, silicon, wet wood or skin.
