# Sorting animals: by body covering, by legs and by food
`sci.classification.sorting-living-things.L2`

**Summary.** A hen and a crow both have feathers and two legs, so they go in one group. A hen and a cow have different coverings and different legs. You can sort animals by their body covering, by their legs and by what they eat. Covering and legs show at a glance, but to find an animal's food you have to watch them. Your child sorts the same animal cards three ways, compares two animals in overlapping hoops, and then puts out banana, rice and a dead insect to find out what the ants at home eat.

**Intro.** *Stroke your arm and feel the tiny hairs. Now think of a crow and a fish. What covers each body? Then the harder question: what do the ants in our kitchen eat?* Hair, feathers and scales are easy to see. Legs are easy to count. But you cannot see what an animal eats by looking at their body. Ask your child how we could find out what the ants eat, and let them guess. Don't give the answer. In this unit we sort animals by covering, by legs and by food, and then we test the ants.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can sort animals by their body covering: hair or fur, feathers, scales, a hard shell or case, or bare skin.
1. By the end I can sort animals by their legs: no legs, 2, 4, 6, 8 or many.
2. By the end I can sort animals by food into plant-eaters, meat-eaters and animals who eat both.
3. By the end I can say one way two animals are similar and one way they are different.
4. By the end I can say that one animal sits in different groups under different rules, and that humans are animals too.
5. By the end I can find out what an animal eats by watching, and say what I saw.

## Terms
- **Body covering**: A body covering is what grows on the outside of an animal's body, like hair, feathers or scales. *e.g.* Your body covering is skin with hair. A crow's covering is feathers.
- **Fur and hair**: Fur is thick hair. Animals with hair or fur feed their babies milk. *e.g.* A cow, a dog, a bat and you all have hair.
- **Feathers**: Feathers are light, soft parts that cover a bird's body. Only birds have feathers. *e.g.* A crow, a hen and a parrot all have feathers.
- **Scales**: Scales are small, hard plates that cover the skin of fish, snakes and lizards. *e.g.* A rohu fish at the market and a snake on a wall both have scales.
- **Shell**: A shell is a hard cover that protects a soft body. Insects have a hard outer case instead. *e.g.* A snail carries a shell. A tortoise has a shell too.
- **Herbivore**: A herbivore is an animal who eats plants and plant parts, like leaves, grass, fruit and seeds. We can say plant-eater. *e.g.* A cow eats grass, and a parrot eats fruit and seeds. Both are herbivores.
- **Carnivore**: A carnivore is an animal who eats other animals. We can say meat-eater. *e.g.* A gecko eats moths and flies. A tiger eats deer. Both are carnivores.
- **Omnivore**: An omnivore is an animal who eats both plant food and animal food. *e.g.* A crow eats rice, fruit and insects. A crow is an omnivore.
- **Similar**: Similar means alike in some way. Two animals are similar when they share a feature. *e.g.* A hen and a crow are similar: both have feathers and a beak.
- **Different**: Different means not the same. Two animals are different when one has a feature the other does not. *e.g.* A cow and a crow are different: the cow has hair and four legs, the crow has feathers and two legs.

## Explanations
### Sorting animals by body covering · *defined*
**Definition:** We look at what grows on the outside of each animal, and put animals with the same covering together.

Most animals have one of a few coverings. Hair or fur grows on cows, cats, bats and humans. Feathers grow only on birds. Scales cover fish, snakes and lizards. Some animals have a hard shell or a hard outer case, like snails, tortoises and ants. Frogs and earthworms have soft, bare, wet skin.
- e.g. Hair or fur: cow, goat, bat, human.
- e.g. Feathers: crow, hen, parrot.
- e.g. Scales: fish, snake, gecko.
- e.g. Hard shell or case: snail, tortoise, ant.
- e.g. Bare wet skin: frog, earthworm.
### Sorting animals by legs · *defined*
**Definition:** We count each animal's legs, and put animals with the same number together.

Snakes, fish and earthworms have no legs. Birds and humans walk on 2 legs. Cows, cats, frogs and lizards have 4 legs. Every insect, like an ant or a butterfly, has 6 legs. A spider has 8 legs. A crab has 10, counting the two big claws. A centipede has many legs, in pairs.
- e.g. No legs: snake, earthworm, fish.
- e.g. 2 legs: crow, hen, you.
- e.g. 4 legs: cow, frog, gecko.
- e.g. 6 legs: ant, cockroach, butterfly.
- e.g. 8 legs: spider.
### Sorting animals by food · *defined*
**Definition:** Herbivores eat plants. Carnivores eat other animals. Omnivores eat both plant food and animal food.

You cannot see food on an animal's body. You find out by watching what they eat. A cow eats grass all day, so a cow is a herbivore. A gecko by the tube light eats moths, so a gecko is a carnivore. A crow eats rice, fruit, insects and scraps, so a crow is an omnivore. A human body can eat both kinds of food. Many people choose to eat only plant food and milk.
- e.g. Herbivores: cow, goat, parrot, fruit bat, butterfly.
- e.g. Carnivores: tiger, gecko, spider, frog, snake.
- e.g. Omnivores: crow, hen, ant, humans.
### One animal, many groups · *defined*
**Definition:** Each sorting rule makes its own groups, so one animal can sit with different animals under different rules.

Sort by covering, and the hen sits with the crow and the parrot, because they all have feathers. Sort by legs, and the hen sits with the crow and with you, because you all have 2 legs. Sort by food, and the hen sits with the crow, the ant and you, because you all eat both plant food and animal food. The hen did not change. Only the rule changed.
- e.g. Covering: hen with crow and parrot.
- e.g. Legs: hen with crow and you.
- e.g. Food: hen with crow, ant and you.
### Similar and different · *defined*
**Definition:** To compare two animals, we list the features they share and the features only one of them has.

A crow and a hen are similar in many ways. Both have feathers, a beak, 2 legs and 2 wings, and both lay eggs. They are different too. A crow flies far, and a hen flies only a short way, up onto a wall. Two overlapping hoops show this well. What both share goes in the middle. What only one has goes on that animal's side.
- e.g. You and a cow: both have hair, both feed babies milk. Different: you walk on 2 legs, a cow on 4.
- e.g. An ant and a spider: both have a hard outer case. Different: 6 legs and 8 legs.
### Food groups are not always neat · *debated*
**Definition:** Most animals eat mostly one kind of food, with some of the other.

Many animals do not fit one food group neatly. Some are mostly one kind of eater, with a little of the other. Scientists still argue about where a few of them belong.
- e.g. A frog tadpole eats tiny water plants, but a grown frog eats insects.
- e.g. A female mosquito drinks blood for her eggs, and also drinks flower nectar.
- e.g. A red panda in Sikkim belongs to the same big group as tigers, yet eats mostly bamboo.
- **Many scientists:** Food groups are useful, but they describe what an animal mostly eats. Most animals eat a little outside their group.
- **Some scientists who study dogs:** Dogs come from wolves, who are carnivores, so dogs are carnivores who also eat some plant food.
- **Other scientists who study dogs:** Dogs have lived with people for thousands of years. Their bodies now digest rice and wheat better than wolves do, so dogs are omnivores.
### Animals choose their food · *debated*
**Definition:** Animals sense food and choose what to eat.

Ants sense food with their antennae, the two thin feelers on their heads. They decide which food to carry home. A colony collects both kinds of food, because the young ants need protein to grow, and the workers mostly drink sugary food. Animals sense food and choose between foods, and many remember where good food is.
- e.g. Ants found the banana and the dead insect, and carried bits home.
- e.g. A crow remembers the window where rice is put out each morning.
- **Scientists who study animal minds:** Ants sense, choose and remember. A colony decides together what to collect, from what each ant senses.
- **Other scientists:** An ant follows simple built-in rules, and the colony's choices come from many ants following those rules.

## Mostly agreed
- Among animals alive today, only birds have feathers, and every bird has 2 legs. *How we know:* Every kind of bird scientists have found has feathers and 2 legs, even birds who cannot fly, like the ostrich.
- Animals with hair or fur feed their babies milk. *How we know:* A cow, a goat, a bat, a dog and a human mother all feed their young milk. Scientists call this group mammals.
- Every insect has 6 legs as an adult. *How we know:* Ants, cockroaches, butterflies, bees and houseflies all have 6 legs. Spiders have 8, so spiders are not insects.
- Humans and many other animals are omnivores. *How we know:* Crows, hens, ants and humans can all eat both plant food and animal food. Jane Goodall found that chimpanzees do too.

## How we found out
**Jane Goodall, an English scientist who studied chimpanzees, helped at first by her mother Vanne, 1960, at Gombe in what is now Tanzania.** Before 1960, most scientists thought chimpanzees ate only plants: fruit, leaves and seeds. Jane Goodall was 26, and she had no university degree. The scientist Louis Leakey sent her to watch wild chimpanzees at Gombe, by Lake Tanganyika, in what is now Tanzania. Her mother, Vanne, came with her for the first months. At first the chimpanzees ran away whenever they saw her. Jane went back every day and sat quietly. Slowly they let her come closer. Late in 1960 she saw an old male, whom she named David Greybeard, push a grass stem into a termite mound, pull it out covered in termites, and eat them. She also saw chimpanzees eat meat from a young wild pig. So chimpanzees eat animal food too. They eat both, like crows and like most people. Nobody had found this out from books. Jane found it by watching chimpanzees in their own home, day after day.  
*What people believed before:* That chimpanzees eat only plants. By watching wild chimpanzees for months, Jane Goodall saw them eat meat and termites, so chimpanzees are omnivores.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A whale is a fish, because a whale swims and has fins.** (A common idea, held by many scientists long ago; *rejected*)  
  For: Whales live in the sea, swim and have fin-shaped flippers.  
  Against: A whale breathes air, has a few hairs, and the mother feeds her baby milk. That puts whales with the mammals, like cows and us.
- **A pangolin is a reptile, because a pangolin is covered in scales.** (A common guess by people who see a pangolin for the first time; *rejected*)  
  For: The Indian pangolin is covered in hard scales, like a lizard.  
  Against: A pangolin has some hair on the belly, and the mother feeds her baby milk. A pangolin is a mammal. One feature alone can fool you.
- **A plant-eater never eats animal food.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Cows, goats and deer eat plants nearly all day.  
  Against: Deer have been seen eating bird chicks. Food groups say what an animal mostly eats.

## Roots
We have sorted animals by covering, by legs and by food. Now we ask what the ant test tells us about eating, and about where we fit.

**Deep truth.** In the Ant Lunch test, the ants came to the banana and to the dead insect. So ants eat food from plants and from animals, like crows and most people. Animals who eat many kinds of food can find a meal almost anywhere. That is one reason ants, crows and people all live in our cities.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of food groups as fixed labels, and we picture each animal with one kind of food. Many animals eat both, and these are often the animals who live closest to us.  
*Evidence:* In the ant test, the ants gathered on the banana and on the dead insect. Crows at the window eat rice, fruit, bread and insects. Both share our flats and streets.

- *who am i:* You are an animal with hair and two legs, whose body can eat both kinds of food. You sit in many groups at once.
- *what is life:* Every animal lives by eating other living beings, or what they made, like fruit and nectar. The ants on the banana were doing what you do at lunch.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of eaters, and each one chooses their food. A crow, an ant and a person can all find a meal in the same flat.

The banana grew on a banana plant, from sunlight, water and air. The ants ate the banana, and the banana became part of the ants. A crow may eat an ant, and one day the crow's body will feed the soil. So the energy that came from the Sun passes from one living being to the next.

- **Bhagavata Purana 1.13.47:** Beings without hands are food for beings with hands. Beings without legs are food for beings with four legs. One living being is the life of another. (This old verse sorts animals by hands, by legs and by who eats whom, as you did with the cards. The ants and the mosquito showed its last line on your own paper.)
- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2:** The young seeker Bhrigu came to understand that food is Brahman, the one reality. From food all beings are born. By food they live. Into food they return. (Every food in the ant test came from a living being: a banana plant, a rice plant and a mosquito. The ants grew from what they ate.)
- Science (settled): In many kinds of ants, the young need protein to grow, and the workers mostly drink sugary food. So a colony collects both, and collects more protein when there are more young to feed.
- Science (open): How the ants in a colony share what the colony needs is still being studied.
- Other view, Jain teachers: Jain teachers say that living by other beings' lives is not something to simply accept. A person can choose to harm as few living beings as possible. Many Jains eat only plant food and milk, and many do not eat root vegetables, to spare the tiny beings in the soil.
- Practice, Where did my lunch live? (3 min): Before you eat, look at your plate. Point to one food. Ask: where did this live before it came to me? A rice plant? A cow? Say it out loud. Now close your eyes. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out slowly. Then eat your first bite slowly.
- Ask: What do you think a fruit bat eats at night, and how could you find out?
- Ask: If you watched a crow for a whole year, would you see them eat something new? What might it be?
- Ask: Why do you think some animals eat only one kind of food, and others eat almost anything?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* You can count a crow's legs in one look. Why does it take days of watching to find out what a crow eats? (hint: A crow's legs are there all the time. Think about when a crow eats, and what is around.)
- *deep:* A butterfly has wings and 6 legs. A fruit bat has wings and 2 legs. Are wings a good way to sort animals? (hint: Think about what else the butterfly and the fruit bat share: covering, legs and food.)
- *big:* A human body can eat both plant food and animal food. Many people choose to eat only plants and milk. Which food group are they in? (hint: Is a food group about what a body can eat, or what someone chooses to eat?)
- *big:* The ants ate the banana. Is the banana now part of the ants? (hint: Think about how your own body grows from what you eat.)

## Story: Revathi and the Window Crow
*The facts in the story are true: house crows and many kitchen ants eat both plant food and animal food, and many families in South India put out rice for crows. Revathi, Nikhil, Paati, Shalini and Miss Selvi are made up.*

Revathi was seven. She lived on the third floor of a building in Coimbatore, with her grandmother, Paati. Her cousin Nikhil was five. He came over every afternoon after school.

Every morning, Paati put a small ball of cooked rice on the kitchen window ledge. Most mornings, a crow came for the rice. The crow had a grey neck and shiny black wings. Revathi called the crow Kaka.

One day Revathi's teacher, Miss Selvi, gave the class a job. "Make a chart of animals," she said. "Put the plant-eaters on one side and the meat-eaters on the other."

That afternoon, Revathi spread out her pictures on the floor. A cow, a goat and a parrot went on the plant side. A tiger, a gecko and a spider went on the meat side. Then she picked up the crow.

"Kaka eats rice," said Revathi. "Rice is a plant. So Kaka goes on the plant side."

Nikhil shook his head. "But I saw Kaka eat a cockroach yesterday! A dead one, on the ledge."

Revathi frowned. "Are you sure?"

"Don't guess," said Paati. "Watch. Write down what Kaka eats every day this week. Then you will know."

So Revathi made a little table in her notebook. On Monday, Kaka ate the rice. On Tuesday, Kaka ate the rice and then a piece of banana that Nikhil had dropped. On Wednesday, Kaka pecked at a dead cockroach by the drain pipe and swallowed the cockroach. On Thursday, Kaka flew down to the bins and came back with something white. "Bread," said Paati. On Friday, Kaka snapped up a moth that was sitting on the window grill.

Revathi read her table out loud. "Rice, banana, bread. Those come from plants. Cockroach and moth. Those are animals." She looked at her chart. "Kaka doesn't fit on either side!"

"Then your chart needs a third group," said Paati.

Revathi drew a line down the middle of her chart. At the top she wrote: EATS BOTH. She stuck Kaka there. "Animals who eat both kinds of food are called omnivores," said Paati. "Your teacher will like that word."

Nikhil had gone quiet. He was lying on his tummy by the window, looking at the ledge. "Revathi, come and see," he whispered.

On the ledge, the banana bit from Tuesday had a crowd of tiny ants around it. A little way off, there was another crowd, around a dead fly. Some ants were walking away from the fly carrying a tiny piece of wing.

"The ants eat both too," said Revathi. She found an ant in her picture pile and put the ant picture next to Kaka.

Nikhil sat up. "Where do I go?"

Revathi thought about it. "You eat fish curry at home. And rice. So you go in the middle." She looked at Paati. "But Paati never eats meat or fish. Does Paati go on the plant side?"

Paati laughed. "A human body can eat both kinds of food. I choose to eat plants and milk. Your Aunty in Kochi chooses fish every day. We are the same kind of animal, and we choose different food."

Revathi drew a picture of a person in the middle column. Underneath, she wrote: "Humans can eat both. Each person chooses."

The next day, Revathi showed her chart to her friend Shalini. Shalini's chart had only two sides, and her crow was on the meat side.

"My crow eats rice," said Revathi. "And bread, and a banana. And a cockroach and a moth. I watched for a whole week."

Shalini looked at the middle column. Then she took out her crow and moved the crow to the middle of her own chart. "I need a third side too," she said.

Miss Selvi saw the two charts and smiled. "How did you find out?" she asked.

"We didn't guess," said Revathi. "We watched."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Revathi could see Kaka's feathers and count Kaka's legs at once. She found Kaka's food group only by watching for a week. Two groups were not enough, so she made a third. Then she learned that humans can eat both, and each person chooses.

- Ask: Why did Revathi first put the crow on the plant side?
- Ask: What did Revathi do to find out what Kaka really eats?
- Ask: Paati eats plants and milk, and Aunty eats fish. Why did Paati say they are still the same kind of animal?

## Experiments
### e1 · Ant Lunch: do ants eat plant food, animal food, or both? (20 min)
- **question:** Do the ants in our home eat plant food, animal food, or both?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the ants come to the banana, the rice, the dead insect, or more than one? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We put the three foods out at the same time, each the same distance from the ants. The pieces are all small. We count the ants on each food at the same times. If one spot gets all the ants, we swap the foods round on another day and try again, to check the spot was not the reason.
- **safety:** Use the small ants of your kitchen, not big red ants, which can bite. Watch the ants, but don't touch them. Pick up the dead insect with a tissue, and wash your hands afterwards. Throw the food away at the end and wipe the spot.
- **record:** Draw the paper with the three foods on it. Draw a dot for each ant you counted on each food after 10 minutes. Write P next to plant food and A next to animal food.
- **results table:** Food | Plant food or animal food? | Ants after 5 minutes | Ants after 10 minutes
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the three foods. 2. Ask which are plant food and which is animal food. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. 4. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* The banana and the rice come from plants. The mosquito is an animal. Which will the ants come to? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Banana and rice come from plants. The mosquito is an animal. Which will the ants eat? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Ants like sweet things, so the banana.; Ants carry dead insects, I have seen it.; They might like all of them.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Every guess with a reason is a good guess. The test will answer it.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a place where ants walk. 2. Put the three foods on the paper, a hand-length apart. 3. Put the paper a hand-length from the ants. 4. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* Let's put the food where the ants can find it. Each food is the same distance from them. Now we wait and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Put each food on the paper. Keep them apart. Put the paper near the ants. Start the timer.
  - Note: Most Indian homes have small ants on a kitchen counter, a floor edge or a window sill. If you cannot find any, leave the paper near a window crack or the kitchen sink, and check every half hour. Start counting when the first ant arrives.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 5 minutes, count the ants on each food and write the numbers. 2. Count again at 10 minutes. 3. Watch what the ants do at each food.  
  *Parent says:* How many ants are on the banana? On the rice? On the mosquito? What are they doing there?  
  *Child mode:* Count the ants on each food. Write the numbers. What are the ants doing?
  - Ask: What are the ants doing? (good: Drinking the banana juice.; Pulling a leg off the mosquito.; Carrying bits away in a line.)
  - Note: Many kitchen ants come to both the banana and the dead insect, and fewer come to plain rice. Kinds of ants differ, so your numbers may be different. If almost all the ants went to the food nearest their line, do the test again on another day with the foods swapped round. If no ants came after 10 minutes, leave the paper and count again in one hour. Whatever you find is a real result.

### Covering, Legs and Food (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (sort) Sort these animals by body covering.  
   **Answer:** cow → Hair or fur; you → Hair or fur; parrot → Feathers; hen → Feathers; snake → Scales; fish → Scales; snail → Hard shell; tortoise → Hard shell. *Why:* The cow and you have hair. The parrot and the hen have feathers. The snake and the fish have scales. The snail and the tortoise have hard shells.
2. (match) Draw a line from each animal to the number of legs.  
   **Answer:** Earthworm → 0; Hen → 2; Goat → 4; Cockroach → 6; Spider → 8. *Why:* An earthworm has no legs, a hen has 2, a goat has 4, a cockroach has 6 like every insect, and a spider has 8.
3. (sort) Colour the plant-eaters green, the meat-eaters red, and the animals who eat both half green and half red.  
   **Answer:** cow → Plant-eater; goat → Plant-eater; tiger → Meat-eater; gecko → Meat-eater; crow → Eats both; hen → Eats both. *Why:* The cow and the goat eat only plants. The tiger and the gecko eat other animals. The crow and the hen eat both.
4. (draw) Draw your ant test: the paper, the three foods, and dots for the ants on each food.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of the banana, the rice and the insect, with dots showing where the ants gathered.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows all three foods, and shows clearly which foods had the most ants.
5. (truefalse) Every animal with feathers has 2 legs.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Only birds have feathers, and every bird has 2 legs, even an ostrich or a hen.
6. (odd) Circle the one who is not a plant-eater.  
   Options: Cow / Parrot / Tiger / Goat  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A tiger eats other animals, so a tiger is a meat-eater. The cow, the parrot and the goat eat plants.
7. (label) Write or say one way a crow and a hen are the same, and one way they are different.  
   **Answer:** ['Same: feathers, a beak, 2 legs, 2 wings, they lay eggs, or they eat both.', 'Different: a crow flies far and a hen flies only a short way, or a crow is black and grey.']. *Why:* Any true shared feature and any true difference is a good answer.
8. (write) A friend says a bat is a bird, because a bat has wings. What would you tell your friend?  
   **Answer:** A bat is not a bird. A bat has fur, not feathers, and the mother feeds her babies milk. Only birds have feathers.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the covering and the milk as evidence, not only a guess.
9. (count) Two hens and one cat sit in a yard. Count all their legs.  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Each hen has 2 legs, so 2 hens have 4. The cat has 4. 4 and 4 make 8.
10. (perspective) Kavya and her sister Anjali are sorting animal cards.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and gives a reason. Covering is quick and needs no watching, which helps a bird watcher. Food tells you how to look after an animal, which helps a zoo keeper or a vet.. *Why:* Both ways are good. Each one is better for a different job.
### Covering, Legs and Food (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) The Indian pangolin is covered in hard scales. A pangolin also has hair on the belly, and the mother feeds her baby milk. Which group is a pangolin in?  
   Options: Animals with hair who feed milk, like the cow / Snakes / Birds  
   **Answer:** Animals with hair who feed milk, like the cow. *Why:* Hair and milk put the pangolin with the mammals, like cows, bats and us. The scales alone could fool you.
2. (sort) Sort these animals by their number of legs.  
   **Answer:** snake → No legs; fish → No legs; crow → 2 legs; you → 2 legs; frog → 4 legs; gecko → 4 legs; ant → 6 legs; butterfly → 6 legs; spider → 8 legs. *Why:* Snakes and fish have no legs. The crow and you have 2. The frog and the gecko have 4. The ant and the butterfly are insects with 6. The spider has 8.
3. (fill) An animal who eats both plant food and animal food is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['omnivore']. *Why:* Omnivores, like crows, hens, ants and humans, eat both kinds of food.
4. (write) A frog tadpole eats tiny water plants. A grown frog eats insects. Which food group is a frog in? Why is this tricky?  
   **Answer:** A grown frog is a meat-eater, but a tadpole is mostly a plant-eater. It is tricky because the same frog changes food as they grow.. *Why:* A strong answer notices that the frog's food group depends on their age, so one label does not fit the whole life.
5. (draw) Draw two overlapping hoops. One is 'has feathers' and one is 'eats both'. Draw a crow, a parrot and yourself in the right places.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which animal has feathers?  
   Options: A fruit bat / A parrot / A gecko  
   **Answer:** A parrot. *Why:* Only birds have feathers, and a parrot is a bird. A fruit bat has fur, and a gecko has tiny scales.
2. (mcq) Which of these animals is covered in scales?  
   Options: A frog / A rat snake / A goat  
   **Answer:** A rat snake. *Why:* A rat snake has dry scales. A frog has bare, wet skin, and a goat has hair.
3. (mcq) An insect, like a cockroach, has how many legs?  
   Options: 4 / 6 / 8  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* Every insect has 6 legs. A spider has 8, so a spider is not an insect.
4. (mcq) Which animal has no legs?  
   Options: An earthworm / A frog / A hen  
   **Answer:** An earthworm. *Why:* An earthworm moves by stretching and squeezing their body. A frog has 4 legs and a hen has 2.
5. (mcq) A goat eats only leaves and grass. What is a goat?  
   Options: A herbivore / A carnivore / An omnivore  
   **Answer:** A herbivore. *Why:* An animal who eats only plants is a herbivore, a plant-eater.
6. (mcq) A gecko by the tube light eats moths and flies. What is a gecko?  
   Options: A plant-eater / A meat-eater / Eats both  
   **Answer:** A meat-eater. *Why:* Moths and flies are animals, so a gecko is a meat-eater, a carnivore. Choosing 'eats both' would need the gecko to eat plants too.
7. (mcq) Which is one way a crow and a cow are similar?  
   Options: Both have feathers / Both have 4 legs / Both have eyes and eat food  
   **Answer:** Both have eyes and eat food. *Why:* Both have eyes and both eat. Only the crow has feathers, and only the cow has 4 legs.
8. (mcq) Sorted by legs, who goes in the same group as you?  
   Options: A crow / A cow / A spider  
   **Answer:** A crow. *Why:* A crow has 2 legs, like you. Humans are animals, and we share the 2-legs group with birds.
9. (mcq) Sorted by covering, the hen goes with the crow. Sorted by food, who else joins them?  
   Options: A cow / An ant / A tiger  
   **Answer:** An ant. *Why:* The hen, the crow and the ant all eat both plant food and animal food. A cow eats only plants and a tiger eats only meat.
10. (mcq) Many scientists once thought chimpanzees ate only plants. How did Jane Goodall find out they eat animal food too?  
   Options: She read it in a book / She watched wild chimpanzees for months / She looked at their fur  
   **Answer:** She watched wild chimpanzees for months. *Why:* Jane Goodall watched wild chimpanzees in their forest home. She saw them eat meat and termites. Food is found by watching, not by looking at fur.
11. (mcq) Kavya, a girl in Class 2, wants to know what the ants in her kitchen eat. What should she do?  
   Options: Count their legs / Put out different foods and watch / Look at their colour  
   **Answer:** Put out different foods and watch. *Why:* You find out what an animal eats by watching what they choose. Legs and colour tell you nothing about food.

## Applications
- Zoo keepers and vets sort animals by food, so that each animal gets the right meals.
- Bird watchers sort birds by feathers, beaks and size, so they can name the bird they see.
- Farmers sort animals by food to plan their feed: hay for cows, grain for hens.
- Scientists who find a new animal compare the animal's covering, legs and food with animals they already know, to decide which group the animal belongs to.

## Spotted around us
- In many South Indian homes, a ball of rice is put out for crows in the morning. Crows come for it, and also eat insects and scraps.
- At a fish market in Kolkata, you can see the shiny scales on rohu and hilsa, and the hard shells of crabs.
- Goats on a lane in Ahmedabad nibble leaves from a hedge. They are plant-eaters.
- A house gecko waits by the tube light on many Indian walls at night, catching moths.

## Open questions
- **How does an ant colony know how much sugar and how much protein to collect?** Scientists know that colonies with many young collect more protein. How the ants share this information is still being studied.
- **How many kinds of insects are there?** About a million kinds of insects have been named. Many more have never been found, and guesses of the total are very different.
