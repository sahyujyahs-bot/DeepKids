# Our environment: the living and non-living world around us, and how we care for it
`sci.environment.caring.L3`

**Summary.** Shut a few moong seeds in a clear box with damp cotton and close the lid. No water is added for a week, yet they stay green, while the seeds in an open box dry out. Everything around a living thing is their environment. It has living parts, such as plants, animals, microbes and people, and non-living parts, such as air, water, soil, light and warmth. Some things in it are natural, and some are made by people. All these parts keep changing each other. Earth works like the closed box: sunlight comes in, but the same water and air go round and round, so what we pour or throw out stays in our shared home.

**Intro.** *Can a plant live inside a closed box, with no new water and no new air? Before you answer, say why you think so. In England, a man named David Latimer planted a bottle garden in 1960. He last watered it in 1972 and closed it with a cork. News reports say the plants were still growing more than forty years later.* In this unit you look at everything around you: the living things, the non-living things, and the things people have made. You find out how they change each other. You shut moong seeds in a box and see how they manage. You follow your kitchen water and your rubbish to find where 'away' really is. And you hear about eight people who lived for two years inside a sealed glass building.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what the environment is, and name living and non-living parts of the environment around my home.
1. I can sort things into natural and made by people, and explain why some things are a bit of both.

## Terms
- **Environment**: Everything around a living thing that affects them: air, water, soil, light, warmth, other living things and the things people make. *e.g.* Your environment today includes the air in your room, the fan, the crow at the window and the people at home.
- **Living thing**: Something that is alive: they take in food, breathe, grow, make young like themselves, sense and respond, and one day die. *e.g.* A crow, a tulsi plant, an ant and you are all living things.
- **Non-living thing**: Something that is not alive and does not grow, breathe or make young by itself. *e.g.* A stone, the air, water, a steel tumbler and a chair are non-living things.
- **Physical environment**: The non-living part of the environment: air, water, soil, rocks, light and warmth, and also buildings and roads. *e.g.* In a Chennai flat, the physical environment includes the hot afternoon air, the tap water and the concrete walls.
- **Living environment**: The living part of the environment: plants, animals, fungi, microbes and people. Books also call it the biological environment. *e.g.* The living environment of a balcony may hold a money plant, ants, a pigeon and the family.
- **Natural**: Made by nature, not by people. *e.g.* A river, a rock, a cloud, a coconut and a neem tree are natural.
- **Made by people**: Made by people from natural materials, using their skill and work. Books also say 'man-made' or 'human-made'. *e.g.* A road, a steel tumbler, a plastic bucket and a cotton shirt are made by people.
- **Evaporate**: When a liquid evaporates, it slowly turns into a gas that mixes into the air. Water that evaporates becomes water vapour, which we cannot see. *e.g.* A wet floor dries because its water evaporates into the air.
- **Condense**: When water vapour in the air cools, it condenses: it turns back into tiny drops of liquid water. *e.g.* Drops form on the outside of a cold steel tumbler because water vapour in the air condenses on it.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air that people and animals breathe in, and that plants and many microbes use too. Green plants and algae give it out when they make food in light. *e.g.* Every breath you take carries oxygen into your lungs.
- **Microbe**: A living thing too small to see without a microscope, such as a bacterium or a yeast cell. *e.g.* The bacteria that turn milk into curd are microbes.
- **Pollution**: Something harmful added to air, water or land, usually by people, in amounts the living things there cannot cope with. *e.g.* Smoke from burning rubbish is air pollution. Soapy water and sewage in a lake is water pollution.

## Explanations
### What the environment is · *defined*
**Definition:** The environment is everything around a living thing that affects them.

Look out of any window and you see part of your environment. It is the air you breathe and the water you drink. It is the sun that warms you, the floor under you and the noise of the road. It is also the crow on the wall, the ants in the kitchen, the people at home and the microbes in your curd. Every living thing has their own environment. An ant's environment is the floor, the crumbs, the other ants and the smell trails they leave. A fish's environment is mostly water. The word comes from an old French word for 'surroundings'.
- e.g. A pigeon's environment includes the ledge they nest on, the grain people throw and the cats that hunt them.
- e.g. A tulsi plant's environment includes the pot of soil, the sunlight, the water you pour and the ants that visit.
- e.g. Your environment at school is different from your environment at home, though you are the same person.
### Living and non-living parts · *defined*
**Definition:** The environment has a living part (plants, animals, fungi, microbes and people) and a non-living, or physical, part (air, water, soil, rocks, light, warmth, and the things people build).

Books split the environment into two parts. The living part is also called the biological environment. It holds every plant, animal, fungus and microbe, and people too. The non-living part is called the physical environment. It holds air, water, soil, rocks, sunlight and warmth. Buildings, roads and fans are non-living too, so they belong to the physical environment. To decide which part something belongs to, ask: does this grow, breathe and make young like itself? A dry seed is tricky. They look lifeless, but they are alive and will sprout when given water.
- e.g. Living: the money plant, the gecko on the wall, the bacteria in curd, your grandmother.
- e.g. Non-living: the tap water, the sunlight on the floor, the concrete wall, the ceiling fan.
- e.g. Tricky: a dry moong seed is alive, though they seem to do nothing. A wooden door was once part of a living tree, but the door is not alive.
### Natural, made by people, or both · *defined*
**Definition:** Natural things are made by nature. Things made by people are made from natural materials using human skill and work.

A river, a cloud and a coconut are natural. A road, a steel tumbler and a plastic bucket are made by people. But everything people make starts as something natural. Steel is made from iron ore dug out of the ground. Plastic is made mostly from oil or gas from deep under the ground. Paper is made from wood. Many things are a bit of both. Curd is made by people, but microbes do the work. A potted tulsi is a natural plant, placed and cared for by people. Many old lakes in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, called eri or kere, were dug and banked by people long ago, and now fish and birds live in them.
- e.g. Natural: a neem tree, rain, a crow, sand.
- e.g. Made by people: a bus, a steel plate, a nylon rope, a brick.
- e.g. Both: curd, a mango tree planted by your grandfather, a temple tank full of fish.
### The parts change each other · *defined*
**Definition:** The living and non-living parts of the environment change each other all the time.

The non-living parts decide who can live where. Moss grows in a damp corner, not on a dry, sunny wall. Geckos wait near the tube light, because insects come to the light at night. Living things change the non-living parts too. Plants take water from the soil and give it out through tiny holes in their leaves, into the air. In light, green plants take carbon dioxide from the air and give out oxygen. Earthworms and microbes turn dead leaves into soil. A peepal seed that lands in a crack in a wall can grow roots that split the wall. People change the environment most of all. We build roads and flats, dig lakes, cut forests, plant trees, and send smoke, sewage and rubbish out of our homes.
- e.g. A moong seedling drinks water from the cotton and gives some of it to the air.
- e.g. A peepal growing on an old wall slowly breaks the bricks with their roots.
- e.g. A city built of concrete gets hotter in summer than the green land around it.
### Earth is like a closed box · *defined*
**Definition:** Earth gets energy from outside, as sunlight, but almost no new water or air. The same water and air are used again and again.

Remember the closed box test, with moong seeds on damp cotton? In the closed box, water evaporated from the cotton and the leaves, condensed on the cold lid, and ran back down. The water went round inside the box. Earth works the same way. Earth has no lid, but gravity holds the air and water close to Earth. Sunlight comes in from outside, and heat goes back out to space. But almost no new water arrives, and almost none leaves. So the water in your glass has fallen as rain and flowed in rivers many times. It has been inside many plants and animals before you. The oxygen in the air was made by plants, algae and some bacteria, and it is breathed in and given out again and again.
- e.g. Water you pour down the sink does not disappear. It goes to a drain, a treatment plant, a river, a lake or the sea, and later rises again as water vapour.
- e.g. Smoke from burning rubbish spreads through the air and comes down with rain, or is breathed in by people far away.
- e.g. Plastic thrown into a drain can reach the sea and stay there for many years.
### Where is 'away'? · *defined*
**Definition:** When we throw something away or wash it away, it does not leave the Earth. It only goes somewhere else in the environment.

We say 'throw it away' as if 'away' were a place outside the world. It is not. In Chennai, much of the city's rubbish is taken to big dump yards at Kodungaiyur and Perungudi. In Mumbai, much of it goes to Deonar. Waste water from homes flows through drains. Some of it is cleaned at sewage treatment plants, and some flows straight into rivers, lakes or the sea. In Bengaluru, soap and sewage flowing into Bellandur Lake made thick white froth that blew onto the roads. Food peels can rot back into soil, if they are kept apart from plastic. Plastic and glass stay much as they are for a very long time. So caring for the environment starts with asking, 'Where will this go next?'
- e.g. A banana peel in a compost pot becomes soil in a few weeks.
- e.g. A plastic wrapper thrown on the road can be washed into a drain by the rain.
- e.g. Cooking oil poured down a sink can stick inside the pipes and block them.
### What helps most when we care for the environment? · *debated*
Everyone agrees that air, water and land need care. People disagree about what helps most. Some say small changes by every family matter most: carrying a cloth bag, sorting waste, not wasting water. Others say the big changes matter most: how cities treat sewage, how factories work, and how much forest is kept. Many people say both are needed, because big changes start when many people care.
- e.g. A family in Mysuru sorts their waste into wet and dry, so the wet waste can be composted.
- e.g. A city builds a sewage treatment plant, so far less dirty water reaches the lake.
- e.g. Women in Reni village in the Himalayas, led by Gaura Devi, stood around their trees in 1974 to stop them being cut. This was part of the Chipko movement.
- **Many teachers and families:** Each family's choices add up, because a city is made of families. Small habits, done every day by millions of people, change how much waste and dirty water there is.
- **Many environmental scientists and planners:** Most pollution comes from big sources: cities without sewage treatment, factories, power plants and vehicles. Changing those makes the biggest difference, so laws and city plans matter most.
- **People in movements like Chipko and Appiko:** Ordinary people acting together can change what governments and companies do. In 1983, villagers near Sirsi in Karnataka hugged trees to stop them being felled. 'Appiko' means 'hug' in Kannada.

## Mostly agreed
- The environment has living parts and non-living parts, and they change each other all the time. *How we know:* We can watch it: plants give water to the air, moss grows only in damp places, and people build, dig and plant.
- Earth gets energy from the Sun but almost no new water or air. Water and air go round and round. *How we know:* Scientists measure the water cycle and the air. Only tiny amounts of matter arrive from space as dust and meteorites.
- Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by living things. *How we know:* Rocks show that early Earth had almost no oxygen in the air. Oxygen built up after tiny living things in the sea, called cyanobacteria, began making it in sunlight, over two billion years ago.
- What we throw out or pour away stays somewhere in the environment. *How we know:* Dump yards, plastic found on beaches and in the sea, and froth on city lakes show where waste ends up.
- A closed container with plants, water and light can keep the plants alive for a long time without new water. *How we know:* Bottle gardens and the closed box test both show water going round inside: drops on the lid, then back into the soil or cotton.

## How we found out
**The eight people of the first Biosphere 2 mission, among them Jane Poynter and Linda Leigh, and the scientist Jeff Severinghaus, 1991 to 1993 (the mission), and 1994 (the oxygen study).** In 1991, eight people walked into Biosphere 2, a huge sealed glass building in the desert of Arizona, USA. Inside were a small rainforest, a desert, a little sea, farm plots and animals. The doors were shut for two years. The planners thought the plants would make all the oxygen the people needed, so the air would stay the same. They were wrong. Month by month, the oxygen fell, until people felt tired and breathless. In January 1993, fresh oxygen had to be pumped in. Scientists, among them Jeff Severinghaus, measured the gases carefully. They found that the soil, full of rich compost, held huge numbers of microbes. The microbes breathed oxygen as they broke down the compost. They should have made extra carbon dioxide, but it went missing too. The bare concrete walls had taken it up. In a closed world, even the soil and the walls are part of the environment.  
*What people believed before:* The planners thought that plants alone would keep the air in a sealed world steady. Careful measuring showed that soil microbes were using up oxygen faster than the plants made it, and that the concrete walls were taking up carbon dioxide.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The environment means wild places far away, like forests and the sea, not the city.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Forests and seas have many more wild plants and animals than a city street.  
  Against: A city flat has an environment too: air, water, light, ants, geckos, pigeons, potted plants and people. City life also depends on rivers, lakes and farms far away.
- **Natural things are always good and things made by people are always bad for the environment.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Many things people make, like plastic bags and smoke, do harm, and natural forests and lakes support a lot of life.  
  Against: Some natural things are harmful, like a snake's venom or a flood. Some made things help, like a sewage treatment plant, a water filter or a bird bath. What matters is what a thing does, not who made it.
- **One family is far too small to change the environment.** (Some people; *debated*)  
  For: One family's rubbish is a tiny part of a whole city's.  
  Against: A city is made of families, and habits spread from family to family. Laws such as India's 2022 ban on many single-use plastic items came after many people asked for change.
- **Biosphere 2 showed that people can never build a working closed world.** (Some writers after the first Biosphere 2 mission; *debated*)  
  For: The oxygen fell so low that fresh oxygen had to be pumped in, and many plants and animals died out.  
  Against: The mission taught scientists exactly why the oxygen fell: rich soil full of microbes and fresh concrete. Small closed worlds, like bottle gardens, can run for decades. Space agencies still study closed life-support systems.

## Roots
We have looked at the living and non-living parts of our environment, and at the closed box where the same water went round. Now we ask what it means to share one world.

**Deep truth.** Your open box got lighter as its water evaporated, but that water did not vanish. It went into the air of your room, and in time out to the clouds. Earth is like your closed box: sunlight comes in, and the same water and air go round. So whatever we throw away stays somewhere in the world we all share.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say we throw things 'away' or wash them 'away', as if they leave the world. Out of sight feels like gone. But the water from the open box, the rasam down the sink and the wrapper in the bin all stay on Earth, somewhere else.  
*Evidence:* The open box dried out and got lighter, while the room's air took its water. The closed box kept drops on its lid all week and stayed almost the same weight, because its water could not leave.

- *who am i:* About two-thirds of your body is water. That water was once in rivers, clouds and other living things, and it will go back to them.
- *what is life:* Every living thing takes in water and air from their environment, uses them, and gives them back changed.
- *what is this world:* The Earth gets light and warmth from the Sun, but keeps almost all its own water and air, held close by gravity.

The same water moves between the sea, the clouds, the rain, the tap, your body and the drain. The air you breathe out is breathed in by plants, and the oxygen they give out is breathed in by you. So every living thing on Earth shares one supply of water and air. What one of us puts into it reaches the others, near and far.

- **Atharva Veda 12.1.12, the hymn to the Earth (Bhumi Sukta):** The Earth is my mother, and I am her child. (Your body is built from the Earth's water, air and food. What you take from the Earth, and what you give back, stays in the same family.)
- **Thirukkural 211, by Thiruvalluvar:** Duty does not ask for a return. What does the world give back to the rain cloud? (Rain falls on every field and every roof, and the cloud asks nothing back. The water cycle gives to all living things alike.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 1:** All this, whatever moves in this world, is filled by the Lord. Enjoy it by giving up. Do not covet anyone's wealth. (Water and air belong to no one person. Each of us uses them for a while, and then they pass on to others.)
- Science (settled): The total amount of water on Earth stays almost the same. It keeps moving between sea, air, land and living things.
- Science (settled): Earth loses a tiny amount of light gas to space and gains some tonnes of space dust each day. Both are tiny next to its air and seas.
- Science (open): Whether people can build a sealed home that keeps them alive for years without help from outside is not yet known.
- Other view, Many engineers and city planners: Things do stay on Earth, but that alone is not the problem. A little waste spread through a lot of water or soil can be broken down safely by microbes. What matters is how much goes where, and whether it is treated. With good treatment plants and recycling, 'away' can be made to work.
- Practice, Where has this water been? (3 min): Let's each hold a glass of water. Look at it quietly. This water may once have been in the sea, in a cloud, in a mango or in a river far away. Now let's drink it slowly, one sip at a time. Now breathe out onto a cold steel plate. See the mist? Some of that water is already leaving you, on its way round again.
- Ask: The water you drank today was somewhere else last year. Where might it have been?
- Ask: If there is no 'away', where does a wrapper go when we drop it on the road?
- Ask: The seedlings in the closed box changed the air and water around them. What do you change in the air of your room every day?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The water in your closed box went from cotton to air to lid and back again. Is the water in your body part of you, or part of the environment passing through? (hint: Think about where the water in you came from this morning, and where it goes when you breathe out or sweat.)
- *big:* If a pigeon lives on our balcony, is the balcony part of the pigeon's home or ours? (hint: Think about who uses the balcony, for what, and at what time of day.)

- *big:* If there is no 'away', what should we do with things we no longer need? (hint: Think of things that can rot, things that can be used again, and things that can be made into something new.)

## Story: The Bottle on Ammamma's Shelf
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: water in a closed bottle evaporates, condenses on the cool glass and runs back down, and kitchen water really does travel through drains to treatment plants, lakes, rivers or the sea.*

In the summer holidays, Arjun came from Hyderabad to stay with his cousin Kavya in Madurai. Arjun was seven. Kavya was nine, and she knew every corner of their grandmother's flat.

On the first morning, Arjun found a big glass bottle on the shelf by the window. It was round like a pot, with a narrow neck and a cork pushed in tight. Inside was a little green world. A money plant curled along the bottom. Soft moss covered a few stones. A tiny fern stood in one corner.

"Who waters it?" Arjun asked.

"Nobody," said Ammamma, from the kitchen. "I closed that bottle eight years ago. I have not opened it since."

Arjun looked at the plants. They were green and fresh. One money plant leaf was new and still pale. "That's not possible," he said. "Plants need water. You must be watering it at night when nobody sees."

Ammamma laughed. "Ask Kavya to help you find out."

Kavya pulled a stool up to the shelf. "Look at the glass, Arjun. Not the plants. The glass."

Arjun pressed his nose close. The inside of the glass was covered with tiny drops, like the outside of a cold tumbler of water. Near the top, the drops were so small they made the glass misty. Lower down, some drops had joined into bigger ones. As he watched, one big drop slid down the glass and disappeared into the moss.

"Water," said Arjun. "But where did it come from? There's a cork."

"Let's watch it all day," said Kavya. "Then you tell me."

So they did. In the cool early morning, the glass was misty all over. Kavya touched the outside. "Feel. The glass is cooler than the air inside the bottle. Water vapour from the soil and the leaves turns back into drops on the cool glass. That's called condensing."

By the afternoon, the sun had warmed the room. The mist on the glass was almost gone. Kavya pointed at the soil. "The drops ran down into the soil this morning. Now the warm soil and the leaves are giving the water back to the air inside. That's called evaporating. You can't see water vapour, but it's in there."

"So the same water goes up and down," said Arjun slowly. "Up from the soil and the leaves. Onto the glass. Down into the soil again."

"Every day," said Ammamma, "for eight years."

"What about air?" asked Arjun. "The plants need air too."

"In the light, the leaves make their food from water and a gas in the air called carbon dioxide," said Kavya. "They give out oxygen. Then the plants, and the tiny microbes in the soil, breathe some of that oxygen and give out carbon dioxide again. So the air goes round too."

Arjun sat back on the stool. He was quiet for a while. Then he said, "Nothing new goes in. Nothing goes out. Only the light."

That evening, after dinner, Arjun scraped the leftover rasam from his plate into the sink. He turned on the tap and washed it down. "There," he said. "Gone."

Kavya looked at him. "Gone where?"

Arjun opened his mouth and shut it again.

"Come," said Kavya. She took a torch, and they went out to the balcony. A grey pipe ran from under the kitchen sink, through the wall, and down the side of the building. They followed it with the torch, down past the flat below, and the one below that, to the ground. There it joined a bigger pipe that went under the road.

"Where does that one go?" Arjun asked.

Ammamma had come out behind them. "To a big drain, and then to a plant where the dirty water is cleaned," she said. "Then the cleaner water goes into a river or out to the sea. Not every city cleans all its drain water. In some places, it flows straight into a lake."

"So the rasam isn't gone," said Arjun. "It's just somewhere else."

"The water in it will rise from the sea one day as water vapour," said Kavya. "Then it will come down as rain. Maybe on Madurai."

Arjun looked up at the sky. The first stars were coming out above the temple towers. "Is the whole world like Ammamma's bottle?"

"In one way, yes," said Ammamma. "There is no glass around the Earth. But the Earth's pull holds the air and the water close. The sunlight comes in. The same water and the same air go round and round. Whatever we pour out or throw out stays in the bottle with us."

The next morning, Arjun was up before Kavya. She found him at the shelf, watching a drop slide down the glass. Beside him, on the floor, was a small steel bowl.

"What's that for?" Kavya asked.

"For the rice water," said Arjun. "Ammamma says it's good for her tulsi. I'm not washing it away any more. I'm sending it somewhere on purpose."

**For the parent:** For the parent: a closed bottle garden keeps going because water evaporates from the soil and leaves, condenses on the cooler glass and runs back down, and because plants and soil microbes keep passing oxygen and carbon dioxide between them. Only light enters. Earth works the same way for water and air: sunlight comes in and heat leaves, but almost no matter arrives or escapes. Arjun's leap is that 'washing it away' only moves the rasam somewhere else in that shared bottle.

- Ask: Where did the drops on the inside of Ammamma's bottle come from?
- Ask: Why was the glass misty in the morning but clear in the afternoon?
- Ask: What happened to Arjun's rasam after it went down the sink?
- Ask: In what way is the Earth like Ammamma's bottle? In what way is it different?
- Ask: What did Arjun decide to do with the rice water, and why?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Closed Box Test: can moong seedlings live with the lid on? (30 min, 7 days)
- **question:** If we shut moong seeds in a closed box with damp cotton, and add no more water or air, will they live longer or shorter than seeds in an open box?
- **predict:** Which box will keep its seedlings green longer, the closed one or the open one? Why do you think so? Think about water, and think about air.
- **fair test:** Both boxes are the same size. Each gets the same cotton, the same 3 tablespoons of water and 10 soaked moong seeds from the same bowl. Both stand side by side in the same place. Only one thing is different: one box has its lid on. We use 10 seeds, not 1, because one seed might be dead.
- **safety:** Wash hands after handling the seeds and cotton. If white or grey fuzzy mould grows, do not open the box near your face. Throw the cotton and seeds in the wet waste at the end, and wash the boxes.
- **record:** Fill in the table on days 2, 4 and 7. On day 7, draw both boxes side by side and draw arrows where you think the water went in each.
- **results table:** Day | Closed box: drops on the lid? | Closed box: cotton damp or dry? | Closed box: seedlings | Open box: cotton damp or dry? | Open box: seedlings
- **schedule:** day 0: The night before: soak 20 moong seeds in a bowl of water. (2 min); day 1: Set up both boxes. Guess what will happen and why. Close one lid. Weigh both boxes if you have a scale. (20 min); day 2: Look for drops on the lid of the closed box. Touch the cotton in the open box. Fill in the table. (3 min); day 4: Look at both boxes again and fill in the table. (3 min); day 7: Last look and weighing. Say your claim, your evidence and your reasoning. (10 min)
- **s0 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The night before, put 20 whole moong seeds in a bowl and cover them with water.  
  *Parent says:* Tonight we soak some moong seeds, so they are ready to sprout tomorrow.  
  *Child mode:* Put 20 moong seeds in a bowl. Cover them with water.
  - Note: Soaking for 8 to 12 hours makes the seeds sprout within a day. Any whole green moong from the kitchen works. Split moong dal will not sprout.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay cotton wool about 1 cm thick in each box. 2. Pour 3 tablespoons of water evenly over each cotton. 3. Spread 10 soaked seeds on each. 4. Label one box 'Lid on' and one 'Lid off'.  
  *Parent says:* Both boxes get exactly the same cotton, the same water and the same number of seeds. Why do you think that matters?  
  *Child mode:* Put cotton in both boxes. Add 3 spoons of water to each. Put 10 seeds on each.
  - Note: Count the spoons aloud together. Keeping everything the same means any difference must come from the lid. The cotton should be damp all through, with no pool of water on top.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold up the lid. Ask your child to guess before you close it. Wait for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* I'm going to close this box tight and leave the other one open. We won't add any more water for a week. Which seeds do you think will do better? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Which box will keep its seedlings green? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen in the closed box? (good: Any guess with a reason. Many children say the closed seeds will die because they have no air. Others say the closed box will keep its water. Both are good guesses.)
  - Note: Do not hint. Write the guess and the reason on the label or in the notebook, so you can look back on day 7.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press the lid on one box firmly all round. 2. Put both boxes side by side in a bright place, out of direct sun. 3. If you have a kitchen scale, weigh each box and write the weights down.  
  *Parent says:* Now the closed box has nothing coming in except light. Let's put them where it is bright, but not in hot sun.  
  *Child mode:* Close one lid tight. Put both boxes in a bright spot. No hot sun!
  - Note: Direct sun can heat a closed box enough to cook the seedlings, so choose a bright windowsill that gets no midday sun, or a table near a window. A sunny balcony is too hot.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Next morning: look closely at the lid of the closed box. Touch the cotton in the open box with one finger. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the lid of the closed box. What do you see on it? Now feel the cotton in the open box. Is it as wet as yesterday?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the closed lid. Drops? Feel the open box cotton. Still wet?
  - Note: By morning, the inside of the closed lid is usually misty or covered with drops. Most seeds in both boxes have small white roots. Do not open the closed box at any point.
  - Wait: Next morning (day 2)
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 4: look at both boxes and fill in the table again.  
  *Parent says:* What has changed in each box since day 2? Look at the cotton, the drops and the seedlings.  
  *Child mode:* Look at both boxes. What changed? Fill in your table.
  - Note: In a warm Indian home, the open box's cotton is often dry by day 3 or 4, and those seedlings start to droop. In cool or rainy weather it may take a few days longer, so keep checking until there is a clear difference. The closed box should still be misty, with seedlings growing up towards the lid.
  - Wait: Day 4
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 7: 1. Look at both boxes. 2. Weigh them again, if you weighed them on day 1. 3. Ask for a claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What happened in each box? What did you see that tells you so? Why do you think it happened? If we weighed them, what happened to each weight?  
  *Child mode:* What happened? What did you see? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Where did the water in the open box go? (good: Into the air of the room.; It evaporated.; Some went into the seedlings and out of their leaves into the room.)
  - Ask: Where did the water in the closed box go? (good: It went into the air in the box, onto the lid, and dripped back down.; It went round and round inside the box.; Nowhere outside the box.)
  - Note: With a scale, the closed box weighs almost the same as on day 1, within a few grams, even though the seedlings grew. Almost nothing left it. A takeaway lid is not perfectly airtight, so a tiny loss is normal. The open box is lighter, because water left it as vapour. If your closed box grew mould, that is another living thing that lived on the same water and air.
  - Wait: Day 7
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about why the closed seedlings did not run out of air. Then talk about Earth.  
  *Parent says:* The closed box never got new air. In the light, the leaves make oxygen, and the seedlings and microbes breathe it again. Earth is a bit like this box. What comes in from outside?  
  *Child mode:* Nothing new came into the closed box. Only light. Is Earth like that?
  - Check: What came into the closed box from outside during the week? → **Only light**
### e2 · The Drain Test: does washing powder in water change how seeds sprout? (25 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Do moong seeds sprout and grow the same in soapy water as in plain water? Does the amount of soap matter?
- **predict:** Which cup of seeds will grow the longest roots by day 3: plain water, a little soap or a lot of soap? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each cup gets the same cotton, 10 seeds from the same packet and 2 tablespoons of its own water. All three cups stand together in the same place. Only the water changes. We keep adding the same water to each cup if it dries.
- **safety:** A grown-up mixes the washing powder. Keep it away from eyes and mouths. Wash hands after touching soapy cotton. Pour the soapy water away at the end into the toilet, not onto a potted plant.
- **record:** Fill in the table on days 2 and 3. Draw one seedling from each cup, the same size as in real life.
- **results table:** Cup | Seeds sprouted on day 2 (out of 10) | Seeds sprouted on day 3 (out of 10) | Longest root on day 3 (cm) | What the roots look like
- **schedule:** day 1: Mix the three waters. Set up three cups with 10 seeds each. Guess and give a reason. (15 min); day 2: Count the sprouted seeds in each cup. Add a spoon of the right water to any cup that is drying. (3 min); day 3: Count again and measure the longest root in each cup. Say your claim, evidence and reasoning. (7 min); day 4: One last look. Clear up. (2 min)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill three tumblers with water. 2. Leave the first plain. 3. Stir a small pinch of washing powder into the second. 4. Stir 1 level teaspoon into the third. 5. Label them.  
  *Parent says:* Water from our washing goes down the drain. Some of it reaches lakes, where seeds and plants live. Let's see what soapy water does to seeds.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up mixes the soap. One plain, one a little, one a lot.
  - Note: A pinch is about what you hold between two fingers. A level teaspoon in a tumbler is far stronger than most drain water, so it makes the effect easy to see.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put cotton in each cup. 2. Pour 2 tablespoons of the matching water on each. 3. Put 10 dry moong seeds on each cup. 4. Label the cups.  
  *Parent says:* Which cup do you think will grow the longest roots? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put 10 seeds on each cup. Guess which grows best. Why?
  - Ask: What do you think the soap will do? (good: Any guess with a reason, for example that soap cleans, so it cannot hurt, or that soap is not meant for plants, so it may harm them.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three cups side by side in the same place, out of direct sun.  
  *Parent says:* All three cups sit together, so they get the same warmth and light. Only the water is different.  
  *Child mode:* Put the three cups together. Wait till tomorrow.
  - Note: If the cups dry out on day 2, add one more spoon of each cup's own water.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2: count how many seeds in each cup have a white root. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* How many seeds have sprouted in each cup? Let's count them together.  
  *Child mode:* Count the seeds with roots in each cup.
  - Wait: Day 2
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 3: 1. Count again. 2. Measure the longest root in each cup with a ruler. 3. Ask for a claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What did the soap do? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think that happened?  
  *Child mode:* What did the soap do? Show me how you know.
  - Note: Usually the plain cup grows the longest white roots. The 'a lot' cup has fewer sprouts, or short, brownish roots. The 'a little' cup is often in between, or close to plain. If all three grow the same in your home, that is a real result too. Write it down, and talk about how much soap a lake would need to get.
  - Wait: Day 3

### c1 · A Water Bowl for Birds on a Hot Balcony (30 min)
- **problem:** Make a water station for birds that stays cool, is easy to clean every day, keeps birds safe from cats and does not become a place for mosquitoes to breed.

- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Build the best idea. Fix it firmly where a grown-up says.  
  *Parent says:* Where can we put it so cats cannot reach it and it will not fall?  
  *Child mode:* Build it with a grown-up. Fix it so it cannot fall.

## Applications
- Sewage treatment plants clean a city's waste water with screens, settling tanks and microbes, before it goes back to a river or the sea.
- The East Kolkata Wetlands clean much of Kolkata's sewage in fish ponds, where sunlight, algae and microbes do the work.
- Tamil Nadu made rainwater harvesting compulsory for buildings in 2003, so rain soaks back into the ground instead of running off into drains.
- India's Solid Waste Management Rules of 2016 ask every home to keep wet, dry and hazardous waste apart, so each can be treated in the right way.
- Space stations recycle water: the International Space Station turns astronauts' breath, sweat and urine back into drinking water.
- Bottle gardens, also called terrariums, are kept in homes and offices because a closed one needs very little watering.
- Air quality stations in Indian cities measure the air every hour and give it a number, the Air Quality Index (AQI).
- Cities restore old lakes, such as the eris of Chennai and the keres of Bengaluru, by stopping sewage and clearing their inlets.
- Compost centres run by city corporations turn wet waste from homes into compost for parks and farms.

## Open questions
- **How long can a sealed bottle garden live?** Some have lived for decades, like David Latimer's. Nobody knows the limit. It depends on whether the microbes keep turning dead leaves back into food for the plants.
- **Can people build a sealed home that keeps them alive for years, on the Moon or on Mars?** Biosphere 2 needed fresh oxygen after about a year and a half. Teams in Russia and China have since kept people for months in sealed homes, but food or supplies still came from outside.
- **How many kinds of microbes live around us?** A pinch of soil holds thousands of kinds of bacteria. Most have never been grown in a lab or given a name, so nobody knows how many there are.
- **Where does most of the plastic that reaches the sea end up?** Scientists find much less plastic floating on the sea than they expect. Some sinks, some breaks into tiny pieces, and some washes onto beaches. How much goes where is still being measured.
- **How much dust from space lands on the Earth each day?** Different ways of measuring give answers from a few tonnes to over a hundred tonnes a day. Either way, it is tiny next to the Earth.

## Connected maths
- In the Closed Box Test, the open box weighed 120 grams on day 1 and 82 grams on day 7. How many grams of water left the open box? **38 grams.**. Take the day 7 weight from the day 1 weight. 120 take away 80 is 40. 40 take away 2 more is 38. So 38 grams of water evaporated into the room's air. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Look at the kitchen scale. Each big mark is 10 grams and each small mark is 2 grams. The pointer is 1 small mark past 110. What does the closed box weigh? **112 grams.**. The pointer has passed the 110 mark by one small mark. Each small mark is 2 grams. 110 + 2 = 112. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- In the Drain Test, 10 seeds were put in each cup. 10 sprouted in plain water, 9 in 'a little' soap and 4 in 'a lot'. How many more seeds sprouted in plain water than in 'a lot'? **6 more seeds.**. 10 take away 4 is 6. So 6 more seeds sprouted in plain water than in the cup with a lot of washing powder. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Farhan's family counted plastic bags brought home: 4 on Monday, 3 on Tuesday, 1 on Wednesday, 0 on Thursday and 0 on Friday. How many bags did they bring home in the 5 days? **8 bags.**. Add the days: 4 + 3 = 7, 7 + 1 = 8, and 0 + 0 adds nothing. So 8 bags in 5 days, and none on the last two days, once they carried a cloth bag. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
