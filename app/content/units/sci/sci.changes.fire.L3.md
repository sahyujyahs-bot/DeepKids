# Fire: what a flame needs, how to stay safe, and how people learned to use it
`sci.changes.fire.L3`

**Summary.** A match held to a lump of wax only melts it, yet the same wax burns for hours in a candle. That is because a flame burns gas. The flame's heat melts the wax, the wick carries it up, and the heat turns it into a gas that joins with oxygen from the air. Every fire needs three things: fuel, oxygen and heat. Take away any one, and the fire goes out, which is how every fire safety rule works. People have used fire for perhaps a million years, first by keeping flames from wildfires alive and later by making fire with friction and sparks.

**Intro.** *Watch with a grown-up. A candle is blown out, and a thin line of white smoke rises. A lit match is held in the smoke, a few centimetres above the wick. The flame runs down the smoke and lights the candle again, without touching it. How did the flame travel down the smoke?* Fire seems to eat wood, wax and oil. But a flame is really burning gas. In this unit you test what a candle really burns. You find out the three things every fire needs, and how taking away one of them puts a fire out. You learn the fire safety rules for a home with a gas stove, lamps and crackers. And you find out how people first tamed fire, and how a scientist made a lamp that could not set fire to the gas in coal mines.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the three things every fire needs: fuel, oxygen from the air, and heat.
1. I can explain, using the smoke test, that a candle's wax burns only after heat turns it into a gas.
2. I can say how each way of putting out a fire takes away fuel, oxygen or heat, and why water must never go on burning oil.
3. I can say the fire safety rules at home and the reason for each: matches, the gas stove, lamps, crackers, clothes on fire and burns.
4. I can tell how people learned to keep, carry and make fire, and what fire changed in their lives.
5. I can tell how Humphry Davy's lamp stopped its flame from lighting the gas in coal mines, and why the lamp was less safe than people thought.

## Terms
- **Fire**: Fire is what we see when a fuel joins with oxygen so fast that it gives out heat and light. *e.g.* A diya's flame, the blue ring on a gas stove and a heap of burning leaves are all fire.
- **Flame**: A flame is the glowing part of a fire, where gas is burning. *e.g.* The pointed yellow light above a candle's wick is a flame.
- **Burning**: Burning, also called combustion, is a change in which a fuel joins with oxygen and gives out heat and light. New substances are made, so it cannot be undone. *e.g.* A matchstick that has burned is black and thin. It can never become a new matchstick again.
- **Fuel**: A fuel is anything that can burn and give out heat. *e.g.* Wax, cooking gas, wood, kerosene, oil in a diya and paper are all fuels.
- **Oxygen**: Oxygen is a gas in the air. It has no colour and no smell. About one part in five of the air is oxygen. Fires and living beings both need it. *e.g.* A candle under a closed box soon goes out, because the fire uses up the oxygen near it.
- **Fire triangle**: The fire triangle is a picture of the three things every fire needs: fuel, oxygen and heat. Take away one side, and the fire goes out. *e.g.* Turning off the gas takes away the fuel, so the stove flame goes out.
- **Wick**: A wick is a string of cotton in a candle or lamp. Melted wax or oil climbs up it to the flame. *e.g.* The twisted cotton thread in a diya is a wick.
- **Gas**: A gas is a form of matter that spreads out to fill any space, like air or steam. Heat can turn a solid or a liquid into a gas. *e.g.* When water boils, it turns into a gas called steam. When a candle burns, wax turns into a gas.
- **Smoke**: Smoke is the cloud of tiny bits of soot, ash and droplets that rises from a fire that is not burning everything fully. *e.g.* A blown-out candle gives a ribbon of white smoke. Burning plastic gives thick black smoke, which is poisonous.
- **Soot**: Soot is a black powder of carbon left when a fuel does not burn fully. *e.g.* The black patch on the wall above an oil lamp is soot.
- **Ignite**: To ignite something is to start it burning. It must get hot enough first. *e.g.* A grown-up ignites the gas stove with a lighter.
- **Extinguish**: To extinguish a fire is to put it out. *e.g.* Covering a burning pan with its lid extinguishes the fire.
- **Flammable**: Something flammable catches fire easily. *e.g.* Petrol, kerosene, cooking gas, dry paper and thin synthetic cloth are flammable.
- **Ember**: An ember is a small piece of wood or charcoal that glows red and keeps burning slowly without a flame. *e.g.* After a wood fire dies down, the embers glow red for hours.
- **Friction**: Friction is the grip between two surfaces that rub. Rubbing turns movement into heat. *e.g.* Rub your palms fast for ten seconds and they get warm. That warmth comes from friction.
- **Fire drill**: A fire drill is a wooden stick spun fast against a piece of wood until the rubbing makes a tiny glowing ember. It is one of the oldest ways to make fire. (A fire drill is also a practice of what to do in a fire.) *e.g.* In some old Vedic rituals in India, priests still light the fire with a wooden fire drill called an arani.
- **LPG**: LPG is the cooking gas in a cylinder. Its name is short for liquefied petroleum gas. The gas has almost no smell of its own, so a strong smell is added to warn people of a leak. *e.g.* If you smell gas in the kitchen, the smell was added on purpose, so that you notice the leak.
- **Firedamp**: Firedamp is the old miners' name for a gas that seeps out of coal. It is mostly methane, and it explodes if a flame lights it. *e.g.* Firedamp caused many deadly explosions in coal mines in the 1700s and 1800s.

## Explanations
### What fire is · *defined*
**Definition:** Fire is a fast chemical change called burning, or combustion. A fuel joins with oxygen from the air and gives out heat and light.

When a fuel burns, it does not just disappear. Its particles join with oxygen from the air and make new substances. Most of them are gases you cannot see: carbon dioxide and water vapour. Some of the fuel may be left as soot or ash. This is a chemical change, because new substances are made. You cannot turn ash and smoke back into wood. Burning also gives out a lot of energy, as heat and light. That is why people use fire to cook, to stay warm and to see at night.
- e.g. A matchstick burns to a black, curled stick. It cannot be made new again.
- e.g. A cold steel tumbler held high above a gas flame gets a little misty, because burning makes water vapour.
- e.g. Firewood leaves only a small heap of ash. Most of the wood went into the air as gases.
### The fire triangle: fuel, oxygen and heat · *defined*
**Definition:** Every fire needs three things at once: a fuel, oxygen, and enough heat. Take away any one, and the fire goes out.

Think of the three things as the three sides of a triangle. The fuel is what burns, such as wax, wood, gas or oil. The oxygen comes from the air around the fire. The heat must be enough to start the burning and to keep it going. A matchbox on a shelf has fuel and air all around it, but it does not burn, because it is not hot enough. A candle under a closed box has fuel and heat, but it goes out when the oxygen near it is used up. A stove with the gas turned off has air and a hot burner, but no fuel.
- e.g. A matchstick has fuel and air. Striking it gives the heat, and it lights.
- e.g. Sand thrown on a small fire covers it and keeps the air away.
- e.g. Water on burning paper cools it below the heat it needs, so the fire goes out.
### A flame burns gas · *defined*
**Definition:** In a flame, the fuel burns as a gas. Solid wax and liquid oil do not burn as they are. Heat first turns them into a gas, and the gas burns.

In a candle, the flame's heat melts the wax near the wick. The melted wax climbs up the cotton wick, the way water climbs up a cloth dipped in it. Near the flame, the heat turns the melted wax into a gas, and that gas burns. When you blow a candle out, wax gas keeps rising for a second or two. Some of it cools into tiny droplets, which you see as white smoke. That is why a flame held in the smoke can run down it and light the wick again. Wood is the same. Heat makes wood give off gases, and the gases burn as flames. When the gases are gone, black charcoal is left. Charcoal burns in a different way, glowing red on its surface without a tall flame.
- e.g. Cooking gas is a gas already, so it lights the moment a spark reaches it.
- e.g. Petrol and kerosene give off flammable gas even when cold. That is why they are so dangerous near a flame.
- e.g. A diya's oil climbs the wick, turns into gas near the flame, and burns.
### Why a flame keeps going on its own · *defined*
**Definition:** Once a flame is lit, its own heat melts and turns more fuel into gas. That gas burns and makes more heat, so the flame keeps itself going until the fuel or air runs out.

A match gives a candle its first heat. After that, the candle needs no more help. The heat of the flame melts the wax below it. The wick lifts the melted wax up. The heat turns it into gas, and the gas burns, making more heat. This loop goes on for hours. It also explains why a lump of wax with no wick will not light from a match. The heat spreads into the big lump and melts it, but it never turns enough wax into gas in one place. The wick gathers a little melted wax right next to the flame, where it is hottest.
- e.g. A big log will not light from one match. Thin dry twigs light first, and their heat lights the log.
- e.g. A wet log hisses and smokes, because the fire's heat goes into drying it out first.
- e.g. A tea light keeps burning for about four hours with no help after the first match.
### How to put a fire out · *defined*
**Definition:** Every way of putting out a fire takes away one side of the fire triangle: the fuel, the oxygen or the heat.

Water puts out burning wood and paper mostly by cooling them below the heat they need. A lid on a burning pan, sand, or a heavy wet cloth keeps the air away, so the fire runs out of oxygen. Turning off the gas regulator takes away the fuel. Fire extinguishers in buildings work in these ways too. Some spray foam or powder that covers the fire and keeps out the air. One rule matters a lot in Indian kitchens: never pour water on burning oil. Water is heavier than oil, so it sinks under the hot oil. It turns into steam at once and throws burning oil up and out. Turn off the stove and cover the pan with a lid instead.
- e.g. A lid on a burning kadai takes away the oxygen.
- e.g. Fire fighters spray water on a burning building to cool it.
- e.g. In a forest fire, fire fighters clear a strip of land of dry plants, so the fire runs out of fuel there.
### Flame colours · *defined*
**Definition:** A yellow flame glows because it is full of tiny hot bits of soot. A blue flame is gas burning with plenty of air, with very little soot.

A candle flame is mostly yellow. Inside it, some of the wax gas breaks into tiny bits of carbon called soot. The heat makes them glow yellow, like the red-hot coil in an electric heater. Most of that soot then burns at the outer edge of the flame. The gas stove flame is blue, because air is mixed into the gas before it burns. The gas burns fully, and very little soot is made. If a stove flame turns yellow and leaves black marks on the vessels, the burner may need cleaning. A grown-up can check it.
- e.g. Hold a steel spoon in a yellow candle flame and it turns black with soot. A blue gas flame leaves a pan almost clean.
- e.g. The bottom of a candle flame is blue, because air reaches the gas there before soot forms.
- e.g. Salt sprinkled into a gas flame makes a flash of orange-yellow light.
### How a safety match lights · *defined*
**Definition:** A match lights when rubbing on the side of the box makes enough heat to start the chemicals in the match head burning.

The brown strip on the side of a matchbox holds red phosphorus and powdered glass. The match head holds chemicals that give out oxygen when heated, and a fuel. When you strike the match, friction heats a tiny bit of the red phosphorus, and it catches fire. That lights the head, and the head lights the wooden stick. These are called safety matches because they light only when struck on the box's strip. Safety matches came from Sweden in the 1840s and 1850s. Today most of India's matchboxes are made in Tamil Nadu, around Sivakasi and Kovilpatti.
- e.g. A match struck on a plain wall usually does not light, because the red phosphorus is on the box, not on the match.
- e.g. A wet matchbox will not light, because the water takes the heat away.
- e.g. The burnt strip on an old matchbox is darker and smoother, because its chemicals are used up.
### How people learned to use fire · *debated*
Fire was on Earth long before people. Lightning starts wildfires, and early people would have seen them. The oldest clear sign of people using fire is about one million years old, in Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa. Scientists found burnt bones and ash from plants there. By about 400,000 years ago, hearths are found in many caves. People first probably kept fires going that they had found, carrying embers from place to place. Later they learned to make fire themselves. They spun a stick against wood, or struck certain stones together to make sparks. Fire let them cook, keep warm, scare away animals and see at night. When exactly people learned to make fire, not just keep it, is still argued about.
- e.g. Even today, some families keep the embers of the kitchen fire covered in ash overnight, so it is easy to light in the morning.
- e.g. Hunter-gatherer groups such as the Hadza of Tanzania make fire by spinning a stick, which takes skill and practice.
- e.g. In some Vedic rituals in Kerala and elsewhere, priests still light the fire with a wooden fire drill called an arani.
- **Scientists who study the oldest caves:** People were using fire at least one million years ago, in small ways at first. Burnt bones and ash at Wonderwerk Cave show this.
- **Scientists who look for everyday hearths:** Fire became part of daily life only around 400,000 years ago, when hearths appear in many places. Older signs could come from wildfires, or from fire used only now and then.
- **Scientists who study how fire was made:** Keeping a fire is far easier than making one. People may have kept fires for a very long time before they could make fire whenever they wanted.
### Did cooking change our bodies? · *debated*
Cooked food is softer and easier to chew. The body also gets more energy from cooked food than from the same food raw. Richard Wrangham, a scientist who studies apes and early humans, thinks cooking changed the human body. He says cooked food gave early people enough energy for big brains, and let their teeth and guts become smaller. Other scientists point out that the oldest signs of fire are not as old as the first changes in the body. They think eating more meat, and cutting and pounding food with stone tools, mattered more at first. Nobody knows yet which idea is right, or whether both are.
- e.g. A raw potato is hard. A boiled potato is soft enough to mash with a spoon.
- e.g. Chimpanzees chew for hours every day. People spend far less time chewing.
- e.g. Ragi and rice must be cooked before we can digest them well.
- **Richard Wrangham:** Cooking let early people get far more energy from their food. That energy let their brains grow larger, and their teeth and guts smaller.
- **Many other scientists:** Our ancestors' bodies began changing before there are clear signs of fire. Eating meat and using stone tools to cut and pound food may explain the first changes.
### Davy's lamp: cooling stops a flame · *defined*
**Definition:** A flame cannot pass through a fine metal mesh, because the metal takes the heat away from the burning gas.

Metal carries heat away fast. When a flame touches a fine metal mesh, the mesh takes heat from the burning gas. On the other side, the gas is too cool to burn. So the flame stays on one side of the mesh, even though gas and smoke pass through. In 1815, Humphry Davy used this to make a safety lamp for coal miners. He put the lamp's flame inside a cylinder of iron wire mesh. The firedamp gas in the mine could reach the flame and burn inside the mesh, but the flame could not get out to set off the gas outside. A taller, bluish flame inside the lamp also warned miners that firedamp was there.
- e.g. A fine steel tea strainer held over a candle flame keeps the flame below it, while smoke rises through.
- e.g. Miners' safety lamps of this kind were used for about a hundred years, until electric lamps took over.
- e.g. Some gas cans and pipes have a mesh inside, called a flame arrester, which works the same way.
### Fire safety at home · *defined*
**Definition:** Fire safety rules keep fuel, oxygen and heat from coming together where we do not want them, and tell us what to do if they do.

At home, fire is used in the kitchen, in lamps and incense, and at festivals with crackers. Matches and lighters stay with grown-ups, out of children's reach. Loose clothes, a davani or a dupatta must be kept away from the stove and from lamps. Lamps stand on a steel plate, away from curtains and paper. If you smell gas, do not touch any switch, because a switch can make a tiny spark. Open the windows, turn off the regulator if you can, and tell a grown-up at once. If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop to the ground and roll, covering your face with your hands. Rolling smothers the flames, and running fans them. Smoke rises, so in a smoky room, crawl low where the air is cleaner. For a burn, hold it under cool running water for 20 minutes. Never put ice, toothpaste, butter or oil on a burn. In India, call 101 for the fire service, or 112 for any emergency.
- e.g. A pooja lamp on a steel plate, away from the curtains.
- e.g. Crackers lit by a grown-up outdoors, with a bucket of water nearby.
- e.g. Practising stop, drop and roll on a mat.

## Mostly agreed
- Burning needs a fuel, oxygen and enough heat at the same time. *How we know:* Take away any one, by covering a candle, turning off the gas, or cooling a fire with water, and it goes out. This has been tested in labs for over 200 years.
- In a flame, the fuel burns as a gas. *How we know:* A flame held in the smoke of a blown-out candle lights the smoke and runs down to the wick. Michael Faraday showed this to children in his candle lectures in London, in 1848 and again in 1860.
- Burning makes new substances, mostly carbon dioxide and water. *How we know:* A cold glass over a flame mists up with water. Limewater shaken with the gases from a flame turns milky, which shows carbon dioxide.
- People have used fire for hundreds of thousands of years. *How we know:* Burnt bones, ash and hearths are found in caves in Africa, Asia and Europe. The oldest clear signs, in Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa, are about one million years old.
- Cool running water for 20 minutes is the best first aid for a burn. *How we know:* Burns doctors and first aid groups, such as the Red Cross, advise this, because studies show cooling for about 20 minutes helps burns heal. Ice, butter and toothpaste do not help, and can harm the skin.

## How we found out
**Humphry Davy, a chemist in London, helped by Michael Faraday, and George Stephenson, a mine engineer in Killingworth, 1812 (the Felling Colliery explosion), 1815 to 1816 (the safety lamps), 1835 (the committee's report).** In the early 1800s, coal mines in England were full of danger. A gas called firedamp seeps out of coal, and a miner's candle could set it off. On 25 May 1812, an explosion at Felling Colliery, near Newcastle, killed 92 men and boys. Miners had tried other lights. A steel mill was a steel wheel spun against a flint, which threw out sparks to see by. It was thought to be safer than a candle, but explosions showed that sparks could light firedamp too. In 1815, the chemist Humphry Davy, helped by young Michael Faraday, studied how flames travel. He found that a flame could not pass through narrow metal tubes or fine wire mesh, because the metal took away its heat. So he put a lamp's flame inside a cylinder of iron mesh. The engineer George Stephenson made a similar lamp at the same time. Many then believed Davy's lamp made mines safe. But in 1835, a committee of the British Parliament found that accidents had gone up. Owners sent men into gassier parts of mines, and a draught or a broken wire could let the flame out.  
*What people believed before:* Miners first believed the sparks of a steel mill were a safe light in firedamp, but the explosion at Wallsend Colliery in 1785 showed that sparks could set it off. Later, many believed the Davy lamp made mines completely safe. The 1835 committee showed accidents had gone up, because a draught or a broken wire could let the flame out, and men were sent into more dangerous parts of the mines.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Toothpaste, butter or ice is good for a burn.** (A very common home remedy in India and elsewhere; *rejected*)  
  For: Toothpaste and ice feel cool at first, so it seems they help.  
  Against: They do not cool the burn deep down, and they can trap heat, damage the skin or cause infection. Cool running water for 20 minutes works far better.
- **Water puts out every fire.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Water does put out most fires of wood, paper and cloth, by cooling them.  
  Against: Water on burning oil turns to steam at once and throws the burning oil out. Water on an electrical fire can carry electricity to a person. These fires need a lid, sand, or the right extinguisher.
- **Blowing on a fire always puts it out.** (A common idea, because we blow out birthday candles; *rejected*)  
  For: Blowing out a candle works. The blow pushes the wax gas away from the wick and cools it.  
  Against: Blowing on embers or a big fire brings more oxygen and makes it burn harder. That is why people fan a chulha or blow through a pipe to light it.
- **Fire is one of the elements, a basic substance of the world.** (Many old traditions: agni is one of the five elements, the pancha bhuta, in Indian thought, and fire was one of four elements in ancient Greece; *rejected*)  
  For: Fire looks like a thing of its own. It moves, spreads and changes everything it touches.  
  Against: Chemistry has found that fire is not a substance. It is a change: fuel joining with oxygen and giving out heat and light. The old idea is still a way of thinking about the world, but not a chemical fact.
- **Learning to cook made us human.** (Richard Wrangham, a scientist at Harvard University, and others; *debated*)  
  For: Cooked food gives more energy and needs less chewing. Human teeth and guts are small for an ape of our size.  
  Against: The oldest clear signs of fire are younger than the first big changes in our ancestors' bodies. Meat and stone tools may explain those changes.
- **Smoke from a fire is just harmless air.** (A common idea, because smoke drifts away; *rejected*)  
  For: Smoke rises and spreads, so it seems to vanish.  
  Against: Smoke holds tiny bits of soot and poisonous gases. In house fires, more people die from breathing smoke than from the flames. Smoke from wood stoves and chulhas also harms the lungs over time.

## Roots
We have tested what a candle really burns, and found what every fire needs. Now we ask what a flame tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A match held to a lump of wax only melted it. Yet a flame above a blown-out wick ran down the smoke and relit it. What burns is wax that heat has turned into a gas. A flame keeps going because its own heat makes more gas to burn.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of fire as eating the solid wax, wood or oil, the way we eat a dosa. But a flame never burns a solid directly. It keeps making its own fuel, by heating the solid into gas.  
*Evidence:* In The Jumping Flame, a match held to a lump of wax for ten seconds only melted it. The same match lit the white smoke of a blown-out candle from a few centimetres above, and the flame ran down to the wick.

- *who am i:* You belong to the only kind of animal that makes fire. For hundreds of thousands of years, people have cooked, kept warm and talked around a fire.
- *what is life:* Some plants in Australia and South Africa grow where bushfires come often. After a fire the ground is open and sunny, so their seeds wait to sprout until they sense the smoke.
- *what is this world:* Fire needs oxygen, and nearly all the oxygen in the air was made by plants and tiny sea beings. Before there was enough of it, nothing on Earth could burn.

Wax, oil, wood and cooking gas look nothing alike. One is a solid, one a liquid, one a gas. Yet in a flame they all do the same thing. Heat turns each into gas, and each gas joins with oxygen and gives out heat and light. So one kind of change shows up as a diya's yellow leaf of light, the blue ring on a stove and a forest fire on a hill.

- **Heraclitus, a Greek thinker of about 500 BCE, fragment 30 (Diels-Kranz numbering):** Heraclitus said the world is like an ever-living fire, kindled in measures and going out in measures. (A candle flame keeps the same shape for hours, yet the wax gas in it is new every moment. Heraclitus chose fire as his picture of something that stays itself only by always changing.)
- **The Buddha's Fire Sermon, Adittapariyaya Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 35.28:** The Buddha taught that the senses and the mind are on fire, burning with wanting. The word nirvana means the blowing out, or going out, of a fire. (A fire goes out when its fuel stops coming, as the dry wick did. The Buddha used this picture: when wanting stops feeding the fire, it goes out by itself.)
- Science (settled): Burning is fuel joining with oxygen. It gives out heat and light and makes carbon dioxide and water.
- Science (settled): Charcoal in rocks shows wildfires on Earth over 400 million years ago, soon after plants spread onto land and raised the oxygen in the air.
- Science (open): When people first learned to make fire, and not just keep it, is still not known.
- Other view, Many chemists: A flame is a chemical reaction that gives out enough heat to keep going. Calling it a flame that feeds itself, or comparing it with the world, adds nothing to the chemistry. It is wax gas and oxygen, and nothing more.
- Practice, Watching one flame (3 min): A grown-up lights one candle on a steel plate. Let's sit an arm's length away. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly. Now just watch the flame for one minute. Notice its shape, its colours, the blue at the bottom. Every moment, new wax gas is flowing into it. Yet it looks the same. Breathe out gently, not at the flame. Now close your eyes. Can you still see it?
- Ask: The flame you see now is made of different gas from a minute ago. Is it the same flame?
- Ask: When a candle goes out, where does the flame go?
- Ask: A flame keeps itself going with its own heat. Do you keep yourself going in any way like that?

## Story: The Lamps on the Balcony Wall
*The story is made up. Everything about the lamps is true: oil climbs a cotton wick and burns as a gas near the flame, a dry wick burns away quickly, and a steel plate over a small fire puts it out by keeping the air away. Water must never go on burning oil.*

It was the evening of Karthigai Deepam in Madurai, and the whole street was getting ready to glow. Meenakshi was eight. She sat on the balcony floor with a tray of little clay lamps, called agal vilakku. Her cousin Arjun, who was six, sat next to her with a bowl of cotton wicks. Paati, their grandmother, poured sesame oil into each lamp from a steel jug.

"Tie your davani back, Meenakshi," said Paati. "Loose cloth and lamps are not friends." Meenakshi tucked the end of her davani into her waist, the way Amma did in the kitchen.

Arjun held up a wick and frowned. "Paati, the lamp burns all evening. Why doesn't the cotton burn away?"

"Good question," said Paati. "What do you think?"

Arjun thought. "Maybe this cotton is a special kind that cannot burn?"

"Let's find out," said Paati. She took one empty lamp, with no oil at all, and put a dry wick in it. She struck a match and held it to the tip. The cotton caught at once. A little flame ran along it, the wick turned black and curled, and in a few seconds the flame was gone. Only a black thread was left, giving off a thin line of smoke.

"It does burn!" said Arjun. "And fast!"

Then Paati lit a lamp that was full of oil. Its wick had soaked up the oil for a few minutes. The flame stood up straight and still, a little yellow leaf of light. They watched it for a long time. The tip of the wick went slightly dark, but the wick did not shrink away.

Meenakshi leaned closer, but not too close. "So the oil protects the cotton," she said slowly. "But the oil isn't burning down in the lamp. I can see it. It's just sitting there, shiny."

"Then what is burning?" asked Paati.

Meenakshi looked at the wick. The oil had crept up it, the way tea creeps up a biscuit dipped in it. "The oil climbs up the wick," she said. "Then the flame's heat does something to it at the top."

"The heat turns it into a gas," said Paati. "You cannot see that gas. It is the gas that burns, right at the tip of the wick. As long as oil keeps climbing, the cotton stays wet and cool enough, and the flame has fresh fuel. When the oil runs out, the cotton itself starts to burn, like the dry wick."

They lit lamp after lamp and set them along the top of the balcony wall. Below, the street was a river of little flames. Every house had them. The air smelled of oil and camphor.

Arjun was carrying the last lamp when he tripped on the edge of the mat. The lamp tipped over onto the sheet of newspaper they had spread on the floor for the oil. Oil spilled out, and the newspaper caught fire. A flame as tall as a hand jumped up.

Arjun grabbed the steel mug of water by the door. "No!" said Paati sharply. "Not water on burning oil!"

Amma had come running from the kitchen with a big steel plate in her hand. She laid it flat over the burning newspaper and held it there, pressing the edges down with a folded towel. For a moment there was a smell of smoke. Then nothing. When she lifted the plate a minute later, the newspaper was black and still. The fire was out.

Arjun's eyes were wide. "Why not water?"

"Water is heavier than oil," said Amma. "It sinks under the hot oil and turns into steam at once. The steam throws the burning oil up and out. The fire can jump onto you."

"But how did the plate put it out?" Arjun asked. "It didn't do anything. It just sat there."

Meenakshi had been thinking hard. "The fire needed the oil, and the heat, and something else," she said. "When the oil ran out, the dry wick went out. So fuel is one thing. The plate didn't take away the oil. It didn't make it cold. So it must have taken away the air."

"Yes," said Amma. "A fire needs fuel, air and heat, all three. Take away any one, and it stops. A lid on a burning kadai works the same way. So does a blanket."

Paati handed Arjun a new lamp, already lit, and helped him set it carefully on the wall. "Now you know more about fire than you did an hour ago," she said. "Fire is a good friend if you know what it needs."

Arjun looked along the wall at the row of flames, each one standing still, each one drinking its oil up its little wick. "Every one of them is burning gas," he whispered. "And not one of them is burning the cotton."

**For the parent:** For the parent: in a lamp, the oil burns only as a gas. The flame's heat turns the oil at the top of the wick into gas, and the gas burns. While oil keeps climbing, the cotton stays soaked and hardly burns. A dry wick has nothing but cotton to burn, so it burns away in seconds. The steel plate stopped the fire by keeping the air away. Water on burning oil is dangerous, because it turns to steam under the oil and throws it out.

- Ask: Why did the dry wick burn away so fast, while the wick in oil lasted all evening?
- Ask: What was really burning at the tip of the wick?
- Ask: Why did Paati stop Arjun from throwing water on the burning oil?
- Ask: Which of the three things a fire needs did the steel plate take away?
- Ask: What did Paati ask Meenakshi to do before the lamps were lit, and why?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Jumping Flame: what really burns in a candle? (30 min)
- **question:** When a candle burns, what is burning: the solid wax, the wick, or something else?
- **predict:** We will try to light three things: a lump of wax with no wick, a candle's wick, and the white smoke from a candle that has just been blown out. Which ones do you think will catch fire? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same kind of candle wax for every test, and the same kind of match. Switch off the fan and close the windows during the smoke test, because a breeze blows the smoke away. In the smoke test, only the height of the match changes: 2 cm, 5 cm and 8 cm above the wick. Try each height three times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights every match and stays at the table the whole time. Work on a steel plate on a bare kitchen counter or floor, away from curtains, paper and gas cylinders. Tie back long hair, and tuck in a davani or dupatta. Keep a bowl of water nearby. Never lean over a flame. The child watches, records and reads the card. The grown-up does all the lighting and blowing out. Open the windows after the smoke test, because candle smoke stings the eyes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the candle, the smoke and the match, with an arrow showing which way the flame travelled.
- **results table:** What we tried to light | My guess: will it catch fire? | Try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | What we saw
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Break a piece about 3 cm long off the bottom of one candle. 2. Pull out its wick, so you have a lump of plain wax. 3. Stand the other candle on the steel plate, fixed with a few drops of melted wax. 4. Tape the marked card to the tumbler and stand it just behind the candle.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to find out what really burns in a candle. Here is a lump of wax with no wick, and here is a whole candle. What do you think a candle burns?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the lump of wax and the candle. What do you think burns in a candle?
  - Note: The bottom of the card should be level with the top of the wick, so the lines show 2, 5 and 8 cm above it. Let the child do the breaking and taping. You do any step with a flame.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, for each of the three things, whether it will catch fire. Write each guess and reason in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Here are the three things we will try to light. A lump of wax. A candle wick. And the white smoke when a candle is blown out. Which will catch fire? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess for each one. Will it catch fire? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think the lump of wax will or will not burn? (good: Candles are made of wax, so wax burns.; It has no wick, so it may not light.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Most children say the wax will burn, because candles are wax, and that the smoke will not, because smoke is what is left after burning. Both are good guesses to test.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the lump of wax on the steel plate. 2. Light a match and hold its flame against the top of the lump for about 5 seconds. 3. Take the match away and drop it in the water. 4. Look at the wax.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the wax closely while I hold the flame on it. Does it catch fire? What happens to it?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the lump of wax. The grown-up holds a match to it. Does it burn?
  - Note: The wax goes shiny and a little of it melts and drips. It does not catch fire, or if a small flame flickers on the melted wax for a moment, it goes out as soon as the match is taken away. If the match burns down too close to your fingers, drop it in the water and use another one.
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Light a new match. 2. Hold it to the candle's wick. 3. Let the candle burn for 2 minutes, until a small pool of melted wax forms around the wick.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same wax, but with a wick. Watch how fast it lights. Look at the pool of melted wax forming at the top.  
  *Child mode:* Now the candle with its wick. Does it light? Look at the melted wax.
  - Note: The wick lights within a couple of seconds. The flame melts a pool of wax around it, which is the fuel climbing up the wick.
  - Check: The lump of wax only melted. The candle with a wick lit at once. What made the difference? → **The wick lifts a little melted wax right up into the heat of the flame**
- **s5 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off the fan. 2. Blow the candle out gently, from the side. 3. At once, hold a lit match in the white smoke, 2 cm above the wick, using the card. 4. Watch whether the flame runs down to the wick. 5. Repeat three times at 2 cm, then at 5 cm, then at 8 cm.  
  *Parent says:* Now the smoke. I will blow the candle out, and quickly hold a match in the smoke. Watch the smoke, not the match. Read me the line on the card.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the white smoke. The grown-up holds a match in it. Does the flame jump down?
  - Note: Have the match already burning before you blow, because the smoke lasts only a second or two. Hold the match flame right inside the white smoke. At 2 cm it usually works every time: a flame runs down the smoke and the wick lights again. At 5 cm it often works. At 8 cm it may work only sometimes, when the smoke is thick and the air is still. Let the candle burn for a minute between tries, so there is a pool of melted wax. Whatever happens, write it down: 'did not jump' is a result too.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think burns in a candle? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A strong answer sounds like this. What I think: the wax burns, but only after it turns into a gas. What I saw: the lump of wax only melted, but the flame lit the smoke from 2 cm and 5 cm above the wick and ran down to it. Why: the white smoke has wax gas in it. The flame's heat turns melted wax into gas, and the gas burns.
  - Check: The match lit the white smoke above a blown-out candle, and the flame ran down to the wick. What does this show? → **The smoke has wax gas in it, and that gas can burn**
### e2 · The Tea Strainer Test: can a metal mesh stop a flame? (20 min)
- **question:** If we hold a metal mesh over a candle flame, will the flame come through it? Does the size of the holes matter?
- **predict:** We will hold a fine steel tea strainer over a candle flame, then a steel spoon or ladle with big holes. Will the flame come through the fine mesh? Will it come through the big holes? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same candle and the same height each time: lower the metal until it is halfway down the flame. Hold it there for 3 seconds only, then lift it away. Only the size of the holes changes. Try each one twice, and let the metal cool in between, because a hot mesh works less well.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights every match and stays at the table the whole time. Work on a steel plate on a bare kitchen counter or floor, away from curtains, paper and gas cylinders. Tie back long hair, and tuck in a davani or dupatta. Keep a bowl of water nearby. Never lean over a flame. Only a grown-up holds the strainer and the spoon, using a folded cloth. Never use a strainer with a plastic rim or handle. Hold the metal in the flame for 3 seconds at most, and put it on the steel plate to cool.
- **record:** Draw the flame under the fine mesh and under the big holes, as you saw them from the side.
- **results table:** What we held over the flame | Size of holes | My guess: will the flame come through? | Try 1 | Try 2
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Light the candle and let it burn steadily for a minute. 2. Show your child the tea strainer and the slotted spoon. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason for each.  
  *Parent says:* This mesh has holes much finer than a grain of rice. The spoon has big holes. When I hold each one in the flame, will the flame come through?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the fine mesh and the big holes. Will the flame come through? Why?
  - Note: Most children say the flame will come through both, because there are holes. Some say the fine mesh will stop it, like a net. Write down every guess.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the tea strainer flat, using a cloth on the handle. 2. Lower it slowly onto the flame until it is halfway down the flame. 3. Hold it for 3 seconds. 4. Your child looks from the side, at eye level with the mesh. 5. Lift it away and put it on the steel plate.  
  *Parent says:* Look from the side, not from above. Is the flame above the mesh, below it, or both? What is coming through?  
  *Child mode:* Look from the side. Where is the flame? What comes out of the top?
  - Note: The flame flattens out under the mesh, and smoke rises through it. The flame does not come through. If it does, the mesh may be too hot or too coarse. Let it cool and try again. The mesh turns black with soot, which is expected.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let the candle burn steadily again. 2. Now lower the slotted spoon onto the flame in the same way. 3. Hold it for 3 seconds and look from the side.  
  *Parent says:* Now the big holes. What do you see this time?  
  *Child mode:* Now the spoon with big holes. Where is the flame now?
  - Note: With holes about half a centimetre wide, little tongues of flame usually poke up through the holes. If they do not, write that down. Holes that are a little bigger show it better.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the results. Then read the explanation on the card about Davy's lamp.  
  *Parent says:* The fine mesh stopped the flame, but gas and smoke still went through. What do you think the metal did to the flame?  
  *Child mode:* The fine mesh stopped the flame. What did the metal do?
  - Note: Steel carries heat away. Each fine wire touches the flame and takes heat from it, so the gas that gets through is too cool to burn. Big holes have less metal close to the gas, so the flame can get through. This is how Humphry Davy's safety lamp kept miners safe in 1815.
  - Check: Why did the fine mesh stop the flame? → **The metal wires took away heat, so the gas above was too cool to burn**
### e3 · Dry Wick or Oiled Wick? How long does each one burn? (25 min)
- **question:** Does a cotton wick burn longer on its own, or sitting in oil? Does the wick itself get used up?
- **predict:** We will light a dry cotton wick in an empty diya, and a wick lying in oil in another diya. Which will burn longer? Will the wick in oil get shorter? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two wicks of the same kind and the same length, and two diyas of the same size. Measure both wicks before lighting. Light both the same way. Time each one with the same clock.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights every match and stays at the table the whole time. Work on a steel plate on a bare kitchen counter or floor, away from curtains, paper and gas cylinders. Tie back long hair, and tuck in a davani or dupatta. Keep a bowl of water nearby. Never lean over a flame. Never use kerosene or petrol, which give off flammable gas even when cold. Use cooking oil or lamp oil only.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both wicks before and after, side by side.
- **results table:** Wick | Length before (cm) | My guess: how long will it burn? | How long it burned | Length after (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure both wicks with the ruler and write the lengths. 2. Put one dry wick in an empty diya, with its tip over the edge. 3. Half fill the other diya with oil. 4. Lay the second wick in it, with its tip over the edge. 5. Wait 5 minutes for the oil to soak up the wick.  
  *Parent says:* Both wicks are the same. One is dry, and one is lying in oil. Which will burn longer? Will either wick get shorter?  
  *Child mode:* Measure both wicks. One is dry. One lies in oil. Which burns longer? Why?
  - Note: Write down your child's guess and reason. Many children guess the dry wick will burn longer, because it is not wet.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start the stopwatch. 2. Light the dry wick. 3. Watch until the flame goes out, and write the time.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the dry wick. Tell me when the flame goes out.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the dry wick burn. Say stop when the flame goes out.
  - Note: The dry wick flares up, turns black and usually goes out within a minute, leaving a black thread and some smoke. It may glow red for a few seconds after the flame goes out.
- **s3 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Light the oiled wick and start the stopwatch. 2. Watch it for 10 minutes while you talk. 3. Then blow it out gently. 4. When it is cool, measure the wick again.  
  *Parent says:* Now the wick in oil. Let's watch it while we talk. Is the flame bigger or smaller than the dry one's? Is the wick getting shorter?  
  *Child mode:* Now the wick in oil. Watch it for 10 minutes. Is the wick getting shorter?
  - Note: The oiled wick burns with a steady yellow flame for the whole 10 minutes, and would go on for an hour or more. Its tip turns black, but it hardly gets shorter. If the flame goes out early, the wick tip may be too far into the oil. Pull it out a little further over the edge.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think was the fuel in the diya? What did you see? Why did the cotton last so much longer in oil?  
  *Child mode:* What was the fuel? What did you see? Why?
  - Note: A strong answer: the oil is the fuel. The dry wick burned out in under a minute, but the oiled wick burned for 10 minutes and hardly got shorter. The oil climbs the wick, turns into gas at the tip and burns. The soaked cotton stays cool enough not to burn itself.
  - Check: The oiled wick burned for 10 minutes and hardly got shorter. What does that show? → **The oil is the fuel, and it burns at the tip of the wick**

### a2 · The Home Fire Safety Walk (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up leads the walk. Look at the gas cylinder and regulator, but only a grown-up touches them.

### a3 · Stop, Drop and Roll, and Crawl Low (10 min)
- **safety:** Clear a space with nothing sharp nearby. This is practice only. There is no fire.

  *Child mode:* Clothes on fire? Do not run. Stop. Drop. Cover your face. Roll.

   Options: Run outside into the wind / Stop, drop to the ground, cover his face and roll / Wave his arms to blow the fire out  
   **Answer:** Stop, drop to the ground, cover his face and roll. *Why:* Rolling keeps the air from the flames, so they go out. Running and waving bring more oxygen, so the fire burns harder.
8. (mcq) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3, burns her finger on a hot vessel. What is the best first aid?  
   Options: Toothpaste / Cool running water for 20 minutes / An ice cube  
   **Answer:** Cool running water for 20 minutes. *Why:* Cool running water takes the heat out of the skin. Toothpaste and ice feel cool for a moment but do not cool the burn deep down, and can harm the skin.
9. (mcq) Why does cooking gas have a strong smell?  
   Options: Cooking gas always smells like that naturally / A smell is added so that people notice a leak / The smell is the gas burning  
   **Answer:** A smell is added so that people notice a leak. *Why:* The gas itself has almost no smell. A strong-smelling chemical is added, so that a leak can be noticed before a spark lights it.
10. (mcq) How did early people make fire, before matches?  
   Options: By spinning a stick fast against wood, or striking stones to make sparks / By leaving wood in the sun / By pouring water on stones  
   **Answer:** By spinning a stick fast against wood, or striking stones to make sparks. *Why:* Rubbing makes heat by friction. A spinning stick can make a glowing ember. Some stones give sparks when struck. Sunlight alone does not light wood, unless it is focused with a lens.
11. (mcq) What is the oldest clear sign that people used fire?  
   Options: Burnt bones and plant ash in a cave in South Africa, about one million years old / Matchboxes found in old temples / Drawings of fire on the Moon  
   **Answer:** Burnt bones and plant ash in a cave in South Africa, about one million years old. *Why:* In Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa, scientists found burnt bones and ash from plants about one million years old. Scientists still argue about when fire became part of everyday life.
12. (mcq) Humphry Davy put a lamp's flame inside a cylinder of fine iron mesh. Why could the flame not light the gas outside?  
   Options: The mesh kept out all the gas / The metal took heat away, so the gas outside was too cool to burn / The mesh made the flame smaller  
   **Answer:** The metal took heat away, so the gas outside was too cool to burn. *Why:* Gas could pass through the mesh and burn inside it. But the metal took heat from the flame, so the gas outside stayed too cool to catch fire. You saw this with a tea strainer over a candle.

## Applications
- Cooking on a gas stove, a chulha or a wood fire.
- Lamps and candles for light in a power cut, and diyas at festivals.
- Firefighting: water, foam, sand and fire blankets each take away one side of the fire triangle.
- Firebreaks cleared in forests to stop wildfires spreading.
- Flame arresters, a mesh like Davy's, inside some fuel cans and gas pipes.
- Smoke alarms that sound when they sense smoke.
- Matches and lighters, which give the first heat.
- Engines in cars, buses and autos, which burn fuel with oxygen inside them.
- Kilns for firing clay pots, and furnaces for making steel and glass.
- Crematoria and the burning of waste, which turn fuel into gases and ash.

## Open questions
- **When did people first learn to make fire whenever they wanted, not just keep fires they found?** Making fire leaves few traces, such as worn sticks or struck stones. Scientists are still looking for clear evidence.
- **Did cooking help human brains grow larger?** The idea fits some facts, but the oldest signs of fire do not go back as far as the first changes in our ancestors' bodies.
- **What exactly happens, step by step, inside a flame?** Even a candle flame involves hundreds of small chemical steps. Scientists use computers to model them, and some steps, such as exactly how soot forms, are still being worked out.
- **How will climate change alter wildfires?** Hotter, drier weather makes fires more likely in many places, but how much depends on rain, plants and people, which are hard to predict.

## Connected maths
- In The Jumping Flame, Meenakshi's family tried each height 3 times. At 2 cm the flame jumped 3 times. At 5 cm it jumped 2 times. At 8 cm it jumped 1 time. How many jumps were there in all? What fraction of the tries at 5 cm worked? **6 jumps in all. At 5 cm, 2 out of 3 tries worked, which is two thirds.**. 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 jumps. At 5 cm there were 3 tries, and 2 worked, so the fraction is 2 out of 3, or two thirds. The pattern shows the flame jumps less often the higher the match. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- In Dry Wick or Oiled Wick?, the dry wick burned for 40 seconds. The oiled wick was still burning after 10 minutes. How many times as long did the oiled wick burn, at least? **At least 15 times as long.**. 10 minutes is 10 times 60 seconds, which is 600 seconds. 600 divided by 40 is 15. The oiled wick was still burning, so it would have lasted even longer. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- A tea light burns for about 4 hours. Lakshmi's family lights 6 tea lights at 6 in the evening on Karthigai Deepam. At what time will they go out? If they light one new tea light every 4 hours, how many do they need to keep one burning for a whole day of 24 hours? **About 10 at night. They need 6 tea lights for 24 hours.**. 6 in the evening plus 4 hours is 10 at night. All 6 burn at the same time, so they all go out at about 10. For one light all day: 24 divided by 4 is 6 tea lights, one after the other. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Anand's family practised their fire plan three times. It took them 90 seconds the first time, 70 seconds the second time and 50 seconds the third time. If the pattern goes on, how long might the fourth practice take? Why might it stop getting faster? **About 30 seconds, but it will stop getting faster at some point.**. Each practice was 20 seconds faster: 90, 70, 50, so the next would be 30. But walking down the stairs always takes some time. So the times would level off instead of reaching zero. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
