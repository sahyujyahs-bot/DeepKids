# Polymers: the very long molecules in plastic, rubber, silk and wool
`sci.chemistry.polymers.L3`

**Summary.** White glue pours off a spoon like a thick liquid. Stir in a little borax water, and the same glue becomes slime you can hold, stretch and bounce. Glue, plastic, rubber, silk, wool and your own hair are made of polymers: molecules that are very long chains of small pieces joined end to end. Long chains tangle, and chains tied to each other at a few places can turn a runny liquid into something you can hold. In this unit you make slime and test how the number of ties changes it.

**Intro.** *Hold a spoonful of white glue over a plate and tip it slowly. Watch it drip. Glue flows like a liquid. Today the same glue will become something you can pick up and roll into a ball. What could change inside it?* Water and glue are both liquids, but glue drips slowly and makes threads. That is a clue about what glue is made of. Its molecules are not small and round like water molecules. They are very long chains. In this unit you find out what happens when long chains tangle, and when they are tied to each other.

## Terms
- **Atom**: An atom is one of the tiny pieces that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with a school microscope. *e.g.* Water is made of hydrogen atoms and oxygen atoms.
- **Molecule**: A molecule is a group of atoms held tightly together. It is the smallest piece of a substance that is still that substance. *e.g.* One water molecule is one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms.
- **Polymer**: A polymer is a very long molecule made of many small pieces joined end to end, like beads on a string. A polymer can have thousands of pieces. *e.g.* The plastic of a milk packet is a polymer. So is the silk in a silk saree.
- **Monomer**: A monomer is one of the small pieces that join up to make a polymer. 'Mono' means one, and 'poly' means many. *e.g.* Starch in rice is a polymer. Its monomer is glucose, a kind of sugar.
- **Chain**: In chemistry, a chain is a row of atoms or pieces joined one after another. A long chain can bend, coil and tangle with other chains. *e.g.* A chain of paper clips hooked together is a good model of a polymer chain.
- **Tangle**: Long chains tangle when they wind around each other, so pulling one drags others along. Short pieces cannot tangle. *e.g.* Pull one strand of cooked noodles from a bowl and other strands come with it. Grains of rice do not do that.
- **Cross-link**: A cross-link is a tie between two polymer chains. It joins one chain to another at one point. A few cross-links turn many loose chains into one net. *e.g.* In slime, borax makes cross-links between the glue's long molecules.
- **Borax**: Borax is a white powder that dissolves in water. In India it is also called suhaga. Dissolved borax can tie certain polymer chains together. It is harmful if swallowed, so a grown-up handles it. *e.g.* A few spoons of borax water turn runny white glue into slime.
- **Latex**: Latex is the milky white liquid that some plants make inside them. The latex of the rubber tree is tiny drops of rubber floating in water. *e.g.* When a rubber tapper in Kerala cuts the bark, white latex drips into a cup.
- **Protein**: A protein is a polymer that living beings make. Its small pieces are called amino acids. Proteins build hair, skin, muscles, silk and wool. *e.g.* Your hair, a sheep's wool and a silkworm's silk are all made of protein.
- **Fibre**: A fibre is a long, thin, bendy strand. Many fibres are made of polymer chains lying side by side along the strand. *e.g.* Cotton, wool, silk, jute and nylon are fibres. They are spun into thread.
- **Plastic**: Plastic is a material made in factories from polymers. Most plastic is made from oil or natural gas. There are many kinds of plastic. *e.g.* A bucket, a milk packet, a water bottle and a toothbrush are made of different plastics.
- **Vulcanised rubber**: Vulcanised rubber is rubber that has been heated with sulfur. The sulfur makes cross-links between the rubber chains, so the rubber stays springy in heat and cold. *e.g.* Bicycle tyres and car tyres are made of vulcanised rubber.

## Explanations
### What a polymer is · *defined*
**Definition:** A polymer is a very long molecule made of many small pieces, called monomers, joined end to end by chemical bonds.

Most molecules are tiny and short. A water molecule has just 3 atoms. A polymer molecule can have tens of thousands of atoms in a row. It is thousands of times longer than it is wide. Polymers are still far too small to see. But their length changes how a material behaves. Materials made of polymers bend, stretch, make threads and are hard to tear.
- e.g. The plastic in a milk packet, called polythene, is long chains of carbon atoms with hydrogen atoms along their sides.
- e.g. Starch in rice and atta is long chains of glucose, a sugar.
- e.g. Silk, wool and your hair are proteins, long chains of amino acids.
### Why long chains tangle · *defined*
**Definition:** Long chain molecules wind around each other and tangle. A pull on one chain drags its neighbours along.

Small molecules, like water or sugar, slide past each other easily. That is why water pours fast. Long chains cannot slide past each other easily. They are wound together like cooked noodles in a bowl. So a liquid made of long chains, like glue, flows slowly and makes threads. A solid made of long chains, like a plastic bag, can stretch instead of breaking at once.
- e.g. Lift a forkful of rice and the grains fall off. Lift a forkful of noodles, or idiyappam, and a whole clump hangs down.
- e.g. Honey flows slowly too, but it breaks into drops. Glue makes long threads because its molecules are long chains.
### How borax turns glue into slime · *defined*
**Definition:** Borax in water makes cross-links: ties that join the glue's long molecules to each other. Many tied chains make one stretchy net, which is slime.

In glue, the long chains are loose. They tangle, but they can slowly slide apart, so glue flows. Borax ties one chain to another at a few places along its length. Soon every chain is tied to others, and the whole cup becomes one net. That is why the glue suddenly clumped. More borax makes more ties, so the slime is firmer and spreads less. The ties in slime keep breaking and joining again. Pull slowly, and the ties have time to let go and re-join, so slime stretches. Pull fast, and they break before they can re-join, so slime snaps.
- e.g. In the borax test, the cup with the most borax water gave the firmest slime, which spread least on the plate.
- e.g. Slime pulled slowly made a long thread. Pulled with a quick tug, it snapped with a clean edge.
### Polymers that living beings make · *defined*
**Definition:** Living beings make many polymers in their bodies: proteins, starch, cellulose and DNA.

A silkworm caterpillar must keep safe while their body changes into a moth. So they spin a cocoon around themselves from one long silk thread. The silk is a liquid inside their body. As the silkworm pulls it out, its long protein chains line up side by side, and it becomes a strong solid thread. A rubber tree has a problem too: insects bite into their bark. When the bark is cut, milky latex flows out and thickens into sticky rubber. Scientists think this seals the wound and gums up the jaws of biting insects. People collect this same latex to make rubber.
- e.g. Cotton and paper are cellulose, the polymer that makes plant walls stiff.
- e.g. A sheep's wool and your hair are keratin, a protein.
- e.g. A spider's web is silk, a protein. Weight for weight, spider silk is stronger than steel wire.
### Plastics: polymers people make · *defined*
**Definition:** Plastics are polymers made in factories, mostly from oil and natural gas. Small molecules from the oil are joined into long chains.

Different small molecules make different plastics. A milk packet is polythene. A water bottle is usually PET. A bucket is often polypropylene. All of them are long chains, so they bend and stretch instead of shattering. Plastic is cheap, light and waterproof, and it lasts a very long time. That is also its problem. Very few living beings can yet break plastic chains, so thrown-away plastic stays in soil, rivers and the sea.
- e.g. Look for a small triangle with a number from 1 to 7 on plastic things. The number says which plastic it is.
- e.g. Nylon, the first fully made-up fibre, was made in 1935. It is used for toothbrush bristles and ropes.
### Tying rubber's chains: vulcanised rubber · *defined*
**Definition:** Vulcanising means heating rubber with sulfur. The sulfur makes cross-links between the rubber chains, much as borax ties the chains in slime.

Raw rubber from latex gets soft and sticky on a hot day and stiff on a cold one. Its chains slide past each other too easily. In 1839, in the United States, Charles Goodyear found that heating rubber with sulfur fixed this. Sulfur ties the chains together. The ties let the rubber stretch, then pull it back to its shape. Unlike the ties in slime, these ties do not keep breaking, so a tyre does not slowly flow flat.
- e.g. A rubber band springs back. A blob of slime slowly spreads out. Both are tied chains, but the rubber band's ties are permanent.
- e.g. Tyres, chappal soles and rubber gloves are made of vulcanised rubber.

## Mostly agreed
- Plastic, rubber, silk, wool, cotton, starch and DNA are polymers: very long molecules made of small pieces joined by chemical bonds. *How we know:* Chemists can build polymers from small molecules on purpose, measure how heavy single polymer molecules are, and even see long chains under special microscopes.
- Ties between polymer chains, called cross-links, make a material firmer and springier. *How we know:* Adding more borax to glue, or more sulfur to rubber, makes the material firmer. Anyone can test the glue at home.
- Most plastics break down very slowly in nature. *How we know:* Scientists have buried and sunk plastic bags and checked them years later. Many were still whole and could still hold things.
- Living beings make polymers to solve their own problems: a cocoon for safety, a web for food, latex to seal a wound. *How we know:* Biologists have watched silkworms spin, spiders build webs, and rubber trees bleed latex where their bark is cut.

## How we found out
**Hermann Staudinger, a German chemist, with Magda Staudinger, a plant scientist; and Wallace Carothers, an American chemist, 1920 (Staudinger's paper on giant molecules) to the 1930s (Carothers's nylon); Nobel Prize 1953.** About a hundred years ago, chemists were puzzled by rubber, starch and cotton. Their molecules seemed enormous. Most chemists thought this was a trick. They said these materials were clumps of small molecules stuck loosely together, like grains in a ball of wet sand. In 1920 Hermann Staudinger, a German chemist, said the opposite. He said rubber is made of giant molecules: very long chains of atoms, held by the same strong bonds as small molecules. Many chemists laughed at him. One told him to purify his rubber properly, and it would turn out to be small molecules. Staudinger tested the clump idea. If rubber were a loose clump, changing its chemistry should break it into small pieces. He added hydrogen to rubber, and it stayed made of giant molecules. In the 1930s, Wallace Carothers, an American chemist, built long chains on purpose by joining small molecules one after another. That is how nylon was made. Staudinger won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953. His wife, Magda Staudinger, a plant scientist, worked with him on giant molecules.  
*What people believed before:* Most chemists thought rubber, starch and cotton were clumps of small molecules stuck loosely together. Staudinger showed that rubber kept its giant molecules even when its chemistry was changed, and Carothers built long chains on purpose.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Rubber and starch are clumps of small molecules stuck loosely together, not giant molecules.** (Most chemists before about 1930; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody had seen a giant molecule, and many chemists thought molecules could not be that big.  
  Against: Hermann Staudinger changed the chemistry of rubber and it stayed made of giant molecules. Wallace Carothers then built long chains from small molecules on purpose.
- **Plastic is one material.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Plastic things look and feel alike: smooth, light and waterproof.  
  Against: There are many different plastics made from different monomers. A milk packet stretches, a bucket is stiff, and thermocol is a foam. The number in the triangle tells them apart.
- **Natural fibres like cotton are always better for the planet than plastic ones.** (Many people; *debated*)  
  For: Cotton rots away when thrown out, and plastic fibres stay for a very long time.  
  Against: Growing cotton needs a lot of land and water. A plastic fibre can be cheap, strong and long-lasting. Which is better depends on how a thing is made, used and thrown away.
- **A plastic bag marked 'biodegradable' rots away quickly wherever it is thrown.** (Many shoppers; *rejected*)  
  For: The word 'biodegradable' means that microbes can break it down.  
  Against: Many of these bags break down only in the heat of a big compost plant. Buried in soil or in the sea, some last for years.

## Roots
We have learned that plastic, rubber, silk and glue are made of very long chain molecules, and that a few ties can join them into one. Now we ask what long chains tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A few teaspoons of borax water turned a cup of runny glue into slime you could hold. Each borax tie joined two long glue molecules. Each molecule is so long that a few ties joined it to many others. So in a material made of long chains, a few links can join the whole into one piece.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a material as a heap of separate tiny pieces, like grains of sugar. But tied long chains stop being separate: the whole cup of slime moved and stretched as one piece.  
*Evidence:* In the borax test, the borax water was a small part of each cup. Yet adding it turned dripping glue into a ball, and more of it made the ball spread less.

- *who am i:* Your hair, skin and muscles are made of proteins, which are long chains. The instructions for your body are written along another long chain, called DNA.
- *what is life:* A silkworm, a spider and a rubber tree each make a polymer to solve a problem in their life: a safe cocoon, a web to catch food, a seal for a cut.
- *what is this world:* Plastic is a polymer that people made. It lasts a very long time, because few living beings can yet break its chains.

A polymer is many small pieces joined into one long molecule. One small piece alone slips about like water. Thousands joined in a row make a silk thread or a strand of hair. Tie those chains to each other, and a whole cup of them moves as one piece, as in your slime.

- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** Over the palace of the god Indra hangs a great net. At every knot is a jewel, and each jewel reflects every other jewel. (In slime, every chain is tied to others, so the whole cup is one net. Pull one part, and the whole slime moves, because each chain is joined to all the rest through its ties.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on a sheet of paper:** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. (Paper is cellulose, long chains that a tree built from water and air using sunlight. The chains in the paper really were made from rain and air.)
- Science (settled): Polymers are real molecules: long chains held by the same strong bonds as small molecules. Staudinger won the Nobel Prize for showing this in 1953.
- Science (open): How the first long-chain molecules, such as RNA, formed on the early Earth is not known.
- Other view, Many chemists: Joining small pieces into chains is simply what chemical bonds do. It is a useful fact about molecules, and it carries no message about the many and the one. Seeing nets and wholes in slime says more about us than about the slime.
- Practice, The human chain (3 min): Let's stand in a line and hold hands, so we are one chain. Close your eyes. I will pull gently on my end. Say 'now' when you feel the pull reach you. Now let go of hands, and I will pull again. Did the pull reach you this time? Take three slow breaths and feel your hands. One hand is a small piece. Joined, we move as one.
- Ask: A few ties joined a whole cup of chains into one slime. Is the slime one thing, or many chains?
- Ask: Your hair is made of chains your body built from the food you ate. Where did your hair begin?
- Ask: A silkworm makes silk for their own cocoon, and people take it for sarees. What do we owe the silkworm?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* One polymer chain is far too small to see. So how can it decide whether a slime is runny or firm? (hint: Think about how many chains there are in one spoon of glue, and how each tie joins two of them.)
- *deep:* Slime stretches when you pull it slowly and snaps when you pull it fast. Is slime a solid or a liquid? (hint: Leave a slime ball on a plate for ten minutes. Then try to bounce it. Does one word fit?)
- *big:* Your hair, a sheep's wool and a silkworm's silk are all proteins. In what way are you made of the same material as a sheep? (hint: Think about what proteins are made of, and where those small pieces come from.)
- *big:* Plastic was invented to last. Now it lasts too long. Is a material good or bad, or does it depend on how we use it? (hint: Think of one plastic thing you would not want to give up, and one you could do without.)
- *big:* A silkworm makes silk without any factory, at room warmth, from mulberry leaves. What could people learn from the way they do it? (hint: Think about what a plastic factory needs: oil, heat and machines.)

## Story: Ammachi's Rubber Sheets
*A made-up story about real work. Rubber tapping in Kerala happens in the early morning, the thin cut, the cup, setting latex with a weak acid, rolling and smoke-drying sheets are all as rubber farmers do them.*

It was the summer holidays, and Meenakshi had come from Chennai to stay with Ammachi, her grandmother, in a village near Kottayam. Ammachi's house stood at the edge of a rubber estate. Rows of tall, thin trees went up the hill, all leaning a little the same way.

Her cousin Arjun was there too. He was six, and he followed Meenakshi everywhere.

On the first morning, Ammachi woke them before it was light. 'Come,' she said. 'Rubber trees give the most latex in the cool of the early morning. By the time the sun is hot, the work is done.'

She carried a torch and a small curved knife. At the first tree, she shone the torch on the bark. A thin groove ran around the trunk in a slanting line. At the bottom of the groove hung a half coconut shell, held by a wire.

Ammachi pressed the knife into the groove and pulled it along. A curl of bark, as thin as a fingernail, peeled away. Almost at once, a white line appeared in the fresh cut. It gathered into drops and began to run down the groove, slowly, like milk. Drip, drip, into the coconut shell.

'Does it hurt the tree?' asked Arjun.

'I take only a very thin strip,' said Ammachi. 'Never too deep. Then the tree heals and gives latex for twenty or thirty years.' She moved on to the next tree. 'The latex is not made for us, you know. It is for the tree. When an insect bites into the bark, latex runs out. It turns sticky and seals the wound. The insect's mouth gets gummed up, and they go away.'

Meenakshi watched the white drops. 'So the tree makes rubber to protect themselves.'

'Yes. We borrow a little.'

When the sun was up, they walked back with a bucket of latex. Ammachi poured it into a flat aluminium tray. Then she put on gloves and added a small amount of acid from a bottle that she kept on a high shelf. 'Nobody else touches this bottle,' she said. She stirred, covered the tray, and left it.

Arjun wanted to know what would happen. Ammachi only smiled. 'Wait till evening.'

In the evening, the milky liquid had gone. In its place lay a soft, white slab, like a big piece of paneer. Ammachi lifted it out, wet and wobbly. She fed it through two heavy rollers turned by a handle. It came out flat and thin. The second rollers pressed lines into it. She hung the sheet up to dry in the smoke shed.

'How did milk become a sheet?' asked Arjun. 'It was runny this morning.'

Meenakshi had an idea. At home in Chennai, her teacher had shown her how to make slime. 'Wait,' she said. 'I can show you something like it.'

Ammachi had made idiyappam for breakfast. A few soft string hoppers were left on the plate. Meenakshi lifted one with her fingers. The thin noodles were wound together, and the whole round came up in one piece.

'Rubber is made of molecules,' she said. 'Molecules are tiny pieces, too small to see. Rubber molecules are not small and round like water. They are very, very long, like these strings of idiyappam. In the latex, the rubber is in tiny drops floating in water. The acid makes the drops stick together. Then all the long molecules tangle into one piece.'

Arjun pulled one string of idiyappam. Three others came with it. 'They hold on to each other,' he said.

Ammachi took a small dry scrap of rubber from her pocket. She stretched it to twice its length, and let go. It sprang back. 'Now you know why it does that,' she said. 'The long chains are coiled up. Pull, and they straighten. Let go, and they coil up again.'

'Is this what tyres are made of?' asked Arjun.

'The sheets go to a factory,' said Ammachi. 'There they heat the rubber with sulfur. Raw rubber goes soft and sticky in the heat. The sulfur ties the long chains to each other, here and there. Then the rubber stays springy, even on a hot road.'

Meenakshi sat up straight. 'That is what borax does to glue! When I made slime, I added borax water. It tied the glue's long molecules together, and the glue went from runny to something I could hold.'

Ammachi laughed. 'So your slime and my tyres are cousins.'

That night, Meenakshi lay awake listening to the trees moving in the wind. Inside every trunk, she thought, the tree was making long chains of rubber, one small piece after another, in the dark.

**For the parent:** For the parent: rubber, glue and slime are all made of very long molecules. Long chains tangle, and ties between them (acid joining latex drops, sulfur in tyres, borax in slime) turn a runny liquid into something you can hold.

- Ask: Why does the rubber tree make latex? How does it help the tree?
- Ask: Why did Ammachi cut only a very thin strip of bark?
- Ask: How did the idiyappam help Arjun understand rubber?
- Ask: What does sulfur do to rubber in a tyre factory? What does borax do to glue?
- Ask: Ammachi said the slime and the tyres are cousins. What do they share?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Borax Test: how much borax makes the firmest slime? (35 min)
- **question:** Does more borax water make slime firmer, so that it spreads less on a plate?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which cup will make the firmest slime: 1, 3 or 6 teaspoons of borax water? Which one will spread out the most on the plate? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every cup gets the same glue, the same water and the same stirring time. Each ball is rolled the same way and measured at the same moments. Only the amount of borax water changes.
- **safety:** Borax is harmful if swallowed and can sting the eyes. A grown-up mixes the borax water and keeps it out of reach. Nobody tastes the slime. Keep it away from babies, toddlers and pets. Wash hands with soap after every step that touches slime. Skip touching slime if your hands have cuts or a rash.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the three balls on the plate after 3 minutes, using the ruler to get the sizes right.

## Worksheets
### Long Chains (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a polymer?  
   Options: A very long molecule made of many small pieces joined in a chain / A very small, round molecule / A kind of metal / A liquid that never flows  
   **Answer:** A very long molecule made of many small pieces joined in a chain. *Why:* A polymer is a very long chain molecule. 'Poly' means many. A strong answer could add an example, such as silk or plastic.
2. (sort) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3 in Mysuru, finds these things at home. Sort each one: is it made of polymers or not?  
   **Answer:** a woollen shawl → Polymer; a steel tumbler → Not a polymer; a plastic bucket → Polymer; a spoon of salt → Not a polymer; a cotton gamosa → Polymer; a glass of water → Not a polymer; a sheet of newspaper → Polymer; a copper wire → Not a polymer. *Why:* Wool, plastic, cotton and paper are made of long chain molecules. Steel and copper are metals, salt is small pieces packed in a pattern, and water molecules are tiny. A strong answer sorts by what the thing is made of, not by whether it is soft or hard.
3. (fill) A tie between two polymer chains is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['cross-link']. *Why:* A cross-link joins one chain to another. Borax makes cross-links in slime, and sulfur makes them in tyres.
4. (truefalse) Only factories can make polymers.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Living beings made polymers long before people did. Silkworms make silk, sheep grow wool, plants make cotton and starch, and you make hair.
5. (write) When you lift cooked rice with a fork, the grains fall off. When you lift noodles, they come up in a clump. Explain what this model tells us about polymers.  
   **Answer:** Rice grains are like small molecules, which slide apart easily. Noodles are like polymers, long chains that tangle, so they hold on to each other and come up together.. *Why:* A strong answer links the grains to small molecules and the noodles to long chains, and uses the word 'tangle'.
6. (mcq) In the borax test, cup 1 had 1 teaspoon of borax water and cup 6 had 6 teaspoons. Which slime spread out more on the plate?  
   Options: Cup 1, because it had fewer ties between its chains / Cup 6, because it had more borax / Both spread the same / Neither spread at all  
   **Answer:** Cup 1, because it had fewer ties between its chains. *Why:* Fewer ties means the chains can still slide past each other, so the slime flows and spreads. Cup 6 had many ties and held its shape.
7. (draw) Draw what you think the glue's long molecules look like before borax is added, and after. Use dots for the ties.  
   **Answer:** Before: loose wavy lines. After: wavy lines joined to each other by dots at several places.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the same chains both times, with only the ties added. Nothing about the chains themselves changes.
8. (match) Match each material to the living being that makes it.  
   **Answer:** Silk → silkworm; Wool → sheep; Cotton → cotton plant; Latex → rubber tree; Hair → you. *Why:* Each of these is a polymer made by a living being. A strong answer could also say why the being makes it, such as the silkworm's cocoon.

   Options: A steel spoon / A silk thread / A rubber band / A milk packet

## Open questions
- **How did the first long-chain molecules, such as RNA, form on the early Earth?** Scientists can join small pieces into short chains in the lab, but nobody knows exactly how long chains first formed before there was life.
- **Can we make plastics that do everything plastic does, and then break down safely?** Some new plastics break down in compost plants, and in 2016 scientists found bacteria that can digest one kind of plastic. None yet does both jobs everywhere.
- **Exactly how does a spider make silk so strong at room warmth?** Scientists know which proteins are in spider silk, but copying the way spiders spin silk is still very hard.
- **How long does a plastic bag really last in the sea?** Plastic has existed for only about a hundred years, so nobody has watched one bag for its whole life. Estimates differ a lot.

## Connected maths
- In the borax test, the ball from cup 3 was 4 cm wide at the start and 7 cm wide after 3 minutes. The ball from cup 6 was 4 cm wide at the start and 5 cm after 3 minutes. How much more did the cup 3 ball spread than the cup 6 ball? **2 cm more.**. The cup 3 ball grew from 4 cm to 7 cm. 7 minus 4 is 3 cm. The cup 6 ball grew from 4 cm to 5 cm. 5 minus 4 is 1 cm. 3 minus 1 is 2. So the cup 3 ball spread 2 cm more. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Cup 3 got 3 teaspoons of borax water and cup 6 got 6. How many times as much borax water did cup 6 get? If you made a cup with twice as much as cup 6, how many teaspoons would that be? **Twice as much. A cup with twice as much as cup 6 would get 12 teaspoons.**. 6 divided by 3 is 2, so cup 6 got twice as much. Twice 6 is 12. The pattern 3, 6, 12 doubles each time. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3, marked a milk packet strip in squares 1 cm long. After she pulled it, square 5 was 4 cm long. How many times longer did square 5 become? If three squares each grew to 4 cm and the other seven stayed 1 cm, how long is the marked part now? **4 times longer. The marked part is now 19 cm long.**. 4 cm divided by 1 cm is 4, so square 5 became 4 times longer. Three squares of 4 cm make 12 cm. Seven squares of 1 cm make 7 cm. 12 plus 7 is 19 cm. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)

How many links does each have now? **Mitali has 7 links and Aaliya has 8.**. Mitali gets 2 links for her two right answers: 4 plus 2 is 6. The cross-link gives her 1 more: 6 plus 1 is 7.
