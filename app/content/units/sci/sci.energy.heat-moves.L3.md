# How heat moves: why a steel spoon in hot tea gets hot
`sci.energy.heat-moves.L3`

**Summary.** Leave a steel spoon standing in hot tea, and in a minute or two its handle is warm, though only the bottom touched the tea. Heat has travelled up through the solid steel. Heat always moves from a hotter thing to a cooler one, and through a solid it passes from each tiny part to the part next to it. This is called conduction. In this unit your child puts butter dots along a steel ladle in hot water and times them as they melt, one after another. They find that heat reaches the near dots fast, the far dots slowly, and the farthest dot perhaps never.

**Intro.** *Stand a steel spoon in a cup of hot tea for two minutes. Touch the top of the handle. It is warm, though it never touched the tea. How did the heat get up there?* No hot tea climbed up the spoon. Only the bottom of the spoon is in the tea. Yet the handle warms, slowly, from the bottom up. In this unit you find out how heat travels through a solid, and how fast. You time butter melting along a ladle, you hold ice in your fist, and you find out why a dosa tawa has a wooden handle.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say which way heat moves: from a hotter thing to a cooler one.
1. By the end I can explain how heat travels through a solid spoon, part by part, and call it conduction.
2. By the end I can show, from my ladle test, that heat reaches near parts first and far parts later, and some heat escapes into the air on the way.
3. By the end I can name materials that carry heat fast (metals) and slowly (wood, plastic, cloth, air), and say why pans have metal bottoms and wooden or plastic handles.
4. By the end I can plan a fair test, guess with a reason, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
5. By the end I can explain that cold does not move into my hand: heat moves out of it.

## Terms
- **Heat**: Energy that moves from a hotter thing to a cooler thing. *e.g.* Heat moves from hot tea into the steel spoon standing in it.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is. We measure it in degrees Celsius, written °C. *e.g.* Hot tea is about 60 °C. A room in Chennai in summer can be about 32 °C.
- **Conduction**: Heat moving through a material, or from one thing into another thing it touches, part by part, with nothing flowing. *e.g.* The handle of a steel ladle gets hot by conduction, from the end that sits in the hot sambar.
- **Conductor of heat**: A material that lets heat move through it quickly. Metals are good conductors of heat. *e.g.* A steel tawa, an aluminium kadai and a copper-bottomed vessel are all good conductors of heat.
- **Insulator of heat**: A material that lets heat through only very slowly. Wood, plastic, cloth and still air are insulators of heat. *e.g.* A pressure cooker has a plastic handle, so the heat of the cooker reaches your hand only very slowly.
- **Particle**: One of the tiny pieces that everything is made of. They are far too small to see, even with a magnifying glass. *e.g.* A steel spoon is made of a huge number of particles packed tightly together.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* In the ladle test, every butter dot is the same size. Only its distance from the hot water changes.

## Explanations
### Heat moves from hotter to cooler · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat moves by itself from a hotter thing to a cooler thing. It keeps moving until both are equally warm.

Hot tea warms the spoon in it. The spoon never cools the tea down to colder than the room. An ice cube in your hand melts because heat moves from your hand into the ice. Your hand feels cold because it is losing heat. No cold comes out of the ice. Heat never moves by itself from a cooler thing to a hotter one. A fridge can make that happen only because it uses electricity to pump the heat out.
- e.g. Hot rasam in a steel bowl warms the bowl, and the bowl warms the table under it.
- e.g. A cold bottle from the fridge gets warmer on the table, because heat moves into it from the warmer room.
### Conduction: heat passed from part to part · *defined*
**Definition:** Conduction is heat moving through a material, from each tiny part to the part touching it.

The particles in a solid are packed tightly and are always shaking a little. The hotter a thing is, the faster its particles shake. Hot tea makes the particles at the bottom of the spoon shake faster. They knock into the particles just above them, and those start to shake faster too. Those knock into the next ones, and so on up the handle. No particle travels up the spoon. Each one stays in its place and passes the shaking on. That passed-on shaking is the heat you feel in the handle.
- e.g. The steel handle of a ladle in hot sambar warms from the bottom up.
- e.g. Your hand warms a cold steel tumbler where your fingers touch it, then the warmth spreads around the tumbler.
### Why heat reaches far parts late, or not at all · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat travelling through a solid reaches nearby parts quickly and far parts much more slowly. Some of it escapes into the air along the way.

Before heat can move past a part of the spoon, it must first warm that part. So each part has to wait for the part before it. That is why the waits grow longer as you go further along. Twice as far takes much more than twice as long. All the way along, the warm steel also gives some heat to the air around it. So less heat is left for the far end. On a long ladle, the far end may never get hot at all. That is one reason ladles for hot sambar have long handles.
- e.g. On the Ladle Line, the dot 2 cm up may melt in under a minute, while the dot 8 cm up may never melt.
- e.g. The end of a long iron skewer held in a fire stays cool enough to hold for a while, while the tip glows.
### Conductors and insulators of heat · *defined*
**Definition:** A conductor of heat lets heat through quickly. An insulator of heat lets it through only very slowly.

Metals are good conductors of heat. Wood, plastic, cloth, paper and still air are insulators. Metals are not all the same, though. Copper and aluminium carry heat many times faster than the stainless steel most Indian spoons are made of. That is why some steel vessels have a copper or aluminium layer on the bottom. Cooks use both kinds of material together. The pan is metal, so heat reaches the food fast. The handle is wood or plastic, so heat reaches your hand slowly.
- e.g. A dosa tawa: an iron or aluminium plate with a wooden handle.
- e.g. A pressure cooker: an aluminium or steel pot with plastic handles.
- e.g. A coconut-shell ladle, used in many Kerala kitchens, stays cool to hold in hot curry.
### Why metals are such good conductors · *defined*
**Definition:** In a metal, some tiny particles called electrons are free to move about between the atoms. They carry heat quickly from the hot part to the cool part.

Every atom has electrons on its outside. In wood or plastic, the electrons stay tied to their own atoms. Then heat can only be passed on by atoms nudging their neighbours, which is slow. In a metal, some electrons wander freely through the whole piece. They pick up energy in the hot part and quickly carry it to the cool part. These free electrons are also why metals carry electricity. So the best conductors of electricity, like silver and copper, are also the best conductors of heat.
- e.g. Copper carries both heat and electricity very well. It is used in electric wires and in the bottoms of good cooking pots.
- e.g. Wood carries neither heat nor electricity well.
### Two more ways heat moves · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat can also be carried by a moving liquid or gas, and it can travel as invisible rays.

In a pan of water on the stove, the hot water at the bottom rises and cooler water sinks to take its place. This is called convection. It is why the whole pan heats, though only the bottom touches the flame. The Sun's heat reaches us across empty space, where there is nothing to pass it on. It comes as rays we cannot see. This is called radiation. You feel it on your face near a fire, without touching anything. A spoon in tea gets hot mainly by conduction.
- e.g. Steam and hot air rising above a pot of boiling rice is convection.
- e.g. Your face feeling warm in the sun is radiation.

## Mostly agreed
- Heat moves by itself only from hotter things to cooler things. *How we know:* Every test ever done agrees: hot things cool to the temperature around them, and cold things warm up to it. No one has ever seen heat move by itself the other way.
- In a solid, heat travels by conduction, passed from part to part. *How we know:* Butter or wax on a metal rod melts in order, starting nearest the heat, as Jan Ingenhousz saw on his wax-coated wires in 1780.
- Metals are good conductors of heat, and wood, plastic, cloth and still air are insulators. *How we know:* Scientists have measured how fast heat moves through thousands of materials. Copper carries heat more than a thousand times faster than wood.
- The hotter a thing is, the faster its particles move. *How we know:* Scientists can measure the speed of particles in gases, and it rises as the gas gets hotter. A drop of food colour also spreads faster in hot water than in cold, because the water particles are moving faster.

## How we found out
**Benjamin Franklin, an American scientist, who suggested the test, and Jan Ingenhousz, a Dutch doctor and scientist, who did it, 1780 (Ingenhousz's letter to Franklin from Vienna, 5 December 1780, gives the results).** In the 1700s, people knew that metals carry heat quickly. A metal pot handle gets hot, and a wooden one does not. It was easy to guess that all metals, being hard and shiny, carry heat equally well. Benjamin Franklin, an American scientist and writer, thought of a simple way to test this. Coat wires of different metals with wax. Heat one end of each wire. Then see how far along each wire the wax melts. In 1780, Franklin gave the idea, and wires he had collected, to his friend Jan Ingenhousz. Ingenhousz was a Dutch doctor and scientist working in Vienna. He dipped each wire in melted wax, then put one end of each into hot oil. The wax melted along every wire, but much farther on some than on others. Silver carried heat best. Then came copper and gold, then tin, then iron and steel. Lead was slowest of all. So metals are not all alike. Your Ladle Line test works the same way, with butter in place of wax. Steel came low on Ingenhousz's list.  
*What people believed before:* It was easy to guess that all metals, being hard and shiny, carry heat equally well. The wax-coated wires showed that heat moves much faster through silver and copper than through iron, steel or lead.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Heat always rises.** (A common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: Hot air and hot water do rise, and steam goes up from a pot. The top of a room is often warmer than the floor.  
  Against: It is hot air and hot water that rise, because they are lighter than the cool air or water around them. Inside a solid, heat goes every way. A spoon lying flat with its bowl in hot water warms along its handle just as an upright one does. Heat goes from hotter to cooler, in whatever direction that is.
- **When you hold ice, cold moves from the ice into your hand.** (A very common way of talking; *rejected*)  
  For: Your hand feels cold, and it really does get colder.  
  Against: Your hand gets colder because heat moves out of it into the ice. That is why the ice melts. Cold is not a thing that moves. It is only less heat.
- **All metals carry heat equally well.** (An easy guess, because metals look and feel alike; *rejected*)  
  For: All metals are conductors of heat, and all feel cold to touch.  
  Against: When Jan Ingenhousz tested wax-coated wires in 1780, the wax melted farthest on silver and least far on lead. Today we know copper carries heat about 25 times faster than stainless steel.

## Roots
We have watched heat travel up a ladle, part by part. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** On your ladle, the peppercorn nearest the water fell first, and each one after took longer. Heat must warm each part of the steel before it can move on to the next. Along the way, some of it leaks into the air. So heat slows down as it travels along a handle, and the far end may stay cool.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture heat running through a spoon like water through a pipe, at one steady speed. But heat in a solid is handed on part by part, so it gets slower the further it goes, and some escapes on the way.  
*Evidence:* In the Ladle Line, the waits between the falling peppercorns grew longer, and the 8 cm dot often never fell. A palm held near the handle felt warmth leaving it into the air.

- *who am i:* Your body is warmer than most things around you, so your heat is moving into the air, the floor and your clothes all day.
- *what is life:* A garden lizard is cold and slow after the night. Lying flat on a sun-warmed wall, they take in its heat through their belly, and soon they can run.
- *what is this world:* The inside of the Earth is very hot. Its heat is creeping out through solid rock towards the surface all the time.

A steel ladle looks like one solid thing. Heat shows that it is also a huge number of tiny parts, each touching the next. The hot water warms the first part, that part warms the next, and so on along the handle. How warm each part gets depends on its neighbours. One ladle, made of many parts, with one heat passing through them all.

- **Heraclitus, fragment B126 (Diels-Kranz numbering):** Cold things grow warm, the warm grows cold, the wet dries and the dry grows wet. (In your tests, the hot water cooled while the ladle warmed, and your warm hand cooled while the ice melted. Heat keeps moving from warm to cool, so warm and cold things keep changing into each other.)
- **Samyutta Nikaya 12.21 (the Buddha's teaching of dependent arising):** When this is, that is. When this arises, that arises. When this is not, that is not. (Each part of the ladle grew warm only because the part before it was warm. When the water was taken away, the warmth along the handle faded too.)
- Science (settled): Heat moves by itself only from hotter to cooler things. Scientists call this the second law of thermodynamics.
- Science (settled): In metals, free electrons carry most of the heat. That is why good conductors of electricity are also good conductors of heat.
- Other view, Many physicists: Heat moving along a ladle is just particles and electrons bumping into each other. Saying that each part 'depends on' its neighbour is a nice way to put it, but it adds nothing to the physics.
- Practice, Warm the Tumbler (3 min): Fill a steel tumbler with cool water. Hold it in both hands and close your eyes. Breathe slowly. Feel the cold steel under your fingers. Now keep holding it for one minute. Does the steel under your fingers feel the same, or less cold? Your warmth is moving into the steel, and through it into the water. Open your eyes and put it down.
- Ask: The far end of your ladle stayed cool. Did the heat stop, or did it go somewhere else?
- Ask: Each part of the ladle was warmed by the part next to it. So where does the ladle's warmth really begin?
- Ask: Your hand warmed the cold tumbler. Once that warmth is in the steel, is it still yours?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* No particle of the spoon travelled up to the handle. So what exactly moved up the spoon? (hint: Think of a line of people passing on a push, each one staying in their place.)
- *deep:* The far end of your ladle stayed cool. Where did the heat go that never reached it? (hint: Hold your hand near the middle of a warm ladle, without touching it.)
- *big:* Your body is warmer than the room almost all the time. So heat is leaving you all day. Where does your body get more? (hint: Think about what you ate today.)
- *big:* Heat always spreads out from hot places to cool ones. If that goes on for ever, will everything in the universe one day be the same temperature? (hint: Scientists have asked this too. Some think so, but nobody knows for sure.)
- *deep:* Why would a cook want one part of a vessel to be a good conductor and another part to be a good insulator? (hint: Think about where the heat should go, and where it should not.)

## Story: The Ladle in the Payasam
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: heat moves up a steel ladle by conduction, near parts first, and wood and coconut shell are insulators of heat.*

It was the morning of Onam, and Meenu's flat in Kochi smelled of ghee and jaggery. Meenu was eight. Her cousin Arjun was six, and he had come from Thrissur for the festival. In the kitchen, Meenu's ammayi, Revathi, was stirring a big pot of payasam on the gas stove.

"Meenu, will you stir for a minute?" said Ammayi. "I must fetch the cashews."

Meenu loved stirring payasam. She took hold of the long steel ladle where Ammayi had left it standing in the pot. Then she let go at once.

"Ow! It's hot!" She shook her fingers.

Arjun peered at the ladle. "But you only touched the top. The top isn't in the payasam."

"I know," said Meenu, frowning. "It was hot anyway."

Ammayi came back with the cashews. She took a folded cloth and lifted the ladle out. "Show me your fingers," she said. They were pink, but fine. Ammayi ran them under the cold tap for a while.

"How did the top get hot?" asked Meenu. "The payasam didn't climb up the ladle."

"No, it didn't," said Ammayi. "Let's find out. You two can do an experiment while the payasam cools."

She gave Meenu a clean steel ladle and a little butter from the fridge. Meenu put four small dots of butter along the handle, a finger-width apart. Arjun pressed a peppercorn into each dot. Ammayi poured hot water from the kettle into a steel tumbler and stood the ladle in it, with the dots facing up.

"Which peppercorn will fall first?" asked Ammayi.

"The top one," said Arjun at once. "Because heat goes up."

"The bottom one," said Meenu. "Because it is nearest the hot water."

They watched. For a little while nothing happened. Then the lowest dot went shiny, and its peppercorn slid down into the tumbler with a tiny plop.

"Mine!" said Meenu.

A long time later, the second peppercorn slid off. Then they waited, and waited, for the third. Arjun counted to a hundred twice before it moved. The top peppercorn did not fall at all.

"The heat is slow," said Arjun. "It got tired."

Ammayi laughed. "Heat doesn't get tired. But you've seen something real. Imagine the ladle is made of tiny pieces, far too small to see, all in a line. The hot water makes the bottom pieces shake faster. They bump the pieces above them, and those start shaking too. The heat is passed up, piece by piece. Each piece has to warm up before it can pass the heat on. So the far pieces have to wait a long time."

"Why didn't the top one ever melt?" asked Meenu.

"Hold your hand near the middle of the ladle," said Ammayi. "Don't touch it."

Meenu held her palm close to the handle. "It's a bit warm, even in the air."

"Yes. The ladle gives some of its heat to the air all along the way," said Ammayi. "By the time it gets near the top, not much is left. This is called conduction. It's how heat moves through a solid."

Arjun looked at the ladle, then at the stove. "Then why doesn't the dosa tawa burn your hand when you hold it?"

"Go and look at its handle," said Ammayi.

Arjun fetched the tawa from the shelf. Its handle was made of wood. "Wood!" he said.

"Wood is an insulator of heat," said Ammayi. "Heat moves through it very slowly. Metal is a good conductor. That's why the pan is metal and the handle is wood. The heat goes into the dosa, not into my hand."

Meenu thought for a moment. Then she opened the bottom drawer and pulled out something brown and round on a stick. It was Ammamma's old chiratta ladle, a ladle made from half a coconut shell with a wooden handle.

"Can we stir the payasam with this one?" she asked.

"That's what Ammamma always did," said Ammayi, smiling. "Try it."

Meenu stirred the payasam slowly, round and round. The handle stayed cool in her hand all the way through. Arjun watched the steam curling up from the pot.

"Ammayi," he said, "the steam goes up. So does heat go up, or not?"

"Hot steam rises, because it is lighter than the air," said Ammayi. "But in the ladle, heat goes from hot to cool, whichever way that is. Up, down or sideways. Tomorrow you can test it with a ladle lying flat."

That afternoon, the whole family ate payasam on banana leaves. Arjun said it was the best payasam in Kerala, because it had been stirred with a cool spoon.

**For the parent:** Heat moves through a solid ladle part by part, from the hot end towards the cool end. It reaches near parts first and far parts much later, and wood and coconut shell let it through so slowly that a handle made of them stays cool.

- Ask: Why did the top of the steel ladle burn Meenu's fingers, when it was not in the payasam?
- Ask: Arjun guessed the top peppercorn would fall first, because heat goes up. What did the test show?
- Ask: Why do you think the top peppercorn never fell?
- Ask: Why does a tawa have a wooden handle?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Ladle Line: how does heat climb a steel ladle? (30 min)
- **question:** When a steel ladle stands in hot water, do all the butter dots on its handle melt at once, or one after another? And does the farthest one melt at all?
- **predict:** Look at the four butter dots on the handle. Which will melt first, and which last? Will they melt close together or far apart in time? Will the top one melt at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every butter dot is the same size, about half a pea, with one peppercorn in it. The dots are 2, 4, 6 and 8 cm above the water. Only the distance changes. One more dot sits on a spoon on a plate, out of the water, to show what the room alone does to butter.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water and keeps the tumbler steady. The child does not touch the ladle while it is in the water. After the test, a grown-up lifts the ladle out with a cloth.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the ladle with the four dots and write the time beside each one.
- **results table:** Dot | Height above the water (cm) | My guess: order it falls | Time it fell (minutes and seconds) | Wait since the dot before

  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the empty ladle in the empty tumbler, leaning on the rim. 2. Mark where the top of the water will be, about two-thirds up the tumbler. 3. Measure up the handle from that line and mark 2, 4, 6 and 8 cm.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to see how heat travels up a ladle. First we mark four places on the handle, each 2 cm further from the water.  
  *Child mode:* Mark the handle at 2, 4, 6 and 8 cm. Measure from the water line.

  *Child mode:* Make four small butter dots, all the same size. Press a peppercorn into each one.

### e2 · Flat or Upright: does heat only go up? (25 min)
- **question:** Does heat move along a steel spoon as fast when the spoon lies flat as when it stands up?

### Which Way Is the Heat Going? (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which way does heat move by itself?  
   Options: From a cooler thing to a hotter thing / From a hotter thing to a cooler thing / Only upwards  
   **Answer:** From a hotter thing to a cooler thing. *Why:* Heat moves by itself from hotter to cooler, until both are equally warm. 'Only upwards' is a common mix-up with hot air rising.
2. (fill) Heat moving through a solid, from each tiny part to the part next to it, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['conduction']. *Why:* Conduction is how heat travels through a solid like a steel spoon.
3. (truefalse) When you hold an ice cube, cold moves from the ice into your hand.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Heat moves from your warm hand into the ice. Your hand feels cold because it is losing heat. Cold does not move.
4. (sort) Sort these into good conductors of heat and insulators of heat.  
   **Answer:** steel spoon → Good conductor; wooden spatula → Insulator; aluminium kadai → Good conductor; plastic bucket → Insulator; copper wire → Good conductor; cotton cloth → Insulator; iron tawa → Good conductor; newspaper → Insulator. *Why:* The metals are good conductors of heat. Wood, plastic, cloth and paper are insulators. A strong sorter asks 'Is it a metal?'
5. (order) Kavya put butter dots on a steel ladle at 2, 4, 6 and 8 cm above hot water. Put the dots in the order their peppercorns fell, first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['the 2 cm dot', 'the 4 cm dot', 'the 6 cm dot', 'the 8 cm dot']. *Why:* Heat reaches the parts nearest the hot water first, then moves further along. So the nearest dot falls first.
6. (write) In the Ladle Line test, the peppercorn at 8 cm often never falls. Give two reasons why.  
   **Answer:** The heat has a long way to go, and each part of the steel must warm up before it passes heat on. Also, the handle gives some of its heat to the air all along the way, so little is left by 8 cm.. *Why:* A strong answer gives both reasons: the slow part-by-part travel, and heat leaking into the air.
7. (draw) Draw a tumbler of hot milk with a steel spoon standing in it. Draw red arrows to show where the heat moves.  
   **Answer:** Arrows from the milk into the spoon, up the spoon handle, and from the handle and the tumbler out into the air.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows heat going from the hot milk to cooler things: up the spoon, and out into the air.
8. (mcq) Lakshmi pours hot rasam into a cold steel bowl. What happens to the bowl?  
   Options: It stays cold, because steel is cold / It warms up, because heat moves from the rasam into the steel / It makes the rasam colder than the room  
   **Answer:** It warms up, because heat moves from the rasam into the steel. *Why:* Heat moves from the hot rasam into the cooler steel, so the bowl warms. It can cool the rasam a little, but never below the room's temperature.
9. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Heat → energy moving from hotter to cooler; Conduction → heat passed through a solid, part by part; Conductor → a material heat moves through quickly; Insulator → a material heat moves through slowly. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit.
10. (truefalse) A wooden spoon left in hot payasam gets a hot handle just as fast as a steel spoon.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Wood is an insulator of heat, so heat moves up a wooden spoon very slowly. Its handle stays cool for a long time.
11. (write) Why does a dosa tawa have a metal plate but a wooden handle?  
   **Answer:** The metal plate is a good conductor, so heat from the flame moves quickly into the dosa. The wooden handle is an insulator, so the heat reaches the cook's hand only very slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer explains both parts: where the cook wants heat to go, and where not.
12. (mcq) Divya tests a ladle. She puts a big butter dot at 2 cm and a tiny butter dot at 6 cm. Why is this not a fair test?  
   Options: The dots are different sizes, so size changes how fast they melt / The ladle is steel / She used butter, not ghee  
   **Answer:** The dots are different sizes, so size changes how fast they melt. *Why:* A big dot takes longer to melt than a tiny one. In a fair test only the distance should change, so every dot must be the same size.
13. (label) Label this picture of a spoon in hot tea: the hot end, the cool end, and the way the heat moves.  
   **Answer:** The hot end is the part in the tea. The cool end is the top of the handle. An arrow goes from the hot end up to the cool end.. *Why:* Heat moves from the hot end towards the cool end.
14. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Steel, copper, wood, aluminium.  
   **Answer:** Wood. *Why:* Wood is an insulator of heat. Steel, copper and aluminium are metals, which are good conductors.
15. (write) Think about your own Ladle Line test. Write what you think, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
   **Answer:** What I think: heat travels up the steel slowly, nearest parts first. What I saw: the 2 cm peppercorn fell first, and each one after took longer. Why: each part has to warm before it can pass heat to the next.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the child's own times as evidence.

   Options: Hot milk climbs up inside the spoon / Heat is passed up the steel, from part to part / The air pushes cold out of the spoon  
   **Answer:** Heat is passed up the steel, from part to part. *Why:* This is conduction. Each part of the steel warms the part next to it. No milk climbs up.
3. (mcq) Meena puts butter dots on a steel ladle at 2 cm and 6 cm above hot water. What will most likely happen?  
   Options: The 6 cm dot melts first / The 2 cm dot melts first, and the 6 cm dot much later or not at all / Both melt at the same moment  
   **Answer:** The 2 cm dot melts first, and the 6 cm dot much later or not at all. *Why:* Heat reaches the nearest part first. The far part waits much longer, and gets less heat, because some leaks into the air.
4. (mcq) Which of these is a good conductor of heat?  
   Options: A wooden spatula / An aluminium kadai / A plastic plate  
   **Answer:** An aluminium kadai. *Why:* Aluminium is a metal, and metals are good conductors of heat. Wood and plastic are insulators.
5. (mcq) Why do pressure cookers have plastic handles?  
   Options: Plastic is cheaper than metal / Plastic is an insulator, so the handle stays cool enough to hold / Plastic makes the food cook faster  
   **Answer:** Plastic is an insulator, so the handle stays cool enough to hold. *Why:* Heat moves through plastic very slowly, so the handle does not get hot quickly.
6. (mcq) Priya holds an ice cube and her hand gets cold. Why?  
   Options: Cold moves from the ice into her hand / Heat moves from her hand into the ice / The ice is wet  
   **Answer:** Heat moves from her hand into the ice. *Why:* Heat moves from warmer to cooler. Her hand loses heat to the ice, so it feels cold, and the ice melts.
7. (mcq) Farhan wants to test how far heat travels up a ladle. Which test is fair?  
   Options: Dots of different sizes at different heights / Dots of the same size at different heights / One dot, moved about during the test  
   **Answer:** Dots of the same size at different heights. *Why:* In a fair test, only one thing changes. Here it is the height, so every dot must be the same size.
8. (mcq) A steel spoon lies flat, with only its bowl in hot water. What happens to its handle?  
   Options: It warms up, because heat moves from hot to cool in any direction / It stays cold, because heat only goes up / It gets colder  
   **Answer:** It warms up, because heat moves from hot to cool in any direction. *Why:* Inside a solid, heat goes from hot to cool, up, down or sideways.
9. (mcq) Why do the particles in a hot spoon pass heat along?  
   Options: They flow up the handle like water / They shake faster and bump the particles next to them / They get bigger  
   **Answer:** They shake faster and bump the particles next to them. *Why:* Hot particles shake faster. They bump their neighbours, which then shake faster too. The particles stay in place.
10. (mcq) Which metal carries heat fastest?  
   Options: Copper / Stainless steel / Lead  
   **Answer:** Copper. *Why:* Copper is one of the best conductors of heat. Stainless steel and lead are much slower.
11. (mcq) Kavya's ladle dots fell at 1 minute, 3 minutes and 8 minutes. What does this tell her?  
   Options: Heat moved at one steady speed / Heat took longer and longer to reach parts further from the water / The water was getting hotter  
   **Answer:** Heat took longer and longer to reach parts further from the water. *Why:* The waits grew from 2 minutes to 5 minutes. Heat gets slower as it travels further along the steel.
12. (mcq) Which of these is mostly conduction?  
   Options: The Sun warming your face / A hot tawa cooking a dosa / Steam rising from a pot  
   **Answer:** A hot tawa cooking a dosa. *Why:* The dosa touches the hot tawa, so heat passes straight into it. The Sun is radiation, and rising steam is convection.

## Applications
- Cooking vessels: metal bodies to carry heat into food, and wooden or plastic handles to keep it from the cook's hand.
- Steel vessels with a copper or aluminium bottom, so heat spreads evenly and food does not burn in one spot.
- Pot holders, oven gloves and the folded cloth a cook uses to lift a hot vessel.
- Computers and phones have metal parts that carry heat away from their chips, so they do not overheat.
- Flasks and thermos bottles keep tea hot by stopping heat from moving out.

## Open questions
- **What is the best possible conductor of heat?** Scientists keep finding materials that carry heat better, such as diamond and very thin sheets of carbon called graphene. Nobody knows the limit.
- **Exactly how does heat move through glass, wood and other materials whose particles are not in neat rows?** Scientists understand heat moving through neat crystals well. In jumbled materials it is harder to work out, and they are still studying it.
- **Will everything in the universe one day be the same temperature?** Heat keeps spreading from hot places to cool ones. Some scientists think the universe will slowly even out, but nobody knows for sure.

## Connected maths
- Meenakshi's peppercorns fell at 40 seconds, 150 seconds and 360 seconds. How long was the wait between the first and the second? And between the second and the third? **110 seconds, then 210 seconds.**. 150 minus 40 is 110. 360 minus 150 is 210. The second wait was almost twice as long as the first. So the heat took longer and longer to reach each dot. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Kavya's ladle handle is 20 cm long. She wants a butter dot every 4 cm, starting 4 cm from the water. How many dots can she fit? **5 dots.**. The dots go at 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 cm. 20 divided by 4 is 5. So she can fit 5 dots. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- The upright spoon's peppercorn fell at 95 seconds. The flat spoon's fell at 105 seconds. What is the difference? Is that enough to say heat goes up faster? **10 seconds. That is too small to be sure.**. 105 minus 95 is 10. A difference of 10 seconds can come from a slightly bigger dot or shallower water. To be sure, you would do the test again. If the flat one were not always slower, the difference was luck. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
- Divya's waits between peppercorns were 1 minute, then 2 minutes, then 4 minutes. If the pattern carried on, how long would the next wait be? **8 minutes.**. Each wait is double the one before: 1, 2, 4. Double 4 is 8. In a real test the pattern is not exact, and the far dot may never fall at all, because heat leaks into the air. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- The butter in the steel katori went soft in 3 minutes. The butter in the plastic lid took 7 minutes, and in the paper cup 11 minutes. How much longer did the paper cup take than the steel katori? **8 minutes longer.**. 11 minus 3 is 8. The steel katori is a good conductor, so heat reached the butter fastest. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
