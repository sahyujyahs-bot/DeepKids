# From flower to fruit: the parts of a flower, pollination, and how each seed begins with one pollen grain
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.pollination.L5`

**Summary.** The pollen of a hibiscus flower lands on the stigma, at the very top of the flower. The ovules, which become the seeds, are several centimetres below, sealed inside the ovary. So how does pollen on the outside start a seed on the inside? Your child takes a flower apart and names the four rings of parts: sepals, petals, stamens and carpels. They learn that pollination only brings the pollen to the stigma. After that, each pollen grain grows a thin tube down to one ovule. A male cell joins the egg cell there. That joining is fertilisation. Then the ovary grows into the fruit, and each fertilised ovule grows into a seed. In the Empty Places Test, your child opens 20 pods and counts the seeds and the ovules that never grew.

**Intro.** *Open a pea pod and count. You may find five fat peas, and two tiny pale specks that never grew. All seven sat in the same pod, on the same plant, with the same water and light. Why did five become seeds and two did not?* The answer starts in the flower, weeks before the pod was picked. In this unit you first take a hibiscus flower apart, ring by ring. You find the parts that make pollen and the part that holds the ovules. You learn how pollen travels from flower to flower, and what happens after the pollen lands. Then you open 20 pods and count the seeds and the empty places. You also drop a rain of moong seeds to see how much pollen the wind wastes. By the end you can say what has to happen before one seed can form.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can find the four rings of a real flower, and name the parts of a stamen and a carpel.
1. I can say what job each part of a flower does.
2. I can explain what pollination is, and tell self-pollination from cross-pollination.
3. I can tell from a flower's features whether the wind or an animal probably carries the pollen.
4. I can explain fertilisation: a pollen tube carries a male cell to the egg cell inside an ovule.
5. I can say which part of a flower grows into the fruit, and which part grows into each seed.
6. I can explain why a fruit can have empty places: each seed needs their own pollen grain.
7. I can count results from many pods or many runs, and say why one count is not enough.

## Terms
- **Flower**: The part of a plant that makes seeds. A complete flower has four rings of parts: sepals, petals, stamens and carpels. *e.g.* A hibiscus flower has 5 sepals, 5 petals, many stamens and 5 joined carpels.
- **Sepal**: One of the small leaf-like parts in the outer ring of a flower. The sepals cover and protect the bud before the flower opens. *e.g.* The green cap on top of a brinjal or a tomato is made of the flower's sepals.
- **Petal**: One of the parts in the second ring of a flower. Petals are often brightly coloured or scented, and they help animal visitors find the flower. *e.g.* A hibiscus has 5 large red petals. A paddy flower has none.
- **Stamen**: The male part of a flower. A stamen is a thin stalk called a filament, with an anther on top that makes pollen. *e.g.* A mustard flower has 6 stamens. A hibiscus has more than 50.
- **Anther**: The head of a stamen. The anther makes pollen grains, and splits open to let them out when they are ripe. *e.g.* Touch the yellow anthers of a hibiscus, and yellow pollen comes off on your finger.
- **Filament**: The stalk of a stamen. The filament holds the anther up where a visitor or the wind can reach the pollen. *e.g.* The anthers of a paddy flower hang outside the flower on long, thin filaments.
- **Pollen grain**: A tiny living grain made in an anther. Each pollen grain carries the male cells that can fertilise an ovule. *e.g.* A maize pollen grain is about one tenth of a millimetre wide, like a speck of yellow dust.
- **Carpel**: The female part of a flower. A carpel has a stigma on top, a style in the middle and an ovary at the bottom. Many flowers have several carpels joined into one. The female part is also called the pistil. *e.g.* A pea flower has 1 carpel, which becomes the pod. A hibiscus has 5 carpels joined together.
- **Stigma**: The top of a carpel. The stigma is sticky or feathery, and catches pollen grains. *e.g.* A hibiscus has 5 round, velvety stigmas at the very tip of the flower.
- **Style**: The stalk of a carpel. The style joins the stigma to the ovary, and pollen tubes grow down through it. *e.g.* Each silk thread on a maize cob is one very long style.
- **Ovary**: The swollen base of a carpel. The ovary holds the ovules, and grows into the fruit after fertilisation. *e.g.* The small green swelling at the base of a bhindi flower is the ovary. It grows into the bhindi.
- **Ovule**: A tiny grain inside the ovary that holds one egg cell. A fertilised ovule grows into a seed. *e.g.* A pea flower's ovary holds about 6 to 10 ovules in a row. Each can become one pea.
- **Nectar**: A sugary liquid that many flowers make. Animals visit flowers to drink nectar, and they carry pollen away on their bodies. *e.g.* Pull out an ixora flower and you find a drop of sweet nectar at the base of the thin tube.
- **Pollination**: The carrying of pollen from an anther to a stigma of the same kind of plant. The wind, water or an animal carries the pollen. *e.g.* A bee who visits one pumpkin flower and then another carries pollen from anther to stigma.
- **Self-pollination**: Pollination in which the pollen comes from the same flower, or from another flower on the same plant. *e.g.* A pea flower pollinates itself inside the closed bud, before the flower opens.
- **Cross-pollination**: Pollination in which the pollen comes from a different plant of the same kind. *e.g.* A female papaya tree can make fruit with seeds only if pollen comes from another papaya tree.
- **Pollinator**: An animal who carries pollen from flower to flower. Bees, flies, butterflies, moths, beetles, birds and bats are pollinators. *e.g.* Sunbirds pollinate the red flowers of the silk cotton tree. Moths pollinate the white tuberose at night.
- **Pollen tube**: A very thin tube that a pollen grain grows after landing on a stigma. The tube grows down the style to an ovule and carries the male cells there. *e.g.* In maize, a pollen tube grows down a silk that can be 20 centimetres long, in about one day.
- **Fertilisation**: The joining of a male cell from a pollen grain with the egg cell inside an ovule. A seed begins only after fertilisation. *e.g.* Pollination puts pollen on a bhindi's stigma. Fertilisation happens later, deep inside the ovary.
- **Embryo**: The young plant inside a seed. The embryo grows from the fertilised egg cell. *e.g.* Split a soaked chana and you find the embryo as a tiny root and shoot between the two halves.
- **Fruit**: The part of a plant that grows from the ovary of a flower and holds the seeds. *e.g.* A mango, a bhindi, a pea pod, a tomato and a grain of paddy are all fruits.

## Explanations
### The four rings of a flower · *defined*
**Definition:** A complete flower has four rings of parts, one inside the other. From the outside in, they are the sepals, the petals, the stamens and the carpels.

Look at a flower from the outside in. The outer ring is the sepals. They are usually green, and they cover the bud before the flower opens. The second ring is the petals. Petals are often large and coloured, and many have a scent. They help animal visitors find the flower. The third ring is the stamens, which are the male parts. Each stamen makes pollen. In the middle are the carpels, which are the female part. The carpels hold the ovules, and the ovules can become seeds. The number of parts in each ring is often the same, or one number is a multiple of the other. A hibiscus has 5 sepals, 5 petals and 5 carpels. A mustard flower has 4 sepals, 4 petals and 6 stamens. Not every flower has all four rings. A paddy flower has no petals. A pumpkin vine makes some flowers with only stamens and other flowers with only carpels.
- e.g. A hibiscus has 5 sepals and 5 petals. It has more than 50 stamens joined in a tube, and 5 carpels joined together.
- e.g. A mustard flower has 4 sepals and 4 yellow petals, which make a cross. It has 6 stamens and 1 long, thin ovary.
- e.g. A tuberose, which is called rajnigandha or sampangi, has 6 white parts that look like petals. It has 6 stamens and 3 carpels joined together.
### The stamen: where pollen is made · *defined*
**Definition:** A stamen is the male part of a flower. A stamen has a stalk called the filament, and a head called the anther. The anther makes the pollen grains.

The anther is a small bag with two halves. Inside, thousands of pollen grains form. When the pollen is ripe, the anther dries and splits open. The filament holds the anther in the right place. In a hibiscus, the filaments are joined into a long tube. The anthers stand out from the tube like small yellow buttons. In a grass flower, the filaments are long and thin, so the anthers hang out and shake in the wind. A pollen grain is alive. Each grain has a tough outer wall, and carries male cells inside. Pollen grains are very small. A maize pollen grain is about one tenth of a millimetre wide, and most pollen grains are smaller. Each kind of plant has pollen with a different shape and pattern. Under a microscope, a scientist can name a plant from one pollen grain. The wall is so tough that pollen grains in old mud can last for thousands of years.
- e.g. One maize tassel sheds millions of pollen grains over several days.
- e.g. Pumpkin pollen grains are large, sticky and spiky, and they cling to the hairs of a bee.
- e.g. Scientists read pollen grains in old lake mud to find out which plants grew there long ago.
### The carpel: stigma, style, ovary and ovules · *defined*
**Definition:** A carpel is the female part of a flower. The stigma catches pollen, the style joins the stigma to the ovary, and the ovary holds the ovules.

The stigma is at the top, where pollen can reach it. A stigma is sticky, velvety or feathery, so pollen grains stay on. The style is a stalk that holds the stigma up. At the bottom is the ovary, a closed chamber. Inside the ovary are the ovules. An ovule is a tiny grain on a short stalk, and each ovule holds one egg cell. A pea flower has one carpel with about 6 to 10 ovules in a row. In many flowers, several carpels are joined side by side into one female part. Then you can often count them. A hibiscus has 5 stigmas at the tip, and the ovary has 5 chambers. So the female part of a hibiscus is 5 carpels joined together. The chambers stay when the ovary grows into a fruit. That is why a bhindi, cut across, shows 5 chambers or more, and a cucumber shows 3. The ovules are sealed inside the ovary. Nothing from outside touches them, except a pollen tube.
- e.g. Pea, bean and groundnut flowers have 1 carpel. The pod is that one carpel grown big.
- e.g. Bhindi belongs to the hibiscus family. A bhindi cut across usually shows 5 chambers.
- e.g. Each segment of an orange grew from one carpel of the orange flower.
### Pollination: how pollen reaches a stigma · *defined*
**Definition:** Pollination is the carrying of pollen from an anther to a stigma of the same kind of plant. In self-pollination the pollen comes from the same plant. In cross-pollination the pollen comes from a different plant.

A plant cannot walk to another plant, so something must carry the pollen. In most kinds of flowering plants, an animal carries the pollen. Nearly 9 out of 10 kinds are pollinated by animals: bees, flies, beetles, butterflies, moths, birds and bats. The animal comes for food, which is nectar or the pollen itself. Pollen sticks to the animal's body and rubs off on the stigma of the next flower. In other plants, the wind carries the pollen. Paddy, wheat, maize, ragi, bamboo and all the grasses are wind-pollinated. A few water plants let pollen float on the water. Some plants pollinate themselves. A pea flower sheds pollen on its own stigma inside the closed bud. Self-pollination is sure, because no carrier is needed. Cross-pollination mixes two different parents, so the young plants differ more from each other. Some of them may then live through a disease or a dry year that kills the others.
- e.g. Mango flowers are pollinated mostly by flies and small bees.
- e.g. Tape grass, a pond plant, lets the male flowers float across the water to the female flowers.
- e.g. Pea, groundnut, paddy, wheat and tomato plants mostly pollinate themselves.
### Wind flowers and visitor flowers look different · *defined*
**Definition:** A flower pollinated by animals has features that bring visitors and put pollen on them. A flower pollinated by wind has features that release pollen into the air and catch it from the air.

You can often tell how a flower is pollinated by looking. A flower pollinated by insects usually has bright or white petals, a scent and nectar. The pollen is sticky or spiky, and there is not very much of it. The stigma is sticky and sits where the visitor's body will touch it. A flower pollinated by birds is often red or orange, with a lot of thin nectar and little scent. A flower pollinated by moths or bats is often white or pale. It opens in the evening and smells strongest at night. A flower pollinated by wind has no need of visitors. The petals are tiny or missing, and there is no scent and no nectar. The anthers hang out of the flower on long filaments. The pollen is dry, smooth and light, and there is a huge amount of it. The stigmas are long and feathery, and they stand out in the moving air. Colour, scent and nectar all cost the plant food. A wind flower spends that food on extra pollen.
- e.g. A paddy flower has no petals, anthers that hang out of the flower, and feathery stigmas. The wind pollinates it.
- e.g. The red silk cotton tree has large red cups full of nectar and very little scent. Birds such as mynas and sunbirds pollinate it.
- e.g. A tuberose is white, opens in the evening and has a strong scent at night. Moths pollinate it.
### Fertilisation: the pollen tube and the joining of two cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Fertilisation is the joining of a male cell from a pollen grain with the egg cell inside an ovule. A pollen tube carries the male cell from the stigma to the ovule.

Pollination ends when a pollen grain lands on a stigma. The grain is still on the outside of the carpel. The egg cell is inside an ovule, deep in the ovary. The pollen grain takes in water and sugar from the stigma. Then the grain grows a tube, much thinner than a hair. The tube pushes down between the cells of the style and into the ovary. The tip of the tube enters one ovule through a tiny opening. The tube carries two male cells. One male cell joins the egg cell, and this joining is fertilisation. The fertilised egg cell now has instructions from two parents. This cell divides again and again and grows into the embryo, the young plant in the seed. The second male cell joins another cell in the ovule. In rice, wheat, maize and coconut, that second joining grows into the seed's food store. One pollen tube fertilises one ovule. A flower with 8 ovules needs at least 8 pollen grains to fill every place.
- e.g. In maize, a pollen tube grows down a silk that can be 20 centimetres long, in about one day.
- e.g. The white part of a grain of rice and the white flesh of a coconut grew from the second joining.
- e.g. In peas and beans, the young plant takes in that food store early. The food is then kept in two thick seed leaves.
### From flower to fruit: what each part becomes · *defined*
**Definition:** After fertilisation, the ovary grows into the fruit and each fertilised ovule grows into a seed. The petals and stamens have finished their work, and they dry up and fall.

A fruit is a grown ovary. A seed is a grown ovule. The wall of the ovary becomes the wall of the fruit: the skin and flesh of a mango, the green case of a pea pod, the shell of a groundnut. The coat of the ovule becomes the seed coat. Inside, the fertilised egg cell becomes the embryo. The other parts of the flower leave signs that you can find. The petals and stamens usually fall within a few days. The sepals often stay on. They are the green cap of a brinjal and the green star on a tomato. The dry tip at the far end of a pea pod is what is left of the style and stigma. The brown threads at the end of a maize cob are dried styles. To a botanist, anything that grew from an ovary and holds seeds is a fruit. So a tomato, a bhindi, a cucumber, a chilli, a pea pod and a grain of paddy are all fruits.
- e.g. A bhindi flower is yellow and lasts only one day. The ovary left behind grows into the pod that we eat.
- e.g. Thousands of tiny mango flowers open on one branch. Only a few of their ovaries grow into mangoes.
- e.g. In a pea pod, the pod is the wall of the ovary. Each pea is one ovule that grew into a seed.
### One pollen grain for each seed: why fruits have empty places · *defined*
**Definition:** Each ovule is fertilised separately, by one pollen tube from one pollen grain. An ovule that is not fertilised does not grow into a seed. That ovule stays as a tiny empty place in the fruit.

A maize cob shows this rule most clearly. A young cob has several hundred ovules in rows. Each ovule has a style of their own, which is one silk thread. The silks grow out past the tip of the husk and catch pollen from the wind. Where a silk gets a pollen grain, a kernel grows. Where a silk gets none, that place on the cob stays bare. The same thing happens inside a pea pod, though you cannot see the styles. An ovule that no pollen tube reached stays tiny and pale. Sometimes an ovule is fertilised and still stops growing, because the plant does not send that seed enough food. By looking, you cannot always tell which of the two happened. The young seeds also tell the fruit to grow. In 1950, the scientist Jean Nitsch picked the tiny seed-like fruits off one side of young strawberries. The red flesh on that side stopped growing. So a fruit often grows lopsided, or pinched, where seeds are missing.
- e.g. An apple has 5 carpels and up to 10 seeds. An apple with seeds on only one side often grows lopsided.
- e.g. A long bean pod is thin and pinched at a place where a seed did not grow.
- e.g. A maize cob is often bare at the tip, because the silks from the tip come out last.
### How flowers keep out their own pollen, and why some do not · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants have features that stop a flower's own pollen from fertilising the ovules. Then every seed has two different parents. Other plants pollinate themselves, which is sure but mixes nothing new.

A flower with stamens and carpels close together could easily pollinate itself. Many plants prevent this. Some keep the two apart in space. In a hibiscus, the stigmas stand beyond the anthers, at the very tip. A pumpkin vine makes male flowers and female flowers. Papaya and date palm go further: some trees make only male flowers, and others only female flowers. Some plants keep the two apart in time. In a custard apple flower, the stigmas are ready first. The anthers shed pollen only later, when the stigmas of that flower have dried. In many other plants, the stigma tests the pollen. The stigma of a cabbage or radish flower recognises pollen from the same plant, and that pollen grows no tube. The plant senses whose pollen has arrived, and decides which grains may grow. Some scientists call this an automatic response. Self-pollinating plants, such as pea and groundnut, take the other way. They are sure of seeds even when no visitor comes.
- e.g. Farmers pollinate custard apple and date palm flowers by hand, to be sure of fruit.
- e.g. A single cabbage plant flowering alone sets almost no seed, though the flowers are full of pollen.
- e.g. A groundnut flower pollinates itself. Then a stalk grows down and pushes the young ovary into the soil.
### Flowers and fruits that are not what they seem · *defined*
**Definition:** Some things we call one flower are many flowers together. Some things we call a fruit grew from more than an ovary, or have no seeds at all.

A marigold or a sunflower looks like one flower. It is a head of many small flowers packed together. Each 'petal' of a marigold is a whole small flower, with an ovary of their own at the base. A sunflower head can hold more than 1,000 small flowers, and each sunflower seed grew from one of them. The flowers of a banyan, a peepal or a cluster fig are hidden. They line the inside of the young fig, and tiny wasps crawl in to pollinate them. Fruits can mislead too. A banana of the kind we eat grows without fertilisation. Cut one across and you see three parts, with tiny dark specks in the middle. The three parts are the three carpels, and the specks are ovules that never grew. Wild bananas are full of hard seeds. In an apple, the part we eat grew mostly from the base of the flower. The ovary is the core. A pineapple and a jackfruit each grew from a whole bunch of flowers joined together.
- e.g. One marigold from a garland can hold more than 100 small flowers.
- e.g. The cashew 'apple' is a swollen stalk. The real fruit is the grey nut that hangs under it.
- e.g. Farmers grow seedless bananas from side shoots, because the fruits hold no seeds to sow.
### How a pollen tube finds an ovule · *defined*
**Definition:** A pollen tube is guided to an ovule by chemical signals. Two cells beside the egg cell give out small proteins, and the tip of the tube grows towards them.

A pollen tube has a long way to go and no eyes. For more than a hundred years, botanists asked how the tube finds the tiny opening of an ovule. In 2001, Tetsuya Higashiyama and his team in Japan worked with the wishbone flower. The ovules of this flower can be watched under a microscope. Beside each egg cell lie two helper cells. The team destroyed these cells one at a time with a very fine laser beam. When both helper cells were gone, the pollen tubes wandered and did not find the ovule. In 2009, the same group found the signals. They are small proteins, which the scientists named LUREs. Each kind of plant makes a slightly different LURE. So a tube is drawn best to ovules of the same kind of plant. Once one tube has entered an ovule, the signal stops. Then later tubes turn away and look for other ovules. The ovule calls the tube, and the tube senses the call and steers. Some scientists describe this only as chemistry.
- e.g. A pollen tube of the wishbone flower turns towards an ovule from a short distance away.
- e.g. In maize, a pollen tube grows down a long silk in about a day, close to 1 centimetre in an hour.
- e.g. Pollen from another kind of plant usually grows no tube, or the tube stops in the style.
### Pollination runs on sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** Nectar, scent, colour and pollen are all made from the food that the plant's leaves make with sunlight. A plant pays visitors with part of that food.

A plant's leaves use the energy of sunlight to make sugar from water and carbon dioxide. Nectar is sugar dissolved in water, and the plant gives the nectar away. A bee drinks the nectar and uses the energy of the sugar to fly. So the energy that carries pollen from flower to flower came from the Sun, through a leaf. Petals, scent and pollen are also built from that sugar, together with minerals from the soil. This is a cost, and plants spend with care. A flower that has been pollinated often stops making nectar and drops the petals within a day. A wind-pollinated plant spends nothing on nectar or petals. The wind is free, but most of the pollen is lost. So the plant spends the food on making far more pollen. The wind itself is also moved by the Sun, which warms some parts of the Earth more than others.
- e.g. A hibiscus flower lasts one day. Keeping a large petal fresh costs water and food.
- e.g. Honey is nectar that bees have dried and stored. The sugar in nectar was made by leaves, using sunlight. So the energy in a spoon of honey came from the Sun.
- e.g. A paddy flower opens for only a short time, sheds pollen into the air and closes.
### Does a plant choose which pollen fertilises the ovules? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that a stigma can recognise pollen and stop some grains from growing tubes. They disagree about how far a plant goes on choosing among the grains that are allowed.

Often a stigma receives far more pollen grains than the ovary has ovules. The grains may come from several different plants. All their tubes grow down the same style, and only the first few reach an ovule. In some plants, tubes from certain parents win more often than their numbers predict. The plant also decides which young seeds to feed. A mango tree drops most of the small young fruits, and many pods fill only some of their seeds.
- e.g. Pollen from the same plant is stopped on the stigma of a cabbage or a radish flower.
- e.g. When pollen from two parent plants is mixed on one stigma, the seeds do not always come half and half.
- e.g. A plant with too many young fruits drops some of them, often the ones with the fewest seeds.
- **Botanists who study choice by the mother plant:** The style is not an empty pipe. It feeds some tubes and slows others, so the mother plant affects which pollen wins. She also stops feeding some young seeds and keeps others. A plant chooses among fathers and among her young.
- **Botanists who study the race between pollen tubes:** The tubes race, and the fastest win. Some pollen grains simply grow faster tubes. The result looks like a choice by the mother plant. The pollen alone could explain the same result. Good tests must tell the two apart, and that is hard.
- **Scientists who avoid the word 'choose':** Proteins on the pollen fit or do not fit proteins on the stigma. A tube grows where the chemistry allows. On this view, a tube that grows where the chemistry allows is like a key that fits a lock. The word 'choose' should be kept for animals with brains.
### Why are the ovules sealed inside a carpel? · *idea*
Pine trees and their relatives have no carpels. Their ovules lie open on the scales of a cone, and wind-blown pollen lands right beside them. Plants like these lived for more than 150 million years before the first flowers. In a flowering plant, the ovules are shut inside a closed carpel. Pollen must grow a tube to reach them. The oldest fossil flowers found so far are about 130 million years old. Nobody saw how the carpel began, so there are only ideas. One idea is protection. Beetles came to eat pollen and would have eaten open ovules too, so a closed case kept the ovules safe. A second idea is testing. With the ovules sealed in, every pollen grain must pass the stigma and the style. There the plant can stop the wrong ones. A third idea is speed. A pine takes about a year from pollination to fertilisation. A flowering plant often takes a day or two. All three may be partly right.
- e.g. A pine seed cone holds the ovules on open scales, with no ovary round them.
- e.g. Some of the oldest known flower fossils come from rocks in Spain and China, about 125 to 130 million years old.
- e.g. Magnolia flowers, which are of an old kind, are still pollinated by beetles.

## Mostly agreed
- A seed forms only after pollination and then fertilisation. Pollen must reach the stigma, and a male cell must join the egg cell in the ovule. *How we know:* When flowers are covered so that no pollen can reach them, plants that cannot pollinate themselves set no seed. Charles Darwin covered 100 heads of red clover, and they made no seeds at all.
- The ovary of the flower grows into the fruit, and the ovules grow into the seeds. *How we know:* Anyone can watch it on a bhindi, a pea or a pumpkin plant. The same swelling at the base of the flower grows, day by day, into the fruit.
- Each ovule is fertilised by one pollen tube. *How we know:* Under a microscope, one tube is seen to enter each ovule. On a maize cob, each kernel has one silk, and a kernel grows only where the silk received pollen.
- A pollen tube carries two male cells. One joins the egg cell, and the other starts the food store of the seed. *How we know:* Sergei Nawaschin saw both joinings under the microscope in lilies in 1898. Many scientists have seen them since, in many kinds of plants.
- Flowers pollinated by wind and flowers pollinated by animals differ in regular ways: in petals, scent, nectar, pollen and stigmas. *How we know:* Botanists have compared thousands of kinds of flowers with the carriers seen on them. Wind-pollinated grasses all share small dull flowers, hanging anthers and feathery stigmas.
- Nearly 9 out of 10 kinds of flowering plants are pollinated by animals. *How we know:* In 2011, scientists put together studies of plant communities from around the world and counted the share pollinated by animals.

## How we found out
**Giovanni Battista Amici, an Italian microscope maker and scientist (1786 to 1863), Amici first saw a pollen tube in 1822. He followed tubes into the ovules by 1830. In 1846 he showed that the egg cell is in the ovule before the tube arrives..** By 1700, botanists knew that a flower makes no seeds without pollen. But the pollen stays on the stigma, and the seeds form far below, inside the ovary. Many thought that the pollen grain burst and sent down a fine vapour. Giovanni Battista Amici of Modena, in Italy, built some of the best microscopes of his time. In 1822 he was looking at the stigma of a purslane flower. He saw a pollen grain put out a thin tube, which grew down into the stigma. By 1830 he had followed such tubes all the way down the style and into the ovules. Then a new argument began. In 1837 the famous botanist Matthias Schleiden wrote that the tip of the pollen tube itself becomes the young plant. If so, the ovule was only a place for the young plant to grow. In 1846 Amici studied orchids, whose ovules are small and clear. He showed that an egg cell is already waiting in the ovule before the tube arrives. The young plant grows from that egg cell, and only after the tube has reached it. Other botanists soon saw the same.  
*What people believed before:* First, that pollen sends a vapour down to the ovary. Later, that the tip of the pollen tube becomes the young plant, so that the ovule gives nothing. Amici's microscope showed a real tube, and then an egg cell that was in the ovule before the tube came.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Once a flower is pollinated, a fruit with seeds is sure to grow.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Fruits do follow pollination, and farmers speak of a flower 'setting' once the bees have come.  
  Against: Pollination only puts pollen on the stigma. Each grain must still grow a tube, and a male cell must join an egg cell. Pollen of the wrong kind, great heat or a dry stigma can stop this.
- **A fruit is something sweet that we eat raw.** (The everyday meaning of the word, used in kitchens and markets.; *debated*)  
  For: In cooking, the word is useful. It separates mangoes and bananas from brinjals and beans.  
  Against: To a botanist, a fruit is a grown ovary that holds seeds. A brinjal, a bhindi, a chilli and a pea pod are fruits. Both meanings are in use, and each suits a different job.
- **Flowers are colourful and scented to please people.** (A common belief, and a belief of many writers in the past.; *rejected*)  
  For: People enjoy flowers, and gardeners have bred roses and marigolds for size, colour and scent.  
  Against: Wild flowers had colour and scent for more than a hundred million years before there were people. The colours match what bees, birds and moths can see. Many flowers have patterns that only insects can see.
- **The pollen grain holds the whole young plant, and the ovule is only a place for the young plant to grow.** (Matthias Schleiden, a famous German botanist, in 1837.; *rejected*)  
  For: The pollen tube does enter the ovule, and the embryo first appears just where the tube arrives.  
  Against: Giovanni Battista Amici showed in 1846 that the egg cell is already in the ovule before the tube arrives. The embryo grows from that egg cell after a male cell joins it.
- **The mother plant chooses which pollen grains become the fathers of her seeds.** (Some botanists who study pollination.; *debated*)  
  For: Stigmas stop pollen from the same plant. In some plants, pollen from certain parents wins more often than chance predicts. Plants also drop some young fruits and keep others.  
  Against: The same results could come from some pollen grains simply growing faster tubes. Tests that separate the two are hard to do, and the results differ from plant to plant.

## Roots
In this unit you took a flower apart, and you counted the seeds and the empty places in 20 pods. Here we ask what those counts show about how a new life begins, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the Empty Places Test, one pod held full seeds and also tiny ovules that never grew. All of them sat in the same ovary, on the same mother plant. Each seed began only when a pollen tube reached that one ovule. So a fruit holds many young, and each of them was begun separately.  
*Habit it breaks:* We count a fruit as one thing, and we say that the plant made a fruit. Inside, the seeds were started one at a time, each by a different pollen grain. Some places in the same fruit were never started at all.  
*Evidence:* In the Empty Places Test, you counted the seeds and the empty places in 20 pods. Most pods have at least one ovule that stayed tiny, beside seeds that grew full. On a maize cob, each kernel has one silk, and a kernel grows only where that silk received pollen. If all your own pods were full, write that down, and look at the tip of a maize cob.

- *who am i:* You also began when two cells joined, one from each parent. Like each pea in a pod, you were begun separately, and nobody else has quite your mix.
- *what is life:* In a flowering plant, a new life begins when a male cell and an egg cell meet. The plant makes many chances for that meeting, and only some of them happen.
- *what is this world:* A fruit on your plate is the result of many meetings: a carrier and a flower, a pollen grain and a stigma, a male cell and an egg cell.

A flower has four rings of parts, and two of the rings make the cells that must meet. The plant cannot bring those cells together alone. The wind, a bee or a bird carries the pollen. Then the pollen grain grows a tube the rest of the way. Each seed is two parents joined into one new plant. Many teachers, in India and elsewhere, have said that every being comes from a meeting of others. Fertilisation is one clear example of this.

- **Tao Te Ching 42:** “道生一，一生二，二生三，三生萬物。” Laozi says that the Tao gives birth to One, and One gives birth to Two. Two gives birth to Three, and Three gives birth to all things. (In fertilisation, two cells from two parents join and make a third being, a new plant. All the cells of a banyan tree come from that one cell. Laozi was writing about the whole world, long before anyone saw a pollen tube. The verse and the science share a pattern, and the science does not prove the verse.)
- **The Jain teaching of anekantavada, many-sidedness, with the old story of the blind men and the elephant:** Each blind man touches one part of the elephant and describes the whole animal by that part. Each is right about what he touched. Jain teachers say that truth has many sides, and one view sees only one of them. (A cook sees a pea pod as one vegetable. A botanist sees one ovary grown big. Your count shows several young, each begun separately, and some places never begun. All three are true of the same pod.)
- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** The sutra describes a net with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel reflects every other jewel, so none can be seen apart from the rest. (A flower's colour fits the eyes of the flower's visitors, and a sunbird's beak fits the flowers she drinks from. Each was shaped over a very long time by the other. This is what science has found. The sutra says the same of all things, which science has not tested.)
- Science (settled): Each ovule is fertilised by a separate pollen tube, so the seeds in one fruit are begun separately.
- Science (settled): When pollen from several plants lands on one stigma, the seeds in one fruit can have different fathers.
- Science (debated): Whether the mother plant chooses among the pollen grains, or the fastest tubes simply win, is still argued.
- Other view, Many botanists who study how fruits grow: The mother plant builds the ovary, feeds every seed, and often stops feeding some of them. Without her, no seed grows at all. On this view a fruit is one piece of the mother plant's work. The seeds become separate beings only when they leave her.
- Practice, One pod, slowly (3 min): Hold a closed pod flat on your palm. A bhindi will also do. Close your eyes and run one finger slowly along the pod. Feel each bump. Each bump is one seed, and each seed began with one pollen grain. Take one slow breath for each bump. Then open your eyes and open the pod. Count the seeds, and look for a place where no seed grew.
- Ask: A pod has five peas and two empty places. Were there seven beginnings in that pod, or five?
- Ask: You also began when two cells joined. What can a new being get from two parents that one parent alone cannot give?
- Ask: The pea plant fed some of the young seeds and stopped feeding others. Does the pod belong to the mother plant, or to the seeds?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A pea pod holds five peas. Is the pod one fruit, or five young plants with a shared case? (hint: Think about what grew from the mother plant's ovary, and what began at each fertilisation.)
- *deep:* Every seed has two parents. Why is having two parents worth the trouble of moving pollen? (hint: Think about a field where every plant is exactly the same, and a new disease arrives.)
- *deep:* A flower's colour is made by the plant, but the colour suits the eyes of a bee. Did the plant decide that colour, or did bees help shape it over a very long time? (hint: Think about red flowers in a place with no birds, or scented flowers in a place with no insects.)
- *deep:* A stigma lets some pollen grow and stops other pollen. Is that choosing? (hint: Think about what you mean when you say that you choose. Then ask which parts of that the stigma does.)
- *big:* Most of the food on your plate began with a pollen grain landing. Who should look after the carriers of pollen? (hint: Think about farmers, city families with balconies, and people who make and sell insect sprays.)
- *big:* Farmers grow bananas that make no seeds. Is it fair to the plant to grow fruit that can never become new plants by seed? (hint: Think about what the banana plant gets from farmers, and what the plant loses.)

## Story: One Thread Each
*Kavana, Ajji, Rudrappa and Sudha Atte are made up, and so are their fields. These things are real. Davanagere district in Karnataka grows a great deal of maize, sown with the June rains. A maize plant has the male flowers in the tassel at the top and the female flowers on the cob below. Each silk thread is the style of one ovule, and a kernel grows only if that silk receives a pollen grain. The wind carries maize pollen. So maize planted in one thin line is often poorly filled, and maize planted in a block fills well. Growers do pollinate small plots by hand, by shaking tassels over the silks.*

Kavana's grandmother grew maize outside Honnali, on two acres of black soil, and she sowed her field the way she had always sown it. The whole field was one great square, row behind row, so close that the leaves of one plant lay on the shoulders of the next.

That June, Ajji counted sixty seeds into Kavana's hand.

"These are yours," she said. "Sow them where you like. What they give, you sell."

Kavana had thought about it for a long time. She had seen that the plants in the middle of Ajji's square stood in each other's shade. The plants at the edge had sun on one whole side, and they were always the thickest. So she did not plant a square. She sowed all sixty seeds in a single line along the bund beside the road, where every plant would have sun on both sides and nobody's leaves on a neighbour.

It worked. By August her line was the tallest maize in the village. People slowed their cycles to look at it. At the top of each plant a tassel opened like a small brown broom, and in the mornings, when the wind came across the bund, yellow dust smoked off the tassels and blew away over the road. Kavana liked to watch it go.

Lower down, each plant put out a cob wrapped in green leaves. From the tip of each cob hung a tuft of pale threads, wet and shining, as fine as hair.

In October they picked. Ajji's cobs came first. Kavana pulled the leaves off one, and the kernels sat in tight rows from the bottom to the very tip, like a mouth with every tooth.

Then she opened the first cob from her own line.

There was a ring of kernels at the bottom. Above that there were a few here and a few there. The rest was bare white cob.

She opened ten more. The best of them was half full.

Everybody had a reason. Rudrappa from the next field said the bund was too dry, so near the road. Sudha Atte said the soil of a bund is always poor. Kavana's cousin said that birds had been at them.

Kavana listened to each of them, and then she went and looked.

A thirsty plant does not grow taller than a watered one, and hers were the tallest. A hungry plant is pale, and hers were dark green to the last leaf. And nothing had eaten her cobs. The leaves around them were whole. The bare places were not chewed. They were smooth, and when she held the cob close she could see that they were not empty either. They were covered with rows of tiny flat white dots, each one sitting exactly where a kernel should have been.

So the places had been there. They had just never grown.

She sat down on the step with one of Ajji's cobs and one of her own, both still in their leaves, and this time she took the leaves off slowly, one at a time. Under the last leaf, the dried brown threads lay flat along the cob, hundreds of them, all running the same way.

She took hold of one thread near the tip and followed it down with her fingernail. It ran between two rows of kernels and ended at a kernel. She tried another. It ended at a different kernel. She tried six more on Ajji's cob. Every thread went to one kernel, and no kernel had two.

Then she did the same on her own cob, over a bare patch. The threads were there too. Each one ended at a flat white dot.

Every place had a thread, then, whether it had grown or not. And every thread ran all the way up and out of the top, into the open air.

"What were you waiting for out there?" she said to the cob.

And she remembered the yellow dust.

She ran to the field. "Ajji! When the dust blows off the tops, where does it land?"

"On everything," said Ajji. "On my head. In August you cannot walk through the middle of the field without turning yellow."

"In the middle of your field. Not on my bund. On my bund the wind crosses the line in one step and takes the dust over the road." Kavana held up the two cobs. "Each thread goes to one grain. I think each thread has to catch a speck of the dust before its grain will grow. Your threads stood in a yellow cloud. Mine caught what they could."

Ajji turned the bare cob over in her hands. "My mother used to say that maize must stand shoulder to shoulder, or the cobs come up toothless," she said. "She never told me why. I do not think she knew."

"I can show you why," said Kavana.

At the far end of her line stood twelve plants she had sown three weeks late, after squirrels dug up the first seeds. Their tassels had only just opened, and their threads were fresh and pale.

She tied a red rag to six of them. Every morning for a week, before the wind got up, she bent each of the six tassels over a steel plate and tapped it until the plate was dusted yellow. Then she tipped the plate over the threads of that plant's cob and turned the cob so that the dust fell on every side. The other six plants she left to the wind.

Three weeks later the cobs were fat enough to roast. She carried all twelve to the step, and Ajji came to watch.

The six that had been left to the wind were like the others, a ring at the bottom and bare cob above.

The six with the red rags were full to the tip. All but one, which had a bare stripe down one side.

"That is where my hand missed," said Kavana. "The dust never reached those threads."

Ajji counted the rows on a full cob and the grains in a row, moving her lips. "More than five hundred," she said.

"Five hundred threads," said Kavana. "So five hundred specks of dust have to find them, one each. Next June I am not leaving that to the road."

Next June she drew her plot in the dust with a stick before she sowed it: eight rows of eight, shoulder to shoulder.

"That is sixty-four," said Ajji. "I gave you sixty."

"Then I need four more," said Kavana, and held out her hand.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavana gives each plant more sun by sowing a single line, and the plants do grow well. But each kernel on a cob has a thread of its own, the silk. A kernel grows only if that thread catches a pollen grain. In a block of maize the pollen falls on the neighbours. In a single line the wind carries the pollen away. Her test with six dusted cobs and six left to the wind shows it.

- Ask: Kavana's plants were the tallest in the village. Why did tall, healthy plants still give bare cobs?
- Ask: The bare places on the cob had tiny white dots, and each dot had a thread. What does that tell you about those places?
- Ask: Why does maize in a square block fill better than maize in one long line?
- Ask: Kavana dusted six cobs and left six to the wind. Why did she need the six that she left alone?
- Ask: One dusted cob had a bare stripe down one side. What does that stripe show about how many pollen grains a cob needs?

### e1 · The Empty Places Test: does every ovule in a pod become a seed? (40 min)
- **question:** Does every ovule in a pod grow into a seed? If not, what share of the ovules became seeds, and where in the pod are the empty places?

- **question:** When pollen falls by chance, how many grains out of 100 land on a small stigma? How many land on a stigma three times as wide?
- **predict:** The wide bowl is three times as wide as the katori. Guess how many of 100 seeds will land in the katori, and how many in the wide bowl. Why do you think so? Write both guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** Both catchers stand in the same rain, at the same distance from the centre mark. The person who drops the seeds keeps their eyes shut, so they cannot aim. The catchers swap sides after each run. Chance decides where each seed falls, so you do three runs and add them up.
- **record:** Fill in one row for each of the three runs. Add up each column. Then write how many times as many seeds the wide bowl caught as the katori. Last, do the visitor run and write its result under the table.
- **results table:** Run | Seeds dropped | Seeds in the katori | Seeds in the wide bowl
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the sheet on the floor and put a coin in the middle. 2. Line the katori and the wide bowl with cloth. Put each one 30 centimetres from the coin, on opposite sides. 3. Measure how wide each catcher is. 4. Ask for the two guesses and the reasons, and write them.  
  *Parent says:* The moong seeds are pollen grains in the wind. The katori is a small stigma. The bowl is a stigma three times as wide. Out of 100 grains, how many will land in each? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The seeds are pollen in the wind. The katori is a small stigma. The bowl is three times as wide. Guess how many seeds land in each. Say why.
  - Ask: The bowl is three times as wide as the katori. How many times as many seeds do you think the bowl will catch? Why? (good: Three times as many, because the bowl is three times as wide.; More than three times, because the bowl is bigger in every direction.; I am not sure. Chance decides where they fall.)
  - Note: Many children guess three times as many. Do not correct this. The count will answer it.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands over the coin and holds the tumbler of 100 seeds at chest height. 2. With eyes shut, they shake the seeds out slowly. They move the tumbler in circles about as wide as a dinner plate. 3. Count the seeds in each catcher and write the numbers. 4. Collect all 100 seeds. Swap the two catchers, and do runs 2 and 3.  
  *Parent says:* Shut your eyes, so that you cannot aim. Shake the seeds out slowly, and move the tumbler in circles. Then we count what each stigma caught.  
  *Child mode:* Stand over the coin. Shut your eyes. Shake the seeds out slowly in circles. Count the seeds in each catcher. Do this three times.
  - Ask: Why must your eyes be shut? (good: So that I cannot aim at a catcher.; The wind does not aim, so I should not aim.)
  - Check: In the Pollen Rain Test, why do the katori and the wide bowl swap sides after each run? → **So one side cannot favour one catcher**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now be a visitor. Pick up 10 seeds in your fingers. 2. Walk to the katori and put the seeds in. 3. Write how many of the 10 arrived.  
  *Parent says:* A bee does not drop pollen from the sky. She carries pollen from flower to flower. Carry 10 seeds straight to the katori. How many arrive?  
  *Child mode:* Now you are a bee. Pick up 10 seeds. Carry them to the katori. How many arrived?
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up each column of the table. 2. Divide the wide bowl's total by the katori's total. 3. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 4. Then read the note below together.  
  *Parent says:* Add up the three runs. How many times as many seeds did the wide bowl catch? Now say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Add up the three runs. Compare the bowl with the katori. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Out of 300 seeds dropped, how many missed both catchers? (good: Most of them.; More than 250.)
  - Ask: The visitor carried 10 seeds and the rain dropped 300. Which put more seeds in the katori? (good: The visitor, with far fewer seeds.; The visitor put in 10. The rain put in only a few.)
  - Note: The katori usually catches 0 to 4 seeds in a run, and the wide bowl about 5 to 25. The numbers jump about, because the katori's count is so small. Over three runs the wide bowl usually catches many more than three times as many. A bowl three times as wide covers nine times as much floor, because 3 × 3 = 9. If the katori caught nothing at all, write that the bowl caught all the seeds that were caught. The reason for the waste: wind-blown pollen lands by chance, and a small stigma covers very little space. So wind flowers make huge amounts of pollen, and their stigmas are long and feathery to cover more space. An animal visitor delivers pollen straight to the stigma, so a visitor flower needs far less pollen.
  - Think: Say a maize cob has 500 silks. If only 1 pollen grain in 100 lands on a silk, how many grains must fall near that cob?

### a3 · The Hidden Journey: draw how a pollen grain reaches an ovule (20 min)

### Rings, Pollen and Fruit (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (order) Put the four rings of a flower in order, from the outside in.  
   **Answer:** ['Sepals', 'Petals', 'Stamens', 'Carpels']. *Why:* The sepals are outside and covered the bud. Then come the petals, then the stamens, and the carpels are in the middle. A strong answer says what each ring does.
2. (match) Match each part of a flower to its job.  
   **Answer:** Sepal → covers the bud before the flower opens; Petal → helps visitors find the flower; Anther → makes pollen; Stigma → catches pollen; Ovary → holds the ovules and grows into the fruit; Ovule → holds an egg cell and can grow into a seed. *Why:* Each part has one main job. A strong answer can say why the ovary and the ovule are different: the ovary is the chamber, and the ovules are the grains inside.
3. (mcq) Which two parts make up a stamen?  
   Options: Anther and filament / Stigma and style / Ovary and ovule / Sepal and petal  
   **Answer:** Anther and filament. *Why:* A stamen is a filament, which is the stalk, with an anther on top. The stigma and style belong to the carpel. Choosing them means the male part and the female part are mixed up.
4. (fill) The stigma, the style and the ovary together make one ____.  
   **Answer:** ['carpel', 'pistil']. *Why:* A carpel is the female part of a flower. The stigma catches pollen, the style joins the stigma to the ovary, and the ovary holds the ovules.
5. (truefalse) True or false: pollination and fertilisation are two names for the same event.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Pollination carries pollen to the stigma. Fertilisation comes later, when a male cell joins an egg cell inside an ovule. A strong answer says where each one happens.
6. (label) Draw a flower cut in half. Label: sepal, petal, anther, filament, stigma, style, ovary, ovule.  
   **Answer:** ['Sepal: outer ring, at the base', 'Petal: second ring', 'Anther: the head of a stamen', 'Filament: the stalk of a stamen', 'Stigma: the top of the carpel', 'Style: the stalk of the carpel', 'Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel', 'Ovule: a small grain inside the ovary']. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the stamens around the carpel. It puts the ovules inside the ovary, not on the petals or in the anthers.
7. (mcq) What is pollination?  
   Options: Pollen carried from an anther to a stigma / A male cell joining an egg cell / A seed starting to grow / A ripe fruit falling off  
   **Answer:** Pollen carried from an anther to a stigma. *Why:* Pollination is the journey of pollen from an anther to a stigma of the same kind of plant. A male cell joining an egg cell is fertilisation, which comes afterwards.
8. (sort) Sort these features: do they belong to a wind flower or an animal-visited flower?  
   **Answer:** bright petals → Animal-visited flower; feathery stigma → Wind flower; nectar → Animal-visited flower; no petals and no scent → Wind flower; anthers hanging outside the flower → Wind flower; sticky, spiky pollen → Animal-visited flower. *Why:* Animal-visited flowers spend food on being found and on paying the visitor. Wind flowers spend food on extra pollen and on stigmas that reach into the air. A strong answer gives the reason for one feature in each group.
9. (write) A pollen grain is on a stigma. Explain how a male cell gets from there to an ovule.  
   **Answer:** The pollen grain grows a thin pollen tube. The tube grows down through the style and into the ovary, and enters one ovule. A male cell travels down the tube and joins the egg cell.. *Why:* A strong answer names the pollen tube and says that the tube grows through the style to one ovule. It ends with the male cell joining the egg cell.
10. (odd) Three of these are parts of a carpel. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Stigma / Style / Ovary / Anther  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The stigma, style and ovary make up a carpel. The anther is part of a stamen, the male part.
11. (truefalse) True or false: the ovary of a flower grows into the fruit.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. After fertilisation the ovary grows into the fruit, and the ovules inside grow into the seeds. A strong answer gives an example, such as a bhindi or a pea pod.
12. (mcq) A pea pod holds 7 full peas and 1 empty place. How many ovules grew into seeds?  
   Options: 7 / 8 / 1 / 0  
   **Answer:** 7. *Why:* Seven ovules grew into seeds. The pod began with 8 ovules, and 1 stayed tiny. Choosing 8 counts the ovule that never grew.
13. (write) Why does a wind-pollinated flower make far more pollen than a bee-pollinated flower?  
   **Answer:** Wind-blown pollen lands by chance, and most of it never reaches a stigma. A bee carries pollen straight from flower to flower. So a wind flower must make a huge amount for a few grains to arrive.. *Why:* A strong answer uses evidence, such as the Pollen Rain Test, where only a few seeds out of 300 landed in the small katori.
14. (fill) Pollination with pollen from a different plant of the same kind is called ____-pollination.  
   **Answer:** ['cross']. *Why:* In cross-pollination the pollen comes from another plant, so the seed has two different parents. In self-pollination the pollen comes from the same plant.
15. (mcq) Which of these is a fruit to a botanist?  
   Options: Bhindi / Potato / Carrot / Cabbage leaf  
   **Answer:** Bhindi. *Why:* A bhindi grew from the ovary of a flower and holds seeds, so it is a fruit. A potato is a stem, a carrot is a root, and cabbage is leaves.
16. (draw) Draw an open pea pod with 5 seeds and 2 empty places. Label one of each, and the sepals.  
   **Answer:** The pod is drawn open, with 5 full seeds and 2 tiny grains on short stalks along the seam. The sepals are the green cap at the stalk end.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows every seed and every empty place on a stalk, because each began as an ovule.

5. (mcq) A flower is red, with a lot of thin nectar and almost no scent. Who most likely pollinates it?

   **Answer:** ['Pollen leaves the anther', 'Pollen lands on a stigma', 'A pollen tube grows down the style', 'A male cell joins the egg cell', 'The ovule grows into a seed and the ovary into a fruit']. *Why:* Pollination comes first, then the tube, then fertilisation, and only then the seed and the fruit. 7. (truefalse) True or false: a marigold is a single flower with many petals.

   Options: Styles / Roots / Petals / Stamens  
   **Answer:** Styles. *Why:* Each thread is a silk, which is the style of one ovule. Pollen lands on the silk, and a pollen tube grows down it to the ovule.
9. (write) A bowl is 3 times as wide as a katori. Why does it catch about 9 times as many seeds?  
   **Answer:** A bowl 3 times as wide is also 3 times as long. So it covers 3 × 3 = 9 times as much floor. Seeds fall over the whole floor, so the bowl catches about 9 times as many.. *Why:* A strong answer says that catching depends on the area covered, and shows 3 × 3 = 9.

   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Paddy, maize and doob grass have small dull flowers with hanging anthers, and the wind carries their pollen. Pumpkin flowers are large and yellow, with nectar, and bees pollinate them.

1. (mcq) Which part of a flower makes pollen?

   **Answer:** The anther. *Why:* The anther is the head of a stamen, and pollen forms inside it. The stigma catches pollen but does not make it.

   Options: Stigma, style and ovary / Anther, filament and pollen / Sepal, petal and stamen / Ovule, seed and fruit  
   **Answer:** Stigma, style and ovary. *Why:* A carpel has a stigma on top, a style in the middle and an ovary at the base. The anther and filament make up a stamen.
3. (mcq) What is the main job of the sepals?  
   Options: To cover the bud / To make pollen / To catch pollen / To hold the ovules  
   **Answer:** To cover the bud. *Why:* The sepals wrap the bud before the flower opens. Pollen is made by the anthers and caught by the stigma, and the ovary holds the ovules.
4. (mcq) A bee carries pollen from one mango tree to another mango tree. What is this called?  
   Options: Cross-pollination / Self-pollination / Fertilisation / Germination  
   **Answer:** Cross-pollination. *Why:* The pollen comes from a different plant of the same kind, so this is cross-pollination. Fertilisation happens later, inside the ovule.
5. (mcq) A flower has no petals, no scent, hanging anthers and feathery stigmas. How is it pollinated?  
   Options: By the wind / By bees / By birds / By moths  
   **Answer:** By the wind. *Why:* These are the features of a wind flower. It has nothing to bring a visitor, and its anthers and stigmas reach out into the moving air.
6. (mcq) Pollen has landed on a stigma. What must happen before a seed can begin?  
   Options: A male cell reaches an egg cell / The petals change colour / The fruit ripens / The stigma falls off  
   **Answer:** A male cell reaches an egg cell. *Why:* The pollen grain grows a tube down to an ovule, and a male cell joins the egg cell. That joining is fertilisation. Choosing another answer treats pollination as the end of the story.
7. (mcq) Where does fertilisation happen?  
   Options: Inside an ovule / On the stigma / In the anther / On a petal  
   **Answer:** Inside an ovule. *Why:* The egg cell is inside the ovule, in the ovary. The pollen lands on the stigma, but the cells join only when the tube reaches the ovule.
8. (mcq) After fertilisation, what does each ovule grow into?  
   Options: A seed / A fruit / A petal / A new flower  
   **Answer:** A seed. *Why:* Each fertilised ovule grows into a seed. The ovary around the ovules grows into the fruit. Choosing the fruit mixes up the ovule and the ovary.
9. (mcq) The green star on top of a tomato grew from which part of the flower?  
   Options: The sepals / The petals / The stamens / The ovules  
   **Answer:** The sepals. *Why:* The sepals often stay on the fruit after the petals and stamens have fallen. The tomato below them grew from the ovary.
10. (mcq) A bean ovary holds 9 ovules. Pollen tubes reach 6 of them. What will the ripe pod hold?  
   Options: 6 seeds and 3 empty places / 9 seeds / 3 seeds and 6 empty places / 15 seeds  
   **Answer:** 6 seeds and 3 empty places. *Why:* Each ovule needs their own pollen tube. Six were fertilised and grew into seeds. The other three stayed tiny, as empty places.
11. (mcq) A maize cob has a bare patch with no kernels. What is the most likely reason?  
   Options: The silks there caught no pollen / The plant had too much sun / The cob had no ovules there / The kernels there ripened early  
   **Answer:** The silks there caught no pollen. *Why:* Each kernel has one silk, and grows only if that silk catches a pollen grain. The bare patch had ovules and silks, but no pollen reached them.
12. (mcq) Why did the Empty Places Test use 20 pods, and not 1?  
   Options: One pod may be unusual / 20 pods are easier to open / Pods must be counted in twenties / One pod has no seeds  
   **Answer:** One pod may be unusual. *Why:* Pods differ from each other. One pod might be full, or half empty, by chance. Counting 20 shows what usually happens.

## Applications
- Farmers who grow date palms, custard apples and vanilla carry pollen to the flowers by hand, to be sure of fruit.
- Fruit growers keep beehives in apple, mustard and sunflower fields at flowering time, so that more flowers are pollinated.
- Farmers and gardeners sow maize in blocks, not in single lines. Then wind-blown pollen falls on the silks of neighbouring plants.
- Plant breeders make a new variety by putting the pollen of one chosen parent on the stigma of another. Then they cover the flower.
- Seed companies grow two parent lines of maize side by side and remove the tassels from one. Then every seed has the other line as father.
- Weather offices in some cities count the pollen in the air, to warn people whose noses and eyes react to pollen.
- Scientists read pollen grains in old mud to learn which plants grew in a place thousands of years ago.
- Growers of seedless watermelons and bananas use plants whose fruits grow without ripe seeds inside.

## Spotted around us
- The green star of sepals on top of a tomato or a brinjal in the vegetable basket.
- The brown, dried silk threads at the tip of a roasted maize cob from a street seller in the rains.
- A hibiscus in a pot or by a temple, with five velvety stigmas standing beyond the yellow anthers.
- A marigold garland at a festival: every marigold is a head of more than a hundred small flowers.
- Yellow dust on a parked scooter under a flowering tree in spring. The dust is pollen.
- Grass on a pavement or a lawn with tiny purple anthers hanging out of the flower heads.
- A pod of peas or beans with one pinched, thin place where a seed did not grow.

## Open questions
- **How does a mother plant decide which young seeds to feed?** Plants drop many young fruits and stop feeding some seeds. Scientists know some of the signals, but they cannot yet predict which seed a plant will keep.
- **How do the pollen and the stigma of most plants recognise each other?** The proteins that do this are known in a few plant families, such as the cabbage family. For most kinds of plants nobody has found them yet.
- **What did the first flower look like, and who pollinated it?** The oldest fossil flowers are about 130 million years old, and fossils of flowers are rare. Scientists have drawn the first flower from the family tree of living plants, but no fossil of that flower has been found.
- **Can a flower sense that a visitor is near?** In 2019, scientists reported that evening primrose flowers made sweeter nectar within minutes of the sound of a flying bee. Other scientists want to see the test repeated, and nobody knows how many flowers can do this.
- **Can food crops be made to form seeds without fertilisation?** A few wild plants make seeds that are copies of the mother. In 2019, scientists made rice plants do this in a laboratory. Nobody knows yet whether it will work well in farmers' fields.

## Connected maths
- In one Empty Places Test, 20 pods held 160 ovules. 132 of the ovules became seeds. What percent of the ovules became seeds? **82.5 percent, or about 83 percent.**. 132 ÷ 160 = 0.825. As a percent that is 82.5, which is about 83 percent. So about 5 ovules in every 6 became seeds. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Pollen Rain Test, a katori 7 centimetres wide caught 5 seeds. A bowl is 21 centimetres wide. If catching depends on the area covered, about how many seeds would the bowl catch? **About 45 seeds.**. 21 ÷ 7 = 3, so the bowl is 3 times as wide. Area grows in both directions, so the bowl covers 3 × 3 = 9 times the area. 5 × 9 = 45 seeds. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L5`)
- A maize cob has 16 rows with 32 places in each row. 28 of the places are bare. How many kernels grew? What fraction of the places is bare, in its simplest form? **484 kernels. 7 out of 128 places are bare.**. 16 × 32 = 512 places. 512 − 28 = 484 kernels. 28 out of 512 is the same as 7 out of 128, after dividing both by 4. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- A young maize cob has 500 silks. Only 1 pollen grain in every 100 that fall near the cob lands on a silk. How many grains must fall near the cob for every silk to get one? **At least 50,000 grains.**. Each silk needs 1 grain, so 500 grains must land. Only 1 in 100 lands, so 500 × 100 = 50,000 grains must fall. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A maize pollen grain is 0.1 millimetres wide. The pollen tube grows down a silk 20 centimetres long. How many times the grain's own width is that? **2,000 times.**. 20 centimetres is 200 millimetres. 200 ÷ 0.1 = 2,000. If the grain were a marble 1 centimetre wide, the tube would be 20 metres long. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- Darwin counted seeds on clover heads that bees could visit. 100 heads of red clover made 2,700 seeds. 20 heads of white clover made 2,290 seeds. Which clover made more seeds on each head, and about how many times as many? **White clover, about 4 times as many.**. Red clover: 2,700 ÷ 100 = 27 seeds on a head. White clover: 2,290 ÷ 20 = 114.5 seeds on a head. 114.5 ÷ 27 is a little more than 4. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- A pod has 8 ovules. Suppose each ovule has a 3 in 4 chance of becoming a seed. How many seeds would you expect in the pod? Will every such pod hold exactly that many? **6 seeds on average. No, single pods will differ.**. 3 quarters of 8 is 6. That is the average over many pods. One pod may hold 4 and another 8, because chance decides each ovule separately. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Name the four rings of a flower, from the outside in. → Sepals, petals, stamens and carpels.
- Which part of a flower makes pollen? → The anther, which is the head of a stamen.
- What are the three parts of a carpel? → The stigma on top, the style in the middle and the ovary at the base.
- What is the difference between pollination and fertilisation? → Pollination carries pollen to a stigma. Fertilisation joins a male cell and an egg cell inside an ovule.
- How does a male cell get from the stigma to an ovule? → The pollen grain grows a pollen tube down through the style.
- Which part of the flower becomes the fruit, and which becomes a seed? → The ovary becomes the fruit. Each fertilised ovule becomes a seed.
- Name two features of a wind-pollinated flower. → Any two of: no bright petals, no scent, no nectar, hanging anthers, feathery stigmas, a lot of light pollen.
- Why can a pod have an empty place? → Each ovule needs their own pollen tube. An ovule that was not fertilised, or that stopped growing, stays tiny.
