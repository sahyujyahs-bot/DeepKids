# Alike and different: DNA, why sisters and brothers differ, and nature with nurture
`sci.evolution.inheritance.L5`

**Summary.** A sister and a brother have the same mother and the same father. So why are they not the same? In this lesson you read a real gene from your own blood, three letters at a time. You make four paper children from two paper parents and count how much each pair shares. You also grow cloves from two garlic bulbs in a warm place and a cold place, to see what DNA alone does not settle.

**Intro.** *Your mother gave you half of her DNA. She gave your sister or brother half too. If you both got half from the same two parents, why are you two different people?* DNA is the long chemical message inside your cells that holds the instructions for building a body. In earlier units you learned that it is written in four letters. Here you find out how a cell reads those letters, and what one changed letter can do. You find out how much DNA two children of the same parents really share. The answer is not the same for every pair. Then you ask a harder question. If two living beings have exactly the same DNA, will they turn out the same? A garlic bulb, a queen bee and a pair of twins help you find the answer.

## Terms
- **DNA**: DNA is a very long, thin chemical found in almost every cell. It holds the instructions for building and running a living body, written in four chemical letters. *e.g.* If the DNA from one of your cells were pulled out straight, it would be about 2 metres long. It is far too thin to see.
- **Base (DNA letter)**: A base is one of the four chemical units that DNA is written in. We call them by the letters A, T, C and G. *e.g.* In the two strands of DNA, A always sits opposite T, and C always sits opposite G.
- **Gene**: A gene is a stretch of DNA that holds the instructions for one job. For most genes, the job is making one protein. *e.g.* The message for one chain of the red protein in your blood is 444 letters long. You have about 20,000 genes that make proteins.
- **Protein**: A protein is a chain of small units called amino acids, folded into a shape. Proteins do most of the work in a cell. *e.g.* Haemoglobin is the protein that carries oxygen in your blood. Keratin is the protein in your hair and nails.
- **Codon**: A codon is a group of three DNA letters. Each codon stands for one amino acid, or says 'stop'. *e.g.* The codon GAG stands for the amino acid called glutamic acid. The codon TAA means stop.
- **Chromosome**: A chromosome is one very long piece of DNA, wound up with proteins. Human cells have 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs. *e.g.* In each pair, one chromosome came from your mother and one from your father.
- **Genome**: A genome is one full set of the DNA of a living being. A human genome has about 3 billion pairs of letters. *e.g.* You have two genomes in each cell, one from each parent. So each cell holds about 6 billion pairs of letters.
- **Egg cell and sperm cell**: An egg cell from the mother and a sperm cell from the father join to start a baby. Each carries only one chromosome from every pair. *e.g.* A human egg cell has 23 chromosomes and a sperm cell has 23. Together they make 46.
- **Sibling**: A sibling is a sister or a brother. Full siblings have the same mother and the same father. *e.g.* Kavya and her younger brother Arun are siblings. Each of them got half of each parent's DNA, but not the same half.
- **Identical twins**: Identical twins grow from one fertilised egg that split into two in the first days. They start with the same DNA. *e.g.* Identical twins have the same blood group. Their fingerprints are still different.
- **Fraternal twins**: Fraternal twins grow from two different eggs, each joined by a different sperm cell. They are as alike as any two siblings. *e.g.* Fraternal twins can be a girl and a boy. They are born together but can look quite different.
- **Clone**: A clone is a living being with the same DNA as another. Most clones grow from one parent, with no mixing of DNA. *e.g.* The cloves in one garlic bulb are clones of each other. So are all the banana plants grown from one plant's side shoots.
- **Mutation**: A mutation is a change in DNA. Most happen by chance when a cell copies its DNA. *e.g.* A study of 1,548 families in Iceland found about 70 new mutations in each child. Neither parent had them.
- **Version of a gene (allele)**: Many genes come in slightly different versions, which scientists call alleles. You carry two copies of most genes, one from each parent. *e.g.* The blood group gene has versions called A, B and O. A person can carry A from one parent and O from the other.
- **Dominant and recessive**: A dominant version of a gene shows even if you have only one copy. A recessive version shows only if both of your copies are that version. *e.g.* The O blood group version is recessive. A person with one A and one O has blood group A.
- **Trait**: A trait is a feature of a living being that can be seen or measured. *e.g.* Your height, your blood group and the language you speak are three traits. They depend on genes and surroundings in very different ways.
- **Nature and nurture**: Nature means what a living being inherits in their DNA. Nurture means everything that reaches them from their surroundings, from food and warmth to teaching and care. *e.g.* Your blood group is set by nature. Your language is set by nurture. Your height needs both.
- **Heritability**: Heritability is a number for one group in one place. It says how much of the differences between the members go with differences in their DNA. *e.g.* In one large study of siblings, the heritability of height was about 80 in 100. It does not mean 80 per cent of your own height comes from genes.
- **Epigenetic mark**: An epigenetic mark is a small chemical tag on DNA, or on the proteins DNA is wound around. Tags help switch genes on or off without changing the letters. *e.g.* A honey bee larva fed only royal jelly ends up with different tags from her sisters. She becomes a queen.

## Explanations
### DNA: a long message in four letters · *defined*
**Definition:** DNA is a long, thin chemical in almost every cell. It holds the instructions for building and running a body, written in four chemical letters.

DNA is shaped like a ladder that has been twisted. Each rung of the ladder is a pair of bases, which we call letters. There are four of them: A, T, C and G. A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G. The reason is their shapes. Only those pairs fit across the ladder. This pairing is why DNA can be copied. When a cell divides, the ladder opens down the middle. Each half then picks up new partners, an A for every T and a C for every G. The result is two ladders with the same order of letters. One full set of human DNA has about 3 billion pairs of letters. You have two sets, one from each parent. So almost every cell in your body holds about 6 billion pairs. Red blood cells are the main exception, because they lose their DNA as they form. Only a small part of the DNA, less than 2 letters in every 100, is used for making proteins. Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling took the X-ray photograph that showed the twisted shape, in London in May 1952. James Watson and Francis Crick used it to build their model in 1953. Franklin was not asked before her photograph was shown to them.
- e.g. In One Changed Letter, you read the first 30 letters of a real human gene. The whole message for that protein chain is 444 letters long.
- e.g. A mango tree, a house crow and a bacterium all write their DNA in the same four letters.
- e.g. The first complete reading of a human genome, with no gaps, was finished in 2022. It has about 3,055 million pairs of letters.
### From gene to protein: the cell reads three letters at a time · *defined*
**Definition:** A gene is a stretch of DNA with the instructions for one job, usually making one protein. The cell reads a gene in groups of three letters, called codons.

A protein is a chain of small units called amino acids. Living beings use 20 kinds of amino acid. DNA has only four letters, so one letter cannot stand for one amino acid. Two letters give only 4 × 4 = 16 pairs, which is still too few. Three letters give 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 codons, which is more than enough. So several codons stand for the same amino acid, and three codons mean 'stop'. The cell first makes a working copy of the gene. Then a tiny machine called a ribosome reads the copy, codon by codon, and joins the amino acids in that order. The order decides how the chain folds, and the shape decides what the protein can do. Har Gobind Khorana, born in 1922 in the village of Raipur in Punjab, now in Pakistan, worked out much of this code. He built short pieces of DNA and RNA with a known order, and found which amino acids they made. He shared the Nobel Prize in 1968 with Marshall Nirenberg and Robert Holley. Almost all living beings use the same code. That is why bacteria can be given the human gene for insulin, and they read it correctly and make human insulin.
- e.g. In One Changed Letter, the codons ATG, GTG, CAT, CTG stand for the amino acids Met, Val, His and Leu.
- e.g. The codons GAG and GAA both stand for glutamic acid. A change from one to the other alters nothing in the protein.
- e.g. Since the 1980s, most insulin for people with diabetes has been made by bacteria or yeast that carry the human gene.
### One changed letter: the haemoglobin gene · *defined*
**Definition:** A mutation is a change in DNA. A change of a single letter can alter one amino acid in a protein, and sometimes that changes how the whole protein behaves.

Haemoglobin is the red protein that carries oxygen in your blood. The message for one of its two kinds of chain is 444 letters long. A real gene also has stretches in between that the cell cuts out of the working copy, and we do not count those here. In some people the 20th letter of the message is T where most people have A. The seventh codon then reads GTG where it usually reads GAG. So the chain gets the amino acid valine where it usually has glutamic acid. That one change makes haemoglobin molecules stick together in stiff rods after they give up their oxygen. The rods bend the red blood cell into a curved shape like a sickle, which is a curved farm blade. A person who inherits this version from both parents has sickle cell disease. Their bent cells block small blood vessels, which causes pain and tiredness, and they need medical care all their life. A person with one changed copy and one usual copy is called a carrier. A carrier is usually healthy, and is also partly protected against malaria. This is why the changed letter is common in places where malaria was common for a long time. In India it is found most among Adivasi communities, in central India and in the Nilgiri hills. In 1949 the American chemist Linus Pauling and his team showed that the haemoglobin itself is different in this disease. In 1956 and 1957 Vernon Ingram found the single changed amino acid. Many single-letter changes have no effect at all.
- e.g. In One Changed Letter, you change letter 20 from A to T and read the gene again. Only the seventh amino acid changes.
- e.g. Sickle haemoglobin was reported among communities of the Nilgiri hills in 1952, by Hermann Lehmann and Marie Cutbush.
- e.g. In July 2023 India began a national mission to test millions of people for sickle cell disease and to care for those who have it.
### Chromosomes: 46 pieces, in 23 pairs · *defined*
**Definition:** A chromosome is one very long piece of DNA, wound up with proteins. Human cells have 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs. One chromosome of each pair came from each parent.

Your DNA is not one long thread. It is in 46 separate pieces called chromosomes. They come in pairs. The two chromosomes of a pair carry the same genes in the same order, but often in slightly different versions. The X and Y pair is the one exception, and it has a card of its own. One came from your mother in the egg cell, and one from your father in the sperm cell. So you have two copies of almost every gene. Other living beings have other numbers. A pea plant has 14 chromosomes, a rice plant has 24 and a dog has 78. The number says nothing about how clever or complicated a living being is. The Indian muntjac, a small deer of Indian forests, has the fewest of any mammal. The females have 6 and the males have 7. Janaki Ammal, born in 1897 in Thalassery in Kerala, spent her life counting and studying the chromosomes of plants. At the Sugarcane Breeding Institute in Coimbatore she crossed sugarcane with related grasses to breed cane that grew well in India. In 1945 she and Cyril Darlington published the Chromosome Atlas of Cultivated Plants. She was treated badly at Coimbatore as a single woman from a caste that others looked down on. She received the Padma Shri in 1977.
- e.g. In the Sibling Share Test, each paper parent has 6 pairs of chromosomes, to keep the tossing short. Real parents have 23 pairs.
- e.g. A chimpanzee has 48 chromosomes and a human has 46. Two chromosomes joined into one in our ancestors.
- e.g. Modern sugarcane has more than 100 chromosomes, because it carries many sets.
### Why sisters and brothers differ · *defined*
**Definition:** Each child gets one chromosome from every pair of the mother's, and one from every pair of the father's. Which one of each pair they get is chance, so each child gets a different mix.

Your mother has two chromosomes of each kind. One came from her mother and one from her father. When she makes an egg cell, the egg gets only one of each pair, and chance decides which. The same happens when your father makes a sperm cell. So you got exactly half of each parent's chromosomes, and so did your sister or brother. But you did not get the same half. For any one of your mother's pairs, there is one chance in two that you and your sibling got the same chromosome. Over all the pairs, siblings end up sharing about half of the copies they got from their parents. That half is only an average. A study of 4,401 pairs of siblings found that the share ran from about 37 in 100 to about 62 in 100. Three more things add to the mixing. First, before the chromosomes are shared out, the two partners of a pair swap matching pieces. This is called crossing over. So a chromosome you got from your mother is part your grandmother's and part your grandfather's. Second, each child has some new mutations. A study in Iceland counted about 70 in each child. Third, only identical twins start from the same egg and sperm. Fraternal twins start from two eggs and two sperm cells, so they are as alike as any two siblings. Even identical twins are not exact copies. A study of 387 pairs found that the two twins differed by about 5 early mutations on average.
- e.g. In the Sibling Share Test, the made-up paper children share 3, 4, 6, 7, 7 and 9 chromosomes out of 12. The average is 6, which is half.
- e.g. Every paper child got exactly 6 chromosomes from the paper mother. No two children got the same 6.
- e.g. Any two people already match in about 999 of every 1,000 DNA letters. 'Sharing half' is about which parent's copy you got, not about the letters.
### XX or XY: the sperm cell settles it · *defined*
**Definition:** In humans, one pair of chromosomes comes in two forms, called X and Y. Most girls and women have two X chromosomes. Most boys and men have one X and one Y.

A mother has two X chromosomes, so every egg cell she makes carries an X. A father has an X and a Y. Half of his sperm cells carry the X and half carry the Y. If a sperm with an X joins the egg, the baby has XX. If a sperm with a Y joins the egg, the baby has XY. So whether a baby is XX or XY is settled by which sperm cell joins the egg. It is chance, about one in two each time. Nothing the mother eats, does or wishes changes it. In many families a mother has been blamed for having daughters. The science shows that this blame has no basis at all. Nettie Stevens, an American scientist at Bryn Mawr College, found this out in 1905. She studied mealworm beetles under a microscope. She saw that the males made two kinds of sperm, one with a large chromosome and one with a small one. Eggs joined by the small one became males. Edmund Wilson found something similar in the same year. For a long time he was given most of the credit, though her work was the more complete. Bodies are more varied than two patterns. A small share of people have a different set, such as one X alone or XXY. In some people the body develops differently from the usual pattern for their chromosomes.
- e.g. In the X or Y activity, the mother's card is always X. Only the father's coin decides between XX and XY.
- e.g. In birds it is the other way round. The mother's egg carries one of two different chromosomes, so her egg settles the sex of the chick.
- e.g. An olive ridley turtle has no such chromosomes. The warmth of the sand around the egg settles the sex. Warmer nests give more females.
- e.g. In honey bees, an egg that is fertilised becomes a female. An egg that is not fertilised becomes a male.
### Two copies of each gene: dominant and hidden versions · *defined*
**Definition:** You carry two copies of most genes, one from each parent. A dominant version shows with one copy. A recessive version shows only when both copies are that version.

Blood groups show this clearly. One gene decides whether a person's blood is group A, B, AB or O. The gene has three versions: A, B and O. A and B are dominant over O. So a person with one A and one O has group A, and the O is hidden. A person with A and B has group AB, because both of those show. Only a person with two O versions has group O. Now think of a mother with group A and a father with group B. If each carries a hidden O, each can pass on the O. Their child can then be group O, unlike both parents. This is how a feature can skip a generation. Gregor Mendel, a monk in the city of Brno, found these rules with pea plants. He worked from 1856 to 1863 and published in 1866. Most features are not this simple. Height is shaped by many thousands of places in the DNA, each with a very small effect. A study of 5.4 million people in 2022 found more than 12,000 such places. Some school examples are not simple either. Tongue rolling is often taught as one dominant gene. But identical twins sometimes differ in it, and many children learn to do it.
- e.g. Parents with blood groups A and B can have children of group A, B, AB or O, if both carry a hidden O.
- e.g. A carrier of the sickle cell version has one changed copy and one usual copy. The usual copy makes enough normal haemoglobin to keep them well.
- e.g. Mendel crossed tall pea plants with short ones. All the young were tall. Short plants appeared again in the next generation.
### Nature and nurture work together · *defined*
**Definition:** Nature is what a living being inherits in their DNA. Nurture is everything that reaches them from their surroundings. Almost every feature needs both.

DNA does not build a body alone. A gene is used only when the cell reads it, and what the cell reads depends on what is going on around the cell. Food, warmth, light, illness, practice and care all reach the cells. So the same DNA can give different results. A Siamese cat has a gene for dark fur colour whose protein works only where the skin is cool. So the cat is dark on the ears, face, paws and tail, and pale on the warm body. Siamese kittens are born white, because the womb is warm all over. The same surroundings can also give different results with different DNA. This is why 'which matters more?' is often the wrong question. Think of the area of a rectangle. The area is the length times the width. You cannot say how much of the area is 'due to' the length. In the same way, a plant's height comes from the genes working in the surroundings, not from two shares added up. A few features are settled almost fully by one side. Your blood group is set by your genes. Which language you speak is set by your surroundings. Height shows how the two work together. Tall parents tend to have tall children. Yet women in South Korea born in 1996 are about 20 centimetres taller than women born there in 1896. Their genes did not change in a hundred years. Their food and health did.
- e.g. In the Two Bulbs, Two Homes Test, cloves with the same DNA grow differently in a warm place and a cold place.
- e.g. Phenylketonuria is an inherited condition that harms the brain if untreated. A special diet started in the first weeks of life prevents that harm. So 'inherited' does not mean 'cannot be changed'.
- e.g. A hydrangea bush grows blue flowers in acid soil and pink flowers in soil that is not acid.
- e.g. In a trial in 12 schools in Guangzhou in China, one extra outdoor class each day lowered the number of children who became short-sighted.
### How can anyone tell what genes do? Twins and adopted children · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists compare people who share more or less DNA, and people who share more or less of their surroundings. The pattern of who is alike shows what genes and surroundings each do.

Families share genes and homes together, so a feature that runs in a family could come from either. Scientists need cases where the two are pulled apart. Identical twins share almost all their DNA. Fraternal twins share about half of their parents' copies, like other siblings. Both kinds of twins usually grow up in the same home at the same time. So if identical twins are much more alike in a feature than fraternal twins are, genes are making a difference to that feature. If both kinds of twins are equally alike, the shared home is doing it. Adopted children give another test. They share a home with one family and DNA with another. Francis Galton in England first used twins this way in 1875. The method of comparing the two kinds of twins came about fifty years later. From such studies scientists work out heritability. It is easy to misread. It describes the differences in one group, living in one range of surroundings. It says nothing about one person. It also changes when surroundings change. If every child in a town eats well, the differences in height that remain are mostly from genes, so heritability is high. If some children go hungry, more of the differences come from food, so heritability is lower. The genes are the same in both towns.
- e.g. Identical twins always have the same blood group. Fraternal twins often differ. So blood group is set by genes.
- e.g. Twins of both kinds who grow up together speak the same language. So which language you speak is set by surroundings.
- e.g. In The Twin Clue activity, you read made-up twin results and work out which features go more with genes.
### Switches on the DNA: the same letters, read differently · *defined*
**Definition:** Cells put small chemical tags on DNA and on the proteins that DNA is wound around. The tags help decide which genes are read. They are called epigenetic marks.

Almost every cell in your body has the same DNA. Yet a skin cell and a nerve cell read different genes. Part of the way a cell keeps a gene off is by tagging it. A common tag is a small group of atoms called a methyl group, stuck onto the letter C. Tags do not change the letters. They change how easily the gene is read. A cell senses what is around and decides which genes to read, and tags are one of the tools. Some scientists call these automatic chemical responses. Tags are copied when a cell divides, so a skin cell makes more skin cells. Surroundings can change tags. A honey bee queen and a worker grow from the same kind of egg. A larva fed only royal jelly becomes a queen. In 2008, scientists switched off the gene for a tagging protein in young bee larvae. Most of those larvae grew into queens without the special feeding. Identical twins show it too. In 2005 a study of 80 pairs of identical twins, aged 3 to 74, compared their tags. The youngest twins had almost the same pattern. The older twins had patterns that differed more, most of all when they had lived apart.
- e.g. In the story The Queen Nobody Chose, worker bees raise a new queen from an ordinary larva by changing what they feed her.
- e.g. Every cell in a Siamese cat has the gene for dark colour. Only cells in cool skin make the colour.
- e.g. Identical twins look most alike as babies. As adults they often differ more in health.
### Can what happens to a parent be passed on without changing the DNA letters? · *debated*
In egg and sperm cells, and again in the very early embryo, most epigenetic tags are wiped off. So each new life mostly starts with fresh tags. But is the wiping complete? If some tags get through, then hunger or stress in one generation might leave a mark on the next. Scientists agree on plants and some small animals. They disagree about humans and other mammals.
- e.g. In some plants and in tiny roundworms, a change in tags can pass down for several generations.
- e.g. In the winter of 1944 to 1945, people in the west of the Netherlands had very little food. Sixty years later, people conceived in that famine had fewer tags on one growth gene than their own sisters and brothers had.
- **Bastiaan Heijmans, Lambert Lumey and their team, who studied the Dutch famine in 2008:** Hunger around the time a baby is conceived can leave tags that last for the whole of that person's life. So a mother's surroundings can reach her child through more than DNA letters.
- **Edith Heard and Robert Martienssen, in a review in 2014:** The Dutch babies were themselves in the womb during the famine, so they met the hunger directly. That is not the same as inheriting a tag. In mammals, proof that tags pass down through the generations is mostly missing.
- **Scientists who study plants and roundworms:** In these living beings, inheritance of tags and of small RNA signals is well shown. It may be a real, if small, second way of inheriting. More work is needed to learn how far it goes in other animals.
### Is DNA a blueprint, or something the cell uses? · *debated*
People often call DNA the blueprint of life. A blueprint is a full plan of a building, and each part of the plan matches a part of the building. Is DNA like that? Scientists agree that DNA stores the order of amino acids for every protein. They disagree about how to describe its part in the whole living being.
- e.g. No gene matches one finger or one tooth. Genes give the order of amino acids in proteins, and a body forms as cells use them.
- e.g. The same DNA makes a caterpillar and then a butterfly.
- **Erwin Schrödinger, a physicist, in his book What Is Life? in 1944, and many biologists after him:** The chromosomes hold a 'code-script' for the whole future living being. On this view DNA is the plan, and the body carries it out.
- **Barbara McClintock, in her Nobel lecture in 1983:** She found pieces of DNA in maize that move from place to place and switch genes on and off. She called the genome 'a highly sensitive organ of the cell' that senses trouble and reorganises itself. On this view the cell uses and even rewrites the DNA.
- **Denis Noble, a British biologist, in The Music of Life in 2006:** DNA does nothing alone. It is read by the cell, and the cell is shaped by the body and the surroundings. He argues that no one level is in charge, neither the gene nor the cell nor the body.

## Mostly agreed
- DNA carries the inherited instructions of living beings, in a code of four letters read three at a time. *How we know:* In 1944 Oswald Avery's team showed that pure DNA could pass a feature from one kind of bacterium to another. By 1966 the meaning of all 64 codons had been worked out.
- A child gets half of their chromosomes from each parent. *How we know:* Under a microscope, egg and sperm cells each show 23 chromosomes and body cells show 46. DNA tests of parents and children match half and half.
- Children of the same parents get different halves, so siblings share about half of their parents' copies on average, not exactly half. *How we know:* A study of 4,401 pairs of siblings measured the share directly from their DNA. It ran from about 37 to about 62 in every 100, with an average of 50.
- Whether a human baby has XX or XY chromosomes is settled by the sperm cell. *How we know:* Nettie Stevens saw the two kinds of sperm in 1905. Every egg cell a mother makes carries an X.
- The same DNA can give different results in different surroundings. *How we know:* Clones of one plant grow differently in different places. Identical twins differ in fingerprints, in health and in the tags on their DNA.
- Any two humans are alike in about 999 of every 1,000 DNA letters. *How we know:* The genomes of many thousands of people from all over the world have been read and compared, letter by letter.

## How we found out
**Wilhelm Johannsen, a Danish plant scientist, testing an idea of Francis Galton's, 1900 to 1903, with the new words in 1909.** For a long time, many scientists thought that every difference between living beings was passed on to their young. Francis Galton in England measured parents and children, and concluded that size and ability were settled by inheritance. In 1900 Wilhelm Johannsen, a plant scientist in Copenhagen, began a test with beans. Bean flowers pollinate themselves. So all the beans descended from one bean have almost exactly the same genes. He called such a family a pure line. He weighed thousands of beans. Inside one pure line, the beans still differed in weight. He sowed the heaviest and the lightest beans of the same line. Their plants gave beans of the same average weight. So the differences inside a pure line were not inherited. They came from each bean's surroundings as the bean grew. Only the differences between one line and another were passed on. In 1909 he gave science three words: 'gene', 'genotype' for what a living being inherits, and 'phenotype' for what we can see and measure.  
*What people believed before:* That every difference between living beings is inherited, so the biggest parents always have the biggest young. Inside a pure line of beans, the heaviest and the lightest seeds gave young of the same average weight.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A person's abilities are fixed at birth by what they inherit.** (Francis Galton, an English scientist, in his book Hereditary Genius in 1869; *rejected*)  
  For: Talents often run in families. Identical twins are alike in many ways, even in some habits.  
  Against: Families share homes, food and schools as well as genes, and Galton did not separate them. Height, health and school results have changed greatly in a few generations, while genes stayed the same. Galton's idea was later used to justify great cruelty to people who were called unfit.
- **A newborn's mind is a blank page, and upbringing writes everything on it.** (John B. Watson, an American psychologist, in the 1920s; *rejected*)  
  For: Language, beliefs, skills and manners all come from the people around a child. No baby is born speaking Tamil or Bengali.  
  Against: Identical twins are more alike than fraternal twins in many features, even when they grow up in the same home. Adopted children are like their birth parents in height and in some other features.
- **Things that happen to parents can be passed to children without any change in the DNA letters.** (Scientists who study epigenetic marks; *debated*)  
  For: In some plants and roundworms, tags on DNA pass down for several generations. People conceived in the Dutch famine of 1944 to 1945 still carried different tags sixty years later.  
  Against: In mammals, most tags are wiped off in egg and sperm cells and in the early embryo. The Dutch babies met the famine themselves, in the womb. Clear proof in humans is missing.
- **Most of our DNA is junk that does nothing.** (Susumu Ohno, a Japanese-American biologist, who used the words 'junk DNA' in 1972; *debated*)  
  For: Less than 2 letters in every 100 are used to make proteins. An onion has about five times as much DNA as a human, with no sign that an onion needs more instructions.  
  Against: Some of the rest holds switches that turn genes on and off. In 2012 a large project reported some chemical activity on most of the genome. Other scientists replied that activity is not the same as usefulness.
- **You can read a person's future from their DNA.** (Some companies that sell DNA tests, and some popular books; *rejected*)  
  For: For a few conditions caused by one gene, such as sickle cell disease, a DNA test tells a great deal.  
  Against: Most features depend on thousands of places in the DNA, each with a tiny effect, working with the surroundings. For one person, such tests predict height, health or school results only weakly.

## Roots
We have learned how DNA is read, why children of the same parents differ, and how genes work with surroundings. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Sibling Share Test, every chromosome in every paper child was an old one, copied from a grandparent. Yet no two of the four children got the same set. So nothing in a new child is new except the combination. A new person is made by mixing old pieces by chance, and that exact mix has never existed before.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a child 'has her mother's nose', as if features were handed down whole. We also think of a new baby as made from something new. What is handed down is a chance half of each parent's chromosomes. The newness is in the mix, and the mix then meets surroundings that no one else has had.  
*Evidence:* In the made-up results, the four paper children shared 3, 4, 6, 7, 7 and 9 chromosomes out of 12, and no two strips matched. Every box on every strip was red, yellow, blue or green, which are the four grandparents' colours. Two parents with 6 pairs each can have 4,096 different children. Real parents, with 23 pairs, can have many million million.

- *who am i:* You are a mix that never existed before. Half of your mother's DNA and half of your father's came together by chance. Then your food, your home, the people around you and your own choices worked on that mix every day.
- *what is life:* Life keeps its instructions by copying them, and makes new beings by shuffling them. Every living being who has two parents is a new arrangement of very old pieces.
- *what is this world:* A banyan, a bee, a bacterium and you all write in the same four DNA letters and read them with almost the same code. The living world keeps making new beings from what is already there.

Life on Earth began once, as far as anyone can tell. Every DNA molecule alive today is a copy of a copy that goes back to that beginning. Small copying changes and endless shuffling turned that one line into all the kinds of living beings. Your family shows the same thing on a small scale. Two parents have children who are each a different mix, and no mix is repeated. So the many different beings we see all come from one source, and each of them is new.

## Story: The Queen Nobody Chose
*Kaveri, Ajji and their bee boxes are made up. These things are real. Families in Kodagu in Karnataka keep the Indian hive bee in wooden boxes among coffee and pepper, and there is a beekeepers' cooperative at Bhagamandala. A queen bee and a worker bee grow from the same kind of fertilised egg. When a colony loses the queen, the workers pick a few very young larvae from ordinary cells. They feed those larvae only royal jelly, and build long cells around them that look like groundnut shells. A new queen comes out about twelve days later, and she usually kills the other queens still in their cells. She mates in the air about a week after that, and starts laying a few days later. The days in the story follow these real timings. Studies show that the workers pick well-fed young larvae, and do not pick by family.*

Ajji kept nine bee boxes under the silver oaks, between the coffee bushes and the pepper vines. In the April holidays, Kaveri was allowed to help with them for the first time.

On the first morning Ajji lifted a wooden frame out of the third box. The comb was covered with bees, walking over one another. "Find the one who is different," she said.

Kaveri looked for a long time. Then she saw a bee with a long, smooth body, nearly twice the length of the others. A ring of workers faced her, touching her with their feelers and feeding her.

"The queen," Kaveri whispered. "She is so grand."

"She is the mother," said Ajji. "Every bee in this box came from an egg she laid. She lays hundreds in a day."

All that week Kaveri talked about the queen. She was sure the queen had come from a special egg, a royal one, bigger and shinier than the rest. Her cousin in Mysuru had a book of kings and queens. In it a princess was a princess from the day she was born. Ajji listened and said nothing either way.

On the ninth day Kaveri lifted the frame herself. A bee walked onto her wrist, and she jerked her hand. The frame slipped and knocked hard against the side of the box. When the bees cleared, the long, smooth body lay still at the bottom.

Kaveri did not cry until Ajji had closed the lid. Then she could not stop. "I have killed all of them," she said. "Nobody else can lay eggs. There is no royal egg left. The whole box will die."

"Listen," said Ajji. From inside the box came a new sound, a rough, unsettled hum. "They know already. We will not open this box for three days. They will do what needs doing, and they do it better without us looking."

They were the longest three days of the holidays. On the fourth morning Ajji lifted the same frame, very slowly.

The comb looked almost as before. In the middle were the small, flat-topped cells where worker bees grow. But in three places something hung down from the face of the comb. Each was a long, bumpy cell the size and shape of a groundnut shell. Bees crowded over all three.

"Queen cells," said Ajji. "There is a queen growing in each one."

Kaveri stared. "But where did they find royal eggs? The queen is dead."

"Look where the cells are built."

Kaveri looked. The long cells were not in some special corner. They hung in the middle of the ordinary cells. Each one had been built outwards from an ordinary cell, one among thousands. It was a cell where the old queen had laid an ordinary egg, a few days before she died.

"Those were going to be workers," Kaveri said slowly.

"Four days ago they were," said Ajji. "Small grubs, a day or two old, the same as every grub around them."

"Then what changed?"

Ajji pointed with a twig. A nurse bee had her head deep inside one of the long cells. "The nurses make a white food in their heads. We call it royal jelly. Every grub gets some for the first days. After that, a worker grub is given pollen and honey. These three are given the white food and nothing else, as much as they can eat, until the cell is closed."

Kaveri sat back on her heels. She thought of the old queen, so long and grand. Once she had been a grub in a plain cell, with thousands of sisters who were no different from her. She thought of the worker who had walked onto her wrist, and of the old foragers with torn wings at the door of the box. Any one of them could have been the queen. The queen could have been any one of them.

There had never been a royal egg. The eggs were the same. What made a queen was what the grub was fed, and it was the sisters who did the feeding.

"So nobody is born a queen," she said.

"Nobody in a bee box," said Ajji. "The egg can go either way. The others decide which way, by how they feed her."

On the twelfth day after the accident, the end of one long cell was cut open in a neat circle, like the lid of a tin. A new queen was walking on the comb. She was thinner than the old one, and quick. By evening the other two long cells had been torn open from the side. Ajji did not explain that, and Kaveri did not ask.

A week later the new queen flew out over the silver oaks on a warm afternoon and came back. A few days after that, Ajji tilted the frame to the light. At the bottom of each clean cell stood one tiny white egg, like a grain of rice cut very thin.

Kaveri looked at the eggs for a long time. Then she looked at the foragers coming in with yellow pollen on their legs.

"Each of these could still be either," she said. "It has not been settled yet."

**For the parent:** For the parent: this is nature and nurture in one bee box. A queen and a worker look and live so differently that they seem to be two kinds of bee. Yet they grow from the same kind of egg, with the same kind of DNA. The difference is made by feeding. A larva fed only royal jelly reads her genes differently and grows into a queen. So the same inherited instructions can build two very different bodies, and the surroundings settle which one. Kaveri's first idea, that a queen is born royal, is the idea that nature alone decides. The comb showed her otherwise.

- Ask: Why did Kaveri think the box would die when the queen died?
- Ask: What did Kaveri notice about where the long queen cells were built? Why did that matter?
- Ask: The queen and the workers came from the same kind of egg. What made them different?
- Ask: Is a queen bee's long body from her genes, from her food, or from both? Give your reasons.
- Ask: Can you think of something about you that needed both your genes and your surroundings?

## Worksheets
### DNA, Genes and Why Siblings Differ (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which four letters is DNA written in?  
   Options: A, B, C and D / X and Y only / A, T, C and G / 0 and 1  
   **Answer:** A, T, C and G. *Why:* DNA has four bases, called A, T, C and G. X and Y are the names of two chromosomes, not of DNA letters.
2. (fill) A group of three DNA letters that stands for one amino acid is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['codon']. *Why:* The cell reads a gene three letters at a time. Each group of three is a codon.
3. (truefalse) True or false: in DNA, the letter A always pairs with the letter T.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A fits T and C fits G. This pairing is what lets a cell copy DNA exactly.
4. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** DNA → the long chemical that holds the instructions; gene → a stretch of DNA for one job; chromosome → one long piece of DNA, wound up; protein → a folded chain of amino acids; mutation → a change in the DNA letters. *Why:* DNA is the chemical. A gene is one stretch of it. A chromosome is one long wound-up piece. A protein is what most genes make.
5. (mcq) How many chromosomes do most human body cells have?  
   Options: 46 / 23 / 2 / 64  
   **Answer:** 46. *Why:* Body cells have 46, in 23 pairs. Egg and sperm cells have 23. The number 64 is the number of codons.
6. (write) A paper mother has 6 pairs of chromosomes. Explain why two of her children can get different sets from her.  
   **Answer:** Each child gets only one chromosome from each pair. Chance decides which one. The coin can land differently for each child, so each child can get a different six.. *Why:* A strong answer says three things: one from each pair, chosen by chance, separately for every child.
7. (truefalse) True or false: two children of the same parents always share exactly half of their chromosomes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They share half on average. Some pairs of siblings share more and some share less, because chance picks each child's half.
8. (order) Put these in order, from smallest to largest.  
   **Answer:** ['a DNA letter', 'a codon', 'a gene', 'a chromosome', 'a genome']. *Why:* Three letters make a codon. Many codons make a gene. A chromosome holds many genes. A genome is the full set of chromosomes.
9. (mcq) Which cell settles whether a human baby has XX or XY chromosomes?  
   Options: The father's sperm cell / The mother's egg cell / Both, equally / Neither: food settles it  
   **Answer:** The father's sperm cell. *Why:* Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries an X or a Y. So the sperm makes the difference. Choosing 'the mother's egg' is a common mistake that has led to mothers being blamed.
10. (draw) Draw a short piece of DNA as a ladder with six rungs. Write the pair of letters on each rung.  
   **Answer:** A ladder with six rungs. Each rung shows A with T, or C with G.. *Why:* A strong drawing never pairs A with C or G with T. The pairing rule is the key to copying.
11. (sort) Sort these statements: identical twins, or fraternal twins?  
   **Answer:** grow from one fertilised egg → Identical twins; grow from two eggs → Fraternal twins; can be a girl and a boy → Fraternal twins; always have the same blood group → Identical twins; share about half, like other siblings → Fraternal twins; start with the same DNA → Identical twins. *Why:* Identical twins come from one fertilised egg that splits. Fraternal twins come from two eggs and two sperm cells, so they are like any two siblings.
12. (odd) Three of these have the same DNA as each other. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Cloves of one garlic bulb / Banana plants from one plant's side shoots / Identical twins / A sister and her brother  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Garlic cloves, banana side shoots and identical twins all began from one set of DNA. A sister and brother each got a different half from each parent.
13. (mcq) A mutation changes the codon GAG to GAA. Both mean the amino acid Glu. What happens to the protein?  
   Options: One amino acid changes / The chain stops early / Nothing changes / The protein doubles  
   **Answer:** Nothing changes. *Why:* Several codons can stand for the same amino acid. If the new codon means the same thing, the protein is the same. Many mutations are like this.
14. (write) The message for a haemoglobin chain has 444 letters. Explain how one changed letter can cause sickle cell disease.  
   **Answer:** The changed letter turns the codon GAG into GTG. So one amino acid in the chain changes from Glu to Val. That makes the haemoglobin molecules stick together, and the red blood cells bend out of shape.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the chain: one letter, one codon, one amino acid, a different protein, a different cell.
15. (perspective) Two neighbours talk about a family with three daughters and no sons.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with the second neighbour. Every egg carries an X, so the mother's cells cannot make the difference. The sperm carries X or Y by chance. Three the same in a row happens in 1 family in 4.. *Why:* The first neighbour's idea is very old and is wrong. The evidence is in the chromosomes.
### Nature with Nurture (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Cloves from one garlic bulb grow long shoots in a warm place and short shoots in the fridge. Why?  
   Options: The cold changed their DNA letters / The cloves had different parents / Same DNA, different surroundings / Garlic has no DNA  
   **Answer:** Same DNA, different surroundings. *Why:* Cloves of one bulb are clones. The DNA is the same, so the difference comes from the warmth. The cold slows growth. It does not rewrite the letters.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a feature that is inherited can never be changed by surroundings.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Phenylketonuria is inherited, but a special diet from the first weeks prevents the harm. Height is partly inherited and also depends on food.
3. (sort) Sort these features: mostly genes, mostly surroundings, or both?  
   **Answer:** blood group → Mostly genes; the language you speak → Mostly surroundings; height → Both; your accent → Mostly surroundings; red-green colour blindness → Mostly genes; being short-sighted → Both; a scar → Mostly surroundings. *Why:* Blood group and colour blindness are set by genes. Language, accent and scars come from surroundings. Height and short sight need both.
4. (fill) A living being with the same DNA as another, grown from one parent with no mixing, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['clone']. *Why:* Garlic cloves from one bulb are clones. So are plants grown from cuttings of one plant.
5. (write) A Siamese cat is dark only on the ears, face, paws and tail. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The cat has a gene for dark colour whose protein works only where the skin is cool. The ears, face, paws and tail are cooler than the body. So only those parts turn dark.. *Why:* A strong answer names both the gene and the temperature. Neither alone explains the pattern.
6. (mcq) Identical twins always match in a feature. Fraternal twins often differ. What mostly sets this feature?  
   Options: The shared home / Genes / Chance alone / School  
   **Answer:** Genes. *Why:* Both kinds of twins share a home. Only identical twins share all their DNA. So if only they always match, genes are setting the feature.
7. (mcq) Identical twins and fraternal twins match equally often in a feature. What mostly sets this feature?  
   Options: Genes / Something the twins share at home / The Y chromosome / Mutations  
   **Answer:** Something the twins share at home. *Why:* If more shared DNA brings no more likeness, genes are not making the difference. What both kinds of twins share is their home.
8. (truefalse) True or false: a queen bee and a worker bee grow from the same kind of egg.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both grow from fertilised eggs. A larva fed only royal jelly becomes a queen.
9. (match) Match each living being to what their surroundings settle.  
   **Answer:** a honey bee larva → queen or worker; an olive ridley turtle egg → female or male; a hydrangea bush → blue or pink flowers; a Siamese cat → which parts of the fur are dark; a flamingo → how pink the feathers are. *Why:* In each case the DNA allows more than one result, and something outside settles which one happens.
10. (write) South Korean women born in 1996 are about 20 centimetres taller than those born in 1896. Why?  
   **Answer:** Genes cannot change that much in a hundred years. The surroundings changed: more and better food, cleaner water and less illness in childhood. So the same kinds of genes built taller bodies.. *Why:* A strong answer rules out genes first, then names something in the surroundings that affects growth.
11. (draw) Draw a rectangle. Label the length 'genes' and the width 'surroundings'. Then draw it again with the width doubled.  
   **Answer:** Two rectangles. The second is twice as wide and has twice the area.. *Why:* The area needs both sides. Changing either one changes the result, and neither side owns a share of the area.
12. (mcq) In one group of people, the heritability of height is 80 in 100. What does this mean?  
   Options: 80 per cent of your height comes from genes / Height cannot be changed by food / Most height differences there go with DNA differences / 80 people in 100 are tall  
   **Answer:** Most height differences there go with DNA differences. *Why:* Heritability describes the differences in one group in one place. It says nothing about one person, and it changes if the surroundings change.
13. (odd) Three of these need both genes and surroundings. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Height / Being short-sighted / The fine detail of a fingerprint / Blood group  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Blood group is set by genes alone. Height depends on food as well, short sight on time outdoors, and fingerprint detail on chance before birth.
14. (perspective) Two friends talk about a girl who sings very well. Her mother sings well too.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the facts fit both ideas, because she got her mother's genes and also heard her mother sing. It then gives a test, such as asking about adopted children or twins.. *Why:* Running in a family does not show genes, because families share homes too. Most skills like this need both.
15. (write) Look at the made-up garlic results. What do the four means show about the bulb and about the place?  
   **Answer:** Bulb A grew 62 millimetres warm and 6 cold, with the same DNA. So the place made a big difference. In the warm place bulb A grew 62 and bulb B grew 38. So the bulb made a difference too. Both matter.. *Why:* A strong answer makes two comparisons: same bulb in two places, and two bulbs in the same place.
### Tags, Twins and Open Arguments (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is an epigenetic mark?  
   Options: A changed DNA letter / An extra chromosome / A kind of amino acid / A chemical tag that helps switch genes on or off  
   **Answer:** A chemical tag that helps switch genes on or off. *Why:* A tag sits on the DNA or on the proteins around it. The letters stay the same. A changed letter is a mutation, which is a different thing.
2. (truefalse) True or false: epigenetic marks change the order of the DNA letters.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Tags change how easily a gene is read. The order of the letters stays as it was.
3. (write) A skin cell and a nerve cell in one body have the same DNA. How can they be so different?  
   **Answer:** Each kind of cell reads a different set of genes. Tags on the DNA help keep some genes off and others on. So the two cells make different proteins and do different jobs.. *Why:* A strong answer separates having a gene from reading a gene.
4. (mcq) Old identical twins have more different DNA tags than young identical twins. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Their lives differed more as years passed / Their DNA letters became completely different / They were never really identical / Tags are inherited from grandparents  
   **Answer:** Their lives differed more as years passed. *Why:* Food, illness, habits and chance all change tags over a lifetime. Twins who lived apart differed most. Their DNA letters stayed almost the same.
5. (order) Put the steps of making a protein in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The cell makes a working copy of the gene', 'A ribosome reads the copy, codon by codon', 'The amino acids are joined in that order', 'The chain folds into a shape', 'The protein does its job']. *Why:* The gene stays in the DNA. A copy goes to the ribosome, which builds the chain. The order of amino acids decides the folded shape.
6. (fill) A codon has three places, with four letters to choose from. So there are 4 × 4 × 4 = ____ codons.  
   **Answer:** ['64']. *Why:* 4 × 4 = 16, and 16 × 4 = 64. That is enough for 20 amino acids and three stop signals, with codons to spare.
7. (mcq) Bacteria given the human insulin gene make human insulin. Why does this work?  
   Options: Bacteria are tiny humans / Insulin is not a protein / The gene turns the bacteria into human cells / Almost all living beings read DNA with the same code  
   **Answer:** Almost all living beings read DNA with the same code. *Why:* A codon means the same amino acid in a bacterium, a banyan and a person. So a bacterium's ribosomes read the human gene correctly.
8. (perspective) Two scientists disagree about what DNA is like.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives evidence. For the plan: DNA holds the order of amino acids for every protein. For the second view: the same DNA makes a queen or a worker bee, and McClintock found DNA pieces that move.. *Why:* Scientists still argue about this. Both views agree on what DNA stores. They differ on who is in charge.
9. (write) The heritability of height is higher in a town where every child eats well. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Where everyone eats well, food no longer causes differences in height. The differences that remain come mostly from genes. Where some children go hungry, food causes many of the differences, so the share that goes with genes is smaller.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that heritability is about differences in a group, so it changes when surroundings become more equal.
10. (truefalse) True or false: scientists agree that hunger in grandparents changes the genes of their grandchildren.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Scientists still argue about whether tags pass down the generations in humans. Clear proof is missing. The DNA letters themselves are not changed by hunger.
11. (label) Label an egg cell and two sperm cells with X or Y. Then label which pair gives XX and which XY.  
   **Answer:** ['The egg cell: X', 'One sperm cell: X', 'The other sperm cell: Y', 'Egg with the X sperm: XX', 'Egg with the Y sperm: XY']. *Why:* The egg always carries an X. The two kinds of sperm are what make the two results.
12. (mcq) A mother has blood group A and a father has group B. Their child has group O. How?  
   Options: The child's blood changed after birth / Each parent carried a hidden O version / A mutation must have happened / Blood groups are not inherited  
   **Answer:** Each parent carried a hidden O version. *Why:* O is recessive. A parent with one A and one O has group A but can pass on the O. A child who gets O from both has group O.
13. (sort) Sort these statements: science agrees, or scientists still argue?  
   **Answer:** DNA is written in four letters → Science agrees; A child gets half their chromosomes from each parent → Science agrees; Tags pass down the generations in humans → Scientists still argue; Most of our DNA does nothing useful → Scientists still argue; The sperm cell settles XX or XY → Science agrees; DNA is best described as a blueprint → Scientists still argue. *Why:* The four-letter code, the half from each parent and the part played by the sperm are settled. Inherited tags in humans, 'junk' DNA and the best picture for DNA are still argued over.
14. (write) One paper child got 5 red boxes from a grandmother. Another got 1. What does this show about grandparents?  
   **Answer:** A child gets exactly half from each parent, but not exactly a quarter from each grandparent. Chance decides how much of the mother's half came from her mother and how much from her father.. *Why:* A strong answer separates parents, where the half is exact, from grandparents, where the quarter is only an average.
15. (draw) Draw how identical twins begin. Start with one egg cell and one sperm cell.  
   **Answer:** One egg and one sperm join into one cell. That cell divides, and the small ball of cells splits into two. Each half grows into a baby.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows one fertilised egg becoming two babies. Two eggs with two sperm cells would be fraternal twins.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does one codon stand for?  
   Options: One whole protein / One chromosome / One parent / One amino acid, or 'stop'  
   **Answer:** One amino acid, or 'stop'. *Why:* A codon is three DNA letters. Each one names an amino acid or tells the ribosome to stop. A whole protein needs many codons.
2. (mcq) Which of these is the biggest?  
   Options: A gene / A codon / A chromosome / A DNA letter  
   **Answer:** A chromosome. *Why:* A chromosome is one long piece of DNA that holds hundreds or thousands of genes. A gene holds many codons, and a codon is three letters.
3. (mcq) One DNA letter changes inside a gene. What can happen to the protein?  
   Options: It always becomes harmful / It always stays the same / The gene disappears / Nothing, or one amino acid changes, or an early stop  
   **Answer:** Nothing, or one amino acid changes, or an early stop. *Why:* It depends on the new codon. It may mean the same amino acid, a different one, or stop. Choosing 'always harmful' forgets that many mutations change nothing.
4. (mcq) Why do two children of the same parents get different DNA?  
   Options: The second child gets what is left over / Chance picks one of each pair for every egg and sperm / Parents' DNA changes every year / Each child chooses their genes  
   **Answer:** Chance picks one of each pair for every egg and sperm. *Why:* Each egg and each sperm gets one chromosome from every pair, picked by chance. So each child starts from a different mix. Nothing is used up.
5. (mcq) How much of their parents' DNA copies do a sister and brother share?  
   Options: Exactly half, always / All of it / About half on average, but it varies / None of it  
   **Answer:** About half on average, but it varies. *Why:* The average is half. Real pairs of siblings run from about 37 to about 62 in every 100. Choosing 'exactly half' mixes up siblings with a parent and child.
6. (mcq) How do identical twins begin?  
   Options: One fertilised egg splits into two / Two eggs join two sperm cells / One egg joins two sperm cells / Two babies swap DNA  
   **Answer:** One fertilised egg splits into two. *Why:* One egg and one sperm make one cell, which later splits. Two eggs with two sperm cells give fraternal twins.
7. (truefalse) True or false: identical twins have the same fingerprints.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The fine detail of a fingerprint forms by chance before birth. So even with the same DNA, the prints differ.
8. (mcq) Which chromosome does every egg cell of a human mother carry?  
   Options: A Y / Either X or Y / An X / Neither  
   **Answer:** An X. *Why:* A mother's cells have two X chromosomes, so each egg gets an X. The sperm carries an X or a Y, and that settles XX or XY.
9. (mcq) Which of these shows the same kind of DNA giving two different results?  
   Options: A cat and a dog / A pea plant and a rice plant / A queen bee and a worker bee / A horse and a donkey  
   **Answer:** A queen bee and a worker bee. *Why:* A queen and a worker grow from the same kind of egg and differ because of feeding. The other pairs are different species with different DNA.
10. (mcq) A baby inherits phenylketonuria and gets the special diet from the first weeks. What happens?  
   Options: The brain is protected from harm / The diet makes no difference / The baby's DNA letters are corrected / The condition passes to the mother  
   **Answer:** The brain is protected from harm. *Why:* The diet keeps a harmful substance from building up. The gene is unchanged, but the harm does not happen. So inherited does not mean fixed.
11. (mcq) Why is 'how many centimetres of my height come from genes?' a question with no answer?  
   Options: Nobody has measured it yet / Height has nothing to do with genes / Every centimetre needs both genes and surroundings / Height comes only from food  
   **Answer:** Every centimetre needs both genes and surroundings. *Why:* Genes and surroundings work together, like the length and width of a rectangle. You cannot split the area into a part from each side.
12. (mcq) Twins of both kinds nearly always speak the same language as their twin. What does this show?  
   Options: Genes set which language you speak / Twins cannot learn languages / Language is set by chance / Surroundings set which language you speak  
   **Answer:** Surroundings set which language you speak. *Why:* If genes set it, identical twins would match more often than fraternal twins. Both kinds match equally, because both share a home.

## Applications
- Hospitals test newborn babies for some inherited conditions. For phenylketonuria, a diet started in the first weeks protects the baby's brain.
- India's sickle cell mission, begun in July 2023, tests people so that those with the disease get care early. Carriers learn what it could mean for their children.
- Before a blood transfusion, the blood groups of the giver and the receiver are matched. Blood groups are inherited, so close relatives are often a match.
- Most insulin for people with diabetes is made by bacteria or yeast that carry the human insulin gene. It works because almost all life reads the same genetic code.
- Plant breeders cross two varieties to bring useful versions of genes together. The sugarcane varieties bred at Coimbatore are grown across India.
- Farmers grow bananas, mangoes and sugarcane as clones, from side shoots, grafts or cuttings. Every plant then has the fruit the farmer wants. The risk is that one disease can harm them all.
- A DNA test can show whether two people are parent and child, because a child shares half of each parent's DNA. Courts and families use it to reunite lost relatives.
- Eye doctors advise more time outdoors for children, because trials show it lowers the chance of short sight, even in families where short sight is common.

## Spotted around us
- Look at a family photograph with sisters, brothers and cousins. You can see shared features, and no two people the same.
- Break open a garlic bulb in the kitchen. Every clove is a clone of the others, with the same DNA.
- A bunch of bananas has no working seeds. Every banana plant of one variety, such as Poovan or Nendran, is a clone grown from a side shoot.
- Every Banganapalli or Alphonso mango tree is a clone, made by grafting a twig onto a seedling. That is why the fruit of one variety tastes the same.
- A litter of street puppies has pups of different colours from one mother. Each pup got a different mix, and a litter can even have more than one father.
- Your blood group may be printed on your school identity card. It was settled by the two gene versions you got from your parents.
- Bee boxes standing among coffee and pepper plants. In each box, the queen and the workers grew from the same kind of egg.
- Sea turtles burying their eggs in beach sand, in a video or on a beach. The warmth of each nest settles whether the hatchlings are female or male.
- A hibiscus or a money plant grown from a cutting is a clone of the parent plant. The two can still look different in sun and in shade.

## Open questions
- **Why are so many twins born in the village of Kodinhi in Kerala?** Kodinhi, in Malappuram district, has far more twins than most places in India. Scientists have collected DNA and asked about food and water there. No cause has been found yet.
- **What does the large part of our DNA that does not code for proteins do?** Less than 2 letters in 100 code for proteins. Some of the rest holds switches for genes. Scientists disagree about how much of the remainder does anything useful.
- **Do tags on DNA pass from parents to children and grandchildren in humans?** It happens in some plants and roundworms. In humans, most tags are wiped off in each generation, and the studies so far cannot separate inheritance from direct effects in the womb.
- **Identical twins raised in the same home still differ. How much of that is chance inside the growing body?** Cells divide, move and connect with a little chance each time. Scientists can see this in fingerprints. They do not know how much of the difference in health and habits comes from it.
- **How do thousands of tiny DNA differences add up to one feature such as height?** More than 12,000 places in the DNA are linked to height, each with a very small effect. How they act together in growing bones, and with food, is not worked out.

## Connected maths
- In the Sibling Share Test, the paper mother has 6 pairs of chromosomes. Each egg gets one from every pair. How many different eggs can she make? How many different children can the two paper parents make? **64 different eggs, and 4,096 different children.**. Each pair gives 2 choices. For 6 pairs, multiply 2 six times: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 64. The father can make 64 different sperm cells in the same way. Any egg can meet any sperm, so there are 64 × 64 = 4,096 different children. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In these made-up results, the six pairs of paper children shared 6, 3, 7, 9, 7 and 4 chromosomes out of 12. What is the mean? What fraction of 12 is that? **The mean is 6, which is one half of 12.**. Add the six numbers: 6 + 3 + 7 + 9 + 7 + 4 = 36. Divide by 6: 36 ÷ 6 = 6. Six out of 12 is one half. So the pairs share half on average, though no pair is forced to share exactly half. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- Paper children 2 and 3 shared 9 chromosomes out of 12. Paper children 1 and 3 shared 3 out of 12. Write each as a percentage. Which pair is more alike? **75 per cent and 25 per cent. Children 2 and 3 are more alike.**. 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75, which is 75 per cent. 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25, which is 25 per cent. All four children have the same parents, yet one pair shares three times as much as the other. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A child has 12 chromosomes and four grandparents. If each grandparent gave an equal share, how many would each give? Paper child 4 has 5 red and 1 yellow from the mother's parents. Why is it not equal? **3 each if the shares were equal. The real shares were 5 and 1, because chance decided each pair.**. 12 ÷ 4 = 3. But the mother passed on one chromosome from each of her 6 pairs by coin toss. For child 4 the coin gave red 5 times and yellow once. The two always add up to 6, which is the mother's half. Only the total from each parent is fixed. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- In these made-up results, bulb A grew 62 millimetres in the warm place and 6 in the cold. Bulb B grew 38 and 4. How many millimetres did the cold take from each bulb? About how many times shorter did each become? **The cold took 56 millimetres from bulb A and 34 from bulb B. Each became about 10 times shorter.**. 62 − 6 = 56 and 38 − 4 = 34, so the cold did not take away the same amount. But 62 ÷ 6 is a little more than 10, and 38 ÷ 4 is a little less than 10. So 'about ten times shorter' fits both bulbs. The cold acted like dividing, not like subtracting. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A code that uses 1 DNA letter can name 4 things. How many can a code of 2 letters name? How many can a code of 3 letters name? Which is the shortest code that can name 20 amino acids? **16 with two letters and 64 with three. Three letters is the shortest that is enough.**. Each place in the code has 4 choices. Two places give 4 × 4 = 16, which is fewer than 20. Three places give 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, which is more than 20. So living cells need codons of three letters. (queues `math.logic.counting-ways.L5`)
- One human genome has about 3,000,000,000 letters. Two people differ in about 1 letter in every 1,000. About how many letters differ between them? **About 3,000,000 letters.**. 3,000,000,000 ÷ 1,000 = 3,000,000. Three million sounds like a lot. But for every letter that differs, 999 are the same. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
