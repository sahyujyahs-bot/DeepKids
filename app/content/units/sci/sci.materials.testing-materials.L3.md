# Testing materials: how to run a fair test
`sci.materials.testing-materials.L3`

**Summary.** A shopkeeper says her umbrella cloth keeps out the most rain. How can you check? Pour the same amount of water on each cloth, from the same height, and wait the same time. Only the cloth is different, so any difference must come from the cloth. This is called a fair test. You also hang coins from strips of paper to test how strong they are. A strip with one small cut tears at the cut, because a material is only as strong as its weakest spot.

**Intro.** *Your family must buy one umbrella for the monsoon. The shopkeeper says, 'This one is the most waterproof in all of India!' How could you find out if that is true, without just believing her?* You cannot tell how waterproof a cloth is just by looking at it. You have to test it. A test means doing something to a material on purpose, and measuring what happens. A good test is fair. It gives every cloth the same chance, so only the cloth decides the result. Before you read on, think. How would you test four cloths so that nobody could call your test unfair? Keep your answer in mind. You are going to design that test yourself.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can plan a fair test myself: I decide what to change, what to keep the same and what to measure, before I start.
1. I can make a guess with a reason before a test, and check it against what happened.
2. I can test materials for how waterproof, strong, absorbent and springy they are, using exact amounts, a ruler, a medicine cup or a count.
3. I can record results in a table with clear headings and units, and show them in a bar chart.
4. I can repeat a test three times, find the middle value, and spot a result that does not fit.
5. I can explain why rain gets through a cloth at its biggest gaps, and why a strip tears first at a cut or a thin spot.
6. I can say what my test did not tell me, and why people test things before trusting them with their lives.

## Terms
- **Material**: What a thing is made of. Wood, steel, cotton, paper, plastic, rubber and glass are all materials. *e.g.* A steel tumbler is made of the material steel. A veshti is made of cotton.
- **Property**: A word that says what a material is like, or what it can do. Waterproof, strong, stretchy and absorbent are properties. *e.g.* Rubber is stretchy. Plastic is waterproof. A paper towel is absorbent.
- **Test**: Doing something to a material, on purpose, to find out what it can do. In a good test you measure the result. *e.g.* Pouring two spoons of water on a cloth and measuring how much comes through is a test.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what caused the difference. *e.g.* In the umbrella patch test, every cloth gets the same 20 ml of rain, from the same height. Every cloth waits the same time. Only the cloth changes.
- **The thing we change**: In a fair test, the one thing you change on purpose. In a materials test, it is usually the material. Scientists call it the independent variable. *e.g.* In the umbrella test, the thing we change is the cloth: cotton, plastic, newspaper or wool.
- **The things we keep the same**: Everything else in the test. If one of these changes too, the test is not fair. Scientists call them control variables. *e.g.* The same cup, the same amount of water, the same time and the same size of cloth.
- **The thing we measure**: The result you look at and write down as a number. Scientists call it the dependent variable. *e.g.* How many millilitres of water came through the cloth. How many coins a paper strip held.
- **Prediction**: A careful guess about what will happen, made before you test. You say why you think so. *e.g.* I predict the old jeans will let the least water through, because they are thick and I can't see light through them.
- **Results table**: A grid for writing down what you found. Each column has a heading and a unit. Each row is one thing you tested. *e.g.* Columns: Material, Try 1 (ml), Try 2 (ml), Try 3 (ml), Middle value (ml).
- **Unit**: The size we count in when we measure. Centimetres (cm) for length, millilitres (ml) for water, grams (g) for weight. *e.g.* The paper strip was 2 cm wide. 12 ml of water came through the cotton hanky.
- **Millilitre (ml)**: A tiny amount of liquid. A teaspoon holds about 5 millilitres. A big glass of water holds about 250 millilitres. *e.g.* A medicine cup from a cough syrup bottle has lines that mark millilitres.
- **Repeat**: Doing the same test again, in exactly the same way. We usually do each test three times. *e.g.* We tested each paper strip three times, with a fresh strip each time.
- **Middle value**: Put your three results in order, smallest to biggest. The one in the middle is the middle value. Scientists call it the median. *e.g.* A strip held 9, 15 and 14 coins. In order: 9, 14, 15. The middle value is 14.
- **Flaw**: A tiny weak spot in a material, such as a thin patch, a nick, a gap or a crack. Most flaws are too small to see. *e.g.* A pinhole in an umbrella is a flaw. Rain comes through there, even if the rest of the cloth is perfect.
- **Claim, evidence and reasoning**: Three sentences a scientist writes after a test. The claim is what I think. The evidence is what I saw. The reasoning is why I think it happened. *e.g.* Claim: old jeans make the best patch. Evidence: only 3 ml came through them. Reasoning: their threads are thick and packed tight, so the gaps are tiny.
- **Odd result**: A result that is very different from the others, often because something went wrong. Scientists call it an anomaly. We check it, and test again. *e.g.* A strip held 14, 15 and 3 coins. The 3 is odd. Maybe that strip had a tiny tear.
- **Reliable**: When you repeat a test and get nearly the same result each time, the results are reliable. You can trust them more. *e.g.* The cotton hanky let through 12, 13 and 12 ml. Those are close, so they are reliable.
- **Waterproof**: Does not let water through. *e.g.* A plastic raincoat and an umbrella are waterproof.
- **Absorbent**: Soaks up water and holds it. *e.g.* A cotton towel, a sponge and a kitchen towel are absorbent.
- **Strength**: How much pulling or weight a material can take before it breaks or tears. *e.g.* Brown paper bags hold more than tissue paper before they tear.
- **Stretchy (elastic)**: A stretchy material gets longer when you pull it, and goes back to its old length when you let go. Scientists call this elastic. *e.g.* A rubber band and the waistband of your pyjamas are elastic.
- **Breaking point**: The load at which a material snaps or tears. Every material has one. *e.g.* One hair snapped when the bag hanging from it held 18 one-rupee coins.
- **Hardness**: How well a material stops itself being scratched. A harder material scratches a softer one. *e.g.* A metal key scratches chalk. Chalk cannot scratch the key.
- **Fibre**: A very thin thread. Paper, cotton and wool are made of millions of fibres tangled or woven together. *e.g.* Tear a piece of paper and look at the edge with a magnifying glass. You can see tiny fibres sticking out.
- **Polymer**: A material made of very long chain-like molecules, like strings of beads. Rubber, plastic, cotton and silk are polymers. *e.g.* A rubber band is made of long, tangled chains that straighten out when you pull it.
- **Crack**: A tiny break in a material. A crack makes the material much weaker, because the force of a pull crowds at the crack's sharp tip. *e.g.* A milk packet has a small notch that says 'tear here'. The notch is a tiny crack, made on purpose.
- **Standard**: An agreed way of testing, or an agreed size, that everyone uses. Then tests in different places can be compared. *e.g.* Helmets for scooters in India must pass tests and carry the ISI mark.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that every material is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* A paper strip holds together because its atoms hold on to their neighbours with electric forces.

## Explanations
### What makes a test fair · *defined*
**Definition:** A fair test changes only one thing and keeps everything else the same. Then any difference in the result must come from the one thing you changed.

Imagine testing two cloths for an umbrella. You pour one spoon of water on the cotton, and five spoons on the plastic. More water comes through the plastic. Is plastic worse? You cannot tell, because two things changed: the cloth and the water. So a fair test asks three questions before you start. What will I change? What will I keep the same? What will I measure? In a materials test, you usually change the material. You keep the amount, the size, the time and the way you do it the same. You measure one result, with a number.
- e.g. Umbrella patch test: change the cloth. Keep the same rain, height, tumbler, patch size and waiting time. Measure the water that comes through.
- e.g. Strength test: change the paper. Keep the same strip size and the same coins. Count the coins it holds before it tears.
- e.g. Width test: this time change the width of the strip, and keep the paper the same. A fair test can change anything, but only one thing at a time.
### Writing results in a table · *defined*
**Definition:** A results table is a grid. Each row is one thing you tested. Each column has a heading that says what was measured, and the unit it was measured in.

Scientists draw the table before they start testing. Then nothing gets forgotten. The first column lists the materials. The next columns hold the tries, for example Try 1, Try 2 and Try 3. The last column holds the middle value. Every heading needs its unit in brackets, like (cm) or (ml). A number with no unit means nothing. Is 12 a length in centimetres or a number of coins? A good table lets someone else read your results without asking you anything.
- e.g. Material | Try 1 (ml) | Try 2 (ml) | Try 3 (ml) | Middle value (ml)
- e.g. Paper | Coins held, try 1 | Coins held, try 2 | Coins held, try 3 | Middle value (coins)
### Why we repeat a test three times · *defined*
**Definition:** One try can be a fluke. Repeating a test, and taking the middle value, gives a result you can trust more.

Small accidents happen in every test. A paper strip may have a tiny tear you did not see. A coin may be dropped instead of placed gently. So scientists repeat each test, usually three times. If the three results are close together, they are reliable. If one result is very different, it is called an odd result. Scientists do not just throw it away. They ask what went wrong, and they test again. To get one number for each material, put the three results in order and take the middle one. That is the middle value. An odd result cannot pull the middle value far off.
- e.g. A toilet paper strip held 14, 15 and 3 coins. In order: 3, 14, 15. The middle value is 14. The 3 is an odd result.
- e.g. The woollen sweater let through 14, 15 and 6 ml in the umbrella patch test. The third square was cut from a tighter-knitted part of the sweater.
### Measuring, not just saying · *defined*
**Definition:** A measured result is a number with a unit. Measuring turns 'this seems strong' into '14 coins', which anyone can check.

Saying 'the cotton got quite wet' is a start. But how wet? Your 'quite' might be my 'very'. A number is the same for everyone. That is why we measure water in millilitres, with a measuring cup. We measure length in centimetres, with a ruler. We measure weight by counting coins of the same kind. The tools must be the same for every material. Every coin in a test must be the same kind. A five-rupee coin is heavier than a one-rupee coin. Even one-rupee coins from different years can weigh different amounts. A number only helps if everyone measures in the same way. People have agreed on standard measures for thousands of years. About 4,500 years ago, the cities of the Indus Valley used bricks of the same shape everywhere. They also used stone weights of fixed sizes, so traders in different cities could trust each other.
- e.g. A medicine cup shows that 12 ml of rain came through the cotton hanky.
- e.g. A ruler shows the strip is exactly 2 cm wide, not 'about two fingers'.
- e.g. Indus Valley bricks were made in the same shape, about four units long, two wide and one thick.
### Why some materials keep water out · *defined*
**Definition:** Rain comes through a cloth by the gaps between its threads, and the biggest gaps let it through first. A waterproof material has no gaps, and water does not soak into it.

Water is made of countless tiny particles called molecules. Water molecules hold on tightly to each other, and they also cling to cotton and wool fibres. Every woven or knitted cloth has gaps between its threads. Where the gaps are big, the water simply falls through. Where they are tiny and the threads are packed tight, the water has to creep slowly along the fibres. So thickness is not what matters most. A thick sweater is knitted in loops, with big holes between them. Old jeans are woven tight, so their gaps are tiny. Their thick cotton also soaks up much of the water before it gets through. Plastic, rubber and wax have no gaps at all. Water clings to them only weakly, so it pulls itself into drops and sits on top. An umbrella is made of tightly woven cloth with a thin waterproof coating. But one pinhole is a gap, and the rain comes through there. In 1823, Charles Macintosh in Scotland found a way to seal rubber between two layers of cloth. Raincoats made this way are still called macs.
- e.g. Hold a cotton hanky and a piece of old jeans up to a window. You can see light through the hanky's gaps.
- e.g. Rain makes round, rolling drops on a lotus or arbi (colocasia) leaf, and the leaf stays dry.
- e.g. An old umbrella with one tiny hole drips in exactly one place.
### Why some materials soak up water · *defined*
**Definition:** Absorbent materials are full of tiny gaps between fibres. Water clings to the fibres and is pulled into the gaps. This pulling is called capillary action, or wicking.

Dip the bottom of a paper towel strip in water. Watch. The water climbs up, against gravity. It happens because water molecules cling to the fibres, and also hold on to each other. In a very narrow gap, the clinging pulls the water up, and the water drags more water behind it. The narrower the gaps, the higher it can climb. Kitchen towels are made fluffy and loose on purpose, with many fine gaps. Printer paper is pressed flat and smooth, so it has fewer gaps, and water climbs it slowly. Capillary action also lifts water a little way up the thin tubes inside plants.
- e.g. Water climbs a kitchen towel strip much higher than a printer paper strip in 5 minutes.
- e.g. A cotton wick in a diya pulls oil up to the flame.
- e.g. Dip a sugar cube's corner in tea and watch the tea climb up.
### Why strips of the same paper tear at different numbers · *defined*
**Definition:** A strip tears at its weakest spot. Every strip has its own weak spots, so strips cut from the same sheet hold different loads. Scientists can say what usually happens, but not exactly what one strip will do.

Hold a paper napkin up to a window. Some patches are bright, where the fibres are thin. Some are darker, where they are thick. When you hang coins from a strip, the pull is shared across its whole width. But the strip does not wait for every part to give way. It starts to tear where it is weakest, and then the tear runs across. So a strip is only as strong as its worst spot. Each strip has a different worst spot, so the strips usually hold different numbers of coins. A bigger piece has more places, so it has more chances to hold a really bad spot. In 1939, a Swedish engineer named Waloddi Weibull worked out the maths of this. Engineers still use his maths to judge how far they can trust parts made of glass, ceramic or steel.
- e.g. For example, three 2 cm strips from one napkin might hold 18, 24 and 21 coins.
- e.g. A hair with a knot in it broke at the knot, with fewer coins than a plain hair.
- e.g. Climbing ropes are tested by breaking many sample pieces, because one piece tells you about only one piece.
### Why things tear where there is a crack · *defined*
**Definition:** A tiny crack makes a material much weaker. The force of the pull crowds at the sharp tip of the crack, so the crack grows easily.

Try to tear a milk packet from its smooth top edge. It is hard. Now find the little notch that says 'tear here'. It rips easily. Here is why. When you pull a strip, the force is shared across its whole width. A crack leaves less material to share it. Worse, the force crowds together at the sharp tip of the crack. So the paper at the tip gives way first, then the next bit, then the next. That is how a small crack grows right across a strong strip. The sharper the tip, the more the force crowds there. This is why we check strips for tiny nicks before a strength test.
- e.g. A 'tear here' notch on a packet of biscuits or milk.
- e.g. A paper strip with a tiny cut at its edge tears with fewer coins, and it tears from the cut.
- e.g. Engineers drill a small round hole at the end of a crack in metal. A round end stops the pull from crowding at a sharp tip.
### Hard is not the same as strong · *defined*
**Definition:** Hardness is how well a material resists being scratched. Strength is how much pull or weight it takes before it breaks. A material can be very hard but break easily.

To test hardness, scientists use a scratch test. A harder material scratches a softer one, never the other way round. In 1812, a German scientist named Friedrich Mohs put ten minerals in order of hardness. Talc is the softest, and diamond is the hardest. Your fingernail can scratch chalk and soap. A metal key can scratch your fingernail. Most keys cannot scratch a window pane, so glass is harder still. But glass shatters if you drop it. It is hard, but it is brittle, which means it breaks without bending. Rubber is soft and easy to dent, but it is very hard to snap. So we need different tests for different properties.
- e.g. A fingernail scratches a bar of soap. Soap cannot scratch a fingernail.
- e.g. A diamond can scratch glass. Glass cannot scratch a diamond.
- e.g. A glass bangle is hard, but it breaks if dropped. A rubber band is soft, but hard to snap.
### Why things break after bending again and again · *debated*
Bend a steel paper clip once. It does not break. Bend it back and forth twenty times, and it snaps. This is called fatigue. Aeroplanes, bridges and bicycle frames suffer from it too. Everyone agrees on the main story. Each bend makes tiny cracks a little longer, until one is long enough to snap. But exactly how the very first crack starts, deep inside, is still studied. And no one can yet predict the exact bend on which a particular clip will snap.
- e.g. A paper clip bent back and forth gets warm, then snaps.
- e.g. A plastic ruler bent many times turns white at the bend before it cracks.
- **Engineers who test machines:** Tiny cracks grow a little with every bend. We cannot see them, so we test many samples and give every part a big safety margin.
- **Scientists who study atoms in metals:** Rows of atoms inside the metal slip a little with every bend. The slips pile up in bands, and the first cracks start where they pile up. How exactly this happens is still being worked out.
- **Scientists who use chance and numbers:** Every clip has its own hidden flaws. So we can say how many clips will usually snap by 20 bends, but not which one.
### Testing without breaking: the tap test · *defined*
**Definition:** Some tests break the thing you test, like pulling a strip until it tears. Other tests check a thing without harming it. These are called non-destructive tests.

You cannot test every bridge by breaking it. So engineers also check things gently. The oldest gentle test is tapping. A whole pot rings clearly when you tap it. A cracked pot sounds dull and buzzy, because the crack stops the pot vibrating as one piece. Potters have used this test for thousands of years. Many railways, in Britain and elsewhere, once had workers called wheel tappers. At stations, they walked along trains, tapping each wheel with a long hammer. A clear ring meant a sound wheel. Today, engineers send sound too high for us to hear into train rails and aeroplane parts. Echoes from inside show hidden cracks. Doctors use the same idea, ultrasound, to see a baby inside their mother.
- e.g. A potter taps a new pot with a knuckle and listens for a clear ring.
- e.g. A coconut seller taps and shakes a coconut to judge how much water is inside.
- e.g. Rail engineers check tracks for hidden cracks with machines that use ultrasound.
### Why materials hold together at all · *defined*
**Definition:** Every material is made of atoms. Neighbouring atoms pull on each other with electric force. To tear or snap a material, you must pull those atoms apart.

Zoom into a paper strip, far past anything you can see. Paper is made of fibres. Each fibre is made of long chains of atoms. Neighbouring chains stick to each other, and fibres stick where they cross. When you hang coins on the strip, the pull spreads through all these links. A strip tears when enough links break at once. In steel, the atoms are packed in neat rows. Each holds tight to all its neighbours, so steel is very strong. In tissue paper, there are few fibres and few links, so it tears easily. Strength comes from how many of these tiny links there are, how tightly each one holds, and where links are missing.
- e.g. Wet paper is much weaker than dry paper. Water slips between the fibres and loosens their grip on each other.
- e.g. A cotton thread is made of many short fibres twisted together. The twist makes them grip each other.
### Nature's long test: living materials · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things make materials that have been tested by survival over millions of years. Bone, spider silk, mother-of-pearl and tooth enamel are some of the best materials we know.

A spider's safety line, called dragline silk, is about as strong as a steel thread of the same thickness. But it is far lighter, and very stretchy too. Mother-of-pearl is the shiny inside of a seashell. It is made of tiny flat bricks of chalky material, glued with a soft protein. A crack in it cannot run straight. It has to wind around every brick, so the shell is very tough. Your bones are hard but slightly bendy, and they repair themselves when they crack. No factory can yet make spider silk as well as a spider does. Scientists test these materials, then try to copy them. Copying nature's designs is called biomimicry.
- e.g. A spider's web survives wind, rain and flying insects crashing into it.
- e.g. The shiny inside of a seashell is mother-of-pearl. Find one on a beach in Kanyakumari or Puri.
- e.g. A broken bone, held still in a plaster cast, mends itself in a few weeks.
### The strongest material in the universe? · *idea*
What is the strongest material of all? On Earth, the answer keeps changing as new materials are made. Graphene, a sheet of carbon just one atom thick, is one of the strongest ever measured. But the strongest material may be far out in space. When a giant star explodes, it can leave behind a neutron star. A neutron star is a ball only about as wide as a city, but heavier than our Sun. In its crust, scientists think matter is squeezed into strange shapes, like sheets and tubes of pasta. They call it nuclear pasta. In 2018, a team of scientists in Canada and the United States used computers to work out how strong it would be. They found it could be about ten billion times stronger than steel. Nobody can test it, because nobody can reach a neutron star. So this is an idea from calculations, not a measurement.
- e.g. Graphene was first made in a lab by peeling graphite, the grey material in a pencil, again and again with sticky tape.
- e.g. The nearest neutron stars are so far away that their light takes hundreds of years to reach us.
- **Matthew Caplan, Andre Schneider and Charles Horowitz, in 2018:** Computer models of nuclear pasta suggest it is the strongest material in the universe.
- **Other scientists:** The models are a good start, but no one can test them directly. Watching neutron stars, and the ripples in space they make when they crash, may one day check the idea.

## Mostly agreed
- A fair test changes only one thing and keeps everything else the same. *How we know:* When two things change at once, nobody can tell which caused the result. Scientists all over the world plan tests this way, and so do children in classrooms.
- Repeating a test gives results you can trust more than one try. *How we know:* Repeated tries show whether a result was a fluke. Scientists publish how many times they repeated each test, so others can judge it.
- Every material has a breaking point. *How we know:* Testing machines have pulled strips of almost every material until they broke. Even steel and diamond break if the load is big enough.
- A material gives way first at its weakest spot. *How we know:* Paper strips tear where they are thinnest or nicked. Rain comes through a cloth at its biggest gaps. Engineers check welds, sharp corners and holes most carefully, because breaks usually start there.
- Tiny cracks and flaws make materials much weaker. *How we know:* Alan Arnold Griffith showed this with glass threads in 1920. Packets with a 'tear here' notch show it every day.
- Things people trust with their lives are tested before they are used. *How we know:* Helmets in India must pass tests to carry the ISI mark. In 1995, engineers bent the wings of a new Boeing 777 aeroplane until they snapped. They broke only at about one and a half times the biggest load the plane should ever meet.

## How we found out
**Charles Inglis (1913), Alan Arnold Griffith (1920) and Constance Tipper (1940s), 1913 to the 1950s.** For a long time, engineers thought each material had one fixed strength. Every rod of the same glass should break at about the same load. But there was a puzzle. Atoms hold their neighbours so tightly that glass should be many times stronger than any real piece. In 1913, Charles Inglis, an engineer at Cambridge, worked out that the force of a pull crowds around the end of a hole or crack. The sharper the end, the more it crowds. In 1920, Alan Arnold Griffith, an engineer at Farnborough in England, drew glass into thinner and thinner threads. The thinnest threads were many times stronger, for their size, than thick rods. He explained that real glass is full of tiny cracks, and it breaks at the worst one. A thin thread has room for fewer cracks. For years, few engineers used his idea. Then, in the Second World War, some welded American ships cracked, and a few broke in two. The cracks often began at sharp corners. Constance Tipper, a scientist at Cambridge, showed that this steel turned brittle in icy water, like glass. After the war, George Irwin and others turned these ideas into the science of cracks, called fracture mechanics.  
*What people believed before:* That each material has one fixed strength, so every piece of the same glass or steel is about equally strong. Griffith's thin glass threads were far stronger for their size than thick rods. That showed that hidden cracks, not the material alone, set the breaking load.

## Challenging perspectives
- **One test is enough to know which material is best.** (A common way of thinking, especially when we are in a hurry; *rejected*)  
  For: It saves time and materials. Sometimes the difference is so big that one try seems to show it clearly.  
  Against: One try can be a fluke. A strip can have a hidden nick, or a coin can be dropped too hard. Repeating the test three times shows whether the result is real.
- **If an advert says a product is the strongest, it must be true.** (Many adverts on television and on packets; *rejected*)  
  For: Companies do test their products. In India, false claims in adverts can be reported and stopped.  
  Against: An advert does not show how the test was done, or what it was compared with. Was it fair? Was it repeated? A claim is only as good as the test behind it. David Kirkaldy, a Scottish engineer, ran a materials testing works in London from 1866. Over its door he put the words 'Facts not opinions'.
- **Every piece of the same material is equally strong.** (A common idea, and the idea most engineers worked with until about 1920; *rejected*)  
  For: Pieces from the same factory look the same, and their strengths are often close.  
  Against: Every piece has its own hidden flaws, and it breaks at the worst one. Three strips from one napkin usually hold different numbers of coins. In 1920, Alan Arnold Griffith showed that hidden cracks set the strength of glass. In 1939, Waloddi Weibull worked out the maths of the spread.
- **If a material passes a test, it will behave the same way in real use.** (A common hope, and the reason tests are made to copy real use; *debated*)  
  For: Good tests are designed to be like the real job, and they catch most problems. Helmets, ropes and wings are all tested this way.  
  Against: A test answers only the question it asks. In the Second World War, some welded ships were built of steel that passed the usual tests. Those tests pulled smooth bars at room temperature. In icy seas, the same steel turned brittle, and cracks started at sharp corners. Constance Tipper, a scientist at Cambridge, showed why the steel turned brittle in the cold. A test for brittle steel, the Tipper test, is named after her.
- **A hard material is always a strong material.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Many hard things, like stone and steel, are also strong.  
  Against: Glass is harder than a metal key in a scratch test, but it shatters when dropped. Rubber is soft, but very hard to snap. Hardness and strength are different properties, so they need different tests.
- **Real testing only happens in big laboratories with expensive machines.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Big testing machines can pull a steel bar with a force far stronger than any person could.  
  Against: Agnes Pockels was not allowed to go to university, because she was a woman. In the 1880s, she ran careful fair tests in her kitchen in Germany. Her results were printed in the famous science journal Nature in 1891. Many good tests need only care, patience and a table.

## Roots
We have tested cloths until rain came through, and paper strips until they tore. Now we ask what those tests tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A napkin strip with one small cut held fewer coins and tore at the cut. However strong a material is, a small crack can grow and break it. An object is only as strong as the crack it has. So engineers hunt for tiny cracks before they grow.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think strength belongs to the material: steel is strong and glass is weak. But a strong thing with a crack can break easily, and a thin glass thread with no cracks is very strong. So strength depends on the cracks as well as the material.  
*Evidence:* In the tiny cut test, napkin strips with one 5 mm cut tore at the cut and held fewer coins than strips with no cut. In 1920, Alan Arnold Griffith found that very thin glass threads were many times stronger for their size than thick rods, because they had fewer hidden cracks.

- *who am i:* Running and jumping make tiny cracks in your bones every day. Your body finds those spots and rebuilds them.
- *what is life:* A stone cannot mend its cracks. A living body keeps finding its weak spots and mending them, all its life.
- *what is this world:* In a cloth, a cup or a mountain, atoms hold their neighbours with the same electric force. A thing usually breaks where that hold is already broken: at a gap, a nick or a crack.

Every material we tested is held together by the same force: the electric force between atoms. It holds cotton, paper, steel and the hair on your head. Yet these materials behave very differently, because their atoms are different and are arranged differently. Our tests showed one more difference: where the hold is missing. The gaps between a sweater's loops, a pinhole and the tip of a cut were the places where rain got in or a tear began.

- **Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 (Pali Canon):** The Buddha told the people of Kesaputta, called the Kalamas, not to believe something only because it is old, or written down, or said by a respected teacher. When you know for yourselves, by looking carefully, that something is true, then accept it. (This is the spirit of a fair test. We did not believe the shopkeeper, or even our own first guess. We tested, and let the rain and the coins decide.)
- **Thomas Reid, Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man (1785):** The Scottish philosopher Thomas Reid wrote that a chain of reasoning can be no stronger than its weakest link, however strong the rest of it is. (Reid was writing about arguments, not materials. But our strips showed the same thing: each strip tore at its one weakest spot, however strong the rest of it was.)
- Science (settled): Real materials break at far smaller loads than the force between their atoms would allow, because breaking starts at tiny flaws. Griffith showed this with glass in 1920.
- Science (open): Nobody can predict exactly where and when one particular piece will break. Scientists can only say how often pieces of a kind break at each load.
- Other view, Materials scientists who study tough materials: Not every material is ruled by its cracks in the same way. In glass, one tiny scratch can be enough to snap it. In rubber, cloth or soft metal, the material stretches at the tip of a crack and makes the tip blunt, so a small cut does much less harm. They would say that cracks matter in every material, but how much they matter depends on the material.
- Practice, Find the thin spots (3 min): Take a paper napkin, or a sheet of paper, and hold it up to a window. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly. Look for the bright patches, where the fibres are thin, and the darker patches, where they are thick. Choose the spot where you think it would tear first. Now put the paper down. Rest one hand on your other arm, and feel the bone under the skin. Right now, your body is finding tiny cracks in that bone and mending them. Breathe in and out three times, and just notice your arm.
- Ask: Is a crack a thing, or a place where a thing is missing?
- Ask: Strips of the same paper held different numbers of coins. Is there such a thing as 'the strength of paper'?
- Ask: Could anything have no weak spot at all? How would you ever test it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Why does a fair test change only one thing? What goes wrong if you change two? (hint: Imagine you changed the cloth and the amount of water. More water came through. What caused it?)
- *deep:* Can you ever be completely sure a material will not break? (hint: Engineers test many samples and then build things much stronger than they need. Why do you think they add extra?)
- *deep:* Why does water roll off a lotus leaf but soak into a cotton hanky? (hint: Think about what water likes to cling to, and what the lotus leaf looks like close up.)
- *big:* Is it fair to test every child with the same test, like we test every cloth with the same water? (hint: A test is fair to materials when only the material changes. But children are different in many ways. What would a fair test of a person look like?)
- *deep:* Three strips cut from the same napkin held different numbers of coins. So what is 'the strength of paper'? Is it one number at all? (hint: Think about the middle value. Is it the strength of any one strip, or something else?)
- *big:* An advert says 'the strongest bag in India'. What would you need to know before you believed it? (hint: Think of the three planning questions: what changed, what stayed the same, what was measured.)
- *big:* Agnes Pockels did real science at her kitchen sink, but she was not allowed to go to university. How many good scientists might the world have missed? (hint: Who is allowed to do science today? Who still finds it hard?)
- *big:* Is it right to test something until it breaks? (hint: Engineers break some wings and helmets so that others never break. Is breaking a few worth keeping many safe?)
- *deep:* Spiders make silk stronger than steel for its weight, at room temperature, from what they eat. Why can't our factories do that yet? (hint: Think about how many millions of years spiders have been 'testing' their silk.)

- *big:* Paper, rubber, steel and a lotus leaf are all made of atoms that hold on to each other with the same electric force. ## Story: The Button on the Water
*Agnes Pockels (1862 to 1935) is real. She lived in Braunschweig, Germany. She could not go to university because she was a woman, and she looked after her ill parents. She tested the skin of water at home with a tray, a sliding tin strip and a small balance that lifted a button. Her brother Friedrich (Fritz) studied physics and sent her books. Her letter to Lord Rayleigh in January 1891 was printed in Nature in March 1891. She received an honorary doctorate in 1932, the year Irving Langmuir won the Nobel Prize for related work. The jumping numbers, the onions, the open window and her exact feelings are imagined.*

In the town of Braunschweig, in Germany, about 140 years ago, there was a kitchen with a window that looked out on a grey street. In that kitchen, every evening, a young woman named Agnes Pockels washed the dishes.

Agnes was clever, and she loved science more than almost anything. But in Germany at that time, women were not allowed to study at a university. Her younger brother, Fritz, went away to study physics. Agnes stayed at home. Her mother and father were often ill, and somebody had to look after them. So Agnes cooked, and cleaned, and washed the dishes.

Fritz sent her his science books. She read them at night by the lamp, with her feet tucked under her.

And at the sink, she noticed things. When the washing-up water was clean, its surface seemed to hold together, like a skin stretched tight across a drum. When a little grease from the plates got into it, the skin went slack. A drop of soap made it slacker still. Nobody had asked her to notice this. It was only dishwater. But Agnes wondered. How strong is the skin of water? And could she measure it?

She built her own instrument from things in the house. She took a long, narrow tin tray and filled it to the brim with water. She laid a thin strip of tin across it, like a little wall that could slide. From one arm of a small balance, she hung a button. She lowered the button until it lay flat on the water. Then, very gently, she added tiny weights to the other side, until the button came free. The weight it took told her how strongly the water's skin had held on. Scientists today call the pull of that skin surface tension.

The first evening, the button came free at one number. The second evening, at another. The third evening, at a different number again. She wrote every number in her notebook in neat columns, and the columns made no sense. She tried again. The numbers jumped about like sparrows on a roof.

Perhaps, she thought, I am not clever enough after all. Perhaps a kitchen is no place for science.

She sat at the table with the notebook open. The lamp hissed. In the next room, her father coughed. And slowly, she began to ask a different question. Not 'What is wrong with me?' but 'What is different each time?'

She made a list. On Monday, she had washed the tray with soap. On Tuesday, only with water. On Wednesday, she had touched the edge of the water with a finger, and her fingers had been peeling onions. Once, the window had been open, and dust had drifted in. The button was the same each time. The balance was the same. But the surface of the water was never the same. Without meaning to, she had changed many things at once. So how could she tell which one mattered?

The next evening, she began again. This time she was strict. She scrubbed the tray the same way, for the same time. She filled it with the same fresh water from the same jug. She kept her fingers out. She shut the window. Before each test, she slid the tin strip slowly along the tray, sweeping the surface clean, the way you sweep a doorstep before you draw a rangoli. Everything stayed the same, except the one thing she wanted to test.

The button came free. She wrote down the number. She swept the surface and tried again. The same number, or very nearly. And again. The same. Three numbers in a row, standing still at last, like sparrows settled on a wire.

Now she could begin. She added one tiny drop of oil, and measured. Then two drops. She tried soap, and then other things from the cupboard. Each time she changed one thing, and only one. She wrote the results in her table: what she added, how much, and the weight it took to lift the button. When she slid the tin strip to squeeze an oily surface into a smaller and smaller space, she found something strange. Nothing changed at all. Then, all at once, when the space was small enough, the skin's pull dropped away.

It was as if the oil were a crowd of people spread out over a big field. They only began to push against each other when the field became small enough.

For about ten years, Agnes kept testing and writing, in the evenings, after the dishes. Who would listen to a woman with no degree, who did her experiments next to the potatoes?

Then Fritz told her about a famous English scientist, Lord Rayleigh, who was studying the very same thing: thin films of oil on water. In January 1891, Agnes sat down and wrote him a long letter. She described her tray, her button, her sliding strip and her tables of numbers.

She expected nothing. But Lord Rayleigh read the letter carefully. He saw that her numbers were steady and her tests were fair. He sent her letter to Nature, one of the most famous science journals in the world. In March 1891, it was printed, with her name on it.

Years later, other scientists worked out what her squeezed oil films really were. They were layers of oil just one molecule thick. A woman at a kitchen sink had measured something far too thin for any microscope of her time to see.

In 1932, when Agnes was seventy years old, the university in Braunschweig gave her an honorary doctorate. That same year, an American scientist named Irving Langmuir won the Nobel Prize, for work that grew from a tray very like hers. Agnes never won a Nobel Prize. But her notebook is remembered. In its early pages, the numbers jump about. Then, on one page, they stand still.

**For the parent:** Agnes's numbers jump about because, without noticing, she changes many things at once: soap, onion fingers, dust. The turn comes when she stops blaming herself and asks 'what is different each time?' She keeps everything the same except one thing, and her repeated results agree. Only then can she test oil, soap and squeezing one at a time and write a meaningful table. That is a fair test and reliable, repeated results. The story also quietly asks who is allowed to do science, and who gets the prize.

- Ask: At first, Agnes's numbers kept jumping about. What was really causing that?
- Ask: What did Agnes do to make every test the same? Name three things.
- Ask: Why did her three numbers in a row matter so much to her?
- Ask: Agnes was not allowed to go to university. Do you think that was fair? Who might still be kept out of science today?
- Ask: Irving Langmuir won a Nobel Prize, and Agnes did not. How do you feel about that?

### e3 · The Width Test: does a strip twice as wide hold twice as much? (30 min)
- **question:** Strips cut from the same paper napkin: does a 4 cm strip hold twice as many coins as a 2 cm strip? And do strips of the same size all hold the same?
- **predict:** Guess first, and say why. If a 2 cm strip holds 20 coins, how many will a 4 cm strip hold? And will three 2 cm strips from the same napkin all hold exactly the same number? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** This time, the thing we change is not the paper. It is the width of the strip. Keep everything else the same: the same napkin, one layer, and strips 15 cm long. Tape them the same way, use the same bag, and place one-rupee coins gently, one at a time. Measure how many coins each strip holds before it tears. Test three strips of each width.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps with the scissors. Keep your feet away from under the bag. When the strip tears, the coins fall.
- **record:** Fill in the results table, with where each strip tore. Write the middle value for each width. Then write: did doubling the width double the coins?
- **results table:** Strip | Try 1: coins held | Try 2: coins held | Try 3: coins held | Middle value (coins) | Where it tore
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child one layer of the napkin. 2. Ask the two guesses below. 3. Write both guesses and the reasons down.  
  *Parent says:* If a strip 2 cm wide holds 20 coins, how many will a strip 4 cm wide hold? Why do you think so? And will three strips of the same size all hold the same?  
  *Child mode:* A 2 cm strip holds 20 coins. Guess: how many will a 4 cm strip hold? Why? Will three same-size strips hold the same?
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: Twice as wide is twice as much paper, so 40 coins.; They will all be the same, because it is the same napkin.; Maybe not exactly, because one might have a weak bit.)
  - Note: Don't correct the guesses. Write them down. Many children say '40' and 'all the same'. The test will show whether that is right.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. With a ruler and pencil, mark three strips 2 cm wide and three 4 cm wide. Make them all 15 cm long. 2. Cut them out slowly. 3. Check every edge for nicks.  
  *Parent says:* Here we change the width on purpose. What must we keep the same?  
  *Child mode:* Mark three strips 2 cm wide. Mark three 4 cm wide. Make all of them 15 cm long. Ask a grown-up to help cut. Check for nicks.
  - Note: Napkin paper tears easily while cutting, so go slowly. Cut all the strips in the same direction across the napkin, because paper is often stronger one way than the other.
- **s3 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Trap the top 3 cm of a strip under the heavy book at the table's edge. 2. Fold the bottom 2 cm round the paper clip hook and tape it. 3. Hang the bag. 4. Add coins gently, one at a time, counting out loud. 5. When it tears, write the number, and mark on a drawing where it tore. 6. Test all six strips.  
  *Parent says:* Gently, one coin at a time. Before each new strip, guess again: more or fewer than the last one?  
  *Child mode:* Hang a strip. Add coins one at a time. Count out loud. When it tears, write the number. Mark where it tore. Test all six.
  - Ask: Did each strip tear in the same place? (good: No, each tore somewhere different.; Some tore at the tape, some in the middle.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results together. Compare the 4 cm middle value with double the 2 cm middle value.  
  *Parent says:* Did twice as wide hold twice as many? And look at the three 2 cm strips. Same napkin, same size. Why do you think they held different numbers?  
  *Child mode:* Did twice as wide hold twice as many? The 2 cm strips held different numbers. Why might that be?
  - Check: Three 2 cm strips from one napkin held 18, 24 and 21 coins. Why were they different? → **Each strip had its own hidden weak spots, and it tore at the weakest**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now write three sentences. First, what you think now. Second, what you saw that shows it. Third, why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Write three sentences. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Note: Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a strip twice as wide holds about twice as many coins, but not exactly. Strips of the same size are not all equally strong. What I saw: the 2 cm strips held 18, 24 and 21. The 4 cm strips held about 44, which is close to double 21. Why: twice as wide means twice as many fibres share the pull. But each strip tears at its own weakest spot, and those are different in every strip.
  - Think: If you tested a strip 100 cm wide, would it hold exactly 50 times as many coins as a 2 cm strip? What could go wrong?
### e4 · The Climbing Water Test: which material soaks up water fastest? (32 min)
- **question:** Which material soaks up water fastest, so that water climbs highest up it in 5 minutes?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put the four materials in order, from the one you think water will climb highest up, to the lowest. Say why you put your top one first.
- **fair test:** Change only the material. Keep everything else the same: strips 2 cm wide and 15 cm long, the same coloured water, each strip dipped exactly 1 cm in, all started at the same moment, and 5 minutes of waiting. Measure how high the colour climbs above the water, in centimetres. Test three strips of each material at once.
- **safety:** Food colouring and ink stain clothes and hands. Wear old clothes and work on newspaper.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Write the middle value for each material. Draw a bar chart.
- **results table:** Material | Strip 1: height (cm) | Strip 2: height (cm) | Strip 3: height (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw three boxes on a sheet of paper. Label them CHANGE, KEEP THE SAME and MEASURE. 2. Fill them in together. CHANGE: the material. KEEP THE SAME: the strip size, the coloured water, how deep each dips, the start time and 5 minutes. MEASURE: how high the colour climbs, in centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* Before any test, scientists answer three questions. What will we change? What will we keep the same? What will we measure? Let's fill in our planning boxes.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three boxes: CHANGE, KEEP THE SAME, MEASURE. What will you change? What stays the same? What will you measure?
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a pencil line 1 cm from the bottom of each strip. 2. Peg the 12 strips along the stick, so they hang down. 3. Rest the stick on the two piles of books, with the strips hanging into the empty tray. 4. Add or take away a thin book. Stop when each strip ends about 1 cm above the floor of the tray. 5. Lift the stick off. Pour water into the tray, about 2 cm deep, and stir in the colour.  
  *Parent says:* The water will reach each pencil line. Same depth for every strip keeps it fair. Why do you think the depth matters?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line 1 cm from the bottom of each strip. Peg the strips on the stick. Rest it on the books. Fix the height. Then pour in the coloured water.
  - Note: If a strip curls or does not touch the water, that strip will give an odd result. Straighten it before the timer starts.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lower the stick back onto the two piles. Every strip now dips to its pencil line at the same moment. 2. Start the timer for 5 minutes. 3. Watch the colour climb.  
  *Parent says:* All of them start at the same moment. Watch closely. Which one is winning the race?  
  *Child mode:* Lower the stick onto the books. All the strips dip in at once. Start the timer. Watch the colour climb. Which is fastest?
  - Ask: What is pulling the water up, against gravity? (good: The water clings to the fibres and climbs the tiny gaps.; The strip is sucking it up.)
  - Note: 'Sucking' is a fine first answer. You can add: water clings to the fibres and to itself, and that pulls it up the narrow gaps. Scientists call this capillary action, or wicking.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 5 minutes, lift the stick out. 2. Lay the strips on newspaper. 3. Measure from the pencil line to the top of the colour on each strip. 4. Write each number in the table and find the middle values.  
  *Parent says:* Measure from the pencil line, not from the very bottom. The line is where the water started.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the strips out. Measure from the pencil line to the top of the colour. Write each one down.
  - Check: Kitchen towel strips gave 9, 2 and 10 cm. What is the middle value? → **9 cm**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tear the end of a kitchen towel strip and a printer paper strip. Look at both torn edges through a magnifying glass.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the torn edges. Which one is fluffy, with lots of gaps? Which is pressed flat?  
  *Child mode:* Tear the end of two strips. Look with a magnifier. Which edge is fluffy? Which is flat?
  - Think: A diya wick is made of cotton. Why not printer paper?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now write three sentences. First, what you think now. Second, what you saw that shows it. Third, why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Write three sentences. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: kitchen towel soaks up water fastest. What I saw: in 5 minutes, the water climbed 9 cm up the kitchen towel. It climbed only 2 cm up the printer paper. Why: kitchen towel is fluffy, with lots of fine gaps between its fibres. The water is pulled up the gaps.

### Testing Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Vidya, a girl in Class 4, hangs 10 coins from a thin rubber band and 20 coins from a thick band. The thick band stretched more. She says thick bands are stretchier. What is the best reply?  
   Options: She is right, because the thick band stretched more / Her test is unfair, because the number of coins changed too / She should have used paper money / Thick bands are always less stretchy, so she made a mistake reading the ruler  
   **Answer:** Her test is unfair, because the number of coins changed too. *Why:* Two things changed: the band and the load. The thick band might have stretched more only because it had twice the coins. Picking 'thick bands are always less stretchy' jumps to a new rule without a fair test. We do not know until we test both with the same coins.
2. (mcq) Vaishali, a girl in Class 4, did the climbing water test three times with kitchen towel. The water climbed 9 cm, 2 cm and 10 cm. What is the middle value?  
   Options: 2 cm / 7 cm / 9 cm / 10 cm  
   **Answer:** 9 cm. *Why:* In order: 2, 9, 10. The middle is 9. The 2 is an odd result, but it cannot pull the middle value far. Picking 7 means working out the average: 21 divided by 3. The average is pulled down a lot by the odd result, which is why the middle value is useful here.
3. (mcq) Agnes Pockels's numbers kept jumping about at first. Why?  
   Options: Her balance was broken / She was changing several things without noticing, like soap, dust and greasy fingers / Water is different every day / She could not read numbers well  
   **Answer:** She was changing several things without noticing, like soap, dust and greasy fingers. *Why:* Many small things changed at once. Once she kept everything the same, her results agreed. Picking 'her balance was broken' is a common guess when results look messy. But the balance was the same each time. The surface of the water was not.
4. (mcq) Thin glass threads are much stronger, for their size, than thick glass rods. Why?  
   Options: Thin threads have fewer hidden cracks / Thin threads are made of a different glass / Thick rods are heavier, so they pull themselves apart / Thin threads are colder  
   **Answer:** Thin threads have fewer hidden cracks. *Why:* Alan Arnold Griffith showed in 1920 that hidden cracks make materials weak. A thin thread has less room for cracks. Picking 'heavier' is a sensible guess, but a short glass rod is not heavy enough to pull itself apart.
5. (mcq) A paper clip bent once doesn't break. Bent twenty times, it snaps. What is this called?  
   Options: Fatigue / Friction / Absorbency / Magnetism  
   **Answer:** Fatigue. *Why:* Fatigue is when tiny cracks grow a little with every bend, until one snaps. Picking friction is a common mix-up, because the clip gets warm. The warmth comes from bending the metal, not from rubbing.
6. (write) An advert says 'Super Towel soaks up twice as much as other towels!' Plan a fair test to check it. Say what you change, keep the same and measure.  
   **Answer:** Change: the towel brand. Keep the same: the size of each sheet, the amount of water, the plate, the time, and the way I squeeze it. Measure: how many millilitres of water each sheet soaks up, by measuring what is left on the plate. Repeat each three times and compare middle values.. *Why:* A claim is only as good as the test behind it. A fair test can check it.
7. (perspective) Helmets are tested by dropping them from a height, again and again, until some crack. People disagree about breaking things to test them.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees the trade-off. Breaking a few helmets protects many lives, so most people think it is worth the waste. Tests that do not break things can check the very helmet you will wear, but they may not show how much force it can take. Many answers say both kinds of test are needed.. *Why:* Engineers really use both: tests that break a few samples, to find the limit, and tests that leave a thing whole, to check each one before use.

   Options: To show off / Every piece of material has hidden flaws, and loads can be bigger than expected / Strong bridges are cheaper / Because the law says bridges must be heavy  
   **Answer:** Every piece of material has hidden flaws, and loads can be bigger than expected. *Why:* Tests show that pieces of the same material vary in strength, and that cracks can grow over time. 9. (write) Spider silk is about as strong as a steel thread of the same thickness, but much lighter and stretchier. Imagine you could make lots of it. What would you make, and how would you test it first?

10. (write) David Kirkaldy carved the words 'Facts not opinions' over the door of his testing works in London. What do you think he meant? Do you agree?  
   **Answer:** He meant that what a test shows counts more than what someone believes or says. I agree, but I think opinions are still useful for deciding what to test.. *Why:* A thoughtful answer explains the motto and gives a view with a reason.

12. (write) Can a test ever be completely fair? Think about the weather, tired hands and tiny flaws in each strip. What can scientists do about this?  
   **Answer:** Not completely. There are always tiny differences. Scientists keep as much the same as they can, repeat each test, use the middle value, and tell others exactly how they did it so others can try it too.. *Why:* This is a deep question. Good answers notice that repeating and sharing methods make tests trustworthy even when they cannot be perfect.

16. (truefalse) Real testing only happens in big laboratories with expensive machines.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Agnes Pockels did tests in her kitchen that were printed in the famous journal Nature. What makes a test good is that it is fair, repeated and written down, not how costly the machine is.

## Applications
- Umbrellas, raincoats and school bag covers are made from materials that passed waterproof tests.
- Helmets for scooters and motorbikes in India must pass tests and carry the ISI mark of the Bureau of Indian Standards.
- Aeroplane wings, bridges and lifts are tested to much more than the biggest load they should ever carry.
- Ropes for climbing, fishing and boats are tested until sample pieces break, so the rest can be trusted.
- Kitchen towels, nappies and sanitary pads are tested for how much they soak up.
- Cricket balls, bats and helmets are tested for hardness and strength.
- Engineers test new bone screws and hip joints by loading them millions of times in machines.
- Rail engineers check tracks and wheels for hidden cracks using ultrasound.
- Potters tap pots, and builders tap tiles, to hear hidden cracks.
- Consumer scientists test things people buy, like soaps and towels, to check the claims on the packets.
- Packets have 'tear here' notches, which use a crack on purpose.
- Self-cleaning paints and windows copy the waxy bumps of the lotus leaf.
- Spider silk is tested in labs, so scientists can try to copy it for strong, light ropes and bandages.
- New materials for spacecraft are tested outside the International Space Station, in the cold, heat and radiation of space.
- Climbers and sailors check their ropes most carefully at the knots, because a knot is a weak spot.

## Open questions
- **Can we ever predict exactly when a material will break?** Every piece has its own hidden flaws, too small to see. Scientists can say what usually happens, but not exactly what one piece will do.
- **Exactly how does the very first crack start inside a metal that is bent again and again?** It starts with rows of atoms slipping, deep inside. Scientists study it with powerful microscopes and computers, and still argue about the details.
- **How do spiders make silk so strong, from a liquid, at room temperature?** Scientists understand parts of it. But nobody has yet made silk as good as a spider's, in large amounts.
- **Is there a strongest possible material?** New materials keep breaking records. Calculations suggest nuclear pasta in neutron stars may be strongest of all, but nobody can test it.
- **How can we test materials that must last hundreds of years, like the walls of a nuclear waste store?** Nobody can wait hundreds of years. Scientists speed up tests with heat and stress, and argue about whether fast tests really match slow time.
- **How long does plastic really last in sun, soil and sea?** Most plastics have only existed for about 70 years. The very long numbers you hear are estimates, still being tested.
- **Why do some tests give different answers in different labs?** Tiny differences in materials, tools and people's methods can change results. Scientists are still finding the best ways to make tests repeatable everywhere.

- Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3, measured the water that came through the cotton hanky with her small spoon. It filled 4 spoons. Her mother measured the same water with her big spoon. It filled 2 spoons. Then they both used the medicine cup, and both got 12 ml. Why did the medicine cup agree, when the spoons did not? **Because a millilitre is the same size for everyone, but spoons are not.**. Lakshmi's spoon is smaller than her mother's, so the same water fills more of her spoons. A millilitre is a standard unit. It is exactly the same amount on every medicine cup in the world. So both of them got 12 ml. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L3`)
- Here are some made-up results from a width test. Strips 1 cm wide held about 9 coins. Strips 2 cm wide held about 20. If the pattern keeps going, how many will 4 cm strips hold? Then the 4 cm strips held 44. Was the pattern right? Why might the 1 cm strips have held a little less than half of 20? **The pattern predicts about 40 coins for 4 cm. The real middle value was 44, which is close. The 1 cm strips may have held less than half because their cut edges are a bigger part of them.**. Doubling the width roughly doubles the coins: 20 + 20 = 40. The test gave 44, so the pattern was close but not exact. When you cut a strip, the fibres at both edges are cut and weakened. In a 1 cm strip, those damaged edges are a big part of the strip. In a 4 cm strip, they are a small part. So narrow strips hold a little less than the pattern says. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Brand A kitchen towel costs 120 rupees for 60 sheets. Brand C costs 60 rupees for 60 sheets. How much does one sheet of each cost? Which is the better buy, if both soak up about the same? **Brand A costs 2 rupees a sheet. Brand C costs 1 rupee a sheet. Brand C is the better buy.**. To find the cost of one sheet, share the price by the number of sheets. 120 divided by 60 is 2, so Brand A is 2 rupees a sheet. 60 divided by 60 is 1, so Brand C is 1 rupee a sheet. If they soak up about the same, Brand C gives the same job for half the money. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Look at the medicine cup in the picture. The big numbers are 0, 10 and 20 millilitres. Each small mark is 2 millilitres. How much rain came through the cotton hanky? **12 millilitres.**. The water is past the 10 line. It reaches the first small mark above 10. Each small mark is 2 millilitres. So it is 10 + 2 = 12 millilitres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- Five strips of the same kitchen towel held 52, 61, 58, 40 and 60 coins. A shop wants a towel strip that never tears below 45 coins. The middle value is 58. Is this towel safe to sell for that job? What would you tell the shop? **No. The middle value is well above 45, but one strip tore at 40. I would tell the shop that some strips are weaker than 45, so they need a stronger towel or more testing.**. In order the results are 40, 52, 58, 60, 61, so the middle value is 58. But the shop's question is not about the middle. It is about the weakest strip. One strip in five held only 40, which is below 45. So about one strip in five might fail. A middle value hides the weakest result, and for safety the weakest result is the one that matters. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)

## Warm-up
- In a fair test, how many things do you change? → Only one. Everything else stays the same.
- Why do we repeat a test three times? → Because one try can be a fluke.
- Three tries gave 9, 15 and 14. What is the middle value? → 14. In order: 9, 14, 15.
- Is a plastic bag waterproof or absorbent? → Waterproof.
- Is a kitchen towel waterproof or absorbent? → Absorbent.
- What unit do we use to measure water in a measuring cup? → Millilitres, written ml.
- Why does a packet tear easily at its notch? → The pull gathers at the sharp tip of the notch.
- Where does a paper strip usually start to tear? → At its weakest spot, like a thin patch or a tiny nick.
- Which is harder: a steel key or chalk? → The steel key. It scratches the chalk.
- Who tested water's skin at her kitchen sink, and got into the journal Nature in 1891? → Agnes Pockels.
- A raincoat has one pinhole. Where does the rain get in? → Only at the pinhole. The rest is still waterproof.
