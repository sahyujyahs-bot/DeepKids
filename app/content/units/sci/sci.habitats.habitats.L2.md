# Habitats: why a frog lives in a pond and moss grows on a wet wall
`sci.habitats.habitats.L2`

**Summary.** Why does green moss grow on an old brick wall, but not on the shiny granite steps beside it? A habitat is the place where a plant or an animal lives and finds what they need: water, food, air, shelter and the right warmth. A pond, a forest, a desert and the seashore are habitats, and each one suits different living beings. Rocks are part of every habitat. They give shelter, some of them hold water, and over a very long time they crumble into soil. Your child stands a clay diya and a smooth stone in water, puts mustard seeds on top of both, and finds out which one can be a home for a seed.

**Intro.** *Imagine a fish in the hot sand of the Thar desert, and a camel standing in the sea. Why would neither of them be all right for long?* The fish needs water all around, to breathe through gills. The camel needs dry land to walk on and fresh water to drink, and the sea is salty. Each living being has a place that suits their body. That place is called their habitat. In this unit we look at habitats near and far: a pond, a forest, a desert, the seashore, and even a rock. Then we test whether a stone can be a home for a seed.

## Terms
- **Habitat**: A habitat is the place where a plant or an animal lives. It gives them what they need: water, food, air, shelter and the right warmth. *e.g.* A pond is a habitat for frogs and lotus. A forest is a habitat for monkeys and tall trees.
- **Shelter**: A shelter is a place that keeps a living being safe from the hot sun, the rain, the cold or animals that hunt them. *e.g.* A gap between two rocks is a shelter for a lizard. A nest in a tree is a shelter for a baby bird.
- **Desert**: A desert is a very dry place, where rain falls only a little in a whole year. *e.g.* The Thar desert in Rajasthan is a habitat for camels and the khejri tree.
- **Forest**: A forest is a place with many trees growing close together. *e.g.* The forests of the Western Ghats are a habitat for lion-tailed macaques and hornbills.
- **Rock**: Rock is the hard, natural material that the land is made of, deep down. Stones and pebbles are small pieces of rock. *e.g.* Granite is a rock. Many kitchen counters and temple steps are made of granite.
- **Soil**: Soil is the loose top layer of the ground. It is made of tiny bits of rock mixed with bits of dead plants and animals. *e.g.* The soil in a flowerpot holds water for the plant's roots.
- **Moss**: Moss is a small, soft green plant that grows in damp places, often on walls, stones and tree bark. *e.g.* In the Kerala monsoon, green moss covers many old compound walls.

## Explanations
### What a habitat is · *defined*
**Definition:** A habitat is the place where a plant or an animal lives and finds what they need to stay alive: water, food, air, shelter and the right warmth.

Every living being needs water, food and air. They need shelter from the sun, the rain and danger, and the right warmth, not too hot and not too cold. A habitat is a place that gives them all of these. Humans are animals too, and our home and town are our habitat.
- e.g. A pond gives a frog water for her eggs, insects to eat and mud to hide in.
- e.g. A tall tree gives a crow a high, safe place for a nest.
- e.g. Your home gives you water, food, a roof and a safe place to sleep.
### Different habitats, different living beings · *defined*
**Definition:** Each habitat has its own water, warmth, food and shelter, so each one suits different plants and animals.

A pond is wet all the time, so fish, frogs and lotus live there. A forest is shady and damp, so monkeys, hornbills and ferns live there. A desert is hot and dry by day, so camels, desert foxes and thorny plants live there. The seashore is sandy and salty, so crabs and coconut palms live there.
- e.g. Fish breathe with gills, which work only in water.
- e.g. A camel can go for many days without drinking water.
- e.g. In the Sundarbans of West Bengal, mangrove trees grow in salty mud where the sea comes in twice a day.
### Plants in different habitats · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants grow in almost every habitat on land and in water, and each kind of plant grows best in the habitat that suits them.

A lotus grows in a pond. Their roots hold the mud at the bottom, and their wide leaves float on top. A cactus grows in dry, sandy places and stores water in the thick stem. Moss grows on damp walls and stones, and drinks water through their leaves. Plants do not walk, so they live where their seed landed and found what they needed.
- e.g. A money plant can grow in a bottle of water on a windowsill.
- e.g. Aloe vera grows well in a sunny pot that dries out between waterings.
- e.g. Ferns and moss grow on the shady, wet walls of old wells.
### Animals in different habitats · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals live in habitats that give them their food, water and shelter, and their bodies suit those places.

A crab on the seashore digs a hole in the wet sand and hides there when the waves come. A squirrel climbs fast and keeps her nest high in a tree, away from cats. A rock agama, a kind of lizard, lies on a warm rock in the morning sun, and he hides in a crack when danger comes. Animals can move, so they can go and look for a better place when their habitat changes.
- e.g. Ghost crabs run across the beach at Marina in Chennai and dive into their holes.
- e.g. Rock agamas sit on the big boulders at Hampi in Karnataka.
- e.g. Pigeons nest on window ledges in Mumbai, much as their wild relatives nest on cliff ledges.
### Rocks in a habitat · *defined*
**Definition:** Rocks are part of every habitat on land. They give shelter, some of them hold water, and over a very long time they crumble into soil.

Animals hide under rocks and in the cracks between them. Brick and laterite have tiny holes that soak up water, so they stay damp and moss can grow on them. Smooth granite lets the water run off, so it dries fast. Rain, sun and plant roots slowly break rocks into small grains, and the grains mix with bits of dead plants to make soil.
- e.g. In the diya and stone test, the clay soaked up water and the smooth stone stayed dry on top.
- e.g. Laterite, a red rock full of holes, is used for walls in Kerala and Goa. In the monsoon, moss grows on these walls.
- e.g. A peepal seed that lands in a crack in a wall can grow into a small tree.
### Does a seed choose when to start growing? · *debated*
A dry mustard seed can stay the same in a jar for months. Give the seed water and warmth, and in a day or two a tiny root comes out. Seeds sense water and warmth, and decide when it is time to grow. A seed that starts on a dry stone would soon die, so waiting keeps the seed alive. Some scientists say it another way: water starts a chain of changes inside the seed, and nothing is chosen.
- e.g. Mustard seeds in the kitchen jar stay dry and still for months.
- e.g. On the wet clay diya, mustard seeds sprouted in a day or two.
- e.g. In the Thar desert, many seeds wait in the dry sand for the monsoon rain.
- **Scientists who study plant minds:** A seed senses water, warmth and sometimes light, and decides when to start growing. Plants feel, remember and decide, and seeds do too.
- **Many other scientists:** Water getting into a seed switches on the seed's growth, step by step. They call this a response, and do not call it a choice.

## Mostly agreed
- Different habitats hold different plants and animals. *How we know:* People who count living beings in ponds, forests, deserts and seashores find different kinds in each. Fish are found in water, camels in deserts and hornbills in forests.
- Soil is made from rock broken into tiny bits, mixed with bits of dead plants and animals. *How we know:* Look at soil with a magnifying glass, and you see tiny rock grains and bits of old leaves and roots. Scientists have watched rocks slowly crack and crumble in sun, rain and frost.
- Seeds need water to start growing. *How we know:* Dry seeds stay unchanged in a jar for months. Given water and warmth, many kinds sprout in a day or two. In the diya and stone test, the seeds on the wet clay sprouted, and the seeds on the dry stone did not.

## How we found out
**Marjory Stoneman Douglas, an American writer, with the water scientist Garald Parker, The 1940s; her book came out in 1947.** More than 100 years ago, many people in Florida, in the United States, called the Everglades a useless swamp. It was a huge, flat, wet land of tall sawgrass, full of alligators, fish and wading birds. Engineers dug long canals to drain the water away, so that the land could be dried out for farms and towns. A writer called Marjory Stoneman Douglas set out to study the Everglades. She spent about five years reading, travelling and asking scientists questions. One of them, a water scientist called Garald Parker, was finding out where the water moved, and how it filled the ground water that the towns drank. Douglas understood that the Everglades was not still, useless water. It was a very wide, very shallow river, moving slowly south through the grass. In 1947 she published a book called The Everglades: River of Grass. That same year, part of the Everglades became a national park. Douglas spent the rest of her long life, until she was 108, working to protect it.  
*What people believed before:* That the Everglades was a useless swamp of still water, best drained away. Douglas, using what water scientists had found, showed that it was a slow, wide river of grass, a habitat for many living beings, and a source of water for people.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Rocks are bare and dead, so nothing lives on a rock.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A smooth, sunny rock often looks bare. It dries fast, and it has no soil on top.  
  Against: Look closely at old rocks and walls. Moss, ferns and flat, crusty patches called lichens grow on them. Lizards, ants and spiders live in their cracks. Damp rocks, and rocks with cracks, are habitats for many living beings.
- **A desert is empty. Nothing lives there.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: A desert has little water, and in the hot daytime you may see no animals at all.  
  Against: The Thar desert is home to camels, chinkara gazelles, desert foxes, great Indian bustards, lizards, beetles and khejri trees. Many desert animals hide in burrows by day and come out in the cool night.
- **Any animal can live anywhere, if people give them food and water.** (Some people; *debated*)  
  For: Zoos keep animals from far away, and pet fish live in tanks in city flats.  
  Against: A fish tank needs clean water, air pumped in and the right warmth. A zoo must build a small copy of each animal's habitat. Even with food, an animal kept in the wrong place can get ill. So people who look after animals well copy the animal's own habitat as closely as they can.

## Roots
We have looked at habitats near and far, and tested whether a seed can live on a stone. Now we ask what that tells us about rocks, seeds and the places we call home.

**Deep truth.** Our clay diya and smooth stone stood in the same plate of water. Water climbed up through the clay, but not through the stone. Mustard seeds sprouted on the wet clay and dried up on the stone. So a rock is a better home for a plant when the rock can hold water.  
*Habit it breaks:* We split the world into living beings and the bare, non-living places they live on, and we think all rocks are the same hard background. But rocks differ in how much water they hold. Rocks with tiny holes stay damp and suit moss and seeds. Smooth rocks dry fast, and few plants grow on them.  
*Evidence:* In the diya and stone test, the water, the seeds, the place and the time were the same for both. Only what the seeds sat on was different, and only the clay's top stayed wet.

- *who am i:* You are an animal who needs water, food and shelter every day, just like a snail or a frog. Your home is your habitat.
- *what is life:* A seed can wait in a dry jar for months. Life starts again when the seed finds a place that gives water.
- *what is this world:* The world is made of many habitats side by side. Even the rocks are not all the same: some become homes, and some stay bare.

The same rain falls on a brick wall and on a granite step. On the brick, the water stays, and moss grows. On the granite, the water runs off, and the step stays bare. Over hundreds of years, rain and roots break rock into soil, the soil feeds plants, and the plants feed animals, including us. So one rock can, in time, become a pond's mud, a forest floor or the field our rice grows in. Everything is conscious in its own way, the waiting seed and the rock where the seed lands too.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** A teacher called Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to break open a banyan fruit, and then one of the tiny seeds inside. Shvetaketu looks inside the seed and sees nothing. Uddalaka says that from this tiny thing that cannot be seen, a great banyan tree grows. (Our mustard seeds looked like dry dots. Given a wet place, each one sent out a root and leaves. A whole plant was waiting inside, for the right habitat.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 78:** Nothing in the world is softer than water. Yet nothing is better at wearing away what is hard and strong. (Rain is soft, but over hundreds of years it cracks and wears down rock. That is how rock slowly becomes soil, the home of roots and earthworms.)
- Science (settled): Mosses and lichens are among the first living beings to grow on bare rock. Over a very long time they help to crumble the rock and start new soil.
- Science (settled): A few centimetres of new soil can take hundreds of years to form, depending on the rock and the weather.
- Other view, Many scientists: Many scientists say minds belong only to animals with brains. To them, a seed sprouting is a chain of chemical changes started by water, and a rock is simply matter. They agree that habitats matter, but they would not call a seed's waiting a choice.
- Practice, Hold a Stone (3 min): Pick up a small stone and close your hand round it. Shut your eyes. Breathe slowly three times. Feel the stone get warm from your hand. Now open your hand. Touch the stone with one finger. Is it rough or smooth? Would rain stay on it, or run off? Who might live on a stone like this?
- Ask: The stone got warm in your hand. Where did the warmth come from?
- Ask: Soil is made from rock that broke very slowly. Is the rock in a flowerpot's soil very old or very new?
- Ask: A seed waits in a jar for months. Is the seed doing anything while they wait?

## Story: The Snail in the Steel Box
*Farhan, Lakshmi and Ammi are made up. What the snail does in the story is true of garden snails: in dry places they pull into their shell and seal the opening with a thin dry layer, and they come out again when it is damp.*

It was the monsoon in Kochi. Rain fell all morning on the old laterite wall behind Farhan's building. The wall was red and rough, and green moss grew all over it.

Farhan was seven. His cousin Lakshmi was ten, and she was staying with them for the holidays. Lakshmi liked to look at small living beings very closely.

"Farhan, come and see," she called from the wall. A snail was moving slowly over the wet moss. Her shell was brown, with a curl like a whirl of jalebi. Two long feelers stood up on her head.

"I want to keep her," said Farhan. "She can be my pet."

He ran upstairs and came back with an empty steel tiffin box. He put the snail inside. He put in a crumb of biscuit too, in case she was hungry. Then he closed the lid and took the box home.

Lakshmi said nothing. She only said, "Let's look at her tomorrow."

Farhan put the box on the sunny windowsill, so that the snail would be warm.

The next morning, he opened the box. The snail was not moving. She had pulled her whole body inside her shell. Across the opening of the shell was a thin, white, dry layer, like a paper door.

"Lakshmi! Is she dead?" Farhan asked. His voice shook.

Lakshmi held the shell up to the light. "I think she is still alive," she said. "Feel the box."

Farhan touched the inside of the steel box. It was warm and dry. The biscuit crumb was dry and hard.

"A snail's body is soft and wet," said Lakshmi. "In a dry place, the water in her body goes out into the air. So she goes inside her shell and makes a door to keep her water in. Then she waits."

"Waits for what?" asked Farhan.

"For the rain," said Lakshmi. "What did the wall have that the box did not?"

Farhan thought. "The wall was wet. It had moss. It was cool, and it was in the shade."

"Yes," said Lakshmi. "The wall is her habitat. It gives her water, cool shade, and moss and old leaves to eat. Your box gave her a roof and a crumb. But it did not give her what her body needs."

Farhan's mother, Ammi, came into the room with a cup of tea. "Why not take her back to her wall?" she said.

So they went down together. Lakshmi showed Farhan how to put the snail on a wet leaf at the foot of the wall, in the shade. They sprinkled a little water over the shell from a tumbler. Then they sat on the step and waited.

For a long time, nothing happened. Then the white door at the opening of the shell went soft. Slowly, a little grey head pushed out. Two feelers rolled out, longer and longer. The snail began to move up the wet red wall, leaving a shiny line behind her.

"She came out!" whispered Farhan.

"She came out because her habitat was here," said Lakshmi.

Farhan looked at the wall. Everything on it was wet, the moss, the ferns in the cracks, the rough red rock itself. Then he looked at the granite step where they were sitting. It was smooth and grey, and it was already drying in the wind.

"Lakshmi," he said, "why is there no moss on the step?"

Lakshmi smiled. "That," she said, "is a very good question. Shall we find out?"

**For the parent:** Farhan finds that a habitat is not just a place with a roof. The wet wall gave the snail everything her body needed, and the dry box did not.

- Ask: Why did the snail close her shell in the steel box?
- Ask: What did the wall give the snail that the box did not?
- Ask: Why do you think there was moss on the wall, but not on the granite step?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Diya and the Stone: can a seed live on a rock? (25 min, 3 days)
- **question:** A clay diya and a smooth stone stand in the same water. Can mustard seeds sprout on top of each of them?
- **predict:** Guess first. On which top will the seeds sprout: the clay diya, the smooth stone, both or neither? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both stand in the same plate, in the same water, in the same place, for the same time. Both get 10 seeds from the same jar. Only one thing is different: what the seeds sit on.
- **record:** Draw the diya and the stone in the plate. Draw the seeds on each top as they looked on day 3. Write the number that sprouted under each.
- **results table:** What the seeds sat on | Top wet or dry after 15 minutes? | Seeds sprouted on day 2 (out of 10) | Seeds sprouted on day 3 (out of 10)
- **schedule:** day 1: Drop water on both, stand them in the plate, wait 15 minutes, feel the tops, and put 10 seeds on each. (15 min); day 2: Top up the water. Count the sprouted seeds on each top. (5 min); day 3: Count again. Say what you found, and why. (5 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn the diya upside down. 2. Let your child drop one spoon of water on the diya and one on the stone. 3. Watch both for a minute.  
  *Parent says:* Drop a spoon of water on each. Watch closely. Where does the water go on the diya? And on the stone?  
  *Child mode:* Drop a spoon of water on each. Watch. Where does the water go?
  - Ask: What happened to the water on the clay? And on the stone? (good: The clay drank it up and went dark.; The water sat on the stone, or ran off.)
  - Note: Unpainted clay soaks up water at once and turns dark. On a smooth stone the drop sits in a bead or runs down the side. If your diya is painted or shiny, use a piece of broken clay pot or brick instead.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the diya, upside down, and the stone side by side in the plate. 2. Pour water into the plate until it is one finger deep. 3. Put 20 seeds in the small bowl with a spoon of water, to soak while you wait.  
  *Parent says:* Seeds will sit on top of each one. On which top will they sprout? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Seeds will sit on top. Where will they sprout? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Where do you think the seeds will sprout? Why? (good: On the diya, because the clay drinks water.; On both, because both are in water.; On neither, because there is no soil.)
  - Note: Write down your child's guess and their reason. Any guess is fine. The test will answer it.
  - Wait: Leave them for 15 minutes.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the diya's colour. 2. Touch the top of the diya, then the top of the stone. 3. Put 10 soaked seeds on each top.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the clay. The dark colour has climbed up. Touch both tops. Which one is wet? Now let's put 10 soaked seeds on each.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the clay. Touch both tops. Which is wet? Put 10 seeds on each.
  - Ask: How did the water get to the top of the diya? (good: The clay pulled it up.; It climbed up through the clay.; The clay has tiny holes that soak it up.)
  - Note: The water climbs through tiny holes in the clay, the way water climbs up a cloth dipped in a bucket. The stone's top stays dry. Put the plate in a bright place, but not in hot afternoon sun.
  - Wait: Leave the plate for two days. Each morning, top up the water so the plate stays one finger deep.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2 and day 3, count the seeds with a tiny white root or green leaves. Count each top. Write the numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count. How many seeds on the clay have sprouted? How many on the stone?  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouted seeds on each top. Write the numbers.
  - Ask: What do the seeds on the stone look like? (good: Dry and hard.; The same as before.; They did not grow.)
  - Note: Usually most seeds on the clay sprout in one or two days, and the seeds on the stone dry out and do not sprout. In very damp monsoon weather, a few stone seeds may sprout. Then compare how many and how fast. If no seeds sprout anywhere in three days, the seeds may be old: try methi seeds or a new packet. If the diya's top looks dry by day 2, the room is very hot or windy, so move the plate to a shadier spot.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say three things: what they think, what they saw, and why. Then look back at their guess together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you found. What did you see? Why do you think the seeds grew on one and not the other? Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. What did you see? Why did it happen?
  - Check: Why did the seeds sprout on the clay diya, and not on the smooth stone? → **The clay soaked up water and kept the seeds wet**
  - Think: Where near your home might moss grow? Look for a damp, shady wall.

## Worksheets
### Habitats (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each living being to their habitat.  
   **Answer:** Camel → Desert; Lotus → Pond; Hornbill → Forest; Ghost crab → Seashore. *Why:* A camel suits the dry desert, a lotus grows from pond mud, a hornbill eats forest fruit and nests in tall trees, and a ghost crab digs in wet sand by the sea.
2. (sort) Sort these into two groups: places that stay damp, and places that dry fast.  
   **Answer:** an old brick wall in the rain → Stays damp; a shiny granite step → Dries fast; moss under a dripping tap → Stays damp; a hot sandy road → Dries fast; soil under a flowerpot → Stays damp; a steel plate in the sun → Dries fast. *Why:* Brick, moss and soil hold water in tiny gaps, so they stay damp. Granite, sand in the sun and steel let water run off or dry quickly.
3. (draw) Draw your diya and your stone in the plate of water. Draw the seeds on each top as they looked on day 3.  
   **Answer:** The diya has sprouted seeds with little roots or green leaves. The stone has dry seeds that did not grow.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the difference the child saw, and the water in the plate under both.
4. (mcq) Remember the diya and stone test? After 15 minutes in the plate of water, which top was wet?  
   Options: The top of the clay diya / The top of the smooth stone / Both tops were dry  
   **Answer:** The top of the clay diya. *Why:* Water climbed up through the tiny holes in the clay, so the diya's top turned dark and damp. The smooth stone let no water climb up. If your stone's top was wet, check that no water was splashed on it.
5. (truefalse) Nothing can live on a rock.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Moss, ferns and crusty lichens grow on damp rocks. Lizards, ants and spiders live in rock cracks.
6. (count) Count the living beings in this pond picture: 3 fish, 2 frogs, 1 lotus plant, 1 stone and 1 plastic bottle.  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* 3 fish, 2 frogs and 1 lotus make 6 living beings. The stone and the bottle are not alive. The lotus is a plant, and plants are living beings.
7. (label) Write or draw one plant and one animal who live in each habitat.  
   **Answer:** ['pond: lotus, frog or fish', 'desert: cactus, khejri, camel or desert fox', 'forest: tall trees, ferns, monkey or hornbill']. *Why:* Any plant or animal who really lives there is right. A strong answer says what the habitat gives them.
8. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: why does a camel do well in the desert but not in the sea?  
   **Answer:** A camel lives on dry land. Wide, padded feet help a camel walk on sand, and the sea is deep and salty. A camel cannot drink salty water.. *Why:* A strong answer links what the camel needs (dry land, fresh water) to what the sea lacks.
9. (perspective) Two friends in Madurai find a snail on a wet wall.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, for example: 'Revathi, because the wet wall gives the snail water and food.' Then it is fair to the other side: Divya wants to care for the snail, and looking closely teaches us a lot.. *Why:* Both friends care. A snail can be watched for a short time in a damp box with leaves, and then put back. A dry box away from her wall would harm her.
10. (odd) Which is the odd one out? Think about where each one lives.  
   Options: Fish / Frog / Lotus / Camel  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Fish, frogs and lotus all live in or by a pond. A camel lives in the dry desert.
### Habitats (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (fill) A habitat gives a living being water, food, air, ______ and the right warmth.  
   **Answer:** shelter. *Why:* Shelter keeps a living being safe from the sun, the rain and danger. A nest, a burrow and a rock crack are all shelters.
2. (mcq) Meena, a girl in Class 2, puts a cactus in a pot of water that is always full. What will most likely happen?  
   Options: The cactus roots will rot / The cactus will grow faster than ever / The cactus will turn into a lotus  
   **Answer:** The cactus roots will rot. *Why:* A cactus lives in dry places and stores water in their stem. Roots that sit in water all the time can rot. A plant does best in a habitat like their own.
3. (write) Look at your diya and stone test. Why did the seeds on the stone not sprout? Use what you saw.  
   **Answer:** The stone's top stayed dry, because water could not climb up through the stone. Seeds need water to start growing.. *Why:* A strong answer uses evidence from the test (the dry top, the dry seeds) and gives the reason (seeds need water).
4. (draw) Draw a rock with three living beings on it, under it or in its cracks.  
   **Answer:** For example: moss on top, a lizard in a crack and ants underneath.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts each living being in a place on the rock that suits them: moss where it is damp, a lizard on the warm top or in a crack, ants underneath.
5. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, to show how rock becomes soil.  
   **Answer:** ['A big rock cracks in the sun and rain', 'The cracks break off small stones', 'The stones wear down into tiny grains', 'The grains mix with dead leaves and become soil']. *Why:* Rock breaks slowly into smaller and smaller pieces. The tiny grains mix with bits of dead plants to make soil. This takes a very long time.
6. (perspective) A builder in Pune is making a new block of flats on land that has an old pond.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one idea and gives a reason, then thinks about the frogs, the fish and the people who need homes.. *Why:* People need homes, and so do frogs and fish. There is no single right answer. A good answer is fair to both.
7. (truefalse) A crab from the seashore would do well in a forest pond.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A ghost crab lives on salty seashore sand and digs holes there. A forest pond has fresh water and no beach sand.
8. (label) Label what each habitat gives: write water, shade, sand or trees by each picture.  
   **Answer:** ['forest: trees and shade', 'pond: water', 'desert: sand', 'seashore: sand and salty water']. *Why:* Each habitat gives different things, so each suits different living beings.
9. (odd) Which is the odd one out? Think about water.  
   Options: A clay pot / A brick / A laterite stone / A smooth granite step  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Clay, brick and laterite are full of tiny holes, so they soak up water. Smooth granite lets the water run off.
10. (write) Your flat is a habitat. Name two other animals who live in it or on it, and say what each one finds there.  
   **Answer:** For example: ants find crumbs in the kitchen. A gecko finds insects near the light.. *Why:* A strong answer names real animals and links each to what the flat gives them: food, shelter or water.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a habitat?  
   Options: The place where a living being lives and finds what they need / A kind of food / A kind of rock  
   **Answer:** The place where a living being lives and finds what they need. *Why:* A habitat gives a living being water, food, air, shelter and the right warmth.
2. (mcq) Which of these does a habitat give a living being?  
   Options: Water and shelter / Toys / Money  
   **Answer:** Water and shelter. *Why:* Every habitat gives the living beings there what they need, such as water, food, air and shelter.
3. (mcq) Who lives in the Thar desert?  
   Options: A camel / A lotus / A ghost crab  
   **Answer:** A camel. *Why:* Camels live in the dry desert. A lotus needs a pond, and a ghost crab needs the seashore.
4. (mcq) Where does a lotus grow?  
   Options: In a pond / On a dry rock / In the desert sand  
   **Answer:** In a pond. *Why:* A lotus has roots in the mud at the bottom of a pond, and leaves that float on the water.
5. (mcq) Why can a fish not live on land?  
   Options: A fish breathes with gills, which work only in water / A fish is too big / A fish does not like the sun  
   **Answer:** A fish breathes with gills, which work only in water. *Why:* Gills take oxygen from water. Out of water, a fish cannot breathe.
6. (mcq) A lizard slips into a crack between two rocks when a bird flies over. What does the rock give the lizard?  
   Options: Shelter / Salty water / Fruit  
   **Answer:** Shelter. *Why:* A crack in a rock is a shelter. Rocks give shelter, some hold water, and over a very long time they crumble into soil.
7. (mcq) What is soil made from?  
   Options: Tiny bits of rock and bits of dead plants / Only sand / Only water  
   **Answer:** Tiny bits of rock and bits of dead plants. *Why:* Over a long time, rocks crumble into tiny grains. These mix with bits of dead plants and animals to make soil.
8. (mcq) Nila, a girl in Class 2, says before the test: "The seeds will sprout on both the diya and the stone." What is Nila doing?  
   Options: Making a guess to test / Counting the seeds / Cleaning the plate  
   **Answer:** Making a guess to test. *Why:* A guess you make before a test is called a prediction. The test then shows whether the guess was right.
9. (mcq) In the diya and stone test, both stood in the same water. Why is that important?  
   Options: So that only what the seeds sat on was different / So that the plate looked nice / So that the seeds could swim  
   **Answer:** So that only what the seeds sat on was different. *Why:* If only one thing is different, we know that thing made the difference. That is a fair test.
10. (mcq) You find a snail on a wet wall. What is the kind thing to do?  
   Options: Look closely, then leave the snail on the wall / Put the snail in a dry box in the sun / Take the snail to the desert  
   **Answer:** Look closely, then leave the snail on the wall. *Why:* The wet wall is the snail's habitat. It gives the snail water, shade and food.

## Applications
- Zoos and animal rescue centres build a copy of each animal's own habitat, with the right water, shade and hiding places.
- Builders who keep old ponds, trees and rock outcrops on their land keep habitats for frogs, birds and lizards.
- Gardeners grow each plant in a place that suits them: ferns in shade, cactus in dry sun, lotus in a tub of water.
- Farmers know that good soil takes a very long time to form, so they try not to let rain wash it away.

## Spotted around us
- Green moss and small ferns on old laterite walls in Kerala and Goa during the monsoon.
- Rock agama lizards sunning themselves on the great boulders of Hampi in Karnataka.
- Mangrove trees standing in salty mud at Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu and in the Sundarbans of West Bengal.
- Camels and thorny khejri trees in the Thar desert of Rajasthan.
- Lotus and water hyacinth on village ponds in Assam and Odisha.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of living beings live in the cracks of one old wall?** Many of the tiniest beings in moss and cracks have never been counted or named, so nobody knows.
- **Exactly how long does it take to make one finger-width of new soil?** It depends on the rock, the rain, the warmth and the plants. Scientists give different answers for different places, from hundreds of years to much longer.
- **How does a seed sense that the time is right to grow?** Scientists know that water, warmth and sometimes light matter. How a seed puts these together and decides is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the diya and stone test, Selvi put 10 seeds on the diya. On day 3, 8 had sprouted. How many did not sprout? **2 seeds did not sprout.**. 10 seeds take away the 8 that sprouted leaves 2. 10 - 8 = 2. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Nila counted the sprouted seeds on day 2 and day 3. On the diya: 6 on day 2, 9 on day 3. On the stone: 0 on day 2, 0 on day 3. What pattern do you see? What would you guess for day 4? **The diya's count went up, from 6 to 9. The stone stayed at 0. On day 4, the diya might have 9 or 10, and the stone still 0.**. More seeds sprouted on the wet clay each day. On the dry stone, nothing changed, so we expect it to stay at 0. The diya cannot go above 10, because there were only 10 seeds. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
- Farida made a tally of animals in her flat in one day: ants, a lot; 1 gecko; 3 pigeons on the ledge; 2 spiders. Leave out the ants. How many animals did she count? Which one did she see most? **6 animals: 1 + 3 + 2 = 6. She saw pigeons the most, 3.**. Add the gecko, the pigeons and the spiders: 1 + 3 + 2 = 6. The biggest number is 3, so pigeons were seen most. Ants are left out because there were too many to count. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
