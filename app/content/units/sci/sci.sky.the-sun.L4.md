# The Sun up close: layers, spots, storms and the wind that blows past the Earth
`sci.sky.the-sun.L4`

**Summary.** A picture of the Sun shows a smooth, bright ball with a sharp edge, as if it had a skin. But the Sun has no ground at all. It is hot gas from the middle to the outside, and its edge is only the place where the glowing gas stops being too cloudy to see through. Dark spots drift across the face, vanish at one edge and come back about two weeks later, which shows that the Sun spins. Your child makes a cloud to find out what an edge really is, boils a pot to see how the Sun churns, splits light into colours, and builds a spinning model to follow a spot. The unit ends with a surprise: the Sun's wind blows past the Earth, so we live inside the Sun's outer atmosphere.

**Intro.** *Imagine you could fly to the Sun in a spacecraft and land. Where would you put your feet? The Sun looks like a ball with a skin, but there is no ground to stand on. It is gas all the way down. So what is the edge that we see in every picture?* The edge is real to our eyes, but it is not a wall. It is the place where the hot gas becomes clear enough for light to get out and reach us. Above that edge the Sun does not stop. Thin, hot gas goes on for millions of kilometres, and a wind from it blows past the Earth. In this unit you make your own glowing cloud to find out what an edge is, and you follow dark spots across the Sun to prove that it turns.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Core**: The very centre of the Sun, where hydrogen joins into helium and makes energy. It is about 15 million degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Almost all of the Sun's energy is made in its core, in the inner quarter of the way out from the centre.
- **Radiative zone**: The thick layer around the core where energy moves outwards as light, being taken in and sent out again again and again. *e.g.* Energy takes thousands of years to cross this zone, because it keeps changing direction.
- **Convection**: Hot material rising, cooling and sinking again, so that it goes round in a loop. *e.g.* Water in a pot on the stove rises in the middle, spreads out, cools and sinks at the edge.
- **Convection zone**: The outer layer inside the Sun, where hot gas boils upwards in huge loops. *e.g.* The Sun's convection zone is the outer third of the Sun, from the surface down.
- **Photosphere**: The layer we call the Sun's surface. It is where the gas becomes clear enough for light to escape into space. It is about 5,500 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Every normal picture of the Sun shows the photosphere. It is only a few hundred kilometres thick.
- **Granule**: One bubble of hot gas rising at the Sun's surface. A granule is about as wide as 1,000 kilometres, and it lasts about ten minutes. *e.g.* A close-up of the Sun looks like boiling rice or a pot of dal, with thousands of granules.
- **Chromosphere**: A thin layer above the photosphere. It glows pinkish red and can be seen only when the Moon covers the Sun in an eclipse. *e.g.* In a total solar eclipse, a thin red rim shows round the edge of the Moon. That is the chromosphere.
- **Corona**: The Sun's outer atmosphere. It is very thin, very hot, over a million degrees Celsius, and it reaches millions of kilometres out. *e.g.* In a total eclipse, the corona shows as a silvery-white glow round the black Moon.
- **Plasma**: A gas so hot that its atoms are broken apart into tiny charged pieces, so it can carry electricity and feel magnets. *e.g.* The Sun is made of plasma, and so is lightning for a moment.
- **Sunspot**: A patch on the Sun's surface that is cooler than the gas around it, so it looks dark. Sunspots are made by strong magnetic fields. *e.g.* A big sunspot group can be wider than the Earth.
- **Magnetic field**: The invisible space round a magnet where it pulls on iron or on charged pieces. The Sun and the Earth both have one. *e.g.* A compass needle turns because it feels the Earth's magnetic field.
- **Solar cycle**: The rise and fall in the number of sunspots, over about 11 years on average. *e.g.* In a year of many spots, the Sun is busy with flares. In a year of few spots, it is quiet.
- **Solar flare**: A sudden flash of light and energy from the Sun, set off by a snap in its magnetic field. *e.g.* A big flare sends a burst of light that reaches the Earth in about 8 minutes.
- **Coronal mass ejection**: A huge cloud of plasma thrown out from the Sun's corona. It takes about a day to a few days to cross space. *e.g.* A cloud that hits the Earth's magnetic field can make a space storm.
- **Solar wind**: A steady stream of charged pieces flowing outwards from the Sun's corona in every direction. *e.g.* The solar wind passes the Earth at about 400 kilometres in every second.
- **Aurora**: Glowing light in the sky near the poles, made when charged pieces from space hit gas high in the Earth's air. *e.g.* Green and red curtains of light over the far north are the aurora.
- **Space weather**: Changes in the Sun's wind and eruptions that can affect the Earth, its satellites and its power lines. *e.g.* A strong space storm can disturb a compass and radio signals.
- **Spectrum**: All the colours that light is made of, spread out in a row. *e.g.* A rainbow is a spectrum of sunlight made by raindrops.
- **Spectral line**: A thin bright or dark line in a spectrum. Each kind of atom makes its own pattern of lines. *e.g.* Dark lines in the Sun's spectrum tell us which atoms are in the Sun.
- **Atmosphere**: The layer of gas round a planet or star. *e.g.* The Earth's atmosphere is the air. The Sun's atmosphere is its chromosphere and corona.

## Explanations
### The Sun is built in layers, from the core to the corona · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun has six main layers. From the inside out they are the core, the radiative zone, the convection zone, the photosphere, the chromosphere and the corona.

In the core, at about 15 million degrees Celsius, hydrogen joins into helium and makes the Sun's energy. Around the core is the radiative zone, so packed that energy is taken in and sent out again again and again, zigzagging outwards. In the next layer, the convection zone, hot gas boils upwards like water in a pot. At the top of that boiling layer is the photosphere, the layer we call the surface. It is about 5,500 degrees Celsius. Above it lies the thin chromosphere and then the huge, thin corona. The Sun is about 109 Earths wide. The photosphere is only a few hundred kilometres thick, thinner compared with the whole Sun than the skin of an apple is compared with the apple. All six layers are gas, with no solid ground anywhere.
- e.g. The core, radiative zone and convection zone are hidden inside. We learn about them from sound waves that shake the Sun and from tiny particles called neutrinos.
- e.g. The chromosphere and corona are easy to see only in a total solar eclipse, when the Moon blocks the bright photosphere.
### The Sun has no solid surface: its edge is where light can escape · *defined*
**Definition:** The photosphere is called the Sun's surface, but it is not a skin. It is the layer where the gas becomes clear enough for light to travel out into space.

Deeper inside the Sun, the gas is so thick and glowing that light cannot get through. It is taken in and sent out again after a tiny distance. Light from deep down never reaches us directly. Near the photosphere, the gas becomes thin enough that light can escape into space. When we look at the Sun, we see back only as far as that layer, and we cannot see deeper. So the sharp edge is where we stop being able to see in. It is a bit like looking into a glass of milky water. You can see only a short way in, and the milky water seems to have a surface of glowing white even though you could put your hand into it. Above the photosphere the gas goes on, but it is so thin that it gives out very little light, so we do not see it in ordinary pictures.
- e.g. A thick fog around a street lamp seems to have a round edge. The edge is only where the fog's light has faded.
- e.g. From far away, a cloud on a hill looks like a solid shape, but if you walk into it you find only thin mist.
### The Sun boils: granules and giant loops of gas · *defined*
**Definition:** Convection is hot material rising, cooling and sinking again in a loop. The Sun's outer layer is boiling with convection.

Heat from the inside makes hot gas rise in bright bubbles to the surface. At the top it gives out its light, cools, spreads sideways and sinks again through dark lanes. Each bubble is called a granule. A granule is about 1,000 kilometres wide, which is about as far as from Chennai to Mumbai in a straight line, and it lasts only about ten minutes. There are millions of granules at once. The surface of the Sun looks like a pot of dal bubbling on a stove. You can see the same loops in a pot of water that is about to boil. Granules are small. The Sun also has much bigger loops that we cannot see so easily. Together they carry the Sun's heat out through the convection zone.
- e.g. A pot of sambar just before it boils moves in the same way, with rising bubbles in the middle and sinking lanes at the sides.
- e.g. A hot tea cup has steam that rises, and the cooler tea at the edge sinks.
### Sunspots are cooler patches held by magnetic fields · *defined*
**Definition:** A sunspot is a patch of the photosphere that is cooler than the gas round it, held that way by a strong magnetic field.

A sunspot looks dark, but only next to the brighter Sun around it. It is still hot, about 4,000 degrees Celsius or a little more, which is hot enough to glow brightly on its own. The gas around it is about 5,500 degrees. Sunspots form where bundles of magnetic field push up from inside the Sun. The strong field slows the boiling gas that would carry heat up, so the patch cools. Spots can be small or wider than the Earth, and a spot lasts from a few days to a few weeks. They often come in groups. Chinese court astronomers wrote down dark spots on the Sun for about two thousand years, seen through haze or dust. In 1611 Galileo and others showed them with the Sun's picture on paper.
- e.g. Ordinary sunspots are best seen by projecting the Sun's picture on paper, or in a picture from a spacecraft.
- e.g. Never look at the Sun to find sunspots.
### The Sun spins, and its middle turns faster than its poles · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun turns once on its axis about every 25 days at its middle and about every 35 days near its poles.

Sunspots appear at the Sun's eastern edge, move across its face for about two weeks, and vanish at the western edge. If a spot lasts long enough, it comes back at the eastern edge about two weeks later. That is how we know the Sun turns like a ball. A spot near the middle of the Sun moves faster than a spot near the pole, which shows something strange. A solid ball turns all in one piece, so every part takes the same time. The Sun takes about 25 days near its middle and about 35 days near the poles, so it cannot be solid. It is a ball of gas, and gas can turn at different speeds in different places. This is called differential rotation. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, and they turn in a similar way.
- e.g. Stir your tea and see the middle spin faster than the edges. A whirl of gas can turn this way too.
- e.g. From Earth, the Sun seems to take about 27 days to turn, a little longer than 25, because the Earth is moving round the Sun as well.
### The sunspot cycle: busy years and quiet years · *defined*
**Definition:** The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of about 11 years.

In 1843 Heinrich Schwabe, a German pharmacist who loved astronomy, announced that he had seen the number of sunspots rise and fall in a rhythm of about ten years. He had drawn the Sun on every clear day for about 17 years. Today we call it the solar cycle. The average is about 11 years, but a cycle can be shorter or longer. The Sun's magnetic field twists up and tangles over the years. At the busiest time, the solar maximum, there are many spots, flares and eruptions. At the quietest, the solar minimum, there may be days with no spot. Later the Sun's whole magnetic field flips, so that north and south swap, and a full magnetic cycle takes about 22 years. Scientists still cannot predict exactly how strong the next cycle will be.
- e.g. Kodaikanal Solar Observatory in Tamil Nadu has kept pictures and drawings of the Sun for more than a hundred years, which lets scientists study many cycles.
- e.g. More flares and eruptions mean more chance of space weather around the solar maximum.
### Flares and giant eruptions · *defined*
**Definition:** A solar flare is a sudden flash of light and energy. A coronal mass ejection is a huge cloud of plasma thrown out from the corona. They are two different things, and they often happen together.

Near a sunspot group, magnetic field lines can snap and join up in a new way, like a stretched rubber band that breaks. The energy that was stored in the field is let out in minutes. That is a flare. It gives out a flash of light and X-rays that reaches the Earth in about 8 minutes. A coronal mass ejection can throw out a billion tonnes of plasma, a cloud wider than the Sun itself. It takes about a day to a few days to cross space. On 1 September 1859, an English amateur astronomer called Richard Carrington was drawing sunspots when he saw a patch of bright white light flash up on the Sun. Within about a day, the sky glowed with aurora as far from the poles as the tropics, and telegraph lines failed in many places. It is called the Carrington Event, and it was the first time anyone linked an eruption on the Sun with an effect on the Earth.
- e.g. The strongest flares are given letters. An X-class flare is the biggest kind.
- e.g. Flares can switch off radio signals on the sunlit side of the Earth for a short time.
### The solar wind: the Earth is inside the Sun's atmosphere · *defined*
**Definition:** The solar wind is a steady stream of charged pieces that flows out from the Sun's corona in every direction, past all the planets.

The corona is so hot, more than a million degrees, that the Sun's pull cannot hold it. It streams outward as a wind. Near the Earth, the wind blows at about 400 kilometres in every second, and sometimes faster. It carries a weak magnetic field with it. This wind fills the whole solar system and blows a huge bubble of the Sun's gas round all the planets. The Earth is inside this bubble, so we are inside the Sun's atmosphere. The wind from the Sun also pushes the tails of comets so they always point away from the Sun. NASA's Parker Solar Probe, launched in 2018, flew through the corona in 2021, the first spacecraft to touch the Sun's atmosphere. Voyager 1 left the Sun's wind bubble in 2012, according to NASA, after 35 years of flight.
- e.g. The wind takes a few days to reach the Earth from the Sun, much slower than light, which takes 8 minutes.
- e.g. A comet's tail points away from the Sun even when the comet is moving away from it, because the wind pushes the tail.
### Aurora: the Earth's magnetic field guides the wind to the poles · *defined*
**Definition:** An aurora is light made when charged pieces from space hit gas high in the Earth's air. The Earth's magnetic field steers them towards the poles.

The Earth has a magnetic field, as a compass shows. It pushes most of the solar wind round the Earth like a stone in a stream, and it forms a protective bubble called the magnetosphere. Some charged pieces slip in and follow the field lines down towards the north and south poles. High in the air, they hit oxygen and nitrogen atoms. The atoms light up for a moment, like the gas in a neon sign. Oxygen glows green lower down and red higher up. Nitrogen can glow blue or purple. This makes glowing curtains in the sky near the poles. In a strong storm the aurora shows much nearer to the equator. In May 2024, the Indian Institute of Astrophysics reported a faint red aurora seen from the Hanle dark-sky site in Ladakh.
- e.g. The northern lights and the southern lights are the same event, in two places.
- e.g. A compass needle can wobble during a strong space storm.
### Space weather, and how India's Aditya-L1 watches the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** Space weather means the changing wind and eruptions from the Sun and their effects near the Earth. Spacecraft watch the Sun to warn us.

A big storm from the Sun can damage satellites, disturb radio and GPS signals, and push extra electric current into long power lines. In March 1989, a space storm helped to cause a power cut across the Canadian province of Quebec that lasted about nine hours. Astronauts and passengers on polar flights can also get a bigger dose of radiation. To get a warning, countries watch the Sun all the time. India's Aditya-L1, launched from Sriharikota in September 2023, was placed near a point called L1, about 1.5 million kilometres from the Earth towards the Sun. From there, the Earth never blocks its view, and it studies the Sun's layers and the wind. A forecast of space weather works a little like a weather forecast, but it is far less exact, and scientists are still learning.
- e.g. Satellite companies sometimes switch their satellites into a safe mode when a storm is coming.
- e.g. Pigeons and some other animals use the Earth's magnetic field to find their way, and scientists are studying whether storms confuse them.
### Reading sunlight: the Sun's barcode of dark lines · *defined*
**Definition:** When sunlight is spread out into its colours, thin dark lines cut across the rainbow. Each kind of atom takes out its own pattern of lines, so the lines tell us what the Sun is made of.

In 1814, Joseph von Fraunhofer, a German glassmaker, spread sunlight with a prism and saw hundreds of thin dark lines across the colours. He mapped about 570 of them. Nobody knew what they meant. In about 1859, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen heated different substances in a flame and found that each gave its own pattern of lines. Sodium, the atom in salt, made a pair of bright yellow lines in exactly the place where the Sun had a pair of dark lines. That meant sodium was in the Sun's outer layers, taking out that light. Today astronomers use the same method on every star. The lines also show how hot a star is, how fast it moves, and how strong its magnetic field is. Light is a message, and the lines are the writing.
- e.g. A candle flame, a neon sign and the Sun each make a different pattern of colours when split.
- e.g. Lines also let astronomers find the helium in the Sun, which was first found there in 1868, before anyone knew it on Earth.
### The colour of a star tells its temperature · *defined*
**Definition:** Very hot things glow white or blue, and cooler things glow red or orange. The same is true of stars.

A coil in an electric heater first glows dull red, then orange as it gets hotter. A flame from a gas stove burns blue when it is very hot. In the same way, a cooler star looks reddish, a middle star like the Sun looks yellow-white, and a very hot star looks blue-white. The Sun's surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius. A red star may be about 3,000 degrees, and the hottest blue stars have a surface above 20,000 degrees. Astronomers call the Sun a yellow dwarf, but the name is a little wrong. The Sun looks white from space, and 'dwarf' only means it is a middle-sized star that burns hydrogen, not a small one. Betelgeuse, the red star in Orion, is much cooler than the Sun but is far, far bigger.
- e.g. The red star Antares in Scorpio and the blue-white star Rigel in Orion can be seen with the naked eye in the right season, and their colours differ.
- e.g. A blacksmith judges the heat of iron by its colour, from dull red to bright orange to near white.
### Why is the corona so much hotter than the surface? · *debated*
The Sun's surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius, but the corona above it is over a million degrees. This seems backwards, like finding that the air is hotter the further you walk from a fire. Scientists have been trying to solve it for about 80 years. Some say the Sun's magnetic field makes waves that carry energy up into the corona and heat it. Others say there are countless tiny flares, called nanoflares, and each one releases a little heat. Both ideas may be partly true. Spacecraft such as Parker Solar Probe and Aditya-L1 are measuring the corona to help decide.
- e.g. The corona is hot but very thin, so it holds much less heat than the surface, even though its temperature is higher.
- e.g. A hot spark from a fire can burn at thousands of degrees, but it holds so little heat that it hardly warms you.
- **Wave heating:** Waves travel along the Sun's magnetic field lines, carrying energy up from the surface. They give it up in the corona, which heats the corona.
- **Nanoflare heating:** The magnetic field lines are tangled and snapped all the time by the churning surface. Each snap is a tiny flare. Billions of them add up to heat the corona.

## Mostly agreed
- The Sun is made of layers of gas, and it has no solid surface. *How we know:* Spacecraft pictures, the way sunspots move, and models of the Sun's inside match a ball of gas that is more tightly packed as you go in.
- The Sun spins, and its middle turns faster than its poles. *How we know:* Sunspots near the middle go round in about 25 days and spots near the poles take about 35 days. Waves inside the Sun give the same result.
- Sunspots are cooler regions held by strong magnetic fields. *How we know:* Instruments split the light of a sunspot into colours and measure its temperature and magnetic strength directly.
- The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of about 11 years. *How we know:* Daily drawings and photographs of the Sun, such as the long series kept at Kodaikanal, show the same rhythm for more than 100 years.
- The Sun gives out a solar wind that blows past the Earth. *How we know:* Spacecraft such as Mariner 2 in 1962 and many since have measured the wind's speed and strength directly. Comet tails also point away from the Sun.
- Aurora happen when charged pieces from the Sun hit gas in the Earth's air, guided by the Earth's magnetic field. *How we know:* Satellites measure the charged pieces coming down at the same places where the aurora glows, and storms on the Sun come before the biggest displays.
- The dark lines in the Sun's rainbow show which atoms are in the Sun. *How we know:* The same patterns of lines are made by those atoms in laboratories on Earth.

## How we found out
**Ludwig Biermann, Eugene Parker and the Mariner 2 team, 1951 to 1962.** In the 1950s, many scientists pictured the Sun's outer atmosphere as a calm shell that hung round the Sun and thinned out with distance. Space between the planets was thought to be empty. But comet tails always point away from the Sun, even when the comet is moving away from it. In 1951 Ludwig Biermann said that something must be flowing out from the Sun and pushing them. In 1958 Eugene Parker, a young American physicist, worked out that gas as hot as the corona cannot stay still. It must stream outwards as a wind. Some experts doubted him. Parker later said that two judges of his paper advised against it, but the journal's editor, the Indian-born astrophysicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, decided to publish it. In 1962 the spacecraft Mariner 2 measured a steady wind of charged pieces on its way to Venus. The wind was real, and space was not empty.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought the Sun's atmosphere stayed still round the Sun and that space between the planets was empty. Parker showed that the hot corona must flow outwards, and Mariner 2 measured the wind.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Sun has a solid surface, like the Earth.** (Many children and grown-ups.; *rejected*)  
  For: In pictures the Sun has a sharp edge, as if it had a skin.  
  Against: The Sun is gas from the inside out. The sharp edge is where the gas stops being too cloudy to see through. Sunspots turning at different speeds at different places also show that it cannot be solid.
- **Sunspots are small planets passing in front of the Sun.** (Christoph Scheiner, a German priest and astronomer, around 1611.; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun was thought to be perfect and unchanging, so dark things on it must be something else. Scheiner thought small planets might circle very close to the Sun.  
  Against: Galileo showed that spots are thin near the edge, change shape, appear and fade, and cross the Sun at different speeds. Objects in orbit would not behave like that. Spots are on or in the Sun's surface.
- **Space between the planets is empty.** (A common idea before the 1950s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Space looks black, and a vacuum has almost nothing in it.  
  Against: The solar wind fills the space between the planets. It is thin, but a spacecraft can measure it, and it pushes comet tails and lights the aurora.
- **The Sun's outer atmosphere hangs around it, still, like a shell.** (Several leading scientists in the 1950s.; *rejected*)  
  For: The corona looks steady, like a calm halo, in eclipse photographs.  
  Against: Eugene Parker showed in 1958 that gas so hot cannot stay still. It must flow outwards as a wind. Spacecraft later found that it does.
- **Solar storms make the Earth's weather hot or cold.** (A popular idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun controls the Earth's warmth, and storms on the Sun are dramatic.  
  Against: Space weather affects satellites, radio and power lines, but the 11-year cycle changes the Sun's total light by only about a tenth of one percent. That is too small to explain big changes in the Earth's weather or climate.
- **The corona is hot because it is closer to a heat source.** (A natural guess.; *debated*)  
  For: Things near a fire are hotter than things far from it.  
  Against: The corona is further from the Sun's core than the surface, and it is hotter than the surface. The exact reason is still being studied. This is a real open puzzle.

## Roots
We have looked at the layers of the Sun, its spots and its wind. Now we ask what the Sun tells us about where things begin and end, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** The Sun's bright edge is only where its gas becomes clear enough for light to escape. Above that edge, thin hot gas goes on, and the solar wind blows past the Earth. So the Earth is inside the Sun's atmosphere, not outside it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of the Sun as a ball with a skin, and we think of the Earth as a separate ball far away. We draw the edge of a thing where we stop being able to see it.  
*Evidence:* In the milk test, the glowing white 'surface' of the water was only the depth you could see into. You could still put your hand in it. And a spacecraft has measured the Sun's wind at the Earth, and has flown through the corona.

- *who am i:* Your body is lit, warmed and fed by a star. The wind of that star also flows past you and round the Earth every day.
- *what is life:* Life on Earth evolved inside the Sun's atmosphere. The Earth's magnetic field and air steer the Sun's wind round living things, and the same wind makes the aurora.
- *what is this world:* The Sun does not stop where we see it stop. Its light, its wind and its magnetic field reach past all the planets.

From the first moment of the solar system, the Sun's gas has flowed outwards. Light, wind and magnetic field leave the Sun and reach the Earth, and they bring changes. The aurora, a compass wobble and a sunburn all begin in the same place. The edge we draw round the Sun in a picture is only where our eyes stopped. The Sun is one, and the Earth is inside it. This is the same oneness that Indian thinkers pointed to when they said the Sun is in everything that moves and everything that is still.

- **Isha Upanishad, verse 15:** The face of truth is covered by a golden disc. Remove it, so that I who love the truth can see. (The Sun's bright disc hides its own atmosphere. Only when the Moon covers the disc in a total eclipse does the corona show. What we take for the whole Sun is a golden disc over something larger.)
- **Rig Veda 1.115.1:** Surya is the self of everything that moves and everything that stands still. (Sunlight feeds the plants, and plants feed us. The Sun's wind passes through the Earth's space. Science can follow the Sun into everything.)
- Science (settled): The Sun has no solid surface. The photosphere is the layer where its gas becomes clear enough for light to escape.
- Science (open): Why the corona is so much hotter than the surface below it.
- Other view, Many astronomers: Where the Sun ends is a matter of choice. Most astronomers call the photosphere the Sun's surface, because that is where its light comes from. It is simply a useful rule, and they do not say the Earth is part of the Sun.
- Practice, Where does the Sun end? (3 min): Sit with your back to a sunny window. Close your eyes. Feel the warmth on your back. That is the Sun's light. Now think about the wind from the Sun, which is too gentle to notice. It flows past the Earth right now. Take six slow breaths. With each breath, imagine the edge of the Sun moving further out, past the Moon, past the Earth, past the planets. Then open your eyes, without turning to look at the Sun.
- Ask: If the Sun's wind blows past the Earth, where would you draw the edge of the Sun?
- Ask: The edge in the milk test was only how far you could see. What other edges are like that?
- Ask: Are you inside the Sun's atmosphere, or beside it? Does the answer change how you feel about the Sun?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The Sun has a sharp edge in pictures, but no ground. What other things do we think have an edge, just because we can see only that far? (hint: Think of a cloud, a fog around a street lamp, the edge of your own breath on a cold morning, or a city seen from far away.)
- *deep:* The Sun's wind blows past the Earth, so we live inside the Sun's atmosphere. Where does the Sun end and the Earth begin? (hint: Think of the light, the wind, and the magnetic field. Which of these do you feel, and which do you not?)
- *deep:* A sunspot looks dark, yet it is hotter than a stove flame. What else looks one way beside its neighbours and another way when you look alone? (hint: Think of a candle in daylight, or a lamp in a dark room.)
- *deep:* The Sun is gas, and gas can turn at different speeds in different places. Are you more like a solid ball or a ball of gas, when your body is made mostly of water? (hint: Think about which parts of you move and which parts stay in one place.)
- *big:* Chinese astronomers recorded dark spots on the Sun for about 2,000 years, yet the idea that spots were on the Sun itself took until Galileo's time to be widely believed. Why do people sometimes look at something for centuries without seeing what it is? (hint: Think of what people believed the Sun ought to be like.)
- *big:* A solar storm can switch off satellites, GPS and power lines. How much of our daily life now depends on the Sun's mood? (hint: Count the things you use in a day that need a satellite or electricity.)
- *big:* Eugene Parker's idea of the solar wind was doubted at first, and the editor of the journal chose to publish it anyway. When should a new idea be published even if experts doubt it? ## Story: The Spot That Went Round the Back
*Made up. Ilakkiya, her Amma, the visitor Mr Venkatesh and this November are invented, and the observatory room is imagined. The science is real: a sunspot that lasts long enough crosses the Sun in about two weeks, goes round the far side, and can come back about two weeks later. Kodaikanal Solar Observatory is a real place that has recorded the Sun since 1899.*

In the cloud forest above Kodaikanal, every November the mist came up the hill by lunchtime and did not leave for days. Ilakkiya was nine. She lived in the staff quarters of the Solar Observatory, because her Amma worked there, and the mist was the one thing she hated about the place.

Amma's job was to draw the Sun. Every clear morning, a big mirror at the top of the tower sent the Sun's picture down a long tube into a dark room. There it landed on a round white table, as wide as a dosa tawa and as bright as a tube light. Amma traced the Sun's edge with a pencil. Then she marked every dark spot she saw, and wrote the date beside it.

Ilakkiya was allowed to sit on a stool and watch, but only if she never looked up at the mirror. 'The table is safe,' Amma said. 'The Sun itself never is.'

That November a huge group of spots appeared. On the first morning it was a thin dark scratch at the Sun's eastern edge, where the Sun's turning was bringing it into view. Each day it crept a little further across the white table. Amma drew it, and numbered it, and called it Group 38.

By the seventh day it sat right in the middle of the Sun, wide and round, with a pale ring round its dark heart. Ilakkiya measured it with her ruler and her tongue sticking out. 'It is bigger than the Earth,' she said, for Amma had told her so. 'It is like a dark thumbprint on a hot dosa.'

By the thirteenth day it had slid to the western edge. It looked thin and squashed there, like a coin seen from the side. On the fourteenth morning it was gone.

That very afternoon the mist came up, and it stayed. Day after day, the white table stayed dark. Amma sat by the window and drank her tea and said nothing.

On the ninth day of mist, a visitor came up from the head office in Bengaluru. He was Mr Venkatesh, a tall man in a grey coat, who carried a folder with the records of every observatory in the country. He sat at Amma's desk and read her notebook.

'Group 38,' he said. 'Observed fourteen days, then vanished. Large groups often die suddenly. I will mark it as finished.' He took out his pen.

Ilakkiya put down her pencil. 'It did not die,' she said.

Mr Venkatesh looked over his glasses at her. 'And how would you know that, young lady?'

'Because it was at the edge,' she said. 'It did not fade. It got thin, like a coin turning. It went round the back.'

He laughed kindly. 'The back of the Sun?'

'The Sun turns,' said Ilakkiya. 'Amma told me. Spots come in at the east side and go out at the west side. So they are on a ball that is turning.' She ran to the kitchen and came back with an orange and a little round sticker. She stuck it on the orange, held the orange up to the lamp on the desk, and turned it slowly on a pencil. The sticker slid across, grew thin at the edge, and disappeared.

'Now keep turning,' she said. 'Where is it? It is not gone. It is behind. The orange has to go all the way round before it comes back.' She turned and turned. At the far side, there was nothing to see. Then, at the other edge, the sticker slid back into view, thin and then round. 'That is how long it takes,' she said. 'About two weeks to cross the front, and about two weeks to cross the back. If Group 38 is still alive, it comes back at the east edge, fourteen days after it left. That is on the twenty-eighth day.'

Mr Venkatesh stopped writing. 'It might have died while it was behind the Sun,' he said slowly. 'We cannot see the back.'

'Yes,' said Ilakkiya. 'It might have. But you do not know it did. And you must not write finished until you know.'

He did not write anything. He closed the folder and said that he would stay in Kodaikanal until the twenty-eighth day. Amma put her hand on her mouth. Ilakkiya was not sure whether she was laughing or crying.

The mist stayed until the twenty-seventh evening. On the twenty-eighth morning, Ilakkiya woke before it was light and ran to the dark room. Amma was already there. The Sun's picture was on the white table, bright and round and clean.

At the eastern edge, just inside the pencil circle, there was a thin dark scratch.

'It is smaller,' said Amma quietly. 'But it is Group 38.'

Mr Venkatesh bent over the table, and for a long time he did not speak. Then he took out his pen and wrote in his folder, very slowly. Ilakkiya read it upside down. It said: Group 38, returned on the twenty-eighth day. Thanks to I. Selvi, aged nine, who worked out when to look.

That night, when the mist came up again, Ilakkiya kept the orange on her windowsill. She turned it a little each evening, to keep time with the Sun.

**For the parent:** A sunspot that disappears at one edge of the Sun has not died. The Sun turns like a ball, so the spot goes round the back and can come into view again about two weeks later.

- Ask: Why did Ilakkiya think the spot had not died when it vanished at the edge?
- Ask: What did the sticker on the orange show her, and what could it not show?
- Ask: Why did the spot look thin and squashed at the Sun's edge?
- Ask: If a spot is in the middle of the Sun today, about how many days until it reaches an edge?
- Ask: Mr Venkatesh wanted to write 'finished' without knowing for sure. What should a scientist do when they cannot see something?

### e3 · The Sun's Barcode: split light into colours with a CD (30 min)

### Layers and Spots (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (order) Put the Sun's layers in order, from the inside out.  
   **Answer:** ['Core', 'Radiative zone', 'Convection zone', 'Photosphere', 'Chromosphere', 'Corona']. *Why:* Energy is made in the core, then crosses the radiative zone and the boiling convection zone. The photosphere is the layer we call the surface, and above it lie the thin chromosphere and the huge corona.
2. (mcq) What is the photosphere?  
   Options: The layer where the Sun's gas becomes clear enough for light to escape / A solid crust over the Sun / The Sun's core / The Sun's wind

## Open questions
- **Why is the Sun's corona so much hotter than its surface?** Two main ideas exist, heating by waves and heating by countless tiny flares. Spacecraft such as Parker Solar Probe and Aditya-L1 are collecting data, but scientists have not decided.
- **Can we predict when a big solar storm will hit the Earth, a week before?** Scientists can watch eruptions leave the Sun, but they cannot yet say reliably which sunspot group will erupt, or how strong the storm will be at the Earth.
- **Why does the Sun's magnetic field flip about every 11 years?** Computer models of the Sun's magnetism give cycles, but they do not agree on every detail, and some cycles come out stronger than the models expect.
- **Where does the Sun's wind end and space between the stars begin?** Voyager 1 left the Sun's wind bubble in 2012, according to NASA, but the shape and size of the whole bubble are still being worked out.
- **Was the Sun brighter or fainter when it was young?** Many scientists think the young Sun was about a quarter to a third fainter, yet the early Earth had liquid water. How that was possible is still debated.
- **Are there sunspots on other stars?** Astronomers have seen signs of huge starspots on some stars, but starspots are hard to see, because other stars look like single points of light.

Near the edge the spot looks slower, because the ball curves away, so it takes a little longer than 5 days. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- The solar wind travels at about 400 kilometres in every second. The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away. How many seconds does it take to reach the Earth, and about how many days is that? A day has 86,400 seconds. **About 375,000 seconds, which is a little over 4 days.**. 150,000,000 divided by 400 is 375,000 seconds. 375,000 divided by 86,400 is about 4.3. So the wind takes a little over 4 days. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- Light from the Sun takes 8 minutes to reach the Earth. The wind takes about 4 days. A day has 24 hours and an hour has 60 minutes. How many times as long does the wind take? **About 720 times as long.**. 4 days is 4 times 24 hours, which is 96 hours. 96 hours is 96 times 60 minutes, which is 5,760 minutes. 5,760 divided by 8 is 720. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- Suppose the number of sunspots was highest in the year 2000. The cycle lasts 11 years. In which years will the next three highs come? **2011, 2022 and 2033.**. 2000 plus 11 is 2011. 2011 plus 11 is 2022. 2022 plus 11 is 2033. The pattern adds 11 each time. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- At the Sun's middle, one turn takes about 25 days. Near a pole it takes about 35 days. How many whole turns will each make in 100 days? **Four turns at the middle, and two whole turns near a pole.**. 100 divided by 25 is exactly 4. 100 divided by 35 is 2 with 30 left over, so two whole turns and most of a third. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A card faces the Sun straight on when the pencil on it casts no shadow. Its tilt from the flat floor is 29 degrees. The Sun's height above the horizon is the rest of the way to a right angle, 90 degrees. How high is the Sun? **61 degrees.**. The card and the pencil make a right angle. The card tilts 29 degrees from flat, so the Sun is 90 take away 29, which is 61 degrees above the horizon. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- In the Glowing Fog test, these were the depths where a black patch vanished: 9 centimetres, 6 centimetres, 3 centimetres, 2 centimetres, 1 centimetre. The milk doubled each time. Between which two steps did the depth fall most? How much? **Between the first and second, and the second and third, it fell 3 centimetres each time.**. 9 take away 6 is 3. 6 take away 3 is 3. 3 take away 2 is 1. 2 take away 1 is 1. The biggest falls were 3 centimetres, at the first two steps. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In the Boiling Pot, one speck takes 15 seconds to go round one loop. How many loops would it make in 2 minutes? **8 loops.**. 2 minutes is 120 seconds. 120 divided by 15 is 8. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Does the Sun have solid ground? → No. It is gas from the outside to the centre.
- What is the layer we call the Sun's surface? → The photosphere.
- Which layer is hotter, the surface or the corona? → The corona, over a million degrees.
- Why does a sunspot look dark? → It is cooler than the gas around it.
- About how long does the Sun take to turn once? → About 25 days at the middle and 35 near the poles.
- What is the solar wind? → A stream of charged pieces flowing out from the Sun.
- What makes an aurora glow? → Charged pieces from the Sun hit gas high in the Earth's air.
- How long does a flare's flash take to reach the Earth? → About 8 minutes.
- What do the dark lines in the Sun's rainbow tell us? → Which atoms are in the Sun.
