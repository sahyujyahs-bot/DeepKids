# Strange life cycles: seventeen years underground, one journey home, and a jellyfish that starts again
`sci.animals.animal-life-cycles.L5`

**Summary.** One kind of cicada lives underground for seventeen years, then flies and sings for about four weeks. A Pacific salmon swims from the sea to the stream where she hatched, lays her eggs once and dies. A jellyfish smaller than your little fingernail can turn from an adult back into a youngster. Why would any animal live like this? Your child tests how a crowd protects each member, and how weak a smell a nose can still follow home. They also find out why a fish may lay millions of eggs, and what a cell does when a body starts again.

**Intro.** *Think of a cicada, a mayfly, a hen, an elephant and yourself. Which one spends the biggest share of life as a youngster? Say what you think, and why.* Most people pick the elephant or the human, because our childhoods feel long. In the first activity, Life Bars, you draw each life as a bar and measure the answer. Then you meet three animals whose life cycles seem strange to us. For each one you ask the same question. What dangers and what food would make this way of living work?

## Terms
- **Timetable of a life**: The timetable of a life says how long each stage lasts, and when and how often the animal breeds. *e.g.* A hen starts laying at about five months old. A periodical cicada breeds only once, at seventeen years.
- **Nymph**: A nymph is the young of an insect that has no pupa stage. A nymph grows by shedding their skin several times. *e.g.* A cicada nymph lives underground and drinks sap from tree roots.
- **Periodical cicada**: A periodical cicada is a cicada whose whole population grows up together and comes out in the same year, after a fixed number of years. *e.g.* The periodical cicadas of the eastern United States come out after 13 or 17 years. One kind in Meghalaya comes out every 4 years.
- **Brood**: A brood is all the periodical cicadas of one region that come out in the same year. *e.g.* In 2024 a 13-year brood and a 17-year brood came out in the same year in the United States.
- **Emerge**: To emerge is to come out. For a cicada it means digging up out of the soil to become an adult. *e.g.* Millions of cicadas emerge within a few evenings.
- **Predator satiation**: Predator satiation means that so many prey arrive together that the predators eat until they are full, and most prey are left alive. *e.g.* Winged termites fly out together after the first rain. Crows, geckos and frogs eat many, and most still escape.
- **Prime number**: A prime number is a whole number bigger than 1 that can be divided exactly only by 1 and by itself. *e.g.* 13 and 17 are prime numbers. 12 is not, because 2, 3, 4 and 6 divide it exactly.
- **Spawn**: To spawn is to release eggs or sperm into the water. A sperm is the tiny cell from the male that joins with an egg to start a new life. *e.g.* A female salmon spawns in a nest she digs in the gravel of a stream.
- **Smolt**: A smolt is a young salmon whose body has changed so that they can live in salt water. *e.g.* A smolt turns silver and swims down the river to the sea.
- **Imprinting**: Imprinting is fast learning at one stage of life that lasts for the rest of that life. *e.g.* A young salmon imprints on the smell of their home stream before they leave for the sea.
- **Osmosis**: Osmosis is the movement of water through a thin skin, from the side with less salt towards the side with more salt. *e.g.* A potato strip in very salty water goes soft, because water leaves the potato's cells.
- **Semelparous**: A semelparous animal breeds only once in their life and then dies. *e.g.* Pacific salmon, mayflies and octopuses are semelparous.
- **Iteroparous**: An iteroparous animal breeds many times in their life. *e.g.* Hens, elephants, crows and humans are iteroparous.
- **Survival curve**: A survival curve is a line graph that shows how many animals out of a starting group are still alive at each age. *e.g.* The survival curve of a fish drops almost to zero in the first weeks. An elephant's curve stays high for many years.
- **Medusa**: A medusa is the swimming, bell-shaped stage of a jellyfish. It is the adult stage, which makes eggs or sperm. *e.g.* The jellyfish you see washed up on a beach are medusae.
- **Polyp**: A polyp is the stage of a jellyfish that stays fixed to a rock or a shell. A polyp looks like a tiny stalk with a ring of tentacles on top. *e.g.* A jellyfish polyp grows buds, and each bud swims away as a small medusa.
- **Cell**: A cell is the smallest living part of a body. Your body is made of about thirty trillion cells of about two hundred kinds. *e.g.* Skin cells, muscle cells and nerve cells are three kinds of cell.
- **Gene**: A gene is one instruction in a cell, written in a long molecule called DNA. A cell uses some of its genes and leaves the others switched off. *e.g.* A muscle cell and a skin cell carry the same genes, but they use different ones.
- **Transdifferentiation**: Transdifferentiation is one kind of cell changing into another kind of cell. *e.g.* In the immortal jellyfish, cells of the bell can change into the cells of a polyp.
- **Senescence**: Senescence is the slow wearing down of a body with age, which makes death more likely each year. *e.g.* An old dog who moves stiffly and falls ill more often is showing senescence.

## Explanations
### Every life cycle has a timetable · *defined*
**Definition:** A life cycle is the set of stages an animal goes through until they have young of their own. Its timetable says how long each stage lasts, and when and how often the animal breeds.

In Class 3 and Class 4 you met the stages: egg, young and adult. Now look at the times. A human is a child for about a fifth of a life. A periodical cicada is a youngster for seventeen years and an adult for about four weeks. A mayfly may be an adult for one day. A Pacific salmon breeds once and dies. An elephant mother has a calf every four or five years, for about forty years. One small jellyfish can go from adult back to young. None of these timetables is the normal one. Each was shaped over millions of years by natural selection. Natural selection means that the animals whose way of living left the most surviving young passed that way of living on. So for every timetable that looks strange to us, we can ask a useful question. What dangers and what food made this timetable work?
- e.g. A hen can lay her first egg at about five months old and may live six years or more. Most of her life is adult life.
- e.g. A mayfly nymph lives in a stream for about a year. The winged adult has no working mouth and lives for a day or two.
- e.g. In Life Bars, the adult part of the cicada's bar was only about one millimetre long.
### The periodical cicada: seventeen years underground · *defined*
**Definition:** A periodical cicada is a cicada whose whole population grows up underground together and comes out in the same year, after a fixed number of years.

Cicadas are insects that suck plant sap. The males sing loudly. In most kinds of cicada, including the ones you hear in India in summer, some adults come out every year. Seven kinds in the eastern United States are different. A female cuts slits in a twig and lays her eggs there. After six to ten weeks the eggs hatch. The young are called nymphs, and each is smaller than a grain of rice. They drop to the ground and dig down. Each nymph pushes a sucking tube into a tree root and drinks xylem sap. Xylem sap is the watery liquid that rises from a tree's roots to the leaves. This sap holds very little food, which is one reason the nymphs grow so slowly. After 17 years, or 13 years for some kinds, the nymphs dig upward. They wait until the soil about 20 centimetres down warms to about 18 degrees Celsius. Then millions come up within a few evenings. Each climbs a tree trunk, splits the old skin down the back and pulls out as a winged adult. The adults sing, mate, lay eggs and die within about a month. All the cicadas of one region that come up in the same year are called a brood.
- e.g. India has one known periodical cicada, in the Ri-Bhoi district of Meghalaya. They come out every four years. Sudhanya Ray Hajong and Salmah Yaakop described them for science in 2013, and local people had known the timetable long before.
- e.g. In 2024, a 13-year brood and a 17-year brood came out in the same spring in the United States. That had last happened in 1803.
- e.g. In a big emergence there can be more than 300 cicadas for every square metre of ground under the trees.
### How does a cicada count the years? · *defined*
**Definition:** A cicada nymph counts years by the yearly changes in the sap of the tree they drink from.

Under the ground there is no summer sky and no winter to see. So how does a nymph know that seventeen years have passed? A tree's sap changes through the year. In spring, when the tree grows new leaves, the sap carries more food. In 2000, Richard Karban, Carrie Black and Steven Weinbaum tested whether the nymphs count these changes. They moved nymphs of a 17-year brood onto the roots of peach trees. The nymphs were 15 years old. Then the scientists made the peach trees grow leaves and flowers twice in one year. The cicadas came out one year early. They had counted two springs in one calendar year. So a nymph counts the tree's yearly cycles and not the days. Nobody yet knows where in the body the count is kept, or how it is kept right up to seventeen.
- e.g. A cicada nymph on a tree that flowers twice in a year counts two years.
- e.g. All the nymphs of a brood count the same trees' seasons. This is how millions of separate animals reach seventeen in the same spring.
- e.g. The last signal is warmth. The nymphs wait just below the surface until the soil reaches about 18 degrees Celsius.
### Safety in a crowd: predator satiation · *defined*
**Definition:** Predator satiation means that so many prey arrive together that the predators eat until they are full, and most of the prey are left alive.

A periodical cicada has no sting, no poison and no speed. Birds, squirrels, snakes, dogs and even fish eat them. Yet most survive, because a bird can eat only so many in a day. In one large emergence that scientists measured, birds ate about 15 of every 100 cicadas. Cicadas that come out in small numbers, or in the wrong year, are almost all eaten. You saw the same thing in the Crowd Test. The picker took about the same number of rajma in ten seconds, whether ten were on the towel or eighty. So the share eaten fell as the crowd grew. The long wait helps too. Predators cannot build up their numbers on a food that comes once in seventeen years. If the cicadas came out every year, more of the birds' chicks would be fed and would live. Then there would be more birds each year to eat cicadas.
- e.g. Winged termites pour out of the ground on the same evening after the first monsoon rain. Crows, geckos, frogs and people eat many, and most still fly on.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtles nest in thousands on the same few nights at Rushikulya and Gahirmatha in Odisha. Jackals and dogs cannot dig up all the nests.
- e.g. Many bamboos flower and seed together once in many years. Rats and birds cannot eat all the seed.
### Why 13 and 17, and not 12 or 16? · *debated*
13 and 17 are prime numbers. A prime number can be divided exactly only by 1 and by itself. No periodical cicada has a cycle of 12, 14, 15, 16 or 18 years. Scientists have three main ideas about why, and no agreed answer. In the game Emerge! you play with the first idea. Remember that it is only one idea.
- e.g. A 12-year cicada would meet a 2-year, a 3-year, a 4-year and a 6-year predator at every emergence. A 13-year cicada would meet each of them far less often.
- e.g. A 13-year brood and a 17-year brood living in the same woods come out together only once in 221 years, because 13 times 17 is 221.
- e.g. Some 17-year cicadas come out four years early, after 13 years. So the two cycles can change into each other.
- **Hiding from predators that have cycles:** Suppose a predator or a parasite had good years every 2, 3 or 4 years. A cicada with a 12-year cycle would meet it every time. A cicada with a 13-year or 17-year cycle would meet it rarely. The scientist Stephen Jay Gould made this idea famous in 1977. The trouble is that nobody has found such a predator.
- **Keeping the broods apart:** If cicadas with different cycles come out in the same year, they may mate with each other. Their young may then have mixed-up timetables and come out in small numbers, and be eaten. Prime cycles overlap very rarely. Jin Yoshimura and others have built this idea into mathematical models since 1997.
- **Partly chance:** The cycles seem to change in jumps of four years. Cold periods in the past may have slowed growth and stretched the cycles step by step. On this view, 13 and 17 need no special reason. They may simply be where the jumps happened to stop.
### The salmon: river, sea and home again · *defined*
**Definition:** A Pacific salmon hatches in a river, grows up in the sea, and swims back to the same river to spawn once and die. To spawn is to release eggs or sperm into the water.

A sockeye salmon begins as an orange egg the size of a pea, buried in the gravel of a cold stream. The egg hatches in late winter. The tiny fish, called an alevin, stays in the gravel and lives on a bag of yolk under their belly. When the yolk is used up, the young fish swims out to feed. After a year or two in a lake, the young salmon turns silver. Now they are called a smolt, and they swim down to the sea. In the North Pacific Ocean the salmon feeds and grows for two or three years, and swims thousands of kilometres. Then she turns back. She finds the mouth of her own river and stops eating. Her body turns red. She swims upstream against the current and leaps up waterfalls. Some sockeye in Idaho swim about 1,400 kilometres and climb about 2,000 metres. At the stream where she hatched, she digs a nest in the gravel with her tail. She lays about 3,000 eggs, and a male covers them with sperm. Within days or weeks both are dead.
- e.g. India has no wild salmon. The hilsa makes a similar journey. Hilsa live in the Bay of Bengal and swim up the Godavari, the Hooghly and the Padma in the monsoon to spawn. In Andhra Pradesh the river-run hilsa is called pulasa.
- e.g. Unlike Pacific salmon, many hilsa and some Atlantic salmon swim back to the sea and spawn again in a later year.
- e.g. Eels do the opposite. An Indian mottled eel grows up in rivers and ponds, then swims to the sea to spawn once and die.
### How a salmon finds home · *defined*
**Definition:** A salmon finds her home stream mainly by smell. As a young fish she learns the smell of her stream, and as an adult she follows it back.

Every stream has its own smell, from its rocks, soil and plants. A young salmon learns this smell at about the time they become a smolt. This fast, lasting learning is called imprinting. In 1954, Warren Wisby and Arthur Hasler tested the idea at a creek in the state of Washington, where the creek splits into two branches. They caught salmon that had already chosen a branch. They plugged the noses of half the fish with cotton. Then they let all the fish go again below the fork. The fish with open noses mostly chose their own branch again. The fish with plugged noses chose the branches almost at random. In the 1970s Hasler's team went further. They raised young salmon in water with a tiny amount of one chemical in it. Later they put that chemical into one stream, and the grown salmon swam into that stream. In the open ocean there is no stream smell to follow. There the salmon may use the Earth's magnetism, as a compass does. This is an idea with some evidence, and it is not settled.
- e.g. In the Home Stream Test you found the weakest smell that your nose could still pick out. A salmon's nose works on smells many thousands of times weaker.
- e.g. A few salmon in every hundred swim into the wrong stream. These strays are how salmon reach new rivers.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtles also return to the coast where they hatched. They seem to use the Earth's magnetism.
### From fresh water to salt water: the body's salt switch · *defined*
**Definition:** Water moves through the thin skin of living cells towards the saltier side. This movement is called osmosis. A salmon's body must handle it one way in a river and the opposite way in the sea.

Salt is the main thing here, though sugar and other dissolved substances draw water in the same way. A fish's blood is about one third as salty as sea water. River water has almost no salt. In a river, water keeps moving into the fish through the thin skin of the gills. So a river fish hardly drinks, and passes a lot of watery urine. In the sea, water keeps moving out of the fish into the saltier sea. So a sea fish drinks sea water, and special cells in the gills pump the extra salt out. A salmon lives both ways. Before a young salmon reaches the sea, their gill cells change, so that they pump salt out and no longer take salt in. This change is what makes a smolt. When the adult returns to the river, the gills change back. Most fish can live only one way. A river fish such as a rohu, put into the sea, would lose too much water and die.
- e.g. In the Salt Switch, a potato strip in very salty water went soft, because water left the potato's cells.
- e.g. Your fingers wrinkle in a long bath for a different reason. Nerves tighten the blood vessels under the skin.
- e.g. Hilsa, salmon and eels can make the switch. Most of the fish in a pond or in the open sea cannot.
### Breed once, or breed many times · *defined*
**Definition:** A semelparous animal breeds once and then dies. An iteroparous animal breeds many times.

A body has a limited supply of energy from food. The body spends it on growing, on repairing itself and on making young. A Pacific salmon spends everything on one spawning. She stops eating and turns her fat, and even some of her muscle, into eggs and into swimming. Why not keep some back and breed again? Think about her chances. To breed twice she would have to swim back down the river, survive two more years at sea and climb the river again. Very few would manage it. So the salmon that put everything into one spawning left more young than the salmon that held back. Over many generations, spending everything became the usual way. Where the journey is shorter or safer, breeding again pays. An elephant has a good chance of living through each year, so her body spends on repair and breeds many times. This is the best explanation scientists have. It fits most animals, though not every case.
- e.g. A female giant Pacific octopus guards her eggs for about six months without eating. She dies around the time they hatch.
- e.g. Adult mayflies have no working mouths. They mate, lay eggs and die, often on the same day.
- e.g. Crows, hens, cats, hilsa and humans are iteroparous.
### A dead salmon feeds a forest · *defined*
**Definition:** The bodies of salmon that die after spawning carry food from the sea into rivers and forests.

A salmon builds nearly all of her body in the ocean. When she dies in a mountain stream, that body stays there. Bears and eagles carry salmon into the forest and leave parts uneaten. Insects, fungi and bacteria break the bodies down. Scientists can trace where the material goes. Nitrogen is a substance that all living things need to build their bodies. Nitrogen atoms come in a lighter kind and a slightly heavier kind. Sea food holds more of the heavier kind than land food does. Trees beside salmon streams hold extra heavy nitrogen, so part of each tree was once salmon. The young salmon gain too. The tiny water animals they eat grow well in a stream fed by their parents' bodies.
- e.g. In salmon rivers in Alaska and Canada, more than fifty kinds of animals feed on salmon or salmon eggs.
- e.g. When a dam stops salmon from reaching a stream, the stream gets less food, and so do the trees beside it.
- e.g. Olive ridley eggs that fail to hatch feed crabs, ants and the plants of the dune.
### Why so many young? The egg budget · *defined*
**Definition:** A mother has a limited amount of material and energy to put into eggs. She can make a few big eggs or many small ones, and never many big ones.

In Class 4 you learned the key number. Where a population stays steady, about two young from each pair grow up to breed. That holds for elephants and for fish. So why does one animal lay millions of eggs? You saw the reason in the Egg Budget. One ball of dough made four big eggs or about fifty small ones. A big egg holds more food, so a big, strong baby hatches from it. A small egg gives a tiny baby with almost no food store. Where a mother can guard or feed her young, a few big ones do well. In the open sea a fish cannot guard eggs that drift away. There, tiny eggs in huge numbers work better, because a few will land by luck where there is food and no hungry mouth. One ocean sunfish was found to carry about 300 million eggs. A survival curve shows the result. For a fish the curve drops almost to zero in the first weeks. For an elephant it stays high for many years.
- e.g. A kiwi lays one egg at a time. The egg weighs about a fifth as much as the mother.
- e.g. A tsetse fly is an insect that has one baby at a time. The young grows inside the mother and is fed on a liquid like milk.
- e.g. A hilsa may lay more than a million eggs in a season and gives them no care.
### If every egg lived · *defined*
**Definition:** If all the young of any animal lived and bred, their numbers would multiply again and again until there was no room or food left. So in every generation most young must die before they breed.

Take a pair of frogs who lay 1,000 eggs. If every egg grew up, there would be 500 pairs. If each of those pairs laid 1,000 eggs, the next generation would have 500,000 frogs, and the one after that 250 million. Growth by multiplying again and again is called exponential growth. Charles Darwin worked this out in 1859 for the slowest breeder he knew, the elephant. He found that one pair would have about 19 million living descendants after about 750 years. But the world is not full of elephants or frogs. So most young of every kind die before they breed. Which ones live is partly luck. It is also partly small differences between them, such as a slightly faster tadpole. Those differences are passed on to the next generation. Darwin's name for this was natural selection.
- e.g. A housefly can lay about 500 eggs, and the young can breed within two or three weeks in warm weather. Hunger, cold, spiders and birds keep the numbers down.
- e.g. When wild rabbits were set free in Australia in 1859 and found few predators, their numbers grew to hundreds of millions within about sixty years.
- e.g. Bacteria in warm milk can double every half hour or so. That is how a spoon of curd sets a whole pot overnight.
### The immortal jellyfish: a life cycle that can run backwards · *defined*
**Definition:** The medusa of one small jellyfish, Turritopsis dohrnii, can change back into a polyp, which is the young stage. Then the polyp grows new medusae.

First, the usual jellyfish life cycle. An egg and a sperm join in the sea. The egg grows into a tiny swimming larva. The larva settles on a rock or a shell and becomes a polyp, a small stalk with tentacles. The polyp grows buds, and each bud swims away as a small medusa. The medusa grows up, makes eggs or sperm and dies. Turritopsis dohrnii has one more path. Their medusa is about 4 millimetres wide. When this medusa is starved, hurt or old, their bell can shrink into a small ball. The ball sinks, sticks to the bottom and grows into a new polyp within about three days. The polyp then buds off new medusae. These medusae have exactly the same genes as the old one. In the laboratory this can happen again and again. 'Immortal' is still not the right word. In the sea these jellyfish are eaten by fish, sea slugs and other jellyfish, and they die of disease. Nobody has yet followed one animal through the change in the wild.
- e.g. The scientist Shin Kubota in Japan kept one colony that went from medusa back to polyp ten times in two years.
- e.g. A few other jellyfish, including the moon jellyfish, have since been seen to do something similar in the laboratory.
- e.g. The Life Bars drawing has no bar for this jellyfish, because their life has no fixed end to measure to.
### Cells that change jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Nearly every cell in a body carries the same full set of genes. What kind of cell it is depends on which genes are switched on.

A medusa has muscle cells, nerve cells and stinging cells. A polyp needs a different mix. When the medusa turns into a polyp, cells do not simply die and get replaced. Many of them change kind. A cell switches some genes off and others on, and becomes a different kind of cell. This is called transdifferentiation. For a long time scientists thought it could not happen. They thought that a cell which had become one kind had lost the instructions for being any other kind. In 1962, John Gurdon showed that this was wrong. He took the centre, called the nucleus, out of a gut cell of a tadpole. The nucleus is where the genes are kept. He put it into a frog egg whose own nucleus had been destroyed. Some of these eggs grew into whole tadpoles, and later into frogs. So the gut cell still held every instruction. In 2006, Shinya Yamanaka and Kazutoshi Takahashi found that switching on just four genes turns a mouse skin cell back into a cell that can become any kind. The jellyfish does this kind of thing with no help.
- e.g. Your skin cells and your nerve cells carry the same genes. They differ in which genes are switched on.
- e.g. In Run It Backwards, you made a dough medusa into a ball and then into a polyp, with no dough added or taken away.
- e.g. A newt can grow back a lost leg. Cells near the wound go back to a simpler state and then build the new leg.
### Why do most bodies wear out? · *debated*
Most animals show senescence. That means the body slowly wears down with age, so that death becomes more likely each year. The immortal jellyfish shows that this is not a law for every animal. So why do most bodies wear out? Scientists give different answers.
- e.g. A Pacific salmon's body breaks down within days of spawning. A landlocked sockeye who never goes to sea dies after spawning too.
- e.g. A hydra, a tiny freshwater relative of jellyfish, showed no sign of ageing in a four-year laboratory study.
- e.g. Humans, elephants and some whales live for many years after their last young is born.
- **The budget idea:** Repairing a body perfectly costs energy. In the wild most animals are killed by hunger, disease or predators long before perfect repair would matter. So bodies spend on breeding and repair themselves only well enough. Thomas Kirkwood set out this idea in 1977.
- **The late damage idea:** A gene that helps an animal when young and harms them when old will still spread, because many animals never reach old age. Over time such genes pile up. George Williams set out this idea in 1957.
- **Not a law at all:** Animals built like hydra and some jellyfish keep cells that can renew every part of the body. For them, wearing out may not be built in. Other scientists reply that we have not watched these animals for long enough to be sure.
### More strange timetables · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals differ in every part of the timetable: when they grow up, whether they need a mate, and even which sex they are.

An axolotl is a salamander from lakes near Mexico City. Most salamanders lose their gills and walk onto land, as frogs do. An axolotl keeps their feathery gills, stays in the water and breeds while still in a young-looking body. An aphid is a small insect that sucks plant sap. In warm months a female aphid gives birth to live daughters with no father. Each daughter is a copy of her mother, and can be carrying her own daughters before she is born. A clownfish begins adult life as a male. When the one female of the group dies, the largest male changes into a female. Some killifish live in pools that dry up each year. The adults die, and the eggs wait in the dry mud for months until rain returns.
- e.g. Look under the young leaves of a hibiscus or an avarakkai plant. A cluster of aphids is usually one mother's family.
- e.g. Clownfish live among the sea anemones of the coral reefs of Lakshadweep and the Andaman Islands.
- e.g. The purple frog of the Western Ghats lives underground nearly all year. They come up for a few days in the first monsoon rains to breed.

## Mostly agreed
- Periodical cicadas come out together after a fixed number of years, and the crowd protects them. *How we know:* Emergences have been recorded in the United States since the 1600s and are mapped brood by brood. Counts show that birds eat a small share in big emergences and nearly all cicadas in small ones.
- Cicada nymphs count the yearly cycles of the trees they feed on. *How we know:* In 2000, nymphs on peach trees that were made to flower twice in one year came out one year early.
- Salmon return to the stream where they hatched, and they find it mainly by smell. *How we know:* Tagged fish return to their home streams. Fish with plugged noses could not choose the right branch. Fish raised with a chemical in the water later followed that chemical.
- Where a population stays steady, about two young from each pair survive to breed, however many eggs are laid. *How we know:* If more survived, numbers would multiply without end. If fewer survived, the animal would die out. Counts of fish, frogs and birds over many years fit this.
- The medusa of Turritopsis dohrnii can turn back into a polyp, and cells change kind as this happens. *How we know:* It was first seen in Italy in the 1980s. It has been repeated in laboratories in Italy, Japan and other countries, and the cells have been followed under the microscope.
- A cell that has become one kind still carries the full set of genes. *How we know:* John Gurdon grew frogs from the nuclei of tadpole gut cells in 1962. Shinya Yamanaka turned skin cells back into unspecialised cells in 2006. They shared a Nobel Prize in 2012.

## How we found out
**Christian Sommer and Giorgio Bavestrello, who first saw it. Stefano Piraino, Ferdinando Boero, Brigitte Aeschbach and Volker Schmid, who showed how the cells change. Shin Kubota, who kept a colony changing back again and again., The 1980s (first seen), 1992 (first report), 1996 (the study of the cells) and 2011 (Kubota's report of ten changes).** For as long as people had studied animals, they took one thing for granted. A life cycle runs one way. The young become adults, and an adult never becomes young again. In the 1980s two students in Italy, Christian Sommer and Giorgio Bavestrello, were keeping tiny jellyfish called Turritopsis in jars of sea water. The adults, called medusae, should have bred and died. Instead, the students found polyps on the bottom of the jars. Polyps are the young, fixed stage. They watched closely. A medusa that was starved or hurt shrank into a ball, sank, stuck to the glass and grew into a polyp. They reported it in 1992. In 1996, Stefano Piraino, Ferdinando Boero and their team showed that medusae of every age could do this. They also showed that the cells change from one kind into another. Later Shin Kubota in Japan kept one colony going back and forth ten times in two years.  
*What people believed before:* That an adult animal can never go back to being young, because growing up runs only one way. Medusae in jars turned back into polyps in front of the scientists.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Periodical cicadas sleep underground for seventeen years.** (A common way of telling the story; *rejected*)  
  For: We never see them in all that time, and they come out all at once, as if an alarm had rung.  
  Against: The nymphs are awake and busy. They dig, drink from roots, grow and shed their skin four times. They even count the years by the trees' sap.
- **The immortal jellyfish can never die.** (Many headlines and videos; *rejected*)  
  For: In the laboratory, an old or starved medusa can turn back into a polyp, again and again.  
  Against: They can be eaten, and they can die of disease. Nobody has followed one animal through the change in the sea. The scientists who study them say 'can escape death from old age', which is a smaller claim.
- **Salmon die after spawning because the journey wears them out.** (Many nature books; *rejected*)  
  For: They arrive thin and battered, having eaten nothing for weeks.  
  Against: Landlocked sockeye salmon never go to sea and make only a short trip, and they still die after spawning. The breakdown is set off by hormones, which are chemical messages inside the body. The journey alone does not cause it.
- **An animal that lays a million eggs and loses nearly all of them is wasting them.** (A common first thought; *rejected*)  
  For: Almost every egg dies, so it looks like a failed plan.  
  Against: The plan leaves about two survivors, the same as an elephant's plan. And the eggs that die are eaten. They are food for small fish, crabs and other animals, so nothing leaves the sea's food supply.
- **Cicadas use prime numbers to hide from their predators.** (Popular science books, following Stephen Jay Gould; *debated*)  
  For: A 13-year or 17-year cycle would rarely match any predator's cycle of 2 to 6 years. No cicada has a 12, 14, 15, 16 or 18 year cycle.  
  Against: Nobody has found a predator with such a cycle. Other ideas explain the primes as well, such as keeping broods from mixing. Cycles also seem to jump by four years, which could give 13 and 17 by chance.
- **Growing old is a law of nature for every animal.** (Common sense for most of history; *debated*)  
  For: Every animal we know well grows old: dogs, cows, crows and people.  
  Against: Hydra showed no ageing in a four-year study. The medusa of Turritopsis dohrnii can become young again. Some scientists still expect that all animals age, only very slowly in a few.

## Roots
We have been learning about animals whose lives are timed very differently from ours. Now we ask what these timetables tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In Life Bars, almost the whole of the cicada's bar was the nymph, and the winged adult was the last millimetre. The mayfly's adult was one pencil line. For these animals the young stage is nearly the whole life, and the adult is a short last stage for breeding. So a nymph is not an unfinished cicada. The nymph is the cicada, for nearly all of the seventeen years.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call the adult the 'real' animal and the young a stage on the way to the adult. We do this because humans are adults for most of their lives. For a cicada or a mayfly it is the other way round. So the habit comes from our own timetable, and not from nature.  
*Evidence:* In Life Bars, the shaded 'young' part was 4 centimetres of the human's 20 centimetre bar, and all but 1 millimetre of the cicada's. The cicada nymph feeds, grows, digs and counts seventeen years. The winged adult lives about four weeks.

- *who am i:* You are a whole human being now, at the age you are, as a nymph is a whole cicada. Nearly every cell in you carries the full set of instructions for all of you.
- *what is life:* A life has no single correct shape. It can be seventeen years underground and one month in the air, or one long journey home, or a turning back to the start.
- *what is this world:* A salmon builds her body in the sea and leaves it in a mountain forest. The sea, the river, the bear and the tree are joined by her one journey.

Every animal in this unit begins as one cell. That one cell divides and becomes the many kinds of cell in a body: muscle, nerve and skin. All of them carry the same genes, so the one is still present in each of the many. In the immortal jellyfish, the many kinds of cell can go back towards one simple kind and begin again. And a brood of cicadas is millions of separate bodies who count the same years and come up as one crowd.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 1 (paraphrased):** The morning mushroom knows nothing of evening and dawn. The summer cicada knows nothing of spring and autumn. (Zhuangzi chose the cicada as his example of a short life, because he saw only the summer adult. He could not see the years underground. The part of a life that we notice may be only its last small part.)
- **Samuel Butler, Life and Habit (1878):** A hen is only an egg's way of making another egg. (Butler turned the usual view round. Seen from the nymph's side, the winged cicada is the nymphs' way of making more nymphs.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.13 (paraphrased):** The one who lives in a body passes through childhood, youth and old age in that body, and then passes on to another body. (The Gita says that something stays the same through all the stages of a life. Science finds one thing that does stay the same from egg to nymph to adult: the genes in every cell. Whether anything more stays the same is a question science has not answered.)
- Science (settled): Nearly every cell of a body carries the same full set of genes at every stage of life. John Gurdon showed this with frogs in 1962.
- Science (settled): The medusa of Turritopsis dohrnii can turn back into a polyp in the laboratory, again and again.
- Science (open): Whether any animal can avoid growing old altogether is not known.
- Other view, Many evolutionary biologists: The adult is the stage that matters most, because only adults breed. Natural selection keeps a way of living only if it ends in surviving young. On this view the nymph's seventeen years exist for the sake of the adult's four weeks, even though they are far longer.

Now close your eyes and take five slow breaths. With each breath, notice that you are fully here, at the age you are now. Open your eyes. Tell me one thing you love doing at this age.

- Ask: Is your childhood a time of getting ready for life, or is it life already?
- Ask: A polyp grows from an old medusa, with the same genes. What would have to stay the same for her to be the same animal?

## Story: The Ones Who Came Early
*A made-up story. The cicada is real. A periodical cicada lives in the Ri-Bhoi district of Meghalaya, comes out every four years in the same years as the football World Cup, and is called niangtaser by local people, who gather them to eat. Ibahun, Kyrshan, Kong Rilin, the school, the birds and the events of these evenings are imagined. The way the nymphs climb, split their skins and are eaten when few is how periodical cicadas are known to behave.*

In Ibahun's village in the Ri-Bhoi hills, people counted some things in fours. There were four years between one football World Cup and the next. There were also four years between one coming of the niangtaser and the next, and the two always fell in the same year.

Ibahun was nine. The last time, she had been five. She remembered a torch, her mother's hand, and a noise in the trees like rain on a tin roof. That was all.

This April everybody talked about it. Her cousin Kyrshan cleaned out an old biscuit tin. "I'll fill this in one night," he said. "Fried, with salt and chilli." Kong Rilin, who sold vegetables at the Nongpoh market and knew the forest better than anyone, only said, "Not yet. The ground is still cold."

Ibahun did not want to wait for everybody. She wanted to see the first one. So each evening she took her small torch to the big trees behind the school and looked at the ground.

On the fourth evening she found a hole, as round as if someone had pushed a finger into the mud. Beside the hole a brown creature was walking slowly towards the nearest trunk. They had bent front legs like little spades, and no wings at all. A nymph. Four years ago an egg had hatched on a twig above this spot. This small animal had dropped to the ground, dug down, and lived in the dark on root sap ever since. They had been down there since Ibahun was five.

She sat on a root and watched. The nymph climbed as high as her knee and held on. For a long time nothing happened. Then the brown skin split down the back, and a pale body pushed out, as soft as wet paper. The wings came out crumpled and slowly spread flat. The whole change took almost an hour. Ibahun forgot about dinner.

"Stay there," she whispered. "I'll come back in the morning."

In the morning the empty skin was still gripping the bark. The cicada was gone. A drongo sat on the school fence, wiping his beak on the wire.

The next evening three came up. She watched all three climb. By breakfast there were three more empty skins, and the hens were scratching about under the trees, looking pleased.

Ibahun went to find Kong Rilin, who was tying mustard leaves into bundles on her veranda.

"They're all being eaten," Ibahun said. "Four years under the ground, then one night, then a bird. Four came out and four are gone. Maybe there aren't many left this year. Maybe the new road killed them."

Kong Rilin went on tying. "How many birds are there behind the school?"

"I don't know. The drongo. Some mynas. The hens."

"And how many can a drongo eat before he is full?"

Ibahun thought. "Ten? Twenty?"

"Then wait for the warm rain," said Kong Rilin, "and count again."

The warm rain came on a Thursday. It fell all afternoon, and the evening was so mild that the windows were left open. After dark Kyrshan banged on the door with his tin. "Now!" he shouted.

Behind the school the ground looked like a sieve. There were holes everywhere, and from every hole a nymph was walking to a tree. They climbed over roots, over each other, over Ibahun's sandals. The trunks were brown with them as high as she could reach. Half the village was there with torches and tins and plastic bags. People picked nymphs off the bark like berries. Kyrshan filled his tin in a quarter of an hour and ran home for another.

Ibahun did not pick any. She walked from tree to tree with her torch. On every trunk the skins were splitting, and pale new cicadas were hanging out to dry. There were hundreds and hundreds, more than she could have counted if she had counted all night.

At sunrise she came back. The drongo was there, and so were the mynas, the hens and two dogs. They had all eaten. The drongo sat on the fence with his feathers puffed out. A cicada flew past his head, slow and clumsy, and he did not even turn to look. The hens had stopped scratching. And still every tree was covered.

By the end of the week the males had begun to sing. It was the sound she remembered, like rain on a tin roof, though the sky was clear.

She thought about her first cicada, the one she had told to stay. That one had waited four years, and found the tree, and climbed well. They had done everything right except one thing. They had come up on an evening when the drongo had nobody else to choose.

Kyrshan's family ate fried niangtaser for three days. So did most of the village. It made no difference that anyone could see.

A few weeks later the trees were quiet again. Under the big tree Ibahun found a fallen twig with a row of small slits cut along it. She knew what was inside. She looked at the ground below, where the new ones would soon dig in.

She did the sum on her fingers. Next time she would be thirteen. The time after that, seventeen. She put the twig back where she had found it.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the first cicada did nothing wrong except come up alone. A drongo can eat only so many insects in a morning. When four cicadas come up, the birds eat all four. When many thousands come up on the same night, the birds, the hens, the dogs and the villagers all eat until they are full, and almost every cicada is still left. Each cicada's safety comes from the timing they share with all the others. Scientists call this predator satiation. It is the reason the nymphs wait for the same warm evening.

- Ask: Four cicadas came up early and all four were eaten. Thousands came up later and most lived. What was different?
- Ask: Kong Rilin asked how many insects a drongo can eat before he is full. Why did that question matter?
- Ask: The villagers filled tins and bags with cicadas. Why did it make no difference to the cicadas as a whole?
- Ask: What signal do you think the nymphs were all waiting for?
- Ask: Where else do animals arrive in a crowd? Think of winged termites after rain, or turtles on a beach.

  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The bird has the same time each evening, so more evenings means more eaten.; In a big crowd the bird cannot get to everyone.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children expect the plans to come out about equal, because the same sixty cicadas come out either way.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Plan A. The bird closes their eyes. 2. Scatter 10 rajma all over the towel. 3. Say 'go' and time 10 seconds, then say 'stop'. 4. Count the rajma in the tumbler and write the number eaten. 5. Clear the towel and empty the tumbler. 6. Do this six times, for six evenings. 7. Add up how many were left over the six evenings.  
  *Parent says:* This is Plan A. Ten cicadas come out each evening. You have ten seconds each evening. Close your eyes while I scatter them.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Open them on 'go'. Pick up rajma for ten seconds. Count what you ate. Do six evenings.
  - Note: Expect the bird to get six to nine of the ten each evening. If the bird gets all ten every time, use a bigger towel or cut the time to seven seconds, and keep that time for the whole test.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Plan B. The bird closes their eyes. 2. Scatter 60 rajma all over the towel. 3. Time 10 seconds. 4. Count the rajma in the tumbler. Take that number from 60 to find how many were left. 5. Do Plan B three times. 6. Add the three numbers left and divide by 3 to find the mean.  
  *Parent says:* This is Plan B. All sixty come out on one evening. You still have only ten seconds. Then no more cicadas come out on the other five evenings.  
  *Child mode:* Now sixty come out at once. Pick for ten seconds. Count what you ate. Do this three times.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what will happen with crowds of 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80. 2. Test each crowd size once, for 10 seconds. 3. Write the number eaten and the number left each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now we change the crowd size step by step. Each crowd is twice as big as the one before. Will you eat twice as many each time?  
  *Child mode:* Test crowds of 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80. Ten seconds each. Write what you ate each time.
  - Ask: What do you expect to eat from a crowd of 80, and why? (good: About the same as from 40, because I cannot pick any faster.; A few more, because they are closer together.)
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each row, write the share eaten as a fraction, such as 8 out of 10. 2. Draw a line graph. Put the crowd size along the bottom and the number eaten up the side. 3. Look at the shape of the line.  
  *Parent says:* Let's write each result as a share. Eight out of ten is a big share. Nine out of eighty is a small share. Now draw the graph.  
  *Child mode:* Write each result as a share, like 8 out of 10. Draw the line graph. What shape is the line?
  - Note: The line usually climbs at first and then goes nearly flat. If one point is far off the others, test that crowd size again and say so in the table.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for the three sentences. 2. Ask what is different for real cicadas and real birds. 3. Only then read the card 'Safety in a crowd' together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then tell me one thing our test leaves out about real birds.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why. What does our test leave out?
  - Ask: The bird ate about the same number from 40 as from 80. So what happened to each cicada's chance of being eaten? (good: It halved, because the same meal was shared among twice as many.; The bigger the crowd, the safer each one is.)
  - Ask: What does our test leave out about real birds and cicadas? (good: Real birds get full, and that also limits them.; More birds may fly in when they see a crowd.; Real cicadas can fly away or hide.)
  - Note: A model shows one idea clearly and leaves other things out. Here the bird is limited by time. Real birds are limited by time and by a full stomach. Both limits give the same result: a predator can eat only so many.
  - Check: In the Crowd Test, a bird ate 8 rajma from a crowd of 10 and 9 from a crowd of 80. What does this show? → **Each one is much safer in the bigger crowd**
### e2 · The Home Stream Test: how weak a smell can your nose follow? (30 min)
- **question:** How much water can be added to a smell before your nose can no longer tell it from plain water?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which is the weakest mixture you will still be able to pick out: 1 part in 10, 1 in 100, 1 in 1,000 or 1 in 10,000? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Change only the strength of the mixture. Use three katoris of the same kind, with the same amount of liquid in each. The child must not see the katoris being filled. The grown-up looks away while the child chooses, so that a glance cannot give the answer. Do three tries at each strength, because one right pick out of three katoris can be luck.
- **safety:** Smell the mixtures and do not drink them. Vanilla essence contains alcohol.
- **record:** Fill in the table with a tick or a cross for every try. Circle the weakest strength where you got 3 out of 3. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think a nose has a limit.
- **results table:** Strength | Try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | Right picks out of 3
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 on four paper slips and stand a cup on each. 2. Put 9 teaspoons of water into each cup. 3. Add 1 teaspoon of the smelly liquid to cup 10 and stir. 4. Rinse the spoon. Move 1 teaspoon from cup 10 into cup 100 and stir. 5. Rinse the spoon. Move 1 teaspoon from cup 100 into cup 1,000. 6. Rinse again. Move 1 teaspoon from cup 1,000 into cup 10,000.  
  *Parent says:* Each cup is ten times weaker than the one before. Cup 10 has one part of smell in ten parts. Cup 10,000 has one part in ten thousand.  
  *Child mode:* Put 9 spoons of water in each cup. Add 1 spoon of smell to the first. Then pass 1 spoon along the row.
  - Note: Rinsing the spoon matters. A spoon that has been in a strong cup carries smell into the weak ones.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child smell cup 10 for a few breaths. 2. Tell them this is the smell of their home stream. 3. Ask for their guess and the reason. 4. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* This is the smell of your home stream. Learn it well. How weak can it get before your nose loses it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Smell the first cup. This is your home stream. Guess the weakest one you can find. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Ten thousand is a lot of water for one spoon of smell.; I can smell food from far away, so maybe my nose is good.)
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start with the weakest, cup 10,000. Your child turns away. 2. Put 3 teaspoons of the mixture into one katori and 3 teaspoons of plain water into each of the other two. 3. Shuffle the katoris, and remember where the mixture is. 4. Look away while your child smells all three and picks one. 5. Write a tick or a cross. 6. Shuffle and try again, until you have three tries. 7. Rinse the katoris. Do the same for cups 1,000, 100 and 10.  
  *Parent says:* Three streams meet here. One is your home stream. Smell each one and choose. Do not taste. I will look away while you choose.  
  *Child mode:* Turn away. Then smell all three katoris. Pick your home stream. Do three tries at each strength.
  - Note: We go from weakest to strongest, because a strong smell tires the nose for a while. Between tries, your child takes two breaths of fresh air. If they get 3 out of 3 at cup 10,000, make a cup 100,000 and test that too. If they get none even at cup 10, start again with a stronger liquid.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the right picks at each strength. 2. Circle the weakest strength with 3 out of 3. 3. Ask how many right picks pure guessing would give.  
  *Parent says:* Where did your nose lose the stream? If you only guessed, how many of three tries would you expect to get right?  
  *Child mode:* Count your right picks. Circle the weakest cup with 3 out of 3. Could that be luck?
  - Ask: If you only guessed, how often would you be right? (good: About one time in three, because there are three katoris.; Getting all three right by luck is rare, about 1 time in 27.)
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for the three sentences. 2. Ask how a salmon's task is like this test, and how it differs. 3. Only then read the card 'How a salmon finds home' together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you found, and why a nose has a limit. Then tell me how a salmon's task is like yours.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed. Say what you found. Say why a nose has a limit. How is a salmon's task like yours?
  - Ask: How is a salmon's task like this test? (good: She learns one smell when she is young.; At each fork in the river she picks the branch with that smell.; The smell gets weaker the further she is from home.)
  - Note: A smell is made of tiny particles that reach cells in the nose. With enough water there are too few particles for the nose to notice. A salmon's nose notices smells many thousands of times weaker than ours can.
  - Check: A girl picks the home katori right in 1 try out of 3. What is the best conclusion? → **She was probably guessing at that strength**

  - Note: There is no right way to draw the jellyfish. A bar that loops back on itself is a good answer. So is 'I cannot, because there is no end to measure to'.
  - Check: A periodical cicada lives 17 years. About how long is the winged adult stage? → **About 4 weeks**
### a2 · Run It Backwards: a dough model of the immortal jellyfish (15 min)
- **record:** Draw the jellyfish life cycle as a circle of stages: egg, swimming larva, polyp, young medusa, adult medusa. Then add one more arrow, from the adult medusa back to the polyp. Write one thing the dough model shows well and one thing it leaves out.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take two thirds of the dough and shape a bell like a small upturned bowl. 2. Roll eight thin tentacles and press them round the rim. 3. Make a short tube and press it under the middle, as the mouth. 4. Ask what job each part does.  
  *Parent says:* This is a medusa, the adult jellyfish. What do you think the bell does? The tentacles? The tube in the middle?  
  *Child mode:* Make a bell. Add eight tentacles. Add a mouth tube underneath. What does each part do?
  - Note: The bell squeezes to swim. The tentacles sting and catch tiny animals. The tube is the mouth and leads to the stomach. Each job is done by its own kinds of cells.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child the medusa is old and has no food. 2. Ask what she could do with the body she has. 3. After the guess, fold the tentacles into the bell and roll everything into one ball.  
  *Parent says:* She is old and starving. She has only the body she has. What could she do? Say your idea first. Then we will do what the real jellyfish does.  
  *Child mode:* She is old and hungry. What could she do? Guess. Then fold her into a ball.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press the ball onto the plate. 2. Pull part of it up into a short stalk. 3. Pinch small tentacles round the top of the stalk. This is a polyp. 4. Pinch two or three small buds off the side and shape each into a tiny medusa.  
  *Parent says:* The ball sinks and sticks to a rock. Now shape the young stage, the polyp, from the same dough. Then let the polyp bud off new little medusae.  
  *Child mode:* Stick the ball down. Pull up a stalk. Add small tentacles. Pinch off two tiny new medusae.
- **s4 (4 min)**

  *Parent says:* Where did the polyp's body come from? Dough has no cells. So what must happen inside the real jellyfish that our dough cannot show?  
  *Child mode:* Where did the polyp's body come from? What does dough leave out? Draw the life cycle with a backward arrow.
  - Ask: What must happen inside the real jellyfish that dough cannot show? (good: The cells have to change kind, for example a muscle cell into another kind of cell.; The same genes are there, and different ones get switched on.)
  - Note: The model shows that nothing is added: the old body is the material for the new one. It leaves out the cells. In the real animal the change takes about three days, and cells switch some genes off and others on.
  - Check: When the immortal jellyfish turns from a medusa into a polyp, where does the polyp's body come from? → **From the medusa's own cells, which change kind**

### c1 · The Fish Pass: get a hilsa past a barrage (30 min)
- **problem:** A barrage 12 centimetres high blocks your river. Build a path of pools from the top to the bottom. Water poured into the top pool must flow through every pool in turn. No drop between pools may be higher than 3 centimetres, because hilsa do not leap as salmon do.
- **your turn:** Now design your own fish pass. Draw it first and label every drop with its height. Build it, pour one tumbler of water in at the top and watch. Where does the water run too fast for a fish? How would you slow it?
- **constraints:** The top pool stands 12 centimetres above the bottom.; No drop from one pool to the next is more than 3 centimetres.; Every pool must hold water at least 1 centimetre deep, so that a fish could rest there.; The water must run from pool to pool, not spill onto the floor.
- **general:** Make a stair of four or five katoris on piles of books, each one 3 centimetres lower than the last.; Tilt each katori a little towards the next, so that the water leaves from one side.; Fold a small foil spout on the rim of each pool, to guide the water into the next.
- **creative:** Build one long, gentle channel that winds round the barrage like a side stream, lined with a wet towel. Real 'nature-like' fish passes work this way.; Build a zigzag, so that the stair fits into a small space.; Build a fish lift: a pool that is filled at the bottom, carried up by hand and emptied at the top.   *Child mode:* Build the barrage. Pour water over the top. Could a fish swim up? What would she need?

  *Child mode:* Pick an idea. Say why it should work. Build it. Measure each drop. Pour the water.
  - Ask: Why does a real fish pass have pools, and not just one long slope? (good: A fish can rest in each pool before the next climb.; The water slows down in each pool, so the fish is not swept back.)
- **s3 (12 min)**

### c2 · Design a Life Cycle: fit a timetable to a home (25 min)
- **problem:** Choose one home. Design an animal's timetable for it. Decide how many eggs, how big, how much care, how long the young stage lasts, when to breed and whether to breed once or many times. Give a reason for every choice.
- **your turn:** Now draw your animal's life as a bar, like the Life Bars. Mark each stage and how long it lasts. Then swap homes: put your animal in one of the other two homes. What goes wrong first?
- **constraints:** Home 1: a rain pool that holds water for four months and is dry mud for eight months, every year.; Home 2: a forest full of hungry birds, where the only food for your young is thin sap in tree roots.; Home 3: the deep, cold sea floor, with very little food and very few predators.; Your animal has a fixed egg budget. She can have many small eggs or a few big ones, and not many big ones.
- **general:** For the pool: grow up fast and breed before the water goes.; For the forest: come out in a big crowd, so that the birds cannot eat everyone.; For the deep sea: lay a few large eggs and guard them.
- **creative:** For the pool: lay eggs that can wait in dry mud for months. Annual killifish do this.; For the forest: stay underground for many years, so that the birds cannot grow in number on you. Periodical cicadas do this.; For the deep sea: guard the eggs for a very long time. One deep-sea octopus was watched guarding her eggs for 53 months.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read out the three homes. 2. Your child chooses one. 3. Ask what the biggest danger is in that home, and what the food is like.  
  *Parent says:* Which home will you design for? What is the biggest danger there? What is the food like? Those two answers will shape everything.  
  *Child mode:* Choose a home. What is the biggest danger there? What is the food like?
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child decides each part of the timetable and writes a reason beside it. 2. They draw the animal. 3. They draw the life as a bar, with each stage marked.  
  *Parent says:* How many eggs, and how big? Will you guard them? How long is the young stage? Will you breed once or many times? Give me a reason for each.  
  *Child mode:* Decide the eggs, the care, the young stage and the breeding. Write a reason for each. Draw the life bar.
  - Ask: Why did you choose to breed once, or many times? (good: Once, because the adult cannot live through the dry months anyway.; Many times, because the deep sea is safe and an adult can live long.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the usual and the creative solutions for the chosen home. 2. Compare them with your child's design. 3. Ask what would go wrong if the animal moved to one of the other homes.

  *Child mode:* Compare your design with a real animal's. Now move your animal to another home. What goes wrong?
  - Note: There is no single right design. A good answer gives a reason from the home for each choice. The swap shows that a timetable is good only for the home it fits.
  - Check: A fish lives in a pool that is dry for eight months of every year. Which timetable fits best? → **Grow fast, and lay eggs that can wait in dry mud**

## Worksheets
### Strange life cycles: cicada, salmon and jellyfish (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is unusual about the life cycle of a periodical cicada?  
   Options: The whole population comes out in one year / The adults live for 17 years / The eggs are laid deep underground / The nymphs can fly  
   **Answer:** The whole population comes out in one year. *Why:* All the nymphs of a brood grow up together and come out in the same few evenings. The adults live only about a month, and the eggs are laid in twigs. Choosing '17 years as adults' mixes up the nymph and the adult.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a cicada nymph sleeps through her years underground.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The nymph digs, drinks sap from roots, grows and sheds her skin four times. She even counts the years by changes in the tree's sap.
3. (fill) When so many prey arrive that predators eat until they are full, it is called predator ____.  
   **Answer:** ['satiation']. *Why:* Satiation means being full. Most of the prey are left alive because the predators can eat only so many.
4. (order) Put the stages of a Pacific salmon's life in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Egg in the gravel of a stream', 'Young fish in the river or a lake', 'Silver smolt swimming to the sea', 'Adult growing in the ocean', 'Adult back in the home stream, spawning']. *Why:* A salmon hatches in fresh water, grows in the sea and returns to fresh water to spawn. A strong answer can say what changes in the body at the smolt stage.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Spawn → to release eggs or sperm into water; Smolt → a young salmon ready for salt water; Polyp → the fixed, young stage of a jellyfish; Medusa → the swimming, adult stage of a jellyfish; Brood → all the cicadas that come out in one year in one region. *Why:* These words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an animal for each.
6. (sort) Sort these animals: breeds once in a life, or breeds many times.  
   **Answer:** Pacific salmon → Breeds once; hen → Breeds many times; mayfly → Breeds once; Asian elephant → Breeds many times; giant Pacific octopus → Breeds once; house crow → Breeds many times. *Why:* Pacific salmon, mayflies and octopuses put everything into one breeding and die. Hens, elephants and crows breed again and again.
7. (mcq) How does a salmon find the stream where she hatched?  
   Options: Mainly by its smell / By following older fish / By remembering what it looks like / Her parents lead her  
   **Answer:** Mainly by its smell. *Why:* She learned the smell of her stream as a young fish and follows it back. Her parents died years before, so nobody leads her.
8. (write) A bird eats the same number from a crowd of 10 and of 80. What does this mean for each cicada?  
   **Answer:** Each cicada in the crowd of 80 is much safer. The same small meal is shared among eight times as many, so each one's chance of being eaten is about eight times smaller.. *Why:* A strong answer talks about the share eaten, or each one's chance, and not only the number eaten.
9. (count) A bird eats 8 rajma from a crowd of 80. How many are left?  
   **Answer:** 72. *Why:* 80 − 8 = 72. That is 72 out of 80 left, or 9 out of every 10.
10. (draw) Draw the life cycle of the immortal jellyfish. Add the one arrow that makes it unusual.  
   **Answer:** A circle of stages: egg, swimming larva, polyp, young medusa, adult medusa. One extra arrow runs back from the adult medusa to the polyp.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the usual cycle first and then the backward arrow from medusa to polyp. An arrow from medusa to egg is the usual way, by breeding, and is not the unusual one.
11. (odd) Three of these give their young no care. Which one cares for her young?  
   Options: Ocean sunfish / Hilsa / Olive ridley turtle / Asian elephant  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* An elephant feeds her calf with milk for two years or more and the herd guards the calf. The sunfish, the hilsa and the turtle leave their eggs.
12. (truefalse) True or false: a fish who lays a million eggs usually leaves about a million grown-up young.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Nearly all the eggs and young are eaten or starve. Where the number of fish stays steady, about two from each pair grow up to breed.
13. (mcq) The immortal jellyfish can turn from adult back to young. Why is 'immortal' still not quite right?  
   Options: She can still be eaten or fall ill / She lives for only one year / The polyp is a plant / Nobody has ever seen the change  
   **Answer:** She can still be eaten or fall ill. *Why:* She can escape death from old age, but fish and sea slugs eat her, and disease kills her. The change has been seen many times in laboratories.
14. (label) On a drawing of a salmon's journey, label where she hatches, where she grows and where she spawns.  
   **Answer:** ['Hatches: in the gravel of a stream', 'Grows: in the ocean', 'Spawns: in the same stream where she hatched']. *Why:* A strong answer puts hatching and spawning at the same place. That return to the home stream is the unusual part.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about which stage is the real cicada.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the Life Bars drawing: the nymph stage is nearly all of the life, and the winged stage is the last month. It chooses one view, gives that evidence, and says what another view has right.. *Why:* All three have a point. The winged stage is the one that breeds, the nymph is the stage that lasts, and both are one animal with the same genes.
### Strange life cycles: evidence and reasons (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) The crowd protects cicadas. How does the 17-year wait help as well?  
   Options: Predators cannot multiply on such rare food / Nymphs need 17 years to learn to fly / Trees make sap only every 17 years / Birds forget what cicadas taste like  
   **Answer:** Predators cannot multiply on such rare food. *Why:* If cicadas came every year, more of the predators' young would be fed, and predator numbers would rise. A meal that comes once in 17 years cannot support extra predators in between.
2. (write) Scientists made peach trees flower twice in one year. Cicadas on their roots came out a year early. What does this show?  
   **Answer:** It shows that the nymphs count the trees' yearly cycles and not real time. Two flowerings in one year counted as two years.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the cicadas were counting, and why two flowerings fooled them.
3. (mcq) Salmon with plugged noses chose between two streams at random. What does this show?  
   Options: They need smell to find home / Cotton makes fish ill / They find home by sight / They have no home stream  
   **Answer:** They need smell to find home. *Why:* Fish with open noses chose their own branch, and fish who could not smell could not. So smell is what they use. Choosing 'cotton makes fish ill' is a fair worry, and it is why the open-nosed fish were handled the same way.
4. (fill) Water moves through the thin skin of a cell towards the ____ side.  
   **Answer:** ['saltier', 'more salty', 'salty']. *Why:* Water moves towards the side with more salt. This movement is called osmosis.
5. (truefalse) True or false: in the sea, a fish keeps losing water from her body to the salty water around her.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The sea is about three times as salty as her blood, so water moves out. She drinks sea water and pumps the extra salt out at her gills.
6. (sort) Sort these animals by the shape of their survival curve.  
   **Answer:** hilsa → Most die very young; a human in India today → Most live to old age; ocean sunfish → Most die very young; Asian elephant → Most live to old age; frog → Most die very young; blue whale → Most live to old age. *Why:* Animals with many eggs and no care lose most young in the first weeks. Animals with few, cared-for young mostly live until they are old.
7. (count) A 13-year brood and a 17-year brood came out together in 2024. In which year will they next come out together?  
   **Answer:** 2245. *Why:* They meet again after 13 × 17 = 221 years, because 13 and 17 share no factor. 2024 + 221 = 2245.
8. (match) Match each animal to the strange part of their timetable.  
   **Answer:** Axolotl → breeds while the body still looks young; Aphid → daughters are born with no father; Clownfish → a male can become a female; Annual killifish → eggs wait for months in dry mud; Tsetse fly → one baby at a time, fed on a milk-like liquid. *Why:* Each of these animals changes a different part of the usual timetable: when to grow up, whether a father is needed, which sex to be, how to wait, or how many young to have.
9. (write) A salmon dies in a mountain stream. Explain how part of her body can end up inside a tree.  
   **Answer:** Bears and birds carry dead salmon into the forest and leave parts of them. Insects, fungi and bacteria break the body down. The nitrogen from her body goes into the soil, and tree roots take it up.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the steps in order. An excellent one adds how we know: trees near salmon streams hold the heavier kind of nitrogen that comes from the sea.
10. (mcq) Which of these animals is semelparous?  
   Options: A mayfly / A hen / A hilsa / A house crow  
   **Answer:** A mayfly. *Why:* A mayfly breeds once and dies within a day or two. A hen, a hilsa and a crow can all breed again. Choosing the hilsa mixes her up with the Pacific salmon.
11. (draw) Draw three survival curves on one graph: elephant type, small bird type and fish type. Label each line.  
   **Answer:** The elephant line stays high and falls near the end. The bird line slopes down steadily. The fish line drops almost to zero at once and then runs flat.. *Why:* A strong drawing starts all three lines at the same point, at 100, and gives each a clearly different shape.
12. (order) Put the steps of the immortal jellyfish's backward change in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The medusa is starved, hurt or old', 'Her bell shrinks into a small ball', 'The ball sinks and sticks to the bottom', 'The ball grows into a polyp', 'The polyp buds off new medusae']. *Why:* The old body is the material for the new polyp, so the shrinking and settling come before the polyp grows.
13. (odd) Three of these animals appear in a huge crowd at the same time. Which one does not?  
   Options: Winged termites after rain / Periodical cicadas / Nesting olive ridley turtles / A tiger  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Winged termites, periodical cicadas and nesting olive ridley turtles all arrive together in great numbers. A tiger lives and hunts alone.
14. (mcq) A muscle cell and a nerve cell in the same body differ mainly in what?  
   Options: Which genes are switched on / Which genes they carry / How old they are / What food reaches them  
   **Answer:** Which genes are switched on. *Why:* Nearly every cell in a body carries the same genes. The kind of cell depends on which genes are in use. Choosing 'which genes they carry' is the old idea that John Gurdon's frog experiment showed to be wrong.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about catching hilsa as the fish swim upriver to spawn.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the life cycle: a fish caught on the way upriver has not yet laid her eggs, so that year's young are lost too. It also takes seriously the fishing family's need to eat during a ban.. *Why:* The scientist is right about the fish, and the other two are right about the people. Several states and Bangladesh now stop hilsa fishing for some weeks of the spawning season, and argue about how to support fishing families then.
### Strange life cycles: argued ideas and big numbers (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) No periodical cicada has a 12-year cycle. Give two different ideas scientists have about why 13 and 17 work better.  
   **Answer:** One idea: a predator with a cycle of 2, 3, 4 or 6 years would meet a 12-year cicada every time, but rarely a 13-year one. Another idea: broods with prime cycles rarely come out in the same year, so they seldom mix and spoil each other's timing.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two ideas and says that scientists have not settled which is right. A third idea, that 13 and 17 are partly chance, is also a good answer.
2. (mcq) The bird in the Crowd Test was limited only by time. What else limits a real bird?  
   Options: A stomach that gets full / The colour of the cicadas / The size of the towel / Nothing else limits her  
   **Answer:** A stomach that gets full. *Why:* A real bird stops eating when she is full. That limit, like the time limit in the model, means a predator can eat only so many.
3. (count) A pair of frogs lays 1,000 eggs. If every egg grew up, how many pairs would the next generation have?  
   **Answer:** 500. *Why:* 1,000 frogs make 1,000 ÷ 2 = 500 pairs. If each pair again laid 1,000 eggs, there would be 500,000 frogs in the generation after.
4. (write) A pair of frogs lays 1,000 eggs every year. Yet the pond has about the same number of frogs each year. Explain.  
   **Answer:** Nearly all the eggs and tadpoles die. Fish, insects and birds eat them, and some starve. On average only about two from each pair's eggs live to breed, which just replaces the parents.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the number that must survive, about two per pair, and says what happens to the rest.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a gut cell still carries the instructions for building a whole animal.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. John Gurdon put the nucleus of a tadpole's gut cell into an egg, and a whole frog grew. Nearly every cell keeps the full set of genes and uses only some of them.
6. (mcq) Landlocked sockeye salmon never go to sea, yet they die after spawning. What does this suggest?  
   Options: The long journey alone is not the cause / Fresh water slowly poisons them / They are not really salmon / They die of cold  
   **Answer:** The long journey alone is not the cause. *Why:* These fish make only a short trip and still die. So the death is set off from inside the body, by hormones, when the fish spawns. The journey makes it harder but does not cause it.
7. (sort) Sort these statements: settled by evidence, or still argued about.  
   **Answer:** Cicada nymphs count the trees' yearly cycles → Settled by evidence; Prime cycles protect cicadas from predators with cycles → Still argued about; Salmon follow smell to their home stream → Settled by evidence; Salmon steer by the Earth's magnetism in the open ocean → Still argued about; Some animals do not age at all → Still argued about; A medusa of the immortal jellyfish can become a polyp → Settled by evidence. *Why:* A settled statement has been tested many times with the same result. An argued one has some evidence, and scientists still disagree about it.
8. (draw) Draw a fish in a river and the same fish in the sea. Add arrows for where water moves.  
   **Answer:** In the river, the arrows point into the fish, and she passes a lot of watery urine. In the sea, the arrows point out of the fish, and she drinks sea water and pumps salt out at the gills.. *Why:* A strong drawing has the arrows pointing towards the saltier side each time: into the fish in the river, and out of the fish in the sea.
9. (fill) Growth by multiplying again and again is called ____ growth.  
   **Answer:** ['exponential']. *Why:* Exponential growth starts slowly and then becomes enormous. It is why most young of every kind of animal must die before they breed.
10. (order) Put these mothers in order, from the fewest eggs or young at one time to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['Asian elephant', 'Olive ridley turtle', 'Sockeye salmon', 'Hilsa', 'Ocean sunfish']. *Why:* An elephant has 1 calf. An olive ridley lays about 100 eggs, a sockeye about 3,000, a hilsa about a million and an ocean sunfish up to about 300 million.
11. (write) A polyp grows from an old medusa. Is she the same animal or a new one? Give one reason for each side.  
   **Answer:** The same animal: the polyp is made from the medusa's own cells and has the same genes, and no egg or parent is involved. A strong answer gives a real reason on each side and says which the writer finds stronger.
12. (mcq) A girl picks the home katori correctly 3 times out of 3. Why is that good evidence?

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Where does a periodical cicada spend most of her life?  
   Options: Underground, as a nymph / In the treetops, as an adult / Inside an egg / In a stream  
   **Answer:** Underground, as a nymph. *Why:* She is a nymph underground for 13 or 17 years and a winged adult for about a month.
2. (mcq) Millions of cicadas come out within a few evenings. How does this help each one?  
   Options: Predators get full, and most cicadas are left / They keep each other warm / Their noise frightens every bird away / They find food faster together  
   **Answer:** Predators get full, and most cicadas are left. *Why:* This is predator satiation. Birds and other predators eat all they can, and that is a small share of a huge crowd. The loud song is for finding mates.
3. (mcq) A bird eats 10 insects from a crowd of 20 and 10 from 200. Which crowd is safer to be in?  
   Options: The crowd of 200 / The crowd of 20 / Equally safe in both / It cannot be worked out  
   **Answer:** The crowd of 200. *Why:* In the crowd of 20, half are eaten. In the crowd of 200, only 1 in 20 is eaten. The same meal is a much smaller share of the bigger crowd.
4. (mcq) What does a young salmon learn before leaving for the sea?  
   Options: The smell of the home stream / The faces of her parents / The number of bends in the river / How to leap waterfalls  
   **Answer:** The smell of the home stream. *Why:* A young salmon imprints on the smell of the home stream and follows it back years later. Her parents died soon after spawning.
5. (mcq) Which test best shows whether salmon need smell to find home?  
   Options: Plug some noses and compare with open noses / Follow one fish up the river / Count the fish in the river / Smell the river water yourself  
   **Answer:** Plug some noses and compare with open noses. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing, the sense of smell, and compares two groups. Following one fish or counting fish cannot show which sense is used.
6. (mcq) What does 'semelparous' mean?  
   Options: Breeds once and then dies / Breeds many times / Lives in both river and sea / Changes from male to female  
   **Answer:** Breeds once and then dies. *Why:* A semelparous animal, such as a Pacific salmon or a mayfly, puts everything into one breeding. An animal who breeds many times is iteroparous.
7. (mcq) Why can it work for a Pacific salmon to spend her whole body on one spawning?  
   Options: Few would survive a second journey / Salmon dislike the sea / Eggs cannot hatch unless the parents die / The river is too warm to leave  
   **Answer:** Few would survive a second journey. *Why:* The return to the sea and a second climb up the river would kill most fish. So a salmon who holds something back for next time usually wastes what she held back.
8. (mcq) A mother has a fixed amount of material for eggs. What must she give up to lay more eggs?  
   Options: The size of each egg / The colour of each egg / Her own fins / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** The size of each egg. *Why:* The same material can be cut into a few big eggs or many small ones. More eggs means smaller eggs, each with less food for the baby.
9. (mcq) Where the number of animals stays steady, about how many young from each pair live to breed?  
   Options: About two / About half of the eggs / About a hundred / All of them  
   **Answer:** About two. *Why:* About two, which just replaces the parents. This is true for an elephant with a few calves and for a fish with a million eggs.
10. (mcq) What happens to the cells when the immortal jellyfish turns from a medusa into a polyp?  
   Options: Many change from one kind of cell into another / All die, and a new egg grows / They stay exactly as they were / They are shed into the sea  
   **Answer:** Many change from one kind of cell into another. *Why:* The medusa's own cells are reused, and many change kind by switching different genes on. This is called transdifferentiation.
11. (mcq) A model uses rajma for cicadas and a child for a bird. What is the best thing to say about its results?  
   Options: They show an idea that real animals must confirm / They prove what real birds do / They tell us nothing / They are true only for rajma  
   **Answer:** They show an idea that real animals must confirm. *Why:* A model shows clearly how one idea works. Counts of real cicadas and real birds are still needed, and scientists have made those counts.
12. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Why cicada cycles are 13 and 17 years / Whether salmon return to their home streams / Whether cicadas come out together / Whether jellyfish have a polyp stage  
   **Answer:** Why cicada cycles are 13 and 17 years. *Why:* The other three are well tested. For the prime-numbered cycles there are at least three ideas and no agreement.

## Applications
- Fish passes and fish lifts let salmon, hilsa and other river-run fish get past dams and barrages to the places where they spawn.
- Many places stop fishing for some weeks in the spawning season. Bangladesh and West Bengal do this for hilsa, so that the fish can lay their eggs first.
- Rules on the smallest fish that may be caught let each fish breed at least once before she can be taken.
- Doctors can now turn a person's skin or blood cells back into cells that can become any kind. In 2014, the eye doctor Masayo Takahashi in Japan was the first to treat a patient with tissue grown from such cells.
- Knowing a pest's timetable tells us when to act. Drying whole dal in hot sun kills the eggs and larvae of pulse beetles before the adults come out.
- Forest guards and volunteers protect the beaches of Odisha on the nights when olive ridley turtles nest together, and again when the eggs hatch.
- Fruit growers in the eastern United States know the cicada years in advance. They cover young trees with nets in those years, because egg-laying damages thin twigs.

## Open questions
- **Where in a cicada nymph's body is the count of years kept, and how does it reach exactly seventeen?** We know the nymphs count the trees' yearly cycles. Nobody has found the part of the body or the chemicals that store the count.
- **Why are periodical cicada cycles 13 and 17 years long?** There are at least three ideas: hiding from predators with cycles, keeping broods apart, and chance. No test has yet ruled any of them out.
- **How does a salmon steer across thousands of kilometres of open ocean to her own river mouth?** Smell works only near the river. There is some evidence that salmon sense the Earth's magnetism, but nobody has found the sense organ.
- **Does the immortal jellyfish turn back into a polyp in the sea, and how often?** The change has been seen only in laboratories. The medusa is 4 millimetres wide, and nobody has been able to follow one in the wild.
- **Could the cells of a mammal be reset safely inside a living body, as the jellyfish's cells are?** In mice, switching on the resetting genes for a short time has made some old tissues work better. Left on too long, the same genes cause tumours.
- **Does a polyp keep anything that the medusa learned before the change?** Jellyfish can learn simple things. Nobody has tested whether anything learned survives when the nerve cells are rebuilt.

## Connected maths
- A periodical cicada is a nymph for 17 years and an adult for 4 weeks. What fraction of her life is adult life? Take a year as 52 weeks. **About 1 part in 222.**. 17 years is 17 × 52 = 884 weeks. The whole life is 884 + 4 = 888 weeks. The adult part is 4 out of 888. Divide both by 4: that is 1 out of 222. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- A bird eats 8 cicadas each evening, whatever the size of the crowd. Sixty cicadas can come out 10 each evening for six evenings, or all on one evening. How many are left alive each way? **12 are left if they come out ten at a time. 52 are left if they all come out together.**. Ten at a time: each evening 10 − 8 = 2 are left, and 6 × 2 = 12. All together: the bird eats 8 on that one evening, and 60 − 8 = 52 are left. On the other five evenings there is nothing to eat. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L5`)
- In the Crowd Test a bird ate 8 rajma out of 10, and later 10 out of 80. What percentage was eaten each time? **80 percent of the small crowd, and 12.5 percent of the big crowd.**. 8 out of 10 is 80 out of 100, which is 80 percent. 10 out of 80 is 1 out of 8. One eighth of 100 is 12.5, so that is 12.5 percent. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- Imagine a 12-year brood and a 16-year brood that both come out this year. After how many years will they next come out together? Compare with a 13-year and a 17-year brood. **After 48 years for 12 and 16. After 221 years for 13 and 17.**. List the multiples of 16: 16, 32, 48. The first one that 12 also divides is 48. For 13 and 17, no number divides both, so the first shared multiple is 13 × 17 = 221. Broods with prime cycles meet far less often. (queues `math.factors-multiples.factors-and-multiples.L5`)
- In the Home Stream Test, each cup is 10 times weaker than the one before. The first cup is 1 part in 10. Which cup in the row would be 1 part in 10 billion? **The tenth cup.**. Each cup adds one zero: 10, 100, 1,000 and so on. Ten billion is 10,000,000,000, which has ten zeros. So it is the tenth cup. Salmon have followed a smell about that weak. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In the Home Stream Test there are three katoris, and one holds the smell. If you only guess, what is the chance of picking the right one three times in a row? **1 chance in 27.**. One guess is right 1 time in 3. For two in a row, multiply: 1/3 × 1/3 = 1/9. For three in a row: 1/9 × 1/3 = 1/27. So three right picks are very unlikely to be luck. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- A pair of frogs lays 1,000 eggs. Suppose every egg grows up, pairs off and lays 1,000 eggs too. How many frogs hatch in the third generation? **250 million frogs.**. First generation: 1,000 frogs, which is 500 pairs. Second generation: 500 × 1,000 = 500,000 frogs, which is 250,000 pairs. Third generation: 250,000 × 1,000 = 250,000,000 frogs. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
