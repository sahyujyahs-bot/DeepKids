# Adaptation: why a fish has fins and a camel has a hump
`sci.habitats.adaptations.L2`

**Summary.** A fish has fins, gills and a small bag of air inside her. A camel has a hump of fat, wide feet and long eyelashes. Each of these body parts helps with a problem of their own place: water all around, or hot, dry sand. A body part like this is called an adaptation. Your child makes a bottle fish with coins inside, and finds that a few spoons of air are enough to stop it sinking. You also look at plants suited to dry places and wet places, and at humans, who are animals suited to places too.

**Intro.** *A camel would sink in a river. A fish would dry up on hot sand. Why is each of them so good at living in one place, and so bad at the other?* Look at a fish and a camel side by side. The fish has flat fins, a smooth body and gills on the sides of her head. The camel has a hump, long legs, wide soft feet and very long eyelashes. Nothing about the fish would help on sand, and nothing about the camel would help under water. In this unit you find out what each body part does, and you make a bottle fish to test how a fish stays up in the water.

## Terms
- **Habitat**: A habitat is the place where a living being lives and finds what they need. *e.g.* A pond is a habitat for fish and frogs. The Thar Desert is a habitat for camels.
- **Adaptation**: An adaptation is a body part, or a way of behaving, that helps a living being live in their habitat. *e.g.* A camel's wide feet are an adaptation for walking on soft sand.
- **Fin**: A fin is a thin, flat part that sticks out of a fish's body. Fins push on the water, steer and keep the fish upright. *e.g.* A fish's tail fin pushes her forward. The fins on her sides help her turn and stop.
- **Gills**: Gills are the parts a fish breathes with. They take oxygen out of the water. *e.g.* If you lift the flap behind a fish's eye at the market, you see the red gills.
- **Swim bladder**: A swim bladder is a small bag of gas inside many fish. It stops the fish from sinking. *e.g.* A goldfish can stay still in the middle of her tank because of her swim bladder.
- **Hump**: A camel's hump is a big lump of fat on her back. Fat is stored food. *e.g.* When a camel has had little food for a long time, her hump gets smaller.
- **Desert**: A desert is a place that gets very little rain. *e.g.* The Thar Desert in Rajasthan is hot in the day and can be cold at night.
- **Spine**: A spine on a cactus is a hard, sharp point. A cactus's spines are leaves that have changed shape. *e.g.* A cactus on a balcony has spines in place of flat green leaves.
- **Float and sink**: Something floats when the water holds it up at the top. It sinks when it goes down to the bottom. *e.g.* An empty bottle with the cap on floats. A steel spoon sinks.
- **Generation**: A generation is one step in a family: grandparents, then parents, then children. *e.g.* You, your mother and your grandmother are three generations.

## Explanations
### What an adaptation is · *defined*
**Definition:** An adaptation is a body part, or a way of behaving, that helps a living being live in their habitat.

Every habitat has its own problems. In water, a body must move, breathe and stay up. In a desert, a body must save water and walk on hot sand. A living being's adaptations are their answers to these problems. An adaptation that helps in one place may be no help in another. Wide feet help a camel on sand, but they would not help a fish.
- e.g. A fish's fins help her swim.
- e.g. A camel's long eyelashes keep sand out of her eyes.
- e.g. A lotus leaf floats on a pond.
- e.g. Your sweat cools you on a hot day.
### Fins: how a fish moves in water · *defined*
**Definition:** Fins are thin, flat parts of a fish's body that push on the water.

A fish swims by bending her body from side to side. Her tail fin pushes the water back, and the water pushes her forward. The fin on her back stops her from rolling over. The fins on her sides help her steer, turn and stop. Water is thick and heavy to move through, so a fish's body is smooth and narrow at the front. Her slime protects her skin from germs.
- e.g. A rohu has seven fins.
- e.g. A sardine, called mathi in Kerala, swims fast with her forked tail fin.
- e.g. A pomfret is flat and tall, and turns quickly between rocks.
### Gills and the swim bladder · *defined*
**Definition:** Gills take oxygen out of water. A swim bladder is a bag of gas inside many fish that stops them sinking.

Water has a little oxygen mixed into it. A fish's gills are many thin red flaps. In water the flaps spread apart and take oxygen into her blood. Out of water, they stick together and dry out, so most fish cannot breathe for long in air. A fish's bones and muscles are a little heavier than water. Many fish carry a swim bladder, a bag of gas inside, so the whole fish weighs the same as the water around her and stays at one depth. Sharks have no swim bladder, and many sharks slowly sink if they stop swimming.
- e.g. A goldfish hangs still in her tank.
- e.g. A carp gulps air at the top of a pond to fill her swim bladder.
- e.g. A shark keeps swimming, or sinks slowly to the sea floor.
### A camel's body for the desert · *defined*
**Definition:** A camel's hump, feet, eyelashes, nostrils and fur are adaptations to hot, dry, sandy places.

The camels of the Thar Desert have one hump. The hump is a store of fat, not water. When food is hard to find, the camel lives on this fat, and the hump gets smaller. Her feet are wide, with two toes joined by a soft, thick pad that spreads out on sand. Two rows of long eyelashes, and a thin third eyelid, keep sand out of her eyes. She can close her nostrils to thin slits in a sandstorm. Thick fur on her back keeps the sun's heat off her skin. Hard, thick pads of skin on her knees and chest let her kneel on hot sand.
- e.g. A camel at the Pushkar fair in Rajasthan kneels on her chest pads to rest.
- e.g. Bactrian camels, with two humps, live in the cold Nubra Valley of Ladakh.
- e.g. A camel can eat thorny babool and khejri branches without hurting her mouth.
### Why is a camel's fat in one hump? · *idea*
Most animals store fat as a layer under the skin, all over the body. A camel keeps much of hers in one hump. Nobody can be completely sure why. The main idea is about heat. A layer of fat all over would act like a blanket and keep heat in. With the fat in one place, heat can leave the rest of her body more easily.
- e.g. A buffalo stores fat all over her body.
- e.g. A camel stores much of her fat in her hump.
- **Many biologists:** Keeping fat in one hump lets heat escape from the rest of the camel's body, which helps in a hot desert.
- **Other scientists:** The hump may simply be the best place to carry a big food store while walking long distances. The heat idea may be only part of the answer.
### Plants suited to their places · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants have adaptations too: in their leaves, stems, roots and seeds.

Plants lose water through their leaves, so many plants of dry places have tiny leaves, or none at all. A cactus has spines in place of leaves, and stores water in her thick, waxy green stem. An aloe keeps water in thick, juicy leaves, and opens the tiny holes in her leaves at night, when the air is cool. In a pond, a lotus leaf floats, and rain rolls off her waxy top. Mangrove trees grow in salty mud by the sea, and push breathing roots up out of the mud to get air. A coconut floats on the sea for weeks, and can grow on a new beach far away.
- e.g. A cactus on a balcony in Chennai.
- e.g. The ker bush of the Thar, who has almost no leaves and makes food in her green stems.
- e.g. Lotus leaves in a temple tank.
- e.g. Mangrove roots at Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu and in the Sundarbans.
- e.g. Khejri trees in the Thar, with very deep roots.
- **Scientists who study what plants sense:** Plants sense their world and decide what to do. An aloe senses the cool of the night and opens her leaf holes then, to save water.
- **Other scientists:** These are automatic responses, built into the plant's cells, with no thinking needed.
### Where do adaptations come from? · *defined*
**Definition:** Adaptations pass from parents to their young, and change slowly over very many generations.

A camel calf is born with wide feet and long eyelashes, before she has ever walked on sand. She got them from her parents. In every family, some young are a little different from others. In a desert, young camels whose feet spread a little wider might walk more easily and live longer. They have more calves, who are born with wide feet too. Over thousands of generations, the whole family changes to suit their place. One camel cannot grow wider feet by walking on sand, and pass them on.
- e.g. A puppy has her mother's ears.
- e.g. A camel calf has long eyelashes at birth.
- e.g. A baby fish hatches with fins.
### Humans are animals suited to places too · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans have adaptations to their habitats, just like other animals.

Humans cool down by sweating. Our skin has millions of tiny sweat glands, and sweat drying on the skin carries heat away. Some groups of people have adaptations to their own places. The Bajau people of Indonesia have dived for fish for over a thousand years. In 2018, the scientist Melissa Ilardo found that their spleens are bigger than those of their neighbours. The spleen is an organ that stores blood, and a bigger one may help them dive for longer. Tibetan people live high in the mountains, where the air is thin. In 2014, Emilia Huerta-Sánchez and her team found a gene version that helps them live there. People also suit themselves to places by what they make: boats, shawls and houses.
- e.g. You sweat when you run on a hot day.
- e.g. Bajau divers can stay under water for a long time.
- e.g. Tibetan people live well in thin mountain air.

## Mostly agreed
- Fish breathe with gills, which take oxygen out of the water. *How we know:* Scientists have watched water flow in through a fish's mouth and out over the gills. The blood leaving the gills carries more oxygen than the blood going in.
- A camel's hump is a store of fat, not water. *How we know:* Scientists have measured what the hump is made of. When a camel has little food for a long time, the hump shrinks and can flop to one side.
- Adaptations pass from parents to their young, and change over many generations. *How we know:* Young animals are born with the adaptations of their family. A camel calf has wide feet and long eyelashes before she ever walks on sand.
- Fish, camels and humans all come from the same ancient ancestors. *How we know:* Fossils show fish with fin bones that slowly became the leg bones of four-legged animals. Your arm has the same pattern of bones.

## How we found out
**Jenny Clack, a scientist who studied fossils at the University of Cambridge, with her team; Alfred Romer, an American scientist who held the older idea, The expedition was in 1987, in East Greenland. The main papers came out in 1990 and 1991..** Your arms and legs came, very long ago, from the fins of fish. But why did fins turn into legs? For many years, many scientists, such as Alfred Romer in America, thought it happened because of land. They said some fish lived in ponds that dried up. Fish with strong fins crawled over land to the next pond, and over a long time their fins became legs. In 1987, Jenny Clack, a scientist from Cambridge in England, led a team to the cold mountains of East Greenland. In the rocks they found skeletons of an animal called Acanthostega, who lived about 365 million years ago. Acanthostega had four legs, with eight fingers on each front leg. But they also had gills like a fish, and a flat tail with a fin. Their legs were too weak to walk well on land. So the first legs grew on animals who still lived in water. Legs may have helped them push through shallow, weedy water. Walking on land came later.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought legs first grew so that fish could crawl over land from one drying pond to the next. Acanthostega had legs, but also gills and a tail fin, and lived in water. So legs first suited life in water.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A camel's hump is full of water.** (A very common idea in many countries; *rejected*)  
  For: Camels can go many days without drinking, so people guessed they carried water.  
  Against: The hump is mostly fat. When a camel has no food for a long time, her hump gets smaller and can flop to one side.
- **Every fish dies quickly out of water.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Most fish cannot breathe for long in air, because their gills stick together and dry out.  
  Against: Mudskippers in mangroves hop about on mud, breathing through their skin and the inside of their mouth. The climbing perch, called koi in Bengal, breathes air with a special organ and can wriggle across wet ground.
- **Legs first grew so that fish could walk on land.** (Many scientists in the 1900s, such as Alfred Romer in America; *rejected*)  
  For: Some fish living in drying ponds might have used strong fins to crawl to the next pond.  
  Against: In 1987, Jenny Clack's team found Acanthostega, an animal with legs who also had gills and a tail fin, and lived in water. So legs first helped in water.
- **Fingers wrinkle in water to help us grip wet things.** (Mark Changizi and other scientists, from 2011; *debated*)  
  For: In a test in 2013, people with wrinkled fingers moved wet marbles faster than people with smooth fingers.  
  Against: In another test in 2014, wrinkled fingers did not help at all. Scientists still argue about it.

## Roots
We have looked at fins, gills, a swim bladder, a camel's hump and a cactus's spines, and made a bottle fish float. Now we ask what being suited to a place tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your bottle fish sank when it was full of coins and water. Swapping just a few spoons of water for air made the same bottle float. Air weighs almost nothing, so a little air was enough to keep the heavy bottle up. A fish's small swim bladder keeps her heavy body from sinking in the same way.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think heavy things sink and light things float. But a body of heavy bones and muscles floats in mid-water with only a small bag of gas inside. Whether a body sinks depends on how heavy it is for its size, compared with the water around it.  
*Evidence:* In the Bottle Fish test, the full bottle with coins sank every time. After two to four spoons of water were swapped for air, the same bottle with the same coins floated.

- *who am i:* You are an animal suited to your place too. You sweat to cool down, and the bones in your arm come from the fin bones of fish long ago.
- *what is life:* Living beings come in shapes that fit their homes: fins for water, wide feet for sand, spines for dry ground.
- *what is this world:* Each place on Earth asks different things of a body. The living beings there answer in their own ways.

Fish, camels, cactus plants and you belong to one very old family of life. Long ago, all of them came from the same early living beings. Over very many generations, that family spread into seas, deserts and snowy mountains, and their bodies changed to suit each place. Your arm and a fish's fin are built on the same pattern of bones. One family of life became many shapes, one for each kind of home.

- **Tirukkural 495, chapter 50 (Knowing the Place), by Thiruvalluvar:** In deep water, a crocodile beats anyone who fights her. Out of the water, anyone can beat the crocodile. (A body's strength belongs to a body in their own place. Your bottle fish needed water to float, and a camel's wide feet help her only on sand.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 18 (Supreme Happiness), the seabird of Lu:** A seabird landed near the city of Lu. The ruler welcomed the bird with music, wine and fine meat. The bird was frightened, ate nothing and died in three days. Zhuangzi says the ruler cared for the bird the way he would care for himself, not the way a bird lives. (What suits one being can harm another. A fish needs water around her gills, and a camel needs dry sand under her feet.)
- Science (settled): The bones in your arm and the bones in a fish's fin come from the same ancient ancestors. Scientists see this in fossils, and in how the bones grow in a baby before birth.
- Science (debated): Fish learn and remember. Some fish have remembered the way out of a net for almost a year. Whether fish feel pain the way we do is still argued about.
- Other view, Many biologists: A body part was not made for a purpose. Over many generations, the young who happened to have useful parts lived longer and had more young. Saying 'fins are for swimming' is a short way of saying this.
- Practice, Float Like a Fish (3 min): Sit down and close your eyes. Breathe in slowly, and feel your chest fill with air, like a swim bladder. Hold it for a moment. Now breathe out slowly, and feel your body sink a little lower. Do this three times. Then sit still, and feel where your body meets the floor.
- Ask: If a camel's family lived in the sea for millions of years, how might their bodies change?
- Ask: Is a fin a good thing, or only a good thing in water?
- Ask: Which place on Earth is your body most suited to? How do you know?

## Story: Moti and the Mathi
*A made-up story. Camels really do have humps of fat, thick fur that keeps out the sun's heat, nostrils that close in sandstorms, two rows of eyelashes, wide padded feet and hard pads for kneeling.*

Anju lived in Kochi, near the backwaters. She was seven, and she knew a lot about fish.

Every Sunday she went to the fish market with her grandmother, Ammamma. Ammamma had sold fish there for forty years. Anju knew the silver mathi, the striped bangda and the flat, shining pomfret.

One December, Anju's Amma went to work for two weeks at a children's health camp. The camp was in Jaisalmer, in the Thar Desert in Rajasthan. Amma was a doctor. Anju and her Appa went with her.

The desert was nothing like Kochi. There were no backwaters, no coconut trees and almost no green. There was sand in long, soft hills, and thorny bushes, and a huge blue sky. The mornings were cold, so cold that Anju wore a shawl. By noon the sun was hot on her head.

On the second day, they met Bhanwari. Bhanwari kept camels at the edge of the village. Her favourite camel was called Moti. Moti was tall and sandy brown, with one big hump.

'Poor Moti,' said Appa. 'Look at all that thick fur in this heat. Someone should cut it short for her.'

Bhanwari laughed. 'Wait until noon,' she said, 'then put your hand on her back.'

At noon Anju put her hand flat on Moti's back. The top of the fur was hot from the sun. Then she pushed her fingers deep into the fur, down near the skin. It was much cooler there.

'The fur keeps the sun's heat off her skin,' said Bhanwari. 'And tonight, when it is cold, it will keep her warm.'

Appa nodded slowly. 'And that hump is her water tank,' he said. 'That is how she goes days without drinking.'

'Many people think so,' said Bhanwari. 'But it is fat. When there is no good food, Moti lives on her fat. Then the hump gets smaller and flops over to one side. When she eats well again, it fills up.'

Anju wrote it all down in her notebook. HUMP: FAT, NOT WATER. FUR: KEEPS THE SUN OUT.

That afternoon a strong wind blew. Sand flew through the air and stung Anju's legs. She shut her eyes tight and coughed. Moti did not seem to mind at all. Bhanwari pointed at the camel's face.

'Look at her nose,' she said. Moti had squeezed her nostrils into two thin slits. Her eyes were half shut behind two rows of long eyelashes.

'She has eyelashes like a film star,' said Anju, and Bhanwari laughed again.

When the wind dropped, they walked out on the dunes. Appa's shoes sank deep into the soft sand with every step. He puffed and slipped. Moti walked beside him easily. Anju watched her big, round feet. Each foot spread out wide and flat as it pressed on the sand.

'Her feet are like two chapatis,' said Anju.

Then Moti folded her front legs and knelt on the hot sand. Under her chest and on her knees there were thick, hard pads of skin, like the heel of a foot. 'She can kneel anywhere,' said Bhanwari, 'even on sand that would burn your hand.'

Two weeks later, Anju was back in Kochi. On Sunday she went to the fish market with Ammamma. She picked up a mathi and looked at the fish carefully. The mathi had no hump, no eyelashes and no feet. She was smooth and slippery, with a forked tail fin and small fins on her sides. Ammamma lifted the flap behind the eye. The gills underneath were bright red.

'Ammamma,' said Anju, 'why doesn't a fish have a hump?'

Ammamma smiled. 'What would a fish do with a hump in the sea?' she asked.

Anju thought about it. The sea is full of water, so a fish never needs to save any. The water holds a fish up, so she does not need wide feet. She needs fins to push the water and to steer. She needs gills to take oxygen out of the water.

'And Moti needs a hump for the days with no food,' said Anju. 'And wide feet for the sand. And eyelashes for the wind.'

She thought of Moti standing in the backwaters, sinking into the mud. Then she thought of the mathi lying on a hot dune. She laughed.

'Moti would be no good here,' she said. 'And the mathi would be no good there. Each one is made for her own place.'

That night she drew Moti and the mathi side by side in her notebook. Under Moti she wrote: MADE FOR SAND. Under the mathi she wrote: MADE FOR WATER.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Anju compares two animals from two very different places. She sees that every part of the camel answers a problem of the desert, and every part of the fish answers a problem of the water. Each body suits their own place, and would be no help in the other.

- Ask: Why did Appa think Moti's fur was a bad idea? What did Anju find when she felt the fur?
- Ask: What did Bhanwari say the hump is for?
- Ask: Why doesn't a fish need a hump?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bottle Fish: how much air stops a heavy body sinking? (25 min)
- **question:** A bottle with coins inside sinks when it is full of water. How many spoons of air does it need before it floats?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the bottle need a lot of air to float, about half a bottle, or only a little? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same bottle, the same coins and the same bucket each time. The only change is how much air is inside. We test each amount twice, because a bubble stuck on the outside can fool us once.
- **safety:** Wipe up spilled water at once, so nobody slips. Never leave a baby or a small child alone near a bucket of water.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the bottle fish on the bottom of the bucket, and again floating at the top. Write how many spoons of air it needed.
- **results table:** Spoons of water poured out (spoons of air in) | Sinks or floats? | Test 2: sinks or floats?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the coins into the empty bottle. 2. Hold the bottle under the water in the bucket. 3. Let it fill right to the top, until no more bubbles come out. 4. Close the cap under the water.  
  *Parent says:* This bottle is our fish. The coins are like a fish's bones and muscles. Bones and muscles are heavier than water.  
  *Child mode:* Put the coins in the bottle. Fill it under the water. Close the cap.
  - Note: Filling the bottle under water gets every bubble out. Even one big bubble can make it float, so fill it slowly.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the closed bottle in the middle of the water. Let go and watch.  
  *Parent says:* Let go. Does our fish sink or float?  
  *Child mode:* Let go. Does the bottle sink or float?
  - Note: The bottle must sink to the bottom. If it floats, open it, add one more coin or stone, and fill it under water again.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess and to say why. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will swap some water for air, one spoon at a time. How many spoons until it floats? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will add air, one spoon at a time. How many spoons to float? Why?
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the bottle out and open it. 2. Pour one big spoon of water out of the bottle. 3. Close the cap. 4. Put the bottle under the water and let go. 5. Write sinks or floats in the table. 6. Test it once more.

  *Child mode:* Pour out one spoon of water. Close it. Let go. Sink or float?
  - Note: Pour slowly from the bottle into the spoon, over the bucket. Each time, push the bottle to the middle of the water before you let go. Many 500 ml bottles with five coins float after two to four spoons.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep going, one spoon at a time, until the bottle floats. 2. Then pour out three more spoons and test again. 3. Look at how high it floats now.  
  *Parent says:* How many spoons did it need? Now let's add even more air. Does it float higher?  
  *Child mode:* Keep going until it floats. Then pour out three more spoons.
  - Ask: How many spoons of air did the bottle need to float? (good: Only two or three spoons.; Just a little air, much less than half the bottle.)
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child three things. What do you think? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Parent says:* The bottle had coins and lots of water inside, but only a little air. Why did a little air help so much?  
  *Child mode:* What did you see? Why did a little air help?
  - Ask: Why did a little air make the heavy bottle float? (good: Air is very light, so the bottle got lighter.; The air took the place of some heavy water.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the result. With no air it sank, and with a little air it floated. Air weighs almost nothing. Swapping a little water for air made the whole bottle light enough to float. Many fish have a bag of gas inside like this, called a swim bladder.
  - Check: Many fish have a small bag of gas inside, called a swim bladder. What does it do for the fish? → **It stops the fish from sinking**

   **Answer:** ['spines: the sharp points all over', 'thick green stem: the fat body of the cactus, which stores water', 'waxy skin: the shiny outside of the stem', 'wide roots: spreading under the soil, near the top']. *Why:* Spines in place of leaves lose less water. The thick stem stores water and makes food. The waxy skin keeps water in. Wide roots near the top soak up rain quickly when it comes.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is an adaptation?  
   Options: A part of a body, or a way of behaving, that helps a living being live in their place / A new home for an animal / A kind of food  
   **Answer:** A part of a body, or a way of behaving, that helps a living being live in their place. *Why:* An adaptation helps a living being live in their habitat, like a camel's wide feet on sand.
2. (mcq) Which fin pushes a fish forward through the water?  
   Options: The fin on her back / The tail fin / The fins near her head  
   **Answer:** The tail fin. *Why:* The tail fin pushes the water back, and the water pushes the fish forward. If you picked the back fin, that one keeps her from rolling over.
3. (mcq) A fish is taken out of water. Why can't she breathe for long?  
   Options: There is no oxygen in air / Her gills stick together and dry out / Her fins stop working  
   **Answer:** Her gills stick together and dry out. *Why:* Air has plenty of oxygen. But out of water, the thin gill flaps stick together, like the paintbrush hairs, so the fish cannot take enough of it in.
4. (mcq) In the Bottle Fish test, the bottle full of coins and water sank. What made it float?  
   Options: Adding more coins / Swapping a little water for air / Shaking it hard  
   **Answer:** Swapping a little water for air. *Why:* Air weighs almost nothing. A few spoons of air in place of water made the whole bottle light enough to float.
5. (mcq) Many fish have a bag of gas inside their bodies. What is it called?  
   Options: A gill / A swim bladder / A hump  
   **Answer:** A swim bladder. *Why:* The swim bladder holds gas. It stops a fish from sinking, like the air in your bottle fish.
6. (mcq) What is a camel's hump mostly made of?  
   Options: Water / Fat / Bone  
   **Answer:** Fat. *Why:* The hump is a store of fat. Many people think it holds water, but it does not. When a camel has little food, the hump shrinks.
7. (mcq) Why does a camel have two rows of long eyelashes?  
   Options: To keep blowing sand out of her eyes / To see in the dark / To keep her cool  
   **Answer:** To keep blowing sand out of her eyes. *Why:* In the desert, the wind blows sand. Long eyelashes help keep it out of her eyes.
8. (mcq) Why does a camel have thick fur on her back in the hot desert?  
   Options: It makes her hotter / It keeps the sun's heat off her skin / It helps her swim  
   **Answer:** It keeps the sun's heat off her skin. *Why:* The fur keeps the sun's heat away from her skin in the day, and keeps her warm on cold desert nights. Deep in the fur, near her skin, it stays much cooler than on top.
9. (mcq) A cactus has spines and no flat leaves. How does that help in a dry place?  
   Options: Spines help the cactus lose less water than wide leaves would / Spines help the cactus float / Spines catch fish  
   **Answer:** Spines help the cactus lose less water than wide leaves would. *Why:* Plants lose water through their leaves. With spines in place of wide leaves, a cactus loses much less, and keeps water in her thick stem.
10. (mcq) Which plant is suited to living in a pond?  
   Options: A cactus / A lotus / A khejri tree  
   **Answer:** A lotus. *Why:* Lotus leaves float on the water, and rain rolls off their waxy tops. A cactus and a khejri tree are suited to dry places.
11. (mcq) How did camels come to have wide feet?  
   Options: Each camel stretched her feet by walking on sand / Wide feet passed from parents to calves, and slowly changed over many generations / People gave camels wide feet  
   **Answer:** Wide feet passed from parents to calves, and slowly changed over many generations. *Why:* A calf is born with her parents' wide feet. Over thousands of generations, camels whose feet suited the sand did best, so the whole family changed.
12. (mcq) Which of these is a human adaptation to hot places?  
   Options: Sweating / Gills / A hump  
   **Answer:** Sweating. *Why:* Humans are animals, and sweating is one of our adaptations. Sweat drying on the skin carries heat away.

## Applications
- Submarines sink and rise by letting water into tanks and pushing it out with air, much like your bottle fish and a fish's swim bladder.
- Life jackets hold air or light foam to keep a person from sinking.
- Farmers grow crops suited to their place: bajra in the dry Thar, and rice in wet Kerala and Bengal.
- In the 1930s at Coimbatore, the plant scientist Janaki Ammal helped breed sugarcane that grows well in Indian fields.
- Boat makers shape boats narrow at the front, like a fish, so they move through water more easily.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of fish live in the deep sea?** The deep sea is huge and dark, and very little of it has been explored. Scientists find new kinds of fish there nearly every year.
- **Why do our fingers wrinkle in water?** Some tests say wrinkles help us grip wet things. Other tests found no help. Scientists still argue.
- **Can living beings change fast enough as the Earth gets warmer?** Adaptations usually change over many generations. Nobody knows how many kinds of living beings can keep up with a fast change.
- **How did the first fish start breathing air?** Fossils show early fish with lungs as well as gills, but how and why air-breathing began is still being worked out.

## Connected maths
- Lakshmi's bottle fish floated after 3 spoons of water were poured out. Her friend Nila's bottle floated after 5 spoons. How many more spoons did Nila's bottle need? **2 more spoons.**. Count up from 3 to 5: 4, 5. That is 2 more. Nila's bottle may have had more coins, or been a different size. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- Three rohu fish lie on the table at the market. Each rohu has 7 fins. How many fins are there in all? **21 fins.**. 7 and 7 is 14. 7 more is 21. That is three groups of 7. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- A picture shows 4 camels. You count 7 humps. Some camels have one hump and some have two. How many camels have two humps? **3 camels have two humps, and 1 camel has one hump.**. Every camel has at least one hump, so 4 camels give 4 humps. There are 7 humps, so 3 humps are extra. Each extra hump belongs to a two-humped camel. So 3 camels have two humps. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L2`)
- Your 500 ml bottle holds about 33 big spoons of water. It floated after 3 spoons of water were poured out. Is 3 spoons more or less than half the bottle? **Much less than half.**. Half of 33 is about 16 or 17. 3 is much less than 16. So the bottle needed only a small amount of air to float. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L2`)
