# Testing materials: how sure can a test make us?
`sci.materials.testing-materials.L4`

**Summary.** Drop a rubber ball ten times from the same height, and it bounces to ten slightly different heights. So when a marble bounces lower than the ball, is the marble really worse, or is that just the ordinary wobble in every test? Your child drops balls again and again, draws every bounce as a dot, and finds out when a difference is real. Along the way they meet three ways a test can fool us: the material varies, our measuring is rough, and we hope for an answer. Scientists have a trick against each one.

**Intro.** *You drop a rubber ball from the height of your hip, and it bounces to 31 centimetres. You drop a table-tennis ball from the same height, and it bounces to 33 centimetres. So the table-tennis ball is bouncier. Is that right? Now drop the rubber ball again. This time it bounces to 34.* The same ball gave two different numbers, with nothing changed. So can one drop tell you anything at all? This is the next question in testing materials. In the Class 3 unit you learned to change one thing at a time and to repeat a test three times. Here you go a step further. You will repeat a test ten times, draw every result as a dot, and ask how big the ordinary wobble is. Only a difference bigger than the wobble can be believed. You will also learn why your own eyes and hopes can fool you, and how scientists test in the dark on purpose.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can repeat a test many times and show all the results as dots on a dot plot, so I can see how much they vary.
1. I can find the range and the middle value of a set of results, and say what each one tells me.
2. I can tell when a difference between two materials is real, and when it is smaller than the ordinary wobble.
3. I can explain the difference between a rough measuring tool, a wrong measuring tool, and a tool that is fine but used badly.
4. I can explain how my own hopes can change a result, and run a blind test with secret codes and random order.
5. I can plan a wear test that copies real use, and say what the test leaves out.
6. I can write a claim, evidence and reasoning that also says how sure I am, and why.

## Terms
- **Variation**: The small differences you get when you do the same thing again and again. Even a careful test gives different numbers each time. *e.g.* Ten drops of the same rubber ball gave ten slightly different bounce heights.
- **Spread**: How far apart your results are, from the smallest to the biggest. A wide spread means the results wobble a lot. *e.g.* The bounce heights of one ball spread from 30 to 35 centimetres.
- **Range**: The smallest result and the biggest result, and the gap between them. You say it as 'from 30 to 35', or you say the gap, which is 5. *e.g.* The marble bounced from 23 to 28 centimetres. Its range is 5 centimetres.
- **Dot plot**: A picture with a number line and one dot for every result. Dots that are the same stack up. You can see the spread at one glance. *e.g.* A dot plot of ten bounce heights, with a tall stack of dots at 32 centimetres.
- **Middle value**: Put your results in order. The one in the middle is the middle value. If there are two in the middle, find the number halfway between them. Scientists call it the median. *e.g.* The ten bounces in order were 30, 30, 31, 31, 32, 32, 33, 33, 34, 35. The two middle ones are 32 and 32, so the middle value is 32.
- **Average (mean)**: Add all the results, then divide by how many there are. It is the number each result would be if they were all shared out equally. *e.g.* The ten bounce heights add up to 321. Divided by 10, the average is 32.1 centimetres.
- **Sample**: The few things you test, taken from a much bigger bunch of the same thing. A sample is only useful if it is a fair picture of the bunch. *e.g.* You test ten strips from one roll of kitchen paper. The ten strips are the sample. The whole roll is the bunch.
- **Real difference**: A difference between two materials that is bigger than the ordinary wobble in each one. You can believe it. *e.g.* The marble's bounces were all lower than every bounce of the rubber ball. That is a real difference.
- **Overlap**: When the results for two materials share some of the same numbers. If they overlap a lot, you cannot tell the materials apart with that test. *e.g.* The rubber ball bounced from 30 to 35 centimetres and the table-tennis ball from 31 to 36. The two ranges overlap.
- **Measurement error**: The small gap between the number you wrote down and the true size. Every measurement has some. It does not mean you made a mistake. *e.g.* Two people read the same bounce and wrote 31 and 33 centimetres.
- **Zero error**: A fault in a measuring tool that is always the same size. A ruler with a chipped end starts at 1 centimetre instead of 0, so every reading is too big by 1. *e.g.* A worn ruler that starts at 0.5 centimetres adds half a centimetre to everything.
- **Resolution**: The smallest step a tool can show. A ruler marked in millimetres cannot show a tenth of a millimetre. *e.g.* A kitchen scale that moves in steps of 5 grams cannot tell a 2 gram difference.
- **Precise**: Giving nearly the same reading every time you measure the same thing. A precise tool can still be wrong, if it has a zero error. *e.g.* A bathroom scale that says 31.2, 31.3 and 31.2 kilograms for the same bag is precise.
- **Accurate**: Giving a reading close to the true size. An accurate tool gives the right answer on average, even if single readings wobble. *e.g.* Readings of 29, 32 and 31 for a bag that really weighs 31 kilograms are accurate on average.
- **Bias**: A push on the result that always goes the same way. Your hopes can cause it without you noticing. *e.g.* A tester who hopes the red cloth wins pulls a little gentler on it, and does not know she is doing it.
- **Blind test**: A test where the tester does not know which sample is which, so hopes cannot change what they do. *e.g.* A friend puts secret codes on three cloths, and you test them without knowing which is which.
- **Random**: Chosen by chance, with no pattern, such as by drawing paper chits from a bowl. A random order does not favour any sample. *e.g.* Draw four chits to decide which cloth is tested first.
- **Control**: A sample where you do nothing to the thing you are testing. It shows what happens anyway. *e.g.* A paper strip left dry while the other strips are wetted. If the dry strip also changes, something else is changing it.
- **Valid test**: A test that really measures what you care about. Tests that are easy to do are not always valid. *e.g.* Pulling a sari thread until it snaps does not tell you if the sari will last in the wash. Washing it ten times does.
- **Wear test**: A test that rubs, bends or washes a material again and again to see how it wears out. *e.g.* Rubbing a cloth on sandpaper and counting rubs until a hole appears is a wear test.
- **Accelerated test**: A test that squeezes years of use into a few minutes by using a harsher rub than real life. *e.g.* A machine that bends a jeans knee 10,000 times in an afternoon tests what a year of walking would do.
- **Calibrate**: To check a measuring tool against something you trust, and to fix or note any error. *e.g.* Put the ruler beside a new ruler to see if both give the same reading for the same strip.
- **Claim, evidence and reasoning**: Three sentences. The claim says what you think is true. The evidence says what you saw. The reasoning says why the evidence supports the claim. *e.g.* Claim: the ball bounces higher. Evidence: ten dots all above the other ball's dots. Reasoning: the gap is bigger than the wobble.
- **Safety factor**: How many times stronger than the biggest load we expect we make a thing. It protects us from the weakest piece and from surprises. *e.g.* A lift cable that will only ever carry 1,000 kilograms is made to hold several times that.

## Explanations
### The same test gives a different number every time · *defined*
**Definition:** Variation means that repeating a test gives results that are close but not equal. Every test has it.

Drop a rubber ball from 50 centimetres onto the kitchen floor ten times. It will not bounce to the same height ten times. You might see 31, 33, 30, 34 and so on. Nothing went wrong. Many tiny things change from drop to drop. Your fingers let go a little differently. The ball lands on a slightly different spot of the floor. It spins a little. A speck of dust is under it once. The ball's rubber is a tiny bit warmer after a few bounces. Each tiny thing nudges the bounce up or down by a little. Together they make a spread of results. Scientists call this variation. Variation is in every test of every material, including tests done by experts in a laboratory. It is not a fault of the child or the ball. It is how the world is.
- e.g. Ten drops of one rubber ball gave heights from 30 to 35 centimetres.
- e.g. Ten strips cut from the same roll of kitchen paper held different numbers of coins before tearing.
- e.g. Ten grains of the same rice are never exactly the same length.
### Spread, range and the dot plot · *defined*
**Definition:** The range of a set of results is the gap between the smallest and the biggest. A dot plot shows every result as a dot on a number line.

To see the spread, draw a dot plot. First draw a number line along the bottom of a page, from your smallest result to your biggest. Then make one dot above the line for every result. If two results are equal, stack one dot on top of the other. Now look. Where the dots pile up tall, results are common. Where the line is empty, results are rare. The range is the gap from the smallest dot to the biggest. For the rubber ball the smallest bounce was 30 and the biggest was 35, so the range is 5 centimetres. A range of 5 tells you how much to trust a single drop. One drop could be out by up to 5. A dot plot shows this at a glance, and a plain list of ten numbers does not.
- e.g. Smallest 30, biggest 35: the range is 5 centimetres.
- e.g. A tall stack of dots at 32 and 33 shows that most drops landed there.
- e.g. An empty space on the number line next to a lone dot is a hint to look at that drop again.
### Is the difference real? Compare the gap with the wobble · *defined*
**Definition:** A difference is real when it is bigger than the ordinary wobble. If the ranges overlap a lot, the test cannot tell the materials apart.

Look at the three made-up balls. The rubber ball's middle value was 32 centimetres. The table-tennis ball's was 34. The marble's was 25. Which differences are worth trusting? Compare each gap with the wobble. The rubber ball and the table-tennis ball differ by 2, but each one wobbles over a range of 5. Their ranges overlap a lot, from 31 to 35. A rubber ball sometimes bounces higher than a table-tennis ball. So this test cannot say which is bouncier. Now compare the rubber ball and the marble. The marble's highest bounce, 28, is lower than the rubber ball's lowest, 30. Their ranges do not overlap at all. That is a real difference. A good rule of thumb: if the gap between the middle values is smaller than the range of each ball, say 'we cannot tell'. If the ranges do not touch, say 'there is a real difference'. Scientists have more careful rules, but this one already stops many mistakes.
- e.g. Rubber 30 to 35 and table-tennis 31 to 36 overlap, so we cannot tell.
- e.g. Rubber 30 to 35 and marble 23 to 28 do not touch, so the difference is real.
- e.g. If you test with 30 drops instead of 10, the ranges may widen a little, but the middle values settle down.
### How many tries are enough? · *defined*
**Definition:** Repeating a test makes luck matter less. It cannot fix an error that always pushes the same way.

More tries make the middle value steadier. One drop can be far from the usual. The middle of three is better. The middle of ten is better still. But there is a limit. Ten tries is much better than three. A hundred is only a little better than ten. And no number of tries can fix a ruler with a chipped end, or a tester who always hopes for the same answer. Repeating a test reduces the effect of luck, but it does not remove an error that always pushes the same way. Also, tries must be fair copies of each other. Ten strips cut from ten different rolls, bought at ten different shops, are a better picture of 'kitchen paper' than ten strips next to each other on one roll. That is why scientists think about the sample. The sample must look like the whole bunch. Engineers who test lift cables break many pieces, taken from different places along different reels.
- e.g. Three drops of one ball: one lucky drop can mislead. Ten drops: much safer.
- e.g. Ten strips from one roll tell you about that roll. Strips from five rolls tell you about the brand.
- e.g. A hundred drops of a ball with a taped ruler on the wall that sits 1 centimetre too high will all be wrong by 1 centimetre.
### Measuring is rough: error, zero error and resolution · *defined*
**Definition:** Error is the small gap between a reading and the true size. Zero error is a fault that is always the same size. Resolution is the smallest step a tool can show.

Every measuring tool has a smallest step it can show. This is its resolution. A school ruler shows millimetres, so a reading of 31.4 centimetres is a guess between two marks. Every reading also has a small error, because eyes, fingers and light are not perfect. Two people reading the same bounce may write 31 and 33. That is measurement error, and it is normal. A second kind of fault is different. If a ruler has a chipped end, every reading is wrong by the same amount. That is a zero error. You can check for it by putting the ruler beside another ruler, which is called calibrating. Scientists describe good measuring with two words. Precise means that readings agree with each other. Accurate means that readings are close to the true size. A target shows the difference. Arrows close together but far from the middle are precise but not accurate. Arrows scattered evenly around the middle are accurate on average, but not precise. The best tools are both.
- e.g. A phone video of a bounce, played slowly, lets you read the top of the bounce from one frame, so the error is smaller than reading it by eye.
- e.g. A ruler that begins at 1 centimetre has a zero error of 1 centimetre.
- e.g. A kitchen scale with steps of 5 grams cannot show a difference of 2 grams.
### Hopes fool us: bias and the blind test · *defined*
**Definition:** Bias is a push on a result that always goes one way. A blind test stops the tester knowing which sample is which.

Imagine you are sure the red cloth is the strongest. You pull the red cloth and the blue cloth to see which tears first. Without knowing it, you may pull the blue one a little faster, because you want to see it lose. This is called bias. It is not cheating. It happens without our noticing, even to expert scientists. A famous case is a horse called Clever Hans. In Berlin, about 1904, people thought he could do sums, because he tapped the right number with his hoof. In 1907 a psychologist named Oskar Pfungst showed that Hans was reading tiny movements of the person who asked the question. The questioners nodded very slightly as the taps reached the right number. They did not know they did it. When the questioner did not know the answer, Hans got it wrong. The fix for bias is a blind test. A helper puts secret codes on the samples and keeps the list. The tester does not know which is which until all the results are written down. Medicine trials work the same way. Often the helper also draws the order of testing from a bowl, which is the random part.
- e.g. Judges at a talent show who cannot see the singers can judge only the voice.
- e.g. Researchers testing a new medicine give half the patients a plain pill, and neither the patients nor the doctors know who got which.
- e.g. A friend puts a secret letter on each of three cloths and keeps the list until you have finished testing.
### A fair order: why we draw lots · *defined*
**Definition:** A random order is chosen by chance, so no sample always gets an early slot or a late slot.

Suppose you test four cloths and always start with the cotton. By the time you reach the last cloth, your arms are tired, the sandpaper is clogged with lint, and the light has changed. The last cloth may look worse only because it came last. This is an order effect. To stop it, make the order random. Write each cloth on a paper chit, fold the chits, shake them in a bowl and draw them one by one. A random order does not favour any cloth. For extra care, test each cloth twice, once early and once late. This is why scientists say the order must be random, not 'whatever order feels natural'. Note that a random order may by chance put your favourite first. That is fine. In the long run, no cloth always gets the best slot.
- e.g. Four chits in a bowl decide which cloth is tested first.
- e.g. A researcher flips a coin to decide which of two plant pots gets the new fertiliser.
- e.g. A teacher draws names from a bowl so that no one can guess who is called next.
### A test must match the job: wear tests and valid tests · *defined*
**Definition:** A valid test measures what you really care about. A wear test rubs a material again and again to see how long it lasts.

A test is valid if it measures what you really care about. Suppose you want to know which cloth lasts longest in a school bag. Pulling one thread until it snaps is easy, but it tells you about threads, not bags. A bag is rubbed against desks, washed, and carried in the rain. So engineers design wear tests that copy this. A wear test rubs a cloth over a rough surface with a fixed push, again and again, and counts the rubs until a hole appears. It is an accelerated test, because it squeezes months of use into minutes. It is also harsher than real life in some ways and kinder in others. It does not include sunlight, sweat or washing. So a wear test answers one question well, and leaves other questions open. Good engineers use several tests, and then check their predictions against real use. Fashion makers, shoe makers and carpet makers all do this.
- e.g. A shoe-testing machine bends a sole thousands of times to copy months of walking.
- e.g. A jeans maker rubs the knee of the cloth against a rough wheel and counts rubs until the cloth thins.
- e.g. A pen maker draws a line on paper with a machine hand, over and over, to see how long the ink lasts.
### A control: do nothing, and watch · *defined*
**Definition:** A control is a sample that gets everything except the thing you are testing.

Sometimes things change by themselves while you are testing. A wet paper strip dries a little. A ball warms up. Ink fades in sunlight. To know what happens anyway, scientists keep a control. A control is a sample that gets everything except the thing you are testing. Say you want to know if rubbing makes a cloth thinner. Keep a piece of the same cloth that is not rubbed, in the same room for the same time. If the untouched piece is also thinner, something else is thinning it. Or say you drop a rubber ball and wonder if the floor matters. Keep one drop from a different, harder floor, but with all else the same. A control is the quiet partner of every fair test. Without it, you may give the credit for a change to the wrong cause.
- e.g. A strip of cloth in a drawer, while its twin is rubbed, is a control.
- e.g. A glass of plain water beside the salty water in a floating test is a control.
- e.g. A plant watered with plain water beside a plant watered with the new plant food.
### Why a ball bounces, and why no two bounces are equal · *defined*
**Definition:** A ball bounces because it squashes, stores energy, and springs back. Some energy always leaves as sound and warmth.

When a ball hits the floor, it squashes a little. While it squashes, it stores energy, like a squeezed spring. Then it springs back and throws itself up. A bouncier ball is one that stores more of the energy and gives more of it back. But no ball gives back all of it. Some energy becomes sound, the 'thud' you hear. Some becomes a tiny amount of warmth inside the rubber, as its tangled chains rub on each other. That is why each bounce is lower than the last. Now think about why two drops differ. The ball's spin, the exact angle, the spot on the floor, the air and your fingers all change a little. Each tiny change moves the bounce by a tiny amount. We cannot control them all, so we cannot predict one drop exactly. We can only say what usually happens, and how much it varies. A simple way to score a ball's bounciness is the fraction of the drop height that comes back: divide the bounce height by the drop height.
- e.g. A ball that bounces from 50 centimetres to 32 centimetres gives back 32 out of 50, which is about 0.64.
- e.g. A ball of clay does not bounce, because it keeps its squashed shape and turns the energy into warmth.
- e.g. A basketball bounces better on a wooden court than on soft sand, because the sand takes the energy.
### Standard tests: so that labs can agree · *defined*
**Definition:** A standard test is an agreed recipe. Anyone who follows it should get close to the same answer.

If a company in Chennai says its rope holds 200 kilograms, and a lab in Delhi says it holds 150, who is right? Perhaps both. The company may have pulled slowly, and the lab quickly. It may have been hot or damp. To make results comparable, countries write down standard tests. A standard says exactly how big a sample is, how it is held, how fast it is pulled and in what air. In India, the Bureau of Indian Standards writes many of these. A product that passes its tests may carry the ISI mark. Labs also check each other. Many labs test the same sample and compare numbers. If one lab is far from the rest, it checks its machine. This is the same idea as your two rulers. A test is only useful if other people can repeat it and get close to the same answer.
- e.g. A helmet that carries the ISI mark has passed fixed tests.
- e.g. Labs in different countries test the same piece of steel and compare numbers, to check their machines.
- e.g. A cricket ball must weigh between set limits, so that a ball in Chennai behaves like a ball in Mumbai.
### Design for the weakest piece, not the average · *defined*
**Definition:** A safety factor is how many times stronger than the expected load we make a thing. It covers the weakest piece and surprises.

A lift cable is made of steel strands. Suppose tests on a hundred pieces show a middle value of 20 tonnes before breaking, but a few pieces broke at 14. An engineer will not use 20 as the number to trust. She asks about the weakest pieces and about the worst day. If a cable only ever carries 2 tonnes, she may build it to hold much more than that. This extra strength is the safety factor. It covers unseen cracks, rust, shocks and mistakes. It is also why the spread matters so much. If every piece broke at nearly the same load, a small safety factor would do. If the spread is wide, the factor must be larger. The same idea is used for ropes, chains, bridges and helmets. A fair test finds how far the weakest piece falls below the middle.
- e.g. A rope sold for 100 kilograms of load may be tested to break above 500 kilograms.
- e.g. A bridge is built to hold many times the heaviest traffic it will carry.
- e.g. A climbing rope is retired after a serious fall, even if it looks fine.
### Can a test ever prove that a material is safe? · *debated*
A test can show that a rope held 500 kilograms ten times. Does that prove it will hold 500 kilograms tomorrow? A rope can fail on the eleventh try. Karl Popper, a philosopher, wrote in 1934 that a test can show a claim is wrong, but can never show it is certainly true. If a thousand swans you see are white, you still cannot be sure that no black swan exists. Engineers and statisticians do not use the word 'prove'. They say how likely a failure is. They say, for example, that fewer than 1 piece in 1,000 will fail at this load. Even this depends on the sample being fair and the future being like the past. Some philosophers say that this is as good as knowing gets. Others say that careful tests build a kind of confidence that deserves to be called knowledge. People still disagree about the best words, but all agree that more tests, more fairness and more honesty make us more sure.
- e.g. A bridge passes a hundred tests, and engineers still inspect it every year.
- e.g. Medicines are tested on thousands of people, and doctors still watch for rare effects after sale.
- e.g. A scientist writes 'the evidence suggests' instead of 'this proves'.
- **Engineers:** We never say safe for ever. We say how likely a failure is, and we keep inspecting and testing.
- **Karl Popper, a philosopher of science:** A test can show that a claim is false. It can never show that a claim about all the future is surely true.
- **Statisticians:** We can put a number on how sure we are, for example one failure in a thousand. The number depends on a fair sample.

## Mostly agreed
- Repeating the same test gives numbers that are close but not equal. *How we know:* Balls dropped ten times, strips pulled ten times and rice grains measured one by one all show a spread. Labs in every country report the spread along with the middle.
- A difference between two materials can be believed only when it is bigger than the ordinary spread within each one. *How we know:* When two ranges overlap, a different set of drops can easily flip the order of the middle values. Scientists check this before they say that one material is better.
- Every measuring tool has a smallest step it can show, and some error. *How we know:* Two careful people reading the same ruler often differ by a millimetre or more. Labs check their tools against agreed standards.
- A tester who knows which sample is which can be nudged without knowing it, so important tests are done blind. *How we know:* Clever Hans seemed to count until Oskar Pfungst showed in 1907 that the horse followed the questioner's tiny movements. Medicine trials use blind tests for the same reason.
- The weakest piece, and not the average, decides how safe a thing is. *How we know:* Cables, ropes and bridges are built several times stronger than the load they will carry, because some pieces are weaker than the middle.
- A test answers only the question it was built to ask, so a test must be chosen to match the job. *How we know:* A thread-pulling test says little about how a school bag will wear. Engineers add wear tests and real-life trials.

## How we found out
**Oskar Pfungst, a German psychologist, who tested Clever Hans, the horse of Wilhelm von Osten, 1904 to 1907.** Around 1904 in Berlin, a horse called Clever Hans became famous. His owner, a retired teacher named Wilhelm von Osten, said that Hans could add, subtract and tell the date. Asked a sum, Hans tapped the answer with his hoof. A committee of experts watched and found no trick by the owner. Then a young psychologist, Oskar Pfungst, tried something new. He had people ask questions in secret, without knowing the answer themselves. He also put screens between Hans and the questioner. Hans got the answers wrong. When the questioner knew the answer and Hans could see their face, he was right. Pfungst saw that people made a tiny movement of the head as the taps reached the right number, and Hans stopped there. No one had meant to give a signal. Pfungst published his work in 1907. Since then, scientists test blind, so that nobody can nudge the result by accident.  
*What people believed before:* That Hans could do sums, and that if the owner was honest the test must be fair. Pfungst showed that the horse followed tiny, unwitting signals from people who knew the answer. A fair test needs the tester to be in the dark.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The bigger number wins, so a ball that bounced higher once is the bouncier ball.** (Many people, and many adverts; *rejected*)  
  For: A bigger number is easy to see and to remember. Sometimes the gap is so big that one try would have shown it.  
  Against: When the ranges of two balls overlap, a repeat of the test can reverse the order. The ten drops of the rubber ball and the table-tennis ball in the made-up data overlapped almost completely. A fair claim says 'we cannot tell'.
- **Only careless people are fooled by their hopes. A careful scientist is always fair.** (A common belief about scientists; *rejected*)  
  For: Careful people do check their work, and they try to be fair.  
  Against: Bias works without our knowing. Physicist Richard Feynman said in 1974 that the first rule of science is not to fool yourself, and that you are the easiest person to fool. That is why scientists use blind tests and random order, even when they are sure they are being fair.
- **An odd result is a mistake, so throw it away.** (Many people when they are in a hurry; *debated*)  
  For: Sometimes an odd result really is a slip, such as a coin dropped hard or a strip with a nick.  
  Against: Sometimes the odd result is the discovery. In 1965, at the DuPont company in the USA, the chemist Stephanie Kwolek made a thin, cloudy liquid that most chemists would have thrown away. She asked for it to be spun into a fibre, and the tests showed the fibre was very stiff and strong for its weight. It became the material called Kevlar. In 1982 the scientist Dan Shechtman saw a pattern in a metal that the textbooks said could not exist. He checked it again and again, many scientists laughed for years, and he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011. The rule is to check an odd result, and not to delete it.
- **More tries are always better.** (A common idea; *debated*)  
  For: More tries make the middle value steadier and make luck matter less.  
  Against: More tries cannot fix a chipped ruler, a hopeful tester or an unfair sample. And after a point, ten more tries add very little. A smarter plan with fewer, fairer tries can beat a hundred careless ones.
- **A good test changes only one thing at a time, and nothing else is fair.** (Many school books, and the rule in the Class 3 unit; *debated*)  
  For: Changing one thing makes the cause clear. It is the best rule for a beginner.  
  Against: In 1926 the statistician Ronald Fisher argued that scientists can ask nature several questions at once, if the tests are planned in a careful pattern. Farm trials and factory tests now often change two or three things together, and then work out each one's effect with sums. One at a time is a good rule to start with, but not the only fair one.
- **The test that copies real life is always better than a simple fair test in the kitchen.** (Many engineers and many parents; *debated*)  
  For: A test that copies real use catches problems that a simple test misses, such as sweat on a shoe or wind on a kite.  
  Against: Real life changes every day, so a real-life trial is hard to compare. A simple test in the kitchen can be repeated ten times and compared fairly. Good testers use both: the fair test to compare, the real-life trial to check.

## Roots
We have dropped balls, drawn dots, and learned that no test gives the same number twice. Now we ask what that means for the things around us, and for ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Drop one ball ten times and you get ten different heights. Often the wobble in one ball is wider than the gap between two balls. So a ball has no single bounce. It has a habit, a range of bounces.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'this ball bounces 31 centimetres' or 'this paper holds 14 coins' as if one number belonged to the thing, like its colour. That leaves out the wobble every test shows.  
*Evidence:* In the Bounce Test, each ball's ten drops spread over several centimetres, and some balls' ranges overlapped. In made-up results, the rubber ball and the table-tennis ball overlapped almost completely. Your own dots will show how wide your wobble is. Write them as they are.

- *who am i:* You never run to the gate in exactly the same time twice. What you can do is a range, and so is what you are like, from day to day.
- *what is life:* Seeds from one packet sprout on different days, in the same pot. This variation is how a group of living beings can meet a late monsoon, a new disease or a hot summer.
- *what is this world:* Even a ball, with no choice at all, will not do the same thing twice. Many tiny causes, such as a speck of dust or a slightly different touch, add up. The world repeats its patterns, but never exactly.

Every test in this unit gave a spread. The same ball, the same strip and the same grain of rice each gave a range of numbers. A material does not have one bounce, one strength or one length. It has a habit that shows up as a range. We learned to describe that range with dots, a range and a middle value. And we learned that our own hopes and tools add their own wobble. A believable result states the habit, the wobble and how sure we are. Saying 'it is exactly this' is a small untruth, even when it is a handy one.

- **Heraclitus of Ephesus, a Greek thinker of about 500 BCE (reported by later writers):** Heraclitus is reported to have said that you cannot step into the same river twice, because other and still other waters flow on those who step in. (The same ball dropped from the same hand is a different drop each time. A pattern can stay, while each case is new.)
- **Richard Feynman, in a talk to graduates at Caltech, 1974:** The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool. (A blind test with secret codes is a way of not fooling yourself. It takes your hopes out of the room.)
- **Thiruvalluvar, Tirukkural, couplet 423:** Whatever is said, and whoever says it, wisdom is to find the truth in it. (An advert, a famous scientist and your own guess are all just claims until a fair test is done.)
- Science (settled): Every measurement has some spread. The spread within a group is measured and reported in every science, from chemistry to medicine.
- Science (debated): Whether the smallest events in nature are truly random, or hide a pattern we cannot yet see, is argued by physicists.
- Science (settled): Variation inside a group of living beings is the raw material of evolution, as Charles Darwin described in 1859.
- Other view, Many metrologists, who study measurement: A thing does have one true size, like a ball's real bounciness. The spread is only the error in our tests. With a perfect test, every drop would give the same number. They would say the wobble is a fault in our tools and hands, not part of the ball. So we should try to shrink the wobble, and not call it part of the thing.
- Practice, Ten claps, none the same (3 min): Sit comfortably. Clap your hands once, and listen. Now clap nine more times, trying to make each clap exactly the same. Listen to each one. No two are quite alike. Now breathe in slowly and out slowly three times. Notice that no two breaths are alike either. You are a pattern that repeats, but never in exactly the same way.
- Ask: Does a ball have a bounce, or does it only have a habit of bouncing?
- Ask: If we could make a perfect test with no wobble, would the ball bounce the same each time?
- Ask: Is the spread in a group of living beings a problem, or something they need?

## Story: Ten Pieces of Rope
*A made-up story. Alappuzha (Alleppey) in Kerala is a real centre of coir making, and coir is the fibre of coconut husks. Sree, her family, Jose chettan, the rope company and the numbers of mugs are imagined. The science is real: a single test piece does not show how much pieces vary, the person pouring can nudge the result without knowing, and blind testing with a random order protects against it.*

Sree lived in Alappuzha, in Kerala, where the backwaters wind between coconut palms. Her family made coir rope. Coir is the rough brown fibre from the husks of coconuts. In the yard behind the house, her mother and aunts spun the fibre into yarn, and the yarn into long ropes that lay across the sand like sleeping snakes.

On a hot Thursday, a man arrived on a scooter, with a shiny bag. His name was Jose, and he came from a rope company in Kochi. Sree's family called him Jose chettan. He put down a coil of bright green rope. 'This is nylon,' he said. 'Twice as strong as coir. I will show you.'

He cut one piece of the green rope and one piece of coir, and hung them from a beam in the shed. To each he tied a steel bucket. Then he filled the buckets with water from the tap, one mug at a time. The coir snapped at nine mugs. The green rope was still holding at twelve, and he stopped. 'You see?' said Jose chettan. 'Twelve against nine.'

Ammamma, Sree's grandmother, had been twisting coir since before Sree's mother was born. She said nothing. But her lips pressed together, and she looked at the broken rope for a long time.

Sree had a teacher at school who said one thing again and again: never trust one drop. One drop of a ball, one pull of a strip, one anything. 'One piece of rope,' said Sree, 'is only one piece.'

Jose chettan laughed kindly. 'A test is a test, child.' But Sree's mother said, 'Let her.' So Sree asked for ten pieces of each rope, all cut to the same length.

They set the first ten pieces in a row. Jose chettan poured the water, mug by mug, and Sree wrote the numbers. After a while Ammamma said, 'You pour the green ones softly, like you are feeding a baby. And you pour the coir ones fast, like you are late for a bus.'

Jose chettan opened his mouth to say no. Then he looked at the notebook, and he closed it again. 'I did not know I did that,' he said slowly.

'Then nobody should know which is which,' said Sree. Her cousin Anu tied a paper tag with a secret letter on each of the twenty pieces. Sree wrote the list of letters in the notebook, folded the page, and put it in her pocket. Then they shook the tags in a steel bowl and drew them, one by one, to decide the order.

Before the first piece was hung, Ammamma took each tagged rope in her hands. She ran the rope between her fingers, and she did not look at the tag. She put two pieces aside. 'These two are poor,' she said. 'I do not know why. They feel thin.'

Now Jose chettan poured, and he did not know whether he was pouring on nylon or coir. He poured every piece the same way, mug after mug, until each snapped. Sree wrote the number for every tag. It took the whole afternoon.

At the end, she opened the folded page. She drew a long line on the floor of the yard with chalk, with numbers from 5 to 15 marked on it. For every rope she put one cashew nut on its number, with the nuts of the same number stacked in a little pile. Coir nuts were brown, and nylon nuts she marked with a dab of green paint.

The pile of brown nuts stood between 7 and 12, with the middle at 9. The green nuts stood between 6 and 14, with the middle at 12 and a half. 'The green rope is stronger in the middle,' said Sree. 'But not every green piece beat every brown piece. One green piece broke at 6. That is lower than any coir.'

Ammamma reached over and touched two nuts. 'These two are the ones I put aside,' she said. One was brown, at 7. The other was green, at 6. They were the two weakest pieces of all twenty. Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Jose chettan smiled and shook his head slowly. 'Your grandmother's hands are a better test than my bucket,' he said.

Sree shook her head. 'Her hands are a test too,' she said. 'But it was the blind test with ten pieces that let us see it. One piece each told us a story that was not true.'

Her mother bought the green rope for the biggest boat, where a snap would be worst. For that rope, she asked Jose chettan to cut her a spare piece from every new coil, so that Ammamma could feel it first. And before any new rope was trusted, Sree tied a ribbon on it, and the family called it 'a rope not yet tested'.

**For the parent:** One piece of rope tells you about one piece. Ten pieces show how much ropes of one kind differ, and the ranges of the two kinds overlap, so 'twice as strong' was too much. The turn comes when the tester is kept in the dark with tags and random order. Then the test is fair, and even Ammamma's hands can be checked.

- Ask: Why was it not enough to test one piece of each rope?
- Ask: How did the secret tags make the test fairer?
- Ask: If the ranges of the two ropes overlap, what can Sree honestly say about which rope is stronger?
- Ask: Ammamma felt two weak pieces before the test. How could Sree check that she was not just lucky?
- Ask: Why did Sree tie a ribbon on any rope that had not been tested?

### e1 · The Bounce Test: ten drops, four balls (45 min)
- **question:** If you drop the same ball ten times from the same height, does it bounce to the same height each time? And can ten drops tell you which of four things really bounces highest?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which of the four things will bounce highest, and which lowest? If you drop one ball ten times, will you get the same height every time? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every ball is let go from the same height, with its bottom at the 50 centimetre flag, onto the same square of floor. You let go and do not throw. Two people read each bounce from eye level. The order of the balls is drawn from a bowl, and then reversed, so no ball always goes first or last. Only the ball changes. Then the ten drops of each ball show how much that ball varies, and you can see whether the difference between balls is bigger than the variation.
- **safety:** Keep marbles away from children under five, and pick up every marble after the test so nobody slips on one. Use a marble without chips or cracks, and keep faces back from the bounce.
- **record:** Draw a table with one row for each ball. Write the ten readings by you, and the ten readings by your helper. Then draw a dot plot with a row for each ball on one number line. Write the range and the middle value of each ball.
- **results table:** Ball | Your readings of the 10 drops (cm) | Your helper's readings of the same 10 drops (cm) | Range: smallest to biggest (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the four things. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will drop four things from the same height. Which will bounce highest? Which will bounce lowest? If we drop one ball ten times, will it bounce the same each time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four things to drop. Which bounces highest? Will one ball bounce the same each time? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that thing will bounce highest? (good: It is the hardest.; It is the lightest.; It is made of rubber.)
  - Ask: Will one ball bounce to the same height every time? Why? (good: Yes, because nothing changes.; No, because it is hard to drop exactly the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Write each guess and each reason on paper. The guess about the same height each time matters most, because the experiment is built to test it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the tape measure up a wall, with zero at the floor. 2. Stick a flag of tape at 50 centimetres. 3. Tape a square on the floor below. 4. Fold the four chits and put them in a bowl. 5. Draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* We need a place where every drop is the same. The flag marks the height. The square marks the spot. The bowl will decide the order.  
  *Child mode:* Tape up the measure. Mark the flag and the square.
  - Note: If the floor is soft, such as carpet or a mat, put a smooth hard board, a ceramic tile or a thick book on the floor under the square. The same hard floor must be used for every ball.
  - Check: Why do we tape a square on the floor and let go from the flag every time? → **So only the ball changes and every drop starts the same**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drop the rubber ball three times for practice. Let go with the bottom of the ball at the flag. Crouch so your eyes are level with the top of the bounce.  
  *Parent says:* Let go, do not throw. Crouch down. Look at the bottom of the ball when it is highest. Read that number.  
  *Child mode:* Let go. Crouch. Look at the top of the bounce.
  - Note: Reading a bounce by eye is hard. If you have a phone, film the three practice drops in slow motion next to the tape measure, then play them back. Show your child how the eye misses the highest point. Roles: with one child and a parent, the child lets go and both of you read. With 2 to 4 children, one lets go, two read, one writes. Swap jobs after each ball, but keep the same person letting go for all ten drops of one ball.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the four chits. That is the order of the balls. 2. Drop each ball once in that order. 3. Then drop each ball once in the reverse order. 4. Both of you write down every reading, without looking at each other's paper. 5. Do this twice.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the chits for the order. Drop each ball once. Then go backwards. Write what you see. Do not look at each other's paper.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the order. Drop each ball. Then go backwards. Write the numbers.
  - Ask: Is the same ball giving the same number each time? (good: No, the numbers are close but different.; Almost, but not quite.)
  - Note: Writing without looking at each other's paper matters. Looking would make the readings agree for the wrong reason. Expect the numbers for one ball to differ by a few centimetres.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep going until each ball has been dropped ten times. Keep the same method. Draw new chits for the order at the start of every second pair.  
  *Parent says:* Ten drops for each ball. Same method, same flag, same square. Keep writing every number, even the odd ones.  
  *Child mode:* Finish ten drops for each ball. Write every number.
  - Note: If a ball rolls off the square, call it a miss and drop again, but write down that it was a miss. Do not hide misses. The clay ball will not bounce. Write 0 for it every time.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each drop, find the gap between your reading and your helper's reading. Circle the biggest gap. Talk about why two people read the same bounce differently.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your numbers next to your helper's numbers. Where is the biggest gap? Why might two careful people read the same bounce differently?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your numbers with your helper's. Circle the biggest gap.
  - Ask: Why did two careful people read the same drop differently? (good: Our eyes were at different heights.; The ball is fast and the top is hard to see.; One of us looked a moment later.)
  - Note: A gap of 1 to 3 centimetres is normal. A gap bigger than that suggests one reader was standing, not crouching.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a number line from 0 to 60 across a page. Make a row of dots for each ball, using your readings. Stack equal results. Write the range and the middle value for each ball.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the dot plot. One row for each ball. Look at the rows. Which rows overlap? Which rows are far apart?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a dot plot. One row for each ball. Which rows overlap?
  - Ask: Which balls have rows that overlap? Which are far apart? (good: The rubber ball and the table-tennis ball overlap.; The clay ball is far from all the others.)
  - Note: Typically each row is about 2 to 6 centimetres wide, but your numbers depend on your balls and your floor. Some may overlap and some may not. Both are good results. If your rows are very wide, look at the biggest gaps in step 6 for a hint.
- **s8 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write a claim, the evidence and the reasoning for the pair of balls whose rows are closest. Add one sentence that says how sure you are, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Pick the two closest balls. Write what you can honestly say about them. Then say how sure you are.  
  *Child mode:* Pick two balls. Write claim, evidence and reasoning. Say how sure you are.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this (example numbers only; yours will differ, and a marble on a tile can bounce as high as a rubber ball): Claim: the rubber ball bounces higher than the marble. Evidence: the rubber ball's ten drops ranged from 30 to 35 and the marble's from 23 to 28. Reasoning: the ranges do not touch, so the gap is bigger than the wobble. I am fairly sure, but the test used one floor.
  - Check: The rubber ball's ten drops ranged from 30 to 35. The marble's ranged from 23 to 28. Which claim is fair? → **The rubber ball bounces higher. The ranges do not touch.**
### e2 · The Secret Code Test: does a label change what we feel? (30 min)
- **question:** Do people feel the same cloth as softer when it has a 'Premium' label, and does the score change when they cannot see the label?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will people give the cloth marked Premium a higher softness score than the cloth marked Basic, even though both squares are cut from the same cloth? Will the answer change when people cannot see the labels? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All squares are cut from one piece of cloth, so they are exactly the same. The same people feel them, with clean dry hands, in the same room. A coin toss decides whether a person feels the Premium or Basic square first. You say nothing while people give their scores, so your face and voice cannot help. The only thing that changes between rounds is whether people can see the label.
- **safety:** Use scissors only with a grown-up. Ask each person if they are happy to take part, and tell everyone the whole truth at the end.
- **record:** Draw a table with one row for each person. Write each person's scores in both rounds. Work out the difference for each person. Then draw a dot plot of the open-round differences and a dot plot of the blind-round differences on the same number line from minus 5 to plus 5.
- **results table:** Person | Open round: Premium score (0 to 10) | Open round: Basic score (0 to 10) | Open round: Premium minus Basic | Blind round: first square score | Blind round: second square score | Blind round: first minus second
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the cloth. Ask for a guess and a reason. Do not say yet what the test will show.  
  *Parent says:* We will ask people how soft two squares feel. One is labelled Premium. One is labelled Basic. Will they say Premium is softer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Two squares. One says Premium. One says Basic. Which will people call softer? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think people will give those scores? (good: Premium sounds better.; The label makes them think it is softer.; They will just feel the cloth and give the same score.)
  - Note: Do not tell your child how the test will turn out, and do not hint. Write the guess and the reason down.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts four squares from the same cloth. Put a Premium label on one square and a Basic label on a second. Leave two squares plain. Mark them K and M underneath in pencil.  
  *Parent says:* The squares are cut from one cloth. They are exactly the same. Two will have labels. Two will have secret letters.  
  *Child mode:* Cut four squares from one cloth. Label two. Mark two with letters.
  - Note: The two plain squares are for the blind round. Mark their letters on the underside, where people will not see. Keep all four away from other cloth, so people cannot compare them with a different towel.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the first person to feel both labelled squares. Toss a coin for who goes first. Heads is Premium first. Ask for a softness score from 0 to 10 for each. Write the scores. Say nothing else.  
  *Parent says:* Please feel these two squares. Give each one a softness score from 0 to 10. Zero is rough. Ten is very soft.  
  *Child mode:* Toss a coin. Ask for two scores from 0 to 10. Say nothing else.
  - Note: Do not smile, nod or say anything while the person is scoring. If you react, your face may tell them which score you hoped for. Repeat with every person. Use the same words for each person. Roles: one child asks and writes, one tosses the coin and hands over the squares. Other children wait in another room, because they know the secret. If you cannot find 5 people, run it with 3 and say that the result is weak.
  - Check: Why do we toss a coin to decide which square goes first? → **So the order does not always favour one square**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now ask each person to close their eyes or wear the scarf. Hand over the squares marked K and M, one after the other. Toss a coin for the order. Ask for a score for each. Write the scores by the letters.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes please. Feel this square, then this one. Give each a softness score from 0 to 10.  
  *Child mode:* Eyes closed. Hand over the K and M squares. Write the scores.
  - Note: Keep a record of which square, K or M, went first. Do not tell anybody what you expect.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell everyone the whole truth. All four squares came from the same cloth. Thank them for helping. Ask them what they thought was happening.  
  *Parent says:* Now the secret. All four squares were cut from the same cloth. Did anyone guess? What did you think you felt?  
  *Child mode:* Tell everyone the secret. All four squares are the same.
  - Ask: What did you think you felt when you saw the labels? (good: The Premium square felt softer.; I felt no difference.)
  - Note: Be kind. No one was foolish. Even expert tasters are moved by labels.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each person, work out 'Premium minus Basic' for the open round, and 'first minus second' for the blind round. Draw both rows of differences as dots on one number line from minus 5 to plus 5.  
  *Parent says:* Take each person's Premium score and subtract the Basic score. Do the same for the blind round. Plot the dots. Where do they sit?  
  *Child mode:* Work out the differences. Plot two rows of dots.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the two rows of dots? (good: The open round dots sit mostly above zero.; Both rows are all over the place.; The blind round dots sit around zero.)
  - Note: Be honest about what you see. With five people, the dots may be messy. If the open round sits mostly above zero and the blind round is spread around zero, the label pushed the scores. If both rows look alike, you have found no push. Both results are fine. Do not delete anyone's score.
### e3 · The Rub Test: which cloth wears through first? (45 min)
- **question:** Which of four cloths wears through first when rubbed on sandpaper with the same push, and is the difference bigger than the wobble between two runs of the same cloth?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which cloth will wear through in the fewest rubs, and which will last longest? How many rubs do you think the longest will take? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each cloth is stretched over the flat bottom of the same steel katori with the same weight inside. It is slid the same 20 centimetres along the sandpaper, and back, at the same speed. Each run uses a fresh lane of sandpaper, so lint from one cloth does not change the next run. The order of the cloths is drawn from a bowl. Each cloth is run twice. Only the cloth changes.
- **safety:** Do this on a table covered with newspaper. Rub gently on the sandpaper only, never on skin. Do not blow the dust. Wipe up the dust with a damp cloth when you finish, and wash your hands. Work in a room with fresh air, and anyone with asthma or a dust allergy should stay out of it. A grown-up should cut the cloth and handle the sandpaper edges.
- **record:** Write the number of rubs until the first hole for every run, or '100 or more' if no hole appeared. Then draw a dot plot with a row for each cloth. Work out the range for each cloth. If a run ends with no hole, hold the cloth up to the window and write a light score from 0 (no light) to 5 (almost a hole), so every run still gives you something to compare.
- **results table:** Cloth | Rubs until the first hole, run 1 | Rubs until the first hole, run 2 | Smaller and bigger of the two runs | What the cloth looked like at the end
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the four cloths. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will rub four cloths on sandpaper until they wear through. Which will wear through first? Which will last longest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four cloths. Which wears out first? Which lasts longest? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that cloth will last longest? (good: It is thick.; It is tightly woven.; It is the strongest.)
  - Ask: What would the real life use of the cloth be? (good: A school bag.; A shirt.; A kitchen duster.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Write each guess and the reason.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the sandpaper flat to the table. 2. Draw eight lanes as wide as the katori, along the long side of the sheets (three lanes fit on one sheet). 3. Number them 1 to 8. 4. Put the coins in the katori. 5. Cut two squares of each cloth.  
  *Parent says:* We are building a rubbing machine. The sandpaper is the rough road. The katori with coins is the weight. Each lane is used only once.  
  *Child mode:* Tape down the sandpaper. Draw eight lanes. Number them.
  - Note: The sandpaper clogs with lint, so a used lane rubs more gently. That is why each run gets a new lane. Calibrate first on the scrap: put 30 coins in the katori and rub a square of the thinnest cotton. If it has no hole after 50 rubs, add more coins or stones and try again on a new patch. Aim for a hole in the thinnest cloth in about 20 to 50 rubs, then keep this load for the whole test. These numbers are only typical; home sandpaper and katoris differ.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fix a square of cotton over the flat bottom of the katori. Hold it with a rubber band. Check that the cloth is tight and has no wrinkles.  
  *Parent says:* Pull the cloth tight. A loose cloth will wrinkle and wear unfairly. Smooth it with your hand.  
  *Child mode:* Stretch the cloth tight. Fix it with a rubber band.
  - Check: Why must every cloth be pulled tight in the same way? → **A loose cloth wrinkles and wears in a different way, so the cloth is not the only thing that changed**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the four chits. That is the order of the cloths for the first round. Write the order down. Practise one rub on the scrap of sandpaper, counting 'one-two' for the way there and back.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the order. Then practise one rub. Slide there, slide back. Count one-two. Keep the same speed.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the order. Practise one rub. Count one-two.
  - Note: The speed matters. A steady count of one second each way is easy to keep. Use a clock with a second hand, or sing a steady beat. Do not press down. Let the weight do the work.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Run each cloth in the drawn order, in a fresh lane. Slide the katori to the end of the lane and back for each rub. Count the rubs aloud. Every ten rubs, stop and hold the cloth up to the window. Look for a point of light. Stop at the first hole, or at 100 rubs.  
  *Parent says:* One rub is there and back. Count out loud. Every ten rubs, hold the cloth up to the light. A tiny point of light means a hole. Write the number.  
  *Child mode:* Slide there and back. Count. Every ten rubs, look for light.
  - Ask: Which cloth is going to last longest, now that you have watched? (good: The jeans.; The cotton.; I cannot tell yet.)
  - Note: If no cloth has a hole after 100 rubs, write '100 or more' and give it a light score. Thick denim often does not wear through at all in this test, and that is a real result. If every cloth lasts to 100, add weight and start again in new lanes, and note that the load changed. Roles: one person slides (the same person for a whole round, so the speed stays the same), one counts, one checks the light, one writes. With one child and a parent, swap sliding and counting between rounds.
- **s6 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Run a second round. Draw the chits again for a new order. Use fresh lanes and new squares of cloth. Write the numbers for the second run.  
  *Parent says:* Run every cloth a second time. Draw a new order. Use new lanes.  
  *Child mode:* Run a second round. Draw a new order. Use new lanes.
  - Note: If a second run is very different from the first, do not be surprised. Cloth is not the same everywhere. Write both.
- **s7 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a dot plot with a row for each cloth, using both runs. Work out the range. Circle any cloth where the two runs are far apart. Write a claim, evidence and reasoning, and say how sure you are.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the dot plot. Which cloth lasted longest? Is the gap bigger than the wobble between its two runs?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a dot plot. Which cloth lasted longest? Say how sure you are.
  - Ask: Is the winner clear, or do two cloths overlap? (good: The winner is clear.; Two cloths overlap.; We would need more runs.)
  - Ask: What does this test leave out about real life? (good: Sunlight.; Washing.; Water and sweat.)
  - Note: With only two runs per cloth, many pairs will overlap, and that is a good result to talk about. Ask: how many more runs would you want before you told a friend which cloth to buy?

## Worksheets
### Spread and Real Differences (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) You drop the same rubber ball ten times from the same height and get ten slightly different bounces. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Many tiny things change a little from drop to drop, such as how your fingers let go and where the ball lands / The ball gets tired and bounces differently each time / The test was done wrongly and the results should be thrown away / The floor changes its mind  
   **Answer:** Many tiny things change a little from drop to drop, such as how your fingers let go and where the ball lands. *Why:* Tiny differences in the release, the landing spot, the spin and the reading add up to a spread. Picking 'done wrongly' usually means expecting a perfect test, and no test is perfect.
2. (truefalse) If the same test gives ten different numbers, the tester must have made a mistake.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Variation is normal in every test. The numbers should be close, not equal. Only a number far from the rest needs a second look.
3. (fill) The gap between the smallest result and the biggest result is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['range']. *Why:* The range tells you how far the results spread. A wide range means the results wobble a lot.
4. (order) Put the steps of drawing a dot plot in order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* You need to know the smallest and biggest first, so the number line fits. Then each result becomes a dot. Equal results stack. Last, you read the picture.
5. (mcq) Look at the table. Which two balls have ranges that overlap?  
   Options: The rubber ball and the table-tennis ball / The rubber ball and the marble / The marble and the table-tennis ball / None of them overlap  
   **Answer:** The rubber ball and the table-tennis ball. *Why:* The rubber ball ranged from 30 to 35 and the table-tennis ball from 31 to 36, so they share the numbers 31 to 35. The marble's 23 to 28 does not touch either of them.
6. (sort) Sort each pair of ranges. Do the two ranges overlap, or are they apart?  
   **Answer:** 10 to 14 and 13 to 17 → Overlap; 20 to 25 and 30 to 35 → Apart; 5 to 9 and 9 to 12 → Overlap; 40 to 50 and 51 to 60 → Apart; 7 to 12 and 3 to 8 → Overlap; 14 to 18 and 15 to 16 → Overlap. *Why:* Ranges overlap when they share at least one number. 5 to 9 and 9 to 12 share the 9. 14 to 18 holds all of 15 to 16. 40 to 50 and 51 to 60 miss each other by one.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Range → The gap from the smallest to the biggest result; Middle value → The result in the middle when all are in order; Zero error → A fault in a tool that is the same size on every reading; Blind test → The tester does not know which sample is which; Control → A sample where you do nothing to the thing you test. *Why:* Each word names one idea in the unit: how far results spread, the middle of the results, a steady fault in a tool, a test that stops hopes from pushing, and a sample that shows what happens anyway.
8. (write) A friend says: 'I dropped a rubber ball once and it bounced to 31 centimetres. I dropped a marble once and it bounced to 25. So the rubber ball is bouncier.' Write two things you would say to your friend.  
   **Answer:** One drop of each is only one drop. Drop each ten times and draw a dot plot. If the two ranges do not touch, you can say there is a real difference. If the ranges overlap, you cannot tell.. *Why:* One drop shows no spread. Many drops show the spread, and the ranges decide whether the difference is real.
9. (mcq) Ten bounces in order are 30, 30, 31, 31, 32, 32, 33, 33, 34, 35. What is the middle value?  
   Options: 32 centimetres / 31 centimetres / 35 centimetres / 33 centimetres  
   **Answer:** 32 centimetres. *Why:* With ten results, the middle is between the fifth and sixth. Both are 32, so the middle value is 32. Picking 35 means picking the biggest, and 31 means counting from the wrong end.
10. (truefalse) If one ball has a bigger middle value than another ball, it is always the better ball.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. If the ranges overlap a lot, another set of drops could reverse the order. A difference is real only when it is bigger than the wobble.
11. (mcq) A ruler's first centimetre has broken off, so it starts at the 1 centimetre mark. A strip really measures 10 centimetres. What does this ruler read when the strip's end lines up with the end of the ruler?  
   Options: 11 centimetres, which is 1 too big / 9 centimetres, which is 1 too small / 10 centimetres, which is right / 12 centimetres, which is 2 too big  
   **Answer:** 11 centimetres, which is 1 too big. *Why:* The ruler starts counting at 1, so every reading is 1 too big. This is a zero error. Picking 9 usually means thinking a missing piece makes a ruler read less.
12. (fill) A test in which the tester does not know which sample is which is called a ____ test.  
   **Answer:** ['blind']. *Why:* A blind test stops the tester's hopes from nudging the result without them knowing.
13. (draw) Draw a dot plot for these five bounce heights, in centimetres: 30, 32, 32, 33, 35.  
   **Answer:** A number line from 30 to 35. One dot above 30, two dots stacked above 32, one dot above 33 and one dot above 35. The range is 5 centimetres.. *Why:* Each result becomes a dot on the number line. Equal results stack. The range is the gap between the smallest and the biggest dot.
14. (mcq) Why do scientists draw the order of testing from a bowl?  
   Options: So that no sample always goes first or last / So the test takes longer / So that the tester can guess the result / So that the samples get tired  
   **Answer:** So that no sample always goes first or last. *Why:* A random order gives every sample a fair chance of an early slot and a late slot. Choosing 'takes longer' misses the reason: order can quietly change results.
15. (sort) Does each fault push every reading the same way, or make readings wobble?  
   **Answer:** A ruler with a chipped end → Same way every time; A thermometer that reads 2 degrees too high → Same way every time; Reading a fast bounce by eye → Wobbles; Strips with hidden nicks → Wobbles; A cloth tape that has stretched → Same way every time; A ball landing on slightly different spots → Wobbles. *Why:* A zero error or a stretched tape adds the same push to each reading. Hidden nicks, fast bounces and different landing spots make the numbers wobble from try to try.
16. (perspective) Jothi wants to find out if a new kind of kitchen paper is stronger than the old kind. She has time to test only 20 strips. Her family has different plans.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer chooses Jothi's plan. Ten strips of each kind lets her compare two ranges. Testing only the new paper gives no comparison. Three each gives a very rough picture. The aunt has a point that a small test gives a rough idea quickly, and the father is right that the new paper is the one to know about.. *Why:* The question is a fair comparison. A comparison needs both kinds, a fair number of tries and a random order.
### Reading Results Like a Scientist (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Five strips held 14, 22, 17, 19 and 9 coins. What is the middle value?  
   Options: 17 coins / 16.2 coins / 19 coins / 14 coins  
   **Answer:** 17 coins. *Why:* In order the strips are 9, 14, 17, 19 and 22. The middle one is 17. Picking 16.2 gives the average, which is 81 divided by 5. The average is pulled down by the 9.
2. (truefalse) The average and the middle value are always the same number.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. For 9, 14, 17, 19 and 22, the average is 16.2 and the middle value is 17. A very low or high result pulls the average but not the middle value.
3. (mcq) Two labs test the same rope. Lab A says it holds 200 kilograms and Lab B says 150 kilograms. What is the best next step?  
   Options: Check whether they used the same method, the same sample size and the same speed, then repeat with one agreed method / Believe the bigger number / Believe the smaller number / Add the numbers together  
   **Answer:** Check whether they used the same method, the same sample size and the same speed, then repeat with one agreed method. *Why:* Different methods give different numbers. A standard test fixes the method, so labs can agree. Believing one lab without checking is a bet.
4. (sort) Does each action help a fair test or hurt it?  
   **Answer:** Drawing the order from a bowl → Helps a fair test; Testing the new bag three times and the old bag once → Hurts a fair test; A helper hiding the names with codes → Helps a fair test; Writing down only the results that agree with your guess → Hurts a fair test; Reading every bounce from eye level → Helps a fair test; Telling the tasters which one is Premium → Hurts a fair test. *Why:* A random order, secret codes and the same way of reading help. Giving one sample more tries, hiding results and telling tasters the label push the result.
5. (write) An advert says: 'This bag is 30 per cent stronger.' Write three questions you would ask before believing it.  
   **Answer:** Stronger than what? How many bags were tested, and how many tries? How much did the results wobble, and did the ranges overlap? Who did the test?. *Why:* A number without a comparison, a number of tries and the spread cannot be checked.
6. (mcq) Arrows land close together, but far from the middle of the target. Which two words fit?  
   Options: Precise but not accurate / Accurate but not precise / Both precise and accurate / Neither precise nor accurate  
   **Answer:** Precise but not accurate. *Why:* Close together means the readings agree, which is precise. Far from the middle means they are not near the true size, which is not accurate. A tool with a zero error gives this pattern.
7. (match) Match each problem to a fix.  
   **Answer:** A bounce that is too fast for the eye → Film it and look frame by frame; A ruler with a zero error → Check it against another ruler; A tester who knows which sample is which → Use secret codes and a helper; Always testing one cloth first → Draw the order from a bowl; Only two tries → Do ten tries and draw a dot plot. *Why:* Each fault has a fix. Slow motion helps the eyes, a second ruler finds a zero error, codes stop hopes, a random order stops order effects, and more tries show the spread.
8. (order) Put the steps of a blind test in order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* The helper prepares first. Then the order is drawn. The tester tests without knowing. All numbers are written. Only at the end does the list open.
9. (draw) Draw two targets. On the first, show arrows that are precise but not accurate. On the second, show arrows that are accurate on average but not precise. Label each one.  
   **Answer:** First target: all arrows in a tight group off to one side. Second target: arrows spread widely, but scattered evenly around the middle.. *Why:* Precise means readings agree with each other. Accurate means they are close to the true size.
10. (truefalse) Clever Hans, the horse, answered correctly even when the person asking did not know the answer.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. When the questioner did not know the answer, or Hans could not see the questioner, he got it wrong. He was following tiny movements that people made without knowing.
11. (mcq) A wear test rubs a cloth on sandpaper until a hole appears. What does it leave out about real life?  
   Options: Sunlight, washing and sweat / How many rubs it takes / How rough the sandpaper is / Which cloth is tested  
   **Answer:** Sunlight, washing and sweat. *Why:* The test copies rubbing, but a real cloth also faces sunlight, washing and sweat. A test answers only the question it asks.
12. (fill) A sample where you do nothing to the thing you test, so that you can see what happens anyway, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['control']. *Why:* A control shows what changes by itself, so you do not give a cause the credit for something else.
13. (write) In the Secret Code Test, all four cloth squares were cut from one cloth. Explain why people could still give different scores.  
   **Answer:** The label can push a person's score up or down without them knowing, which is bias. Also, touch is rough. A person's score can wobble from one moment to the next, even for the same square. The blind round shows how much is just wobble.. *Why:* Scores include a push from the label and a wobble from the person. The blind round takes away the push.
14. (perspective) A school ruler has a worn end, so it reads 1 centimetre too big. Three children disagree about what to do.  
   **Answer:** Imran and the teacher use the fault wisely. A steady fault can be found and removed. In a difference between two readings, the same error cancels. Anjali is right that it is not safe for measuring a true size without a correction.. *Why:* A zero error is the same on every reading, so it can be found and handled. A wobbling error cannot.
15. (fill) A dot plot shows 3 dots at 31, 2 dots at 33 and 1 dot at 40. The range is ____ centimetres.  
   **Answer:** ['9']. *Why:* The range is the biggest minus the smallest. 40 minus 31 is 9. The dot at 40 is odd and widens the range a lot, which is a reason to check that drop.
16. (mcq) A lift cable is tested on many pieces. The middle piece broke at 20 tonnes and the weakest broke at 14 tonnes. The lift will carry 2 tonnes. Which number does an engineer worry about most?  
   Options: 14, because the weakest piece sets how safe the cable is / 20, because the middle is the usual / 2, because it is the smallest / The average of all three  
   **Answer:** 14, because the weakest piece sets how safe the cable is. *Why:* A cable can contain a weak piece. Safety comes from the weakest, with a big gap between it and the load. Choosing 20 usually means trusting the middle too much.
17. (mcq) Which is the best claim from a dot plot where two ranges overlap a lot?  
   Options: We cannot tell which is better with this test / The bigger middle value is better / The test is broken / Both are exactly the same  
   **Answer:** We cannot tell which is better with this test. *Why:* When ranges overlap a lot, the wobble is bigger than the gap. The honest claim says the test cannot tell. Saying 'exactly the same' goes too far, because a bigger test might show a small difference.
### Testing Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In the Eight Matchboxes game, 4 of 8 boxes are heavy. A player picks 4 boxes at random. There are 70 ways to pick 4 boxes from 8. In how many of these 70 ways are all 4 chosen boxes heavy?  
   Options: 1 / 4 / 8 / 70  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* Only one choice of four is exactly the four heavy boxes. So the chance of finding all four by luck is 1 in 70.
2. (fill) So the chance of finding all four heavy boxes by luck alone is 1 in ____.  
   **Answer:** ['70']. *Why:* One lucky way out of 70 possible ways gives all four heavy boxes. That is why finding all four several times in a row would be strong evidence of skill.
3. (write) In one round you find 3 of the 4 heavy boxes. Explain why this one round is not strong evidence that your hands are good.  
   **Answer:** There are 16 ways to find exactly 3, and 1 way to find all 4. So 17 of the 70 ways give 3 or more right. That is about 1 time in 4. It happens fairly often by luck. More rounds are needed.. *Why:* A result that luck gives about one time in four is not strong evidence. Repeating the test gives stronger evidence.
4. (perspective) A chemist makes a cloudy, thin liquid that is not what she expected. Her colleagues argue about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer chooses to keep and test it. Odd results can be slips or discoveries, and testing finds out which. Colleague 3 is right that a few repeats are a sensible start, and Colleague 1 is right that many odd results are mistakes. Throwing it away risks losing a discovery.. *Why:* An odd result needs to be checked. Deleting it hides evidence. Repeating it, and writing down its cause, is what finds the truth.
5. (mcq) A cloth test gives 20 for cloth A and 22 for cloth B, with ranges that overlap a lot. A buyer must choose one. What else should she think about?  
   Options: Price, how the cloth will be used and the weakest piece in each / Only the bigger number / Only the colour / Nothing, because the test says they are equal  
   **Answer:** Price, how the cloth will be used and the weakest piece in each. *Why:* When a test cannot tell two things apart, other facts decide: cost, use and the weak end of the range. Choosing 'the bigger number' ignores the wobble.
6. (sort) Does each test break the thing, or leave it whole?  
   **Answer:** Pulling a paper strip until it tears → Breaks it; Tapping a pot and listening to the ring → Leaves it whole; Bending a paper clip until it snaps → Breaks it; Shining ultrasound into a railway line → Leaves it whole; Looking at a bridge through a magnifier for cracks → Leaves it whole; Loading a rope until it snaps → Breaks it. *Why:* Some tests ask 'how much can it take?' and must break the sample. Others look for hidden cracks without harm. Engineers use both.
7. (truefalse) A test can prove that a rope will never break.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A test can show how a rope behaved on the day, but not what will happen on every day to come. Engineers talk about how likely a failure is, and keep inspecting.
8. (match) Match each fault to an example.  
   **Answer:** Bias → A tester pulls the cloth she hopes will lose a bit faster; Order effect → The last cloth is tested when the tester's arms are tired; Zero error → A ruler with a chipped end; Variation → Ten strips from one roll hold different loads; Control → A strip left dry beside the strips that are wetted. *Why:* Each example shows a different thing: a hope that pushes, an order that favours, a steady fault in a tool, a natural spread in the material, and a sample that does nothing.
9. (mcq) A ball dropped from 50 centimetres bounces to 30 centimetres. What fraction of the drop height did it keep?  
   Options: 3 out of 5, or 0.6 / 5 out of 3 / 2 out of 5 / 30 out of 80  
   **Answer:** 3 out of 5, or 0.6. *Why:* The ball kept 30 of the 50 centimetres. 30 divided by 50 is 0.6, which is the same as 3 out of 5. The rest of the energy went into sound and warmth.
10. (draw) Draw a plan for a blind test of two kinds of tissue paper. Show who does what, and how the order is chosen.  
   **Answer:** A helper tags 10 pieces of each kind with secret letters and keeps the list. The tester draws chits to set the order, tests each piece the same way, and writes the number against its letter. At the end the helper opens the list and the numbers are sorted into two dot plots.. *Why:* A blind test needs a helper with a secret list, a random order, one method for all pieces and a list opened only at the end.
11. (truefalse) If two labs get different numbers for the same rope, at least one lab is cheating.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Different methods, speeds, air or samples can give honestly different numbers. This is why labs agree on standard tests and compare.
12. (write) Seeds from one packet sprout on different days in the same pot. Say how this spread could help the seeds when the monsoon is late in some years.  
   **Answer:** If all seeds sprouted on the same day and the rains were late, they might all die. Seeds that sprout on different days give a chance that some meet the rain. The spread works like a safety margin for the plant.. *Why:* Variation in a group, such as sprouting day, can protect the group when the future is not certain.
13. (mcq) Why do labs in different countries agree on standard tests?  
   Options: So that results from different places can be compared / So that every lab gets the same answer by magic / So that no one needs to repeat a test / So that tests become shorter  
   **Answer:** So that results from different places can be compared. *Why:* A standard fixes the size of the sample, the speed and the air, so results from different labs can be compared. It does not remove the wobble, but it makes comparisons fair.
14. (fill) A test that rubs a material again and again to find how long it lasts is called a ____ test.  
   **Answer:** ['wear']. *Why:* A wear test copies rubbing in real use, in a shorter time.
15. (perspective) A weaver in Kanchipuram says she can feel with her fingers whether a silk thread is strong. A factory wants to pull every thread on a machine. They disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer values both. The weaver's hands carry long experience and are quick, but they need checking. The machine gives numbers but can miss what hands feel. A blind test, as the teacher says, finds out how good the hands are, and the two together can be better than either one.. *Why:* Experience and measurement can check each other. A blind test gives a fair way to compare.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does repeating a test ten times show you that one try cannot?  
   Options: How much the results vary / The exact true size of the thing / That the first try was wrong / Which sample you like best  
   **Answer:** How much the results vary. *Why:* Ten tries show the spread, so you know how much to trust one number. Picking 'the exact true size' is common but wrong: repeating shows the wobble, not the perfect number.
2. (mcq) Two groups of results have ranges 40 to 55 and 52 to 70. What can you honestly say?  
   Options: We cannot tell if they are really different, because the ranges overlap / The second is certainly bigger / The first is certainly bigger / The test must be wrong  
   **Answer:** We cannot tell if they are really different, because the ranges overlap. *Why:* The ranges share the numbers 52 to 55, so the wobble is bigger than the gap. Choosing 'certainly bigger' ignores the overlap.
3. (mcq) Six results in order are 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 19. What are the range and the middle value?  
   Options: Range 7, middle value 15 / Range 19, middle value 14 / Range 7, middle value 14 / Range 6, middle value 16  
   **Answer:** Range 7, middle value 15. *Why:* The range is 19 minus 12, which is 7. With six results, the middle is between 14 and 16, so the middle value is 15. Picking 14 means taking only the third result.
4. (mcq) A ruler with a chipped end reads 1 centimetre too much on every strip. What kind of fault is this?  
   Options: A zero error, which is the same on every reading / Variation in the material / Bias from the tester's hopes / A random wobble that changes each time  
   **Answer:** A zero error, which is the same on every reading. *Why:* The error is steady and comes from the tool. It can be found by comparing with another ruler. Choosing 'random wobble' mixes up two kinds of fault.
5. (mcq) Why is a helper who knows the secret list not the tester?  
   Options: The tester must not know which sample is which, so hopes cannot nudge the result / The helper is better at measuring / It makes the test faster / It makes the samples stronger  
   **Answer:** The tester must not know which sample is which, so hopes cannot nudge the result. *Why:* A blind test works because the tester cannot favour any sample. Choosing 'faster' misses the reason.
6. (mcq) Why is a random order better than 'start with my favourite'?  
   Options: Because no sample always goes first or last / Because it is more exciting / Because the tester can finish early / Because favourites are always the strongest  
   **Answer:** Because no sample always goes first or last. *Why:* An order can quietly change results as the tester tires or the materials change. A random order gives every sample the same chances.
7. (mcq) A bag for the monsoon is tested only when dry. What is the main problem?  
   Options: The test does not copy the real job, so it may not be valid / The test uses too many tries / The bag is too heavy / Dry tests are never used  
   **Answer:** The test does not copy the real job, so it may not be valid. *Why:* A test is valid if it measures what you care about. A bag meets rain, so its wet strength matters. A dry test answers a different question.
8. (mcq) Which claim best says how sure you are?  
   Options: The rubber ball bounces higher. I am fairly sure, because the ranges do not touch, but I tested on only one floor. / The rubber ball is the best ball. / The rubber ball won. / Rubber is always bouncy.  
   **Answer:** The rubber ball bounces higher. I am fairly sure, because the ranges do not touch, but I tested on only one floor.. *Why:* A good claim names the evidence, the reasoning and its limits. The other sentences give no evidence or go too far.
9. (truefalse) Repeating a test a hundred times will fix a ruler with a zero error.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Repeating shrinks the effect of luck but not a fault that pushes every reading the same way.
10. (mcq) In a test of tissue strength, one strip is left dry in the same room while the others are wetted. What is the dry strip?  
   Options: A control / A bias / A zero error / An odd result  
   **Answer:** A control. *Why:* A control gets everything except the thing being tested. It shows what changes anyway.
11. (mcq) A cable's middle piece breaks at 20 tonnes and its weakest piece at 14. Why does an engineer use the weakest piece?  
   Options: The weakest piece sets how safe the cable is / The weakest piece is always the average / The strongest piece is the least likely / The middle piece is not important  
   **Answer:** The weakest piece sets how safe the cable is. *Why:* A chain gives way at its weakest link. Engineers look at the low end of the spread and add a safety factor.
12. (mcq) In the Secret Code Test, why did people give different scores to identical squares?  
   Options: Labels can push scores, and touch also wobbles from moment to moment / The squares were different cloths / The squares changed during the test / People were told the wrong answer  
   **Answer:** Labels can push scores, and touch also wobbles from moment to moment. *Why:* The squares were the same cloth. The differences came from the label's push and ordinary wobble. A blind round takes away the push.

## Applications
- Testing ropes, helmets, lift cables and bridge parts on many samples, and designing for the weakest piece.
- Medicine trials in which patients and doctors do not know who has the real medicine.
- Factories that test a few items from every batch and plot the spread to see if a machine has drifted.
- Sports makers who test balls, bats and shoes with machines that repeat the same drop or kick thousands of times.
- Wear tests for jeans, school bags, shoes and carpets that squeeze months of use into a few hours.
- Food makers who run blind taste tests with codes and a random order.
- Crop scientists who sow the same seed in many fields to see how much yield varies.
- Labs that check each other by testing the same sample and comparing numbers.
- Weather and cricket scientists who report a range of possible results, not a single number.
- Standards offices, such as the Bureau of Indian Standards, that write agreed test recipes.

## Open questions
- **Is anything in nature exactly repeatable, or is some wobble built into the world?** At the smallest scale, physicists still argue whether chance is built into nature or only hides a pattern we cannot see.
- **How many tries are enough to be sure about a material that must never fail?** Engineers use maths to estimate, but the answer depends on how rare the failure is. For very rare failures, no test can be large enough.
- **Can a computer predict how a given piece of material will break before it is tested?** Computers can predict the usual, but the exact first crack in one piece depends on tiny flaws that no one can see.
- **How much do our hopes change our results without us knowing?** Psychologists keep finding new ways in which expectation changes what we see. Nobody has a full list.
- **Why do living things keep so much variation, even when it seems wasteful?** Biologists agree that variation helps groups survive changes, but they still argue about how much variation is the right amount.
- **Will a perfect test of 'strong' ever be found, one that works for every job?** Strong means different things for pulling, bending, hitting and wearing, so there may be no single best test.

## Connected maths
- The ten bounce heights of the rubber ball are 31, 33, 30, 34, 32, 31, 35, 30, 33 and 32 centimetres. Add them and divide by 10. What is the average? How does it compare with the middle value, which is 32? **The average is 32.1 centimetres. It is very close to the middle value of 32.**. The ten heights add up to 321. Dividing by 10 gives 32.1. The middle value is 32. They are close because there is no odd result pulling the average. If one drop had been 50, the average would jump and the middle value would hardly move. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the Bounce Test, the rubber ball's middle value was 32 and the marble's was 25. Each ball's ten drops had a range of 5. Is the gap between the middle values bigger or smaller than the range? What does this tell you? **The gap is 7, which is bigger than the range of 5. The difference is real.**. 32 take away 25 is 7. The gap of 7 is bigger than the wobble of 5 in each ball. So the marble's drops are not just a lucky low set. A difference bigger than the wobble can be believed. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
- A ball is dropped from 50 centimetres and bounces to 32 centimetres. What fraction of its drop height did it keep? Write it as a decimal and as a percentage. **32 out of 50, which is 0.64 or 64 per cent.**. 32 over 50 is the same as 64 over 100, because both numbers can be doubled. 64 over 100 is 0.64, or 64 per cent. The ball kept about two thirds of its height. The rest left as sound and warmth. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- If each bounce keeps about 64 per cent of the last height, and the first bounce was 32 centimetres, about how high is the second bounce? Round to the nearest centimetre. **About 20 centimetres.**. 64 per cent of 32 is 32 times 0.64. 32 times 0.6 is 19.2. 32 times 0.04 is 1.28. Together they are 20.48. To the nearest centimetre, that is 20. A real second bounce may be a little different, because every bounce wobbles. (queues `math.decimals.multiply-decimals.L4`)
- In the Eight Matchboxes activity, there are 70 ways to pick 4 boxes from 8. One way finds all four heavy boxes. Sixteen ways find exactly 3 heavy boxes. What fraction of the 70 ways gives 3 or more heavy boxes? About how often is that? **17 out of 70, which is about 1 time in 4.**. 1 plus 16 is 17. So 17 of the 70 ways give 3 or more. 70 divided by 17 is a little over 4. So about 1 time in 4 you would find 3 or more by pure luck. This is why one good round is not enough evidence. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- In the Rub Test, the middle values of rubs until a hole were: thin cotton 45, polyester 70, kitchen duster 62 and old jeans 100 or more. A cloth is rubbed 40 times a minute. How many minutes does the polyester need to reach its middle value? And how many minutes of rubbing does thin cotton need? **Polyester: 70 divided by 40 is 1 and three-quarter minutes, which is about 1 minute 45 seconds. Thin cotton: 45 divided by 40 is a little over 1 minute.**. 70 divided by 40 is 1.75 minutes, which is 1 minute and 45 seconds. 45 divided by 40 is 1.125 minutes, which is about 1 minute 8 seconds. So the whole wear test for cotton takes about a minute, but copies much longer of real use. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- A katori goes 20 centimetres along the sandpaper and 20 centimetres back for each rub. How far, in metres, does the cloth travel in 60 rubs? **24 metres.**. One rub is 20 plus 20, which is 40 centimetres. 60 rubs are 60 times 40, which is 2,400 centimetres. 100 centimetres make 1 metre, so 2,400 centimetres is 24 metres. The cloth is rubbed over a long distance in a small space. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)
- A school bag is carried twice a day on 200 school days. Say one carry does as much damage to a strap as 10 rubs on the machine, which does 40 rubs a minute. How many minutes does the machine need to copy one school year? **100 minutes.**. 2 carries a day on 200 days is 400 carries. 400 carries times 10 rubs is 4,000 rubs. 4,000 divided by 40 rubs a minute is 100 minutes. So a year of carrying is copied in about an hour and forty minutes. The number 10 rubs per carry is a guess, so the answer is a rough one. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A paper bag costs 6 rupees and holds a middle value of 40 coins. A jute bag costs 30 rupees and holds 50 coins. How many rupees does each bag cost for every coin it holds? Which is cheaper for each coin? **Paper: 6 divided by 40 is 0.15 rupees, or 15 paise. Jute: 30 divided by 50 is 0.60 rupees, or 60 paise. The paper bag is cheaper for each coin.**. 6 divided by 40 is 0.15. 30 divided by 50 is 0.6. The paper bag costs 15 paise for each coin, and the jute bag 60 paise. But a jute bag can be used many times, so the best choice also depends on how long each bag lasts. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)
- A lift cable is made so that its middle piece breaks at 20 tonnes and its weakest piece at 14 tonnes. The lift carries at most 2 tonnes. How many times bigger than the load is the weakest piece, and how many times bigger is the middle piece? **The weakest piece is 7 times the load. The middle piece is 10 times the load.**. 14 divided by 2 is 7. 20 divided by 2 is 10. A safety factor of 7 is the smaller of the two, and it is the one engineers trust. The gap between 7 and 10 shows why the spread matters. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
