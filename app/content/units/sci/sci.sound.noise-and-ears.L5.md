# Noise and our ears: what loud sound does, who it reaches, and how sounds warn
`sci.sound.noise-and-ears.L5`

**Summary.** A drummer loves her drum, but its beat wakes the baby next door and sends a myna away from her nest. Is it noise, or is it music? In this lesson you measure the sounds of your own home and street in decibels. You find out what loud sound does to the tiny hair cells inside the ear, and why that damage does not heal. You also look at what noise does to sleep, learning and other animals, at India's noise rules, and at warning sounds that are loud on purpose.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes for one minute and count every different sound you can hear. Now open them. Which of those sounds did you want to hear, and which would you switch off if you could?* You can shut your eyes, but you cannot shut your ears. Sound reaches them all day and all night, wanted or not. Some of it is only a nuisance. Some of it is strong enough to harm the ear for life, and gives no pain while it does so. Today you will measure sound with a number and learn what happens deep inside the ear. Then you will work out how a family, a street and a city can keep their hearing.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say why 'noise' depends on the listener and 'loudness' can be measured.
1. I can measure sound levels around my home in decibels and compare them with safe limits.
2. I can explain what loud sound does to the hair cells in the ear, and why the damage stays.
3. I can name harms of noise beyond the ear, for people and for other animals.
4. I can state India's main rules on noise from loudspeakers, crackers and horns.
5. I can protect my ears in three ways: a quieter source, more distance or a barrier, and less time.
6. I can say what makes a good warning sound, and why too many warnings stop working.

## Terms
- **noise**: Sound that the listener does not want. *e.g.* A film song you love is music at a wedding. The same song, played loudly next door at midnight, is noise to you.
- **vibration**: A very fast shaking to and fro. *e.g.* Touch a steel plate while a mixer-grinder is running. The shaking you feel is vibration.
- **sound wave**: A moving pattern of air that is squeezed and then stretched, which carries sound from its source to your ear. *e.g.* Clap once. The air is pushed outward in a sound wave that reaches the wall and your ear.
- **signal**: A message carried by something that changes, such as a sound, a light or a nerve's tiny electric pulse. *e.g.* A pressure cooker's whistle is a signal that the steam inside is ready.
- **pollutant**: Something put into the air, water or soil that can harm the beings who share it. *e.g.* Smoke from a bonfire is a pollutant of the air. Indian law also counts loud noise as one.
- **decibel**: The unit for how strong a sound is. It is written dB. *e.g.* Whispering is about 30 decibels. Ordinary talking is about 60. A mixer-grinder at arm's length is about 90.
- **sound energy**: The energy a sound carries as it moves through the air. *e.g.* A loud horn carries far more sound energy to your ear in each second than a whisper does.
- **sound-level meter**: A tool with a microphone and a display that shows how strong a sound is, in decibels. *e.g.* A teacher holds a sound-level meter at the back of the hall to check the band is not too loud.
- **pitch**: How high or low a sound is. *e.g.* A mosquito's whine has a high pitch. A truck's rumble has a low pitch.
- **A-weighted**: A way of measuring sound so that each pitch counts about as much as a human ear hears it. It is written dB(A). *e.g.* India's noise limits are given in dB(A), because they are meant to protect human ears.
- **microphone**: A small part that turns the shaking of sound into a tiny electric signal. *e.g.* The tiny hole at the bottom of your phone is the opening to its microphone.
- **average reading**: The middle value of a measurement that keeps jumping up and down, taken over a set time. *e.g.* A meter jumps between 55 and 75 on a street. Over 30 seconds the average reading may be 66.
- **cochlea**: The coiled, liquid-filled part of the inner ear where vibration becomes nerve signals. It is about the size of a pea. *e.g.* Your cochlea is curled like a tiny snail shell, deep inside the bone beside each ear.
- **hair cell**: A sensing cell in the cochlea with a tuft of tiny stiff hairs. When the tuft bends, the cell sends a signal. *e.g.* A loud night of crackers can bend the hair cells so far that some do not recover.
- **nerve signal**: A tiny electric pulse that travels along a nerve to carry a message to the brain. *e.g.* When a hair cell bends, a nerve signal runs to your brain and you hear a sound.
- **hertz**: The unit for how many vibrations happen in one second. It is written Hz. *e.g.* A note of 4,000 hertz is a very high whistle. The air shakes 4,000 times each second.
- **regrow**: To grow back after being lost or damaged. *e.g.* A lizard can regrow her tail. A bird can regrow the hair cells in her ear. A person cannot.
- **hearing aid**: A small device worn in or behind the ear that makes sounds louder. *e.g.* A grandfather with a hearing aid can follow the talk at the dinner table again.
- **cochlear implant**: A device placed inside the ear in an operation. It sends electrical signals straight to the hearing nerve. *e.g.* A child may receive a cochlear implant when her hair cells cannot send signals.
- **Deaf**: A word, written with a capital D, for people who do not hear much or at all and who often belong to a community that signs. *e.g.* Many Deaf people in India use Indian Sign Language and see it as a full language of their own.
- **sign language**: A full language made with the hands, face and body, with its own grammar. *e.g.* Indian Sign Language has signs for words, and a person can tell a story in it, just as in Tamil or Hindi.
- **muffled**: Dull and hard to hear, as if wrapped in cotton. *e.g.* After a loud wedding band, the world may sound muffled for some hours.
- **tinnitus**: A ringing, buzzing or hissing heard in the ear when no sound is outside. *e.g.* After a loud function, a boy hears a thin ringing in bed at night. That is tinnitus.
- **hidden hearing loss**: The idea that loud sound can destroy links between hair cells and the nerve, though a normal hearing test still passes. *e.g.* A person passes a hearing test, yet cannot follow talk in a noisy crowd.
- **connection**: A link that joins one cell to another so a signal can pass. *e.g.* The connections between a hair cell and its nerve carry each sound towards the brain.
- **acoustic reflex**: The ear's automatic tightening of a tiny muscle when a loud sound arrives. It lowers the sound that reaches the inner ear. *e.g.* When a loud lorry passes, your acoustic reflex tightens in about a tenth of a second.
- **eardrum**: The thin skin stretched across the inside of the ear, which shakes when sound reaches it. *e.g.* A sound enters your ear and shakes your eardrum, like the skin of a drum.
- **middle ear**: The air-filled space behind the eardrum that holds three tiny bones. *e.g.* Vibration from the eardrum crosses the middle ear along three tiny bones.
- **bang**: A sound that rises to full strength almost at once and is over in a moment. *e.g.* A burst balloon, a cap gun and a cracker all make a bang.
- **impulse sound**: The scientists' name for a bang: one very short, very sharp jump in air pressure. *e.g.* A cracker makes an impulse sound, which is why its loudness is measured in a special way.
- **firecracker limit**: India's rule that a cracker must not make more than 125 decibels measured 4 metres from where it bursts. *e.g.* A cracker sold in a shop must stay under the firecracker limit, but a child holding one stands much closer than 4 metres.
- **stress**: The body's alarm reaction, with faster heartbeat and tense muscles, when something seems like danger. *e.g.* A sudden horn makes your heart beat faster. That is the body's stress reaction.
- **blood pressure**: How hard the blood pushes against the insides of the blood tubes. *e.g.* When you are startled, your blood pressure goes up for a short time.
- **noise pollution**: Unwanted or harmful sound that is put into the shared air. *e.g.* Constant horns on a street are noise pollution for everyone and everything that lives beside it.
- **residential area**: A part of a town where people live in houses and flats. *e.g.* A lane of homes, a small temple and a corner tea shop is a residential area.
- **silence zone**: The area within 100 metres of a hospital, a school or a court, where India's rules set the strictest noise limits. *e.g.* A 'No Horn' board on a road beside a hospital marks a silence zone.
- **loudspeaker**: A device that turns an electric signal back into sound, often to reach many people. *e.g.* A loudspeaker on a pole carries a festival song across the whole neighbourhood.
- **source**: The place where a sound begins. *e.g.* The source of a bus horn is the horn. The source of your voice is your throat.
- **barrier**: Something placed in the path of sound that blocks or reduces it. *e.g.* A thick wall between a road and a school is a barrier to traffic sound.
- **dose**: The total amount of something a body takes in. For sound, it depends on how loud it is and for how long. *e.g.* A loud sound for a short while and a quieter sound for much longer can give the same dose to the ear.
- **earplug**: A small soft plug that fits in the ear canal and lowers the sound that reaches the ear. *e.g.* A drummer pushes foam earplugs gently into her ears before practice.
- **ear muff**: A pair of cups that cover the whole ear and lower the sound that reaches it. *e.g.* A road-drill worker wears ear muffs over both ears.
- **earphone**: A small speaker worn in or on the ear, which puts sound straight into the ear canal. *e.g.* A boy listens to songs through earphones on the bus.
- **weekly allowance**: The total amount of listening an ear can safely take in one week. *e.g.* For a child, the World Health Organization's weekly allowance is 75 decibels for up to 40 hours.
- **absorb**: To soak sound up so that little of it bounces back. *e.g.* A thick curtain absorbs sound, so a room with curtains sounds softer.
- **reflect**: To bounce back from a surface. *e.g.* A bare wall reflects sound, so a shout in an empty room comes back to you.
- **cancel**: To remove each other when two opposite things are added together. *e.g.* Two waves that squeeze and stretch the air in exactly opposite ways cancel each other and leave a flat line.
- **noise-cancelling**: Using a speaker to play the opposite of an incoming sound so the two cancel. *e.g.* Noise-cancelling headphones quieten the steady drone of an aeroplane engine.
- **silencer**: A part that lets air or gas pass through but makes the sound bounce around inside until it fades. It is also called a baffle. *e.g.* The silencer under a scooter is a metal box that makes the engine's bang much quieter.
- **warning**: A signal made to tell you that danger is near, so you can act. *e.g.* The shrill beep of a smoke alarm is a warning to wake up and leave.
- **siren**: A loud warning sound that usually rises and falls in pitch. *e.g.* An ambulance siren tells the traffic ahead to move aside.
- **locate**: To find where something is. *e.g.* With two ears you can locate a ringing bell, even with your eyes shut.
- **alarm call**: A sound an animal makes when she or he senses danger, which warns others. *e.g.* A langur's bark is an alarm call that makes chital deer run.
- **playback**: Playing a recorded sound to animals to see how they respond. *e.g.* Scientists hid a loudspeaker in the grass and played back a leopard alarm call to vervet monkeys.
- **alarm fatigue**: Becoming so used to frequent warnings that you stop responding to them. *e.g.* A nurse who hears hundreds of beeps a shift may stop noticing a real one. That is alarm fatigue.
- **silence**: Having no sound at all. Nobody who can hear has ever found it fully. *e.g.* A very quiet room still has your breath, your blood and the faint hiss of your own ears. So full silence is hard to find.
- **echo**: A sound that bounces back from a hard surface so that you hear it again. *e.g.* If you shout in a stairwell with bare walls, you hear your own voice come back.

## Explanations
### Noise is sound that the listener does not want, so no instrument can measure it · *defined*
**Definition:** Noise is unwanted sound. Whether a sound is noise depends on who is listening, when, and why.

A sound is a vibration travelling through the air. That part is physics, and it is the same for everyone. Noise is a judgement about a sound. The mixer-grinder at seven in the morning is a useful sound to the person making chutney. It is noise to the person asleep in the next room. The vibration is the same in both rooms. So a meter can tell you how strong a sound is. No meter can tell you that a sound is noise. For that you have to ask the listeners, and they will not all agree. This is why arguments about noise are so hard to settle.
- e.g. A film song you love, played by a neighbour at midnight, becomes noise.
- e.g. A dripping tap is very quiet, and it can still be noise to someone trying to sleep.
- e.g. A mridangam is music in a concert and noise during an examination next door.
- **Sound engineers and radio engineers:** Noise is anything that gets in the way of the signal you are trying to receive. The hiss under a phone call is noise because it carries no message. On this view, noise depends on what you are listening for.
- **R. Murray Schafer, a Canadian composer, in his book 'The Soundscape' in 1977:** Noises are the sounds we have learned to ignore. He asked people to listen to their surroundings as carefully as to a piece of music, and to decide which sounds they wish to keep.
- **The law of India, since the Air Act was changed in 1987:** Noise is counted as a pollutant of the air, in the same law as smoke. So the law treats it as something put into shared air that can harm others, whatever the maker thinks of it.
### Decibels do not add like ordinary numbers: two horns are 3 decibels more than one · *defined*
**Definition:** The decibel is the unit for how strong a sound is. Every 10 decibels more means 10 times the sound energy, and doubling the energy adds about 3 decibels.

Sound strength is measured in decibels, written dB. The scale is built so that each step of 10 decibels means 10 times as much sound energy reaching the ear. That gives the scale an odd arithmetic. Suppose one auto horn measures 90 decibels where you stand. Two such horns together do not make 180. They make about 93. Ten horns make 100, and a hundred horns make 110. This has a hopeful side and a hard side. The hard side is that removing half the vehicles from a road lowers the level by only 3 decibels. The hopeful side is that every 3 decibels you do remove halves the energy reaching the ear.
- e.g. A whisper is about 30 decibels and ordinary talk about 60. Talk carries about 1,000 times the sound energy of a whisper.
- e.g. A mixer-grinder at arm's length is often 85 to 95 decibels.
- e.g. If a classroom of 40 children measures 80 decibels, 20 children talking the same way would measure about 77.
### A sound-level meter listens the way a human ear does, and a phone can be one · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound-level meter is a microphone joined to a display that shows decibels. Most readings are 'A-weighted', written dB(A), which means the meter counts each pitch about as strongly as a human ear hears it.

A human ear does not hear every pitch equally well. We hear the pitches of speech best, and very low rumbles poorly. So most sound-level meters are set to copy this. Such a reading is called A-weighted and is written dB(A). India's noise rules use it. A phone has a microphone, and free apps turn a phone into a rough meter. In 2014 two scientists at the safety institute of the United States, Chucri Kardous and Peter Shaw, tested many such apps. A few read within about 2 decibels of a proper meter, and many were far off. So a phone is good for comparing one place with another, and poor for proving that a law was broken.
- e.g. Hold the phone at arm's length, with the microphone end towards the sound, and read the average over 30 seconds, not the jumping number.
- e.g. A dog hears much higher pitches than we do, so a meter set for human ears leaves out sounds that matter to a dog.
### Deep in the ear, about 15,000 hair cells turn vibration into nerve signals · *defined*
**Definition:** Hair cells are sensing cells in the cochlea, the coiled part of the inner ear. Each carries a tuft of tiny stiff hairs. When sound bends the tuft, the cell sends a signal to the brain.

Sound entering the ear ends its journey in the cochlea, a coiled tube about the size of a pea, filled with liquid. Along the inside stand rows of hair cells, about 15,000 in each ear. They are named for the tuft of very fine, stiff hairs on top of each cell. A sound makes the liquid move, the movement bends the tufts, and each bent cell sends a signal along a nerve. A quiet sound bends the tufts a little. A loud sound bends them a lot. A very loud sound bends them too far. Tufts go limp, break or fuse together. With enough loud sound, over minutes or over years, the hair cells themselves die.
- e.g. The hair cells nearest the cochlea's entrance sense high pitches, and loud sound usually harms them first. People with noise damage often first lose the hearing of pitches around 4,000 hertz.
- e.g. Under a powerful microscope, healthy tufts stand in neat rows like combs, and noise-damaged ones lie flat or tangled.
### In people, dead hair cells are not replaced. In birds and fishes, they are · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans and other mammals are born with all the cochlear hair cells they will ever have. Birds, fishes and frogs can grow new ones.

Skin heals, and bone heals. The hair cells of the human cochlea do not. You are born with about 15,000 in each ear. When one dies, a scar forms in their place. This is why hearing lost to loud sound never comes back. For a long time scientists thought that was true of all animals. In 1987 and 1988, researchers in the United States deafened young chickens and adult quail with loud sound and looked again days later. New hair cells had grown, and the birds could hear again. The work was done by Douglas Cotanche and Jeffrey Corwin, and by Brenda Ryals and Edwin Rubel. Fishes and frogs can do this too. Why mammals cannot is still being worked out.
- e.g. A hearing aid makes sounds louder for the hair cells that remain. A cochlear implant sends electrical signals straight to the hearing nerve.
- e.g. A zebrafish, a small striped fish from India's rivers, regrows hair cells within days, and laboratories around the world study them to learn how.
- **Researchers trying to regrow human hair cells:** The cells beside each hair cell in a bird's ear can turn into new hair cells. Mammals have the same neighbour cells, and they stay as they are. If the switch can be found, hearing loss might one day be reversed. Trials in people have not yet restored hearing.
- **Many Deaf people who use Indian Sign Language and other sign languages:** Being Deaf is a way of living with a full language and a community of its own. On this view deafness is not always something broken that waits to be fixed. They ask that hearing people do not assume every Deaf person wants to hear.
### Muffled hearing and ringing after loud sound are the ear's report of overwork · *defined*
**Definition:** After loud sound, hearing may be dulled for hours, and a ringing or buzzing may be heard that has no outside source. The ringing is called tinnitus.

After a loud wedding band or a night of crackers, the world can sound as if wrapped in cotton. Quiet sounds go missing. This is called a temporary shift in hearing. The overworked hair cells respond weakly for some hours or a day, and then most recover. Often there is also a ringing, hissing or buzzing that nobody else can hear. That is tinnitus. Both are signs that the sound was more than the ear could take. If they happen again and again, the muffling stops going away. Scientists do not fully know where tinnitus is made. The leading idea is that the brain, getting less signal from the ear, turns up its own sensitivity and begins to hear its own activity.
- e.g. If you must shout to be heard by someone an arm's length away, the sound around you is strong enough to cause this.
- e.g. For some people tinnitus never stops. There is no cure yet, though there are ways to make it easier to live with.
- **Sushruta Samhita, Uttara Tantra, chapter 20, an Indian medical text whose oldest parts are more than 2,000 years old:** The chapter lists 28 diseases of the ear. Two of them are karnanada, a sound heard in the ear such as a ringing or a drum, and badhirya, deafness. The text explains them by a disturbance of vata, the moving principle of the body, in the channels of hearing.
- **Hearing scientists today:** Most place the start of tinnitus in damage to the ear and its continuing in the brain. They agree that the details are not settled, and that different people's tinnitus may have different causes.
### Hearing can come back after loud sound while hidden damage stays · *debated*
**Definition:** Hidden hearing loss is the idea that loud sound can destroy the connections between hair cells and the hearing nerve. A normal hearing test can still show full recovery.

If your hearing returns the next day, was any harm done? For a long time the answer given was no. In 2009 Sharon Kujawa and Charles Liberman, at a hospital in Boston in the United States, tested this in mice. They gave the mice loud sound for two hours, enough to dull their hearing for a while. Within two weeks the mice passed a hearing test again. Their hair cells were alive. But under the microscope, up to half of the connections joining those hair cells to the nerve were gone, and they did not grow back. A standard hearing test, which uses quiet beeps in a silent room, cannot detect this. It might show up instead as trouble following speech in a noisy place.
- e.g. A person may pass a hearing test and still say, 'I can hear you, but in a crowd I cannot make out the words.'
- **Sharon Kujawa, Charles Liberman and others who study animals' ears:** The same loss of connections has been found in guinea pigs and monkeys, and in the ears of older people examined after death. So 'my hearing came back' may not mean 'no harm was done'.
- **Garreth Prendergast and colleagues in Manchester, Britain, in 2017, and other groups testing living people:** They compared young adults who had heard a lot of loud noise with young adults who had heard little. They found no clear difference in the nerve signals or in understanding speech in noise. Human ears may be tougher than mouse ears, or the tests may be too blunt to show the loss.
### The ear has its own guard, and a sudden bang is too quick for it · *defined*
**Definition:** The acoustic reflex is the automatic tightening of a tiny muscle in the middle ear when a loud sound arrives. It lowers the sound passed to the inner ear, but it needs about a tenth of a second to act.

Behind the eardrum sit three very small bones that pass vibration inwards. A tiny muscle is attached to the last of them. When a sound louder than about 80 decibels arrives, this muscle tightens, without your choosing, and stiffens the chain of bones. Less vibration gets through, mostly of low pitches. This is the acoustic reflex. It has two weaknesses. It tires during long loud sound. And it takes about a tenth of a second to work. The bang of a cracker reaches full strength in less than a thousandth of a second. So the loudest and most sudden sounds arrive before the guard is up.
- e.g. The same muscle tightens just before you speak, which helps to stop your own voice from deafening you.
- e.g. A single very loud bang close to the ear can cause lasting loss in one moment. Doctors call this acoustic trauma.
### Crackers and other bangs: the law sets a limit of 125 decibels at 4 metres · *defined*
**Definition:** India's rules forbid making or selling a firecracker that produces more than 125 decibels of impulse sound measured 4 metres from where it bursts.

A bang is a single sharp jump in air pressure. Because it is over so fast, people often think it is harmless. The ear disagrees, as the last card showed. India has had a rule about this since 1999. A cracker must not make more than 125 decibels, measured in a special way for bangs, at 4 metres from the bursting point. Now think about where people stand. A child lighting a cracker is often 1 metre away or less, where the sound is far stronger than at 4 metres. A cracker that reads 125 decibels at 4 metres would sound about 12 decibels stronger at 1 metre. In 2018 the Supreme Court of India limited the bursting of crackers on Diwali to two hours, from 8 to 10 in the evening. It also allowed only lower-smoke 'green' crackers.
- e.g. Crackers are burst at Diwali, at weddings, at Vishu in Kerala, at Christmas and New Year, at cricket wins and at election results. The ear responds the same way at each.
- e.g. A burst balloon close to the ear and a cap gun fired beside the head are bangs too.
- e.g. People who make crackers, many of them in and around Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu, face the greatest risks of all, from explosions at work.
- **Families and communities for whom crackers are part of celebration:** The sound is the joy. A festival without it feels smaller, and many people's living depends on making and selling crackers. They ask why a few nights a year should be singled out when traffic is loud every day.
- **Ear doctors, and people who care for babies, the old, the sick and animals:** One night is enough to cause a loss that lasts for life. Babies cannot cover their ears or move away. Dogs, cats, cattle and roosting birds are terrified, and cannot know that it will end. They ask for light without the bang, or for one shared display at a distance.
### Noise harms more than the ears: sleep, the heart and the body's alarm system · *defined*
**Definition:** Long-lasting noise, even at levels too low to damage hearing, raises the body's stress responses, breaks up sleep, and is linked with heart disease.

Hearing never switches off, not even in sleep. That served our ancestors well, because a sound in the night could mean danger. So when a lorry passes at night, your heart beats faster and your blood pressure rises, though you may not wake or remember it. If this happens night after night for years, the body pays. In 2018 the World Health Organization reviewed the studies for Europe. It advised that road traffic noise outside homes should average below 53 decibels over the day and below 45 at night. It judged that the evidence was strong that road noise raises the risk of heart disease. India's rule for homes, 55 by day and 45 by night, is not far from these values, though the two are measured in different ways.
- e.g. You can 'get used to' the trains passing your house and stop noticing them. Measurements show the sleeping body still responds to each one.
- e.g. In 1859 Florence Nightingale wrote in 'Notes on Nursing' that unnecessary noise is the cruellest absence of care. She said it harms the sick and the well alike.
- **The World Health Organization's expert group, in 2018:** The link between road noise and heart disease is strong enough to act on. The link with some other illnesses is weaker, and they said so.
- **Scientists who urge caution about these studies:** Homes on noisy roads also have dirtier air, and the families in them are often poorer. Separating the effect of noise from the effects of smoke and of poverty is hard. Most agree that noise does harm, and disagree about how much.
### Noise slows learning: the school beside the railway · *defined*
**Definition:** Children taught in steadily noisy rooms often read worse than children in quiet rooms. In one New York school the gap was up to 11 months, and it closed when the noise was cut.

In the 1970s, a school in New York stood beside a raised railway line. A train went past the classrooms on one side about every four and a half minutes. It measured about 89 decibels, and teachers had to stop speaking each time. The psychologist Arline Bronzaft compared the children on the railway side with the children on the quiet side of the same school. By the sixth class, the railway-side children were up to 11 months behind in reading. The city then fitted rubber pads to the track and sound-absorbing ceilings to the rooms. When Bronzaft tested again, the two sides of the school read equally well. So the cause had been the noise, and the harm could be undone.
- e.g. A teacher who must stop for every passing train or horn loses many minutes of each lesson.
- e.g. A child trying to read while a television plays must work harder to hold each sentence in mind.
### Other animals live inside our noise, and some change their voices because of it · *defined*
**Definition:** Human-made noise changes how other animals communicate, find food, and cope with stress, on land and in the sea.

Most animals depend on sound more than we do. A bird's song tells other birds, 'This patch is mine,' and calls a mate. A whale's call keeps a group together across many kilometres of dark water. Our noise covers these sounds. In 2003 Hans Slabbekoorn and Margriet Peet reported something surprising in the journal Nature. Great tits, small songbirds, sing at a higher pitch in the noisy parts of a Dutch city. Traffic rumble is low, so a higher song gets through. In the sea, a team led by Rosalind Rolland was collecting dung from right whales near Canada in September 2001. Ship traffic dropped for some days, and the water became quieter. The stress chemicals in the whales' dung fell.
- e.g. In many Indian cities, magpie-robins, koels and other birds sing well before dawn, when the roads are quiet.
- e.g. Street dogs and pet dogs hide, shake and run during cracker nights, and many are lost.
- e.g. In 2014 Heidi Appel and Reginald Cocroft played thale cress plants the vibrations of a chewing caterpillar. The plants then made more of their defence chemicals, which make leaves harder for caterpillars to eat. So plants respond to vibration, and very little is known about what our noise does to them.
- **Biologists who study animals in cities:** Some beings adjust: they sing higher, louder or at different hours. Others simply leave. A quieter-looking city park may have lost the birds who could not adjust.
- **The usual way noise rules are written:** Limits are set in dB(A), which is shaped to the human ear, and are measured where people live. They take no account of the pitches other animals hear, or of forests and seas.
### India's noise rules: four kinds of area, day and night limits, and quiet from 10 at night to 6 in the morning · *defined*
**Definition:** The Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000, set limits in dB(A) for four kinds of area and forbid loudspeakers at night.

India has had national noise rules since the year 2000. They sort every place into four kinds of area and give each a day limit and a night limit, in dB(A). Industrial areas: 75 by day and 70 by night. Commercial areas, such as markets: 65 and 55. Residential areas, where people live: 55 and 45. Silence zones: 50 and 40. A silence zone is the area within 100 metres of a hospital, a school or a court. Day means 6 in the morning to 10 at night. A loudspeaker needs written permission, and may not be used between 10 at night and 6 in the morning except inside a closed hall. States may relax the night hours to midnight on up to 15 days a year for festivals.
- e.g. A home is meant to have no more than 55 dB(A) outside the window by day. Ordinary talk at one metre is about 60.
- e.g. Blowing a horn in a silence zone is an offence, which is why some roads near hospitals carry 'No Horn' boards.
### Why rules alone have not made India quiet: whose sound counts? · *debated*
**Definition:** Noise limits are widely exceeded in Indian cities. People disagree about why, and about whose sounds should give way.

Measure a busy junction in almost any Indian city and you will often read about 80 decibels or more, well above every limit in the rules. Why? Horns are one reason, and they are a habit, not a need. Another reason is that the rules depend on someone complaining about a neighbour, a wedding, a place of worship or a political meeting. Few people want to. In Mumbai, Sumaira Abdulali began measuring noise with a meter in the early 2000s and took her numbers to the Bombay High Court. The court ordered silence zones to be marked and enforced. In 2020 the Mumbai police tried a traffic signal that stayed red for longer whenever honking at the junction went above 85 decibels.
- e.g. Think of a wedding band, a festival loudspeaker, a call from a place of worship and an election rally. Each is precious to some people and a burden to others nearby.
- e.g. A night-shift nurse who sleeps by day has no 'quiet hours' in the rules at all.
- **Campaigners such as Sumaira Abdulali and the Awaaz Foundation:** The limits protect health, so they should apply to every source equally, whoever is making the sound and for whatever reason. A number on a meter treats everyone the same.
- **People who defend loud celebration and worship:** Sound in public is how a community shows that it is there. Rules, they say, are often used against the poor, street vendors and smaller communities. Traffic and construction are louder and last longer, yet they go on unchecked.
- **Traffic researchers:** Fines for honking change little. Road design, signal timing and quieter horns change more. They point out that drivers honk because roads are shared by walkers, cycles, carts and lorries with no separate space.
### Three ways to protect ears: make less sound, get farther or put something between, and stay for less time · *defined*
**Definition:** The dose of sound an ear receives depends on the level at the source, the distance and barriers in the path, and the length of time. Lowering any one protects the ear.

Every plan to protect hearing uses one of three levers. The first is the source: turn it down, or choose a quieter one. The second is the path between the source and you: move away, shut a door, or wear ear protection. The third is time: stay for a shorter while, and give your ears quiet hours afterwards. Here 'time' only means how long, counted on a clock. What time itself is, nobody knows for certain, and that is a question for another day.
- e.g. At a loud function, sitting at the back and far from the speakers changes the level more than anything else you can do.
- e.g. People who work with loud machines, such as road drills and factory looms, should be given ear protection by their employer.
- e.g. A baby cannot move away or say that they are hurt, so the family must carry the baby away from the sound.
### Ear protection in numbers: foam earplugs and ear muffs cut a lot, and cotton wool cuts very little · *defined*
**Definition:** A well-fitted foam earplug or a pair of ear muffs lowers sound by about 15 to 30 decibels. Dry cotton wool lowers it by only about 5 to 7.

Ear protection is worth knowing in numbers. Soft foam earplugs, rolled thin and pushed gently into the ear canal, cut sound by about 15 to 30 decibels when they fit well. Ear muffs that cover the whole ear cut about as much. A ball of dry cotton wool cuts only about 5 to 7 decibels. That is why cotton in the ears on a cracker night helps so little. Take 25 decibels off, and the sound energy that reaches your ear falls to roughly one three-hundredth. Take 6 off, as cotton does, and it falls to about one quarter.
- e.g. A foam earplug that sits loosely cuts far less than one that fits snugly, so the fit matters as much as the kind.
- e.g. Earplugs do not make a friend's voice vanish. They make everything softer, so you can still follow talk beside you.
### Earphones put the source inside the ear, so volume and hours both count · *defined*
**Definition:** Earphones deliver sound straight into the ear canal with no distance to weaken it. Safe listening depends on both the level and the total hours each week.

With earphones, two of the three levers disappear. There is no distance and no barrier. Only the volume and the time are left. Some phones at full volume can send more than 100 decibels into the ear. The World Health Organization gives a simple weekly allowance. For an adult it is 80 decibels for up to 40 hours a week. For a child it is 75 decibels for up to 40 hours. Every 3 decibels above that halves the safe hours. The World Health Organization estimates that more than one billion young people in the world are at risk of hearing loss from unsafe listening. A practical rule is to keep the volume at or below 60 per cent. You should also be able to hear someone speaking to you at arm's length.
- e.g. In a noisy bus people turn the volume up to beat the engine, so earphones in traffic are riskier than the same earphones at home.
- e.g. Many phones can show your listening level for the week, and can be set to limit the maximum.
### Engineering quiet: block it, soak it up, or cancel it with its own mirror image · *defined*
**Definition:** Engineers reduce noise in three ways. Barriers reflect sound, soft materials absorb it, and active cancelling uses a speaker that plays the opposite of the incoming sound wave.

A wall beside a flyover reflects traffic sound back. A heavy, solid barrier that hides the road from view can lower the level behind it by 5 to 10 decibels. Soft, porous things such as thick curtains, mats and foam soak sound up. Their many small air spaces turn the vibration into a tiny amount of heat. The cleverest method adds sound to remove sound. A sound wave is a pattern of air squeezed and stretched. If a speaker plays the exact opposite pattern, stretched where the noise is squeezed, the two cancel. The German physicist Paul Lueg patented the idea in the 1930s. In 1978 the engineer Amar Bose, whose father had come to the United States from Bengal, sketched a headphone using it during a flight. His company sold the first such headsets to pilots in 1989.
- e.g. Cancelling works best on steady, low sounds such as an aeroplane's engine drone. It works poorly on sudden or high sounds such as a horn.
- e.g. A room with bare walls and floor echoes. The same room with mattresses, curtains and people in it sounds much quieter.
- e.g. A belt of trees hides a road well, but it must be thick and dense to lower the measured level by much.
### Some sounds are loud on purpose: what makes a good warning · *defined*
**Definition:** A warning sound is made to be noticed over everything else, to show where it comes from, and to carry a meaning that listeners have learned.

Sound is the best carrier for a warning. It travels round corners, works in the dark, and reaches people who are looking the other way or asleep. A good warning does three things. It stands out: engineers aim for a warning about 15 decibels above the background sound. It can be located: a siren that sweeps up and down in pitch is easier for two ears to place than one steady note. And it has a meaning people have been taught. A pressure cooker's whistle says the steam inside has reached full pressure. A train's horn says keep off the track. An ambulance siren says move aside. A sound with no agreed meaning warns nobody.
- e.g. In coastal villages of Odisha and Tamil Nadu, sirens on towers warn of cyclones, and some also warn of tsunamis.
- e.g. A smoke alarm is made shrill and unpleasant on purpose, so that nobody can sleep through it.
- e.g. Deaf people are warned by other means: flashing lights, or a pad under the pillow that shakes.
### Other animals give warnings too, and scientists argue about what their calls mean · *debated*
**Definition:** Many animals give alarm calls when they detect danger. Some species use different calls for different dangers, and listeners respond differently to each.

In an Indian forest, a langur high in a tree sees a tiger and gives a harsh bark. The chital deer grazing below cannot see the tiger. They run at once. The deer have learned the monkeys' alarm, and the monkeys in turn heed the deer's. In Kenya in 1980, Robert Seyfarth, Dorothy Cheney and Peter Marler studied vervet monkeys. These monkeys give one call for a leopard, another for an eagle and another for a snake. The scientists hid a loudspeaker and played recorded calls when no danger was near. At the leopard call the monkeys ran up trees. At the eagle call they looked up and dived into bushes. At the snake call they stood up and searched the grass.
- e.g. Forest guides in tiger reserves such as Bandipur, Kanha and Periyar find a tiger by listening for the alarm calls of deer, langurs and peafowl.
- e.g. A babbler's alarm sends a whole mixed flock of different bird species into cover.
- **Seyfarth, Cheney and Marler, in the journal Science in 1980:** The calls work like words. Each one stands for a particular kind of danger, and a listener who has seen nothing knows what to do from the call alone.
- **Michael Owren and Drew Rendall, in the 1990s and 2000s:** A call may work by startling and stirring up the listener, as a scream does, and not by naming anything. The different responses could be learned links between a sound and an escape, without any meaning being passed.
- **Toshitaka Suzuki, a Japanese biologist, in 2016:** Japanese tits join two calls in a fixed order, and listening tits respond properly only to that order. He argues that this is a simple grammar, which goes beyond a mere startle.
### Too many warnings become noise, and a vehicle with no sound is a danger too · *defined*
**Definition:** Alarm fatigue is the dulled response of people who hear warnings too often. A warning protects only if it is rare enough to be believed and present when it is needed.

A warning that sounds all the time stops being a warning. In hospital wards, machines beside each bed beep whenever a number crosses a line. Studies find that most of these alarms need no action. Nurses, hearing hundreds in a shift, begin to tune them out, and now and then a real one is missed. This is called alarm fatigue. The horn on an Indian road has gone the same way. It is used so often that it tells nobody anything. The opposite problem also exists. Electric cars and scooters are nearly silent at low speed, and people who are blind, and anyone stepping off a kerb, rely on hearing a vehicle. So since 2019 the European Union has required new kinds of electric cars to make an artificial sound at low speeds.
- e.g. The reversing beep of a lorry is heard streets away by people it does not concern. Deborah Withington at the University of Leeds in Britain helped develop a 'shh-shh' reversing alarm. It is easy to find when you are near and fades quickly with distance.
- e.g. A car alarm that goes off every time a lorry passes is soon ignored by the whole street.
### Is there such a thing as silence? · *idea*
**Definition:** Silence is usually taken to mean no sound. In practice, a hearing person has never been found to experience a complete absence of sound.

The opposite of noise seems to be silence. But try to find it. In 1951 the American composer John Cage went into a special room at Harvard University. Its walls had deep foam wedges, so that no sound came in and nothing echoed. He expected to hear nothing. He heard two sounds, one high and one low. The engineer told him that the low one was his blood moving and the high one was his nervous system. Some hearing scientists think the high sound was more likely a faint tinnitus, which many people have. Cage concluded that silence does not exist for a living listener. Indian thinkers had examined the same inner sound long before, as something to attend to and not as a fault.
- e.g. Press your palms gently over your ears in a quiet room. You will hear a low rumble, made partly by the tiny shaking of the muscles in your own hands, arms and head.
- e.g. The quietest rooms ever built measure below zero decibels. Zero on the scale is only the faintest sound a young human ear can hear, not the absence of sound.
- **John Cage, whose piece 4'33" was first performed in 1952:** In the piece the performer plays nothing for 4 minutes and 33 seconds. The music is whatever the audience hears in that time: rain, coughs, their own breath. For Cage, what we call silence is only the sounds we did not intend.
- **Chandogya Upanishad, chapter 3, section 13, verse 8:** When a person covers the ears, they hear a sound like the rumbling of a blazing fire. The text takes this as a sign of the light and warmth that is within the body and beyond the sky alike.
- **Svatmarama, in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika, chapter 4, about the 15th century CE:** The chapter teaches nada, attention to inner sound. The listener closes the ears and attends to finer and finer sounds within, until the mind becomes still. Here, quiet is reached by listening more closely, not by having nothing to hear.
- **Physicists:** Sound needs a material to travel in. In the near-emptiness of space between the planets there is almost nothing to carry it, so there is no sound there in the ordinary sense. Wherever there is air and anything moving, there is some sound.

## Mostly agreed
- People whose work is very loud lose their hearing in a pattern that matches the loudness and the years. *How we know:* In 1886 the Scottish doctor Thomas Barr tested 100 men who hammered rivets inside ship boilers in Glasgow and found that hardly any had normal hearing. He compared them with men in quieter jobs, who heard far better. The same pattern has since been measured in weavers, miners, soldiers and musicians.
- Hearing loss is very common, and a large share of it could be prevented. *How we know:* The World Health Organization published a World Report on Hearing in 2021. It estimates that more than 400 million people have hearing loss serious enough to need care. It names loud sound, ear infections and some medicines as leading causes that can be prevented.
- People who are deaf can perceive rhythm and loud sound through the skin, the bones and the chest. *How we know:* Sound is vibration, and the body has touch sensors that respond to vibration. The Scottish percussionist Evelyn Glennie, who has been profoundly deaf since childhood, performs barefoot to feel the music through the floor. In 2001 Dean Shibata scanned the brains of deaf people. The part of the brain that usually handles hearing became active when their hands felt vibration.

## How we found out
**Bernardino Ramazzini, Alfonso Corti, Thomas Barr, Johann Habermann, Douglas Cotanche, Jeffrey Corwin, Brenda Ryals, Edwin Rubel, 1700 to 1988.** For centuries people said that ears get used to noise. It fitted what they felt. A new worker in a smithy suffered for some weeks, and then the din stopped troubling him. In 1700 the Italian doctor Bernardino Ramazzini visited the coppersmiths of Venice, who hammered metal all day in one quarter of the city. He wrote that they first grew hard of hearing and, if they lived long in the trade, became wholly deaf. So getting used to the noise was the beginning of losing the hearing. In 1851 the Italian anatomist Alfonso Corti described, under his microscope, the rows of hair cells in the cochlea. In 1886 Thomas Barr in Glasgow measured the hearing of 100 boilermakers and compared them with men in quieter work. Almost none of the boilermakers heard normally. In 1890 the Austrian doctor Johann Habermann examined, after death, the inner ear of a metal-worker. In the part of the cochlea that senses high pitches, the hair cells were gone. A century later, in 1987 and 1988, Douglas Cotanche, Jeffrey Corwin, Brenda Ryals and Edwin Rubel found that birds grow such cells back. That showed the loss is not a law for all ears, and opened the search for a way to mend ours.  
*What people believed before:* Ears toughen with use, so people in loud work are in no danger once they stop minding it. It was believable because the discomfort really does fade. It was shown wrong by measuring the hearing of such workers and by looking at their hair cells.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The ears get used to noise, so people who live or work in loud places are not harmed by it.** (A common belief among people in loud trades and loud neighbourhoods, and the usual view before the late 1800s; *rejected*)  
  For: It matches experience. After some weeks, a new worker stops being bothered by the machines, and a family stops noticing the trains.  
  Against: Thomas Barr's tests in 1886 showed that what feels like getting used to noise is partly the loss of the hair cells that sensed it. Many studies since have agreed. The body's stress response to night noise also continues after the person has stopped noticing.
- **A person's own taste decides the harm: sound you enjoy does not damage the ear the way unwanted noise does.** (Many music lovers and musicians. A few researchers have also asked whether the stress of unwanted sound adds to its damage.; *minority*)  
  For: Unwanted noise raises stress chemicals in the body and enjoyed music does not, so the whole-body harm really does differ. Some animal studies suggest that stress can change how vulnerable the ear is.  
  Against: Hair cells respond to the energy of the vibration. Surveys of orchestra players and rock musicians find more hearing loss and tinnitus than in other people of the same age. A large German study of insurance records in 2014 found that professional musicians were nearly four times as likely to have hearing loss from noise.
- **Quiet should be treated as a right that belongs to everyone, like clean air, and not as a matter of manners.** (Julia Barnett Rice, who founded the Society for the Suppression of Unnecessary Noise in New York in 1906. The Supreme Court of India held a similar view in its noise judgment of 2005; *debated*)  
  For: The Supreme Court held that the right to life in the Constitution includes the right to live in peace and to sleep. It also held that nobody can claim a right to force sound on unwilling listeners. Noise falls hardest on those who cannot move away: the poor, the sick, babies.  
  Against: A right to quiet can clash with freedoms of speech, worship and assembly, which the Constitution also protects. Critics add that in practice such rights are enforced for well-off neighbourhoods and against street life.

## Roots
You have measured the sounds around you and followed them into the ear. Now we ask what it means that sound cannot be kept out, and what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You measured the street's sound from inside your own room, with the window shut. A cracker burst three lanes away bends hair cells inside your head. Your eyelids can shut out light almost completely. Nothing on your body can do that for sound, and even earplugs only lower it. So a sound made by one being becomes a change inside the bodies of all who are near. In sound, nobody's doing stays inside their own skin.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think 'my sound is my own business' and 'my body ends at my skin'. Both ideas treat each person as sealed off from the others. Sound shows that the air we stand in is one shared thing, and that what one being does to it reaches inside the rest.  
*Evidence:* In your noise map, the meter inside the home will pick up sources you cannot see: a horn, a neighbour's television, a far loudspeaker. The readings should fall with distance and walls, and will not reach zero anywhere.

- *who am i:* Your ears were open before you were born, and they stay open while you sleep. All your life the world has been arriving in you as sound, without asking. Your own voice and footsteps arrive in others in the same way. You are a listener who cannot stop listening, and a maker of sound that others cannot refuse.
- *what is life:* A langur's bark saves a deer. A bird sings higher to be heard above traffic. Plants make defence chemicals when they sense the chewing of a caterpillar. Living beings are joined through the air, the ground and the water by vibration. To be alive is to be within reach of the others.
- *what is this world:* A sound is the air itself moving, and the air does not belong to anyone. Each sound spreads until it fades into the small random motion of the air, which is warmth. Even the quietest room holds that motion. The world is never still, and what we call quiet is a level of motion that suits us.

This lesson began with a question about a nuisance and found something larger under it. The harm of noise and the help of a warning are possible for the same reason. Bodies are open to each other through the air, and that openness cannot be switched off. One person's celebration lands in another's hair cells. One monkey's fear becomes a deer's leap. One view in the Indian tradition of Advaita Vedanta holds that consciousness is the ground from which every listener and every sound arise. On that view, hearing is that one ground reaching itself across what looks like a gap. Most hearing scientists describe it more simply, as pressure waves and nerve signals, and their view is given below. Both descriptions leave you with the same practical fact: your sound is never only yours.

- **Kena Upanishad, part 1, verse 2:** The sage speaks of 'the Ear of the ear': that by which the ear hears, which the ear itself cannot hear. (The lesson followed sound from the air to the eardrum, to the hair cells and to the nerve. Science can trace every step of that chain and still cannot say how a nerve signal becomes the experience of hearing a drum. The verse points at that same gap. Science has not confirmed the sage's answer, and it has not closed the question either.)
- **Tiruvalluvar, Tirukkural, chapter 42, 'Listening', verses 411 and 418:** Verse 411 says that the wealth gained through the ear is the chief of all wealth. Verse 418 says that an ear which has not been opened by careful listening is deaf, even though it hears. (The Tamil poet separates hearing from listening, as this lesson separates a decibel number from what a sound means to someone. Protecting hair cells keeps the hearing. What a person does with it is the other half.)
- **Jalaluddin Rumi, Masnavi, book 1, the opening lines known as the Song of the Reed, 13th century CE:** The poem begins with the words 'Listen to the reed flute, how it tells a tale, complaining of separations'. The reed was cut from the reed bed, and its sound is its longing for where it came from. (Rumi makes listening the first act of understanding another being. The science is plainer: a flute's sound is air vibrating. The link is only this, that sound is how beings who seem separate first reach each other.)
- Science (settled): Sound energy does not vanish. It spreads out and ends as a very slight warming of the air and of whatever it strikes. This follows from a basic rule of physics: energy is never lost, only moved or changed.
- Science (open): How the activity of hair cells and nerve cells becomes the felt experience of a sound is not known. The philosopher David Chalmers named this the hard problem in 1995.
- Other view, Most hearing scientists and neuroscientists, who hold that hearing is nothing more than the body's physical activity: Hearing is what ears and brains do. A sound wave is a pressure change, the ear turns it into nerve signals, and the brain's activity is the hearing. On this view there is no deeper joining of beings in sound. There are separate bodies that affect each other through the air, and that is enough reason to be considerate.
- Practice, Far, near, within (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. For one minute, listen for the farthest sound you can find. Do not name it. Just notice it. Now, for one minute, listen for the nearest sound outside your body. Now cup your hands gently over your ears and listen, for one minute, to the sound inside. Take your hands away slowly. Notice that all three were there the whole time.
- Ask: When you covered your ears, the outside went quiet and something else was heard. Who was making that sound, and who was hearing it?
- Ask: The sound you make by walking, talking and playing goes into other beings. Which beings near your home hear you most?
- Ask: If no sound can be kept fully in or fully out, where exactly does your home end?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The same drumbeat is music to the drummer and noise to the neighbour. Is the noise in the air, in the neighbour, or somewhere else? (hint: Ask what a meter would find in each room, and what it could never find.)
- *deep:* If you have stopped noticing a sound, are you still hearing it? (hint: Think about what your sleeping body does when a lorry passes, and about who or what 'you' means in the question.)
- *deep:* A bird can regrow her hair cells and you cannot. Does that make a bird's ear better than yours, or only different? (hint: Think about what each animal's ear has to do in a lifetime, and what 'better' would be measured by.)
- *deep:* In a perfectly quiet room you would hear your own body. Is your body then a source of noise, or is that sound part of you? (hint: Go back to what makes a sound noise, and ask if you can be an unwilling listener to yourself.)
- *big:* A wedding band gives joy to two hundred guests and keeps one sick neighbour awake. How should a street decide? (hint: Try counting people, then try asking who can move away and who cannot. See if the two methods agree.)
- *big:* Whales, birds and dogs did not agree to our noise and cannot complain. Who should speak for them when rules are made? (hint: Think about how rules are made now, and whose ears the meter is built to copy.)
- *big:* A horn that is always blowing warns nobody. Can you think of other things in life that lose their meaning when they are used too much? (hint: Think of words, of praise, of scolding, of alarms on a phone.)
- *big:* If scientists learn to regrow human hair cells, should every deaf person be expected to have their hearing 'fixed'? (hint: Listen to what Deaf people who sign say about their own lives before you answer.)
- *deep:* You cannot close your ears as you close your eyes. Why might bodies have been built with ears that never shut? (hint: Think about what could happen to a sleeping animal, and what hearing can do that sight cannot.)

## Story: The Number on Ammini's Phone
*A made-up story set in a village near Palakkad in Kerala. The people are imagined. The chenda is a real drum of Kerala, played with sticks, and groups of chenda players are very loud at close range. The decibel numbers in the story are invented but realistic: sound falls by about 6 decibels each time the distance doubles in the open. That deaf people feel drums through the floor and the body is real.*

Ammini, who was ten, had a school project and a borrowed phone. The project was called 'The Sounds of My Village'. The phone had an app that showed a number, and the number was decibels.

By four o'clock she had measured the fan, which was 48, the mixer-grinder, which was 88, and the bus horn at the junction, which was 101 and made her jump. Then she walked to the shed behind the coconut trees, where her uncle Raman and five others practised the chenda every evening before the festival season.

She stood one metre from her uncle and held up the phone. The sticks came down. The number went to 104 and stayed there.

When they stopped to tighten the ropes, she showed him. 'Maama, your chenda is louder than the bus.'

Raman laughed. 'The bus is noise. This is chenda. Do not put them on the same page.'

'The phone puts them on the same page,' said Ammini.

'Then the phone knows nothing about sound,' said Raman. But he was rubbing his left ear as he said it. 'What did you say just now, before the bus?'

'I said it is louder than the bus. Maama, I already said it twice.'

He did not answer that. At night, Ammini knew, he heard crickets that were not there. He had told her once, as if it were a joke.

Her friend Jomol came round the shed just then. Jomol was eleven and had been deaf since she was born. She and Ammini talked with their hands, in signs they had half learned at school and half made up. Jomol pointed at the drummers and signed: again?

The drummers began. Jomol walked straight up to the wooden wall of the shed and put both palms flat on it. Then she took off her chappals and stood barefoot on the packed earth. Her face opened into a huge smile. She put Ammini's hand on the wall beside hers. The planks were jumping.

Good, Jomol signed. Best one. Better than the grinder. The grinder is only buzzing. This one has steps.

Ammini held the phone against the wall. It still said 104. The phone did not know about steps either.

On the way home she passed Shanta chechi's house. Shanta was a nurse who worked nights at the hospital in town and slept in the day. Her window was shut and a blanket was nailed over it, and behind the blanket the baby was crying. Ammini held up the phone. 71. Not very much. It was plainly enough.

That night she wrote three lines in her project book. To Maama it is his life. To Jomol it is the best touch in the village. To Shanta chechi's baby, the drumming is the thing that will not let her sleep. Under them she wrote: all three are 104 at the shed. Then she wrote a fourth line, slowly, because she did not like it. Maama loves it, and his ear is ringing anyway.

That was the part she could not get past. She had thought that noise was sound you did not want. But her uncle wanted every beat, and his ear had not asked what he wanted.

The next evening she did not tell anyone to stop. She walked away from the shed with the phone, counting steps. At two metres, 98. At four metres, 92. At eight metres, 86. At the far end of the grove, behind the old cattle shed wall, the phone showed 74, and at Shanta chechi's window she could hardly find the drums at all.

She drew it as a map and gave it to her uncle. He looked at it for a long time. 'So we carry six chendas to the far end every evening because of a phone.'

'Because of a baby,' said Ammini. 'And these are because of your ear.' She held out a small packet of foam earplugs from the medical shop.

Raman turned the packet over. 'With these I will hear my own chenda like a man under water.'

'Jomol does not hear any of it at all,' said Ammini, 'and she says it has steps.'

He put one plug in, then the other, and played a short pattern with his eyes shut. When he opened them he looked surprised. 'It is still in the arms,' he said.

Ammini wrote one more line in her book. The phone can say how much. It cannot say what it is. Then she sat and wondered who could.

**For the parent:** The meter gives one number for a sound, and every listener gives a different meaning. Yet the ear's hair cells take the number and not the meaning. A loved sound and a hated sound of the same strength do the same work on them.

- Ask: Jomol felt the chenda with her hands and feet. Was she hearing the drums? What would you need to decide what 'hearing' means?
- Ask: Raman's ear rang though he loved the sound. Does that change what you think noise is?
- Ask: Ammini moved the drummers and did not stop them. Can you think of another loud sound in your street where moving it would help more than banning it?

## Games
### g1 · Quiet Street: a team card game about bringing a street's sound down (25 min)
- **rules:** This is a team game. All the players win together or lose together. You can play alone, with a parent or with friends.; Choose a zone card and put it above the street. For one or two players, choose Commercial. For three or four, choose Residential. Use an easier zone if the street keeps winning against you and a harder one if you win every time.; Shuffle the 12 sound cards and put them in a pile. Shuffle the 20 fix cards and deal four to each player. Put the rest in a pile for drawing.; Each round has two steps. First, turn the top sound card over and put it in the next empty box on the street. Second, the players take turns. Each plays one fix card from their hand on any sound in the street and puts it under that card. Then each draws a new card to bring their hand back to four. A player playing alone may play two fix cards on their turn. Then the next round starts.; A fix card changes the level of the sound it is placed under: take that many decibels off. A 'Stop it for now' card takes the sound out of the street altogether. Several fix cards can go on one sound. You can also choose not to play a card.; After the fifth sound has joined the street and the players have played their cards for that round, the game ends. Work out the street's level with the adding table card. Start with the loudest sound after its fixes and add each next one, loudest to quietest.; If the street's level is the same as or lower than the zone card's number, the team wins. ### e1 · A noise map of my home and street: which places are loudest, and are they within the limit? (45 min, 2 days)
- **question:** How loud is each place in and around your home, and which of them are above India's limit for homes?
- **predict:** Before you measure each place, write a guess in decibels and say why you think so. Is the kitchen with the mixer-grinder louder than the street? Is the bedroom window quieter when it is shut?
- **fair test:** Only the place or the time changes. The same phone is held the same way each time: at arm's length, at chest height, with the microphone end towards the sound. Each reading is the average over 30 seconds, taken twice. If the two readings differ by more than 5 decibels, a passing horn probably spoiled one, so take a third and use the middle one. For the distance readings, the sound source, its volume and the room stay the same, and only your distance changes.
- **safety:** Make no loud sound near anyone's ears for this test. Keep the played sound at the volume of ordinary talk, and never use crackers or horns. A grown-up runs the mixer-grinder and takes the late-night reading.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all eight readings. Draw a rough map of your home and street, and write each middle reading on the place where you took it. Colour the street readings above India's limit for homes red. Then write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Place and time | My guess (dB) | Reading 1 (dB) | Reading 2 (dB) | Middle reading (dB) | India's limit for homes (dB) | How much I wanted this sound (1 to 5) | Backup: steps away I can still hear a ticking clock
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up the meter, write all eight guesses, then measure the bedroom, the kitchen, the distance steps and the street by day. (40 min); day 2: At about a quarter past ten at night, a grown-up takes the street reading from the same spot, and you finish the table. (5 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pick a free sound-meter app and open it. Hold the phone at arm's length, at chest height, with the microphone end (the bottom edge on most phones) towards the sound. Practise in the room where you are. Watch the number for 30 seconds on a timer and write the average if the app shows one. If it shows only a jumping number, write the number it shows most often.  
  *Parent says:* A sound meter turns the shaking of air into a number of decibels. Hold the phone out, away from your body, and watch it for half a minute. What do you see the number do?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the phone out at arm's length. Watch the number for 30 seconds. Write the average.
  - Note: Phone apps can differ from one another by several decibels, so this test compares one place with another. It does not prove that a law was broken. If you cannot get an app, use the backup. In every place, count the big steps you can walk away from a ticking clock while still hearing it. Write the number in the last column of the table. Examples of apps are the NIOSH Sound Level Meter on iPhone, or any free decibel app on Android. The farther you can hear it, the quieter the place.

Write all the numbers.

  - Note: The rules measure outside homes, so only the street readings can be compared with them. The other places are for comparing one place with another. Day means 6 in the morning to 10 at night.

### e2 · Which cuts a steady sound the most: more distance, a quieter source or a barrier? (30 min)
- **question:** The three ways to protect the ear are a quieter source, more distance or a barrier, and less time. Which of the first two cuts a steady sound the most, and which material makes the best barrier?

- **safety:** Keep the sound at the volume of ordinary talk. Do not play anything loud near anyone's ears.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out the drop in each row by taking the middle reading from the starting reading. Put the changes in order from the biggest drop to the smallest. Then write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.

  - Ask: Which change do you think will cut the sound the most, and why? (good: Any guess with a reason, such as the barrier because it stands in the way, or the distance because the sound spreads out.; An answer that thinks about sound spreading or being blocked.)
  - Note: Do not move the speaker or the phone angle again. If the readings jump about by more than 5 decibels, close the door, switch off the fan and try again. - **s3 (10 min)**

  *Child mode:* Point to each tap with your eyes closed.
  - Ask: Which places were hardest to point to? (good: Straight ahead, straight behind or above, where the sound reaches both ears at the same time.; Any honest answer. Sounds on the side are usually easier.)
- **a3s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now ask your child to rest one palm gently over one ear. Do the same eight taps, in a different order. Count the ticks again.  
  *Parent says:* Now one ear is covered. Is it as easy? Which places are hardest now?  
  *Child mode:* Cover one ear with a palm. Point to each tap again.
  - Ask: Why is it harder to find a sound with one ear covered? (good: A sound reaches one ear a tiny bit sooner and louder than the other. The brain uses that to tell where it is.; With one ear, the brain has no difference to compare.)
  - Note: A sound from far to one side reaches the nearer ear up to about half a thousandth of a second before the other. The brain compares the two. A siren that sweeps up and down gives more to compare than one steady note.
- **a3s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap places so that your child taps and you point. Then talk about it: why do a train's horn, an ambulance siren and a smoke alarm all sound so different from a steady hum?  
  *Parent says:* A good warning has to be heard and also found. What kind of sound is easy to find?  
  *Child mode:* Swap with your helper and tap for them. Talk about warnings.
  - Think: Why might a siren sweep up and down rather than stay on one note?

### p1 · My week of listening: how many hours, how loud? (60 min, 7 days)

  - Think: Which is easier to change for your ears, the volume or the hours?

## Worksheets
### Decibels, the ear and India's rules (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these can a sound-level meter measure?  
   Options: Whether a sound is noise / How strong a sound is / Who wants the sound  
   **Answer:** How strong a sound is. *Why:* A meter measures strength in decibels. Whether a sound is noise is a judgement by the listener, so no meter can say it.
2. (fill) A whisper is about 30 decibels and ordinary talk about 60. Talk carries about ____ times the sound energy of a whisper.  
   **Answer:** ['1000', '1,000']. *Why:* The gap is 30 decibels. Every 10 decibels is 10 times the energy, so 30 decibels is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.
3. (truefalse) Ten horns of 90 decibels each, sounding together, make roughly 100 decibels.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Ten times the energy adds 10 decibels. Doubling adds only about 3, so decibels do not add like ordinary numbers.
4. (match) Match each part of the ear to what it does.  
   **Answer:** eardrum → Shakes when sound reaches it; three tiny bones → Pass the shaking inwards; cochlea → Holds the hair cells in liquid; hair cells → Send a signal when their tufts bend. *Why:* Sound is passed inwards from the eardrum, along the bones, into the liquid of the cochlea, and the hair cells turn the movement into signals.
5. (order) Put the journey of a sound into the ear in order, first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['The air shakes', 'The eardrum shakes', 'Three tiny bones pass the shaking on', 'The liquid in the cochlea moves', 'Hair cell tufts bend', 'A nerve signal goes to the brain']. *Why:* Each step hands the shaking to the next, and the hair cells are the last place where shaking becomes a signal.
6. (sort) Sort each harm by where it happens.  
   **Answer:** a ringing heard after a loud wedding → Harm to the ear; a raised heartbeat while asleep as a lorry passes → Harm beyond the ear; losing high pitches around 4,000 hertz → Harm to the ear; falling behind in reading beside a noisy railway → Harm beyond the ear; higher stress chemicals in whales → Harm beyond the ear. *Why:* Noise reaches the ear first, but it also raises the body's stress, breaks sleep and slows learning, and it affects other animals too.
7. (mcq) Which kind of area has the lowest noise limits in India's rules?  
   Options: Industrial area / Commercial area / Silence zone  
   **Answer:** Silence zone. *Why:* A silence zone, the area within 100 metres of a hospital, school or court, has 50 decibels by day and 40 at night. Industrial areas are allowed the most.
8. (count) A residential area's limit is 55 decibels by day and 45 at night. How many decibels lower is the night limit?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* 55 − 45 = 10. Ten decibels means one tenth of the sound energy, so the night limit is much stricter than it looks.
9. (truefalse) A ball of cotton wool in the ear cuts sound as much as a well-fitted foam earplug.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Cotton wool cuts about 5 to 7 decibels. A well-fitted foam earplug cuts about 15 to 30.
10. (mcq) Which of these protects the ear by changing the time?  
   Options: Walking away from the speaker / Leaving the function after an hour / Turning the volume down  
   **Answer:** Leaving the function after an hour. *Why:* Staying for less time lowers the dose. Walking away changes the path, and turning the volume down changes the source.
11. (write) Your hearing feels normal the morning after a loud function. Does that prove no harm was done? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Not proven. In 2009, mice that passed hearing tests had lost up to half the links between hair cells and nerve. Whether this happens in people is still being debated, so a normal test does not prove there was no harm.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a normal test and no harm are different things, and that scientists still argue about hidden hearing loss in people.
12. (mcq) Why is a smoke alarm made shrill and unpleasant on purpose?  
   Options: So nobody can sleep through it / It costs less to make / Loud sounds use less battery  
   **Answer:** So nobody can sleep through it. *Why:* A warning has to be noticed over everything, even in sleep. A pleasant sound is easier to ignore.
13. (odd) Which is the odd one out and why? Broken sleep, raised blood pressure, tinnitus, stress chemicals in whales.  
   **Answer:** Tinnitus, because it is a harm to the ear. The other three are harms beyond the ear.. *Why:* Noise acts on the whole body, and on other animals, as well as on the ear.
14. (write) A friend reads 82 decibels on a phone and says, 'My neighbour is breaking the law.' What do you say?  
   **Answer:** Ask where and when it was measured, for how long, and which kind of area it is. Phone apps can be off by several decibels. The limits apply outside homes, and the zone and the time of day change the limit.. *Why:* A good answer treats the number as a pointer, not proof, and asks about the place, the time and the kind of area.
15. (perspective) A band with a loudspeaker marches past a hospital at noon.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer looks for a way that works for both, such as a different road, a quieter band near the hospital, or a shorter time.. *Why:* Both views are real. The rules and the doctor protect people who cannot move away. The procession shows that a rule must also respect what people value.
16. (draw) Draw a hair cell before and after very loud sound. Show the tuft of hairs.  
   **Answer:** Two drawings of a cell with a tuft. The first has a neat, upright tuft. The second has a bent, flattened or broken tuft, or no cell at all.. *Why:* Loud sound bends the tuft too far, and with enough sound the cell dies and is not replaced.
### Distance, dose and other animals (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (count) A steady sound reads 96 decibels at 1 metre in the open. What does it read at 8 metres?  
   **Answer:** 78. *Why:* From 1 metre to 8 metres is three doublings (1, 2, 4, 8). Each doubling takes off about 6 decibels, so 96 − 18 = 78.
2. (fill) A child's safe listening is 40 hours a week at 75 decibels. At 81 decibels the safe time is ____ hours.  
   **Answer:** ['10']. *Why:* 81 is 6 decibels above 75, which is two steps of 3. Each step halves the time, so 40 → 20 → 10.
3. (mcq) A hall of 40 children talking reads 80 decibels. What would 20 children talking the same way read?  
   Options: 40 decibels / About 77 decibels / 60 decibels  
   **Answer:** About 77 decibels. *Why:* Halving the number of equal sources takes off about 3 decibels, not half the number.
4. (truefalse) Noise-cancelling headphones work best on a sudden sharp horn.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They work best on steady, low sounds, such as an aeroplane's engine drone. A sudden or high sound changes too fast to cancel.
5. (match) Match each living being to what scientists found.  
   **Answer:** great tit → Sings at a higher pitch in noisy cities; right whale → Had fewer stress chemicals when ship noise fell; thale cress plant → Made more defence chemicals after chewing vibrations were played; street dog → Hides and shakes on cracker nights. *Why:* Noise reaches many kinds of living beings, and each responds in a way that fits the body and the life.
6. (sort) Sort each way of protecting the ears by the lever it uses.  
   **Answer:** choosing a quieter mixer-grinder → The source; sitting at the back of the hall → The path; leaving after one hour → The time; shutting the window → The path; turning the volume down → The source; taking a quiet break after loud sound → The time. *Why:* Source is where the sound is made, path is what lies between, and time is how long you stay. Each lowers the dose.
7. (mcq) After a loud night you hear a faint ringing. What does this most likely show?  
   Options: The ear is now cleaner / The sound was more than the ear could take / Hearing has improved  
   **Answer:** The sound was more than the ear could take. *Why:* Ringing and muffled hearing are signs that the sound was too much. If they come again and again, the loss may stay.
8. (order) Put the steps of a fair noise test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Write a guess for each place', 'Play the same steady sound at the same volume', 'Hold the phone at the same height and distance', 'Read the 30-second average twice', 'Compare the readings']. *Why:* The guess comes first, so the test can surprise you. Only one thing changes at a time, and each reading is repeated because one try can be a fluke.
9. (write) Plants may respond to the vibration of a chewing caterpillar. What do we still not know about plants and our noise?  
   **Answer:** Appel and Cocroft found in 2014 that plants responded to vibrations of chewing. Very few studies have looked at what human noise does to plants, so it is an open question.. *Why:* A strong answer names the finding and says that almost all noise research has been on people, birds and sea mammals.
10. (mcq) Which is true of the 15 days in India's noise rules?  
   Options: Loudspeakers may be used all night on them / Crackers have no limit on them / A state may relax night quiet hours to midnight  
   **Answer:** A state may relax night quiet hours to midnight. *Why:* A state may allow loudspeakers until midnight on up to 15 days in a year, for festivals. The rest of the rules still apply.
11. (odd) Which is the odd one out: smoke alarm, train horn, ambulance siren, ticking clock?  
   **Answer:** The ticking clock, because it is not made to warn.. *Why:* The others are made loud on purpose, to stand out and to carry a meaning that people have learned.
12. (draw) Draw two opposite waves and the line they make when added together.  
   **Answer:** One wave that rises and falls, another that falls where the first rises, and a flat line for their sum.. *Why:* Noise-cancelling headphones play the opposite of the incoming sound, so that squeezing and stretching of the air add to nothing.
13. (perspective) Noise limits are set for human ears and measured where people live.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says that the human limits are a start. Animals could be added by measuring the pitches they hear and the places they live. This is not done yet.. *Why:* Both views are real. Neither is wrong, and the first has been the way rules have been written so far.
14. (truefalse) A chicken's ear can grow new hair cells after loud sound, and a person's ear cannot.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Researchers found this in birds in 1987 and 1988. Why mammals cannot is still being worked out.
15. (fill) A sound with 100 times the energy of another is ____ decibels higher.  
   **Answer:** ['20']. *Why:* Every factor of 10 in energy is 10 decibels. 100 is 10 × 10, so it is 20 decibels.
16. (mcq) Why can a silent electric scooter be a danger?  
   Options: It uses more power / People who cannot see it rely on hearing it / It runs slower than other scooters  
   **Answer:** People who cannot see it rely on hearing it. *Why:* People who are blind, and anyone stepping off a kerb, use hearing to know a vehicle is near. The European Union has required new electric cars to make a sound at low speeds since 2019.
### Reasoning with decibels and evidence (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (count) A cracker reads 125 decibels at 4 metres. In the open, how many decibels would it read at 2 metres?  
   **Answer:** 131. *Why:* Halving the distance adds about 6 decibels, so 125 + 6 = 131. At 1 metre it would be close to 137.
2. (mcq) A road reads 70 decibels. Half of the cars are taken off. What is the new level?  
   Options: About 35 decibels / About 67 decibels / About 60 decibels  
   **Answer:** About 67 decibels. *Why:* Half the sources takes off only about 3 decibels. The lower numbers come only when most of the cars are gone.
3. (truefalse) A sound too quiet to hurt the ear cannot harm health, even if it goes on night after night.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In 2018 the World Health Organization found strong evidence that long-lasting road noise at night raises the risk of heart disease. This happens at levels far below those that damage hearing.
4. (mcq) Cotton wool cuts about 6 decibels. About how many times less sound energy reaches the ear?  
   Options: 2 times / About 4 times / 6 times  
   **Answer:** About 4 times. *Why:* 3 decibels is half the energy and 6 decibels is half again, so about one quarter, which is 4 times less.
5. (mcq) A foam earplug cuts about 25 decibels. About how many times less energy reaches the ear?  
   Options: 25 times / Roughly 300 times / 2,500 times  
   **Answer:** Roughly 300 times. *Why:* Every 10 decibels is 10 times less, so 20 decibels is 100 times less and 5 more decibels is about 3 times again. That makes close to 300.
6. (write) Scientists still disagree about hidden hearing loss in people. What evidence would you try to collect to settle it?  
   **Answer:** A test that can see the links between hair cells and nerve in living people. Or a large study of young people with a lot of noise in their past and with little, using careful tests of hearing speech in noise.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the evidence in mice is clear, and that people need a test that can detect what a normal test misses.
7. (sort) Sort each statement by whether India's noise rules contain it.  
   **Answer:** Loudspeakers need written permission → It is in the rules; A night-shift worker has quiet hours by day → It is not in the rules; A silence zone is within 100 metres of a hospital, school or court → It is in the rules; The limits take account of what dogs and birds hear → It is not in the rules; Limits are given in dB(A) → It is in the rules. *Why:* The rules set limits for areas and times and set conditions for loudspeakers. They do not cover shift workers by day or the hearing of other animals, which is part of the debate about whose sound counts.
8. (mcq) Which result best shows that noise, and not something else, slowed the reading of children beside a railway?  
   Options: Teachers said so / One school showed a lag / The lag ended when the noise was cut  
   **Answer:** The lag ended when the noise was cut. *Why:* If removing the noise removes the lag, noise was the cause. A single school or a teacher's opinion cannot rule out other causes.
9. (write) A friend says, 'I am used to the trains, so they cannot harm me.' Give two reasons this is wrong.  
   **Answer:** Hair cells may be lost, which makes the noise seem less. Also the body's stress response and the heart rate during sleep still respond to each train, though you may not wake.. *Why:* A good answer gives the hair-cell reason and the sleeping-body reason.
10. (perspective) A festival loudspeaker plays until midnight on the last night.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer looks for a middle way, such as turning the speakers away from homes, lowering the volume, ending earlier or moving the stage.. *Why:* Both are real. The rules allow a few relaxed days, and the child's health and the community's joy are both worth protecting.
11. (mcq) A hospital ward has many alarms, and most do not need action. What is the best fix?  
   Options: Make every alarm louder / Rank alarms so only the urgent ones sound loudly / Remove all alarms  
   **Answer:** Rank alarms so only the urgent ones sound loudly. *Why:* A warning that sounds too often is ignored. Ranking keeps the urgent ones rare enough to be believed.
12. (fill) A classroom reads 78 decibels by day. A silence zone's limit by day is 50. The gap is ____ decibels.  
   **Answer:** ['28']. *Why:* 78 − 50 = 28. That is a very big gap, since every 10 decibels is 10 times the energy.
13. (match) Match each warning to what it tells us.  
   **Answer:** pressure cooker whistle → The steam has reached full pressure; train horn → Keep off the track; ambulance siren → Move aside; coastal siren tower → A cyclone or a tsunami is coming. *Why:* A warning works because people have learned what it means. A sound with no agreed meaning warns nobody.
14. (write) The sound of your own heartbeat in a quiet room: is it noise? Argue it in two sentences for each side.  
   **Answer:** Noise: it is unwanted when you are trying to sleep. Not noise: it is your own body and a sign of life, and you did not choose to hear it but it is yours.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that noise is about the listener, and a meter would give a single number for both views.
15. (truefalse) Plants have no ears, so noise and vibration cannot matter to them.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In 2014 Appel and Cocroft showed that plants made more defence chemicals when played the vibrations of a caterpillar chewing. They sense vibration through their leaves.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Two neighbours hear the same drumbeat. One calls it music and the other calls it noise. What does this show?  
   Options: The meter must be broken / Noise depends on the listener / Music is always quieter than noise  
   **Answer:** Noise depends on the listener. *Why:* A meter reads the same for both, so the difference is in the listeners. Noise is a judgement, which is why people disagree about it.
2. (mcq) One auto horn reads 90 decibels at your window. About what do two such horns read together?  
   Options: 93 decibels / 180 decibels / 100 decibels  
   **Answer:** 93 decibels. *Why:* Twice the sound energy adds about 3 decibels. Choosing 180 adds the numbers like ordinary numbers, which decibels do not do.
3. (mcq) A phone reads 80 decibels at 1 metre from a steady sound in the open. About what at 4 metres?  
   Options: About 76 decibels / About 68 decibels / About 40 decibels  
   **Answer:** About 68 decibels. *Why:* 4 metres is two doublings of distance, and each takes off about 6 decibels, so 80 − 12 = 68.
4. (mcq) Why does hearing lost to loud sound not come back in people?  
   Options: The nerve to the brain is cut / Hair cells that die are not replaced / The eardrum wears out  
   **Answer:** Hair cells that die are not replaced. *Why:* Humans are born with all the hair cells they will ever have. Birds and fishes can grow new ones, and people cannot.
5. (mcq) A cracker bang can harm the ear even though the ear has a guard muscle. Why?  
   Options: The guard works only when asleep / Crackers are quieter than horns / The muscle needs a tenth of a second, and the bang is quicker  
   **Answer:** The muscle needs a tenth of a second, and the bang is quicker. *Why:* The acoustic reflex takes about a tenth of a second to tighten. A bang reaches full strength in less than a thousandth of a second.
6. (mcq) After a loud wedding your hearing returns by morning. What may still be true?  
   Options: Some links to the hearing nerve may be lost / All the hair cells have grown back / The ear's guard has been mended  
   **Answer:** Some links to the hearing nerve may be lost. *Why:* In mice, loud sound can destroy up to half of the nerve links while hearing tests pass. Whether this happens in people is still debated.
7. (mcq) A lorry passes at night and you sleep on. Is your body affected?  
   Options: No, nothing happens unless you wake / Only if the lorry is very loud / Yes, heartbeat and blood pressure can rise  
   **Answer:** Yes, heartbeat and blood pressure can rise. *Why:* Hearing never switches off in sleep. The body can react to each passing noise, and years of this are linked with heart disease.
8. (mcq) Great tits sing at a higher pitch in noisy cities. Why does that help?  
   Options: Traffic rumble is low, so a higher song is heard above it / High songs are quieter / It makes them look bigger  
   **Answer:** Traffic rumble is low, so a higher song is heard above it. *Why:* Low rumble covers low songs. A higher song sits above the rumble and carries better.
9. (mcq) In India's 2000 noise rules, loudspeakers may not be used in the open when?  
   Options: Between 6 and 10 in the morning / At any time of the day / Between 10 at night and 6 in the morning, unless allowed  
   **Answer:** Between 10 at night and 6 in the morning, unless allowed. *Why:* The quiet hours are 10 at night to 6 in the morning. A state may relax them to midnight on up to 15 days in a year.
10. (mcq) A cracker reads 120 decibels at 4 metres. A child fires it at 1 metre. What level reaches the child?  
   Options: Roughly 132 decibels / Roughly 120 decibels / Roughly 100 decibels  
   **Answer:** Roughly 132 decibels. *Why:* Each halving of distance adds about 6 decibels, and 4 metres to 1 metre is two halvings. So the child gets about 12 more.
11. (mcq) Which best protects your ears at a loud function?  
   Options: Cotton wool, near the speaker / Foam earplugs and sitting far from the speakers / Sitting beside the speaker and enjoying it  
   **Answer:** Foam earplugs and sitting far from the speakers. *Why:* Cotton wool cuts only 5 to 7 decibels. Earplugs cut 15 to 30, and distance cuts it more. Close to the speaker the sound is strongest, and enjoying it does not protect hair cells.
12. (mcq) With earphones, which of the three levers are left to you?  
   Options: Only volume and time / Distance and barrier / All three, as usual  
   **Answer:** Only volume and time. *Why:* The earphone sits inside the ear, so there is no distance or barrier. Volume and hours are what you can change.
13. (mcq) A child's weekly allowance is 40 hours at 75 decibels. What is the safe time at 84 decibels?  
   Options: 20 hours / 13 hours / 5 hours  
   **Answer:** 5 hours. *Why:* 84 is 9 decibels above 75, which is three steps of 3. The time halves three times: 40 → 20 → 10 → 5.
14. (mcq) What makes a warning sound work?  
   Options: It stands out, can be located and has a meaning people have learned / It is the highest pitch possible / It lasts as long as it can  
   **Answer:** It stands out, can be located and has a meaning people have learned. *Why:* A warning is noticed because it stands out, found because it can be located, and acted on because people know what it means.
15. (mcq) A hospital ward's machines beep all shift, and most beeps need no action. Why is that a danger?  
   Options: Beeps harm patients' ears / Staff may stop responding, even to real alarms / The machines wear out sooner  
   **Answer:** Staff may stop responding, even to real alarms. *Why:* A warning that sounds too often is tuned out. That is alarm fatigue.

## Applications
- Workers who use road drills, looms or grinders wear ear muffs or foam earplugs. Look for them at a building site, or ask a relative who works in a loud place. Notice what they use and how well it fits.
- The silencer on an auto or a scooter is a metal box on the exhaust. Inside, the gas passes through chambers and holes, and the sound bounces around and loses energy. Look under a parked scooter to find it.
- Phones and earphones now warn you when you listen too loud for too long. This follows a global standard for safe listening devices set by the World Health Organization and the International Telecommunication Union in 2019. Find the setting on a phone with a grown-up.
- Many hospitals and some schools have silence-zone boards on the road outside. They apply the rule that sets lower limits within 100 metres. Look for one, and notice whether the traffic obeys it.
- Cities draw noise maps by measuring many places and times, and use them to decide where to put barriers and where to reroute a road. Your noise map of home and street is a small one.
- Hearing aids and cochlear implants help people whose hair cells cannot do their work. One makes the sound louder, and the other goes round the damaged cells. Many Deaf people use sign language, with or without either.
- Tall solid panels stand beside some flyovers, railway lines and metro viaducts to lower the sound reaching homes. Look for them, and notice which side of the panel is quieter.
- In some coastal villages, sirens on towers warn of cyclones and tsunamis. Alerts from India's tsunami warning centre reach the coastal authorities, who can set them off. Each siren is made loud and made to carry far, because a warning has to reach everyone.

## Spotted around us
- In your kitchen, switch on the mixer-grinder or listen to the pressure cooker's whistle from the next room. Notice how much louder it sounds at arm's length than from two rooms away, and how a closed door changes it.
- On the bus or in an auto, notice how often horns blow and what they were for. Count the horns in one minute and ask how many told anybody something they did not already know.
- Look for a 'No Horn' or 'Silence Zone' board near a hospital or school on your way. Notice if the horns stop, and how far from the board the quiet starts.
- At a festival or a wedding, notice where the loudspeakers are and where the guests stand. Notice who sits near the speakers, and who sits at the back, and which babies and older relatives have been taken outside.
- On a bus or train, watch someone's earphones. Notice if you can hear their music from the next seat. If you can, it is a sign the volume is too high.
- Listen to the birds at dawn and again at the busiest time of day. Notice whether the song is the same, and whether the birds sing before the traffic starts.
- Watch a street dog or a pet on a night with crackers. Notice where the animal goes to hide and what she or he does, and how long it takes to settle afterwards.
- Listen for the pressure cooker's whistle, a train's horn and an ambulance siren on a normal day. Notice that each one makes you do something without a word being said.

## Open questions
- **Why can birds and fishes regrow hair cells while humans and other mammals cannot?** The neighbour cells that become new hair cells in a bird exist in mammals too. Researchers have found some of the genetic switches involved, and switching them on in mice has produced only a few, immature cells. Nobody knows why mammals lost the ability, or what it would cost to restore it.
- **Where does tinnitus come from, and why does it stay in some people and fade in others?** Most evidence points to the brain raising its own sensitivity after the ear is damaged. But people with the same ear damage differ greatly. No test can measure tinnitus from outside, so it is hard to study and to treat.
- **Does loud sound cause hidden damage in people whose hearing tests stay normal?** The loss of nerve connections is clear in mice and other animals. In living people it cannot be seen directly, and the indirect tests give mixed results. Until a reliable test exists, nobody knows how common it is.
- **What do alarm calls mean to the animals who give and hear them?** Playback tests show what listeners do, and cannot show what they understand. Researchers disagree on whether calls name things, pass on feeling, or both, and the answer may differ from species to species.
- **How much are plants and small animals such as insects affected by human noise?** A few studies show that plants and insects respond to vibration. Almost all noise research has been done on people, birds and sea mammals, so for most living beings the question has hardly been asked.

## Connected maths
- One auto horn measures 90 decibels at your window. Every doubling of the number of horns adds about 3 decibels, and every ten times as many horns adds 10. Find the level for 2 horns, 10 horns and 100 horns. A friend says 2 horns make 180. Say what is wrong. **2 horns: about 93. 10 horns: 100. 100 horns: 110. The friend added the numbers, but decibels count how many times the energy has grown.**. Two horns carry 2 times the energy, so 90 + 3 = 93. Ten horns carry 10 times the energy, so 90 + 10 = 100. A hundred carry 100 times, so 90 + 20 = 110. Energy adds. Decibels count the tens of times it has grown, so they do not add like ordinary numbers. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A mixer-grinder reads 90 decibels and ordinary talk reads 60. How many times more sound energy does the mixer-grinder carry? What roughly 104 decibels against 74? **1,000 times in both cases.**. Both gaps are 30 decibels. Each 10 decibels is 10 times the energy, so 30 decibels is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. The same gap gives the same ratio wherever the two numbers sit on the scale. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A steady source reads 100 decibels at 1 metre in the open. Each doubling of distance takes off about 6 decibels. Find the level at 2, 4, 8 and 16 metres. At about what distance does it fall to 64 decibels? **94, 88, 82 and 76 decibels. It falls to 64 at 64 metres.**. Each doubling takes off 6: 100 → 94 → 88 → 82 → 76. To reach 64 we must take off 36, which is 6 doublings. Doubling 6 times from 1 metre gives 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 64 metres. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A child's safe weekly listening is 40 hours at 75 decibels, and every 3 decibels more halves the time. Fill in the safe hours for 75, 78, 81, 84, 87 and 90 decibels. Then say how long is safe at 100 decibels, which many phones can reach at full volume. **40, 20, 10, 5, 2.5 and 1.25 hours. At 100 decibels, only about 7 minutes in a week.**. Each step of 3 halves the time. From 75 to 100 is 25 decibels, about 8 steps of 3 and a bit more. Halving 8 times gives 40 ÷ 256, about 0.16 hours. The extra third of a step makes it about 0.12 hours, or about 7 minutes. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A foam earplug cuts 25 decibels and cotton wool cuts 6 decibels. About how many times less sound energy does each let through? How many times better is the earplug? **Earplug roughly 300 times less. Cotton wool about 4 times less. The earplug is about 80 times better.**. 6 decibels is two halvings of the energy, which is 4 times less. 25 decibels is 20 decibels, which is 100 times, and 5 more, which is about 3 times, so roughly 300 times. 300 ÷ 4 is about 75, so about 80 times better. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A class of 40 children talking reads 80 decibels. Each time you halve the number of equal talkers, about 3 decibels come off. Find the level for 20, 10, 5 and 1 children. Why does one child alone not read 40? **77, 74, 71 and about 64 decibels. One child carries one fortieth of the energy, which is only about 16 decibels less, not 40.**. Halving 40 children to 20 gives 77, halving to 10 gives 74, and halving to 5 gives 71. From 40 children to 1 child is about 5.3 halvings, so 80 − 16 = 64, close to the 60 of ordinary talk. A level of 40 would be 40 decibels below 80, which is only one ten-thousandth of the energy. One child is one fortieth of it. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
