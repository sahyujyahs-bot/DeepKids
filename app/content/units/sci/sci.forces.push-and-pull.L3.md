# Pushes and pulls: what a force is and which way it acts
`sci.forces.push-and-pull.L3`

**Summary.** When you push a wall, the wall pushes you back just as hard. Every push and every pull is called a force. Forces start things moving, stop them, speed them up, slow them down and turn them. Every force acts in a direction, so we draw it as an arrow, and a longer arrow means a bigger force. Some forces work only by touching. Others, like gravity and a magnet's force, work across a gap. Underneath, every touching push is the electrons of two things repelling each other. Scientists still argue about what a force really is.

**Intro.** *Try this. Stand facing a wall, about one step away. Put both palms on the wall and push. Did the wall move? Did you? Something pushed you back. What was it?* In a tug of war, everyone watches the rope. Each team pulls as hard as it can. One team slides forward and loses. Most people say the winners pulled harder on the rope. But the rope is one rope. Can it pull one end harder than the other? By the end of these lessons, you will be able to answer that. You will also know where to look to see who will win.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that a force is a push or a pull, and sort everyday actions into pushes, pulls, or both.
1. I can say what forces do to moving things: start them, stop them, speed them up, slow them down and change their direction.
2. I can explain that every force acts in a direction, and that the same push in a different direction gives a different result.
3. I can draw a force as an arrow. It starts on the thing, points the way of the push or pull, and is longer for a bigger force. I can use arrows to say which way a tug of war will move.
4. I can explain that forces come in pairs. When I push or pull something, it pushes or pulls me back.
5. I can tell touching forces from forces that work from far away, like magnets and gravity.
6. I can explain some deep ideas. Touching pushes are electrons pushing apart. Muscles can only pull. The Earth and I pull on each other. Scientists still argue about what a force really is.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces can start or stop things, speed them up, slow them down, turn them or change their shape. *e.g.* Kicking a football is a force. So is pulling open a drawer.
- **Push**: A force that moves something away from you, or presses on it. *e.g.* You push a door shut. You push the button on a mixer.
- **Pull**: A force that moves something towards you, or drags it along behind you. *e.g.* You pull a drawer open. A bullock pulls a cart.
- **Twist**: A turning force. Your hand pushes one side of the thing away and pulls the other side towards you at the same time. *e.g.* Opening a tap or a jar lid is a twist.
- **Direction**: The way something is going or pointing. Up, down, left, right, forwards and backwards are all directions. *e.g.* A carrom striker flicked to the left goes left. Flicked forwards, it goes forwards.
- **Force arrow**: A drawing that shows a force. The arrow starts on the thing being pushed or pulled. It points the way the force goes. A longer arrow means a bigger force. *e.g.* A short arrow for a gentle push on a box, and a long arrow for a hard push.
- **Size of a force**: How big or strong a force is. A gentle tap is a small force. A hard shove is a big force. *e.g.* Lifting a feather needs a tiny force. Lifting a bucket of water needs a big one.
- **Contact force**: A force that works only when two things touch. *e.g.* Your hand pushing a swing. The floor pushing up on your feet.
- **Force at a distance**: A force that can push or pull without touching. Scientists call it a non-contact force. *e.g.* A magnet pulls a pin from across the table. The Earth pulls a falling mango down.
- **Gravity**: The force that pulls every object towards every other object. The Earth's gravity pulls everything towards the middle of the Earth. *e.g.* A mango falls down from the tree, never up.
- **Magnetic force**: The push or pull of a magnet. A magnet attracts iron and steel, which means it pulls them towards itself. Two magnets can attract each other or repel each other, which means push each other away. *e.g.* A fridge magnet holds a list on the steel door.
- **Balanced forces**: Two or more forces that cancel each other out. Balanced forces do not change how something moves. *e.g.* Two equal teams in a tug of war. The rope stays still.
- **Unbalanced forces**: Forces that do not cancel out. Unbalanced forces change how something moves. *e.g.* One team pulls harder, so the rope starts to move that way.
- **Force pair**: Every force comes with a partner. When you push or pull something, it pushes or pulls you back just as hard, the opposite way. *e.g.* Push a wall and the wall pushes you. A balloon pushes air out, and the air pushes the balloon forward.
- **Tension**: The pull along a rope, a string or a chain when it is pulled tight. A rope can only pull. It can never push. *e.g.* The rope of a well bucket is in tension when the bucket hangs from it.
- **Support force**: The push of a floor, a table or a chair holding something up. Scientists call it the normal force. *e.g.* The table pushes up on your plate, so the plate does not fall through.
- **Thrust**: The push that moves a rocket, a boat or a balloon forward. It comes from pushing something else backwards. *e.g.* A boatman pushes water back with an oar. The water pushes the boat forward.
- **Muscle**: The soft parts of your body that move you. A muscle can only pull, by getting shorter. It can never push. *e.g.* The muscle at the front of your upper arm pulls to bend your elbow.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* Your hand, the wall and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Electron**: A particle far tinier than an atom, found in the outer part of every atom. Electrons carry electric charge, and electrons repel other electrons, which means they push each other away. *e.g.* When your hand presses on a wall, the electrons of your hand and the wall push each other apart.
- **Newton**: The unit for measuring the size of a force. It is named after the scientist Isaac Newton. You will learn to measure forces in newtons in the Measuring forces unit. *e.g.* Holding up a small apple takes a force of about 1 newton.
- **Vector**: Anything that has both a size and a direction, so it is drawn as an arrow. Forces are vectors. So is the wind. *e.g.* A weather map shows the wind as arrows. The arrow says which way and how strong.

## Explanations
### What a force is · *defined*
**Definition:** A force is a push or a pull on something. Forces change how things move, and they can change their shape.

Nothing starts moving by itself. A ball on the floor stays still until something pushes it. Once it is rolling, something has to push against it to stop it. That push or pull is a force. You cannot see a force. You can only see what it does. Some actions are a push and a pull at once. Turning a tap pushes one side of it away and pulls the other side towards you. We call that a twist.
- e.g. Opening the fridge is a pull. Closing it is a push.
- e.g. Squeezing toothpaste is a push. Picking up a school bag is a pull.
- e.g. Turning a key in a lock is a twist.
### What forces do to moving things · *defined*
**Definition:** A force can start a thing moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down or change its direction.

Think of one cricket ball in one over. The bowler's push starts it moving. The bat's push changes its direction and speeds it up. The air and the grass slow it down. The fielder's hands stop it. Each change needs a force. If no force acts, nothing changes. A still thing stays still, and a moving thing keeps going the same way. Forces can also squash, stretch and twist things out of shape. You will meet that in the Stretching springs unit.
- e.g. Start: you push a toy car and it begins to roll.
- e.g. Stop: a goalkeeper catches the ball.
- e.g. Speed up: you push a swing as it moves away from you.
- e.g. Slow down: you put your feet down on a cycle.
- e.g. Change direction: a carrom coin hits the edge of the board and bounces off sideways.
### Every force points somewhere · *defined*
**Definition:** Every force acts in a direction. The same force in a different direction has a different result.

Push a carrom striker forwards and it goes forwards. Push it the same amount to the left and it goes left. The push was just as big. Only its direction changed. So to describe a force, you must say two things. How big is it? Which way does it point? Pulling a door handle towards you opens the door. Pushing the same handle away shuts it. A push on the side of a moving ball turns it. A push from behind speeds it up. A push from the front slows it down.
- e.g. Pushing a swing from behind speeds it up. Pushing it from the front slows it down.
- e.g. A batsman can send the same ball to the left or right of the field, just by changing the angle of the bat.
- e.g. Pushing down on a table does not move it. Pushing sideways can slide it.
### Drawing forces as arrows · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists draw a force as an arrow. The arrow starts on the thing the force acts on. It points the way the force goes. Its length shows how big the force is.

An arrow is the perfect drawing for a force, because an arrow has a length and a direction. Here are the three rules. One: start the arrow on the thing being pushed or pulled. Two: point it the way of the push or pull. Three: draw a longer arrow for a bigger force. Then write what the force is next to it, for example 'my push' or 'gravity'. A thing can have many arrows on it at once. A book on a table has an arrow down for gravity, and an arrow up for the table's push. Anything with a size and a direction, drawn as an arrow, is called a vector. Weather maps draw the wind as vectors. Ship captains and pilots use them every day.
- e.g. A gentle push on a box gets a short arrow. A hard push gets an arrow twice as long.
- e.g. A kite on a string has an arrow along the string, pulling towards the person holding it.
- e.g. India's weather maps show the monsoon wind as arrows sweeping in from the sea.
### Adding forces: when do they balance? · *defined*
**Definition:** When forces point the same way, they add up. When they point opposite ways, they work against each other. If they cancel out, they are balanced and nothing changes. If they do not, they are unbalanced and the motion changes.

Imagine a tug of war. On one side, three children pull. On the other side, two children pull. If every child pulls the same amount, the side with three wins. The forces are unbalanced. Now give the two children a grown-up. If both sides pull equally, the rope does not move. The forces are balanced. Balanced does not mean no forces. It means the forces cancel. A book lying on a table has two balanced forces on it. Gravity pulls it down, and the table pushes it up just as hard. You will go much further with this in Class 4, in the unit on balanced forces.
- e.g. Two children push a box from opposite sides equally hard. It stays put.
- e.g. Two children push a heavy box from the same side. Their pushes add up and it moves more easily.
- e.g. A hanging lamp is still. The cord pulls it up, and gravity pulls it down, equally.
### Every push gets a push back · *defined*
**Definition:** Forces always come in pairs. When one thing pushes or pulls another, the second thing pushes or pulls back on the first, just as hard, in the opposite direction. This is Newton's third law of motion.

Push against a wall and you feel it push your hands. If you are on a skateboard, you roll backwards. The wall pushed you. Isaac Newton wrote this rule down in 1687, in a book called the Principia. The two forces in a pair act on different things. Your push acts on the wall. The wall's push acts on you. This rule explains how we move at all. To walk forward, your foot pushes the ground backwards. The ground pushes you forwards. A swimmer pushes water back, and the water pushes the swimmer forward. A rocket pushes hot gas down, and the gas pushes the rocket up.
- e.g. A balloon lets out its air. It pushes the air backwards, and the air pushes the balloon forwards.
- e.g. Jump off a small boat and the boat moves backwards.
- e.g. A boatman on the backwaters pushes a long pole back against the river bed. The river bed pushes the pole forward, and the boat moves forward with it.
### Pushes and pulls from far away · *defined*
**Definition:** Some forces only work when things touch. These are contact forces. Others can push or pull across a gap. These are forces at a distance, also called non-contact forces. Gravity, magnetic forces and electric forces can act at a distance.

Most pushes and pulls need touching. You cannot push a swing without touching it. But a magnet can pull a pin from across the table. A balloon rubbed on your hair attracts little bits of paper up into the air. And the Earth pulls a mango down from a tree without any rope. Gravity even reaches out to the Moon, about 3,84,000 kilometres away, and holds it in its path. How a pull can cross empty space puzzled even Isaac Newton. He wrote, in 1713, that he would not make up a guess about how gravity works.
- e.g. A magnet under a sheet of paper drags a paper clip on top of it.
- e.g. A rubbed balloon makes your hair stand up towards it.
- e.g. The Moon's gravity pulls on the sea and helps make the tides.
### Some things can only pull: ropes and muscles · *defined*
**Definition:** A rope, a string or a chain can only pull. So can a muscle. None of them can push.

Try to push a ball across the floor with a piece of string. The string just folds up. Ropes only work when they are pulled tight. Your muscles are the same. A muscle can only get shorter and pull on a bone. It cannot get longer by itself and push. So muscles work in pairs. To bend your arm, the muscle on the front of your upper arm pulls. To straighten it, the muscle on the back pulls instead. Your heart is a muscle too. It squeezes inwards, and the squeeze pushes blood out round your body. Inside every cell, tiny machines called motor proteins walk along thin tracks. They pull loads behind them, one tiny step at a time.
- e.g. Feel the front of your upper arm as you bend your elbow. It bunches up as it pulls.
- e.g. Puppets on strings can only be pulled, never pushed, by the strings.
- e.g. A crane lifts things with a cable. It always pulls up, never pushes down.
### Plants and nature push and pull too · *defined*
**Definition:** Pushes and pulls are not only made by people and animals. Wind, water, growing plants and the Earth itself push and pull all the time.

A growing seedling pushes up through the soil. Tree roots push so hard as they grow that they can crack a footpath. Mushrooms sometimes push up through a tarred road. Rivers push sand and stones along, all the way to the sea. The wind pushes sailing boats and kites. Inside the Earth, huge slabs of rock slowly push against each other. Over millions of years, the push of India against Asia lifted up the Himalayas. That push has not stopped. The mountains are still rising, a few millimetres a year.
- e.g. A peepal tree growing out of a crack in an old wall slowly pushes the bricks apart.
- e.g. The monsoon wind pushes clouds from the sea over the land.
- e.g. India is still moving north and pushing into Asia, about 4 to 5 centimetres a year.
### Do things ever really touch? · *debated*
Zoom in on your hand pressing a wall, far past anything a microscope can show. You reach atoms. The outside of each atom is a cloud of electrons. Electrons push other electrons away with electric force. So when you press the wall, the electrons of your hand and the wall push each other apart. That is the push you feel. It is also why you do not sink through the floor. So every contact force, deep down, is an electric force. This much scientists agree on. But does that mean you never really touch anything? Here people disagree, because it depends on what 'touch' means.
- e.g. The floor holding you up is electrons in the floor pushing on electrons in your feet.
- e.g. A rope pulling is atoms in the rope holding on to their neighbours with electric force.
- **Many popular science books:** You never truly touch anything. Your atoms always stay a tiny gap away from the wall's atoms, held apart by electric pushes.
- **Many physicists:** That is just what touching is. Atoms have no hard edge, so there is no gap to measure. Touching means being close enough to push each other. By that meaning, you touch things all the time.
- **Wolfgang Pauli, an Austrian physicist, in 1925, and physicists since:** There is a second reason solids push back. A rule of quantum physics, found by Wolfgang Pauli in 1925, stops electrons from crowding into the same state. So squeezed atoms resist being pushed together.
### You pull the Earth too · *defined*
**Definition:** Gravity is a force pair too. The Earth pulls you down, and you pull the Earth up, just as hard.

This sounds impossible, but it is true. When you jump, the Earth pulls you back down. At the same moment, you pull the Earth up towards you, with exactly the same size of force. So why does the Earth not move? It does, but by far too little to ever notice, because the Earth is so enormous. The same thing happens in space. The Earth pulls the Moon, and the Moon pulls the Earth. The Sun pulls the Earth, and the Earth pulls the Sun. Everything pulls everything. So right now, as you sit reading, you are pulling the whole planet.
- e.g. A falling mango pulls the Earth up a tiny, tiny bit as it falls.
- e.g. The Moon's pull lifts the sea into tides. The Earth's pull keeps the same side of the Moon always facing us.
### Light can push · *defined*
**Definition:** Light pushes very gently on anything it hits. Scientists call this radiation pressure.

Sunlight on your face pushes on you, but far too gently to feel. In space, with no air and no friction, even this tiny push adds up. That is why the tail of a comet always points away from the Sun. Sunlight pushes the comet's dust outwards. A stream of tiny particles from the Sun, called the solar wind, pushes its gas outwards. Engineers have used this push to sail through space. In 2010, Japan's space agency flew a spacecraft called IKAROS. It had a huge, thin sail, and sunlight pushed it between the planets. It was the first sail of its kind to work far out in space.
- e.g. A comet's tail points away from the Sun, even when the comet is moving away from the Sun.
- e.g. A solar sail is thinner than a hair, and as big as a room or bigger.
### Why does a thrown ball keep going? · *defined*
**Definition:** A moving thing keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction unless a force changes it. You only need a force to change motion, not to keep it going.

Throw a ball. Once it leaves your hand, nothing is pushing it forward. So why does it keep going? People argued about this for about 2,000 years. Here are the main answers. Only the last one is accepted today. We know because spacecraft prove it. Voyager 1 was launched in 1977. For more than 40 years no engine has pushed it forward, yet it is still coasting away from us, now more than 24 billion kilometres from Earth.
- e.g. A carrom coin keeps sliding after you flick it, until friction stops it.
- e.g. In a space station, a pushed pen floats across the room in a straight line until it hits a wall.
- **Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,350 years ago:** Moving things stop by themselves unless something keeps pushing them. A thrown stone is pushed along by the air rushing in behind it.
- **The Vaiśeṣika school of Kaṇāda, in ancient India, more than 2,000 years ago (scholars disagree on the exact date):** They studied a flying arrow. The first movement comes from the push of the bowstring, which they called nodana. After that, the arrow keeps moving because of a moving-tendency the push gave it, called vega or saṃskāra. It slowly fades.
- **John Philoponus in Alexandria, about 1,500 years ago, Ibn Sina in Persia, about 1,000 years ago, and Jean Buridan in France, about 700 years ago:** The air does not push the stone. The thrower puts something into the stone, a kind of inner push, and the stone uses it up as the air and ground resist it.
- **Galileo in Italy, about 400 years ago, and Isaac Newton in England, in 1687:** Nothing gets used up. A moving thing simply keeps moving until a force, like friction or air, slows it. This is Newton's first law. It is the accepted answer today.
### What is a force, really? · *debated*
We can measure forces very well. We can predict where rockets will go. But what a force really is turns out to be a deep question. Different great thinkers have answered it differently, and the question is not settled.
- e.g. Two magnets push apart across a gap with nothing visible between them.
- e.g. The Earth pulls the Moon across 3,84,000 kilometres of nearly empty space.
- **Isaac Newton, 1687:** A force is whatever changes a thing's motion. He described gravity exactly, but he refused to guess what it is made of.
- **Heinrich Hertz, a German physicist, in a book published in 1894:** Perhaps forces are not real things at all. He tried to write all of mechanics using only movement, with no forces in it.
- **Albert Einstein, 1915:** Gravity is not a pull. Heavy things bend space and time around them, and other things follow the bends. Experiments since 1919 keep showing he was right.
- **Particle physicists today:** Forces are carried by tiny particles passing between things. Particles of light, called photons, carry the electric force. Some physicists think gravity is carried by a particle called the graviton, but nobody has ever found one.

## Mostly agreed
- A force is a push or a pull, and it always acts in a direction. *How we know:* Every push we measure has a size and a way it points. Change the direction of the same push and the result changes. Engineers rely on this to build every bridge, crane and rocket.
- Forces change motion. A force is needed to start, stop, speed up, slow down or turn something, but not to keep it moving. *How we know:* Voyager 1 was launched in 1977. For more than 40 years no engine has pushed it forward, yet it keeps coasting away from the Sun. On Earth, things only stop because friction and air push against them.
- Forces come in pairs that are equal in size and opposite in direction. *How we know:* Hook two spring forcemeters together and pull them apart. Both always show the same number. Rockets work in empty space because of this rule.
- Gravity, magnetic forces and electric forces can act across a gap, without touching. *How we know:* A magnet pulls a clip through a sheet of paper. Spacecraft travel by the pull of planets millions of kilometres away.
- Deep down, almost every push and pull in daily life is an electric force between atoms. Physicists know of four basic forces in all: gravity, the electromagnetic force, and two forces that act only inside atoms. *How we know:* Experiments in the 1800s showed that electricity and magnetism are one force, and that light is a wave of that force. Experiments in the 1900s found the two forces inside atoms.

## How we found out
**Robert Goddard, an American rocket scientist, using Isaac Newton's rule that forces come in pairs, 1687 to 1969.** For a long time, many people believed that a rocket needs air to push against. A swimmer pushes water and a bird pushes air, so it seemed a rocket must push on something outside it too. In 1919, the American scientist Robert Goddard wrote that a rocket might one day reach the Moon. In January 1920, the newspaper The New York Times laughed at him. It said a rocket could not work in empty space, because there is nothing there to push against. Goddard had already tested this. He fired small rockets inside a closed container with most of the air pumped out. They still pushed forward, as well as in air or even better. The answer was a rule Isaac Newton wrote down in 1687: every push comes with an equal push back. A rocket pushes its own hot gas out backwards, and the gas pushes the rocket forwards. No outside air is needed. On 17 July 1969, as Apollo 11 flew towards the Moon, the newspaper printed a correction.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a rocket needs outside air to push against, so it could never work in empty space. Goddard's rockets pushed forward inside a container with the air pumped out.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A moving thing needs a push to keep it going.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,350 years ago. Most of us also feel this without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: Push a box and stop pushing, and it stops. A cycle slows down when you stop pedalling.  
  Against: Things only stop because friction and air push against them. Galileo saw that a ball rolls further on smoother ground. In space, with nothing to slow it, a pushed thing keeps going. Voyager 1 has coasted for nearly 50 years.
- **In a tug of war, the rope pulls the losing team harder than the winning team.** (A common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: The losing team gets dragged forward, so it feels as if the rope beat them.  
  Against: A rope that is tight pulls equally hard at both ends. The winning team wins by pushing harder on the ground with their feet. The ground then pushes them back, away from the middle line.
- **If you push something and it does not move, there is no force.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Nothing happened, so it seems nothing was there.  
  Against: Your push is real. It is balanced by another force, for example the floor gripping the box, or the wall pushing back. A wall even bends a tiny bit when you push it, too little to see.
- **Rockets cannot work in space, because there is no air there to push against.** (A newspaper, The New York Times, in 1920, mocking the rocket scientist Robert Goddard; *rejected*)  
  For: On Earth, a swimmer pushes water and a bird pushes air. It seemed a rocket must need air to push on too.  
  Against: A rocket pushes its own hot gases out backwards, and the gases push the rocket forwards. No air is needed. In July 1969, as Apollo 11 flew to the Moon, the newspaper printed a correction.
- **Gravity is not really a pull at all. Heavy things bend space and time, and other things follow the bends.** (Albert Einstein, in 1915; *accepted*)  
  For: His idea predicted that starlight would bend round the Sun. This was seen in 1919, and in many experiments since. Satellite navigation in phones must use his idea, or it goes wrong.  
  Against: Thinking of gravity as Newton's pull still works almost everywhere, and engineers use it to send spacecraft to other planets. Some physicists hope to describe gravity as a force carried by particles, like the other forces. Nobody has managed it yet.
- **Isaac Newton alone discovered the laws of force.** (Many books and posters; *debated*)  
  For: Newton wrote the three laws of motion together, clearly, in one book in 1687.  
  Against: He built on Galileo, René Descartes, Christiaan Huygens and others. Robert Hooke argued with him about the idea of gravity. And for many readers it was a woman, Émilie du Châtelet, who made the book understandable. She translated it into French with her own notes. Her translation came out in 1759, ten years after she died, and is still used today.

## Roots
We have been learning that every push and pull is a force, and that forces come in pairs. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You cannot start moving by pushing on yourself. To go forward, you must push something else backwards. A walker pushes the floor, and a balloon pushes out its air. Even a rocket in empty space moves only by throwing its gas behind it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a person walks, or a rocket flies, by its own power, as if moving were something done alone. But every start needs something else to push against: the floor, the water, the air or the rocket's own gas.  
*Evidence:* In Push the Wall, you moved only because you pushed the wall and the wall pushed you back. The balloon rocket went forwards only by pushing its air backwards.

- *who am i:* Every time you push something, it pushes you back. You are never only the one who pushes.
- *what is life:* Your muscles can only pull. Yet by pulling on your bones, they let you push, lift and hug.
- *what is this world:* The Sun pulls the Earth, and the Earth pulls you. You pull them both back, just as hard.

Every force comes in a pair, acting on two different things. When you walk, you push the floor back, and the floor pushes you forward. The whole Earth even moves back a tiny bit under your feet. DeepKids believes this shows that no movement belongs to one thing alone: each step is shared between you and the ground. Science measures the sharing exactly, as two equal and opposite pushes.

- **Empedocles, fragment DK B17:** Empedocles, a Greek thinker about 2,450 years ago, said two powers move everything. Love brings things together. Strife drives them apart. (Physics also finds two kinds of force everywhere: attraction, which pulls things together, and repulsion, which pushes them apart. Electrons repelling electrons is what makes every touching push.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 3.27:** All actions are done by the powers of nature. Only someone confused by pride thinks, 'I alone am the doer.' (The Gita is talking about who does our actions, not about physics. But this unit finds something close: you could not slide away from the wall by yourself. You moved only because the wall pushed back.)
- **Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.40:** Think of the universe as one living being. All things in it act together and help cause everything that happens. (Every push has a push back, and everything pulls everything with gravity. No force in the world acts on one thing only.)
- Science (settled): Every touching push you feel is the electrons in your hand and in the wall repelling each other.
- Science (open): Scientists can measure every force very well. But what a force really is, deep down, is still argued about.
- Other view, Many physicists: A force pair is just a rule for how two separate things act on each other. It shows they affect each other. It does not show they are one, or that a step is truly shared.
- Practice, Which hand is pushing? (2 min): Let's put our palms together in front of our chest. Press them gently into each other. Breathe in slowly. Press a little harder as you breathe out. Now relax. Do it three times. Each hand pushes, and each hand is pushed. Which hand is doing the pushing?
- Ask: When you press your hands together, which one is pushing?
- Ask: When you push a wall, which comes first, your push or the wall's push?
- Ask: Is the wall pushing you back on purpose? How could you tell?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* You can't see a force. You can only see what it does. So how do we know forces are real? (hint: Push your palm against a friend's palm. What do you feel? Is feeling a kind of seeing?)
- *deep:* When you jump, you pull the whole Earth up towards you a tiny bit. Why don't you notice? (hint: Think of an ant pushing an elephant. Who moves more?)
- *deep:* If you push a wall, the wall pushes back. Is the wall doing it on purpose? (hint: Does a thing need to be alive, or to want something, to push?)
- *deep:* A rope can only pull, and a muscle can only pull. How does your body push a door, if all its muscles only pull? (hint: Watch your arm straighten. Which muscle is pulling now, and where does it pull the bone?)

## Story: The Rope That Could Only Pull
*Onam's tug of war, called vadamvali, is real, and so are the coir rope makers of Alappuzha in Kerala. The rope, Anjali, her mother and the two teams are made up. The physics is true: a tight rope pulls equally at both ends, and the team that pushes the ground harder wins.*

I was born on a beach in Alappuzha, in Kerala, out of coconut husks. Women soaked the husks in the backwaters for months. They beat them into soft brown hair, and spun the hair into yarn on wheels that hummed all day. Then they twisted the yarn into strands, and the strands into me. I am thirty metres long and as thick as a child's wrist. Every twist in me was made by a woman's hands.

I learned the first fact about myself very early. I cannot push. Hold one end of me and push it towards the other end, and I only fold into a heap, like a sleepy snake. Ropes can only pull. When I am pulled tight, every fibre in me holds on to the next fibre, all the way along. That holding on is my whole life.

For eleven years I have lived in the storeroom of a village school beside the river. I come out once a year, at Onam, the harvest festival, for the vadamvali. That is the tug of war. And for eleven years, the boatmen have won.

The boatmen row the long snake boats in the races. Their arms are like young coconut trunks. Every year they take one end of me, and another team takes the other end. Every year, after a lot of shouting, I slide across the chalk line towards the boatmen. People say the boatmen pull harder.

Here is a secret that only a rope knows. While I am stretched tight between two teams, I am pulled exactly as hard at one end as at the other. I feel it in every twist. If the boatmen pull me with the strength of ten men, the other team is pulling me with the strength of ten men too. Nobody believes this, because nobody asks the rope.

This year, the other team was the coir spinners. Some of them were daughters and granddaughters of the women who made me. Their team was one short, so they let a girl of nine join them. Her name was Anjali. She was small, with a long plait and a scab on one knee, and she held me as if I might bite.

The first round went the way it always did. The spinners gripped me and pulled with their arms, standing tall, as if lifting a bucket from a well. The boatmen crouched low, dug their heels into the wet earth and walked backwards, step by step. I slid. The crowd laughed kindly. One round to the boatmen.

Between rounds, the spinners sat on the grass to rest their hands. Anjali did not rest. She sat beside me and laid her palm on me. She was quiet for a long time. She was not looking at me. She was looking at the boatmen's feet.

'Amma,' she said at last, 'when the boatmen pulled harder, did the rope pull us harder?'

'Of course,' said her mother, rubbing her palms.

'Then it pulled them harder too,' said Anjali. 'It's one rope. It can't pull one end more than the other.'

Her mother laughed. 'Then how do they always win?'

Anjali did not answer. She stood up and pressed one bare foot hard into the ground, as if starting a race. She leaned back against nothing. 'The rope isn't what moves them,' she said slowly. 'The ground is. They push the ground towards the middle with their heels. And the ground pushes them back, the other way. We didn't push the ground at all. We just held on.'

She picked up a stick and drew in the mud. First she drew a long arrow pointing away from the chalk line. 'That's where we want to go.' Then, under it, she drew a short arrow pointing the other way. 'That's where our feet must push.' The spinners looked at the arrows. They looked at each other. Then one of the old women, who had spun yarn for forty years, began to laugh. It was the laugh of somebody who has just seen a thing that was always in front of her.

In the second round, the spinners did not stand tall. They crouched low, like the boatmen. They dug their heels into the mud. And they moved together, the way their mothers had always twisted yarn together, to a song. One, two, push. One, two, push.

I felt it. I was not pulled any harder at their end. I am never pulled harder at one end. But now the spinners were moving away from the line, one push at a time. The ground was pushing them back, and they were bringing me with them. The boatmen, who had stood up straight to laugh, sat down very suddenly in the mud.

The spinners won the second round. In the third round, the boatmen crouched again and pushed with all their strength. For a long time, nobody moved at all. I hung over the chalk line, stretched so tight that I sang a little. Two teams, pulling equally, pushing the ground equally. Then a boatman's heel slipped on the wet grass. The spinners did not slip. They had found the dry patch, because Anjali had been looking at the ground all day.

Afterwards, the boatmen's captain came to look at the arrows in the mud. He was a big man with a grey moustache. He crouched beside Anjali, and she explained the arrows to him. He nodded slowly and scratched his head.

Tonight I am back in the storeroom, coiled in the dark, smelling of mud and coconut. For eleven years, everybody watched me. Everybody thought the answer was in the rope. A girl of nine was the first one to look at the ground. Somewhere in me there is a twist her grandmother made. I like to think it held on a little tighter today.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the rope is pulled equally at both ends, so 'pulling harder on the rope' cannot be what wins. Every pull needs a push. Each team's feet push the ground towards the middle, and the ground pushes the team back the other way. Whichever team gets the bigger push from the ground wins. Anjali solves it by drawing force arrows: the way she wants to go, and the opposite way her feet must push. The story also asks who notices things, and whose hands made what everyone else uses.

- Ask: The rope says it is always pulled equally hard at both ends. If that is true, how can one team win?
- Ask: Anjali drew two arrows in the mud. Which way did each one point, and why?
- Ask: Why do you think nobody noticed the answer for eleven years?
- Ask: The old spinner laughed when she understood. Have you ever laughed when you suddenly understood something?
- Ask: Who made the rope? Why do you think the rope tells us that at the very beginning?

### g1 · Arrow Wars: the tug of war card game (20 min)
- **rules:** Put the rope marker on the chalk line. Shuffle the arrow cards and deal 5 to each player.; Each round, both players secretly choose 2 arrow cards and lay them face down on their side of the rope.; Turn the cards over. Each player adds up the lengths of their arrows.; Take the smaller total from the bigger total. Move the rope marker that many squares towards the player with the bigger total.; If the totals are the same, the forces are balanced. The marker does not move.; Special cards change the round as they say. Draw back up to 5 cards after each round.; The first player to pull the marker onto their square 5 wins.

  *Child mode:* Draw the last round. Draw one arrow for each team. Make each arrow as long as its total, in centimetres.
  - Think: Two short arrows the same way can add up to more than one long arrow. Is there something at home that two people can push, but one cannot?
- **s5 (2 min)**

  *Child mode:* Who won? Now a puzzle. A real rope pulls both teams equally. So what really decides the winner?
  - Note: A good answer: the team that pushes harder on the ground with their feet. The ground pushes them back, away from the middle. If your child is unsure, leave it as a puzzle. The story and the Push the Wall experiment come back to it.

## Experiments
### e1 · Push the Wall: does the wall push back? (25 min)
- **question:** When you push a wall, does the wall push you? Does a harder push make you go further?
- **predict:** Guess first. You sit on a towel on a smooth floor, with your feet against the wall. You push the wall with your feet. Will you move? Which way? Will a hard push send you further than a gentle push? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only how hard you push: gentle, medium and hard. Keep the same towel, the same floor and the same starting place against the wall. Try each push 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays close. Clear the floor of furniture and anything sharp. Push with your feet, not your head. Never stand on a skateboard for this.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw yourself pushing the wall, with one arrow for your push on the wall and one arrow for the wall's push on you.
- **results table:** How hard the push was | Try 1: distance slid (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear a space on a smooth floor next to a wall. 2. Fold the towel and put it against the wall. 3. Your child sits on it, knees bent, feet flat on the wall.  
  *Parent says:* You're going to push the wall with your feet. The wall is very heavy and fixed to the house. Do you think you'll move? Which way?  
  *Child mode:* Fetch a grown-up. Sit on a towel by the wall. Put your feet flat on the wall. Will you move when you push?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen when you push? (good: I'll slide away from the wall.; The wall won't move, but I will.)
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark the floor at the back edge of the towel. 2. Your child pushes the wall gently with both feet. 3. Mark where the back edge of the towel stops. 4. Measure between the two marks. 5. Do it 3 times each for a gentle, a medium and a hard push.  
  *Parent says:* Push gently. Now let's measure how far you slid. Now try a medium push. Now your hardest push.  
  *Child mode:* Push gently. Mark where the towel stops. Measure from the first mark. Then push medium, then hard. Do each push 3 times.
  - Note: Measure from the starting mark, not from the wall, so the length of your child's legs is not counted. If your child hardly slides, try a smoother floor, or a flattened cardboard box instead of the towel. To find the middle value, put the three numbers in order and take the middle one.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what pushed your child.  
  *Parent says:* You pushed the wall towards the wall. But you moved the other way. What pushed you?  
  *Child mode:* You pushed the wall. But you moved away from it. What pushed you?
  - Note: Wait for your child's answer before you explain. Then: this is a force pair. Your child pushed the wall, and the wall pushed back on your child, just as hard, the opposite way. The only thing touching their feet was the wall. The wall does not move because it is fixed to the whole house and the Earth.
  - Check: You push a wall with your feet and slide away from it. What pushed you? → **The wall pushed you back**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw two arrows of the same length. One goes on the wall, pointing into the wall. The other goes on your child, pointing away from the wall.  
  *Parent says:* Draw your push on the wall as an arrow. Now draw the wall's push on you. Should the arrows be the same length?  
  *Child mode:* Draw an arrow for your push on the wall. Draw an arrow for the wall's push on you. Make them the same length.
  - Think: A harder push on the wall sent you further. So did the wall push you harder too?
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: when I push the wall, the wall pushes me back. What I saw: my gentle push slid me about 30 cm, but my hard push slid me about 140 cm. Why: the harder I push the wall, the harder the wall pushes me.

## Worksheets
### Push, Pull, Point (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a force?  
   Options: A push or a pull / Only something that makes things go fast / Only something living things do / Only something heavy things have  
   **Answer:** A push or a pull. *Why:* A force is any push or pull. Picking 'only living things' is a common idea. But magnets, wind and the Earth push and pull too.
2. (sort) Sort these. Is each one a push or a pull?  
   **Answer:** closing a door → Push; opening a drawer → Pull; kicking a ball → Push; lifting a bucket from a well → Pull; pressing a lift button → Push; flying a kite on its string → Pull. *Why:* A push moves something away from you or presses on it. A pull moves something towards you or drags it along.
3. (mcq) Turning a tap is…  
   Options: Only a push / Only a pull / A twist: you push one side away and pull the other side towards you / Not a force  
   **Answer:** A twist: you push one side away and pull the other side towards you. *Why:* Your fingers push one side of the tap away and pull the other side towards you, so it turns. Picking only a push or only a pull is close, but misses the other side of the hand.
4. (match) Match each moment to what the force did.  
   **Answer:** A goalkeeper catches the ball → stopped it; You push a toy car on the floor → started it moving; A batsman hits the ball to the side → changed its direction; You push a swing from behind as it swings away → sped it up. *Why:* Forces start, stop, speed up, slow down and turn moving things.
5. (fill) A force always acts in a ____. That is why we draw it as an arrow.  
   **Answer:** ['direction']. *Why:* Every force points some way. An arrow shows which way.
6. (mcq) You draw a gentle push on a box and a hard push on the same box. Which should have the longer arrow?  
   Options: The gentle push / The hard push / Both the same / Neither, pushes don't get arrows  
   **Answer:** The hard push. *Why:* A longer arrow means a bigger force. Picking 'both the same' often means the arrow is seen as just a direction. It shows the size too.
7. (mcq) Where should a force arrow start?  
   Options: On the thing being pushed or pulled / Anywhere on the page / At the edge of the page / On the person's head  
   **Answer:** On the thing being pushed or pulled. *Why:* The arrow starts on the thing the force acts on, and points the way the force goes.
8. (truefalse) If something is not moving, there are no forces on it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A book on a table has gravity pulling it down and the table pushing it up. The two forces balance, so it stays still.
9. (mcq) In a gentle tug of war, both sides pull equally hard. What happens to the rope?  
   Options: It moves to the left / It moves to the right / It stays still, because the pulls are balanced / It snaps  
   **Answer:** It stays still, because the pulls are balanced. *Why:* Equal pulls in opposite directions cancel out. They are balanced. Picking left or right means one side was imagined as stronger.
10. (mcq) Look at the chart. In a tug of war, the left team pulls with 5 and the right team pulls with 3. Which way does the rope move?  
   Options: Left / Right / It does not move  
   **Answer:** Left. *Why:* 5 is bigger than 3, so the forces are unbalanced. The rope moves towards the left team. Picking 'right' usually means the shorter bar was read as the winner.
11. (mcq) You sit on a towel and push a wall with your feet. You slide away from the wall. What pushed you?  
   Options: The wall / Nothing / The air / The towel  
   **Answer:** The wall. *Why:* When you push the wall, the wall pushes you back, just as hard. Picking 'nothing' is very common. But you cannot start moving without a force.
12. (mcq) A balloon lets its air rush out backwards. Which way does the balloon go?  
   Options: Backwards, with the air / Forwards / Nowhere  
   **Answer:** Forwards. *Why:* The balloon pushes the air backwards, so the air pushes the balloon forwards. Picking 'backwards' means the balloon was pictured being carried along with the air.
13. (sort) Sort these forces. Do they need touching, or can they work from far away?  
   **Answer:** pushing a swing → Needs touching; a magnet pulling a pin → Works from far away; the Earth pulling a falling mango → Works from far away; kicking a ball → Needs touching; a rubbed balloon lifting bits of paper → Works from far away; pulling a cart with a rope → Needs touching. *Why:* Gravity, magnetic pulls and electric pulls can work across a gap. Most other pushes and pulls need touching.
14. (truefalse) A rope can push a ball across the floor.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A rope only works when it is pulled tight. Push it and it just folds up.
15. (forces) A book lies still on a table. Draw the forces on it with arrows.  
   Options: gravity / table pushes up / push / friction  
   **Answer:** gravity down, table pushes up up. *Why:* Gravity pulls the book down. The table pushes it up just as hard. There is no push or friction, because nobody is pushing it sideways.
16. (write) Name one push and one pull you used today. Say which way each one pointed.  
   **Answer:** Any one push and one pull, each with a direction. For example: I pushed the bathroom door away from me. I pulled my socks up.. *Why:* Any correct push and pull, each with a direction, is right.
17. (draw) Draw a child pushing a box to the right. Draw an arrow for the push. Make it start on the box.  
   **Answer:** An arrow starting on the box and pointing right.. *Why:* The arrow starts on the thing being pushed and points the way of the push.
### Arrows at Work (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Two children push a heavy box from the same side. One pushes with 4 units and one with 3 units. What is the total push?  
   Options: 1 / 7 / 12 / 3  
   **Answer:** 7. *Why:* Pushes in the same direction add up. 4 + 3 = 7. Picking 1 means the pushes were taken away, which only happens when they point opposite ways.
2. (mcq) Now the two children push from opposite sides. One pushes the box towards the right with 4 units. The other pushes it towards the left with 3 units. What happens?  
   Options: The box moves right with a push of 7 units / The box moves right with a push of 1 unit / The box moves left with a push of 1 unit / Nothing happens  
   **Answer:** The box moves right with a push of 1 unit. *Why:* Opposite pushes work against each other. 4 take away 3 is 1. The bigger push points right, so the box is pushed right with 1 unit. Picking 7 means the pushes were added, which only happens when they point the same way.
3. (fill) When forces cancel out and nothing changes, we say the forces are ____.  
   **Answer:** ['balanced']. *Why:* Balanced forces cancel each other out, so the motion does not change.
4. (mcq) A carrom striker is flicked with the same strength, once forwards and once to the left. What is different?  
   Options: Only the size of the push / Only the direction of the push / Both size and direction / Nothing  
   **Answer:** Only the direction of the push. *Why:* The strength was the same, so the size was the same. Only the direction changed, so the striker went a different way.
5. (mcq) You blow on a ping-pong ball from the bottom of a tray, and a friend blows from the left side, at the same time. Which way does the ball go?  
   Options: Straight up the tray / Straight to the right / Slantwise, towards the top right corner / It doesn't move  
   **Answer:** Slantwise, towards the top right corner. *Why:* Your blow pushes it up the tray. Your friend's blow pushes it to the right. The two pushes add up into a slanting push, towards the top right. Picking one straight direction means only one push was counted.
6. (mcq) How does walking forward work?  
   Options: Your feet push the ground backwards, and the ground pushes you forwards / Your legs pull the air / Gravity pulls you forwards / You don't need any force to walk  
   **Answer:** Your feet push the ground backwards, and the ground pushes you forwards. *Why:* This is a force pair. You push the ground back, and the ground pushes you forward. Picking gravity is a common mix-up. Gravity pulls you down, not forward.
7. (mcq) A boatman on the Kerala backwaters pushes a long pole against the river bed. Which way does the boat move?  
   Options: Opposite to the way he pushes the pole / The same way he pushes / It doesn't move  
   **Answer:** Opposite to the way he pushes the pole. *Why:* He pushes the river bed one way, and the river bed pushes the pole, and the boat, the other way.
8. (mcq) In a tug of war, the rope is tight and still. The left end of the rope is pulled with a force of 300. What is the pull on the right end?  
   Options: 0 / 150 / 300 / 600  
   **Answer:** 300. *Why:* A tight, still rope is pulled equally at both ends. So the right end is also pulled with 300. Picking 600 means the two ends were added. They are the same pull, not two pulls on top of each other.
9. (mcq) So what decides who wins a tug of war?  
   Options: Which team pulls the rope harder / Which team pushes the ground harder with their feet / Which team has longer arms / Which team shouts loudest  
   **Answer:** Which team pushes the ground harder with their feet. *Why:* The rope pulls both teams equally. The winning team pushes the ground harder towards the middle. The ground pushes them back, away from the middle. Picking 'pulls the rope harder' is what nearly everyone thinks at first.
10. (truefalse) Your muscles can push as well as pull.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Muscles can only pull, by getting shorter. That is why they work in pairs, one to bend a joint and one to straighten it.
11. (mcq) Which pair of arrows shows a book lying still on a table?  
   Options: A long arrow down and a short arrow up / Two arrows the same length, one down and one up / Only one arrow, down / No arrows  
   **Answer:** Two arrows the same length, one down and one up. *Why:* The table pushes up exactly as hard as gravity pulls down. So the arrows are the same length. Picking 'only down' means the table's push was forgotten.
12. (forces) A child sits still on a swing. Draw the forces on the child.  
   Options: gravity / seat pushes up / push / magnet pull  
   **Answer:** gravity down, seat pushes up up. *Why:* Gravity pulls the child down. The seat pushes up just as hard. Nobody is pushing, so there is no push arrow.
13. (forces) A box is being pulled to the left with a rope across a floor. Show the forces with arrows.  
   Options: rope pulls / friction / gravity / floor pushes up  
   **Answer:** rope pulls left, friction right, gravity down, floor pushes up up. *Why:* The rope pulls left. Friction works against the sliding, so it points right. Gravity pulls down, and the floor pushes up.
14. (match) Match each thing to the force that moves it.  
   **Answer:** A sailing boat → the wind pushes its sail; A falling coconut → the Earth's gravity pulls it; A balloon rocket → the air it pushes out pushes it back; A paper clip jumping to a magnet → the magnet pulls it from a distance. *Why:* Every movement needs a push or a pull. Some touch, and some work from far away.
15. (write) Tipu Sultan's army used iron rockets more than 200 years ago. Explain how a rocket is pushed forwards. Use the words push and backwards.  
   **Answer:** The rocket pushes hot gas out backwards. The gas pushes the rocket forwards, just as hard.. *Why:* Every push has a push back. The rocket's push on the gas is paired with the gas's push on the rocket.
16. (write) A swing is moving away from you. Why can't you stop it by pushing it from behind? Use the word direction.  
   **Answer:** Pushing from behind is in the same direction the swing is going, so it speeds the swing up. To stop it, you must push in the opposite direction, from the front.. *Why:* The direction of a force decides what it does to a moving thing.
17. (draw) Draw a kite flying, with its string held by a child. Draw an arrow for the pull of the string on the kite.  
   **Answer:** An arrow starting on the kite, pointing along the string towards the child.. *Why:* A string can only pull. It pulls the kite towards the person holding it, along the string.
### Pushes, Pulls and the Universe (challenge) (challenge, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) When you jump, the Earth pulls you down. What does your pull do to the Earth?  
   Options: Nothing, people can't pull planets / It pulls the Earth up towards you, just as hard, but the Earth is so huge it barely moves / It pushes the Earth away / It makes the Earth spin faster  
   **Answer:** It pulls the Earth up towards you, just as hard, but the Earth is so huge it barely moves. *Why:* Gravity is a force pair. You pull the Earth up as hard as it pulls you down. The Earth is enormous, so it moves far too little to notice. Picking 'nothing' is what nearly everyone guesses.
2. (mcq) Deep down, what is happening when your hand presses on a wall?  
   Options: Electrons in your hand and the wall push each other apart / Tiny hooks catch on each other / Gravity pushes your hand back / Air is trapped between them  
   **Answer:** Electrons in your hand and the wall push each other apart. *Why:* Contact pushes come from electric forces between atoms. Picking gravity mixes up two forces. Gravity only pulls, and it is far too weak to push your hand back.
3. (mcq) Why does a comet's tail always point away from the Sun?  
   Options: Sunlight and the solar wind, a stream of tiny particles from the Sun, push the dust and gas outwards / The comet is always moving away from the Sun / The Sun's gravity pulls the tail backwards / The tail is a shadow  
   **Answer:** Sunlight and the solar wind, a stream of tiny particles from the Sun, push the dust and gas outwards. *Why:* Light really pushes. In space, the gentle push of sunlight and the solar wind blows the tail outwards. Picking 'moving away' is a sensible guess, but the tail points away from the Sun even when the comet moves towards it.
4. (truefalse) A spacecraft coasting in empty space needs its engine on all the time to keep moving.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. With nothing to slow it, it keeps going. Voyager 1 has coasted since the early 1980s with no engine pushing it forward. You only need a force to change motion.
5. (match) Match each thinker to their idea about a thrown arrow or stone.  
   **Answer:** Aristotle, in ancient Greece → the air pushes it along from behind; Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika school, in ancient India → the push gives it vega, a moving-tendency, that slowly fades; Isaac Newton, in England → it keeps going by itself until a force slows it. *Why:* Thinkers in many places tried to explain why thrown things keep moving. Newton's answer, building on Galileo, is the one we use today.
6. (mcq) In 1920 a newspaper said rockets could never work in space. Why were they wrong?  
   Options: A rocket pushes its own gas out backwards, so it needs no air to push on / There is air in space / Rockets are pulled by the Moon / Rockets float  
   **Answer:** A rocket pushes its own gas out backwards, so it needs no air to push on. *Why:* The rocket and its gas push on each other. No outside air is needed. Picking 'there is air in space' is a common guess, but space is almost empty.
7. (mcq) Einstein said gravity is not really a pull. What did he say it is?  
   Options: Heavy things bend space and time, and other things follow the bends / A kind of magnetism / Air pressing down / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** Heavy things bend space and time, and other things follow the bends. *Why:* Einstein's idea, from 1915, has passed many tests, starting with starlight bending round the Sun in 1919. Newton's pull is still a very good way to calculate most things.
8. (truefalse) Physicists have found a particle that carries gravity, called the graviton.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Some physicists think gravitons exist, but nobody has ever found one. It is one of the big open questions.
9. (mcq) Who translated Newton's great book on forces into French, with her own notes, so many more people could read it?  
   Options: Marie Curie / Émilie du Châtelet / Ada Lovelace / Kalpana Chawla  
   **Answer:** Émilie du Châtelet. *Why:* Émilie du Châtelet's translation was published in 1759, ten years after she died, and is still used. Picking Marie Curie is a common guess because she is the most famous woman scientist, but she lived over 100 years later.
10. (mcq) Inside each of your cells, tiny machines called motor proteins carry loads along thin tracks. What do they do to their loads?  
   Options: They pull them along, one tiny step at a time / They throw them / They melt them / They wait for gravity  
   **Answer:** They pull them along, one tiny step at a time. *Why:* Motor proteins walk along tracks and pull their loads behind them. Even inside a cell, moving things needs pushes and pulls.
11. (mcq) India is slowly pushing north into Asia. What has that push made over millions of years?  
   Options: The Himalayas / The Thar desert / The Bay of Bengal / The Deccan monsoon  
   **Answer:** The Himalayas. *Why:* The push of India into Asia folded the rocks up into the Himalayas, and they are still rising a little each year. Picking the desert or the sea mixes up pushing rock with wind and water.
12. (forces) A ping-pong ball is blown from the left and from below at the same time, equally hard. Which two pushes act on it?  
   Options: push from the left blow / push from the lower blow / magnet pull / rope pull  
   **Answer:** push from the left blow right, push from the lower blow up. *Why:* A blow from the left pushes the ball right. A blow from below pushes it up. Together the ball goes slantwise, up and to the right.
13. (write) Your muscles can only pull. So how can you push a door open? Explain.  
   **Answer:** My muscles pull on my bones. The bones swing like levers, and my hand at the end pushes the door. So a pull inside my body becomes a push outside it.. *Why:* A good answer says that muscles pull on bones, and the bones then press on the door.
14. (write) In a tug of war, the rope pulls both teams equally hard. Explain how one team can still win. Draw arrows if it helps.  
   **Answer:** The winning team pushes the ground harder with their feet, towards the middle. The ground pushes them back, away from the middle, harder than it pushes the other team. So the winners move back and drag the rope.. *Why:* This is the puzzle from the unit's story. The key is the push between the feet and the ground.
15. (write) Some books say you never really touch anything, because your atoms always stay a tiny gap apart. Do you agree? Give a reason.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer with a reason. For example: I don't agree, because touching just means being close enough to push. My hand and the wall push on each other, so they touch.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. It depends on what 'touch' means. What counts is a clear reason.
16. (write) Everything pulls everything with gravity. You are pulling on the Moon right now. Why can't you feel it?  
   **Answer:** Any answer with a reason. For example: my pull on the Moon is very small, because I am small and the Moon is far away. The Moon is so huge that my pull hardly changes how it moves.. *Why:* Gravity between two things is bigger when they are heavier and closer. A child and the Moon pull each other very weakly.
17. (draw) Draw yourself standing on the Earth. Draw an arrow for the Earth pulling you, and an arrow for you pulling the Earth. How long should each arrow be?  
   **Answer:** One arrow on the child pointing down towards the Earth, and one equal arrow on the Earth pointing up towards the child.. *Why:* The two pulls are a force pair, so they are the same size and point opposite ways. Each acts on a different thing.

   Options: Started it / Stopped it / Sped it up  
   **Answer:** Stopped it. *Why:* Her hands pushed against the ball's movement, so it stopped. Picking 'sped it up' means the push was pictured going the same way as the ball.

   Options: It stays about where it is / It races away / It goes slantwise

14. (mcq) Your muscles can…  
   Options: Only pull / Only push / Push and pull  
   **Answer:** Only pull. *Why:* Muscles only pull, by getting shorter. So they work in pairs. Picking 'push and pull' is natural, because we push doors. But muscles pull on bones, and the bones push.

   Options: The Earth pulls you down, and you pull the Earth up just as hard / Only the Earth pulls / You push the Earth away

## Applications
- Engineers draw force arrows to design bridges, buildings, cranes and lifts, so nothing bends or falls.
- Pilots and ship captains use arrows called vectors to add up the push of their engines and the push of the wind or the sea current.
- Weather maps show the wind as arrows. India's weather forecasters use them to follow the monsoon.
- Rockets, from Tipu Sultan's iron rockets to India's moon missions, move by pushing hot gas backwards.
- Boats are rowed and poled by pushing water or the river bed backwards.
- Doctors and physiotherapists study which muscles pull on which bones, to help people walk again after an injury.
- Lifts, cranes and well buckets use ropes and cables, which can only pull.
- Magnets pull and push in fridge doors, speakers, electric motors and some fast trains.
- Sports coaches study pushes: a sprinter pushes the blocks backwards, and the blocks push the sprinter forwards.
- Tug of war teams crouch low and push the ground, because the ground's push is what wins.
- Solar sails let spacecraft be pushed through space by sunlight, with no fuel.
- Video game designers use force arrows, called vectors, to make characters and balls move in a real way.

## Open questions
- **What is a force, really?** Scientists can measure forces almost perfectly. But whether a force is a real thing, a bend in space and time, or particles passing between things, is still argued about.
- **Is gravity carried by a particle?** The other basic forces are carried by particles. Many physicists think gravity is carried by a graviton, but nobody has found one. It may be far too weak to ever detect directly.
- **How does a pull cross empty space?** Newton refused to guess. Einstein said space itself bends. How that fits with quantum physics, the science of the very tiny, nobody yet knows.
- **What is pushing the universe apart?** In 1998, astronomers found that the universe is spreading out faster and faster. Something seems to push it apart. They call it dark energy, but nobody knows what it is.
- **Are there only four basic forces?** Physicists know of four: gravity, the electromagnetic force, and two inside atoms. Some experiments search for a fifth force. None has been confirmed.
- **Why do the four forces have the strengths they do?** Gravity is far, far weaker than the electric force. If the strengths were very different, stars and atoms might not exist. Nobody knows why they are what they are.
- **How exactly do the tiny motor proteins in our cells pull their loads so well?** Scientists can watch them take steps. How thousands of them work together in a living cell is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the Arrow Wars game, you played arrows of 3 and 2. Your friend played arrows of 4 and 4. Which way does the rope move, and by how many squares? **Towards your friend, by 3 squares.**. Your total is 3 + 2 = 5. Your friend's total is 4 + 4 = 8. The pulls point opposite ways, so take the smaller from the bigger. 8 take away 5 is 3. Your friend's pull is bigger, so the rope moves 3 squares towards your friend. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In the Push the Wall experiment, these were the three tries for a hard push: 130 cm, 150 cm and 140 cm. What is the middle value? **140 cm.**. Put the three numbers in order: 130, 140, 150. The middle one is 140. So the middle value is 140 cm. We use the middle value because one try can be a fluke. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- The balloon rocket went 90 cm with 3 breaths, 200 cm with 6 breaths and 300 cm with 9 breaths. Look at the chart. How much further did it go with 9 breaths than with 3 breaths? **210 cm further.**. 300 take away 90 is 210. So with 9 breaths, the balloon went 210 cm further than with 3 breaths. That is 2 metres and 10 centimetres. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In Arrow Spotting, you draw 1 centimetre of arrow for each unit of force. A push of 6 units and a push of 2 units act the same way on a cart. How long should the arrow for the total push be? **8 centimetres.**. The pushes point the same way, so they add up. 6 + 2 = 8 units. At 1 centimetre for each unit, the arrow is 8 centimetres long. Measure it with a ruler, starting from zero. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- In the Blow the Ball experiment, you blow from the bottom edge of the tray, and the ball rolls up. Then you move round a quarter turn and blow from the left edge. Which way does the ball roll now? **To the right.**. Blowing from the bottom pushes the ball up. Moving a quarter turn round to the left edge turns the push a quarter turn too, which is a right angle. A push from the left edge points to the right. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L3`)
- In the Gentle Tug of War, a child pulls with 4 units and a grown-up pulls the other way with 9 units. How much more must the child's team pull to make the forces balanced? **5 more units.**. Balanced means both sides pull equally. The grown-up pulls 9. The child pulls 4. 4 + ? = 9. The missing number is 5, because 9 take away 4 is 5. So the child's team needs 5 more units, perhaps from a friend. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)

- Name the five things a force can do to a moving thing. → Start it, stop it, speed it up, slow it down and change its direction.
- On a force arrow, what does a longer arrow mean? → A bigger force.
- Two equal pulls in opposite directions. Balanced or unbalanced? → Balanced. Nothing changes.
- You push a wall. What does the wall do? → It pushes you back, just as hard.
- Can a rope push? → No. A rope can only pull.
- Can a muscle push? → No. Muscles only pull.
- Name a force that works from far away. → Gravity, a magnet's pull, or the pull of a rubbed balloon.
- When you jump, do you pull the Earth? → Yes. You pull it up as hard as it pulls you down, but it barely moves.
- Why does a comet's tail point away from the Sun? → Sunlight and the solar wind push it outwards.
