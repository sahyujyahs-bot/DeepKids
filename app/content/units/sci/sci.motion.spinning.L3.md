# Spinning tops and wheels: why a spinning top stays up
`sci.motion.spinning.L3`

**Summary.** Stand a top on its point and let go, and it falls at once. Spin it, and it stands up on that same point. A spinning top keeps its axis, the line it turns around, pointing the same way. When gravity tries to tip it over, the lean slowly circles round instead. Friction at the tip slowly takes the spin away, so in the end every top wobbles and falls. Where the weight sits matters too. A top or wheel with its weight near the edge is harder to start turning, and it keeps turning longer. On a merry-go-round, the outside goes round a bigger circle than the middle, so it moves faster.

**Intro.** *Try this. Stand a top, or a pencil stuck through a cardboard circle, on its point. Let go. It falls over at once. Now spin it hard and let go. It stands on the same tiny point for many seconds. Why does spinning keep it up?* Children in India have played with tops for hundreds of years. In Tamil a string top is a bambaram, and in Hindi it is a lattu. In this unit you make your own top, time how long it spins, and find out what makes it spin longer. You also find out why wheels are round, and why the outside of a merry-go-round moves fastest.

## Terms
- **Spin**: To turn round and round on the spot. Scientists also call this rotating. *e.g.* A ceiling fan spins. It turns round and round but stays in the same place.
- **Axis**: The straight line that a spinning thing turns around. The axis itself does not go round. *e.g.* The axis of a top runs from its tip up through its handle.
- **Axle**: A real rod that a wheel turns on, or turns with. It sits along the wheel's axis. *e.g.* The two wheels of a cycle rickshaw's back seat turn on one long steel axle.
- **Rim**: The outside edge of a wheel or a disc. *e.g.* A cycle tyre sits on the steel rim of the wheel.
- **Hub**: The middle part of a wheel, where the axle goes through. *e.g.* The spokes of a cycle wheel run from the hub to the rim.
- **Friction**: The force between two surfaces that rub. It works against sliding and slows moving things down. *e.g.* A top stops spinning because its tip rubs on the floor.
- **Precession**: The slow circling of a spinning top's lean. The top's handle draws a slow circle in the air, instead of falling over. *e.g.* A leaning bambaram's head moves slowly round in a circle while the top spins fast.
- **Wobble**: When a slowing top leans more and more and circles round in big, uneven loops. *e.g.* Just before a top falls, it wobbles and makes a rattling sound.
- **Gyroscope**: A spinning wheel set in a frame, so that its axis can keep pointing the same way while the frame moves. *e.g.* Ships and spacecraft have used spinning gyroscopes to sense which way they are turning.
- **Flywheel**: A heavy wheel, with most of its weight near the rim, that keeps a machine turning smoothly between pushes. *e.g.* The big heavy wheel on a roadside sugarcane juice machine is a flywheel.
- **Merry-go-round**: A ride that turns round a middle pole, with seats near the middle and near the edge. *e.g.* At a mela, a merry-go-round takes the same time to go round for every rider.

## Explanations
### What spinning is · *defined*
**Definition:** Spinning, or rotating, is turning round and round about a straight line called the axis.

Every part of a spinning thing goes round in a circle, except the parts on the axis. Those parts stay where they are. Look at a spinning fan. The blades whirl round, but the little cap in the middle only turns on the spot. A wheel spins too, on a rod called an axle. When a wheel rolls along the road, it spins and moves forward at the same time.
- e.g. A ceiling fan spins about an axis that points up and down.
- e.g. A cycle wheel spins about its axle.
- e.g. A door turns about its hinges, so its axis runs down the hinge side.
### Why a still top falls over · *defined*
**Definition:** A top balanced on its point falls over because gravity pulls it down as soon as it leans even a tiny bit.

A top's point is very small. It can never stand exactly straight, so it always leans a little. The Earth's gravity pulls the top's weight down. Once it leans, that pull tips it further, and it falls. Nothing stops the fall.
- e.g. A pencil stood on its point falls at once.
- e.g. A still bambaram rolls onto its side as soon as you let go.
### Why a spinning top stays up · *defined*
**Definition:** A spinning top keeps its axis pointing the same way. When gravity tries to tip it, the lean slowly circles round instead of falling. This slow circling is called precession.

Every bit of a spinning top is racing round in a circle. To tip the top over, gravity would have to change the direction all those bits are moving. A fast top resists that change. So when the top leans, it does not fall. Instead its handle slowly moves round in a circle. You can see this when a top leans: its head draws slow circles in the air. The faster the top spins, the better it stays up. A spinning cycle wheel held by its axle does the same thing.
- e.g. A leaning bambaram circles slowly round instead of falling.
- e.g. A coin spun on its edge stands up while it spins, and falls when it stops.
- e.g. A spinning gyroscope can balance on the tip of a finger.
### Why every top falls in the end · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction at the tip and the push of the air slowly take a top's spin away. When it spins too slowly, it can no longer resist gravity, so it wobbles and falls.

A top's tip rubs on the floor. The air rubs on its sides too. Both are friction, and both slow the spin a little every second. As the top slows down, its lean circles round wider and wider. This is the wobble. Then it falls. On a smooth steel plate a top spins longer than on a rough floor, because there is less rubbing. On a towel it hardly spins at all.
- e.g. A top on a steel plate spins longer than on a carpet.
- e.g. A coin spun on a towel falls at once.
### Weight near the edge keeps a spin going · *defined*
**Definition:** The further a thing's weight is from its axis, the harder it is to start it turning, and the harder it is to stop it turning.

When a disc spins, weight near the edge goes round a big circle on every turn. Weight near the middle goes round a tiny circle. So the weight near the edge is moving much faster, and it carries more motion. The rubbing at the tip takes longer to use all of that up. That is why a top with coins taped near its edge spins longer than the same top with the same coins taped near its middle. It is also why a ring rolls down a slope more slowly than a solid round thing. More of the ring's motion goes into spinning.
- e.g. A potter's wheel has a heavy rim, so it keeps turning after the potter stops pushing.
- e.g. The big wheel on a sugarcane juice machine keeps the rollers turning smoothly.
- e.g. Spinning toy tops with a heavy metal ring round the edge spin for a long time.
### Why wheels are round · *defined*
**Definition:** Every point on the edge of a circle is the same distance from its middle. So a round wheel keeps its axle at the same height all the time as it rolls.

Roll a round lid along the floor, and its middle moves along in a straight, level line. A square wheel would lift its middle up and drop it down on every corner, so the ride would be very bumpy. Wheels also help because a rolling wheel does not slide on the road. Rolling has much less friction than dragging. The wheel turns on its axle, and grease on the axle makes the rubbing there small too. In one turn, a wheel rolls as far as the distance round its edge. A big wheel goes further in one turn than a small wheel.
- e.g. A suitcase with wheels is easier to pull than one without.
- e.g. A bullock cart has big wooden wheels on one axle.
- e.g. A cycle's big wheels go further in one turn than a toy car's small wheels.
### The outside of a merry-go-round goes fastest · *defined*
**Definition:** Every part of a merry-go-round takes the same time to go round once. The outside goes round a bigger circle, so it moves faster.

On a merry-go-round, a rider on an outside horse and a rider near the pole both go round once in the same time. But the outside rider travels round a much bigger circle. So the outside rider is moving faster. The middle pole only turns on the spot. Riders also feel thrown outwards. In fact their bodies are trying to keep going straight, and the seat keeps pulling them round in a circle. If a rider let go, they would fly off in a straight line, not straight outwards.
- e.g. On a giant wheel at a mela, the seats go round a big circle while the middle only turns.
- e.g. When children walk round in a ring holding a dupatta, the child at the end has to run.
### Does a bicycle stay up because its wheels spin? · *debated*
Many people think a moving cycle stays up because its spinning wheels act like tops. The spinning wheels do help a little. But in 2011, a team of engineers in the Netherlands and the USA built a special bicycle whose spinning effects cancelled out. When pushed along without a rider, it still balanced itself. So spinning wheels are not the main reason. Most of the balance comes from steering: when a cycle starts to lean, the front wheel turns towards the lean and brings the cycle back under the rider. Scientists still study exactly how all the parts work together.
- e.g. A rider stays up by steering into a lean, even when riding slowly.
- e.g. A cycle pushed along without a rider rolls upright for a while, then falls when it slows.
- **The common view:** The spinning wheels act like tops and hold the cycle upright.
- **The 2011 bicycle test (Kooijman, Schwab, Papadopoulos, Meijaard and Ruina):** A bicycle built so its wheels' spinning effects cancel can still balance itself. The shape of the cycle and how the front wheel steers matter more.
- **Most cycle scientists today:** Spin helps a little. Steering into the lean does most of the work, and the rider does a lot of it without thinking.

## Mostly agreed
- A spinning thing keeps spinning until friction or another force slows it down. *How we know:* Tops spin longer on smooth steel than on cloth. The Earth, with almost nothing to rub against in space, has been spinning for billions of years.
- A fast-spinning top or gyroscope keeps its axis pointing the same way, and a lean makes it circle slowly instead of fall. *How we know:* Anyone can watch a leaning top circle. Gyroscopes in ships, aircraft and spacecraft have used this for over a hundred years.
- Weight far from the axis makes a thing harder to start spinning and harder to stop. *How we know:* A ring rolls down a slope more slowly than a solid disc. Flywheels with heavy rims keep machines turning smoothly. Physicists measure and calculate this exactly.
- Every part of a turning thing takes the same time to go round, so the parts further out move faster. *How we know:* On a merry-go-round, an outside rider and an inside rider pass the starting point together every turn, but the outside rider goes round a longer path.
- The Earth's axis slowly circles like the lean of a top, once in about 26,000 years. *How we know:* Astronomers since Hipparchus, about 2,100 years ago, have seen the stars slowly shift. Today it is measured very exactly.

## How we found out
**Jean Buridan, a French teacher at the University of Paris, and later Isaac Newton, an English scientist, Around 1350 (Buridan) and 1687 (Newton's laws of motion).** For about two thousand years, many teachers in Europe and the Middle East followed Aristotle, a Greek thinker. He taught that a moving thing needs something pushing it all the time. So why does a thrown stone keep flying after it leaves your hand? Many of his followers said the air rushes round behind it and pushes it along. Around 1350, Jean Buridan, a teacher at the University of Paris, asked a simple question about a spinning top and a smith's grinding wheel. Both keep turning for a long time after the push. Yet neither one moves from its place. There is no gap behind them for air to rush into. So the air cannot be what keeps them going. Buridan said the push puts something into the moving thing itself. He called it impetus. He said it would last for ever, if the air and the rubbing did not slowly take it away. About 300 years later, Isaac Newton made this into his first law of motion: a moving thing keeps moving until a force changes it.  
*What people believed before:* People thought moving things need a push all the time, and that the air pushes a thrown stone along. Buridan pointed out that a spinning top keeps turning in one place, where air cannot rush in behind it. So the motion must be carried by the top itself.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A heavier top always spins longer.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Heavy tops with metal rims often spin a long time.  
  Against: In the coin top experiment, the same coins spun much longer at the edge than at the middle. The weight was the same. Where the weight sat made the difference.
- **A spinning top stays up because the air holds it up.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Spinning things push the air around them.  
  Against: Air rubs on a spinning top and slows it down. A top spins longest where the air and the floor rub on it least. It stays up because of its own spin, not because of the air.
- **A bicycle stays up mainly because its spinning wheels act like tops.** (A very common idea, even in some books; *rejected*)  
  For: Spinning wheels do resist tipping a little.  
  Against: In 2011 engineers built a bicycle whose spinning effects cancelled out, and it still balanced itself when pushed. Steering into the lean matters more.
- **A spinning gyroscope weighs less than a still one.** (Eric Laithwaite, a British engineer, in a famous lecture in London in 1974; *rejected*)  
  For: A heavy spinning gyroscope feels surprisingly easy to lift and swing round on the end of a rod.  
  Against: Careful weighing shows a spinning gyroscope weighs exactly the same as a still one. It feels easier only because of the way its spin makes it move.
- **On a merry-go-round, a force throws you straight outwards.** (How it feels to almost everyone; *debated*)  
  For: You feel pressed against the outside of your seat.  
  Against: If you let go, you would fly off in a straight line along the edge of the circle, not straight outwards. Your body is trying to keep going straight, and the seat pulls you round. Physicists say both descriptions can be used, if you are careful about who is watching.

## Roots
We have learned why a spinning top stays up and why weight near the edge keeps it spinning. Now we ask what spinning tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your top spun longer with four coins at its edge than with the same coins at its middle. The coins at the edge raced round a bigger circle on every turn. Faster coins carry more motion, so the rubbing at the tip took longer to use it up. So a turning thing keeps going longest when its weight sits far from its middle.  
*Habit it breaks:* We usually think of weight as one number: a thing is heavy or light. But the same four coins made the top spin twice as long, or half as long, depending on where they were taped. How a thing moves depends on how its weight is spread, not only on how much there is.  
*Evidence:* In the coin top test, the edge top spun clearly longer than the middle top, with the same card, pencil and coins. In the rolling race, a tape roll with its weight at the edge lost to a solid battery.

- *who am i:* Your head turns to say 'no' on a neck bone that doctors call the axis. You carry a wheel and axle in your own neck.
- *what is life:* Some bacteria swim by spinning a long thin tail. A tiny motor in their body turns it round, like a wheel on an axle.
- *what is this world:* The Earth spins once a day. Its axis slowly circles round like the lean of a top, once in about 26,000 years.

At the middle of every spinning top is a line that does not go round: the axis. Around it, every bit of the top races in its own circle, small near the middle and big at the edge. One still line holds all those different circles together. The many moving parts are one top because they all turn around one middle.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes meet at one hub. It is the empty space in the middle that makes the wheel useful. (A wheel's hub is where the axle goes. The middle of a wheel hardly moves, yet the whole wheel turns around it.)
- **Rig Veda 1.164.11:** The wheel of order, with twelve spokes, rolls round the sky and never wears out. (The old poets saw the turning of the year as a wheel. Today we know the Earth itself spins and circles the Sun, and it has kept turning for billions of years because almost nothing in space rubs it to a stop.)
- **The Buddha's first teaching, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11):** The Buddha's first teaching is called 'setting the wheel of the teaching rolling'. The wheel with 24 spokes on India's flag comes from the same idea, through the Ashoka Chakra. (Once a wheel is set rolling, it keeps going on its own until something slows it. Buridan said the same about a spinning top.)
- Science (settled): A spinning thing keeps its spin, and keeps its axis pointing the same way, unless a twisting force changes it. Physicists call this conservation of angular momentum.
- Science (settled): Many bacteria swim using a tail turned by a real rotary motor in the outer layers of their cell, one of the smallest motors known.
- Science (settled): In 1918 Emmy Noether, a German mathematician, proved that spin is kept because the laws of nature are the same in every direction.
- Other view, Many physicists: A top stays up because of a law of motion, and there is nothing more to it. The still middle of a wheel is a fact of geometry. Reading a lesson about oneness into it says more about us than about tops.

Breathe in slowly as it spins. Breathe out slowly. Keep watching until it wobbles and falls. Then sit still for three more breaths.

## Story: The Bambaram on the Terrace
*Bambarams, string tops with a metal tip, are really played across Tamil Nadu, and lattus across North India. Plastic tops with heavy metal rings really are sold as battle tops. Meenakshi, Rohan, Rajam Paati and this contest are made up.*

It was the first week of the May holidays in Madurai. The terrace was still cool, because the sun had only just come up over the water tank.

Meenakshi was nine. She was the best bambaram spinner on her street, and she knew it. Her grandmother, Rajam Paati, had taught her. Paati had been the best on her street too, sixty years ago.

Meenakshi's cousin Rohan had come from Delhi for the holidays. He was eleven, and he had brought a top of his own. It was bright blue plastic, with a shiny metal ring round its edge. It came with a launcher. You pulled a toothed strip through it, and the top shot out spinning.

"Mine will beat yours," said Rohan. "It's heavier. And the launcher spins it faster than any string."

"We'll see," said Meenakshi.

Paati came up the stairs with her tumbler of coffee and sat on the low wall. "A contest," she said. "Good. The top that spins longest wins."

First, Meenakshi wanted to show Rohan something. She took her wooden bambaram. It was shaped like a pear, painted red and yellow, with an iron nail for a tip. She stood it on the floor on its point, held it straight, and let go.

It fell over at once and rolled in a little half-circle.

"Of course it falls," said Rohan. "It's standing on a nail."

"Then watch this," said Meenakshi. She wound the string tightly round the bambaram, from the tip upwards. She held the end of the string between two fingers. Then she threw the top down hard and pulled back. The string unwound and the bambaram landed spinning on its iron point.

It stood up straight. It hummed. It stood on the same nail it had just fallen off.

Rohan crouched down to look. "Why doesn't it fall now?"

"Look at its head," said Meenakshi. "Watch carefully."

The bambaram was leaning a tiny bit. But it was not falling. Its head was moving slowly round in a circle, like the hand of a slow clock. The body of the top was a blur, but the lean went round and round, calmly.

"When a still top leans, it falls," said Meenakshi. "When a spinning top leans, the lean just goes round. Paati says it walks round instead of falling."

The bambaram slowed down. Its humming dropped lower. The slow circles of its head grew wider and wider. It began to wobble, rattling on its nail. Then it tipped over and rolled across the floor.

"Twenty-six seconds," said Paati, who had been counting on her fingers. "Now Rohan."

Rohan clicked his blue top into the launcher, held it low over the floor and pulled the strip. Zzzip! The top shot out and spun. It spun, and spun, and spun. Meenakshi counted. At twenty-six it was still spinning. At forty it was still standing. It fell at fifty-one.

"Told you," said Rohan. "Heavier wins."

Meenakshi picked up his top. It was heavier than hers. But it was also a different shape. Her bambaram was fat in the middle and pointed at the bottom. Rohan's top was flat and wide, and almost all its weight was in the metal ring round its edge.

"Is it heavier," she asked, "or is it the ring?"

Paati smiled and put down her coffee. "Go and ask Selvi downstairs if you may look at her mother's old pot wheel."

Selvi's family had made clay pots for three generations. In the corner of their yard stood an old potter's wheel. It was a big flat stone wheel, thick and heavy all round its edge. Meenakshi pushed a stick into a hole near its rim and gave it one hard turn. Then she let go.

The wheel kept turning. It turned slowly and steadily for a long, long time, even though nobody was touching it.

"The weight is all at the edge," Meenakshi said slowly. "Like Rohan's ring."

Back on the terrace, she had an idea. She asked Paati for four one-rupee coins and some cello tape. Rohan did not want his top stuck with tape, so they made two tops instead. They cut two circles from a sweet box and pushed a short pencil through the middle of each one. On one top, they taped the four coins close to the pencil. On the other, they taped the same four coins near the edge.

"Same card, same pencil, same coins," said Meenakshi. "Only the place is different."

They spun each top three times, as hard as they could. The top with the coins near the middle never got past ten seconds. The top with the coins near the edge spun for more than twenty seconds every time.

Rohan stared at them. "So it's not that mine is heavier. It's where the heavy part is."

"The coins at the edge go round a big circle every time the top turns," said Meenakshi. "They are moving fast. It takes the floor longer to rub all that spinning away."

Paati nodded. "And as long as it keeps spinning, it keeps standing."

That afternoon, Rohan asked Meenakshi to teach him the string. He wound it too loosely, and the bambaram flopped onto its side. He wound it again, tighter. On his ninth try, the bambaram landed on its point and hummed.

He watched its head draw slow circles, and he did not say anything for a long time.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a still top falls because gravity tips it as soon as it leans. A spinning top keeps its axis pointing the same way, so when gravity tries to tip it, the lean slowly circles round instead. This circling is called precession. Friction at the tip slows the spin, the circles grow into a wobble, and the top falls. Rohan's top spun longer mainly because its weight was in a ring at the edge. Weight near the edge goes round a bigger circle, moves faster and carries more motion, so the floor takes longer to rub the spin away. The potter's wheel works the same way.

- Ask: Why did the bambaram fall over when Meenakshi stood it still on its point?
- Ask: What did the head of the spinning bambaram do when it leaned?
- Ask: Why did Meenakshi want the two cardboard tops to have the same card, the same pencil and the same coins?
- Ask: What did the potter's wheel help Meenakshi understand about Rohan's top?
- Ask: Rohan was sure that heavier wins. What changed his mind?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Coin Top: edge or middle? (35 min)
- **question:** Will a top spin longer with its coins near the edge, or with the same coins near the middle?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will tape four coins near the edge of a top, and then the same four coins near the middle. Which top will spin longer? Or will they spin the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same card, the same pencil and the same four coins both times. Spin on the same steel plate, the same way, as hard as you can. Only the place of the coins changes. Spin each top 3 times, because one spin can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the card and makes the hole in the middle with the pencil point. Keep coins away from children under 5.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both tops from above, showing where the coins were. Circle the one that spun longest.
- **results table:** Where the coins are | Spin 1 (seconds) | Spin 2 (seconds) | Spin 3 (seconds) | Longest spin
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw round the katori on the card and cut out the circle. 2. To find the middle, cut a paper circle the same size. Fold it in half, then in half again. The corner of the fold is the middle. 3. Lay it on the card and poke the middle with the pencil point. 4. Push the pencil through, so about 2 cm pokes out below. 5. Tape it firmly on both sides.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make a top. The pencil must go exactly through the middle, or the top will wobble.  
  *Child mode:* Draw round the katori and cut out the circle. Find the exact middle. Push the pencil through it. Tape it tight.
  - Note: An off-centre pencil makes the top wobble and fall fast, so take care finding the middle. If the card bends, glue on a second layer. Spin it once without coins to check it works. It should stand and spin for a few seconds.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, and to give a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We'll tape four coins near the edge, then move the same coins near the middle. Which top will spin longer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the top spin longer with coins at the edge, or in the middle? Why?
  - Note: Write down the guess and the reason before testing. Many children guess the middle, because it feels more balanced. Any reason is fine. The test will answer it.
  - Check: What will you change in this test? → **Where the coins are taped**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the four coins flat near the edge of the card. Space them evenly, like the 12, 3, 6 and 9 on a clock.  
  *Parent says:* Let's put the coins near the edge. They must be evenly spaced, or the top will be lopsided and wobble.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the four coins near the edge. Put them where 12, 3, 6 and 9 are on a clock.
  - Note: Coins spaced unevenly make the top wobble. Check by balancing the top on a finger under the pencil point. It should sit fairly level.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the top of the pencil between thumb and finger, with the point on the steel plate. 2. Twist hard and let go. 3. Start the stopwatch as you let go, and stop it when the card touches the plate. 4. Do 3 spins and write each time in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Twist as hard as you can, the same way every time. I'll time it. We stop the clock when the card touches the plate.  
  *Child mode:* Twist the pencil hard and let go. Time it until the card touches the plate. Do it 3 times.
  - Note: If the top skates off the plate, tilt the plate edges up a little, or use a big plate. A spin that hits the side does not count, so do it again. Watch the top of the pencil while it spins. When it leans, it circles slowly round. Point this out.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Peel the coins off. 2. Tape the same four coins flat around the pencil, touching it. 3. Spin 3 times, the same way, and write each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same coins go next to the pencil. Everything else stays the same.  
  *Child mode:* Move the coins next to the pencil. Spin it 3 times again. Write down the times.
  - Check: Your top spun longer with the coins near the edge. Why? → **The edge coins went round a bigger circle, so they moved faster and carried more motion**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Compare the longest spin of each top.  
  *Parent says:* Which top spun longer? How much longer? Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Which top spun longer? Was your guess right?
  - Note: Most families find the edge top spins clearly longer, often about twice as long. Spins can vary a lot, which is why we do three. If the two are close, do three more spins of each.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a top spins longer with its weight near the edge. What I saw: with the coins near the edge, it spun for 22, 25 and 19 seconds. Near the middle it spun for 11, 9 and 12 seconds. Why: the edge coins go round a bigger circle, so they move faster and carry more motion, and the rubbing at the tip takes longer to stop them.
### e2 · The Spinning Coin: which floor lets it spin longest? (25 min)
- **question:** Does a spinning coin spin longer on some surfaces than on others?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will spin a coin on a steel plate, a tiled floor, a sheet of newspaper and a towel. Put them in order, from longest spin to shortest. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same coin and spin it the same way each time. Only the surface changes. Spin 3 times on each surface, because one spin can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Keep coins away from children under 5.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar for the longest spin on each surface.
- **results table:** Surface | Spin 1 (seconds) | Spin 2 (seconds) | Spin 3 (seconds) | Longest spin
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the coin on its edge on the steel plate. 2. Hold it lightly with one finger on top. 3. Flick its side hard with a finger of the other hand. Practise a few times.  
  *Parent says:* Let's learn to spin a coin. Hold it upright with one finger, then flick its side hard.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the coin on its edge. Hold it with one finger. Flick its side hard. Practise!
  - Note: It takes a few tries to get a good spin. A big coin is easier.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Which surface will let the coin spin longest? Which will be shortest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which surface will let the coin spin longest? Which shortest? Why?
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spin the coin 3 times on each surface: the steel plate, the floor, the newspaper and the towel. 2. Time each spin, from the flick until the coin lies flat. 3. Write each time in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Time each spin from the flick until the coin lies flat. Listen to it as it slows down.  
  *Child mode:* Spin the coin 3 times on each surface. Time each spin. Listen as it slows down.
  - Note: On the towel, the coin may not spin at all. Write 0 seconds, which is a real result. At the end of a good spin on steel, the coin whirrs faster and faster as it settles flat. Its edge rolls round quicker and quicker as it tips over.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the times.  
  *Parent says:* Where did it spin longest? Where did it hardly spin? Why do you think the towel stopped it so fast?  
  *Child mode:* Where did it spin longest? Why did the towel stop it?
  - Check: Why does a coin spin longest on a smooth steel plate? → **There is less friction between the coin and the smooth steel, so less spin is rubbed away**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer: the coin spins longest on a smooth hard surface. On steel it spun up to 9 seconds, and on the towel 0 seconds. A rough or soft surface rubs more, so friction stops the spin sooner.
### e3 · The Rolling Race: ring or solid? (25 min)
- **question:** If a ring and a solid round thing roll down the same slope together, which reaches the bottom first?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will race a roll of cello tape against a battery down a tray. Which will win, or will it be a tie? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same slope. Hold both at the same starting line and let go at the same moment. Race them 5 times, and swap sides each time, in case one side of the tray is smoother.
- **safety:** Use a metal or plastic bangle, never glass.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the ring and the solid round thing, and shade where their weight is.
- **results table:** Race | Winner | Which side was the tape on?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift one end of the tray onto 2 or 3 books. 2. Lay the ruler across the top as a starting gate. 3. Put the tape roll and the battery side by side behind the ruler.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make a slope and a starting gate. The ruler holds both until we lift it.  
  *Child mode:* Put the tray on some books to make a slope. Hold the tape and battery behind a ruler.
  - Note: A gentle slope is best. If they roll too fast to see, take away a book.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Which will reach the bottom first, the tape or the battery? Or will they tie? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which will win, the tape or the battery? Why?
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the ruler straight up, quickly. 2. Watch which reaches the bottom first. 3. Swap their sides and race again. 4. Race 5 times and write each winner.  
  *Parent says:* Lift the ruler fast so both start together. Watch the bottom of the slope.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the ruler quickly. Which one gets to the bottom first? Race 5 times.
  - Note: The solid battery usually wins, by a few centimetres on a short tray. If one rolls off sideways, start again. The tape roll has most of its weight far from its middle, like the edge coins in the coin top. More of its motion goes into spinning, so it rolls forward more slowly.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the result.  
  *Parent says:* The battery won almost every time. The tape roll has its weight near its edge. Remember the coin top? What does weight near the edge do?  
  *Child mode:* Which won most races? Where is the tape roll's weight? Think of the coin top.
  - Check: Why does the tape roll lose the race? → **Its weight is near its edge, so it is harder to get spinning, and it rolls forward more slowly**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer: a solid round thing rolls down faster than a ring. The battery won 5 races out of 5. The ring's weight is near its edge, so it is harder to get spinning.

## Worksheets
### Spin Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Lakshmi stands a top on its point without spinning it and lets go. What happens?  
   Options: It stands up for a long time / It falls over at once / It starts spinning by itself  
   **Answer:** It falls over at once. *Why:* A still top always leans a tiny bit, and gravity tips it over as soon as it leans. Only spinning keeps it up. A strong answer says why: gravity pulls the leaning top down.
2. (mcq) What is the axis of a spinning top?  
   Options: The straight line it turns around / The string you spin it with / The circle its edge makes  
   **Answer:** The straight line it turns around. *Why:* The axis is the line a spinning thing turns around. For a top, it runs from its tip up through its handle. The string only starts the spin.
3. (mcq) Meera spins a coin on a towel and on a steel plate. Where will it spin longer?  
   Options: On the towel, because it is soft / On the steel plate, because it rubs less / The same on both  
   **Answer:** On the steel plate, because it rubs less. *Why:* Smooth hard steel causes less friction than soft cloth. The coin sinks into a towel, which rubs its spin away almost at once.
4. (mcq) A spinning top leans a little. What does its head do?  
   Options: It falls straight down / It moves slowly round in a circle / It stays perfectly still  
   **Answer:** It moves slowly round in a circle. *Why:* A spinning top's lean slowly circles round instead of falling. This is called precession. The head of the top draws a slow circle in the air.
5. (mcq) Why are wheels round?  
   Options: Round things are prettier / Every point on the edge is the same distance from the middle, so the axle stays at one height / Round wheels are lighter  
   **Answer:** Every point on the edge is the same distance from the middle, so the axle stays at one height. *Why:* Whichever part of a round wheel touches the road, the axle stays the same height. So the ride is smooth. A square wheel bumps up and down at every corner.
6. (truefalse) Divya says, 'A top falls in the end because friction slows its spin.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Friction at the tip and the rub of the air slowly take the spin away. When it spins too slowly, it wobbles and falls. A strong answer names where the rubbing happens.
7. (truefalse) Farhan says, 'On a merry-go-round, the rider near the pole goes round faster than the rider on the outside.' Is he right?

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Anjali stands a top on its point without spinning it. Why does it fall over?  
   Options: Gravity tips it as soon as it leans / The floor pushes it over / It is too light  
   **Answer:** Gravity tips it as soon as it leans. *Why:* A still top always leans a little, and gravity pulls it over. Picking 'the floor pushes it' mixes up the cause: the floor only holds the tip.
2. (mcq) What do we call the line a spinning thing turns around?  
   Options: The rim / The axis / The hub  
   **Answer:** The axis. *Why:* The axis is the line a spinning thing turns around. The rim is the outside edge, and the hub is the middle part of a wheel.
3. (mcq) A leaning top keeps spinning. What does its head do?  
   Options: Falls at once / Circles slowly round / Jumps up and down  
   **Answer:** Circles slowly round. *Why:* The lean of a spinning top circles slowly round. This is called precession.
4. (mcq) Sana tapes the same four coins near the edge of a card top, and then near its middle. Which way does it usually spin longer?  
   Options: With the coins near the edge / With the coins near the middle / No difference  
   **Answer:** With the coins near the edge. *Why:* Edge coins go round a bigger circle each turn, so they move faster and carry more motion. The rubbing at the tip takes longer to use it up.
5. (mcq) Why does a top spin longer on a steel plate than on a carpet?  
   Options: Steel is shiny / There is less friction at the tip on smooth steel / Carpets are warmer  
   **Answer:** There is less friction at the tip on smooth steel. *Why:* Smooth, hard steel rubs less than soft carpet, so less spin is taken away each second.
6. (mcq) Why does a top always fall in the end?  
   Options: Friction slowly takes its spin away / It gets tired of spinning / Gravity gets stronger  
   **Answer:** Friction slowly takes its spin away. *Why:* Friction at the tip and in the air slows the top down. When it spins too slowly, it wobbles and falls.
7. (mcq) Why is a wheel round?  
   Options: So its axle stays at the same height as it rolls / So it looks nice / So it is lighter  
   **Answer:** So its axle stays at the same height as it rolls. *Why:* Every point on the edge of a circle is the same distance from the middle, so the axle never goes up and down.
8. (mcq) What is an axle?  
   Options: The rod a wheel turns on / The tyre on a wheel / The edge of a wheel  
   **Answer:** The rod a wheel turns on. *Why:* An axle is the rod through the middle of a wheel. The wheel turns on it, or with it.
9. (mcq) On a merry-go-round, Priya sits on the outside and Ravi sits near the pole. Who moves faster?  
   Options: Ravi / Priya / Both the same  
   **Answer:** Priya. *Why:* They go round once in the same time, but Priya's circle is much bigger, so she moves faster.
10. (mcq) A ring and a solid disc roll down the same slope together. Which usually reaches the bottom first?  
   Options: The ring / The solid disc / They always tie  
   **Answer:** The solid disc. *Why:* The ring's weight is near its edge, so it is harder to get spinning, and it rolls forward more slowly.
11. (mcq) Which of these is a flywheel?  
   Options: The heavy wheel on a sugarcane juice machine / A cycle bell / A bottle cap  
   **Answer:** The heavy wheel on a sugarcane juice machine. *Why:* A flywheel is a heavy wheel with weight near its rim that keeps a machine turning smoothly.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how does a riderless bicycle balance itself?** Engineers showed in 2011 that it does not need spinning wheels. They can calculate it for some bicycle shapes, but there is still no simple rule that explains every bicycle.
- **Why does a spinning thing keep its axis pointing the same way at all?** Emmy Noether proved in 1918 that spin is kept because the laws of nature are the same in every direction. But why the laws are the same in every direction, nobody knows.
- **Why does the Earth spin at all?** Scientists think it comes from the spinning cloud of gas and dust that the Sun and planets formed from. The details of how each planet got its own spin are still studied.

## Connected maths
- In the coin top test, Kavya's top spun for 24 seconds with the coins near the edge, and 12 seconds with the coins near the middle. How many times as long did the edge top spin? **2 times as long.**. 24 divided by 12 is 2. So the edge top spun twice as long. Check: 12 + 12 = 24. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Arjun spun his edge top 3 times: 19 seconds, 25 seconds and 22 seconds. Put the times in order. Which is the middle time? **19, 22, 25. The middle time is 22 seconds.**. In order from shortest to longest: 19, 22, 25. The one in the middle is 22. Scientists often use the middle time, because one very lucky or unlucky spin does not change it. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- A steel plate rolls 78 cm in one turn. How far does it roll in 3 turns? **234 cm.**. 78 + 78 + 78 = 234. Or 78 times 3 is 234. That is a little more than 2 metres. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the dupatta merry-go-round, Amma near the middle took 4 steps to go round once. Meenu at the end took 20 steps in the same time. How many times as far did Meenu walk? **5 times as far.**. 20 divided by 4 is 5. They took the same time, so Meenu also walked 5 times as fast. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- A merry-go-round at a Madurai mela goes round once every 10 seconds. How many times does it go round in 1 minute? **6 times.**. 1 minute is 60 seconds. 60 divided by 10 is 6. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Look at the coin spinning results: steel plate 9 seconds, tiled floor 7 seconds, newspaper 4 seconds, towel 0 seconds. How many seconds longer did the coin spin on steel than on newspaper? What pattern do you see? **5 seconds longer. The smoother and harder the surface, the longer the spin.**. 9 take away 4 is 5. The times go down from steel to floor to newspaper to towel, as the surfaces get rougher and softer. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
