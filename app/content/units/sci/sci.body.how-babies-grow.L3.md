# How a baby grows: from one cell to birth
`sci.body.how-babies-grow.L3`

**Summary.** Every person began as one cell, smaller than a grain of salt. This cell was made when a cell from each parent joined. That cell split into two, then four, then eight, and on and on. For about nine months the baby grew in their mother's womb, a stretchy bag of muscle. They floated in warm water and got food and oxygen from her blood, through a cord. At birth they took their first breath, and the cord left a belly button. Scientists still do not fully know how each cell knows what to become.

**Intro.** *Look at your hand. Spread your fingers wide. Once, all of you, your hands, eyes, bones and brain, was one single cell. That cell was smaller than the full stop at the end of this sentence. Where did all the rest of you come from?* Put a grain of salt on a dark plate and look at it closely. You began even smaller than that, as one cell. A cell is the smallest living building block of a body. Now you are made of about 30 trillion cells. Every one of them came from that first cell.

## Terms
- **Cell**: The smallest living building block of a body. Your body is made of trillions of them. *e.g.* Your skin, your blood and your bones are all made of cells, far too small to see with your eyes.
- **Trillion**: A million millions. Written as 1 followed by 12 zeros. *e.g.* Your body has about 30 to 40 trillion cells. That is about 4,000 cells for every person on Earth.
- **Egg cell**: A special cell from the mother's body. A baby starts when this cell joins with a cell from the father's body, called a sperm cell. *e.g.* A human egg cell is about as wide as a hair. That is much smaller than a hen's egg.
- **Fertilisation**: The moment a cell from each parent joins and makes one new cell. That new cell is the very start of a baby. *e.g.* After fertilisation, the new cell has a mix of instructions from both parents.
- **Embryo**: The name for a baby in their first eight weeks of growing, while their body parts are first forming. *e.g.* At six weeks, the embryo is about as long as a grain of rice, and their heart is already beating.
- **Foetus**: The name for the growing baby from about nine weeks until they are born. It is said 'fee-tus'. *e.g.* At five months, the foetus can kick, suck their thumb and get hiccups.
- **Womb**: A stretchy bag made of strong muscle inside the mother's tummy, where a baby grows. Doctors call it the uterus. *e.g.* Before a baby, the womb is about the size of a small pear. By the end, it holds a whole baby.
- **Placenta**: A soft, flat, round body part that grows on the wall of the womb. It passes food and oxygen from the mother's blood to the baby. *e.g.* If the placenta does not work well, the baby gets less food and grows more slowly.
- **Umbilical cord**: A soft tube that joins the baby to the placenta. Food and oxygen travel along it to the baby. *e.g.* When the cord is cut after birth, the place where it joined becomes your belly button.
- **Amniotic fluid**: The warm water inside the womb that the baby floats in. It cushions and protects the baby. *e.g.* If the mother bumps into a table, the water spreads out the bump, so the baby is hardly jolted.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air that every cell needs to stay alive. We breathe it in. *e.g.* Before birth, the baby gets oxygen from the mother's blood, through the cord. After birth, the baby breathes air.
- **Pregnant**: When a baby is growing inside a person's womb, we say that person is pregnant. *e.g.* A person is usually pregnant for about nine months before the baby is born.
- **Ultrasound**: A machine that uses sounds too high for us to hear to make a picture of the baby inside the womb. *e.g.* Doctors move a smooth tool over the mother's tummy and the baby appears on a screen.
- **Birth**: The moment the baby comes out of the mother's body into the world. *e.g.* Most babies are born after about nine months, or 40 weeks.
- **Contraction**: When the muscles of the womb squeeze tight and then relax. Contractions slowly push the baby out at birth. *e.g.* It is a bit like squeezing toothpaste out of a tube, slowly, many times.
- **Caesarean birth**: A way of being born where doctors carefully open the mother's tummy and womb and lift the baby out. It is said 'si-zair-ee-an'. *e.g.* About one in five babies in India is born this way. It is just as much a birth as any other.
- **Newborn**: A baby in their first few weeks after birth. *e.g.* A newborn sees best things about 20 to 30 centimetres away. That is the distance to the face of the person holding them.
- **Premature**: Born early, before about 37 weeks. Premature babies often need extra warmth and care in hospital. *e.g.* A premature baby may sleep in a warm, see-through box called an incubator.
- **Adoption**: When a child grows up in a family that did not give birth to them, and becomes part of that family for ever. *e.g.* An adopted child grew in one mother's womb. They are loved and raised by their adoptive family.
- **DNA**: The instruction book inside every cell. It tells the cell how to grow and what to do. *e.g.* Half your DNA came from the egg cell and half from the sperm cell.

## Explanations
### Everyone begins as one cell · *defined*
**Definition:** Every person begins as one single cell. This cell is made when a cell from the mother and a cell from the father join together.

A cell is the smallest living building block of a body. The first cell of a baby is about as wide as a hair. This cell is hard to see without a microscope. Inside this cell is a mix of instructions from both parents. This is why children often look a little like both of their birth parents. That one cell holds everything needed to start building a whole person.
- e.g. You, your parents, your grandparents and your friends all began as one cell.
- e.g. An elephant and a mouse also each began as one cell.
- e.g. A mango tree began as one cell too, inside the flower that became the seed.
### One cell becomes many · *defined*
**Definition:** The first cell splits into two cells. Each of those splits again, and again. This is called cell division, and it is how a body grows.

About a day after being made, the first cell splits into two. Two become four, and four become eight. For the first five days or so, the cells get smaller with each split, and the ball of cells stays the same size. Then the ball of cells settles into the wall of the womb. Now food from the mother's blood reaches them, and the cells grow between splits. A grown-up has about 30 to 40 trillion cells. Every one came from that first cell.
- e.g. 1 cell splits into 2, then 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64.
- e.g. After ten rounds of splitting, there are 1,024 cells.
- e.g. Your body still makes new cells every day, to heal a cut or grow your hair.
### Cells become different kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** As the cells multiply, they start to become different kinds: skin cells, bone cells, heart cells, brain cells and many more. This is called specialising.

At first, all the cells look the same. Then they begin to change. Each cell has the same instruction book, called DNA. But each one reads different pages of it. A cell that reads the 'heart' pages becomes a heart cell and starts to beat. A cell that reads the 'skin' pages becomes skin. Scientists understand a lot about this. But they are still finding out exactly how each cell knows where they are and what to become.
- e.g. Heart cells beat by themselves, even in a dish in a laboratory.
- e.g. Nerve cells grow long thin arms to carry messages.
- e.g. Red blood cells are flat discs, thinner in the middle. This shape helps them carry oxygen and squeeze through tiny tubes.
### The womb: the baby's first home · *defined*
**Definition:** The womb is a stretchy bag of strong muscle inside the mother's tummy, below her stomach. The baby grows inside it for about nine months.

Before a baby, the womb is about the size of a small pear. As the baby grows, the womb stretches and stretches. By the end it can hold a baby about 50 centimetres long. Inside the womb, the baby floats in a bag of warm water. It is dark, warm and full of sounds, like the mother's heartbeat and her voice. The womb is made of muscle, so at birth it can squeeze to push the baby out.
- e.g. Before a baby, the womb is the size of a small pear. By the end, it is bigger than a football.
- e.g. From about six months, the baby can hear their mother's heartbeat, all day long.
- e.g. Light reaches the baby only a little, like sunlight through your fingers.
### The placenta and the cord: food and air · *defined*
**Definition:** The placenta grows on the wall of the womb. It takes food and oxygen from the mother's blood and passes them along the umbilical cord to the baby. It also takes the baby's waste away.

A baby in the womb is surrounded by water. They cannot eat, and there is no air to breathe. So the mother's body feeds them. Her blood carries food and oxygen to the placenta. The baby's blood comes to the placenta too, through the cord. The two bloods stay apart, with a very thin wall between them. Food and oxygen pass through that wall into the baby's blood. Waste passes back out, and the mother's body gets rid of it.
- e.g. The dal and rice the mother eats become part of the baby's growing body.
- e.g. When the mother breathes in, some of that oxygen ends up in the baby.
- e.g. The cord is about as long as your arm, and has no nerves, so cutting it does not hurt.
### The water around the baby · *defined*
**Definition:** The baby floats in warm water called amniotic fluid, inside a thin bag in the womb. The water cushions the baby, keeps them warm and lets them move.

A growing baby is soft, and their mother walks, bends and sometimes bumps into things. The water around the baby solves this. A bump on the mother's tummy spreads out through the water, so the baby is hardly jolted. The water also stays at the same warm temperature, and gives the baby room to stretch and kick. The baby swallows some of the water. This is practice for swallowing, and the water carries tastes from the mother's food.
- e.g. A raw egg in a jar filled to the top with water stays whole when you shake the jar. In an empty jar, it cracks.
- e.g. Babies whose mothers ate a lot of carrot liked carrot flavour more after birth, in one study.
- e.g. The baby practises breathing movements in the water, though there is no air to breathe.
### Nine months of growing · *defined*
**Definition:** A human baby usually grows in the womb for about 40 weeks, which is about nine months. Doctors split this time into three parts of about three months each.

In the first three months, the body parts form. By about six weeks a tiny heart is beating. By twelve weeks the baby is about as long as your finger and has tiny fingers and toes. In the middle three months, the baby grows and moves. Fingerprints form, and the baby starts to hear sounds. In the last three months, the baby gets much bigger and fatter. Their lungs get ready to breathe air. By birth, a baby is usually about 50 centimetres long and weighs about 3 kilograms. Doctors count the 40 weeks from about two weeks before the first cell is made, so the baby really grows for about 38 weeks.
- e.g. At 8 weeks: about the size of a raspberry.
- e.g. At 20 weeks, halfway: about as long as a banana, from head to bottom.
- e.g. At 40 weeks: about as heavy as a small watermelon.
### What an unborn baby can do · *defined*
**Definition:** Before being born, a baby moves, kicks, sucks their thumb, gets hiccups, swallows, sleeps and wakes. In the last months they can hear sounds.

Doctors have watched babies in the womb using ultrasound. They have seen them yawn, stretch and suck their thumbs. From about six months, babies react to loud sounds. They hear the mother's voice best, because it travels through her own body. After birth, newborns can tell their mother's voice from other voices. In one famous study, newborns even preferred a story their mother had read aloud many times before they were born.
- e.g. Mothers often feel the baby kick, like a little flutter or a thump.
- e.g. A baby can get hiccups in the womb. The mother feels a gentle, regular tap.
- e.g. A newborn often calms down when they hear their mother's voice.
### Birth · *defined*
**Definition:** Birth is when the baby leaves the mother's body. Most often the strong muscles of the womb squeeze and push the baby out. Sometimes doctors lift the baby out through a cut in the mother's tummy. This is called a caesarean birth.

When the baby is ready, the womb starts to squeeze, again and again. These squeezes are called contractions. They slowly push the baby down and out. It can take many hours, and it is hard work for the mother. A baby's skull bones are not yet joined, so their head can squeeze through safely. Sometimes it is safer for the baby to be lifted out by doctors in a caesarean birth. Both ways are birth. Both bring a new person into the world.
- e.g. A baby's head has soft spots where the skull bones have not yet joined.
- e.g. Doctors, nurses and midwives help with births in hospitals and at home.
- e.g. Some babies are born early. A few are born a little late.
### The first breath and the belly button · *defined*
**Definition:** Right after birth, the baby takes their first breath of air and usually cries. Then the cord is clamped and cut. The little stump dries and falls off, and leaves the belly button.

Before birth, the lungs are filled with fluid, not air. At birth, the baby breathes in, and air fills their lungs for the first time. A good cry helps open them. Now the baby gets oxygen by breathing, so the cord is no longer needed. Doctors clamp it and cut it. The cord has no nerves, so this does not hurt. After one or two weeks, the dry stump falls off. What is left is the belly button.
- e.g. Look at your belly button. It is where your cord once joined you to the placenta.
- e.g. Some belly buttons go in and some stick out. Both are normal.
- e.g. Newborns are checked at one minute and five minutes after birth, with a score invented by Virginia Apgar.
### A newborn and the people who care for them · *defined*
**Definition:** A human newborn cannot walk, feed themself or even hold up their own head. They need grown-ups to feed them, keep them warm and keep them safe for many years.

A calf can stand within an hour of being born. A human baby takes about a year to walk. Their brain is only about a quarter of its grown-up size, and it keeps growing fast after birth. A newborn sees best things about 20 to 30 centimetres away. That is the distance to the face of the person holding them. Babies stay warm and calm when held skin to skin. Families are made in many ways. A baby may be raised by the parents who gave birth to them, by an adoptive family, by grandparents or by others who love them.
- e.g. Holding a tiny early baby against a grown-up's bare chest keeps them warm. This is called kangaroo care.
- e.g. A newborn grabs your finger tightly if you put it in their palm.
- e.g. Adopted children grew in a womb like everyone else, and were welcomed into the family that raises them.
### How each cell knows what to become · *idea*
Scientists know that cells send each other chemical messages. A cell can tell where they are by how strong the message around them is. Near the source, the message is strong. Far away, it is weak. This idea was made popular by the English scientist Lewis Wolpert, around 1969. It explains a lot, like why your thumb grows on one side of your hand and your little finger on the other. But a whole body has millions of choices like this. How they all fit together so neatly is still being worked out.
- e.g. In a growing hand, a message spreads from the little-finger side. Each finger becomes the kind it is from how strong the message is where it grows.
- e.g. Scientists can grow tiny clumps of cells, called organoids, that start to form bits of organs by themselves.
- e.g. Nobody yet fully knows how a body ends up just the right shape and size.
### Why human babies are born so helpless · *debated*
Human babies are born less ready than many animal babies. Scientists argue about why. Here are the two main ideas.
- e.g. A foal walks within hours. A human baby walks at about one year.
- e.g. A human newborn's brain is about a quarter of its grown-up size.
- e.g. A chimpanzee newborn's brain is about two fifths of its grown-up size.
- **The 'tight squeeze' idea (Sherwood Washburn, 1960):** People walk on two legs, so the bones around the mother's hips are narrow. A baby with a bigger head could not get out. So babies are born early, before their heads get too big.
- **The 'energy' idea (Holly Dunsworth and others, 2012):** A mother's body can only make so much energy each day. By nine months, the growing baby needs almost as much as her body can give. So the baby is born when feeding them from inside gets too hard.
- **Many scientists today:** Both reasons may matter, and maybe others too. Scientists are still collecting evidence.

## Mostly agreed
- Every person begins as a single cell, made from a cell from each parent. *How we know:* Scientists have watched the first cells split under microscopes, for example in clinics that help families have babies. The first cell has instructions from both parents.
- A baby's body is built by cells splitting again and again, and then becoming different kinds. *How we know:* Microscope pictures show the cells doubling, from 1 to 2 to 4 to 8, and then forming layers that become skin, gut, muscle and bone.
- The baby gets food and oxygen from the mother's blood through the placenta and cord. *How we know:* Doctors can measure oxygen and food in the cord's blood. If the placenta does not work well, the baby grows less.
- Babies in the womb move, swallow and hear. *How we know:* Ultrasound pictures show them kicking and sucking their thumbs. Newborns react more to their mother's voice than to strangers'.
- Most human babies are born after about 40 weeks. *How we know:* Doctors have recorded millions of births around the world. Most happen between 37 and 42 weeks.

## How we found out
**Nicolaas Hartsoeker, Caspar Friedrich Wolff, Karl Ernst von Baer and Oscar Hertwig, 1694 (Hartsoeker's drawing), 1759 (Wolff), 1827 (von Baer) and 1876 (Hertwig).** After the first microscopes, about 330 years ago, many scientists thought a tiny, ready-made person was hidden inside the egg or the sperm cell. In 1694 the Dutch scientist Nicolaas Hartsoeker drew a little curled-up man inside a sperm cell. Growing, they thought, was just this tiny person getting bigger. In 1759 the German scientist Caspar Friedrich Wolff tested the idea. He opened hen's eggs on different days and looked closely. He saw no tiny chick at the start. Instead, the parts, like the blood vessels and the gut, formed one after another. In 1827 Karl Ernst von Baer found the real egg cell of a mammal, inside a dog. That egg was a single tiny cell, with no body parts in it. In 1876 Oscar Hertwig watched a sperm cell enter a sea urchin's egg under a microscope. He saw their two centres join into one. So a baby is not hidden in a cell, waiting to grow. They are built, step by step, from one new cell.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a tiny, ready-made person was inside the egg or sperm, and only grew bigger. Wolff watched hen's eggs day by day and saw the parts form one after another. Von Baer found that a mammal's egg is a single cell with no parts in it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A tiny, ready-made person is curled up inside the egg or the sperm cell, and they just grow bigger.** (Many scientists about 300 years ago, after the first microscopes. In 1694 Nicolaas Hartsoeker drew a tiny person inside a sperm cell. Many children think this too.; *rejected*)  
  For: Babies do look like small people. And the first microscopes showed blurry shapes that people could imagine as tiny bodies.  
  Against: In 1759 Caspar Friedrich Wolff watched hen's eggs day by day, and saw the body parts form one after another. Karl Ernst von Baer studied this carefully in the 1820s. Better microscopes showed the first cell has no body parts at all.
- **What a mother thinks or sees changes how the baby looks. For example, seeing a rabbit gives the baby long ears.** (An old belief in many countries, including India and Europe.; *rejected*)  
  For: People noticed babies with marks or unusual features, and looked for a reason.  
  Against: Thoughts and sights do not travel to the baby. Only things in the mother's blood, like food, medicine and some germs, reach the baby. Birthmarks and features come from how cells grow, not from what the mother saw.
- **The baby's blood and the mother's blood mix together freely.** (Many people think this, and some old books said it.; *rejected*)  
  For: Food and oxygen get from the mother to the baby, so it seems the blood must mix.  
  Against: The two bloods stay mostly apart, separated by a very thin wall in the placenta. Things pass through the wall. Babies can even have a different blood group from their mothers.
- **A few of the baby's cells stay in the mother's body for years after the birth.** (Diana Bianchi and her team, USA, 1996, and many scientists since.; *accepted*)  
  For: They found cells from sons in their mothers' blood up to 27 years after birth. Such cells have been found in many parts of mothers' bodies.  
  Against: Nobody yet knows if these cells help, harm or do nothing. Some studies say they help heal, others link them to illness.
- **An unborn baby can learn and remember things before birth.** (Anthony DeCasper and Melanie Spence, USA, 1986, and others.; *debated*)  
  For: Mothers read a story aloud many times before birth. After birth, their babies sucked in a way that showed they preferred that story to a new one.  
  Against: Some scientists say this is a simple getting-used-to a sound, not real remembering. How much a baby learns before birth is still argued about.

## Roots
We have been learning how a baby grows from one cell into a whole person. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Everyone in your home has a belly button. Each one is the scar left by a cord. Before birth, that cord brought food and oxygen from a mother's blood. So every person was fed by someone else's body before they could eat or breathe.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a person as starting on their birthday, and as eating and breathing for themselves. But for nine months, each of us got all our food and oxygen through another person's body.  
*Evidence:* Every person who was born has a belly button. It is where the umbilical cord joined the baby to the placenta, which passed food and oxygen from the mother's blood. In the story, Paati shows Kavya her own belly button.

- *who am i:* You are about 30 trillion cells, and every one came from a single first cell.
- *what is life:* Life is passed on. Every living thing came from another living thing, as you came from your parents' cells.
- *what is this world:* Your body was built from the rice, sambar, water and air your mother took in. Those came from fields, rivers and sunlight.

You began as one cell, made from a cell of each parent. That one cell became trillions of cells of many kinds: skin, bone, heart and brain. All of them were built from food your mother ate, and that food came from plants, soil, rain and sunlight. So each new person is built from many other lives: the plants, the farmers who grew them, and the mother who carried the baby.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to split a tiny banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing at all.' 'From that tiny thing you cannot see, this huge banyan tree grows.' (You began as one cell, too small to see. Everything you are now grew out of that one cell.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, the idea of 'interbeing':** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in it. Nothing exists all by itself. (A baby is built from their mother's meals. In them are the farmer's rice, the rain and the sun.)
- Science (settled): Every cell in your body came from one first cell, and almost every one has the same DNA.
- Science (open): A few cells from a baby can live on in the mother's body for decades. What they do is not yet known.
- Other view, Many biologists, and the Charvaka thinkers of ancient India: A baby is the result of cells, chemistry and food. Nothing more is needed to explain them, and there is no deeper oneness behind it.

This is where you were once joined. Close your eyes. Who fed you before you had a name? Who feeds you now?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* You started as one cell. Are you still the same person as that cell? (hint: Almost nothing of that first cell is left. But you grew from that cell without ever stopping.)
- *deep:* Every cell in your body has the same instructions. So why is an eye cell so different from a toe cell? (hint: Think of a cookbook. Two cooks with the same book can make different dishes.)
- *deep:* The food your mother ate became part of you. Where did that food come from before that? (hint: Follow a grain of rice back: to the plant, the soil, the rain and the sunlight.)
- *big:* When did you start being you? When you were one cell, when your heart started, or when you were born? (hint: Scientists cannot point to one exact moment. People in different families and religions answer this differently.)
- *big:* Nobody has a memory of being born. So how do you know it happened to you? (hint: Think of your belly button, photos and the stories people tell you.)
- *deep:* Why do human babies need looking after for so many years, when a calf walks in an hour? (hint: What does a human child need to learn that a calf does not?)
- *big:* Is a family made by birth, by love, or by both? (hint: Think of adopted children, grandparents who raise children, and friends who feel like family.)
- *deep:* Before you were born, you heard your mother's heartbeat all day. Do you think it still calms you now? (hint: Put your hand on your chest. How does a slow beat make you feel?)
- *big:* The atoms in your body came from food, and the atoms in food came from stars long ago. Are you made of star stuff? (hint: Scientists say almost all atoms heavier than hydrogen and helium were made inside stars.)
- *big:* Is it fair that some babies are born in places with good doctors, and some are not? (hint: What could people do so that every mother and baby is safe?)

## Story: The First Page
*Kavya, her family and Paati are made up. The science is real: fingerprints form in the womb at about four months and never change, the cord has no nerves, and every belly button is the mark of a cord.*

On Monday, Miss Fernandes gave the class a job. "Make a book called 'The Story of Me'. Page one is you as a newborn baby. Bring a photo."

Everyone started talking at once. Nila said her photo had her wrapped in a yellow towel, looking like an angry potato. Zoya said hers was taken in a hospital in Kochi, with a tiny cap on her head. Kavya said nothing at all. She drew a little square in the corner of her notebook, and coloured it in, very hard, until the pencil tip snapped.

Kavya had come to her family when she was three months old. Amma and Appa had driven all night to fetch her from a home for babies in another city. She knew the story by heart. There was the long drive, and the rain, and Appa singing old film songs badly to keep awake. There was Amma holding her for the first time and laughing and crying at once. It was a good story. But it started at three months. Before that, there was nothing. No photo. No first page.

That evening she sat on the steps outside the kitchen, where Paati was cleaning curry leaves. Paati had been a midwife for forty years. A midwife is a person who helps babies to be born. Paati said she had helped more babies into the world than there were people in their whole colony. Her hands were brown and wrinkled, and very quick.

"My book has no first page," Kavya said. "Everybody else has a baby photo. My story starts in the middle. Like a book with the first pages torn out."

Paati put down the curry leaves. She wiped her hands on her sari. "Come here," she said. "Let me read your first page to you."

"There isn't one."

"There is. It is written on you. Lift up your kurta a little."

Kavya did, and frowned down at her own tummy. Paati pressed one finger, very gently, on her belly button.

"Do you know what this is?"

"My belly button."

"It is a mark," said Paati. "Before you were born, you lived inside a woman's womb. That is a stretchy bag of muscle in her tummy. You floated in warm water, curled up like a prawn. You could not eat. You could not breathe air. So she fed you. Her blood carried food and oxygen to you, every minute of every day, for nine months. It came through a soft cord, joined to you right here. When you were born, the cord was cut. It does not hurt, because a cord has no nerves in it. This little mark is what is left. It says, somebody carried me. Somebody fed me before I had a name."

Kavya looked at her belly button as if she had never seen it before.

"Now give me your hand," said Paati. She turned Kavya's palm up, under the kitchen light. "Look at the tips of your fingers. Look very closely."

Kavya looked. There were tiny lines, curving and looping, like the rings in a cut tree, or ripples in a pond.

"These are your fingerprints," said Paati. "Nobody else in the whole world has these. Not me, not Amma, not anybody who has ever lived. Not even a twin. Do you know when they were made?"

Kavya shook her head.

"Before you were born. When you were about four months along in the womb, the skin on your fingertips grew these lines. Tiny chance differences in how your skin grew made a pattern that nobody else has. And then they stayed. They grow bigger with you, but they never change their pattern."

Kavya held her fingers up close to her eyes. The lines seemed to swirl.

"And before that," said Paati, "you were smaller still. You were one cell, smaller than a grain of salt. That cell was made when a cell from each of the two people who made you joined together. Then that one cell split into two. Two became four. Four became eight. On and on, for months. Some cells became your heart, and it started to beat when you were no bigger than a grain of rice. Some became your eyes. Some became these fingers. Every bit of you came from that one cell. Everybody's did. Nila's too. Zoya's too. Mine too."

Kavya giggled. Then she was quiet for a while. "The lady who carried me," she said slowly. "She fed me for nine months. Even though she didn't keep me."

"Yes," said Paati. "Every day. Through this." She tapped the belly button once more. "And then Amma and Appa drove all night in the rain. You have been fed and carried by a lot of people, Kavya. Most people have no idea how many."

"So my story doesn't start in the middle."

"No. Your story starts the same way every story starts. With one cell. You just know the middle part better than most."

That night, Kavya took out the blank first page of her book. Amma found the ink pad from the drawer with the tape and the old keys. Kavya pressed her thumb into it, then onto the very middle of the page. She pressed all ten fingers, one by one, in a circle around it. Underneath, in her best writing, she wrote: 'Made before I was born. Four months old, in the womb.'

Next to it she drew a tiny circle, no bigger than a full stop. She labelled it: 'Me, at the very start.'

On Friday, Miss Fernandes held each book up for the class. When she reached Kavya's, she held it up for a long time. Nila leaned over to look at her own fingers. Then Zoya did. Then the whole class was holding their hands up to the windows, turning them in the light, looking for the page that had been written on them before they had names.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavya thinks her story has no beginning because there is no baby photo. Paati shows her that the beginning is on her body. The belly button is the mark of the cord, through which a mother's blood fed the baby for nine months. Fingerprints formed in the womb at about four months, and they never change. However a family is made, by birth or by adoption, every child began as one cell and was carried and fed by someone.

- Ask: Why did Kavya feel her story had no first page?
- Ask: What does a belly button tell us about what happened before we were born?
- Ask: When were Kavya's fingerprints made?
- Ask: Paati says Kavya has been fed and carried by a lot of people. Who has fed and carried you?
- Ask: Why do you think the whole class started looking at their own fingers at the end?

### e1 · The Floating Safe: does water protect an egg? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a raw egg stay safer from bumps in a jar full of water, or in an empty jar?
- **predict:** Before you shake anything, guess. Will the egg in the empty jar crack, the egg in the water jar crack, both, or neither? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, keep everything the same except the water. Use two jars of the same size with lids. Use eggs of the same size. Shake each jar the same way, the same number of times. Count the shakes out loud.
- **safety:** Use plastic jars, not glass. Shake over a tray. Wash hands with soap after touching raw eggs.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then write three sentences. My claim is... My evidence is... My reasoning is...
- **results table:** Jar | What I guessed | After 5 gentle shakes | After 5 harder shakes | Cracked: yes or no
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two jars on a tray. 2. Ask your child to guess what will happen to each egg when shaken.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a pretend baby, a raw egg. Before birth, a real baby floats in water in the womb. Let's find out if water protects them. What do you think will happen?  
  *Child mode:* This egg is a pretend baby. One jar will have water. One will be empty. Which egg will stay safe? Make a guess.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: The water is soft, like a cushion.; In the empty jar, the egg will hit the sides.)
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Gently put one egg in the empty jar. 2. Fill the other jar right to the top with water. 3. Gently put the second egg in it. 4. Close both lids tightly.  
  *Parent says:* We fill this one right to the top. A real womb is full, with no air gap. Then the baby cannot slosh about.  
  *Child mode:* Put one egg in the empty jar. Fill the other jar to the very top with water. Put the egg in. Close both lids tight.
  - Note: Filling to the very top matters. An air gap lets the egg bang about. Help with raw eggs over the tray.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shake the empty jar gently, 5 times, side to side. 2. Look at the egg. 3. Do the same with the water jar. 4. If neither cracked, repeat both with 5 harder shakes.  
  *Parent says:* We shake both jars exactly the same way, 5 times each. Count with me. That keeps the test fair.  
  *Child mode:* Shake the empty jar 5 times. Count out loud. Look at the egg. Now shake the water jar the same way.
  - Note: The egg in the empty jar usually cracks after a few firm shakes. The egg in the full water jar usually stays whole. If both crack, you shook too hard, so try again more gently. With banana pieces, the one in the empty jar gets squashed and bruised against the sides. The one in water keeps its shape.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at both eggs. Fill in the results table together.  
  *Parent says:* Which egg cracked? Let's write it down. Then we'll work out why.  
  *Child mode:* Which egg cracked? Write it in your table.
  - Check: In the Floating Safe test, which egg usually stays whole? → **The egg in the jar full of water**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child write a claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's explain it like a scientist. First, what do you think? That's your claim. Then, what did you see? That's your evidence. Then, why? That's your reasoning.  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Ask: What is your claim, evidence and reasoning? (good: Claim: water protects the egg. Evidence: the egg in water did not crack, but the egg in the empty jar did. Reasoning: water spreads out the bump, like the water around a baby in the womb.)
  - Think: A real baby is not in a hard jar, but in a soft womb inside a body. What other things protect the baby?

3. (mcq) A cell splits into two. What is each new cell?  
   Options: Half of a cell / A dead piece of the old cell / A whole living cell with the same DNA  
   **Answer:** A whole living cell with the same DNA. *Why:* Each new cell is whole and alive, with a full copy of the instructions. Later, cells read different parts of them and become different kinds.
4. (mcq) What is the placenta's main job?  
   Options: To push the baby out at birth / To pass food and oxygen from the mother's blood to the baby's blood / To keep the baby's eyes shut  
   **Answer:** To pass food and oxygen from the mother's blood to the baby's blood. *Why:* The placenta grows on the womb wall. Food and oxygen pass through it. Picking 'push the baby out' mixes it up with the womb's muscles.
5. (mcq) Why does a baby in the womb hear their mother's voice best?  
   Options: Her voice travels through her own body to the baby / The womb has a little window / Babies can only hear high sounds  
   **Answer:** Her voice travels through her own body to the baby. *Why:* The mother's voice travels through her body and the water. Low sounds, like voices, get through water best.
6. (mcq) In the Floating Safe test, what was the one thing you changed between the two jars?  
   Options: The size of the egg / How hard you shook / Whether the jar was full of water  
   **Answer:** Whether the jar was full of water. *Why:* Only the water was changed. Everything else stayed the same, so any difference came from the water. That is what makes it a fair test.
7. (mcq) Sarala, a girl in Class 3, says, 'The egg in the water jar did not crack. So water protects a baby.' Which part is her evidence?  
   Options: The egg in the water jar did not crack / Water protects a baby / Both parts are claims  
   **Answer:** The egg in the water jar did not crack. *Why:* Evidence is what she saw: the egg did not crack. The claim is the idea she draws from it: water protects a baby.
8. (mcq) When does a baby's heart first start to beat?  
   Options: On their birthday / About six weeks after the first cell / When they first cry  
   **Answer:** About six weeks after the first cell. *Why:* A tiny heart beats by about six weeks, when the baby is no bigger than a grain of rice. Picking 'birthday' forgets the months of growing before birth.
9. (mcq) About how long does a human baby grow in the womb?  
   Options: About nine weeks / About two years / About nine months  
   **Answer:** About nine months. *Why:* About nine months. Picking two years mixes it up with an elephant, whose baby grows for almost two years.
10. (mcq) Why does a newborn often cry right after birth?  
   Options: The cry helps air fill their lungs for the first time / The cord hurts them / They want to go back  
   **Answer:** The cry helps air fill their lungs for the first time. *Why:* Before birth, the lungs were full of fluid. A good cry helps open the lungs and fill them with air.
11. (mcq) Once the baby is breathing, what do doctors do with the cord?  
   Options: Pull the baby with it / Clamp it and cut it / Leave it on for a year  
   **Answer:** Clamp it and cut it. *Why:* The baby now gets oxygen by breathing, so the cord is no longer needed. It is clamped and cut, and the stump later falls off.
12. (mcq) Anjali was adopted when she was a baby. Which of these is true?  
   Options: She began as one cell and grew in a womb, like everyone / She began life in a different way / Her family is not a real family  
   **Answer:** She began as one cell and grew in a womb, like everyone. *Why:* Every person began as one cell. Adoption is about which family raises and loves a child. Every family is a real family.
13. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not know for sure?  
   Options: Whether a baby needs oxygen / Whether a baby grows in a womb / Exactly what signal starts birth  
   **Answer:** Exactly what signal starts birth. *Why:* Scientists know the baby and the mother both seem to play a part. But exactly which signal starts birth is still being studied.

## Applications
- Doctors use ultrasound to check that a baby is growing well in the womb.
- Midwives and nurses help mothers give birth safely, at home and in hospitals.
- Kangaroo care, holding tiny babies skin to skin, saves the lives of many early babies.
- Incubators keep premature babies warm while they finish growing.
- The Apgar score checks every newborn's breathing, heartbeat and colour in the first minutes.
- Doctors advise pregnant mothers to eat well, because the baby is built from her food.
- Iron and folic acid tablets, given free in India to pregnant mothers, help babies grow healthily.
- Vaccines for pregnant mothers protect newborn babies from some illnesses.
- Fingerprints, which form before birth, are used to identify people, for example for Aadhaar.
- Scientists study how cells become different kinds, to learn how to heal injuries.
- Clinics help families who find it hard to have a baby, by helping the first cell start in a dish.
- Car seats and baby slings are designed to support a newborn's heavy head.

## Open questions
- **How does each cell know exactly where they are and what to become?** Scientists know cells send chemical messages. But how millions of messages fit together into a whole, right-sized body is still being worked out.
- **What starts birth? How does the body know the baby is ready?** Signals from both the baby and the mother seem to matter. Scientists still do not know exactly which signal starts it, or why some babies come early.
- **When does a growing baby begin to feel things, or be aware?** Nobody can ask a baby in the womb. Scientists argue about it, and different families and traditions also answer it differently.
- **Why are human babies born so helpless, compared with calves or foals?** Two main ideas compete: narrow hips and the mother's energy. There may be other reasons too.
- **What do the baby's cells that stay in the mother's body actually do?** They have been found in many parts of mothers' bodies, for decades. Some studies say they help heal, others link them to illness. Nobody knows yet.
- **How much does a baby learn and remember before they are born?** Newborns know their mother's voice. But how much more they learn in the womb is still argued about.
- **Why do some babies grow very well and others less, even with healthy mothers?** Food, genes, the placenta and many other things all matter. How they work together is not fully known.

## Connected maths
- Remember the dough doubling, where one ball split into 2, then 4, then 8? If you keep going, how many balls after 6 rounds of splitting? **64 balls.**. Each round doubles the number. 1, then 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. That is 6 rounds. So there are 64 balls. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- A baby grows in the womb for about 40 weeks. There are 7 days in a week. About how many days is that? **280 days.**. 40 weeks times 7 days. 4 times 7 is 28. So 40 times 7 is 280. A baby grows for about 280 days. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- In the Who Waits Longest? card game, an Indian elephant baby grows for about 650 days and a human baby for about 270 days. How many more days does the elephant baby grow? **380 more days.**. 650 take away 270. 650 take away 200 is 450. Then 450 take away 70 is 380. So the elephant baby grows about 380 days longer. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- On the fruit line, a newborn is about 50 centimetres long. You are now about 125 centimetres tall. How many newborn lengths tall are you? **2 and a half newborn lengths.**. Two newborns make 50 plus 50, which is 100 centimetres. That leaves 25 centimetres, which is half of 50. So you are 2 and a half newborn lengths tall. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- In Keep the Baby Warm, the bottle on a chest was 35 degrees after 20 minutes. The bottle alone was 29 degrees. How much warmer was the bottle on the chest? **6 degrees warmer.**. 35 take away 29 is 6. So the bottle held on the chest stayed 6 degrees warmer than the bottle left alone. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
