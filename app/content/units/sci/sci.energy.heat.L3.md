# Heat and temperature: how a thermometer works, and why hot things cool down
`sci.energy.heat.L3`

**Summary.** Leave a tumbler of hot water and a tumbler of cold water on the same table, and the hot one cools while the cold one warms. Both stop at the temperature of the room. Temperature is how hot or cold something is, and a thermometer measures it in degrees Celsius. Heat always moves by itself from a warmer thing to a cooler one, until both are equally warm. A cold thing is a thing with less heat, and no cold flows anywhere.

**Intro.** *Put one hand in a bowl of warm water and the other in a bowl of ice water for one minute. Then put both hands into one bowl of tap water. One hand says the water is warm. The other hand says it is cold. Which hand is right?* Neither hand is right, because your skin compares the water with how warm it was a moment ago. To know how hot something really is, we need a thermometer. In this unit you learn to read one. You watch hot water and cold water change, minute by minute, and find out where they stop. You make a thermometer from a bottle and a straw. And you find out what really moves when a hot thing cools down.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can read a thermometer in degrees Celsius, and I wait until the reading stops changing.
1. I can explain why my hand is not a good way to measure temperature, using the two-hands bowl test.
2. I can explain how a liquid thermometer works: the liquid expands when it warms and climbs up a narrow tube.
3. I can say which way heat moves between two things that touch, and predict that they end up at the same temperature.
4. I can explain, using my own readings, why a hot tumbler and a cold tumbler both end up at room temperature.
5. I can tell heat and temperature apart: a small cup can be hotter than a big pot, but hold less heat.
6. I can tell how Pictet's snow seemed to send out cold, and how Prévost showed that only heat was moving.

## Terms
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is, shown as a number. *e.g.* On a May afternoon in Chennai, the air can be 38 degrees Celsius. Inside a fridge it is about 4 degrees.
- **Heat**: Energy that moves from a warmer thing to a cooler thing. *e.g.* When you wrap your hands round a tumbler of hot coffee, heat moves from the coffee into your hands.
- **Thermometer**: A tool that measures temperature. *e.g.* A laboratory thermometer has a thin glass tube with red liquid inside and numbers along the side.
- **Degree Celsius**: The unit we use for temperature in India. It is written °C. Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. *e.g.* A healthy person's body is about 37 °C inside.
- **Bulb (of a thermometer)**: The small round end of a thermometer, which holds most of the liquid. It is the part that must touch what you are measuring. *e.g.* To measure water, dip the bulb right into the water, not just the tip of the glass.
- **Expand**: To take up more space. Most things expand a little when they get warmer. *e.g.* The red liquid in a thermometer expands when it warms, so it climbs up the tube.
- **Particles**: The tiny pieces that everything is made of, far too small to see. *e.g.* In hot water, the water particles jiggle faster than in cold water.
- **Room temperature**: The temperature of the air in a room. Things left in a room for a long time end up at this temperature. *e.g.* A bottle of water left on the shelf overnight is at room temperature in the morning.
- **Clinical thermometer**: A thermometer made for measuring body temperature. It reads only from about 32 to 42 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* When you have a fever, a grown-up puts a clinical thermometer under your arm.
- **Laboratory thermometer**: A thermometer for measuring water, air and other things. It usually reads from about minus 10 to 110 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* We use a laboratory thermometer to measure the hot and cold tumblers in this unit.

## Explanations
### Temperature, and the Celsius scale · *defined*
**Definition:** Temperature is how hot or cold something is. In India we measure it in degrees Celsius, written °C.

On the Celsius scale, 0 is the temperature at which pure water freezes. 100 is the temperature at which it boils, at sea level. The space between them is cut into 100 equal steps called degrees. Anything colder than freezing water gets a minus number. A freezer is about minus 18 °C. The scale is named after Anders Celsius, a Swedish astronomer. In 1742 he made a scale with 100 for freezing and 0 for boiling. A few years later, others turned it the other way round, which is how we use it today.
- e.g. A fridge is about 4 °C inside.
- e.g. A healthy body is about 37 °C inside.
- e.g. Tea people drink is often about 60 to 65 °C.
### How a liquid thermometer works · *defined*
**Definition:** A liquid thermometer has a bulb of liquid joined to a very narrow tube. When the liquid warms, it expands and climbs up the tube. When it cools, it shrinks and goes down.

The liquid expands only a tiny bit. But the tube is so narrow that this tiny bit of extra liquid makes a long line. The numbers on the side were marked by putting the thermometer in melting ice and in boiling water. A thermometer shows its own temperature. Put it in hot water, and heat moves into the bulb until the bulb is as warm as the water. That takes about a minute for a glass thermometer. So you wait until the line stops moving before you read it. Most laboratory thermometers in India use red-coloured spirit. Old ones used silvery mercury, which is poisonous if the glass breaks.
- e.g. Dip the bulb in hot water and the red line climbs for about a minute, then stops.
- e.g. A digital thermometer has a tiny metal part instead of liquid, and it shows a number on a screen.
- e.g. If you read too early, the number is too low for hot water and too high for cold water.
### Two kinds of thermometer · *defined*
**Definition:** A clinical thermometer is made for bodies and reads only from about 32 to 42 °C. A laboratory thermometer reads a much wider range, usually from about minus 10 to 110 °C.

A body's temperature changes only a little, so a clinical thermometer has a short range with small, exact steps. Most digital fever thermometers show 'Lo' or 'Hi' outside their range. That is why a fever thermometer cannot measure fridge water or hot tea. A laboratory thermometer is long and has a wide range, so it can measure ice, a room and hot water. But it must never go in the mouth, and it is not made for fevers.
- e.g. A fever thermometer in fridge water shows 'Lo'.
- e.g. A laboratory thermometer in the same water might show 8 °C.
- e.g. A glass clinical thermometer has a small kink in its tube, so the line stays put after you take it out.
### Heat moves from warmer to cooler · *defined*
**Definition:** When two things at different temperatures touch, heat moves from the warmer one to the cooler one. It keeps moving until both are at the same temperature.

Hot water on a table gives heat to the air and the table, so it cools. Cold water on the same table takes heat from the air, so it warms. Both stop changing when they reach room temperature. Then there is no difference left, and no more heat moves either way. This always happens by itself. Heat never moves by itself from a cooler thing to a warmer one. A fridge makes its inside cold by pumping heat out, and that is why the back of a fridge feels warm.
- e.g. Curd taken out of the fridge warms up on the table.
- e.g. A hot idli steamer cools down after the stove is turned off.
- e.g. Drops form on the outside of a tumbler of cold water, because the cold tumbler cools the damp air next to it.
### Cold is less heat · *defined*
**Definition:** Cold is not a separate thing that moves. A cold thing has less heat than a warm thing.

People say 'Shut the door, the cold is coming in.' What really happens is that heat from the warm room is going out. When you hold an ice cube, the ice does not send cold into your hand. Your hand sends heat into the ice. Your hand cools down because it is losing heat, and the ice melts because it is gaining heat. Scientists in the 1790s argued about this, and the idea that only heat moves won.
- e.g. Ice in your hand melts because your hand's heat goes into it.
- e.g. A room gets cold on a winter night because its heat leaks out through windows and walls.
- e.g. A steel tumbler of fridge water feels cold because heat is leaving your fingers.
### What heat really is: jiggling particles · *defined*
**Definition:** Everything is made of tiny particles that are always jiggling. The hotter a thing is, the faster its particles jiggle. Temperature tells us how fast they jiggle, on average.

When hot water touches a cooler tumbler, the fast-jiggling water particles bump into the slower tumbler particles. Each bump passes on a little movement. So the tumbler's particles speed up, and the water's particles slow down. That passing-on of movement is heat moving. When both jiggle equally fast, the bumps pass on as much one way as the other, so the temperatures stay the same. Scientists showed in the 1800s that heat is this movement, and not a hidden fluid inside things.
- e.g. Sugar dissolves faster in hot water, because the fast water particles knock the sugar particles apart sooner.
- e.g. Rubbing your palms makes their particles jiggle faster, so they get warm.
- e.g. The smell of hot sambar spreads faster than the smell of cold curd.
### Heat and temperature are not the same · *defined*
**Definition:** Temperature is how hot a thing is. Heat is the energy it can give to something cooler. A big amount of warm water can hold more heat than a small cup of hotter water.

Fill a small katori with water at 70 °C, and a big bucket with water at 40 °C. The katori is hotter. But put the same lump of ice in each, and the bucket melts much more ice, because it has so much more water to give heat. Temperature does not tell you how much heat a thing holds. It only tells you how hot it is, and so which way heat will move.
- e.g. A drop of hot tea that splashes on your hand stings, but cools in a moment. A cupful would burn.
- e.g. A big pot of sambar stays hot for much longer than a small bowl of it.
- e.g. The sea in Chennai or Kochi warms up and cools down far more slowly than the sand.
### Why your hand is not a thermometer · *defined*
**Definition:** Your skin feels whether it is warming up or cooling down. So the same water can feel warm to one hand and cold to the other.

In the two-hands bowl test, one hand had been in warm water, so it was warm. In the middle bowl it lost heat, and it felt cold. The other hand had been in ice water, so it was cold. In the middle bowl it gained heat, and it felt warm. The water was one temperature all along, and a thermometer showed one number. The English thinker John Locke wrote about this same test, with one warm hand and one cold hand in the same water, in 1690.
- e.g. After a cold bath, ordinary tap water feels warm.
- e.g. Coming out of an air-conditioned shop, the street feels even hotter than it is.
- e.g. A parent's hand on a child's forehead can tell 'hot' or 'not hot', but not 38.5 or 39.5.

## Mostly agreed
- Heat moves by itself from a warmer thing to a cooler thing, and never the other way. *How we know:* Every time anyone measures it, hot things in a room cool down and cold things warm up, until they reach the same temperature. Scientists call this the second law of thermodynamics.
- Things that touch for long enough end up at the same temperature. *How we know:* A thermometer shows that a tumbler of hot water and a tumbler of cold water left in a room both reach room temperature within a few hours.
- Temperature is how fast the particles of a thing jiggle, on average. *How we know:* In the 1800s, scientists such as James Joule showed that stirring or rubbing always makes the same amount of heat for the same amount of work. Later, Albert Einstein and Jean Perrin showed that the tiny particles really are always moving.
- Most liquids, solids and gases expand when they warm up. *How we know:* This is how liquid thermometers work. It is also why railway tracks and bridges are built with small gaps, so they have room to expand on hot days.

## How we found out
**Marc-Auguste Pictet and Pierre Prévost, scientists in Geneva, 1790 (Pictet's mirrors and snow) and 1791 (Prévost's explanation).** In 1790, Marc-Auguste Pictet, a scientist in Geneva in Switzerland, set up two curved metal mirrors facing each other across a room. In front of one mirror he put a thermometer. In front of the other he put something hot. The thermometer rose, though nothing touched it. The mirrors were bouncing the heat across the room. Then Pictet put a flask of snow where the hot thing had been. The thermometer fell. It looked as if the snow was sending out rays of cold, and the mirrors were carrying them. Some scientists thought cold might be a real thing that travels, just like heat. In 1791, Pierre Prévost, another scientist in Geneva, explained it differently. Everything sends out heat all the time, he said, and warmer things send out more. The thermometer was sending heat to the snow and getting less back than it gave. So the thermometer cooled. Scientists today agree with Prévost. Cold is not a thing that moves. Only heat moves, and a cold thing is one that has less of it.  
*What people believed before:* When snow in front of a mirror made a distant thermometer fall, it seemed that cold was a thing that could travel. Prévost showed that the thermometer was simply losing more heat to the snow than it got back.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Cold is a thing that flows into warm things, like through an open door.** (A very common idea, which some scientists also held in the 1790s; *rejected*)  
  For: When you open a door on a winter night, the room feels as if cold is pouring in. Pictet's snow even seemed to send out cold through mirrors.  
  Against: A thermometer shows heat leaving warm things, never cold arriving. Prévost explained Pictet's snow: the thermometer gave out more heat than it got back from the snow.
- **Heat is an invisible fluid, called caloric, stored inside things.** (Most scientists until the early 1800s; *rejected*)  
  For: Heat seemed to flow from hot to cold like water running downhill, and a warm thing seemed to hold a certain amount of it.  
  Against: Rubbing, stirring and drilling can make heat for as long as you keep going. A stored fluid would run out. So heat is a kind of movement of particles.
- **A thermometer shows the right temperature as soon as it touches something.** (A common mistake when people first use a thermometer; *rejected*)  
  For: A digital thermometer shows a number at once, so it seems ready.  
  Against: The thermometer shows its own temperature. Heat takes time to move in or out of it, so the number keeps changing for a while. You must wait until it stops.
- **Hot water can freeze faster than cold water.** (Erasto Mpemba, a schoolboy in Tanzania, who noticed it while making ice cream in 1963. He wrote about it with the physicist Denis Osborne in 1969.; *debated*)  
  For: Some careful experiments have found hot water freezing first, perhaps because it loses water by evaporating, or because of how it moves as it cools.  
  Against: Other careful experiments do not find it at all. The result seems to depend on the container, the freezer and exactly how 'frozen' is measured.

## Roots
We have measured hot and cold water, watched heat move, and found where things stop. Now we ask what heat moving tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your hot tumbler cooled and your cold tumbler warmed, until both read the same as the room. Nothing in the room was making cold. Heat moved out of the hot water, and from the air into the cold water. So cold is only less heat, and heat always moves from warmer to cooler.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about hot and cold as two opposite things that both flow, as in 'the cold is coming in'. But only heat ever moves. Cold is our name for having less of it.  
*Evidence:* In the hot and cold tumbler test, the hot water's temperature went down and the cold water's went up, and both stopped at room temperature. In the tumbler-in-a-bowl test, the water around the hot tumbler warmed while the tumbler cooled.

- *who am i:* Your body is about 37 degrees, warmer than most rooms. So heat leaves you all day long, and the food you eat makes more.
- *what is life:* A lizard on a wall basks in the morning sun to take in heat, because their body warms up from the world around them.
- *what is this world:* Left alone, a hot tumbler, a cold tumbler and a room all end up at one temperature. The Sun keeps making new warm places, and that is why the weather keeps changing.

In a room where everything is at one temperature, no heat moves. A difference is what makes heat move: a hot stove, a cold drink, a sunny wall. Heat moving makes winds blow, water evaporate and rain fall. So the many changes in the world start with differences, and each change is the world evening one of them out.

- **Heraclitus, fragment 126 (Diels-Kranz numbering):** Cold things grow warm, the warm grows cold, the wet grows dry, and the dry grows wet. (Heraclitus, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,500 years ago, saw that hot and cold keep changing into each other. Your tumblers showed the same thing, and showed which way it goes: the hot one and the cold one both moved to the middle.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.14:** Krishna tells Arjuna that cold and heat, pleasure and pain, come from the senses touching things. They come and go, and do not last, so bear them calmly. (In the two-hands bowl test, the feeling of warm or cold came from your skin touching the water, and it changed when your hands changed. The thermometer gave one steady number.)
- **Tao Te Ching 77:** The way of heaven takes from what has too much and gives to what has too little. (Heat does exactly this. It moves from the hotter thing to the cooler one until neither has more. Laozi wrote that people often do the opposite.)
- Science (settled): Heat moves by itself only from warmer to cooler. Scientists call this the second law of thermodynamics.
- Science (debated): This one-way spreading of heat may be part of why time seems to go only forwards, never backwards. Physicists still argue about how the two are linked.
- Other view, Many physicists: Heat spreading out is just countless particles bumping at random. Fast ones pass movement to slow ones simply because there are more ways for movement to be shared out than kept in one place. Calling it 'evening out' or 'giving to what has too little' is a nice picture, but nature has no aim to balance anything.
- Practice, Warm the tumbler (3 min): Hold this steel tumbler of room water in both hands. Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly. Feel the steel against your palms. Keep holding it for one minute. Now open your eyes. Does the tumbler feel cooler or warmer than at the start? Which way did the heat go?
- Ask: Your body sends heat into the room all day. Is part of the room's warmth yours?
- Ask: If nothing ever had a different temperature, would anything ever happen?
- Ask: Your hand felt one bowl as warm and cold at once. Is a feeling wrong if it does not match the thermometer?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your hot tumbler gave its heat to the room. Where is that heat now? (hint: Think about the air, the table and the walls. Did the room get warmer? Why was it too little to notice?)
- *big:* If heat always spreads from warmer to cooler, will everything in the world one day be the same temperature? (hint: Think about what keeps making new warm places, like the Sun and your own body.)
- *deep:* A thermometer tells you its own temperature. How do you know it is telling you the water's temperature too? (hint: Think about what happens when two things touch for a while.)
- *deep:* Is there a coldest possible temperature? What would happen to the particles there? (hint: If temperature is how fast particles jiggle, what is the slowest they could go?)
- *big:* Your hand and a thermometer said different things about the same water. Which one tells the truth about the world? (hint: Think about what each one is really measuring. Is a feeling of warmth wrong, or is it about something else?)

## Story: Where Did Paati's Coffee Go?
*The story is made up. The science is true: hot coffee in a room cools to room temperature, a tumbler standing in water gives its heat to the water until both meet in the middle, and pouring coffee between a tumbler and a davara cools it faster.*

Every morning in Kumbakonam, Paati made filter coffee. She poured it from the steel tumbler into the wide davara and back again, high in the air, until it was frothy. Then she put the tumbler on the window sill and went to bathe.

Lakshmi was nine, and her aunt Revathi Athai was a science teacher. Athai had lent Lakshmi a long glass thermometer for the holidays. It had red liquid inside and numbers from minus 10 to 110 along the side. Lakshmi had already measured the fridge, the tap water and the air on the balcony.

That morning she dipped the bulb of the thermometer into Paati's coffee. The red line climbed and climbed, then stopped. "Seventy degrees," she read.

Her cousin Arjun, who was seven, looked over her shoulder. "That is very hot," he said.

Lakshmi wrote 70 in her notebook. Then she measured the air in the room. The line slid down to 29. She wrote that too.

When Paati came back from her bath, the coffee had gone lukewarm. Lakshmi measured it again. "Forty-two," she said. "It went down by 28 degrees."

"Of course it went down," said Paati, drinking it anyway. "Coffee always goes cold if you leave it."

Lakshmi frowned. "But where did the heat go, Paati? The coffee had lots of heat, and now it has less. Heat must go somewhere."

"It just disappears," said Arjun. "Like steam."

Lakshmi measured the air in the room again. Still 29. "The room did not get hotter," she said. "So maybe Arjun is right. Maybe heat just vanishes."

But she was not happy with that answer. That evening she phoned Revathi Athai.

"Try this," said Athai. "Stand a tumbler of hot water inside a bigger vessel of ordinary water. Then measure both, again and again."

The next morning, Paati made extra coffee. Lakshmi poured one tumbler of it and stood it inside Paati's big steel vessel. Arjun poured tap water into the vessel, all around the tumbler, until the water came most of the way up the side. Lakshmi measured the coffee. Seventy. She measured the water around it. Twenty-nine, the same as the room.

Every two minutes, Lakshmi stirred the water in the vessel gently with a spoon. Then she measured the coffee, and then the water, and Arjun wrote the numbers down.

The coffee went down. 62. 55. 49. 45.

And the water around it went up. 31. 33. 35. 36.

"Look!" said Arjun. "The water is getting hotter. And nobody is heating it."

"The coffee is heating it," said Lakshmi slowly. "The heat is going out of the coffee, through the steel, into the water. It did not disappear. It moved."

After twenty minutes, the coffee read 39 and the water read 38. After half an hour, both read 38. Then the numbers stopped changing.

"They met in the middle," said Arjun.

"Not quite the middle," said Lakshmi. "There is much more water than coffee. So the coffee came down a long way, and the water went up only a little."

Then she sat very still. "That is what happened yesterday too," she said. "The coffee gave its heat to the air in the room. But the room has so much air that it warmed up too little for the thermometer to show."

Paati had been listening from the kitchen. "So when I pour the coffee between the tumbler and the davara," she said, "am I giving its heat to the air?"

Lakshmi thought about it. "Yes, Paati. When you pour it high, lots of coffee touches the air at once. The heat leaves faster, so the coffee cools down enough to drink."

"Then tomorrow," said Paati, "I will pour it only twice, and you two can drink it hot."

Arjun tapped the notebook. "I still don't understand one thing," he said. "If the coffee gives heat to the water, why doesn't the water give cold to the coffee?"

Lakshmi smiled, because she had asked Athai the same question. "There is no such thing as giving cold," she said. "Cold just means less heat. The heat only ever goes one way, from the hotter thing to the cooler thing. It stops when they are the same."

That night, Lakshmi wrote at the bottom of her page: The heat did not vanish. It spread out.

**For the parent:** For the parent: when a hot thing cools, its heat does not vanish. It moves into whatever is cooler around it: the air, the table, the water. In a big room the air gets only a tiny bit warmer, too little to measure, so the heat seems to disappear. A vessel of water is small enough to show the rise. The coffee and the water stopped at the same temperature, nearer the water's starting temperature, because there was more water. Heat moves only from hotter to cooler, and stops when they are equal. Cold is not a thing that moves.

- Ask: Why did the room still read 29 degrees after the coffee had cooled?
- Ask: In the vessel test, what happened to the water around the tumbler? Why?
- Ask: Why did the coffee and the water end up nearer 29 than 70?
- Ask: Why does Paati's high pouring cool the coffee faster?
- Ask: Arjun asked why the water did not give cold to the coffee. How would you answer him?

## Experiments
### e1 · Hot Tumbler, Cold Tumbler: where do they both end up? (40 min)
- **question:** If a tumbler of hot water and a tumbler of cold water stand on the same table, what happens to each one's temperature, and where does it stop?
- **predict:** Guess the temperature of each tumbler after 20 minutes, and again after 3 hours. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two tumblers of the same size and metal, filled to the same height. Stand them side by side, away from the fan, the sun and the stove. Use the same thermometer for every reading, and read both tumblers at the same times. Stir gently and wait until the line stops moving before reading, because a thermometer takes about a minute to catch up.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water, mixes it and pours it. The child does not touch the hot tumbler. Glass thermometers break easily: hold them by the top, never stir with them, and lay them flat on a cloth so they cannot roll off the table.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw two lines on one graph, one for each tumbler, with time along the bottom. Draw a dotted line across for the room.
- **results table:** Time | Room air (°C) | Hot tumbler (°C) | Cold tumbler (°C)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the numbers on the thermometer. 2. Hold it by the top, in the air, in a shady place. 3. Wait 2 minutes, then read where the red line ends. 4. Write it as the room temperature.  
  *Parent says:* This is a thermometer. The red line climbs when it gets warmer. First let's measure the air in this room. We wait until the line stops moving.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the thermometer in the air. Wait 2 minutes. Where does the red line stop? Write that number.
  - Note: Most homes in South India read between about 26 and 32 degrees. Show your child how to read the line at eye level. If the line is between two marks, choose the nearer one. A digital probe just shows the number, but still wait until it stops changing.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the two empty tumblers. 2. Explain that one will hold hot water and one cold water. 3. Ask for a guess for each tumbler, after 20 minutes and after 3 hours, with a reason. 4. Write the guesses.  
  *Parent says:* One tumbler will be hot and one will be cold. What will each one read after 20 minutes? And after 3 hours? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One tumbler hot, one tumbler cold. Guess each number after 20 minutes. Then after 3 hours. Why?
  - Ask: Will the cold tumbler stay cold? (good: It will warm up, because the room is warmer.; It will get closer to the room's number.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Many children think the hot water will cool right down to cold, or that the cold one will stay cold. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Boil some water. 2. Mix it half and half with tap water in a jug. 3. Fill one tumbler to about 2 cm below the rim. 4. Fill the other with fridge water to the same height. 5. Stand them a hand apart on the table, away from the fan.  
  *Parent says:* I am making the hot water now, because it can burn. I am filling both tumblers to the same height. Why should they be the same?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours the hot water. Do not touch that tumbler. Why fill both the same?
  - Note: Half boiled and half tap water should be about 60 to 65 degrees, which is hot but not scalding. Keep the fan off if you can, or keep it the same all the time, because moving air cools things faster.
- **s4 (22 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the thermometer in the hot tumbler, stir gently with a spoon, and wait until the line stops. 2. Read it and write it. 3. Wipe the thermometer, and do the same with the cold tumbler. 4. Repeat every 5 minutes, at 0, 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Let's read both tumblers now, and then every 5 minutes. Read the hot one first, then the cold one. What do you notice about the numbers?  
  *Child mode:* Read the hot tumbler. Then the cold one. Write both numbers. Do it again every 5 minutes.
  - Note: Each reading takes about a minute, so a 5-minute gap gives time for both. A typical result with a 30 degree room: the hot tumbler goes 62, 56, 52, 48, 45. The cold one goes 8, 11, 13, 15, 16. Notice that the hot one falls fastest at the start, when it is furthest from the room. Drops of water may appear on the outside of the cold tumbler. They come from the damp air next to it, not from inside.
  - Check: The cold tumbler's number went up. Where did its heat come from? → **From the air and the table around it, which were warmer**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave both tumblers where they are. 2. After 3 hours, read both and the room. 3. Write the three numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Three hours have gone by. Let's read both tumblers and the room. Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Three hours later. Read both tumblers. Read the room. Are they close?
  - Note: Both tumblers should now read within a degree or two of the room. The tumbler that started hot may even read a degree below the room, because water evaporating from its top cools it a little. That is real, not a mistake.
  - Wait: Come back in 3 hours.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: hot things and cold things in a room both end up at room temperature. What I saw: the hot tumbler went down from 62 to 31, and the cold one went up from 8 to 29. The room was 30. Why: heat moves from the warmer thing to the cooler thing until they are the same.
  - Check: After 3 hours, why did both tumblers stop changing? → **They were both at room temperature, so there was no difference left to make heat move**
### e2 · Tumbler in a Bowl: where does the heat go? (30 min)
- **question:** When a hot tumbler stands in a bowl of room-temperature water, what happens to the tumbler and to the water around it?
- **predict:** Will the water in the bowl get warmer, colder or stay the same? Where will the two numbers stop? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Read the tumbler and the bowl at the same times, every 2 minutes. Stir the bowl water gently before each reading, so the warm water near the tumbler mixes in. Do not move the tumbler.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water and lowers the tumbler into the bowl. The child stirs only the outer water, with a spoon.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both lines on one graph and circle the place where they meet.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Hot tumbler (°C) | Water in the bowl (°C)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour about three tumblers of tap water into the big bowl. 2. Read its temperature. 3. Ask your child to guess what will happen to it when a hot tumbler stands in it, and why.  
  *Parent says:* This water is the same as the room. Soon a hot tumbler will stand in the middle. Will this water change? Where will the two numbers stop?  
  *Child mode:* A hot tumbler will stand in this water. Will the water change? Why?
  - Ask: Where do you think the two numbers will stop? (good: Somewhere in between.; They will become the same.)
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix hot water as before. 2. Fill the tumbler to the same height as the water in the bowl will reach. 3. Lower it gently into the middle of the bowl. 4. Read the tumbler at once.  
  *Parent says:* I am lowering the hot tumbler into the bowl. Watch the thermometer in it.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up puts the hot tumbler in the bowl. Read the tumbler.
  - Note: If the tumbler floats or tips, pour a little water out of the bowl. The bowl water should come most of the way up the tumbler's side.
- **s3 (18 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every 2 minutes, stir the bowl water gently. 2. Read the bowl, then the tumbler. 3. Write both. 4. Stop when the two numbers are the same for two readings in a row.  
  *Parent says:* Let's read both every 2 minutes. What is happening to each number? Are they getting closer?  
  *Child mode:* Stir the bowl. Read the bowl. Read the tumbler. Write both. Again in 2 minutes.
  - Note: With one thermometer, wipe it and wait a moment between the two readings. A typical result: the tumbler goes 62, 54, 48, 44, 41, 39, 38. The bowl goes 30, 32, 34, 35, 36, 37, 37. They meet nearer the bowl's start, because the bowl holds more water.
  - Check: The water in the bowl got warmer, though nobody heated it. Why? → **Heat moved from the hot tumbler, through the steel, into the cooler water**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the hot tumbler's heat went into the water around it. What I saw: the tumbler went down and the bowl went up, until both read 37. Why: heat moves from hotter to cooler until they are the same. The bowl had more water, so it went up only a little.
### e3 · Half and Half: what do you get when you mix hot and cold? (25 min)
- **question:** If you mix equal amounts of hot water and cold water, what temperature do you get?
- **predict:** Read the hot water and the cold water first. Then guess the temperature of the mix. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Measure the same amount of hot and cold water with the same katori or measuring cup. Mix them quickly and stir. Read the mix as soon as the line stops moving.
- **safety:** A grown-up measures and pours the hot water. Mix over a tray or the sink in case of spills.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Next to each try, write how far your guess was from the real mix.
- **results table:** Try | Hot water (°C) | Cold water (°C) | My guess for the mix (°C) | The mix (°C)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour one katori of hot water into one tumbler and one katori of cold water into another. 2. Read and write both temperatures. 3. Ask for a guess for the mix, with a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Here is one katori of hot water and one of cold. Let's read both. If we mix them, what will the mix read? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Read the hot water. Read the cold water. Guess the mix. Why?
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour both into the third tumbler. 2. Stir for 10 seconds. 3. Read the mix and write it.  
  *Parent says:* Now I pour them together. Stir it. What does it read? How close was your guess?  
  *Child mode:* Stir the mix. Read it. Was your guess close?
  - Note: Equal amounts at 60 and 10 give a mix close to 35, the number exactly in the middle. It is usually a degree or two lower, because some heat goes into the tumbler and the air.
  - Check: Equal amounts of water at 60 °C and 10 °C are mixed. About what will the mix read? → **About 35 °C, halfway between**
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now use 1 katori of hot water and 2 katoris of cold. 2. Ask for a new guess with a reason. 3. Mix, stir and read.  
  *Parent says:* This time there is twice as much cold water. Will the mix be nearer the hot number or the cold number? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Now 1 katori hot and 2 katoris cold. Will the mix be nearer hot or cold? Why?
  - Note: With 60 and 10, the mix should read about 25 to 27, nearer the cold number. A good way to think: one hot katori gives its heat to share among three katoris.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: when hot and cold water mix, they meet somewhere in between. What I saw: equal amounts met near the middle, and more cold water pulled the mix nearer the cold number. Why: the hot water gives heat to the cold water, and more water needs more heat to warm up.

### a3 · Make a Straw Thermometer (25 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the hole in the cap and mixes the hot water. Hold the bottle by its neck when it is in the hot bowl.
- **record:** Mark the straw at room temperature, in hot water and in ice water. Write the real thermometer's reading next to each mark.
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make a hole in the cap just wide enough for the straw. 2. Fill the bottle to the very top with coloured water. 3. Push the straw through the cap so it goes about 3 cm into the water. 4. Screw on the cap and seal round the straw with dough. 5. The water should show a little way up the straw.  
  *Parent says:* We are making our own thermometer. The bottle is the bulb and the straw is the tube. The bottle must be full, with no air inside.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bottle right to the top. Seal the straw in the cap with dough.
  - Note: If the water does not show in the straw, squeeze in a little more water through the straw with a dropper, or press the dough down. Air bubbles inside make it less clear, so fill right to the brim.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark the level on the straw with the marker. Write 'room' and the real thermometer's room reading next to it.  
  *Parent says:* Let's mark where the water is now. What will happen to it in hot water? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Mark the water level. Now guess: what will hot water do to it? Why?
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the bottle in the bowl of hot water. 2. Watch the straw for 5 minutes. 3. Mark the highest level, and write the hot water's real temperature beside it.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the straw closely. What is the coloured water doing?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the straw. Is the water going up or down?
  - Note: In the first few seconds the level may dip a little, because the glass warms and expands first. Then the water warms and climbs, often several centimetres. This is real and worth pointing out.
  - Check: Why did the coloured water climb up the straw? → **The water in the bottle warmed, expanded and needed more space, so it went up the straw**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Move the bottle into the ice water. 2. Watch for 5 minutes. 3. Mark the lowest level.  
  *Parent says:* Now into the cold. What do you think will happen? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Now put it in ice water. What happens to the level?
  - Note: The level falls, often below the room mark. Your child now has a thermometer with three marks. A real thermometer was made the same way, with marks for ice and boiling water.

## Worksheets
### Heat Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (fill) The tool we use to measure how hot or cold something is, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['thermometer']. *Why:* A thermometer measures temperature. Your hand can only tell you whether it is warming up or cooling down.
2. (fill) On the Celsius scale, pure water freezes at ____ degrees and boils at ____ degrees.  
   **Answer:** ['0', '100']. *Why:* The Celsius scale was made by marking melting ice as 0 and boiling water as 100, and cutting the space between into 100 equal degrees.
3. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 3, dips a thermometer into hot water and reads it straight away. What is wrong with her reading?  
   Options: Nothing, a thermometer is right at once / It will be too low, because the thermometer has not warmed up to the water yet / It will be too high, because thermometers start hot  
   **Answer:** It will be too low, because the thermometer has not warmed up to the water yet. *Why:* Heat takes about a minute to move into the thermometer's bulb. Until then the thermometer is cooler than the water, so it reads too low. A strong answer says: wait until the line stops moving.
4. (mcq) Farhan wants to measure the water in the fridge. He has a fever thermometer and a laboratory thermometer. Which should he use?  
   Options: The fever thermometer, because it is digital / The laboratory thermometer, because a fever thermometer reads only from about 32 to 42 °C / Either one  
   **Answer:** The laboratory thermometer, because a fever thermometer reads only from about 32 to 42 °C. *Why:* Fridge water is about 4 to 8 °C, far below what a fever thermometer can read, so it would just show 'Lo'. A laboratory thermometer reads from about minus 10 to 110 °C.
5. (truefalse) When you hold an ice cube, cold moves from the ice into your hand.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Heat moves from your warmer hand into the ice. Your hand cools because it is losing heat, and the ice melts because it is gaining heat. Nothing called cold moves.
6. (truefalse) A tumbler of hot water and a tumbler of cold water left in the same room for 3 hours will read about the same.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both share heat with the room until they reach room temperature. In the Hot Tumbler, Cold Tumbler test, both ended within a degree or two of the room.
7. (sort) Sort these. Is each thing gaining heat or losing heat?  
   **Answer:** curd just taken out of the fridge, on the table → Gaining heat; a hot idli steamer after the stove is off → Losing heat; an ice cube in your hand → Gaining heat; your hand holding an ice cube → Losing heat; a cold bottle of water on a sunny sill → Gaining heat; hot rasam in a bowl on the table → Losing heat. *Why:* A thing gains heat when what is around it is warmer, and loses heat when what is around it is cooler. ## Applications
- Doctors and parents use clinical thermometers to check for fever.
- Weather stations measure the air temperature in a shady box, so the Sun does not heat the thermometer itself.
- Cooks use probe thermometers to check that milk is warm enough for curd, or that oil is hot enough for frying.
- A fridge and an AC pump heat out of a room or a box, and let it out outside.
- Railway tracks and concrete roads are laid with small gaps, so they have room to expand on hot days.
- Hospitals keep vaccines between 2 and 8 °C, and check the temperature every day.
- A pressure cooker cooks dal faster because the water inside boils at about 120 °C instead of 100 °C.
- Engines have radiators that pass their heat into the air, so they do not overheat.

## Open questions
- **Does hot water really freeze faster than cold water, and if so, why?** Some careful experiments find it does, and others find it does not. Scientists still argue about whether the Mpemba effect is real and what causes it.
- **Is the one-way spreading of heat the reason time only goes forwards?** Heat always spreads from hot to cold, and never by itself the other way, just as time never runs backwards. Many physicists think the two are linked, but nobody is sure exactly how.
- **How do our skin cells sense warmth and cold so precisely?** Scientists have found special gates in nerve cells that open at certain temperatures. But how the brain turns their signals into the feeling of warmth is not fully understood.

## Warm-up
