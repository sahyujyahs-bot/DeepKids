# How animals learn: by trying, and by copying others
`sci.animal-behaviour.animal-learning.L3`

**Summary.** Watch someone tap a box twice before opening it, and you will probably tap it too, even if the tap does nothing. Animals learn in three main ways: from what happened before, by watching others, and by working things out. A dog learns a trick because the trick brought a treat, or because she watched her owner do it first. Crows learn from other crows which people are dangerous, and some crows use one stick to reach another stick. Young chimpanzees learn to fish for termites by watching their mothers for years. You will test whether people copy a step that does nothing, and compare your results with what chimpanzees did.

**Intro.** *Try this with a grown-up. Put a sweet inside a clear box and close the lid. Tap the lid twice with a spoon, then open it and take the sweet out. Put the sweet back and say, 'Your turn.' Did they tap the lid too? The tap did nothing, so why would anyone copy it?* Learning means changing what you do because of what you have seen or what has happened to you. Every animal learns, from a bee to an elephant. Some animals mostly learn alone, by trying. Others learn a lot by watching. In this unit you find out how dogs, crows and chimpanzees learn, and you test how closely people copy.

## Terms
- **Learning**: Learning is changing what you do because of what you saw or what happened to you before. *e.g.* A street dog who was once given biscuits at a tea stall comes back to the same stall every evening.
- **Instinct**: An instinct is something an animal can do without learning. They are born able to do so. *e.g.* A baby spider spins her first web without ever having seen one made.
- **Reward**: A reward is something good that comes after an action, so the animal is more likely to do that action again. *e.g.* A dog gets a piece of chapati each time she sits when asked, so she sits more quickly next time.
- **Trial and error**: Trial and error means trying different ways until one works, then keeping the way that worked. *e.g.* A crow pecks at a tied plastic bag in different places until one peck tears it open.
- **Social learning**: Social learning is learning from others: by watching them, listening to them or following them. *e.g.* A young elephant learns which plants to eat by eating what her mother eats.
- **Imitation**: Imitation is copying the exact actions you saw someone do, step by step. *e.g.* Your little cousin holds a phone to her ear and nods, just the way her mother does.
- **Emulation**: Emulation is copying the goal, not the exact actions. You see what someone got, then find your own way to get it. *e.g.* You see your friend get a mango off a branch with a stick. You get one by climbing a stool instead.
- **Overimitation**: Overimitation is copying steps that do nothing, even when you could see that they do nothing. *e.g.* A girl watches her uncle tap a jar lid three times before opening it, and taps it three times too.
- **Tool**: A tool is an object an animal holds or moves to get something done, such as a stick for reaching food. *e.g.* A chimpanzee pokes a grass stem into a termite mound and pulls it out covered in termites.
- **Tradition**: A tradition is a way of doing something that spreads through a group by social learning and lasts for a long time. *e.g.* Some groups of chimpanzees in West Africa crack nuts with stones. Many other groups of chimpanzees never do.

## Explanations
### Born knowing, or learned? · *defined*
**Definition:** An instinct is something an animal can do without learning. Learning is changing what you do because of what you saw or what happened before.

A baby sea turtle hatches at night and crawls towards the sea. Nobody showed her the way. That is an instinct. A street dog who runs to the gate when the milk van comes has learned it, because the van has meant food before. Most of what animals do mixes the two. A weaver bird is born able to weave, but young males make messy nests at first and better ones with practice.
- e.g. A chick pecks at grains soon after hatching. That is an instinct.
- e.g. A cat runs to the kitchen at the sound of the fish seller's bicycle bell. That is learned.
- e.g. A koel chick raised by crows still grows up calling 'koo-oo' like a koel, not like a crow.
### Learning from what happened before · *defined*
**Definition:** When an action brings something good, an animal does it more often. When an action brings something bad, they do it less. The good thing is called a reward.

This is how most dogs learn tricks. The dog sits, and a treat comes. After a few times, she sits as soon as she hears 'sit'. Trainers reward small steps, one at a time, to build a long trick. Some dogs also learn tricks by watching. Claudia Fugazza and Adam Miklósi, scientists in Hungary, tried another way. The owner did an action with an object, then said 'Do it!'. Dogs taught this way learned some object tricks faster than dogs taught only with rewards.
- e.g. A dog learns to give her paw because a treat followed each time.
- e.g. Pigeons fly down to a man who scatters grain at the same spot every morning.
- e.g. Honeybees learn which colour of flower has the most nectar after a few visits.
### Learning by working it out · *defined*
**Definition:** Working it out means solving a new problem by putting together what you already know, without trying every way first.

Chaser was a border collie, a kind of sheepdog, in America. Her owner, John Pilley, taught her the names of 1,022 toys over three years. Then he put one new toy among toys she knew, and asked for it by a name she had never heard. She picked the new toy. She worked out that the new name must belong to the new toy. New Caledonian crows, from islands in the Pacific Ocean, solve puzzles with several steps. In one test in 2010, crows used a short stick to pull out a longer stick, then used the long stick to reach meat.
- e.g. Chaser picked the only new toy when she heard a new name.
- e.g. A crow used a short stick to get a long stick, then the long stick to get food.
- e.g. You can do the same. If someone asks for 'the dax' and only one thing on the table is new to you, you pick that one.
### Learning by watching others · *defined*
**Definition:** Social learning is learning from others. Animals learn which foods are safe, which places are dangerous and how to use tools by watching others.

At Gombe in Tanzania, young chimpanzees learn to fish for termites. They poke a grass stem into the mound, wait, and pull it out covered in termites. It takes years to learn. In 2004, Elizabeth Lonsdorf and her team found that daughters watched their mothers fish more than sons did. The daughters learned it younger, and they pushed their stems in as deep as their mothers did. Crows learn this way too. In Seattle, crows that had never been caught learned to scold a mask that other crows scolded.
- e.g. A young chimpanzee watches her mother fish for termites, then tries with her own stem.
- e.g. A crow that was never harmed scolds a person whom other crows scold.
- e.g. A young elephant eats the plants her mother eats.
### Copying every step, or copying what works? · *debated*
Chimpanzees shown a clear puzzle box skipped a step that did nothing. Children copied it. This is called overimitation. Grown-ups do it even more than children. Scientists agree that it happens, in children from Scotland to the Kalahari Desert in Africa. They argue about why people do it.
- e.g. Most children aged 3 and 4 copied a useless poke into a puzzle box, even when the box was clear.
- e.g. In a 2011 study in Scotland, grown-ups copied useless steps even more than children did.
- e.g. Chimpanzees copied the useless step only when they could not see inside the box.
- **Some scientists, such as Derek Lyons and Frank Keil:** People think that every step a grown-up shows must have a reason, even if they cannot see it. So they copy everything, to be safe.
- **Other scientists, such as Harriet Over and Malinda Carpenter:** People copy to fit in and to show they are like the person who showed them. Copying exactly is a way of being friends.
- **Others again:** Copying everything is a quick way to pick up skills too hard to work out alone, such as making a fishing net. It helps more often than it wastes time.
### Do animals have traditions? · *debated*
In 1953, on the island of Koshima in Japan, a young macaque monkey called Imo began washing sweet potatoes in water. Over the next years, most of her group did it too. In 2015, Lucy Aplin and her team in Wytham Woods, near Oxford, taught two great tits in each of several groups to open a puzzle box by sliding its door one way. Each group spread its own way, and the habit was still there the next year. Scientists argue about whether to call these traditions 'culture'.
- e.g. Macaques on Koshima wash sweet potatoes, a habit that began with Imo.
- e.g. Great tits in each group slid the puzzle door the same way as their group.
- e.g. Some groups of chimpanzees crack nuts with stones, and others nearby do not.
- **Many animal scientists, such as Andrew Whiten and Lucy Aplin:** Animals have culture: ways of doing things that are learned from others and last in a group.
- **Some other scientists:** Animal traditions are real, but human culture is different, because people build on each other's ideas for thousands of years. They keep the word 'culture' for that.

## Mostly agreed
- Almost all animals learn, from bees and fish to birds and apes. *How we know:* Scientists have trained bees, fish, birds and mammals to tell colours, smells or sounds apart for a reward.
- Many animals learn by watching others, not only by trying alone. *How we know:* Crows that were never caught learned to scold a dangerous mask. Young chimpanzees copy their mothers' termite fishing.
- People copy steps that do nothing more often than chimpanzees do. *How we know:* In puzzle box tests, chimpanzees skipped a useless step when they could see it did nothing. Children and grown-ups kept copying it.
- Some animals keep ways of doing things in their group for years. *How we know:* Great tits in Wytham Woods kept their group's way of opening a puzzle box into the next year. Koshima macaques still wash food in water.

## How we found out
**Victoria Horner and Andrew Whiten, at the University of St Andrews, Scotland, 2005.** People have long said 'monkey see, monkey do'. They thought apes copy everything they see, without thinking. In 2005, Victoria Horner and Andrew Whiten, scientists at the University of St Andrews in Scotland, tested this. They made a puzzle box with a reward inside. A person showed young chimpanzees how to open it. First the person poked a stick into a hole at the top. That step did nothing. Then the person opened a door at the front and pulled the reward out. When the box was black, so nobody could see inside, the chimpanzees copied both steps. When the box was clear, they could see that the top hole led nowhere. Then most chimpanzees skipped it and went straight to the door. Children aged 3 and 4 were shown the same boxes. Most of them copied the useless poke, even with the clear box. So the old saying was wrong. Chimpanzees copied what worked. The children copied everything. In 2007, other scientists gave this a name: overimitation.  
*What people believed before:* People thought apes copy everything they see without thinking: 'monkey see, monkey do'. The clear box showed that chimpanzees skip a step they can see is useless, while children copy it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Monkey see, monkey do: apes copy everything without thinking.** (An old saying; *rejected*)  
  For: Apes do copy a lot, and they learn to use tools by watching.  
  Against: Chimpanzees shown a clear puzzle box skipped the useless step and went straight for the reward. People were the ones who copied everything.
- **Animals act only by instinct. Only people really learn.** (A common idea in the past; *rejected*)  
  For: Many animals do things they were never taught, such as a spider spinning a web.  
  Against: Dogs, crows, bees, fish and chimpanzees all change what they do after experience. Chaser learned over 1,000 names.
- **Copying is the lazy way to learn. Working things out yourself is always better.** (A common idea; *debated*)  
  For: Working it out helps you understand why something works.  
  Against: Some skills, like termite fishing or weaving a basket, are too hard to work out alone. Young chimpanzees take years and learn faster by watching.
- **Only people have culture.** (Some scientists today; *debated*)  
  For: People build on each other's ideas over thousands of years, from the wheel to the phone. No animal does that as far as we know.  
  Against: Chimpanzees, macaques, great tits, whales and dolphins keep group habits learned from each other for years.

## Roots
We have learned how animals learn by trying, by watching and by working things out, and we tested how people copy. Now we ask what copying tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In your test, most people copied the tap, though the clear box showed it did nothing. Chimpanzees shown a clear box mostly skipped such a step. People copy whole ways of doing things before they understand them. So a habit can pass down for generations without anyone knowing why.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call copying the simple way to learn, and say 'monkey see, monkey do'. But the chimpanzees skipped the useless step, and the people copied it. Copying every step exactly is a strong human way of learning, and it carries reasons we may have forgotten.  
*Evidence:* In Horner and Whiten's clear box test, most chimpanzees skipped the useless poke and most children copied it. Grown-ups copy useless steps even more. In your own test, count how many people tapped the lid and how many could say why.

- *who am i:* Much of what you do, from how you eat to how you draw a kolam, you copied from people before you understood it.
- *what is life:* A crow, a dog and a chimpanzee keep learning all their lives, from what happens and from each other.
- *what is this world:* Ways of doing things travel from mind to mind, among birds, apes and people, and some last for years.

One great tit in each group was shown one way to open a box. Within weeks, most of the group used that same way. One bird's way became the way of many birds, carried by watching. Every tradition, in a crow family, a chimpanzee group or a home in Madurai, began as one mind's way of doing something and spread into many minds.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.2:** The teacher tells the students who are leaving: follow the good things we did, and not the others. (The teacher asks the students to copy only what is good, not every step. That is the choice the chimpanzees made with the clear box, and the one people often skip.)
- **Kalama Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 3.65):** The Buddha tells the Kalama people not to accept something only because it has been handed down, or because a teacher said it. Test it for yourself, and keep what you find to be good. (Meenakshi tested the tap and the turn one at a time. She kept the tap, which really called the crows, and dropped the turn.)
- Science (settled): Children in many countries, from Scotland to the Kalahari Desert, copy useless steps, and grown-ups copy them even more.
- Science (debated): Why people overimitate is still argued. Some scientists say we expect every step to have a reason. Others say we copy to fit in.
- Other view, Harriet Over and Malinda Carpenter, psychologists: Over-copying may not be about learning at all. Children copy useless steps mainly to show they are like the person who showed them, the way friends copy each other's words.

- Ask: If you copied a step and never found out why, is it still yours?
- Ask: Is there a step in your home that holds a reason nobody remembers?
- Ask: When is it wise to copy everything, and when is it wise to test each step?

## Story: The Tap on the Plate
*Meenakshi, her grandmother, Arjun and the crows are made up. Feeding crows a little rice before lunch is a real custom in many Tamil homes. Animals learning that a sound comes before food is real and well studied, but this story's crows are invented.*

Meenakshi was nine. She lived with her Paati, her grandmother, on the fourth floor of a building in Madurai.

Every day, just before lunch, Paati did the same three things. She put a spoonful of rice on a small steel plate. She tapped the edge of the plate three times with a steel ladle. Ting, ting, ting. Then she turned the plate around once, set it on the balcony ledge, and stepped back.

Soon, the crows came. One, then two, sometimes four. They hopped along the ledge, looked at Paati with one eye, and ate the rice.

"Paati, why do you tap the plate?" Meenakshi asked one day.

Paati thought about it. "My mother tapped it," she said. "So I tap it."

"And why do you turn it around?"

"My mother turned it too." Paati laughed. "I never asked her why. I just did what she did."

That summer, Meenakshi's cousin Arjun came from Bengaluru to stay. He was seven. On his first day, he watched Paati feed the crows. On the second day, he asked if he could do it.

He put rice on the plate. Ting, ting, ting. He turned the plate around once, exactly as Paati did. He even stepped back and stood with his hands behind his back, the way Paati stood.

Meenakshi giggled. "You copied everything," she said. "Even the hands."

"That's how you do it," said Arjun.

Meenakshi wasn't sure. The tapping and the turning looked useless. Crows eat rice. Why should they care about a ting or a turn?

So she decided to find out. She would do one test each day, and change only one thing.

On Monday, she put the rice on the plate. She did not tap. She turned the plate, put it on the ledge and stepped back. Then she waited.

Two crows were sitting on the neighbour's water tank. They did not look round. Five minutes went by, then ten. A pigeon came. At last, after nearly twenty minutes, one crow flew down.

On Tuesday, she did everything the usual way. Ting, ting, ting. The two crows on the water tank turned their heads at once. In less than a minute, they were on the ledge.

Meenakshi ran to Paati. "The crows know the tap!" she said. "They hear the ting, and they know rice is coming."

Paati put down her vegetables. "So that is why my mother tapped," she said slowly. "She was calling them. And I never knew."

On Wednesday, Meenakshi tested the turn. She tapped, but she did not turn the plate. The crows came just as fast as on Tuesday.

"The turning does nothing," she told Arjun. "The crows don't see it, and they don't care."

Arjun frowned. "Then why did Paati's mother turn it?"

Nobody knew. Paati rang her older sister in Thanjavur that evening. Her sister remembered. Their mother's old plate had a dent in one side. Rice rolled into the dent, so she always turned the plate to put the dent at the back.

"That plate was thrown away forty years ago," Paati said. "And I have been turning a plate with no dent ever since."

Everyone laughed, Paati most of all.

The next day, Meenakshi showed Arjun how to feed the crows. "Tap three times," she said. "That part calls them. You can skip the turn. That part was for a plate with a dent."

Arjun tapped. Ting, ting, ting. The crows came. Then, very quickly, he turned the plate around anyway.

"Arjun!"

"Just in case," he said, and grinned.

Meenakshi wrote it all in her notebook. Some steps we copy carry a reason that people have forgotten. Some steps are left over from a time that is gone. The only way to tell which is which is to test them, one at a time.

And the crows? The crows had been learning all along. They had learned that a ting from the fourth floor meant rice. Nobody had taught them. They had worked it out from what happened, day after day, for years.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the crows had learned that the tap comes before the rice, so the tap really did call them. That is learning from what happened before. The turning was a step copied from a mother whose plate had a dent, long after the plate was gone. Paati and Arjun copied both steps without knowing which one mattered. That is overimitation, and people do it all the time. Meenakshi changed one thing at a time to find out which step mattered. That is a fair test.

- Ask: Why did the crows come so quickly when Meenakshi tapped the plate?
- Ask: Why did Paati turn the plate around? Did the crows care?
- Ask: Meenakshi changed only one thing each day. Why was that important?
- Ask: Arjun turned the plate 'just in case'. Do you think he was being silly or careful?
- Ask: Is there something in your home that everyone does, but nobody knows why?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Useless Step Test: do people copy a step that does nothing? (30 min)
- **question:** If you show people how to open a box, and one step clearly does nothing, will they copy that step?
- **predict:** Guess first. When each person opens the box, will they tap the lid like you did? Will they turn the box around? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every person sees exactly the same show, with the same words. Test one person at a time, while the others wait in another room, so nobody copies anybody but you. Put the box, the spoon and the prize back in the same place each time.
- **record:** Fill in the results table, one line for each person. Then write: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Person | Tapped the lid? (yes or no) | Turned the box around? (yes or no) | Opened the lid and took the prize? (yes or no) | Why they said they did it
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the prize in the clear box and close the lid. 2. Put the box and the spoon on a table. 3. Practise the show three times. Tap the lid twice with the spoon. Turn the box around once. Lift the lid and take the prize.  
  *Parent says:* You are the scientist today. You will show people how to open this box. The tap and the turn do nothing. Only lifting the lid matters.  
  *Child mode:* Put the prize in the box. Close the lid. Tap the lid twice with the spoon. Turn the box around once. Lift the lid and take the prize. Practise three times.
  - Note: The box must be clear, so everyone can see that tapping and turning do nothing. Your child should do the same show every time, at the same speed.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what the people will do, and why. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think? Will they tap the lid too? Will they turn the box? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Will they tap the lid? Will they turn the box? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why might someone copy the tap, even though it does nothing? (good: They think it might matter.; They want to do it the right way.; They just copy what they see.)
  - Note: Don't say whether the guess is right. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Bring in the first person. Everyone else waits in another room. 2. Your child says: 'Watch me.' 3. Your child does the show. 4. They put the prize back, close the lid and say: 'Now you get the prize out.' 5. Watch and write down what the person does.  
  *Parent says:* Say only 'Watch me', and then 'Now you get the prize out'. Don't say 'copy me'.  
  *Child mode:* Say 'Watch me.' Do the show. Put the prize back. Say 'Now you get the prize out.' Watch what they do. Write it down.
  - Note: Saying 'copy me' would make people copy everything. The test is whether they choose to.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test each other person the same way, one at a time. Don't let the next person watch.  
  *Parent says:* Same show, same words, for every person. Then it is fair.  
  *Child mode:* Test the next person. Same show. Same words. Write what they do.
  - Note: If you can, include one younger child and one grown-up. In a 2011 study in Scotland, grown-ups copied useless steps even more than children did.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When everyone has had a turn, ask each person why they tapped the lid, or why not. Write their answers.  
  *Parent says:* Now we ask them why. Their answers are evidence too.  
  *Child mode:* Ask each person why they tapped, or why they didn't. Write their answer.
  - Ask: What did people say when you asked why they tapped? (good: 'I thought I had to.'; 'You did it, so I did it.'; 'I didn't think about it.')
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count how many people tapped and how many turned the box. 2. Help your child say: what I think, what I saw, and why. 3. Then tell them what chimpanzees did.  
  *Parent says:* Scientists did this test with chimpanzees and a clear box. Most chimpanzees skipped the useless step and went straight for the reward. Children kept copying it.  
  *Child mode:* Count how many people tapped. Say what you think, what you saw, and why. Then hear what the chimpanzees did.
  - Note: If nobody tapped, that is a real result too. Ask them why. Perhaps they saw the tap did nothing, as the chimpanzees did, or guessed it was a test. Scientists find that most people copy, but not everyone.
  - Check: Chimpanzees were shown a clear puzzle box with a useless step. Most skipped that step. What did most children do? → **They copied the useless step**
### e2 · Pass It On: does a way of opening a box travel down a chain? (30 min)
- **question:** If a box can be opened two ways, and each person shows the next, will the way you showed travel down the whole chain?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the last person in the chain open the box the same way you showed the first person? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each person sees only the person just before them. Nobody sees the card that says which way you chose. If you can, one extra person gets the box with no show at all, so you can see which door people pick on their own.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the flaps in the box with scissors or a cutter.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the chain as a row of stick people, with an arrow from each to the next and the door they used.
- **results table:** Person in the chain | Who showed them | Door they used (top or side) | Did they also show the next person? (yes or no)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the prize in the box. 2. Choose a door in secret: top or side. 3. Write it on a slip of paper and fold it.  
  *Parent says:* This box opens two ways. Both work. You choose one way to show, and keep it secret.  
  *Child mode:* Put the prize in the box. Choose a door: top or side. Write it on paper. Keep it secret.
  - Note: If you are making the box now, cut the flaps for your child. Both flaps must open just as easily, or people will choose the easy one.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Will the last person use the door you chose? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the last person use your door? Why?
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Bring in person 1. 2. Open your door, take the prize, put it back and close the door. 3. Say: 'Now you get the prize out.' 4. Write which door they use.  
  *Parent says:* Show your door once, slowly. Say only, 'Now you get the prize out.'  
  *Child mode:* Show your door once. Put the prize back. Say 'Now you get the prize out.' Which door did they use?
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask person 1 to show person 2 how they got the prize. 2. If there is a person 3, person 2 shows them. 3. Record each door.  
  *Parent says:* Now you only watch. Each person shows the next.  
  *Child mode:* Now just watch. Each person shows the next one. Write which door each person uses.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If you have one more person, give them the box with no show. Just say: 'There is a prize inside. Get it out.'  
  *Parent says:* This person had no show. Which door do they pick on their own?  
  *Child mode:* Give the box to one more person. Show them nothing. Which door do they pick?
  - Note: This is the comparison. If the no-show person picks a door at random, and the chain kept yours, the chain shows copying, not just the easy door.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the secret slip aloud. Compare it with the doors in the table.  
  *Parent says:* In 2006, scientists did this with chimpanzees. The 'lift the door' way travelled down a chain of six chimpanzees.  
  *Child mode:* Read your secret slip. Did your door reach the end of the chain?
  - Check: A box opens by the top door or the side door. You showed the side door. Everyone in the chain then used the side door. What does that show? → **Each person learned the way from the person before them**

### How Animals Learn (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (fill) Something an animal can do without learning, from birth, is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['instinct']. *Why:* An instinct is something an animal is born able to do, like a spider spinning a web.

   **Answer:** A chick pecks at grains soon after hatching → Instinct; A dog sits when asked, for a treat → Learned; A baby turtle crawls to the sea → Instinct; Pigeons gather where a man scatters grain every morning → Learned; A young chimpanzee fishes for termites with a stem → Learned; A baby spider spins her first web → Instinct. *Why:* Instincts need no teaching and no practice. Learned things come from what happened before or from watching others. A strong answer says how each learned thing was learned.
3. (mcq) Which of these is a reward for a dog learning to sit?  
   Options: A piece of chapati / A loud noise / Being ignored / A bath  
   **Answer:** A piece of chapati. *Why:* A reward is something good that comes after an action, so the action happens more. Food is a reward for most dogs. A loud noise would make sitting happen less.
4. (truefalse) A friend says: 'Only people can learn. Animals do everything by instinct.' Is your friend right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dogs learn tricks, crows learn which people are dangerous, and bees learn flower colours. A strong answer gives one example.

   **Answer:** Imitation → copying the exact actions you saw; Emulation → copying the goal, in your own way; Reward → something good that comes after an action; Tool → an object used to get something done; Tradition → a way of doing things that spreads and lasts in a group. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit.    Options: By instinct / By watching others / By a reward from people / She is not learning

   **Answer:** Nut cracking is a tradition, learned from others in the group. *Why:* Differences between groups that are not explained by instinct or by what is around them point to traditions. Young chimpanzees learn nut cracking by watching for years.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A baby sea turtle hatches on a dark beach and crawls straight to the sea. Nobody showed her. How does she know what to do?  
   Options: She learned it by watching her mother / She was born knowing: it is an instinct / She was rewarded for it / She worked it out from a map  
   **Answer:** She was born knowing: it is an instinct. *Why:* Sea turtle mothers lay their eggs and swim away, so the babies never meet them. Crawling to the sea is an instinct. Picking 'watching her mother' would fit a chimpanzee, but not a turtle.
2. (mcq) Revathi, a girl in Kochi, gives her dog a biscuit each time he gives his paw. Soon he gives his paw as soon as she holds out her hand. How did he learn?  
   Options: He was born knowing it / From what happened before: the paw brought a biscuit / By watching another dog / By luck  
   **Answer:** From what happened before: the paw brought a biscuit. *Why:* The biscuit is a reward. Actions that bring a reward happen more often. That is how most dogs learn tricks.
3. (mcq) At Gombe in Tanzania, young chimpanzees learn to fish for termites with grass stems. How do they mostly learn it?  
   Options: They are born knowing how / By watching their mothers, for years / Scientists teach them / They read the termites' smell  
   **Answer:** By watching their mothers, for years. *Why:* Young chimpanzees watch their mothers fish and practise for years. Daughters who watched more learned it younger. Termite fishing is not an instinct.
4. (mcq) Crows in Seattle, in America, scolded a person wearing a certain mask. Some of the scolding crows had never been caught or harmed. How did they learn the mask was dangerous?  
   Options: From other crows scolding the mask / They were born afraid of masks / The mask smelled bad / The person shouted at them  
   **Answer:** From other crows scolding the mask. *Why:* Crows that had never been caught learned from seeing other crows scold the mask. That is social learning. The scolding spread over the years.
5. (mcq) Chimpanzees and children watched a person open a clear puzzle box. First the person poked a stick in a top hole that did nothing. Then they opened the door. What happened?  
   Options: Both chimpanzees and children skipped the poke / Both copied the poke / Most chimpanzees skipped the poke, and most children copied it / Most children skipped the poke, and chimpanzees copied it  
   **Answer:** Most chimpanzees skipped the poke, and most children copied it. *Why:* The clear box showed the poke did nothing. Most chimpanzees went straight for the door. Most children copied every step. This is overimitation.
6. (mcq) What is the difference between imitation and emulation?  
   Options: Imitation copies the exact actions. Emulation copies the goal, in your own way. / They mean the same thing / Imitation is for animals, emulation is for people / Emulation is copying sounds  
   **Answer:** Imitation copies the exact actions. Emulation copies the goal, in your own way.. *Why:* Imitation copies every step. Emulation sees what someone got, and gets it your own way. The chimpanzees with the clear box emulated. The children imitated.
7. (mcq) In the Useless Step Test, why do you test one person at a time, with the others in another room?  
   Options: So it is quicker / So each person copies only you, not the person before them / So nobody eats the prize / Because the box is small  
   **Answer:** So each person copies only you, not the person before them. *Why:* If the next person watched, they might copy the person before instead of you. Then you would not know what your show did. Keeping things the same for every person makes the test fair.
8. (mcq) Lakshmi, a girl in Mysuru, does the Useless Step Test with 4 people. 3 of them tap the lid. What can she say?  
   Options: Everyone in the world taps the lid / Most of the people she tested copied the useless tap / The tap opens the box / Nobody copies  
   **Answer:** Most of the people she tested copied the useless tap. *Why:* Her evidence is about the 4 people she tested. 3 out of 4 is most of them. To say more, she would need to test many more people.
9. (mcq) Great tits in Wytham Woods, in England, were shown a puzzle box that opened by sliding a door left or right. Each group kept using the way their first birds were taught. What is this called?  
   Options: An instinct / A tradition / Trial and error / An accident  
   **Answer:** A tradition. *Why:* A way of doing something that spreads by social learning and lasts in a group is a tradition. Both ways worked, so the birds were copying their group.
10. (mcq) In 1953, a young macaque called Imo, on the island of Koshima in Japan, began washing sweet potatoes in water. What happened next?  
   Options: She stopped after a day / Over the next years, most of her group began washing their food too / Only Imo ever did it / The sweet potatoes stopped growing  
   **Answer:** Over the next years, most of her group began washing their food too. *Why:* Others watched Imo and began to wash their food too. The habit spread and lasted. Macaques on Koshima still wash food today.

## Applications
- Dog trainers use rewards to teach guide dogs, rescue dogs and sniffer dogs at airports.
- Some trainers use 'Do as I do', where the dog watches a person first.
- Zoos give animals puzzle feeders, so they can learn and solve problems every day.
- Teachers show a skill step by step, because children learn so well by watching.
- Scientists who protect animals teach young ones raised by people the skills their parents would have shown them, such as which foods are safe.
- Farmers and city workers learn how monkeys, crows and pigeons learn, to keep them away from crops and food without harming them.

## Open questions
- **Why do people copy useless steps so much more than chimpanzees do?** Some scientists say we think every step must have a reason. Others say we copy to fit in. The evidence supports parts of both.
- **What did Chaser understand when she heard a toy's name?** She could fetch the right toy, but scientists can't ask her what the word meant to her. People still argue about how close it is to understanding a word.
- **Do crows teach their young, or do the young just watch?** Teaching means changing what you do to help another learn. It is hard to tell from watching wild crows.
- **How many animals have traditions that we haven't noticed yet?** Finding a tradition takes years of watching many groups. Most animals have never been watched that long.

## Connected maths
- In the Useless Step Test, Nandini tested 4 people in her family. 3 of them tapped the lid. What fraction of the people tapped the lid? **3 out of 4, or three quarters.**. 4 people were tested, so the whole is 4. 3 of them tapped. That is 3 out of 4, which we write as 3/4, three quarters. Only 1 out of 4 did not tap. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- In Termite Fishing, Kavya caught 2, 1, 3, 2 and 2 grains in her 5 dips before watching. After watching, she caught 5, 6, 4, 7 and 8. How many grains did she catch before watching, and after? How many times as many did she catch after? **10 before, 30 after. 3 times as many.**. Before: 2 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 10. After: 5 + 6 + 4 + 7 + 8 = 30. 30 divided by 10 is 3, so she caught 3 times as many after watching. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Chaser learned the names of 1,022 toys. Rico, another dog, learned about 200 names. About how many more names did Chaser learn than Rico? **About 822 more.**. Take the smaller number from the bigger one. 1,022 take away 200 is 822. So Chaser learned about 822 more names. Rico's number is rounded, so the answer is also 'about'. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In the Plate Lesson, the first bird each day went to: day 1 red, day 2 red, day 3 blue, day 4 red, day 5 red. Make a tally. How many times was it red, and how many blue? Do the results suggest the birds learned red means food? **Red 4 times, blue once. Yes, it suggests they learned, but 5 days is a small test.**. Count the reds: days 1, 2, 4 and 5, so 4. Blue: day 3, so 1. If birds picked at random, you would expect about half red and half blue. 4 out of 5 is more than half, which suggests learning. More days would make it surer. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
