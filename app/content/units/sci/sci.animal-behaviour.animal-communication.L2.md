# How animals talk: the messages in barks, purrs and birdsong
`sci.animal-behaviour.animal-communication.L2`

**Summary.** A dog barks one way at a stranger and another way when she wants to play. Animals send each other messages with sounds, with their bodies and with touch. A sound or movement that carries a message to another animal is called a signal. Your child sends four messages using only the made-up word 'la-la', from behind a door, and finds out whether the tune of a sound can carry a message with no words at all.

**Intro.** *A dog is barking at the gate downstairs. You cannot see the dog. Is the dog saying 'go away', 'let's play' or 'I am lonely'? How could you tell, just by listening?* Animals talk to each other all day. Dogs bark and growl. Cats purr and hiss. Crows caw, koels call and crickets chirp at night. Each sound is a message for someone. In this unit you listen to animal sounds and find out what they mean. Then you test whether a message can get through with no words at all.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what communicating means: one animal sends a message, and another animal notices and changes what they do.
1. By the end I can name three ways animals send messages: with sounds, with their bodies and with touch.
2. By the end I can say how a cat purrs, and that a purr does not always mean 'I am happy'.
3. By the end I can tell a bird's song from a bird's call, and say what each one is for.
4. By the end I can say that low, rough sounds often mean 'stay away', and high, soft sounds often mean 'I am friendly' or 'help me'.
5. By the end I can test whether a message gets through from the tune of a sound alone, and say what I found.

## Terms
- **Communicate**: To communicate is to send a message to someone else, who notices it and acts on it. *e.g.* A mother hen clucks, and her chicks run to her. She has communicated with them.
- **Signal**: A signal is a sound, a movement, a touch or a smell that carries a message. *e.g.* A cat walking towards you with her tail straight up is giving a signal. It usually means 'hello, I am friendly'.
- **Call**: A call is a short animal sound with one message, such as 'danger', 'food here' or 'where are you?'. *e.g.* A crow gives a loud, harsh caw when a cat walks below the tree.
- **Song**: A song is a long pattern of sounds that a bird repeats, often to say 'this is my place' or to attract a partner. *e.g.* A magpie robin sings a long, sweet song from the top of a water tank early in the morning.
- **Alarm call**: An alarm call is a call that warns others that danger is near. *e.g.* When a cat comes close, a squirrel on a tree gives a fast 'chip-chip-chip' and flicks her tail.
- **Purr**: A purr is the soft, buzzing rumble a cat makes with her voice box, breathing in and breathing out. *e.g.* A cat curled up on your lap purrs, and you can feel the buzz in your legs.
- **Pitch**: Pitch is how high or low a sound is. A whistle has a high pitch. A big drum has a low pitch. *e.g.* A puppy's whine has a high pitch. A big dog's growl has a low pitch.
- **Vibrate**: To vibrate is to shake back and forth very fast. Every sound is made by something vibrating. *e.g.* Put your fingers on your throat and hum. You can feel your voice box vibrate.

## Explanations
### What communicating is · *defined*
**Definition:** Communicating is sending a signal that another being notices and acts on.

Every message needs two: a sender and a receiver. The sender gives a signal. The signal can be a sound, a movement, a touch or a smell. The receiver notices the signal and does something because of it. A crow caws at a cat, and the other crows fly up. A hen clucks, and her chicks come running. Humans are animals too, and we communicate with words, faces, hands and the tune of our voice.
- e.g. A street dog barks at a stranger, and the watchman comes to the gate.
- e.g. Two ants meet and touch antennae, then one turns round and follows the other's path.
- e.g. A baby cries, and her mother picks her up.
### How birds talk: songs and calls · *defined*
**Definition:** A bird's call is short and carries one message. A bird's song is longer, is repeated again and again, and often says 'this is my place' or 'choose me'.

Calls are for everyday messages: 'danger', 'I am here', 'feed me'. Songs are longer, and many songbirds learn them by listening to grown-up birds, the way you learned to talk. People once thought only male birds sing. In 2014 a team led by Karan Odom checked over a thousand kinds of songbirds. In most of the kinds that they could check, the females sing too.
- e.g. A male koel calls 'ku-oo, ku-oo', higher each time, again and again in the hot months. A female koel has her own sharp 'kik-kik-kik' call.
- e.g. A magpie robin sings a long, sweet song from a rooftop at dawn.
- e.g. A bulbul gives a sharp alarm call when a cat comes near the nest.
### What a dog's bark says · *defined*
**Definition:** A bark is a short, loud sound a dog makes to send a message. Dogs bark in different ways in different situations.

When a stranger comes, a dog's barks are often low, rough and fast, with no gaps. When a dog wants to play, the barks are often higher, with gaps between them. A dog left alone may give high barks and whines. In 2005 Péter Pongrácz and his team in Hungary played recordings of dog barks to people. Most people could tell the situations apart better than by chance, even people who had never owned a dog.
- e.g. Low, rough, fast barks at the gate: 'A stranger is here. Stay back.'
- e.g. Higher barks with gaps, and a play bow: 'Let's play!'
- e.g. A growl over a bowl of food: 'This is mine. Do not come closer.'
### Why cats purr · *debated*
A cat purrs with her voice box, the part of the throat that makes sounds. The purr goes on while she breathes in and while she breathes out. Her voice box shakes more than 20 times every second, so you can feel the buzz. Cats purr when they are calm and close to someone. They also purr when they are hungry, hurt or giving birth. So a purr does not always mean 'I am happy'.
- e.g. A cat purrs on a lap, with eyes half shut.
- e.g. A mother cat purrs while her kittens drink her milk.
- e.g. A cat with a hurt paw purrs at the vet.
- **Many vets and cat scientists:** A purr often means 'stay close' or 'I am calm with you'. A mother's purr may help her kittens find her, because they are born with their eyes and ears shut and can feel the buzz.
- **Karen McComb and her team, in England (2009):** When some cats purr for food, they hide a high cry inside the purr. People find this purr harder to ignore. So a purr can also be a way of asking.
- **Other scientists:** A cat may purr when hurt to calm herself. Some think the buzz may even help the body heal. This idea has not been shown yet.
### The tune carries the message · *idea*
In 1977 Eugene Morton, a bird scientist in America, compared the sounds of many birds and mammals. Low, rough sounds often mean 'stay away'. High, soft, clear sounds often mean 'I am friendly', 'I am scared' or 'help me'. A big body makes a deeper sound, so a low growl makes an animal sound bigger. In 2017 Piera Filippi and her team played sounds of frogs, alligators, pandas and elephants to people, and most people could hear whether the animal was calm or excited. This is a pattern with exceptions, not a rule for every sound.
- e.g. A dog growls low over a bone. A puppy whines high when left alone.
- e.g. A cat hisses with a rough sound when a strange dog comes near.
- e.g. People speak low and rough when they are angry, and high and soft to a baby.
### Messages without a voice · *defined*
**Definition:** Many signals are not made with a voice at all. Animals send messages with how they hold their bodies, with sounds made by other body parts, and with touch.

A cat with her tail straight up is saying hello. A cat with puffed-up fur and an arched back is saying 'stay away'. A dog does a play bow, front legs down and back end up, to say 'this is a game'. A male cricket chirps by rubbing one wing against the other, and female crickets hear him with ears on their front legs. In 2020 Tasmin Humphrey and Karen McComb found that cats often blink slowly back when a person slow-blinks at them.
- e.g. A dog doing a play bow, tail waving.
- e.g. A cricket rubbing his wings together in a potted plant at night.
- e.g. A cat slowly closing and opening her eyes at you from across the room.

## Mostly agreed
- Many animals send messages with sounds, body movements, touch and smell, and other animals act on them. *How we know:* Scientists record a signal, then play it back with a hidden speaker when nothing else is happening. If animals act on the sound alone, the sound carries a message. Tests like this have been done with monkeys, birds, frogs, crickets and many more.
- Cats purr with their voice box, and the purr goes on while they breathe in and out. *How we know:* Scientists have measured the voice box and breathing of purring cats. The buzz carries on through both breaths, which is why a purr sounds unbroken.
- Many songbirds learn their songs by listening to grown-up birds. *How we know:* In the 1950s William Thorpe raised young chaffinches who never heard a grown-up sing. They grew up singing a much plainer song than wild chaffinches.

## How we found out
**Dorothy Cheney and Robert Seyfarth, American scientists, with Peter Marler, after Thomas Struhsaker first noticed the calls, Struhsaker described the calls in 1967. Cheney, Seyfarth and Marler published their speaker tests in 1980..** Until the 1970s, many scientists thought animal calls were only cries of feeling, like a scream of fear. They did not think a call could tell others what kind of danger it was. In Amboseli, in Kenya, vervet monkeys give different alarm calls for leopards, eagles and snakes. Thomas Struhsaker had noticed this in the 1960s. Dorothy Cheney, Robert Seyfarth and Peter Marler wanted to test whether the sound alone carried the message. They recorded the calls. Then they hid a loudspeaker in a bush and played a call when no danger was there. At the leopard call, the monkeys ran up into the trees. At the eagle call, they looked up at the sky or ran into bushes. At the snake call, they stood up on their back legs and looked down at the grass. So each call told the others which danger was near. They published this in 1980.  
*What people believed before:* That animal calls are only cries of feeling and cannot tell others about the world. The hidden-speaker tests showed that each vervet call tells the others which danger is near.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only male birds sing.** (A common idea in many old bird books, written mostly in Europe and North America, where in many kinds of birds mostly males sing.; *rejected*)  
  For: In many birds near those scientists' homes, the males sang far more than the females.  
  Against: In 2014, a team led by Karan Odom checked over a thousand kinds of songbirds around the world. In most of the kinds they could check, females sing too. In many tropical birds, a female and male even sing a duet together.
- **A wagging tail always means a dog is happy and friendly.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Happy, friendly dogs often wag their tails loosely, with the whole back end swinging.  
  Against: Dogs also wag when they are excited, unsure or ready to snap. A stiff tail held high, wagging fast in small movements, can be a warning. You have to look at the whole dog: ears, mouth, body and tail.
- **A purring cat is always a happy cat.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Cats often purr when they are calm, warm and close to someone they trust.  
  Against: Cats also purr when they are hurt, hungry, frightened or giving birth. Vets often hear cats purr on the table.
- **Only humans have language.** (Many scientists who study language, such as Noam Chomsky; *debated*)  
  For: Humans put words together to say endless new things, even things that are far away or did not happen. No other animal has been shown to do this the way we do.  
  Against: Vervet monkeys have different alarm calls for leopards, eagles and snakes, and other vervets act on each call in its own way. Some birds and monkeys put calls together in a set order. Scientists are still finding out how much other animals can say.

## Roots
We have listened to barks, purrs and birdsong, and sent messages with only 'la-la'. Now we ask what the la-la test tells us about ourselves and other animals.

**Deep truth.** In your test, the listener guessed 'go away' or 'scared' from a sound with no words. Dogs, cats and people all make low, rough sounds to warn others off. High, soft sounds often mean 'I am friendly' or 'help me'. So the tune of a sound carries a message that many kinds of animals understand.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a message lives in its words, so animals without words have nothing to say. But in your test the word never changed, and the message still got through.  
*Evidence:* In the la-la test, the listener got far more right than guessing alone would give. In 1977 Eugene Morton found that many birds and mammals use low, rough sounds to threaten and high, clear sounds when friendly or afraid. In 2017 Piera Filippi and her team found that people can hear whether frogs, alligators, pandas and elephants are calm or excited.

- *who am i:* You are an animal who talks with words, and also with the tune of your voice. A dog notices your tune as well as your words.
- *what is life:* Living beings send messages all the time: to warn, to call, to ask for food, and to say 'I am here'.
- *what is this world:* The air around a flat is full of messages: barks, caws, chirps and purrs, each one meant for someone.

Every message needs two beings: one who sends and one who receives. A crow's alarm call works only when another crow hears and flies up. A purr is felt by the kitten pressed against her mother. So a message lives between two beings, not inside one. Each kind of animal speaks in their own way, and yet a dog's growl and an angry human voice share the same low, rough tune.

- **Tirukkural 706:** Just as a crystal shows the colour of whatever is beside it, the face shows what the heart is feeling strongly. (Thiruvalluvar wrote about reading feelings without words. In the la-la test the face was hidden, yet the voice still showed what the sender felt, the way a dog's growl or a cat's hiss does.)
- **The Quran, Surah An-Naml 27:16:** Solomon says, 'O people, we have been taught the language of the birds.' (This old teaching treats birds as beings with a language worth learning. Scientists who record calls and play them back are trying to learn that language, one call at a time.)
- Science (settled): Vervet monkeys have different alarm calls for leopards, eagles and snakes, and act on each call even when no danger is there.
- Science (open): Whether any animal other than humans has words, or a language like ours, is still argued about.
- Other view, Many scientists who study language, such as Noam Chomsky: They say animal sounds are signals, not language. A call can say 'danger', but animals do not put sounds together to say endless new things, as people do with words. Some scientists also think many animal calls simply burst out with a feeling, the way you shout 'ow!', and are not chosen.
- Practice, Listen for the tune (3 min): Sit by the window and close your eyes. Listen for three sounds outside. For each one, ask yourself: is this sound calm or excited? Now put your hand on your chest. Hum low and slow, like a purr, for three long breaths. How do you feel now?
- Ask: When you are cross, does your voice go higher or lower? Why do you think that happens?
- Ask: A purr can mean 'I am calm' and 'I am hurt'. How do you know what someone means, when the sound is the same?
- Ask: Why does a message need two beings, one to send it and one to notice it?

## Story: The Bark at the Gate
*Kavya, Rohan, Shanthi Atthe, Fathima Aunty, Rani and Bekku are made up. These facts are real: dogs often bark low and fast at strangers and higher, with gaps, in play, dogs use a play bow to start a game, cats purr when calm and also when hurt, and male and female koels have different calls.*

Kavya lived on the second floor of a flat in Mysuru. She was seven. Her little brother Rohan was five. Their aunt, Shanthi Atthe, was a vet. She looked after sick animals at a shelter near the lake.

A street dog called Rani slept by the gate of their building. Rani was brown, with one white ear. Every morning, the watchman's wife gave her a bowl of rice and curd.

One Saturday morning, Rani began to bark. "Woof! Woof! Woof!" The barks were low, rough and fast, with no gaps. Kavya ran to the balcony. A man in a red shirt stood at the gate with a big parcel.

"Rani is angry," said Rohan. "She hates that man."

Shanthi Atthe came out with her cup of coffee. "Listen to the bark, not just the noise," she said. "Low, rough and fast. Rani has never seen this man. She is telling him, 'This is my place. Stay back.'"

The watchman came out. He spoke to the man and took the parcel. Rani stopped barking at once. She lay down again, with her nose on her paws.

"So the bark was for the watchman too?" asked Kavya.

"Maybe," said Atthe. "One bark can have many listeners."

That evening, Rohan went down to the gate with his ball. Rani jumped up. She put her front legs flat on the ground and kept her back end high. Her tail waved from side to side. Then she barked again. But these barks were higher, with gaps between them. "Woof! ... Woof! ..."

Rohan hid the ball behind his back. "Is she angry again?" he asked.

Kavya looked at Rani. She thought about the morning. "No," she said. "Listen. Her barks are higher and slower. And look at her. Her front legs are down and her tail is up. She wants to play."

Rohan threw the ball. Rani raced after it and brought it back, tail waving.

"That bow is called a play bow," said Atthe from the balcony. "Dogs use it to say, 'What comes next is a game.'"

On Sunday, Fathima Aunty from next door came to visit with her cat, Bekku. Bekku was grey, with a white chin. She climbed onto Kavya's lap and curled up. Soon a soft rumble came from her. Kavya could feel the buzz in her own legs.

"She is purring!" whispered Kavya. "She is happy."

"Often, yes," said Atthe. "When a cat purrs, her voice box shakes more than twenty times every second. That is why you can feel it."

A few weeks later, Bekku jumped off a wall and hurt her paw. Fathima Aunty carried her to the shelter. Bekku lay on Atthe's table, and she purred the whole time.

"Why is she purring?" asked Kavya. "She is hurt."

"Cats purr when they are hurt or frightened too," said Atthe. "Scientists still argue about why. One idea is that the purr helps to calm her. So a purr does not always mean 'I am happy'. You have to look at what is happening too."

Outside, a bird called from the neem tree. "Ku-oo! Ku-oo! Ku-oo!" Each call went higher than the one before.

"That is a male koel," said Atthe. "He calls again and again in the hot months. He is saying, 'I am here.'"

A little later came a different sound, sharp and bubbling. "Kik-kik-kik-kik!"

"And that is a female koel," said Atthe. "She has her own call."

That night, Kavya had an idea. She made four cards. The first said 'Go away!'. The second said 'I am happy'. The third said 'Help, I'm scared!'. The fourth said 'Come here!'. She took the cards behind the bedroom door.

"Rohan, I will only say 'la-la'," she called. "No other words. You guess which card I am holding."

She picked 'Go away!'. "La-la!" she said, low and rough, like Rani at the gate.

"Go away!" shouted Rohan at once.

She picked 'Help, I'm scared!'. "La-la?" she squeaked, high and shaky.

Rohan thought for a moment. "Scared?"

They played twelve times. Rohan got nine right. He mixed up 'I am happy' and 'Come here' twice, and missed 'scared' once.

Kavya came out from behind the door. "You knew what I meant, and I said no real words," she said. "My voice was low and rough when I said go away. Just like Rani's bark at the stranger."

Atthe smiled. "Many animals do the same," she said. "Dogs growl low. Cats hiss. People make their voices low and rough when they are angry. High, soft sounds often mean 'I am friendly' or 'help me'."

Rohan looked out of the window at the gate. Rani was asleep under the light. "Can Rani understand us, then?" he asked.

"She hears our tune," said Kavya. "Tomorrow, let's find out how much."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavya learns that one kind of sound, a bark or a purr, can carry different messages, and that you read a message from its tune and from what is happening. In her la-la game she finds that a message gets through even with no real words.

- Ask: How did Kavya know that Rani's evening bark meant 'let's play'?
- Ask: Why was Bekku purring on the vet's table?
- Ask: In the la-la game, how did Rohan guess 'Go away!' with no words?
- Ask: What do you think Kavya and Rohan will try with Rani tomorrow?

## Experiments
### e1 · The La-La Test: can a message get through with no words? (20 min)
- **question:** Can a listener tell which message you are sending, from the tune of 'la-la' alone, with no words and no face to see?
- **predict:** Guess first. Out of 12 tries, how many will your listener get right? Which message will be easiest to guess, and which will be hardest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The word is always 'la-la', so only the tune changes. The sender hides behind a door, so the face and hands cannot help. The slips are mixed in a bowl, so the listener cannot guess the order. Each message is sent 3 times, because one try can be luck.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then draw a tally for each message: how many times out of 3 the listener got it right. Circle the easiest message.
- **results table:** Try | Message on the slip (secret) | Listener's guess | Right or wrong
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut 12 small slips of paper. 2. On 3 slips, draw an angry face and write 'Go away!'. 3. On 3 slips, draw a smiling face and write 'I am happy'. 4. On 3 slips, draw a scared face and write 'Help, I'm scared!'. 5. On 3 slips, draw a waving hand and write 'Come here!'.  
  *Parent says:* Animals send messages without words. Let's see if we can do it too. We need four messages, and each one three times.  
  *Child mode:* Make 12 slips. Draw the four messages. Make each one three times.
  - Note: Your child can draw and you can write the words. The drawings help a child who is not reading yet.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a guess and a reason before you start.  
  *Parent says:* You will only say 'la-la'. I will not see you. Out of 12 tries, how many will I guess right? Which message will be easiest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. How many will the listener get right? Which one is easiest? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that message will be easiest? (good: 'Go away' is easy because I can make my voice cross.; 'Scared' is easy because my voice goes high and wobbly.)
  - Note: Write the guess at the top of the page. Any guess is fine. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold all 12 slips and mix them in the bowl. 2. The sender takes the bowl behind the door. 3. The listener sits on the other side, back to the door.  
  *Parent says:* You are the sender first. Take the bowl behind the door. I will sit here and not look.  
  *Child mode:* Take the bowl behind the door. Do not let the listener see you.
  - Note: Hiding matters. If the listener can see a face, the face gives the answer away. Then we would not know what the sound alone can do.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The sender picks one slip and reads it silently. 2. The sender says 'la-la' once or twice, in the way the slip says. 3. The listener says their guess. 4. Write the slip, the guess, and right or wrong. 5. Put the slip aside. 6. Do all 12 slips.  
  *Parent says:* Pick a slip. Don't tell me what it says. Just say 'la-la' the way the message feels. I'll guess.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a slip. Say only 'la-la'. Make it sound like the message.
  - Note: Do not give hints, and do not say whether a guess was right until all 12 are done. A hint helps the next guess and makes the test unfair. If your child says a real word by mistake, put that slip back in the bowl.
- **s5 (0 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Optional, about 8 more minutes: swap. Now the parent hides and sends, and your child listens. Fold the slips and mix them again first. Do all 12 again, and write the results in a second table.  
  *Parent says:* If we have time, let's swap. I will hide, and you listen. Let's see if you can read my la-la.  
  *Child mode:* Now you listen. Guess each message from the sound.
  - Note: Swapping shows whether it was one good sender, or whether the tune works for anyone.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count how many guesses were right in each table. 2. Make a tally for each message. 3. Ask how the 'Go away!' la-la sounded, and how the 'Help, I'm scared!' la-la sounded.  
  *Parent says:* How many did we get right? Which message got through best? When you said 'go away', was your voice high or low? Rough or smooth? And when you were scared?  
  *Child mode:* Count the right guesses. Which message was easiest? Was 'go away' low or high?
  - Ask: What did you hear that told you the message? (good: The 'go away' was low and loud and rough.; The scared one was high and shaky.; The happy one went up and down, like singing.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses what your child saw: 'I got 9 right. The go away one was low and rough, and I got it every time.' Now read the card 'The tune carries the message'. Messages that are often mixed up, like 'happy' and 'come here', are normal. Both are friendly sounds.
  - Check: In the la-la test, the word was the same every time. What carried the message? → **The tune of the voice: high or low, rough or smooth, loud or soft**
### e2 · Do They Know Their Name? A test with a pet cat or dog (if you have one) (20 min)
- **question:** Does a pet cat or dog move more when they hear their own name than when they hear other words like it?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the pet move their ears or head more for their name, or the same for every word? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every word is said in the same voice, at the same loudness, from the same place. The words have the same number of beats as the name. You wait the same time between words. The pet cannot see your face, so only the sound can help.
- **safety:** Do this only with a pet who knows you, and only when they are calm. Never wake a sleeping animal or disturb one who is eating. Do not try it with a street dog you do not know.
- **record:** Fill in the table with a tick or a cross for each word. Draw the pet the moment you said their name.
- **results table:** Word said | Ears moved? | Head turned? | Came to you?
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose four words with the same number of beats as the pet's name. For a cat called Bekku, as in the story, you might use 'pakku', 'tikku', 'mukku' and 'sakku'. 2. Write them in the table, with the name last.  
  *Parent says:* We will say four words that sound a bit like the pet's name. Then the real name. Which words shall we use?  
  *Child mode:* Choose four words that sound like the pet's name. Write them down.
  - Note: This test comes from Atsuko Saito and her team in Tokyo. In 2019 they found that many pet cats moved their ears and heads more for their own name than for similar words.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Will the pet move more for the name, or the same for every word? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the pet notice their name more? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Yes, because we call the name when we give food.; No, because all the words sound the same.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand where the pet cannot see your face. 2. Say the first word in a normal voice. 3. Watch for 15 seconds. Did the ears move? Did the head turn? 4. Write ticks or crosses. 5. Do the same for words 2, 3 and 4. 6. Then say the name, the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Same voice every time. Now watch closely. Did the ears move? Did the head turn?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the ears and the head. Tick what you see.
  - Note: Ears can move very quickly. Watch the ears, not just the head. If the pet walks away, stop and try another day. A pet who comes to you is giving a big answer.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Another day, or after an hour, let a different family member say the words and the name. Use the same words in the same order.  
  *Parent says:* Let's try with another voice. Does the pet notice their name from a different person too?  
  *Child mode:* Let someone else say the words. Watch again.
  - Wait: Do this an hour later, or the next day, so the pet is calm again.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Did the pet move more for their name? What did you see? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Did the pet notice their name most? What did you see?
  - Ask: Why might a pet notice their name? (good: Because good things happen after the name, like food or a cuddle.; Because people say the name a lot.)
  - Note: Some pets show little difference, and that is a real result too. In Saito's test, not every cat answered. Talk about what else the pet might be noticing: your footsteps, the time of day, the smell of food.
  - Check: Why did we say every word in the same voice? → **So that only the word changed, and not the tune**

## Activities
### a1 · Hum a Purr: feel a sound in your throat (10 min)
- **record:** Draw your throat and the buzz you felt for a low hum and for a high hum. Write which one buzzed more.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child puts two fingers gently on the front of their throat. 2. They hum 'mmmm' with lips closed, low like a big drum. 3. Then they hum high, like a whistle.  
  *Parent says:* Put your fingers on your throat. Hum low, mmmm. What do you feel? Now hum high. Is the buzz the same?  
  *Child mode:* Fingers on your throat. Hum low. Now hum high. What do you feel?
  - Ask: Which hum made a bigger buzz under your fingers? (good: The low hum buzzed more.; Both buzzed, but the low one felt stronger.)
  - Note: The low hum usually feels stronger. Your child is feeling their vocal folds vibrate inside the voice box. A cat's purr comes from her voice box too.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to hum while breathing out, then try to keep humming while breathing in.  
  *Parent says:* A cat purrs while she breathes in and while she breathes out. Can you hum while you breathe in? Try it.  
  *Child mode:* Hum while you breathe out. Now try to hum while you breathe in. Is it hard?
  - Note: Most people find humming while breathing in very hard, and it sounds strange. A purring cat keeps going through both breaths.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If a cat lives with you, wait until she purrs on her own, then rest a hand gently on her side. If there is no cat, hum low again and rest your hand on your own chest, and skip the question.  
  *Parent says:* Can you feel the buzz in the cat's body? Is it faster or slower than your hum? When does she purr?  
  *Child mode:* Rest your hand gently on the cat. Can you feel the purr?
  - Ask: What do you think a purring cat is saying right now? (good: She is calm and likes being with us.; She wants us to stay close.)
  - Note: Never hold or squeeze a cat to make her purr. A purr is more than 20 vibrations every second, too fast to count.
### a2 · Messages Without a Voice: be a cricket and a woodpecker (15 min)
- **record:** Draw the three messages you made and write who makes each one in nature.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the comb still. 2. Run the edge of the card quickly along the comb's teeth. 3. Try fast and slow.  
  *Parent says:* A male cricket rubs one wing against the other. One wing has a row of tiny teeth, like this comb. Listen.  
  *Child mode:* Run the card along the comb. Fast, then slow. You sound like a cricket!
  - Note: A real cricket has a row of tiny ridges on one wing, called a file, and a scraper on the other. Female crickets hear the song with ears on their front legs.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child drums the pencil on the box: first a slow knock-knock, then a very fast drum roll.  
  *Parent says:* Woodpeckers drum on wood with their beaks to say 'this is my place'. Drum on the box. Slow, then fast.  
  *Child mode:* Drum on the box with the pencil. Slow, then very fast.
  - Note: Woodpeckers drum on dead branches and even on metal poles, because these make a loud sound that carries far.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child presses one ear flat on the table and covers the other ear. 2. You knock gently at the far end: once, then three times, then a fast roll. 3. Swap.  
  *Parent says:* Ear on the table. Cover your other ear. How many knocks did you feel? Was it loud through the table?  
  *Child mode:* Ear on the table. Count the knocks. Then swap.
  - Ask: Was the knock louder through the table or through the air? (good: Through the table. It was much clearer.; Through the table, I could feel it.)
  - Note: Sound travels well through solid things. Some termites knock their heads on the walls of their tunnels to warn the others. The warning travels through the wood.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask which message would work best in the dark, and which would work far away.  
  *Parent says:* Which of our messages would work in the dark? Which one could a friend hear far away?  
  *Child mode:* Which message works in the dark? Which one goes far?
  - Check: A cricket sings at night in a potted plant. Why is a sound a good message at night? → **Sound works in the dark, when nobody can see a movement**
### a3 · Say Hello Like a Cat: the slow blink (if a cat is near) (10 min)
- **safety:** Stay about two big steps away, and let the cat choose whether to come to you. Do not try this with a cat who is hiding, hissing or has her ears flat.
- **record:** Write how many times the cat blinked slowly with a still face, and how many times after your slow blinks.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit on the floor about two big steps from the cat. 2. For one minute, look at the cat with a still, plain face. 3. Count any slow blinks the cat makes.  
  *Parent says:* First, just look at the cat with a still face. Does she blink slowly? Let's count.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Look at the cat. Count her slow blinks.
  - Note: Do not stare hard. A hard stare can feel like a threat to a cat. A soft look is enough.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For the next minute, slow-blink at the cat: narrow your eyes slowly, close them for about a second, then open them. Do it three or four times. Count the cat's slow blinks.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's say hello the cat way. Close your eyes slowly, then open them. Does she blink back?  
  *Child mode:* Blink slowly at the cat. Does she blink back? Count.
  - Note: In 2020, Tasmin Humphrey and Karen McComb tested this with cats in their homes. Cats slow-blinked more after people slow-blinked at them. They also came closer to a stranger who slow-blinked.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what happened and what it might mean.  
  *Parent says:* Did the cat blink more after your slow blinks? Did she come closer? What do you think she was saying?  
  *Child mode:* Did she blink back? What was she saying, do you think?
  - Ask: What might a cat's slow blink mean? (good: I feel safe with you.; Hello, I am friendly.)

### p1 · Our Sound Dictionary: one animal, three days (20 min, 3 days)
- **record:** A sound dictionary: one row for each sound, with how the sound goes, what was happening, a tally of how many times you heard it, and your guess of the message.
- **schedule:** day 1: Choose an animal. Listen for 5 minutes. Write each sound and what was happening. (7 min); day 2: Listen to the same animal at about the same time. Add new sounds to the dictionary. (6 min); day 3: Listen again. Then guess what each sound means and write your guesses. (7 min)

  *Parent does:* 1. Choose one animal you can hear from home. 2. Draw three columns: 'How the sound goes', 'What was happening', 'Guess of the message'. 3. Listen for 5 minutes. Write each sound the way it sounds, and what was happening.  
  *Parent says:* Let's be animal-talk scientists. Which animal shall we listen to? Each time they make a sound, what is going on?  
  *Child mode:* Choose an animal. Listen. Write each sound and what was happening.

  *Parent does:* On day 2, listen to the same animal at about the same time, for 5 minutes. Add any new sounds. Make a tally mark when a sound comes again.  
  *Parent says:* Same animal, same time. Any new sounds today? Did an old sound come again? What was happening then?  
  *Child mode:* Listen again. Is there a new sound? Make a tally for old ones.
  - Note: If the same sound comes with the same thing happening, that is good evidence of its message. For example, loud caws every time a cat walks past.
  - Wait: Do this on day 2.

  - Note: A guess backed by 'every time X happened, they made this sound' is real science. Scientists then test a guess by playing the sound back and watching.
  - Wait: Do this on day 3.

## Challenges
### c1 · Be a Tree Cricket: make a phone louder with no electricity (20 min)
- **problem:** Can you make a phone's music reach farther across your flat using only paper, cups, tumblers or cardboard, with no extra speaker and no electricity?
- **your turn:** Design your own phone speaker. Count how many steps away you can still hear the music with no speaker, and then with your design. Change one thing and test again.
- **constraints:** Keep the phone at the same low loudness every time, so the sound fades away in a few steps.; Use only things from home: paper, a steel tumbler, a plastic cup, a bowl, a cardboard tube.; Do not cut or wet the phone. A grown-up handles any scissors.; Test by walking away from the phone until you can no longer hear the music. Count your steps.
- **general:** Stand the phone upright in a steel tumbler or a bowl, with the speaker facing up.; Roll a sheet of newspaper into a cone and hold the narrow end over the phone's speaker.
- **creative:** Cut a slot in the middle of a cardboard tube so the phone fits in tightly. Push a cup onto each end of the tube, facing outwards.; Cut a hole in a big leaf-shaped card, like the tree cricket's leaf. Hold the phone's speaker at the hole, so the card stands between the front and the back of the speaker.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child about the tree cricket. Read the problem together.  
  *Parent says:* A tree cricket chews a hole in a leaf and sings through it. The leaf makes his song much louder. Can we make a phone louder like that?  
  *Child mode:* A tree cricket sings through a hole in a leaf. The leaf makes him louder. Can you do that for a phone?
  - Note: This is real. Natasha Mhatre, Rohini Balakrishnan and their team studied tree crickets in India. In 2017 they showed that the leaf makes the male's song louder, and that the crickets pick leaves of a good size and make the hole in a good place.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play music on the phone at the a low loudness. 2. Lay it on the table. 3. Your child walks away slowly, counting steps, until they cannot hear it. 4. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* First, the phone alone. Walk away slowly. Stop when you can't hear the music. How many steps?  
  *Child mode:* Walk away from the phone. Count your steps. Stop when you can't hear it.
  - Note: Turn off the fan and close the windows if you can. Set the music low, so your child stops hearing it within a few steps. Keep the loudness the same for every test.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child builds a design and tests it the same way. Try two or three designs.  
  *Parent says:* Now build your design. Will the tumbler, the cone or the tube work best? Why do you think so? Let's test.  
  *Child mode:* Build a design. Put the phone in. Walk away and count again.
  - Ask: Which design let you walk the most steps away? (good: The tumbler. I could hear it from the kitchen.; The tube with the cups, because the sound came out of both ends.)
  - Note: A tumbler, bowl or cone may make the music a little louder, because the hard sides send more of the sound the same way. The difference can be small. If you hear no difference, write that down. It is a real result, and another shape may do better.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask why the designs worked, and link back to the cricket.  
  *Parent says:* Why do you think the tumbler or cone changed the music? Why might a cricket want to be loud?  
  *Child mode:* Why was it louder? Why does a cricket want to be loud?
  - Check: Why does a male tree cricket want his song to be loud? → **So female crickets far away can hear him and come to him**

## Worksheets
### Who Is Talking? (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each sound to its message.  
   **Answer:** A cat's purr on a lap → I am calm. Stay close.; Low, fast barks at the gate → A stranger is here. Stay back.; Loud caws, and more crows flying in → Danger! A cat is here.; A male koel's 'ku-oo, ku-oo' → I am here.. *Why:* Each sound carries one message. A purr on a lap means calm and close. Low, fast barks warn a stranger. Loud caws call other crows to a danger. A koel's 'ku-oo' says 'I am here'.
2. (mcq) A boy sees a dog give a low, rough growl over a bone. What is the dog most likely saying?  
   Options: Come and play with me. / This is my bone. Stay away. / I am lost.  
   **Answer:** This is my bone. Stay away.. *Why:* A low, rough sound, often with lips pulled back, usually means 'stay away'. A play invitation sounds higher, with gaps, and comes with a play bow.
3. (truefalse) A dog's bark always means the same thing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Low, rough, fast barks at a stranger mean 'stay back'. Higher barks with gaps, with a play bow, mean 'let's play'. The tune of the bark tells them apart.
4. (sort) Sort these messages: made with a sound, or made with the body?  
   **Answer:** a cat's purr → Made with a sound; a cat's tail held straight up → Made with the body; a dog's bark → Made with a sound; a cat's slow blink → Made with the body; a magpie robin's song → Made with a sound; a dog's play bow → Made with the body. *Why:* Purrs, barks and songs are sounds. A raised tail, a slow blink and a play bow are body signals, which others see instead of hear.
5. (draw) Remember the la-la test, where you said only 'la-la' from behind a door. Draw the message slip that your listener guessed right most often.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of one slip, for example the angry face with 'Go away!'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the message that got through best in your own test, often 'Go away!' or 'Help, I'm scared!'.
6. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: in the la-la test, how did your listener know the message when you said no real words?  
   **Answer:** From the tune of my voice. 'Go away' was low and rough. 'Scared' was high and shaky.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the word never changed, so the tune of the voice carried the message: high or low, rough or smooth.
7. (odd) Three of these send a message with a sound. Which one sends a message with the body?  
   Options: A dog barking / A cat walking with her tail straight up / A koel calling / A cat purring  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* A cat with her tail straight up is giving a body signal that means 'hello'. Barking, calling and purring are all sounds.
8. (fill) When a cat's voice box buzzes very fast while she breathes in and out, the sound is called a ________.  
   **Answer:** purr. *Why:* A purr is made by the voice box, more than 20 times every second, through both breaths.
9. (perspective) Meera, aged 7, lives in Chennai. A street dog barks at her every morning at the gate of her building.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and gives a reason, then says what to check: is the bark low and fast, or higher with gaps? Is his tail stiff or loose? Does he stop when she walks past, or follow her to where food is?. *Why:* A bark can mean 'stay back', 'feed me' or 'let's play'. The tune of the bark and the dog's body help to tell them apart.
10. (write) Rohan, a boy aged 5, says, 'Only people can talk. Animals just make noise.' Is he right? Say one thing from this unit that helps you answer.  
   **Answer:** He is not quite right. A dog barks differently at a stranger and in play, and the watchman comes. Vervet monkeys have a different call for each danger.. *Why:* A strong answer uses one example where an animal's sound carries a message that others act on. It may also say that humans are animals too.
### Songs, Calls and Purrs (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a bird's song, not a call?  
   Options: A sharp 'chip!' as a cat comes near / A long tune sung again and again from the same rooftop every morning / A quick 'tik' as a bird flies off  
   **Answer:** A long tune sung again and again from the same rooftop every morning. *Why:* A song is long and repeated, often from the same place, and says 'this is my place' or 'choose me'. Short single sounds, like 'chip' or 'tik', are calls.
2. (truefalse) In many kinds of songbirds, the females sing as well as the males.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 2014 Karan Odom and her team found that in most of the kinds of songbirds they could check, females sing too.
3. (order) Put these sounds in order, from the lowest pitch to the highest pitch.  
   **Answer:** ["a lion's roar", "a big dog's growl", "a cat's meow", "a sparrow's chirp"]. *Why:* Bigger bodies usually make lower sounds. A lion is the biggest here and a sparrow is the smallest.
4. (label) Label the cat's body messages: tail straight up, fur puffed up, eyes half shut, ears flat back.  
   **Answer:** ['tail straight up: hello, I am friendly', 'fur puffed up: I am scared or angry, stay away', 'eyes half shut: I feel calm and safe', 'ears flat back: I am frightened or cross']. *Why:* A cat sends messages with her whole body. Read the tail, the fur, the eyes and the ears together.
5. (write) A kitten is born with her eyes and ears shut. How might her mother's purr help her in the first days? Say it, and a grown-up can write it.  
   **Answer:** The kitten can feel the buzz of the purr through her body, so she can find her mother and stay close, even before she can see or hear.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a purr can be felt as well as heard, so it reaches a kitten who cannot yet see or hear. Scientists think this may help, but it is not fully shown.
6. (mcq) Dorothy Cheney and Robert Seyfarth hid a speaker in a bush in Kenya. They played a vervet monkey's eagle alarm call. No eagle was there. What did the monkeys do?  
   Options: They ran up into the trees. / They looked up at the sky and ran into the bushes. / They stood up and looked at the grass.  
   **Answer:** They looked up at the sky and ran into the bushes.. *Why:* At the eagle call, vervets looked up and hid in bushes. At the leopard call they ran up trees, and at the snake call they stood up and looked down. The sound alone told them which danger.
7. (draw) Draw two birds. One sings a song on a rooftop at dawn. The other gives a short alarm call at a cat. Show how the two sounds are different.  
   **Answer:** A bird with a long, wavy line of notes, and a bird with one short, sharp mark pointed at a cat.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the song as long and repeating, and the call as short and sharp, aimed at the danger.
8. (match) Draw a line from each animal to how they make their message.  
   **Answer:** A male cricket → rubs one wing against the other; A woodpecker → drums on wood with the beak; A cat → purrs with the voice box; A human → speaks with the voice box, tongue and lips. *Why:* A cricket's chirp comes from the wings, a woodpecker's drumming from the beak on wood, and a purr and human speech from the voice box.
9. (perspective) Lakshmi and Farhan, two friends in Hyderabad, hear a male koel calling 'ku-oo, ku-oo' from a tree every morning.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then names a clue to watch: does the koel call more in the hot months, does another koel call back, or does a female come near?. *Why:* We cannot know how a koel feels, but we can watch what happens after the call. A call that others answer is a message.
10. (fill) A short sound that warns others that danger is near is called an ________ call.  
   **Answer:** alarm. *Why:* An alarm call warns others of danger, like a crow's loud caws at a cat or a squirrel's fast 'chip-chip-chip'.
### Can We Understand Them? (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (write) Many animals use low, rough sounds to say 'stay away'. Why might a low sound help an animal look stronger?  
   **Answer:** Big animals and big drums make low sounds. So a low growl makes an animal sound bigger than they are, and others keep away.. *Why:* A strong answer links low pitch with big size: bigger bodies make deeper sounds, so a low sound is a way of sounding big.
2. (mcq) Sita is doing the la-la test. She makes her 'Go away!' la-la high and squeaky. What will the listener most likely guess?  
   Options: Help, I'm scared! / Go away! / The listener will always get it right anyway  
   **Answer:** Help, I'm scared!. *Why:* High, shaky sounds usually mean fear or 'help'. A 'go away' message is usually low and rough. So the listener will probably guess 'scared'.
3. (perspective) Two people argue about whether animal calls are like words.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with evidence, such as the vervets running up trees only at the leopard call. Then it gives the other side its due: people can say endless new things, and no monkey has been shown to do that.. *Why:* Scientists still argue about this. Vervet calls name the kind of danger, which is a bit like a word. But human language puts words together in endless new ways.
4. (write) A pet cat meows at people a lot. Scientists have noticed that grown-up cats rarely meow at each other. Who do you think the meow is for? Why?  
   **Answer:** The meow is mostly for people. Cats may have learned that meowing gets people to open doors or give food.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the clue that cats meow far more at people than at cats, and says what the cat might be asking for.
5. (truefalse) A wagging tail always means a dog is friendly.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dogs also wag when excited, unsure or ready to snap. A stiff tail held high and wagging fast in small movements can be a warning. Look at the whole dog.
6. (draw) Invent an animal who lives in a dark cave. Draw how they tell the others 'food is here!'. Will it be a sound, a touch or both? Write why.  
   **Answer:** For example, a cave animal who clicks loudly and taps the ground, because in the dark nobody can see a body signal.. *Why:* A strong design uses a signal that works in the dark, such as a sound or a touch, and says why a body signal like a raised tail would not work there.
7. (sort) Which messages still work in the dark?  
   **Answer:** a koel's call → Works in the dark; a cat's tail held straight up → Needs light to be seen; a cricket's chirp → Works in the dark; a cat's slow blink → Needs light to be seen; a mother cat's purr against her kittens → Works in the dark; a peacock spreading his tail feathers → Needs light to be seen. *Why:* Sounds and touch work in the dark. Body signals, like a raised tail or a spread of feathers, must be seen, so they need light.
8. (mcq) A small tree cricket chews a hole in a leaf and sings with his head pushed through the hole. Why?  
   Options: To hide from birds / The leaf makes his song louder / To eat the leaf while he sings  
   **Answer:** The leaf makes his song louder. *Why:* The leaf stops the sound from the front and the back of his wings from cancelling each other out. So the song is much louder and can be heard from farther away.
9. (mcq) In the la-la test, why were the slips mixed in a bowl before the sender picked one?  
   Options: So the listener could not guess the order of the messages / So the slips would not get lost / So the sender could read them more easily  
   **Answer:** So the listener could not guess the order of the messages. *Why:* If the slips were in a fixed order, the listener could guess the next message without listening to the tune. Mixing them makes the test fair.
10. (write) A mother hen clucks, and her chicks run to her. Who is the sender? What is the signal? Who is the receiver? Say it, and a grown-up can write it.  
   **Answer:** The hen is the sender. The cluck is the signal. The chicks are the receivers, because they notice it and run to her.. *Why:* A strong answer names all three and says that the receiver does something because of the signal.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does it mean when an animal communicates?  
   Options: They send a message, and another animal notices and acts on the message. / They make a noise by accident. / They eat together.  
   **Answer:** They send a message, and another animal notices and acts on the message.. *Why:* Communicating needs a sender and a receiver. The signal changes what the receiver does, like crows flying up at an alarm call.
2. (mcq) Which of these is a message sent with the body, not with a sound?  
   Options: A cat purring / A dog doing a play bow, front legs down and back end up / A crow cawing  
   **Answer:** A dog doing a play bow, front legs down and back end up. *Why:* A play bow is a body signal that others see. Purring and cawing are sounds.
3. (mcq) How does a cat make a purr?  
   Options: By clicking her teeth together / Her voice box vibrates very fast as she breathes in and out / Her tummy rumbles after food  
   **Answer:** Her voice box vibrates very fast as she breathes in and out. *Why:* A purr comes from the voice box, more than 20 times every second, through both breaths. That is why you can feel it.
4. (mcq) A cat is purring while her paw is hurt. What does this tell you?  
   Options: A purr can mean more than 'I am happy'. / The cat is happy to be hurt. / Only kittens purr.  
   **Answer:** A purr can mean more than 'I am happy'.. *Why:* Cats purr when hurt or frightened too. You read a purr with the rest of the cat and with what is happening.
5. (mcq) Which sound is a bird's alarm call?  
   Options: A long tune at sunrise from a rooftop / A sharp, loud cry when a cat comes near the nest / A loud 'ku-oo' again and again from a tree  
   **Answer:** A sharp, loud cry when a cat comes near the nest. *Why:* An alarm call is short and sharp and warns of danger. A long tune is a song, and 'ku-oo' is a koel's call to say 'I am here'.
6. (mcq) In many kinds of songbirds, who sings?  
   Options: Only the males / Only the females / Both females and males  
   **Answer:** Both females and males. *Why:* In most kinds of songbirds that scientists checked, females sing as well as males.
7. (mcq) A cat puffs up her fur and hisses with a rough sound. What is she most likely saying?  
   Options: Stay away from me! / Please feed me. / I am sleepy.  
   **Answer:** Stay away from me!. *Why:* Rough sounds and a puffed-up body usually mean 'stay away'. Puffed fur also makes her look bigger.
8. (mcq) Which kind of sound more often means 'I am friendly' or 'help me'?  
   Options: A high, soft sound / A low, rough sound / Any sound, it makes no difference  
   **Answer:** A high, soft sound. *Why:* In many birds and mammals, high, soft sounds go with being friendly or scared, and low, rough sounds go with 'stay away'.
9. (mcq) A male cricket chirps all night in a potted plant. How does he make the sound?  
   Options: With his voice box / By rubbing one wing against the other / By stamping his feet  
   **Answer:** By rubbing one wing against the other. *Why:* One wing has a row of tiny ridges and the other has a scraper. Rubbing them together makes the chirp.
10. (mcq) In the la-la test, why did the sender hide behind a door?  
   Options: So the listener could not see the face or hands, and only the sound could help / So the sound would be louder / So the sender could read the slips more easily  
   **Answer:** So the listener could not see the face or hands, and only the sound could help. *Why:* A face can give the answer away. Hiding makes sure the test is only about the sound.
11. (mcq) Your listener got 10 out of 12 right in the la-la test. What does this show?  
   Options: The tune of a sound can carry a message, even with no words. / 'La-la' is a real word. / The listener could see you.  
   **Answer:** The tune of a sound can carry a message, even with no words.. *Why:* The word never changed and the sender was hidden, so the tune carried the message. Guessing alone would give only about 3 right out of 12.

## Applications
- Vets and animal shelter workers read barks, purrs, tails and ears to tell whether an animal is in pain or frightened.
- Bird scientists count birds in forests by their songs and calls, often with microphones left in the trees for days.
- People who train guide dogs and police dogs use the tune of their voice as much as their words.
- Farmers who keep hens and cows learn their animals' calls, and can hear when a hen has seen a hawk or a calf is hungry.

## Open questions
- **Why do cats purr when they are hurt?** Some scientists think the purr calms the cat. Some think the buzz might help the body heal. Nobody has shown which is true.
- **Do any animals have words, or a language like ours?** Some monkeys and birds have calls for different dangers, and some put calls in a set order. Scientists still argue about whether this is like human language.
- **How much of what a dog's bark says is meant for people?** Dogs bark far more than wolves do. Scientists think that dogs may have come to bark more while living with people, but nobody is sure how much of the barking is for us.

## Connected maths
- In the story, Rohan, a boy aged 5, listened in the la-la test. He got 3 right for 'Go away!', 2 for 'Come here!', 2 for 'I am happy' and 2 for 'Help, I'm scared!'. How many did he get right in all, out of 12? How many were wrong? **9 right and 3 wrong.**. 3 and 2 make 5. 5 and 2 make 7. 7 and 2 make 9. There were 12 tries, and 12 take away 9 is 3. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- There were 4 kinds of message and 12 tries. If Rohan had covered his ears and only guessed, about how many would he get right? He got 9. How many times more is that? **About 3 by guessing. 9 is 3 times as many as 3.**. A pure guess is right about 1 time in 4. 12 shared into 4 equal groups is 3. 3 and 3 and 3 make 9, so 9 is three times 3. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L2`)
- A cat purrs about 25 buzzes every second. How many buzzes in 4 seconds? **About 100 buzzes.**. 4 groups of 25. 25 and 25 make 50. 50 and 50 make 100. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- In her sound dictionary, Ayesha, a girl in Class 2, heard the crow caw 14 times, the pigeon coo 6 times and the street dog bark 9 times. Put the animals in order, from most sounds to fewest. How many more caws than coos? **Crow 14, dog 9, pigeon 6. There were 8 more caws than coos.**. 14 is more than 9, and 9 is more than 6. Count on from 6 to 14: 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14. That is 8 more. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- A cricket chirps in a pattern: chirp, chirp, rest, chirp, chirp, rest. What comes after the third rest? If the pattern goes round 5 times, how many chirps is that? **Chirp comes next. 5 rounds make 10 chirps.**. Each round is chirp, chirp, rest, so it has 2 chirps. 5 rounds of 2 chirps: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. (queues `math.patterns.repeating-patterns.L2`)
