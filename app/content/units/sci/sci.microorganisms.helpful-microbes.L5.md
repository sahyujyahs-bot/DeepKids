# Why food spoils, and how drying, salt, sugar and cold keep it
`sci.microorganisms.helpful-microbes.L5`

**Summary.** Honey keeps for years on a shelf, but cooked dal spoils in a day. Why? In this lesson you will test how much salt stops dal water from spoiling, find out whether jaggery water can be too sweet to spoil, and play a card game on the ways families keep food.

**Intro.** *A jar of honey can sit on a shelf for years and never spoil. A bowl of cooked dal spoils in one day. Both are wet. So why does one keep and the other spoil?* Food spoils because microbes eat the food before we do. Microbes are living beings too small to see, such as bacteria, yeasts and moulds. They are in the air, on our hands and on every food. They grow fast in cooked dal, because dal gives them food, water and warmth. In this lesson you find out what stops them. You set up five glasses of dal water with more and more salt in each, which we call the Salt Ladder. You keep thin jaggery water beside thick jaggery syrup. You dry tomato slices and weigh the water that leaves. By the end you can explain why honey keeps. You can also explain why a wet spoon must never go into the pickle.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can explain that microbes spoil food, and say what bacteria, yeasts and moulds need to grow.
1. By the end I can explain how drying, salt, sugar, sourness, oil, cold and heat each keep food.
2. By the end I can use my own test results to explain why the amount of salt or sugar matters.
3. By the end I can tell spoiled food from fermented food, and from changes that microbes do not cause.
4. By the end I can say what helpful microbes do in curd, in my gut and in the soil.
5. By the end I can plan a fair test with a ladder of amounts, and chart the results.
6. By the end I can say what scientists still argue about, such as fermented foods and gut microbes.

## Terms
- **Microbe**: A living being too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria, yeasts and moulds are microbes. *e.g.* One spot of mould on bread is many thousands of microbes living together.
- **Bacteria**: Microbes made of one very small cell each. One alone is called a bacterium. In warm, wet food some bacteria can split in two every 20 to 30 minutes. *e.g.* Bacteria turn milk into curd. Other bacteria turn cooked dal frothy and sour on a warm counter.
- **Mould**: A fungus who grows as fine threads and makes fuzzy patches on food. Moulds need air, and they can grow on food that is too dry for most bacteria. *e.g.* The green and white fuzz on old bread, on a forgotten lemon or on top of a pickle is mould.
- **Yeast**: A fungus who lives as single round cells. Yeasts eat sugar and give off carbon dioxide gas and a little alcohol. *e.g.* Yeasts make bread rise. Wild yeasts also make jaggery water and fruit juice bubble when they are left out.
- **Spore**: A tiny, tough cell that moulds and some bacteria make. A spore can wait, dry, for months or years, and grows when water and food are there. *e.g.* Mould spores float in the air of every room. They land on a papad and wait. If the papad gets damp, they grow.
- **Spoilage**: The change in food when unwanted microbes grow there. The smell, taste, colour or feel changes, and the food may become unsafe. *e.g.* Milk that smells sour and splits, bread with green spots and slimy spinach are all spoiled.
- **Preservation**: Keeping food safe to eat for longer, by stopping the growth of microbes. *e.g.* Drying fish, salting mango, making jam and keeping milk in the fridge are all ways of preserving food.
- **Free water**: Water in a food that microbes are able to take in. Water that salt or sugar is holding is not free. Scientists call the measure of free water 'water activity'. *e.g.* Honey is about one-sixth water, but sugar holds nearly all of that water. So honey has very little free water, and microbes do not grow in honey.
- **Dehydration**: Taking the water out of something. Dehydrated food is dried food. *e.g.* Red chillies spread in the sun in Guntur lose their water in a few days. The dry chillies keep for a year.
- **Brine**: Water with a lot of salt dissolved in it. Brine for pickling holds 10 grams of salt or more in every 100 millilitres. *e.g.* In Kerala, small green mangoes are kept for months in a jar of brine. This pickle is called uppu manga.
- **Osmosis**: The movement of water through the thin skin of a cell. The water moves towards the side with more salt or sugar dissolved. *e.g.* A microbe who lands in thick sugar syrup loses water through their cell skin by osmosis. They shrink and stop growing.
- **Acid**: A substance that tastes sour. Lemon juice, tamarind, vinegar and curd all hold acids. Most microbes that spoil food grow poorly in strong acid. *e.g.* Tamarind rice keeps on a train journey for a day or more, partly because tamarind makes the rice sour.
- **Fermentation**: A change that chosen microbes make in food as they eat sugar. They leave behind acid, gas or alcohol. The acid also helps to keep the food. *e.g.* Bacteria ferment milk into curd. The acid they make gives curd a sour taste and holds back other microbes.
- **Pasteurisation**: Heating a food enough to kill most microbes, followed by fast cooling. Milk is heated to 72 degrees Celsius for 15 seconds. *e.g.* A milk packet says 'pasteurised'. The milk still has a few microbes, so the packet must stay in the fridge.
- **Airtight**: Closed so well that air and water vapour cannot get in or out. *e.g.* Biscuits stay crisp in an airtight dabba. In an open plate they take in water from the air and go soft.
- **Evaporation**: Water slowly turning into a gas in the air. As it does this, it takes heat from what it leaves. *e.g.* A wet cloth round a mud pot dries in the sun. The drying takes heat from the pot, so the water inside stays cool.
- **Hurdle**: Any one thing that makes a food harder for microbes to grow in. Preserved foods often use several small hurdles together. *e.g.* Mango pickle uses four hurdles: a lot of salt, sour mango, mustard and a layer of oil that keeps air out.
- **Preservative**: A substance added to food in a small, measured amount to slow microbes. A label lists each one by name or number. *e.g.* A squash bottle may list 'preservative (211)'. That number means sodium benzoate.
- **Toxin**: A poison made by a living being. Some bacteria and moulds leave toxins in food, and some of these toxins stay even after the food is boiled. *e.g.* Rice left out all day can hold a toxin from bacteria. Heating the rice again kills the bacteria but may not remove the toxin.
- **Gut microbes**: The bacteria and other microbes who live in your intestines. There are tens of trillions of them. They eat the parts of plants that you are unable to digest. *e.g.* When you eat dal, vegetables and fruit, your gut microbes ferment the fibre. They make substances that feed the cells of your gut wall.
- **Sprouting**: A seed starting to grow, with a tiny root and shoot, after it has been soaked in water. *e.g.* Moong soaked overnight and kept in a damp cloth sprouts in a day or two.

## Explanations
### Why food spoils: microbes eat the food before we do · *defined*
**Definition:** Food spoils when bacteria, yeasts or moulds grow in the food. They eat the food, multiply, and leave behind acids, gases and other substances that change the smell, taste and look.

Microbes are everywhere. Mould spores float in the air, bacteria live on hands, spoons and vegetable skins, and wild yeasts sit on fruit. So every food already carries some. They feed by breaking the big molecules of the food, such as starch and protein, into small ones. They take the energy and let the leftovers go. Those leftovers are what we notice. Acids taste sour. Gases make froth and bubbles. Some small molecules smell very bad, such as the ones from spoiled fish or eggs. Bacteria multiply by splitting in two. In warm, wet food some kinds can split every 20 to 30 minutes. So a few bacteria can become millions in a few hours. This is why cooked dal that was fine at lunch can be spoiled by the next morning.
- e.g. Cooked dal left on the counter overnight in summer turns frothy and sour. Bacteria have multiplied in the dal.
- e.g. A lemon forgotten in a basket grows blue-green mould. The mould's threads spread through the fruit as they feed.
- e.g. Sweet palm sap, called neera, starts to ferment within a few hours in warm air. Wild yeasts eat the sugar. Kamala Sohonie, the first Indian woman to earn a PhD in science, studied neera as a food.
### What spoiling microbes need · *defined*
**Definition:** Microbes that spoil food need five things: food, free water, enough warmth, enough time, and food that is not too sour. Moulds also need air.

Take away any one of these and growth slows or stops. Most spoiling bacteria grow fastest between about 20 and 45 degrees Celsius, which is the warmth of an Indian kitchen. They grow slowly in a fridge at about 4 degrees, and stop in a freezer. They need water they can take in. So they grow in dal, milk, cooked rice and cut fruit, but not in dry rice or atta. Most stop in very sour food. Moulds are tougher in two ways: they grow on drier and more sour food than most bacteria. But moulds must have air. Some bacteria are the opposite, and grow only where there is no air at all. So no single method stops every microbe, and that is why many foods use more than one method.
- e.g. Raw rice keeps for a year. Cook the same rice, which adds water, and the rice spoils in a day.
- e.g. Bread in a closed plastic bag grows mould within a week in the monsoon. The bag holds the dampness in.
- e.g. Milk in the fridge keeps for days. The same milk on a summer counter can spoil within a day.
### Free water: why honey is too dry for microbes · *defined*
**Definition:** Microbes can use only free water, which is water that nothing else is holding. Salt and sugar hold on to water. So a very salty or very sweet food can feel wet and still have too little free water for microbes.

A microbe is a cell with a thin skin, and water passes through that skin. Water moves towards the side with more salt or sugar dissolved in it. This movement is called osmosis. In thick syrup or strong brine, the water inside the microbe moves out. The cell shrinks and stops growing. Scientists measure free water on a scale from 0 to 1, called water activity. Pure water is 1. Fresh bread is about 0.95, jam about 0.8 and honey about 0.6. Most bacteria stop growing below about 0.9, and most moulds below about 0.8. Almost nothing grows below 0.6. Honey is about one-sixth water, and bread is more than one-third water. But in honey the sugar holds the water, and in bread much of the water is free. So bread grows mould and honey keeps.
- e.g. In the Thick or Thin experiment, thin jaggery water ferments within a few days. Thick jaggery syrup usually stays unchanged.
- e.g. Jam is about two-thirds sugar. An opened jar still keeps for weeks.
- e.g. Honey mixed with water can ferment, because yeasts now have free water. People have made a drink from honey and water this way for thousands of years.
### Drying: taking the water away · *defined*
**Definition:** Drying removes most of the water from a food. With too little free water, microbes on the food stop growing. They are not killed. Many wait as spores, and grow again if the food gets damp.

Sun and moving air carry water away from thin pieces of food. A tomato is about 94 parts water in every 100, so a dried tomato slice weighs very little. Dried food must then be kept dry. Dry food takes in water from damp air. That is why papad in an open tin goes soft and mouldy in the monsoon. Families dry food in the hot, dry months for this reason. Ghee is a dried food too. Butter is about one-sixth water. When butter is heated, the water boils away as bubbles, and the milk solids are strained out. What is left is almost pure fat with almost no water, so ghee keeps for months without a fridge. Freeze-drying is a modern way of drying. The food is frozen, and the ice is drawn off as vapour, so the food keeps its shape.
- e.g. Vadam and vathal are dried on terraces in Tamil Nadu in March and April, before the rains.
- e.g. Salted fish dries on racks along the coasts of Kerala, Konkan and Odisha.
- e.g. In Kashmir, brinjal, gourd and tomato are dried on strings in autumn. They are cooked in winter, when snow closes the roads.
- e.g. Milk powder, instant coffee and the food astronauts carry are dried in factories.
### Salt and sugar: the amount matters · *defined*
**Definition:** Salt and sugar preserve food only in large amounts. A little salt or sugar does not stop microbes, and a little sugar feeds them.

The salt we add for taste is about 1 gram in 100 grams of food. That is far too little to hold back microbes. Sea water has about 3.5 grams of salt in 100 millilitres, and many bacteria live in the sea. Pickling brine has 10 to 20 grams in 100 millilitres. Sugar works the same way. A spoon of sugar in a glass of water is food for yeast. Jam and murabba are more than half sugar, and at that strength the sugar holds the water. This is why pickle is very salty and jam is very sweet. They could not keep otherwise. It is also why we eat them in small amounts. Too much salt or sugar is not good for the body.
- e.g. In the Salt Ladder experiment, the glasses with no salt and a quarter teaspoon of salt usually spoil first. The glasses with 2 and 4 teaspoons usually stay clear.
- e.g. Amla murabba from Uttar Pradesh keeps for a year in thick sugar syrup.
- e.g. Lemon pickle in salt needs about one part salt to five or six parts lemon by weight.
### Sourness and fermentation: helpful microbes guard the food · *defined*
**Definition:** Most microbes that spoil food or make people ill grow poorly in strong acid. We can add an acid, such as vinegar, lemon or tamarind. Or we can let helpful bacteria make the acid, which is fermentation.

When a spoon of curd goes into warm milk, the curd bacteria multiply fast. They eat the milk sugar and make lactic acid. The acid sets the milk and makes it sour. Now the curd is a poor place for most other microbes. So curd keeps longer than the milk it came from. The curd bacteria were there first. They used up the milk sugar and made the milk too sour for most other microbes to grow. The same thing happens in idli batter and in gajar kanji from North India. It also happens in gundruk, the fermented leafy greens of Sikkim and Nepal. Fermenting keeps food by choosing which microbes grow there. Some of these bacteria also make substances that harm other bacteria. One of them, nisin, is now used as a food preservative.
- e.g. Curd keeps for a few days in the fridge, and pasteurised milk from an opened packet for about two.
- e.g. Puliyodarai, tamarind rice, is packed for long journeys in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. The tamarind and oil help the rice keep.
- e.g. Vinegar onions and kasundi, the mustard sauce of Bengal, are kept by their sourness.
### Cold, heat and sealing · *defined*
**Definition:** Cold slows microbes down but does not kill them. Strong heat kills them. Sealing a food after heating stops new microbes from getting in.

In a fridge at about 4 degrees Celsius, most bacteria split only once in many hours, so food lasts days. In a freezer at minus 18 degrees there is no liquid water and growth stops. When the food warms up, the microbes who lived through the cold grow again. Boiling kills most microbes, but the spores of some bacteria live through boiling. Pasteurised milk is heated to 72 degrees for 15 seconds, which kills most microbes and leaves the taste. Some milk is sold in cartons that keep for months without a fridge. That milk has been heated to about 140 degrees for a few seconds and packed with no microbes. Tinned food is sealed and then heated to about 121 degrees. That heat is needed because one kind of bacterium grows without air and makes a deadly toxin. A tin or packet that has swollen has gas made by microbes inside, and must be thrown away unopened. In the early 1900s the American chemist Mary Engle Pennington studied eggs, milk and chicken on the way to shops. She worked out how cold they must be kept. She also led a study of cold railway wagons for carrying food. Cool food does not always need electricity. In many villages, families keep water in a mud pot called a matka. They wrap a wet cloth round the pot, or pack wet sand round it. Water dries off the cloth into the air, and as it dries it takes heat from the pot. This is called evaporation. It works best in hot, dry weather, when water dries fast. It will not freeze anything, but a cooler pot slows microbes a little, so vegetables and milk last longer than on an open shelf.
- e.g. An unopened carton of long-life milk keeps for months on a shelf. Once opened, it needs the fridge like any milk.
- e.g. Apples from Himachal Pradesh are kept in cold stores, so they can be sold many months after the harvest.
- e.g. Frozen peas keep for a year, and cook like fresh ones.
- e.g. A mud pot with a wet cloth round it keeps water and vegetables cooler than the room, on a hot dry afternoon in Rajasthan.
- e.g. Farmers in Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal keep their potatoes in big cold stores after the harvest, so families can buy them all through the year.
### Hurdles: several small barriers together · *defined*
**Definition:** A hurdle is anything that makes a food harder for microbes to grow in. Many preserved foods use several hurdles at once, even though each one on its own would be too weak to keep the food safe.

The German food scientist Lothar Leistner gave this idea its name. A microbe who can get past one hurdle is often stopped by the next. Mango pickle is a good example. The salt holds the water. The raw mango is sour. Mustard and chilli slow some microbes. The layer of oil on top keeps air away from moulds. If one hurdle fails, the others may still hold. But a wet spoon brings in fresh water. As the oil is used up, pieces of mango stick out above it, and mould can start there. A food label often tells you about its hurdles. When a label says 'Refrigerate after opening', it means that the sealed jar was keeping microbes out. Once you break the seal, that hurdle is gone, so the cold of the fridge has to do the job instead.
- e.g. Tomato ketchup uses vinegar, sugar, salt, heating and a sealed bottle.
- e.g. Dried fish is both salted and dried.
- e.g. Smoked meat and fish in Nagaland hang above the kitchen fire. They are dried by the heat, and the smoke leaves substances that slow microbes.
### Spoiled, fermented, or only changed? · *defined*
**Definition:** Not every change in food is spoiling. Fermenting is a change made by microbes that we chose. Some other changes are not made by microbes at all.

A cut apple turns brown within 20 minutes. Substances in the apple react with oxygen from the air. No microbe is needed, and the apple is safe. A banana skin turns black as the banana ripens, which is the fruit's own work. Old fried snacks smell like paint because oxygen has changed the oil. This is called going rancid. A biscuit that bends has taken in water from the air. Honey that turns grainy has only formed sugar crystals. A potato that grows shoots is alive and growing. To tell these apart, ask three questions. Did someone add a starter or set this food to ferment on purpose? Is there fuzz, slime, froth or a bad smell that was not there before? Could air or water alone have done this? When food that was not meant to ferment is frothy, slimy, fuzzy or foul-smelling, throw it away.
- e.g. Idli batter that has risen and smells pleasantly sour is fermented. Dal that has frothed and smells sharp is spoiled.
- e.g. Milk that splits when lemon juice is added has been curdled by acid. This is how paneer is made.
- e.g. At the market, fresh fish has clear eyes and a firm body, and fresh leaves are crisp and not slimy.
### Food that looks fine can still be unsafe · *defined*
**Definition:** Spoiled food usually warns us with smell or looks. But some microbes that make people ill do not change the food in a way we can notice.

The microbes that make food smell bad are often not the ones that make people ill. Some harmful bacteria can reach dangerous numbers while the food still looks and smells normal. Some leave toxins that boiling does not destroy. Moulds send fine threads deep into soft food, far beyond the fuzzy patch, and some moulds make toxins. So cutting the mould off bread, fruit or cooked food does not make the food safe. A few simple rules follow from the science. Eat cooked food within about two hours, or put it in the fridge. Heat food from the fridge until it is steaming. Keep raw meat, fish and eggs away from food that will not be cooked. If spoiled food is eaten by mistake, it can cause vomiting, loose motions and stomach pain. The body loses water, so the person should sip ORS or water. See a doctor if it lasts more than a day, or at once for a small child.
- e.g. Cooked rice kept warm all day can cause vomiting even after it is fried again.
- e.g. Groundnuts and maize stored damp can grow a mould whose toxin harms the liver. This is one reason farmers dry their grain well.
- e.g. Unsafe food makes about 600 million people ill every year, by the World Health Organization's count.
### Helpful microbes in your gut · *defined*
**Definition:** Tens of trillions of bacteria live in your large intestine. They digest parts of food that you are unable to digest, and they make some vitamins. They also take up space that harmful microbes would otherwise use.

Your own enzymes are unable to break down most plant fibre. Your gut microbes ferment the fibre. As they do, they make small molecules that feed the cells of your gut wall. They also make vitamin K and some B vitamins. A person carries about as many bacteria as they have cells of their own. A gut with many kinds of microbes is usually a sign of good health. One large study looked at more than 10,000 people. Those who ate 30 or more kinds of plants in a week had more kinds of gut microbes. Those who ate fewer than 10 kinds had fewer. After an upset stomach or a course of antibiotics, the gut microbes take time to return to their usual numbers. You can add more kinds of plants to your week in small ways. Soak whole moong or chana overnight, drain them, and keep them in a damp cloth. In a day or two the seeds sprout, and you have one more plant food for your salad or chaat. Sprouts grow in the same warm, wet place that microbes like, so rinse them well, and cook them lightly if they have sat for long. Curd or buttermilk with lunch is another easy change. Scientists are still testing what it does for the gut, as the next card shows.
- e.g. A meal of rice, sambar and a vegetable poriyal can hold eight or more kinds of plants.
- e.g. Babies are born with almost no gut microbes. They collect them from their mother, from milk and from the world around them.
- e.g. Outside the body, soil bacteria and fungi do the same work on fallen leaves and peels. They turn them into food for plants.
- e.g. A bowl of moong sprouts, grown in a damp cloth on the kitchen shelf, counts as one more kind of plant in the week.
### Do fermented foods and probiotics change your gut microbes? · *debated*
**Definition:** Curd, buttermilk, kanji and other fermented foods hold living bacteria. Scientists agree these foods are safe and nourishing. They disagree about whether the bacteria in them settle in the gut and change a person's health.

More than a hundred years ago, the scientist Elie Metchnikoff suggested that bacteria in sour milk help people live longer. Today shops sell probiotic drinks and capsules with the same promise. The tests so far give mixed answers, and what holds for one person may not hold for another.
- e.g. Many families give curd rice or buttermilk after an upset stomach.
- e.g. Probiotic drinks are sold with claims about digestion and immunity.
- **Scientists at Stanford University, in a study published in 2021:** They asked 36 healthy adults to eat either more fermented foods or more fibre for ten weeks. The people who ate more fermented foods ended with more kinds of gut microbes. They also had lower signs of inflammation in their blood.
- **Scientists at the Weizmann Institute in Israel, in a study published in 2018:** They gave people probiotic bacteria and then looked inside the gut. In some people the new bacteria settled. In others they passed straight through. So a probiotic may do something for one person and nothing for another.
- **Many doctors:** Fermented foods are good foods, and eating them is sensible. But most studies are small and short. No one should expect curd or a probiotic drink to cure an illness. A healthy child does not need costly capsules.
### Preserving food across India · *defined*
**Definition:** Every region of India has its own preserved foods. Each one fits the local weather, the local crops and the months when fresh food is hard to get.

People preserve food when there is plenty, to eat when there is little. Mangoes come only in summer, so they are pickled, dried or cooked in sugar. Fish are plentiful after the monsoon, so they are salted and dried. In the Himalayas, snow closes roads for months, so vegetables and cheese are dried in autumn. The method fits the weather. Hot, dry places dry their food in the sun. Wet places, such as the Northeast, use smoke and fermentation, because food does not dry well in damp air. These methods were worked out over hundreds of years, mostly by women, long before anyone had seen a microbe. People tested, watched what kept and what spoiled, and passed on the rules.
- e.g. South: avakaya in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, vathal in Tamil Nadu, sandige in Karnataka, dried fish and uppu manga in Kerala.
- e.g. West: chhundo in Gujarat, kokum in Konkan and Goa, ker sangri in Rajasthan.
- e.g. North: amla murabba in Uttar Pradesh, wadiyan in Punjab, dried vegetables in Kashmir, hard dried cheese called chhurpi in Ladakh.
- e.g. East and Northeast: badi in Odisha, kasundi in West Bengal, gundruk in Sikkim, fermented bamboo shoot in Manipur, smoked meat in Nagaland.
### The same rules keep a mummy, a mammoth and an astronaut's dinner · *defined*
**Definition:** The things that keep food also keep anything else that microbes would eat. Dryness, salt, sugar and cold have preserved bodies, seeds and food for thousands of years.

In ancient Egypt, bodies were packed in a natural salt called natron for about 40 days. The salt drew out the water, and the dried bodies have lasted for thousands of years. Honey was also left in Egyptian tombs, and sealed honey is known to keep for a very long time. In Siberia, frozen ground has kept woolly mammoths, with hair and flesh, for tens of thousands of years. A man died in the Alps about 5,300 years ago. He was found in the ice in 1991, with his last meal still in his stomach. On an island near the North Pole, a vault holds dried seeds from all over the world. The vault is kept at minus 18 degrees Celsius. Astronauts eat food that has been dried or heated and sealed. The Defence Food Research Laboratory in Mysuru has prepared packed foods such as idli and upma for India's astronauts. Nobody yet knows how to keep a full, tasty diet fresh for a journey to Mars that could last years.
- e.g. Egyptian mummies were dried with salt.
- e.g. Frozen mammoths were kept by cold.
- e.g. Space food is dried, or heated and sealed.

## Mostly agreed
- Food spoils because microbes grow in the food. Air alone does not spoil food. *How we know:* In the 1860s Louis Pasteur kept boiled broth open to the air in flasks with long bent necks. The necks trapped dust and microbes. The broth stayed clear. Tinned food that was not heated enough spoils even though no air is inside.
- Microbes need free water to grow, and salt and sugar take free water away. *How we know:* In the 1950s an Australian scientist, William James Scott, measured how microbes grow in foods. Their growth followed the free water, and not the total amount of water. Food scientists have used his measure ever since.
- Cold slows the growth of microbes but does not kill most of them. *How we know:* Food taken out of a freezer and left warm spoils as fast as fresh food. Bacteria have been grown from food kept frozen for years.
- Heating food in a sealed container preserves the food, if the heat is strong enough to kill spores. *How we know:* Nicolas Appert showed this with bottles in 1810. In the 1890s Samuel Prescott and William Underwood found living bacteria in swollen tins that had been heated too little. They worked out the heating times that prevent this.
- The acid made by fermenting bacteria holds back most other microbes. *How we know:* Curd, pickled vegetables and fermented batters have been measured in many laboratories. As the acid rises, the numbers of other bacteria fall.
- About as many bacteria live in and on a person as the person has cells of their own. *How we know:* In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo counted again with better data. They found about 38 trillion bacteria and about 30 trillion human cells in an adult. Older books said ten times as many bacteria, which was wrong.

## How we found out
**Nicolas Appert, a cook in Paris. Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac and Louis Pasteur, French chemists. Samuel Prescott and William Underwood in the United States., Appert published his method in 1810. Pasteur's work was in the 1860s. Prescott and Underwood worked on tins from 1895 to 1898..** In the 1790s, armies and ships needed food that would keep for months. Nicolas Appert, a cook and sweet-maker in Paris, tried for many years to find a way. He packed soups, peas and fruit into glass bottles, corked them tightly and heated them in boiling water. The food kept. In 1810 the French government paid him 12,000 francs, on the condition that he made his method public. Appert did not know why it worked. The chemist Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac tested the bottles. He is said to have argued that air spoils food. He thought the bottled food kept because no air was left to act on it. Many scientists accepted this for years. In the 1860s Louis Pasteur showed that living microbes, carried on dust in the air, do the spoiling. The heat had killed the microbes in the bottle, and the cork kept new ones out. In the 1890s Samuel Prescott and William Underwood, in the United States, opened swollen tins of clams and corn. No air had got in, yet the food had spoiled. They found living bacteria inside, whose spores had survived too little heating.  
*What people believed before:* That air itself spoils food, and that food keeps when the air is taken away. Pasteur showed that microbes do the spoiling. Prescott and Underwood found food spoiled by bacteria inside tins that no air had entered.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Air spoils food, so food keeps if all the air is removed.** (The French chemist Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac, in 1810, and many scientists for years after; *rejected*)  
  For: Food sealed in bottles and heated did keep. When the bottles were opened to the air, the food spoiled.  
  Against: Pasteur showed that microbes carried in the air do the spoiling. Some bacteria grow best with no air at all, and they spoil tins that were heated too little.
- **If you cut the mould off, the rest of the food is fine.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: The fuzzy patch is the only part that looks or smells bad, and the rest tastes normal.  
  Against: The fuzz is only the part of the mould that makes spores. Fine threads run deep into soft foods such as bread, fruit and cooked food, and some moulds leave toxins there. Food safety agencies say to throw such food away.
- **A fridge kills microbes, so food in the fridge is safe for as long as you like.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Food in the fridge lasts far longer than food on the counter.  
  Against: Cold only slows microbes. A few kinds keep growing slowly at fridge temperature. Cooked food in a fridge is best eaten within two or three days.
- **Food with added preservatives is harmful, and food without them is always better.** (Many shoppers; *debated*)  
  For: Some people react badly to certain preservatives. Food made fresh at home needs none.  
  Against: Preservatives are allowed only in small, tested amounts, and they prevent food poisoning and waste. Salt and sugar are preservatives too, and eating a lot of them harms health more surely. The fair question is how much of each, not whether a label has a number.
- **People in hot countries use more spices because spices stop food from spoiling.** (Jennifer Billing and Paul Sherman, two American biologists, in 1998; *debated*)  
  For: They studied thousands of recipes from 36 countries. Hotter countries used more spices, and many spices slow bacteria in laboratory tests.  
  Against: In 2021 a team led by Lindell Bromham studied many more recipes. They found that spice use follows other things as closely as it follows heat. It does not match the risk of illness from food. The amounts of spice in a dish are also much smaller than the amounts used in the laboratory tests.
- **Eating fermented foods improves the microbes in your gut.** (Some scientists, and many makers of probiotic drinks; *debated*)  
  For: In one ten-week study, adults who ate more fermented foods ended with more kinds of gut microbes.  
  Against: The study had only 36 people. Other studies found that swallowed bacteria settle in some people and pass through others. Nobody yet knows how long any change lasts.

## Roots
We have tested how salt and sugar stop microbes, and learned how people keep food. Now we ask what this tells us about water, about life and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In Thick or Thin, the thin jaggery water fermented and the thick jaggery syrup did not. Both glasses held the same jaggery and the same pinch of atta, which carries wild yeasts. In the thick syrup, the sugar holds on to the water, so the yeasts can take almost none. So a food can feel wet to us and still be too dry for a microbe.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call a food wet or dry by how it feels to our fingers. We treat wetness as one fact about the food. For a living cell, what matters is how much of the water is free to take. Honey is about one-sixth water and keeps for years. Bread feels dry and grows mould in a week.  
*Evidence:* In Thick or Thin, the thick syrup stays unchanged for five days. When a spoon of that same syrup is stirred into water, it ferments within a few days. In the Salt Ladder, the glass with 4 teaspoons of salt is still a liquid. Yet it stays clear while the unsalted glass spoils. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your cells need free water too. After very salty food you feel thirsty, because the salt in your blood is holding water that your cells need.
- *what is life:* A living being can wait. A dried yeast cell, or a mould spore on a papad, does nothing we can see for months. Give them water, and they grow again.
- *what is this world:* Water is in most places on Earth, but living beings can use it only where it is free. A salt pan, a jar of honey and a desert are each dry in their own way.

Every living being, from a yeast cell to you, is mostly water and must take in free water to grow. All life shares this need, because all life descends from the same first cells, who lived in water. So one rule explains many kitchens. Avakaya in Andhra Pradesh, dried fish in Kerala and murabba in Uttar Pradesh all keep free water from microbes. People who never met each other found the same answer. They were all dealing with the same living beings and the same need.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.2:** “अन्नाद्वै प्रजाः प्रजायन्ते / याः काश्च पृथिवीँ श्रिताः / अथो अन्नेनैव जीवन्ति / अथैनदपि यन्त्यन्ततः” From food all beings are born. By food they live, and into food they pass when they die. (A mango that we do not eat is eaten by moulds and bacteria. The mango returns to the soil as food for plants. In this lesson's science too, everything alive is food for someone.)
- **Digha Nikaya 16, the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (the Buddha's last words):** “handa dāni, bhikkhave, āmantayāmi vo, / vayadhammā saṅkhārā appamādena sampādethā.” All things that have been put together come apart. Carry on your work with care. (Preserving slows spoiling but never ends it. Honey keeps for years, yet spoils once water gets in. A cook who keeps food well is working carefully with that fact.)
- Science (settled): All known life needs liquid water to grow. No living being has been found growing where the measure of free water is below about 0.6.
- Science (debated): Whether the living bacteria in fermented foods settle in a person's gut for long is still being tested.
- Science (open): Nobody knows how long a dried or frozen microbe can stay alive. Reports of bacteria revived after millions of years are disputed.
- Other view, Doctors and scientists who work on food safety: They would say that it is too gentle to talk about microbes as neighbours who share our food. Unsafe food makes about 600 million people ill every year, and small children suffer most. For a family with little to eat, a spoiled meal is a real loss. In their view, the first duty is to keep microbes out of food, and wonder comes second.
- Practice, A raisin takes back water (3 min): Hold one raisin in your palm. Look at the wrinkles. A raisin is a grape that has lost most of its water to the sun and the air. Now put the raisin on your tongue, and do not chew. Breathe slowly ten times. Feel the raisin soften as it takes water from your mouth. Now chew slowly. - Ask: Honey feels wet to you, and it is too dry for a yeast. So is honey wet or dry?
- Ask: A dried yeast cell can wait for years and then grow. Is the cell alive while waiting? How could you find out?
- Ask: Families all over India worked out how to keep food long before anyone had seen a microbe. How did they know what works?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* To a mould, an old lemon is dinner and a home. To us the lemon is spoiled. Who decides what 'spoiled' means? (hint: Think about who is using the word, and what they wanted the lemon for.)
- *deep:* Honey feels wet to your finger, but for a yeast cell honey is too dry to live in. So is honey wet or dry? (hint: Think about what 'wet' measures: the water that is there, or the water someone can use.)
- *deep:* A mould spore on a dry papad can wait for a year and then grow. Is the spore alive while waiting? (hint: List what living things do. Which of those is the waiting spore doing?)
- *big:* When you set curd, you help one kind of microbe so that others cannot grow. Are you stopping life, or choosing which life? (hint: Compare a bowl of curd with a tin of food that was heated until nothing in it was alive.)

## Story: The Wet Spoon
*Sravani, Karthik and Ammamma are made up. These facts are real. Avakaya is the mango pickle of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. It is made in May from raw mango, salt, mustard powder, chilli powder and oil. Families keep it for a year in a tall ceramic jar called a jaadi. Many homes follow the rules about dry mangoes, dry jars, dry spoons and a layer of oil over the pieces. White mould can grow on pickle pieces that stick out above the oil.*

Every May, Ammamma made avakaya, the mango pickle that her family in Tenali ate all year. This year Sravani, who was ten, was allowed to help. Her cousin Karthik, who was eight, was allowed to carry things.

Ammamma had rules. There were so many that Sravani counted them on her fingers. Wipe each mango dry with a cloth. Never wash the pieces after cutting. Put the big jar in the sun for a whole day before filling it. Dry your hands. Dry the ladle. And pour oil until it stands two fingers above the mango.

'Why two fingers?' asked Sravani.

'Because my mother did it that way,' said Ammamma, 'and her pickle never spoiled.'

That was not a reason, thought Sravani. But she poured the oil.

They filled the tall brown jar, the jaadi, for the storeroom. Ammamma tied a clean cloth over its mouth. Then she filled one small glass jar for the dining table, so that nobody would open the big jaadi every day.

For three weeks the pickle was wonderful. It was red and sharp, and it turned plain curd rice into a feast. Karthik ate it at every meal. He fetched the small jar himself, and he was careful. Before each meal he washed the pickle spoon at the tap, so that it would be clean.

Then the first rain came. The air turned heavy, and the papads in the tin went soft. One morning Ammamma opened the small jar and stood very still.

'Someone has put a hand in this,' she said.

Sravani looked. On top of the pickle sat a patch of white fuzz, like a scrap of cotton.

'I never used my hand!' said Karthik. His eyes filled with tears. 'I used the spoon. I washed it every time.'

Ammamma reached for the jar, to throw the pickle out.

'Wait,' said Sravani. 'Please. Let me look first.'

She carried the jar to the window. The fuzz was not everywhere. The oil had gone down, because three weeks of spoonfuls had taken oil out with the mango. A few pieces now stuck up above the oil, like rocks above a pond. The white fuzz grew on those pieces and nowhere else. Under the oil, the pickle was as red and clean as on the first day.

Then she looked at the spoon in Karthik's hand. It was shining wet. A drop of water hung from its tip.

Sravani ran to the storeroom and untied the cloth on the big jaadi. The oil stood two fingers above the mango. There was no fuzz at all. Nobody had put a spoon into this jar since May.

She walked back slowly, thinking. The mangoes were the same. The salt and the mustard were the same, and both jars were filled on the same day. One jar was fine and one was not. So what was different? The small jar had been opened every day. Its oil had gone down. And every day a spoon had gone into it, wet from the tap.

'Karthik,' she said, 'you didn't make the pickle dirty. You made it wet.'

'But water is clean!' said Karthik.

'It is clean for us,' said Sravani. 'Think of the papads. They were fine all summer. Then the air turned damp, and they went soft. Ammamma said they would grow fungus if we left them like that. A fungus needs water. The pickle is so salty that it has no water a fungus is able to use. Then your spoon brought fresh water, a few drops every day. And the top pieces were out in the air.'

Ammamma was listening now. Sravani held up her fingers again.

'Wipe the mangoes dry. Dry jar. Dry hands. Dry ladle. Those are all one rule, Ammamma. Keep water out. And the oil two fingers high is the second rule. Keep air out.'

'My mother only said that pickle hates water,' said Ammamma. 'She never said why.'

'She knew what worked,' said Sravani. 'She must have watched which jars kept and which jars spoiled.'

Ammamma threw away all the pickle in the small jar, even the part that looked clean. 'Once the fungus is in, we do not eat from that jar,' she said. She washed the jar with hot water and stood it in the sun. The next day she filled it again from the big jaadi, with a dry ladle, and poured oil until every piece was covered.

Karthik made a label and tied it to the spoon with thread. It said: DRY SPOON ONLY. Then he added a line underneath, in smaller letters.

Washed is not the same as dry.

**For the parent:** Each of the grandmother's pickle rules kept water or air away from microbes. A washed spoon was free of dirt, but it carried fresh water, which was the one thing the mould needed.

- Ask: The spoon was washed and clean. Why did the pickle still grow mould?
- Ask: The mould grew only on the pieces above the oil. What does that tell you about what a mould needs?
- Ask: Which rules in your own kitchen keep water or air away from food?

### e1 · The Salt Ladder: how much salt stops dal water from spoiling? (50 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Does a little salt stop microbes, or is a lot of salt needed? How much salt in 100 millilitres of dal water keeps it from spoiling for three days?
- **predict:** Guess first. Five glasses of dal water get no salt, a quarter teaspoon, 1 teaspoon, 2 teaspoons and 4 teaspoons. Which glasses will spoil, and which will spoil first? Is there a glass that will not spoil at all? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Every glass gets dal water from the same pot, in the same amount, in the same kind of glass. All five stand open in the same air for the same 30 minutes. Then they sit side by side in the same place. Only the amount of salt changes. Stir from the least salty glass to the most salty, so the spoon carries no extra salt backwards.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils and strains the dal. Never taste anything from the glasses, not even on the first day. Smell by waving your hand over the glass towards your nose, not by putting your nose in. At the end, pour everything down the sink and wash the glasses with soap and hot water.

  *Parent does:* 1. On the morning of day 4, do the last check. 2. For each glass, find the number of the check at which it first spoiled. Count the evening of day 1 as check 1. If it never spoiled, write 7. 3. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each glass, or build the bars from coins. 4. Ask your child the question below. 5.   *Child mode:* Do the last check. Show each glass as a bar. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: Glass E is still mostly water. Why did microbes not grow there? (good: The salt holds on to the water, so the microbes are unable to take any in.; Water moves out of the microbes into the salty water.)
  - Ask: Pickle is very salty. Could we make pickle with only a little salt? (good: No. A little salt did not stop the dal water from spoiling.; It would spoil unless something else, like the fridge or sourness, helped.)
  - Note: If glass C spoiled late, point to it: it shows that the change from 'spoils' to 'keeps' happens somewhere between 1 and 2 teaspoons. That is 6 to 12 grams in 100 ml, about the strength of pickling brine.
  - Check: In the Salt Ladder, a quarter teaspoon of salt did not slow the spoiling. What does that show? → **A little salt is too little to stop microbes**
### e2 · Thick or Thin: can jaggery water be too sweet to spoil? (45 min, 8 days)
- **question:** Yeasts eat sugar. So does more sugar mean more fermenting, or can there be so much sugar that the yeasts stop?
- **predict:** Guess first. One glass has a little jaggery in a lot of water. One has more jaggery. One is a thick syrup with very little water. Which will bubble and smell fermented first? Which will change the least? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** All three glasses get jaggery from one block, the same small pinch of atta and the same kind of cover. They stand side by side. Only the share of jaggery to water changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water. Do not taste or drink anything from the glasses. Fermented jaggery water holds a little alcohol and may grow mould. Keep the glasses covered, so flies and ants stay out. At the end, pour everything down the sink.
- **record:** Each day, write for each glass: bubbles (yes or no), froth or skin on top (yes or no), smell (same, sour, fruity or bad). Circle the first day each glass changed. At the end, write what you found (your claim), which days show it (your evidence) and why you think it happened (your reasoning).
- **results table:** Glass | Jaggery and water | Day 2 | Day 3 | Day 4 | Day 5 | First day with bubbles or a new smell
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Mix the thin, medium and thick glasses. Add a pinch of atta to each, cover them and put them in a warm place. (15 min); day 2: Look for bubbles, froth and cloudiness, and smell by waving. Write what you find. (3 min); day 3: Check and write again. (3 min); day 4: Check and write again. (3 min); day 5: Do the last check and say what you found. Then thin one spoon of the thick syrup with water in a new glass. (18 min); day 8: Look at the thinned glass after three days. Write what you find, and pour everything away. (3 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the jaggery, the water and the three labels: Thin, Medium, Thick. 2. Say how each will be mixed. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Yeasts are tiny living things. They eat sugar and make bubbles. One glass will have a little jaggery, one more, and one will be thick syrup. Which will ferment first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Yeasts eat sugar. One glass is thin, one is medium, one is thick syrup. Which will bubble first? Say why. Write your guess.
  - Note: Most children guess the thick syrup, because it has the most food for the yeasts. That is a sensible guess. Do not correct it.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Thin: stir 1 tablespoon of jaggery into 8 tablespoons of water. 2. Medium: stir 4 tablespoons of jaggery into 8 tablespoons of water. 3. Thick: put 8 tablespoons of jaggery in the third glass. A grown-up adds 3 tablespoons of hot water. Stir until it is a thick syrup, and let it cool. 4. Label the glasses.  
  *Parent says:* Count the spoons aloud as you measure. The thin glass has 1 spoon of jaggery to 8 of water. The thick one has 8 of jaggery to 3 of water.  
  *Child mode:* Mix the thin glass and the medium glass. A grown-up adds hot water to the thick glass. Stir each one well. Stick the labels on.
  - Note: The thick syrup should pour slowly, like honey. A few grains of jaggery left at the bottom do not matter. Let it cool before the next step, because heat would kill the yeasts.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add one small pinch of atta to each glass, the same size each time. 2. Stir each with a clean spoon. 3. Cover each glass with thin cloth and a rubber band. 4. Put all three in a warm place out of the sun.  
  *Parent says:* Atta carries wild yeasts and bacteria, as fresh grain does. Now every glass has the same microbes. Only the share of jaggery and water is different.  
  *Child mode:* Add a small pinch of atta to each glass. Stir. Cover each glass with cloth. Put them in a warm place.
  - Ask: Why must every glass get the same pinch of atta? (good: So every glass starts with the same microbes.; Otherwise we would not know whether the atta or the jaggery made the difference.)
- **s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Once a day for the next three days, look at each glass through its side. 2. Look for bubbles rising, froth on top and cloudiness. 3. Lift the cloth and smell by waving. 4. Write it in the table. Do not stir and do not taste.  
  *Parent says:* What has changed since yesterday? Look for bubbles first. Then smell by waving your hand. Is the smell sour, fruity or the same?  
  *Child mode:* Once a day, check each glass. Are there bubbles? Is there froth? Is the smell new? Write it down. Never taste.
  - Note: In a warm kitchen the thin glass usually bubbles and smells sour or fruity within two or three days. The medium glass often follows a day later. The thick syrup usually stays the same. In a cool room, allow up to a week. A white skin or spots of mould on the thin glass also count as a change.
  - Wait: Check once a day on days 2, 3 and 4
- **s5 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 5, do the last check. 2. Circle the first day each glass changed. 3. Ask your child to explain the result, and ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Which glass changed first, and which changed last?  
  *Child mode:* Do the last check. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: The thick syrup had the most food for yeasts. Why did it change the least? (good: There was too little water that the yeasts could use.; The sugar holds on to the water.; Water moves out of the yeast cells into the syrup.)
  - Ask: How could we test whether the thick syrup harms the yeasts, or is only short of water? (good: Add water to some of the thick syrup and see if it ferments then.)
  - Note: If your child suggests adding water to the thick syrup, they have designed the next step themselves. Say so.
  - Check: In Thick or Thin, thin jaggery water fermented but thick jaggery syrup did not. Why? → **In thick syrup, sugar holds the water**
- **s6 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 1 tablespoon of the thick syrup into a clean fourth glass. 2. Stir in 8 tablespoons of water, and add nothing else. 3. Label it 'Thick, thinned' and cover it. 4. Keep it beside the old thick glass. 5. Check both once a day for three more days. Then pour everything away.  
  *Parent says:* Now we test your idea. This is the same syrup that did not ferment. We add only water. What do you think will happen? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Take one spoon of the thick syrup. Add 8 spoons of water and nothing else. Watch it for three days.
  - Ask: The thinned syrup fermented. What does that tell you about the thick syrup? (good: Nothing in the jaggery was stopping the yeasts.; The thick syrup was only short of free water.)
  - Note: The thinned glass usually ferments within two or three days, like the first thin glass. This is the strongest evidence in the experiment, because the only thing added was water.
  - Wait: Check the thinned glass once a day for three more days
### e3 · Sun-Dried Tomato: how much of a tomato is water? (45 min, 4 days)
- **question:** How much of a tomato's weight is water? And which will spoil first: slices sealed in a clean jar, or slices left in the open sun?
- **predict:** Guess first. Six tomato slices will dry in the sun for three days. Will they end at about half their weight, a quarter, or less? Three more slices will be sealed in a clean jar. Which will spoil first, the open slices or the sealed ones? Why do you think so? Write your guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** All the slices come from the same tomatoes and are cut to the same thickness. Only one thing differs. One set loses water in the sun and moving air. The other set keeps all its water in a closed jar.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting. These slices are for testing, not for eating. Do not open the jar of sealed slices indoors once they have gone mouldy. Throw a zip bag away closed. Empty a jar straight into the wet waste, at arm's length, and wash it with hot water and soap.
- **record:** Each day, write the weight of the six sun slices, and how both sets look. On day 4, subtract the last weight from the first to find the water that left. Draw a line graph of weight against day, or write the weights in a row. Write what you found (your claim), the numbers that show it (your evidence) and why you think it happened (your reasoning).
- **results table:** Day | Weight of the six sun slices (grams) | How the sun slices look and feel | How the jar slices look
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. A grown-up slices the tomatoes. Weigh six slices and put them in the sun under a cloth. Seal three slices in a jar on the kitchen shelf. (20 min); day 2: Weigh the sun slices and turn them over. Look at the jar slices without opening the jar. (5 min); day 3: Weigh, turn and look again. (5 min); day 4: Weigh the sun slices once more. Work out how much water left. Compare the two sets and explain. (15 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the tomatoes, the plate and the jar. 2. Ask for both guesses and the reasons. 3. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Six slices will dry in the sun. How much lighter will they be after three days? And which slices will spoil first, the open ones or the sealed ones? Why?  
  *Child mode:* How much lighter will dried slices be? Which will spoil first: open slices or sealed slices? Say why. Write your guesses.
  - Note: Many children expect the open slices to spoil first, because flies and dust can reach them. They expect the sealed ones to stay clean. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts both tomatoes into slices about 4 millimetres thick. 2. Choose six slices of about the same size for the sun. 3. Choose three more for the jar. 4. Put the three slices in the clean jar and close the lid.  
  *Parent says:* The slices must be thin and even. Thin slices dry fast. Thick ones stay wet in the middle.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts thin, even slices. Put six on the plate. Put three in the jar. Close the jar.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Weigh the six sun slices on a kitchen scale, and write the grams. 2. With no scale, hang a paper cup from each end of a hanger. Hang the hanger from a door handle. 3. Put the slices in one cup. 4. Add water to the other cup with the 5 ml spoon until the hanger is level. 5. Count the spoons. Each spoon is 5 grams.  
  *Parent says:* We need the weight at the start, so we can tell how much water leaves. One 5 ml spoon of water weighs 5 grams.  
  *Child mode:* Weigh the six slices. With no scale, use the hanger balance. Count spoons of water until it is level. Each spoon is 5 grams.
  - Note: A hanger balance is good to about one spoon, 5 grams. Six slices usually weigh 60 to 90 grams, which is 12 to 18 spoons. Check first that the empty cups hang level. If they do not, add a paper clip to the lighter side.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the six slices on the steel plate, not touching each other. 2. Put the plate in the sunniest place you have. 3. Cover it with thin cloth held up on pegs or stones, so the cloth does not touch the slices. 4. Put the closed jar on a kitchen shelf, not in the fridge.  
  *Parent says:* The cloth keeps flies off and lets the air through. The jar stays closed from now on. We only look through the glass.  
  *Child mode:* Spread the slices on the plate. Put it in the sun. Cover it with thin cloth. Put the jar on a shelf.
  - Note: Bring the plate indoors at night. In the monsoon, or with no sunny spot, put the plate under a ceiling fan. If the open slices still do not dry and begin to grow mould, write that down. It shows why families dry food in the hot, dry months.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Each day for two days, weigh the six sun slices and write the weight. 2. Turn each slice over. 3. Feel one slice: is it wet, leathery or crisp? 4. Look at the jar slices through the glass. Look for liquid, slime and fuzz. Do not open the jar.  
  *Parent says:* What has changed in the sun slices? What has changed in the jar? Which set still has all its water?  
  *Child mode:* Weigh the sun slices each day. Turn them over. Look at the jar slices. Do not open the jar. Write it all down.
  - Note: The sun slices usually lose more than half their weight on the first sunny day. The jar slices usually sit in a pool of juice by day 2 or 3. They may show slime or fuzz by day 3 to 5.
  - Wait: Weigh and look once a day on day 2 and day 3
- **s6 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 4, weigh the sun slices once more. 2. Take the last weight away from the first weight. That is the water that left. 3. Draw a line graph of the four weights, or write them in a row and circle the biggest drop. 4. Compare the sun slices with the jar slices. 5. Put the dried slices in a dry, closed jar, and look at them again in a week.  
  *Parent says:* Compare the sun slices with the jar slices. Which ones spoiled, and which ones kept?  
  *Child mode:* Weigh the dried slices. Subtract to find the water lost. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: The jar was clean and closed. Where did the microbes inside come from? (good: They were already on the tomato skin and the knife.; Microbes are on every food.)
  - Ask: The dried slices sat in open air with microbes landing on them. Why did they not spoil? (good: They had too little water left for microbes to grow.)
  - Ask: What would happen if the dried slices got damp again? (good: The microbes waiting on them could start to grow.)
  - Note: A tomato is about 94 parts water in 100. After three days of home drying, slices usually weigh between one-tenth and one-fifth of their starting weight. If your child's result is far from this, ask what could have gone wrong with the weighing.
  - Check: In Sun-Dried Tomato, the sealed slices spoiled and the dried slices did not. What explains this best? → **Only the sealed slices kept their water**

## Worksheets
### Why food spoils and how we keep it (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What makes food spoil?  
   Options: Air touching the food / Microbes growing in the food / The food simply getting old / The food being kept in the dark  
   **Answer:** Microbes growing in the food. *Why:* Bacteria, yeasts and moulds eat the food and multiply. Air alone does not spoil food, and food does not go bad just by being old: dry rice keeps for years.
2. (fill) Bacteria, yeasts and moulds are all ____: living beings too small to see.  
   **Answer:** ['microbes']. *Why:* Microbe is the word for any living being too small to see without a microscope.
3. (sort) Sort these foods by what keeps them.  
   **Answer:** papad → Dryness; milk powder → Dryness; honey → Salt or sugar; mango in brine → Salt or sugar; frozen peas → Cold; milk in the fridge → Cold. *Why:* Papad and milk powder have had their water removed. In honey and pickle brine, sugar or salt holds the water. Frozen peas and milk in the fridge are kept by cold.
4. (truefalse) The pinch of salt we add to dal for taste keeps the dal from spoiling.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the Salt Ladder, a quarter teaspoon of salt in 100 ml did not stop the dal water from spoiling. Salt preserves only in large amounts.
5. (match) Match each way of keeping food to what it does to microbes.  
   **Answer:** Drying → Removes the water they need; A lot of salt → Holds the water so they get none; The fridge → Slows their growth; Boiling → Kills most of them; A layer of oil → Keeps air away from moulds. *Why:* Each method takes away one thing microbes need, or kills them. Drying and salt take away free water, cold takes away warmth, oil takes away air, and boiling kills.
6. (mcq) Thick jaggery syrup has more sugar than thin jaggery water. Why does the thick syrup keep better?  
   Options: Sugar is a poison for yeasts / Thick syrup is colder / The sugar holds the water / Yeasts dislike the dark colour  
   **Answer:** The sugar holds the water. *Why:* Sugar holds on to water. In thick syrup almost no water is free, so yeasts lose water and stop growing. Sugar does not poison them: thin sugar water is their food.
7. (order) Put these foods in order, from the one that spoils fastest on a shelf to the one that keeps longest.  
   **Answer:** ['Cooked dal', 'Bread', 'Opened jam', 'Raw rice']. *Why:* Cooked dal is wet and warm and spoils in a day. Bread has free water and grows mould in about a week. Jam is held by sugar for weeks. Dry rice keeps for a year or more.
8. (truefalse) Freezing kills all the microbes in food.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Freezing stops microbes from growing, because there is no liquid water. Most are still alive, and grow again when the food warms up.
9. (mcq) Which of these changes is caused by microbes?  
   Options: A cut apple going brown / A biscuit going soft / Bread growing green fuzz / Honey turning grainy  
   **Answer:** Bread growing green fuzz. *Why:* Green fuzz is a mould growing. A cut apple browns by reacting with air, and a biscuit softens by taking in water. Honey turns grainy as sugar forms crystals.
10. (fill) Curd bacteria turn milk sugar into an ____, which makes curd sour.  
   **Answer:** ['acid', 'lactic acid']. *Why:* The acid is lactic acid. The acid sets the milk and makes the curd sour. Most other microbes grow poorly in that acid.
11. (write) Milk powder keeps for a year. Explain what changes once water is stirred in.  
   **Answer:** Milk powder is dry, so the microbes on the powder have too little water to grow. Stirring in water gives them free water again. Now it is like fresh milk and will spoil in about a day unless it is kept cold.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the microbes were there all along, waiting, and that water is what lets them grow.
12. (write) Explain why a wet spoon can spoil a jar of pickle.  
   **Answer:** Pickle keeps because the salt holds the water and the oil keeps air out. A wet spoon brings in fresh water that the salt has not yet held. Mould spores can grow in that water, most of all on pieces that stick out above the oil.. *Why:* A strong answer names the hurdle that the water weakens. It also says that a perfectly clean spoon can still do this.
13. (draw) Draw a jar of mango pickle. Label four hurdles that keep the pickle from spoiling.  
   **Answer:** A jar with mango pieces fully under a layer of oil. Labels: a lot of salt, sour mango, mustard and chilli, and oil that keeps air out.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows every piece below the oil, and each label says what the microbes are missing.
14. (perspective) A lemon has grown blue-green mould. Is the lemon spoiled?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. The cook is right that the lemon is unsafe for people. The mould is doing what every living being does, which is to eat and grow. The gardener points out that where the lemon is changes what we call it.. *Why:* Each view is true from where it stands. A strong answer keeps two things apart: whether the lemon is safe for us to eat, and whether something has gone wrong in nature.
15. (mcq) A sealed tin of food has swollen ends. What should be done with the tin?  
   Options: Open it and smell the food / Boil the food and eat it / Throw it away unopened / Put it in the fridge  
   **Answer:** Throw it away unopened. *Why:* Gas from bacteria that grow without air has swollen the tin. They can make a deadly toxin that tasting or boiling at home will not make safe.
16. (order) Put the steps for growing moong sprouts at home in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Soak the moong in water overnight', 'Drain the water from the moong', 'Keep the soaked moong in a damp cloth for a day or two', 'Rinse the sprouts well, and cook them lightly if they sat long']. *Why:* A seed needs water to wake up, then damp air to grow. The warm, wet cloth also suits microbes, so the last step keeps the sprouts safe.
### Amounts, hurdles and reasons (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) With no salt or a quarter teaspoon, dal water spoiled. With 2 teaspoons it kept. What does this show?  
   Options: Any amount of salt stops spoiling / Salt works only in large amounts / Salt makes food spoil faster / Salt has no effect on microbes  
   **Answer:** Salt works only in large amounts. *Why:* A small amount of salt made no difference, and a large amount stopped the spoiling. So salt preserves only above a certain strength.
2. (mcq) Honey is one-sixth water. Bread is over one-third water. Why does only the bread go mouldy?  
   Options: Bread has more free water / Honey is kept colder / Bread has more sugar / No spores ever land on honey  
   **Answer:** Bread has more free water. *Why:* What matters is free water. In honey the sugar holds nearly all the water. In bread much of the water is free for moulds to use.
3. (mcq) Why do moulds not grow under the oil in a pickle jar?  
   Options: Oil is too cold for them / Oil is a strong acid / Moulds are afraid of oil / Moulds need air, and oil keeps air out  
   **Answer:** Moulds need air, and oil keeps air out. *Why:* Moulds must have air. The oil keeps air away from the pickle. Pieces that stick out above the oil can grow mould.
4. (truefalse) No living microbes are left on dried food.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Drying stops microbes from growing but does not kill them all. Many wait as spores, and grow if the food gets damp.
5. (truefalse) Curd keeps longer than the milk it was made from.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The curd bacteria fill the milk with acid, and most other microbes grow poorly in that acid.
6. (sort) Sort these into spoiled, fermented, or only changed without microbes.  
   **Answer:** frothy dal → Spoiled; slimy spinach → Spoiled; risen idli batter → Fermented; set curd → Fermented; brown cut apple → Only changed; soft biscuit → Only changed. *Why:* Frothy dal and slimy spinach were changed by microbes nobody chose. Idli batter and curd were set to ferment on purpose. A cut apple reacts with air, and a biscuit takes in water from the air.
7. (odd) Three of these keep food by leaving too little free water. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Drying in the sun / Packing in salt / Thick sugar syrup / The fridge  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Drying removes water, and salt and sugar hold it. The fridge leaves the water where it is and works by slowing microbes with cold.
8. (count) A brine needs 12 grams of salt in every 100 ml. How many grams are needed for 500 ml?  
   **Answer:** 60. *Why:* 500 ml is 5 lots of 100 ml. 5 times 12 grams is 60 grams.
9. (order) Put the steps of a fair Salt Ladder test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Make one pot of dal water', 'Pour the same amount into each glass', 'Add a different amount of salt to each glass', 'Leave all the glasses open for the same time, then cover them', 'Check every glass twice a day and write what you see']. *Why:* Everything is kept the same except the salt: one pot, equal amounts, equal time in the air. Then the checks show what the salt alone did.
10. (write) Thick jaggery syrup did not ferment. How could you show that it was only short of water?  
   **Answer:** Take a spoon of the same thick syrup and stir it into water. If it now ferments, nothing in the syrup was stopping the yeasts. The only thing added was water.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing only, the water, and says what result would support the idea.
11. (write) Dried fish is salted as well as dried. Give one reason why two hurdles are better than one.  
   **Answer:** If the fish takes in a little water from damp air, the salt still holds that water. A microbe that can stand dryness may not stand salt. So when one hurdle weakens, the other still works.. *Why:* A strong answer says what could go wrong with one hurdle alone, and how the second covers for it.
12. (write) Tomato slices sealed in a clean jar spoiled. Slices dried in open air did not. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Microbes were already on the tomato skin and the knife, so they were in the jar too. The sealed slices kept all their water, and the microbes grew. The dried slices lost most of their water, so the microbes on them were unable to grow.. *Why:* A strong answer says the microbes were on both sets from the start, and that water made the difference.
13. (perspective) Should food made in factories have added preservatives?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that every preserved food uses hurdles, and salt and sugar are preservatives too. Another is that food poisoning and wasted food are real harms.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. A strong answer weighs a harm on each side. It does not treat either 'natural' or 'tested' as the end of the matter.
14. (label) Label this thermometer with what most microbes do at minus 18, 4, 30 and 72 degrees Celsius.  
   **Answer:** ['Minus 18 degrees (freezer): they stop growing but most stay alive', '4 degrees (fridge): they grow slowly', '30 degrees (kitchen): they multiply fast', '72 degrees (pasteurising): most are killed']. *Why:* Cold slows and then stops growth without killing. Kitchen warmth is where spoiling microbes grow fastest. Strong heat kills most of them.
15. (fill) Water moving out of a microbe's cell into salty water is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['osmosis']. *Why:* In osmosis, water passes through the cell's thin skin. The water moves towards the side with more salt or sugar dissolved.
16. (fill) A wet cloth round a mud pot keeps the water inside cool, because the water drying off the cloth takes ____ from the pot.  
   **Answer:** heat. *Why:* When water dries into the air, it takes heat from what it leaves. This is evaporation. The pot loses heat, so it is cooler than the room.

The fridge does not kill them. It makes each doubling slow.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the same doubling happens in both places. Only the time for each doubling is different.

14. (perspective) A packet of food is one day past its 'best before' date. Eat it or throw it away?

   Options: Bacteria, yeasts and moulds / Ants, flies and cockroaches / Dust, air and light / Salt, sugar and acid

   **Answer:** Free water. *Why:* Microbes can grow only where there is water they can take in. Salt and cold both hold them back, and light makes little difference.
3. (mcq) How does drying keep food?  
   Options: It kills every microbe / It adds sunlight to the food / It leaves too little water for microbes / It makes the food sour  
   **Answer:** It leaves too little water for microbes. *Why:* Dried food has too little free water for microbes to grow. Many microbes are still on the food, waiting. That is why dried food must stay dry.
4. (mcq) Why does pickle have to be very salty?  
   Options: Salt works only in large amounts / Salt adds colour / People like the taste / Salt makes mango softer  
   **Answer:** Salt works only in large amounts. *Why:* A little salt leaves plenty of free water for microbes. Only a lot of salt holds enough of the water to stop them.
5. (mcq) What does a fridge do to most microbes in food?  
   Options: Kills them / Slows them down / Removes them / Turns them into spores  
   **Answer:** Slows them down. *Why:* Cold slows the growth of microbes. They are still alive, so food from the fridge spoils once it is left warm.
6. (mcq) Jam and murabba keep for months because they are:  
   Options: More than half sugar / Brightly coloured / Made from fruit / Kept in glass  
   **Answer:** More than half sugar. *Why:* At that strength the sugar holds the water, so microbes get almost none. Fruit alone spoils quickly, and glass does nothing without the sugar.
7. (mcq) Idli batter has risen overnight and smells a little sour. The batter is:  
   Options: Spoiled / Fermented / Only changed by air / Dried  
   **Answer:** Fermented. *Why:* Bacteria and yeasts that we wanted have made gas and acid in the batter. That is fermentation. Spoiling is the work of microbes nobody chose.
8. (mcq) A crisp biscuit left out overnight now bends. What happened to the biscuit?  
   Options: Microbes ate part of the biscuit / It took in water from the air / It lost its sugar / The air cooled it  
   **Answer:** It took in water from the air. *Why:* Dry food takes in water vapour from damp air. No microbes are needed for that, though mould can follow if the biscuit stays damp.
9. (mcq) Why does curd keep better than milk?  
   Options: Curd is thicker / Curd is whiter / Curd bacteria made acid / Curd has no bacteria  
   **Answer:** Curd bacteria made acid. *Why:* Curd is full of living bacteria. The acid they made holds back most other microbes.
10. (mcq) What do your gut microbes eat that your own body is unable to digest?  
   Options: Sugar / Plant fibre / Water / Salt  
   **Answer:** Plant fibre. *Why:* Your enzymes do not break down most fibre, so it reaches the large intestine. Gut microbes ferment the fibre, and what they make feeds your gut wall.
11. (mcq) In a fair test of how much salt keeps food, what is the one thing that changes?  
   Options: The kind of food / The size of the glass / The amount of salt / The place the glasses stand  
   **Answer:** The amount of salt. *Why:* Only the amount of salt changes. If the food, the glass or the place also changed, nobody could tell which one caused the result.
12. (mcq) What are scientists still unsure of about curd and other fermented foods?  
   Options: Whether they hold living bacteria / Whether their bacteria settle in the gut / Whether they are safe to eat / Whether bacteria make them sour  
   **Answer:** Whether their bacteria settle in the gut. *Why:* Fermented foods are safe and do hold living bacteria. What is still being tested is whether those bacteria stay in the gut and change a person's health.
13. (mcq) Where would a mud pot with a wet cloth round it keep water coolest?  
   Options: In hot, dry air on a summer afternoon in Rajasthan / In damp air on the Kerala coast during the monsoon / Inside a closed steel cupboard  
   **Answer:** In hot, dry air on a summer afternoon in Rajasthan. *Why:* The cloth cools the pot as water dries off it. In hot, dry air the water dries fast and takes away more heat. In damp air it dries slowly, and in a closed cupboard it hardly dries at all.
14. (mcq) Farmers dig potatoes in February. How does a cold store help families eat them in August?  
   Options: Cold slows the microbes that would rot the potatoes / Cold kills every microbe on the potatoes / Cold turns the potatoes into dry flour  
   **Answer:** Cold slows the microbes that would rot the potatoes. *Why:* Cold slows microbes but does not kill them. The potatoes rot far more slowly, so they last for months. Once they come out into warm air, microbes speed up again.
15. (mcq) Priya soaks moong overnight and keeps it in a damp cloth for two days. Why should she rinse the sprouts well?  
   Options: The warm, wet cloth is also a good home for microbes / Sprouts are poisonous until they are rinsed / Rinsing makes the sprouts grow taller  
   **Answer:** The warm, wet cloth is also a good home for microbes. *Why:* Warmth and water are what microbes need too. So a damp cloth that helps a seed sprout can also help harmful microbes grow. Rinsing, and light cooking, makes sprouts safer.
16. (mcq) A family eats rice, dal and one vegetable every day. Which small change adds the most kinds of plants to their week?  
   Options: Sprouting moong, and using a different vegetable each day / Eating the same vegetable, but twice as much / Drinking a probiotic drink instead of lunch  
   **Answer:** Sprouting moong, and using a different vegetable each day. *Why:* The large study on gut microbes counted kinds of plants, not amounts. A new sprout and a new vegetable each day are new kinds. More of the same vegetable adds none, and a drink is not a plant meal.

## Applications
- Long-life milk in cartons is heated to about 140 degrees Celsius for a few seconds and packed with no microbes. It keeps for months without a fridge until it is opened.
- Farmers dry rice, wheat and groundnuts well before storing them. Damp grain grows moulds, and some of those moulds make toxins.
- Cold stores in Himachal Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal keep apples and potatoes for many months after the harvest.
- Fishing families along the coasts of Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and Odisha salt and dry part of the catch. This is the part that cannot be sold fresh.
- Small sachets of silica gel are packed with snacks, shoes and medicines. They take water out of the air inside, so the contents stay dry.
- Vaccines are carried to villages in cold boxes. Cold slows the changes that would spoil them, as it does for food.
- Seed banks keep dried seeds at minus 18 degrees Celsius. Dry and cold together let seeds wait alive for many decades.
- Food for astronauts and for soldiers on mountains is dried, or heated in sealed pouches. It keeps for a year or more without a fridge.

## Open questions
- **Do the living bacteria in fermented foods settle in our gut, and does that change our health?** The studies so far are small and short. In some people swallowed bacteria settle, and in others they pass straight through, and nobody yet knows why.
- **How long can a dried or frozen microbe stay alive?** Bacteria have been grown from ice and from salt crystals that are very old. Some scientists think those bacteria got in later, and the oldest claims are disputed.
- **How does a microbe sense that water is running short, and decide to form a spore?** Scientists know some of the signals inside the cell. They do not know the whole chain from sensing dryness to making a spore.
- **Do bacteria on food signal to each other before they spoil the food?** Many bacteria release chemical signals, and act differently once they are crowded. Whether these signals set off spoiling in real foods is still being studied.
- **Can a full, tasty diet be kept fresh for a journey of several years, such as a journey to Mars?** Packed food stays safe for years, but vitamins and taste slowly fade. Nobody has yet shown a food system that keeps people well fed for that long.

## Connected maths
- In the Salt Ladder, the glass with 2 teaspoons of salt in 100 ml held about 12 grams of salt. How much salt makes 500 ml of brine of the same strength? How many teaspoons is that? **60 grams, which is 10 teaspoons.**. 500 ml is 5 lots of 100 ml. 5 times 12 grams is 60 grams. Each teaspoon is about 6 grams, and 60 divided by 6 is 10. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- These are made-up results from a Salt Ladder. Each bar shows the check at which a glass first spoiled, and 7 means it never spoiled. What is the smallest amount of salt that kept the dal water? Between which two amounts does 'spoils' change to 'keeps'? **2 teaspoons kept it. The change comes between 1 and 2 teaspoons.**. The bars for 2 and 4 teaspoons both reach 7, so those glasses never spoiled. The bar for 1 teaspoon stops at 5, so that glass spoiled late. So the change lies between 1 and 2 teaspoons in 100 ml. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L5`)
- In Sun-Dried Tomato, six slices weighed 80 grams fresh and 8 grams after drying. What percentage of the fresh weight was water that left? **90%.**. The water that left weighed 80 minus 8, which is 72 grams. 72 out of 80 is the same as 9 out of 10, or 90 out of 100. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- Suppose the bacteria in warm dal double every 30 minutes. There are 100 at lunch. How many are there 4 hours later? In the fridge, suppose they double every 12 hours. How many are there after the same 4 hours? **25,600 in the warm dal. Still about 100 in the fridge.**. 4 hours is 8 lots of 30 minutes, so the number doubles 8 times: 200, 400, 800, 1,600, 3,200, 6,400, 12,800, 25,600. In the fridge, 4 hours is less than one doubling time, so the number has hardly changed. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- 100 grams of honey hold about 17 grams of water. 100 grams of bread hold about 38 grams of water. Which holds more water? Which one grows mould? What does this tell you about total water and free water? **Bread holds more than twice as much water, and bread grows mould. So free water matters, and the total amount of water does not.**. 38 is more than twice 17. In honey, sugar holds nearly all of the 17 grams. In bread, much of the 38 grams is free. So counting water alone does not tell you whether a food will keep. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L5`)
- In Thick or Thin, the thin glass has 1 tablespoon of jaggery to 8 of water. The thick glass has 8 tablespoons of jaggery to 3 of water. What fraction of each mixture is jaggery? In which glass is more than half the mixture jaggery? **Thin: 1 ninth. Thick: 8 elevenths. Only the thick glass is more than half jaggery.**. The thin glass has 1 + 8 = 9 spoons in all, and 1 of them is jaggery. The thick glass has 8 + 3 = 11 spoons in all, and 8 of them are jaggery. Half of 11 is 5 and a half, and 8 is more than that. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
