# Compost: how kitchen waste becomes soil without a bad smell
`sci.waste.compost-and-disposal.L3`

**Summary.** A bucket of vegetable peels can turn into dark, crumbly soil in about two months. Or it can turn into a sour, slimy mess in a few days. What decides it is air. Tiny living things called microbes, and worms too, feed on the peels. Microbes that use air make compost, which smells like damp earth. Microbes that live without air make sour and rotten smells, and a gas called methane. In a dump, garbage is pressed down with almost no air, so it rots very slowly for years. Mixing peels with dry leaves or paper, and stirring, lets air in and turns kitchen waste into food for plants.

**Intro.** *Smell the soil in a potted plant. Then smell the kitchen bin that holds yesterday's peels. Soil is made partly from old dead leaves. The bin is full of old dead plants too. So why does one smell like the first rain, and the other smell sour?* In this unit you find out. You set up two jars of peels and watch what happens to each over ten days. You meet the bacteria, fungi and worms who do the work, and find out why some of them need air. You also find out what happens to garbage after the truck takes it away, and how a family in a flat can turn their peels into soil.

## Terms
- **Compost**: Dark, crumbly soil made when microbes and worms break down dead plants and food waste. Plants grow well in it. *e.g.* Peels and dry leaves left in a pot with air holes turn into compost in about two months.
- **Decompose**: To break down into smaller and simpler pieces, because living things are feeding on it. Rotting is another word for it. *e.g.* A banana peel goes brown, soft and then crumbles away as it decomposes.
- **Microbe**: A living thing too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria are microbes, and so are some fungi. *e.g.* One spoonful of compost holds many millions of microbes.
- **Bacteria (one is a bacterium)**: Microbes made of just one tiny cell. They live almost everywhere: in soil, water, air and inside our bodies. *e.g.* Bacteria turn milk into curd, and they do most of the work in a compost pot.
- **Fungus (more than one: fungi)**: A living thing that grows as fine threads and feeds by digesting food outside their body. Moulds and mushrooms are fungi. *e.g.* The white fluffy threads on old peels in a compost pot are a fungus.
- **Aerobic**: Using air. Aerobic microbes need oxygen, a gas in the air, to live and break food down. *e.g.* In a stirred compost pot, aerobic microbes do the work, and it smells of damp earth.
- **Anaerobic**: Living without air. Anaerobic microbes break food down where there is no oxygen. Many of them make sour or rotten smells. *e.g.* At the bottom of a pressed-down bin of wet peels, anaerobic microbes make a sour, slimy layer.
- **Greens**: Wet, fresh waste for a compost pot, such as vegetable peels, fruit skins, tea leaves and coffee grounds. *e.g.* Drumstick ends, onion skins and papaya peels from the kitchen are greens.
- **Browns**: Dry, brown waste for a compost pot, such as dry leaves, torn newspaper, egg cartons and cocopeat. Browns soak up water and keep air spaces open. *e.g.* A handful of torn newspaper on top of the day's peels is a brown.
- **Earthworm**: A soft, long animal with no legs, who lives in damp soil and eats dead plants mixed with soil. *e.g.* After rain, earthworms sometimes come up onto paths and grass.
- **Vermicompost**: Compost made with the help of worms. 'Vermi' comes from the Latin word for worm. *e.g.* Many plant nurseries in India sell bags of vermicompost for pots.
- **Methane**: A gas with no smell that anaerobic microbes make. It burns easily, and it traps the Sun's heat in the air even more than carbon dioxide does. *e.g.* Methane from rotting garbage helps dumps catch fire.
- **Landfill or dump**: A place where a city's garbage is piled up or buried. A landfill is built to keep the garbage in. A dump is an open pile. *e.g.* Chennai's garbage has gone to dumps at Kodungaiyur and Perungudi.
- **Wet waste and dry waste**: Wet waste is food and plant waste that can rot. Dry waste is everything else that is not dangerous, such as plastic, paper, glass and metal. India's rules ask every home to keep them in separate bins. *e.g.* Peels go in the green bin. A milk packet, rinsed, goes in the dry-waste bin.

## Explanations
### What compost is · *defined*
**Definition:** Compost is what is left when microbes, fungi and small animals such as worms have fed on dead plants and food waste. It is dark, crumbly and full of nutrients, the things plants need to grow.

A peel is made of the same things the plant was made of. When decomposers feed on it, they break it into smaller and simpler pieces. Some of it goes into the air as water and a gas called carbon dioxide. The rest becomes compost. A pot of peels shrinks a lot as it turns to compost, because so much goes into the air. Plants take the nutrients from compost through their roots and use them to grow.
- e.g. A bucket of peels and paper shrinks to a small heap of compost after two months.
- e.g. Farmers in India have spread rotted cow dung and leaves on their fields for thousands of years. That is a kind of compost.
### Who does the work: bacteria, fungi and worms · *defined*
**Definition:** Decomposers are living things that feed on dead plants and animals and break them down. In a compost pot, bacteria do most of the work. Fungi, worms, mites and other small animals help.

Bacteria and fungi have no mouth. They let out juices called enzymes that break food into tiny pieces outside their body. Then they soak those pieces up. Worms and small animals chew and tear the waste into smaller bits. That gives the bacteria more surface to work on. Bacteria sense what is around them and move towards food. Fungi spread their threads towards the food they find. Some scientists call these automatic responses.
- e.g. White threads on old peels are a fungus feeding.
- e.g. Tiny white mites and springtails often appear in a healthy compost pot. They are helpers, not pests.
### With air and without air · *defined*
**Definition:** Aerobic microbes use oxygen from the air to break down food. Anaerobic microbes break down food without oxygen. Different microbes are at work in each case, and they make different things.

When there are air spaces in the waste, aerobic microbes grow fast and do most of the work. They turn the waste into compost, water and carbon dioxide, which has no smell. When wet waste is pressed together, the air cannot get in. Then anaerobic microbes take over. They make acids that smell sour, like old curd or vinegar. Some make a gas that smells like rotten eggs. Some make methane. In 1861, the French scientist Louis Pasteur showed that some microbes live without any air at all. Before that, most people thought every living thing needed air.
- e.g. Peels pressed down in a closed bin smell sour after a few days.
- e.g. The same peels mixed with torn paper, with holes for air, smell much milder.
### Greens and browns: why the mix matters · *defined*
**Definition:** Greens are wet, fresh waste such as peels. Browns are dry waste such as dry leaves and paper. A good compost pot has more browns than greens.

Peels are mostly water. On their own, they slump into a wet, airless mess. Browns soak up the extra water and hold the waste apart, so air can get in. Browns also give the microbes a different kind of food. Microbes need both kinds to grow well. A simple rule is to cover each day's peels with about twice as much browns. If the pot smells sour, add more browns and stir. If it looks dry and nothing is happening, add a little water.
- e.g. A handful of peels covered with two handfuls of torn newspaper.
- e.g. Dry leaves swept from the building compound, kept in a sack beside the compost pot.
### Why good compost smells like the first rain · *defined*
**Definition:** The earthy smell of soil and good compost comes mostly from a substance called geosmin. It is made by soil bacteria called Streptomyces.

Streptomyces bacteria live in almost all soil. They grow well where there is air and damp. So when a compost pot has plenty of air, they grow, and it smells like damp earth. Our noses can smell a very tiny trace of geosmin. That is why we notice the smell of rain on dry ground so quickly. Tiny soil animals called springtails follow the smell of geosmin to find these bacteria and eat them. The bacteria's spores stick to the springtails, who carry them to new places.
- e.g. The smell when the first monsoon rain falls on dry ground.
- e.g. The smell of a handful of good compost from a nursery.
### Why big heaps get hot · *defined*
**Definition:** When microbes break food down, they give out heat, the same way your body gives out heat. In a big heap, the heat cannot escape quickly, so the heap gets hot.

The middle of a large compost heap can get hotter than 55 degrees Celsius. That is hotter than a cup of tea you can drink. Different microbes take over as it heats up, ones that live well in heat. The heat kills many seeds and germs. A small pot in a flat loses its heat too fast, so it only gets a little warm, or not warm at all. It still makes compost. It just takes longer.
- e.g. On a cold morning, steam rises from big compost heaps on farms.
- e.g. A small balcony pot may feel slightly warm in the middle, or just cool and damp.
### How an earthworm eats · *defined*
**Definition:** An earthworm has no teeth. They swallow soil and soft bits of dead plants. A part of their gut called the gizzard grinds the food with tiny grains of sand. What comes out at the other end is called a worm cast.

A worm's problem is how to eat tough plant waste with only a soft mouth. They wait until bacteria and fungi have softened the waste, then swallow it with soil. The gizzard squeezes and grinds it with sand, the way a grinding stone works. Worms breathe through their skin, which must stay damp, so they live in damp places and stay away from light. Worm casts are full of nutrients for plants. The worms used for vermicompost live in the top layer of rotting leaves, not deep in the soil.
- e.g. Small piles of muddy worm casts on the ground after rain.
- e.g. Red worms in a vermicompost bin crowd under the peels, away from the light.
### What happens to garbage in a dump · *defined*
**Definition:** In most Indian cities, mixed garbage goes to a dump or landfill. There it is pressed down by machines and more garbage piles on top.

Inside a dump, food and paper are squashed together with plastic, with almost no air. So the anaerobic microbes take over. They work very slowly, and they make methane. Methane burns easily, so dumps often catch fire. In March 2023, the Brahmapuram dump near Kochi burned for almost two weeks. Its smoke covered the city. Rain soaks through the garbage and comes out as a dark, smelly liquid that can reach the water underground. When wet waste is kept apart and composted, far less of it reaches the dump.
- e.g. Mumbai's Deonar dumping ground has been used for nearly a hundred years.
- e.g. Mysuru and Alappuzha are known for helping homes keep wet waste separate and compost it.
### Compost at home, or in a big city plant? · *debated*
Everyone agrees wet waste should be kept out of dumps. People disagree about the best way to do it.
- e.g. A family in a Bengaluru flat composts in stacked terracotta pots on the balcony.
- e.g. Indore collects sorted wet waste from homes and turns it into gas that runs city buses.
- **People who compost at home:** Composting at home means no truck has to carry the waste. Families see how much they waste and start to waste less. The compost feeds their own plants.
- **City planners and engineers:** Many families have no space or time to compost. A city plant can handle tonnes of wet waste every day, and can also make biogas, a gas used for cooking or running vehicles. But it only works if homes sort their waste well.
- **Waste pickers' groups:** Sorting at home is what matters most. When homes keep wet and dry waste apart, both compost plants and the people who sort dry waste can do their work safely.

## Mostly agreed
- Food and plant waste rot because living things feed on them: mainly bacteria and fungi, helped by worms and small animals. *How we know:* Peels kept away from all microbes, for example in a sealed can after heating, do not rot. Under a microscope, rotting waste is crowded with microbes.
- Compost made with air smells earthy. Waste rotting without air smells sour or rotten and makes methane. *How we know:* Compost scientists measure oxygen and gases in heaps. Turning a smelly heap to let air in makes the smell fade within days.
- Garbage in a dump or landfill rots very slowly, because it is pressed tight with little air. *How we know:* Scientists who dug into landfills found newspapers that could still be read decades after they were thrown away.
- Keeping wet waste separate at home is the first step to composting it. India's Solid Waste Management Rules of 2016 ask every home to do this. *How we know:* Cities where most homes sort their waste send far less to their dumps.

## How we found out
**William Rathje and his students, the Garbage Project, University of Arizona, The Garbage Project began in 1973. The landfill digs began in 1987..** For a long time, most people thought a landfill worked like a giant compost heap. A landfill is a huge pit or pile where a city's garbage is dumped, pressed down and covered. People believed that food, paper and wood soon rotted away there. William Rathje, an archaeologist at the University of Arizona in the United States, decided to check. Archaeologists usually dig up old cities to learn how people lived. Rathje and his students dug into garbage instead. In 1973, in the city of Tucson, they began sorting and recording what families threw away. In 1987 they began drilling deep into landfills and pulling out what was buried there. They found newspapers that could still be read, decades after they were thrown away. The dates printed on them showed how long they had been buried. They found hot dogs and other food that could still be recognised. Packed tight, with almost no air and little water, the microbes that rot food and paper could hardly work. A landfill turned out to keep garbage for a very long time, not to compost it.  
*What people believed before:* A landfill rots food and paper away quickly, like a giant compost heap. Digging showed newspapers that could still be read and food that could still be recognised after decades.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A compost bin always smells bad.** (A common worry in flats; *rejected*)  
  For: A closed bin of wet peels does smell sour within days.  
  Against: With enough browns, air holes and stirring, a compost pot smells like damp soil. The bad smell is a sign of too little air or too much water.
- **Food and paper rot away quickly in a dump.** (What most people believed until the 1980s; *rejected*)  
  For: On a compost heap, peels disappear in weeks.  
  Against: Digging into landfills found readable newspapers and food that could still be recognised after decades. Without air, rotting almost stops.
- **You need worms to make compost.** (Many people new to composting; *rejected*)  
  For: Worms do speed up composting, and vermicompost is very good for plants.  
  Against: Bacteria and fungi do most of the work. A pot with no worms at all still turns peels into compost.
- **Cooked food should never go in compost.** (Advice in many home composting guides; *debated*)  
  For: In a small home pot, cooked food, milk and meat smell strongly and bring rats and flies.  
  Against: Big hot heaps, city plants and a method called bokashi, which ferments food in a closed bucket, can handle cooked food.
- **Burning dry leaves is a clean way to get rid of them.** (A common habit in many cities and villages; *rejected*)  
  For: The leaves are gone in minutes.  
  Against: Burning fills the air with smoke that harms our lungs. Composted, the same leaves become browns for a compost pot and food for plants.

## Roots
We have been learning how microbes and worms turn peels into compost, and why air matters. Now we ask what compost tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In your test, the peels pressed alone went slimy and smelled sour. The same peels mixed with dry paper smelled like damp soil. Paper let air in, and microbes that use air did the work. So the smell of rotting tells us which microbes are at work, and whether they have air.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'garbage stinks', as if the bad smell were packed inside the waste. But the same peels smelled sour in one jar and earthy in the other. The smell depends on who is feeding on the waste, and that depends on air.  
*Evidence:* In the paper jar test, jar A, with peels alone, collected liquid and smelled sour within a few days. Jar B, with the same peels and paper, stayed drier and smelled milder, like damp earth. Stirring a jar every day also made it smell less sour. In dumps, where garbage is pressed down with no air, food and paper last for decades.

- *who am i:* Your gut is full of microbes that live without air. They help digest the fibre in your food.
- *what is life:* Every peel you throw away is food for millions of microbes, fungi and worms. Their meal becomes soil for new plants.
- *what is this world:* The tiny particles in a peel are never lost. They go into the air and the soil, and plants build new leaves from them.

A peel was once part of a living mango tree or tomato plant. Microbes and worms feed on it, and break it into water, carbon dioxide and nutrients. A new plant takes these up and builds new leaves and fruit from them. Then someone eats the fruit, and the peel goes back to the microbes. The same tiny particles pass from plant to microbe to soil to plant again, through countless lives. Many different beings are made, in turn, of the same one store of stuff.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2 (Bhrigu Valli):** Bhrigu came to understand that food is Brahman, the whole. From food all beings are born. By food they live. Into food they return when they leave. (A peel was food for us. In the compost pot it becomes food for microbes and worms. Their compost becomes food for plants, and the plants become food for us again.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, Peace Is Every Step (1991), the chapter 'The Rose and the Garbage':** Look deeply at a rose, and you can see the garbage it will become. Look deeply at garbage, and you can see the compost and the roses it will become. (In the project, peels became the compost that a tulsi or marigold grows in. The flower and the garbage are the same stuff at different times.)
- Science (settled): Decomposers return the carbon, nitrogen and other nutrients in dead things to the air and the soil, where plants use them again.
- Science (settled): Some microbes need oxygen, some are harmed by it, and some can live either way. Louis Pasteur first described microbes living without air in 1861.
- Other view, Many chemists and microbiologists: A smell is just particles of certain gases reaching the nose. Microbes making those gases are following their chemistry. Talking of food 'becoming' us, or of microbes being 'at work', is a way of speaking, and adds nothing to the science.

That smell is made by bacteria, millions of them, living in your hand. Now breathe out slowly. Your breath carries carbon dioxide, just like the breath of the microbes in the compost. Let's breathe in and out three more times, and feel that we are breathing alongside them.

## Story: Chithappa's Smelly Bucket
*Meenakshi, her family and their flat are made up. The way they fix the bucket is the real way to fix a smelly compost pot.*

Meenakshi lived on the fourth floor of a building in Velachery, in Chennai. She was nine. Her Chithappa, her father's younger brother, had just come to stay with them. He was full of plans.

"From today," he said on the first evening, "this family will make its own compost. No more peels for the garbage truck."

He found an old blue paint bucket with a tight lid. Into it went the morning's onion skins and drumstick ends. Then the papaya peels. Then a plate of leftover sambar rice that nobody wanted. Every evening he pressed it all down hard with a ladle. "Make room for tomorrow," he said, and snapped the lid shut.

Meenakshi watched. Something about it bothered her, but she could not say what.

On the fourth day, Amma opened the balcony door and stepped back. "Ayyo! What is that smell?"

It was the bucket. Even with the lid on, it smelled like curd left out for a week, mixed with something worse. Small flies were walking on the lid. When Chithappa lifted it, the smell rushed out. At the bottom of the bucket, a dark, slimy liquid had collected. The peels on top were grey and wet.

That evening, Mrs Fernandes from the flat next door knocked. "Is something dead on your balcony?" she asked, kindly but firmly.

Chithappa looked sad. "Composting does not work in a flat," he said. "Tomorrow I will throw it all in the garbage."

Meenakshi was thinking. She remembered her summer holidays at Paati's house near Thanjavur. Paati had a compost pit behind the house. It never smelled bad. It smelled like the ground after rain. What did Paati do differently?

She called Paati on Amma's phone and told her everything.

Paati laughed. "Kanna, tell me one thing. Can the bucket breathe?"

"Breathe? It has a lid," said Meenakshi. "And Chithappa presses it down every day."

"Then the good workers are choking," said Paati. "The tiny living things that make compost need air. When there is no air, other tiny things take over. They are the ones who make the bad smell. In my pit, every basket of peels gets covered with dry leaves. Twice as many leaves as peels. And every few days I turn it with a stick. And no cooked rice. That brings rats."

Meenakshi put the phone down and turned to Chithappa. "Paati says we need three things," she said. "Holes for air. Dry things, like leaves or paper, to soak up the wet. And stirring. And no rice."

Chithappa raised his eyebrows. Then he smiled. "Show me."

So Meenakshi showed him. First, they put on gloves and took out the sambar rice, and put it in the wet-waste bin for the truck. Then Chithappa heated a thick steel skewer on the stove, and Meenakshi held a torch while he melted twenty small holes into the sides and the lid of the bucket. They stood the bucket on an old tray.

Meenakshi tore up a whole week of newspapers into strips. The watchman gave them a sack of dry leaves he had swept from under the neem tree by the gate. They mixed the strips and leaves into the slimy peels with a stick, until the mess looked like a brown, damp salad.

"It is still smelly," said Chithappa.

"Give the workers two days," said Meenakshi.

Two days later, they lifted the lid together. Chithappa sniffed carefully. Then he sniffed again. The sour smell had almost gone. Under the top layer, there were fine white threads spreading over the peels, like cotton. The bucket felt a little warm when Meenakshi put her hand on its side.

From then on, every evening, Meenakshi put the day's peels in. Chithappa covered them with two handfuls of paper and leaves. Every Sunday they stirred it with the stick. Mrs Fernandes stopped knocking. One day she brought over her own peels in a steel box and asked if she could add them.

Eight weeks later, Meenakshi tipped the bottom of the bucket out onto a newspaper. It was dark brown and crumbly. She could not find a single peel in it. She held a handful to her nose. It smelled exactly like Paati's garden after the first rain.

She mixed it into the soil of a pot and planted a tulsi seedling in it. Chithappa watched her press the soil around the roots.

"So the same peels can stink, or turn into this," he said slowly. "It depends on who gets to eat them."

"And on whether they can breathe," said Meenakshi.

**For the parent:** For the parent: pressed-down wet peels with a tight lid leave no air. Then anaerobic microbes, who live without air, take over, and they make sour acids and smelly gases. The holes, the dry paper and leaves, and the stirring all let air in. The browns also soak up the extra water. Then aerobic microbes, who use air, grow fast. They make compost that smells of damp earth, and their work gives out a little heat, which is why the bucket felt warm. The white threads were a fungus. Cooked rice was taken out because in a small pot it smells strongly and brings rats.

- Ask: Why did Chithappa's bucket smell so bad by the fourth day?
- Ask: What three things did Paati say the bucket needed? Why does each one help?
- Ask: Why did the bucket feel a little warm after the fix?
- Ask: Meenakshi showed her uncle how to fix it, even though he was the grown-up. Have you ever known something a grown-up did not?

## Experiments
### e1 · Peels Alone or Peels with Paper: which jar smells of earth? (50 min, 10 days)
- **question:** Two jars hold the same chopped peels. Jar B also has torn dry newspaper mixed in. Which jar turns slimy and sour, and which smells more like damp soil?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which jar will smell worse after a week, A with peels alone or B with peels and paper? And why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both jars are the same size, hold the same amount of the same peels, get one spoon of soil each, are pressed down the same amount and stand side by side under the same cloth. Only one thing is different: the paper in jar B. So any difference comes from the paper.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the bottles and chops the peels. Never put your nose into a jar. Lift the cloth and waft the smell towards you with your hand. Use a spoon, not fingers, to poke the peels. Wash hands with soap after every check. Keep the jars away from the food and cooking area.

## Worksheets
### Compost Basics (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these can go in a small compost pot at home?  
   Options: Onion skins / A plastic spoon / Leftover chicken curry / A used battery  
   **Answer:** Onion skins. *Why:* Onion skins are raw plant waste, so microbes turn them into compost. Plastic does not rot, cooked meat brings rats and smells, and a battery is hazardous waste.
2. (sort) Sort these for a compost pot.  
   **Answer:** banana peel → Greens; dry leaves → Browns; milk packet → Not in the pot; used tea leaves → Greens; torn newspaper → Browns; glass bottle → Not in the pot; carrot peel → Greens; egg tray → Browns. *Why:* Greens are wet plant waste. Browns are dry plant waste and paper. Plastic and glass do not rot, so they go in dry waste.
3. (truefalse) Bacteria do most of the work of turning peels into compost.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Bacteria are too small to see, but there are so many of them that they do most of the work. Fungi and worms help.
4. (truefalse) A compost pot works best with a tight lid and no holes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Without air, microbes that live without air take over, and the pot turns sour and slimy. A compost pot needs air holes.
5. (fill) Microbes that use air to break down food are called ____ microbes.  
   **Answer:** ['aerobic']. *Why:* Aerobic means using air. Aerobic microbes make compost that smells like damp earth.
6. (fill) Dry things for a compost pot, such as torn newspaper and dry leaves, are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['browns']. *Why:* Browns are dry. They soak up water and keep air spaces open.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Compost → dark, crumbly soil made from rotted plants; Decompose → to break down because living things feed on it; Microbe → a living thing too small to see with your eyes alone; Earthworm → an animal with no legs who eats dead plants mixed with soil. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit.
8. (order) Put the steps for starting a compost pot in order.  
   **Answer:** ['A grown-up makes air holes in the bucket', 'Put browns and a little soil at the bottom', "Add today's chopped peels", 'Cover the peels with twice as much browns']. *Why:* First the holes, so air can get in. Then a dry bottom layer to soak up drips. Then peels, covered so flies can't reach them.
9. (mcq) Remember the paper jar test. Jar A had peels alone, and jar B had peels with paper. Which jar usually collects brown liquid at the bottom?  
   Options: Jar A, peels alone / Jar B, peels with paper / Neither  
   **Answer:** Jar A, peels alone. *Why:* Peels are mostly water. In jar A, the water drains to the bottom. In jar B, the paper soaks it up.
10. (mcq) What does good compost smell like?  
   Options: Damp earth, like the first rain / Old curd / Rotten eggs  
   **Answer:** Damp earth, like the first rain. *Why:* Good compost is made by microbes that use air. Soil bacteria in it make the smell of damp earth. Sour or rotten smells mean there is too little air.
11. (write) In the paper jar test, jar B smelled less than jar A. Explain why. Use the words 'paper' and 'air'.  
   **Answer:** The paper soaked up the water from the peels and held them apart. So air could get in. Microbes that use air did the work, and they do not make sour smells.. *Why:* A full answer says what the paper did (soaked up water, kept spaces open) and why that matters (air for the microbes).
12. (draw) Draw a compost pot that will not smell. Show the air holes, the browns and the peels. Draw arrows to show where air gets in.  
   **Answer:** A pot with holes in the sides and lid, layers of peels covered with browns, and arrows going in through the holes.. *Why:* The drawing shows the three things a pot needs: air, browns to soak up the wet, and peels covered so flies can't reach them.
13. (write) Mrs Rao, who lives in the next flat, says a compost pot will make the whole balcony stink. What would you tell her, and what would you show her?  
   **Answer:** I would tell her that a pot only stinks when it has too little air or too much water. With holes, plenty of browns and stirring, it smells like damp soil. I would let her waft the smell of our pot or of jar B.. *Why:* A strong answer explains the cause of the smell and offers evidence she can check herself.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a green for a compost pot?  
   Options: Torn newspaper / Papaya peel / A dry leaf / An egg tray  
   **Answer:** Papaya peel. *Why:* A papaya peel is wet, fresh plant waste, so it is a green. Newspaper, a dry leaf and an egg tray are dry, so they are browns. Picking one of those usually means mixing up the two groups: greens are wet, browns are dry.
2. (mcq) Why should leftover cooked rice stay out of a small compost pot at home?  
   Options: It smells strongly and brings rats and flies / Microbes cannot eat rice / Rice turns into plastic  
   **Answer:** It smells strongly and brings rats and flies. *Why:* Microbes can eat rice, but in a small home pot cooked food smells strongly and brings rats and flies. Big city plants and hot heaps can handle it.
3. (mcq) Priya puts the same peels in two jars. She mixes torn paper into jar B only. After a week, jar A has liquid at the bottom and smells sour. What does this show?  
   Options: Paper keeps peels drier and lets air in / Paper makes peels rot faster than anything / Jar A had no microbes  
   **Answer:** Paper keeps peels drier and lets air in. *Why:* The only difference was the paper. It soaked up the liquid and held the peels apart, so air could get in and microbes that use air could work. Jar A had plenty of microbes, but they were the kind that live without air.
4. (mcq) In the paper jar test, why do both jars get exactly one cup of the same peels?  
   Options: So the only difference is the paper / Because one cup is the most a jar can hold / So the jars look nice  
   **Answer:** So the only difference is the paper. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. If jar A had more peels, we could not tell whether the smell came from the extra peels or from having no paper.
5. (mcq) Who does most of the work of turning peels into compost?  
   Options: Bacteria / Earthworms / Flies / The Sun  
   **Answer:** Bacteria. *Why:* Bacteria do most of the work. Worms help a lot, but a pot with no worms still makes compost. If you picked earthworms, remember that they are the decomposers we can see. Most of the work is done by the ones we cannot.
6. (mcq) Microbes that live without air are called anaerobic. What do many of them make?  
   Options: Sour and rotten smells / Sweet smells, like flowers / Plastic  
   **Answer:** Sour and rotten smells. *Why:* Many anaerobic microbes make sour acids and smelly gases. Some also make methane, which has no smell but burns easily.
7. (mcq) Arjun's compost pot is dry and dusty, and nothing has changed for two weeks. What should he do?  
   Options: Sprinkle a little water and stir / Add a tight lid / Put it in the fridge  
   **Answer:** Sprinkle a little water and stir. *Why:* Microbes need water to digest food. A dry pot is too dry for them to work. A little water, until it feels like a squeezed sponge, gets them going again.
8. (mcq) Why does a compost pot need holes in its sides?  
   Options: To let air in for the microbes that use air / To let the compost fall out / To let worms escape  
   **Answer:** To let air in for the microbes that use air. *Why:* Air holes let oxygen reach the waste. Then aerobic microbes do the work, and the pot smells of damp earth instead of turning sour.
9. (mcq) Food and paper in a dump can last for many years. Why?  
   Options: The garbage is pressed tight with almost no air / There are no microbes in a city / Dumps are kept very cold  
   **Answer:** The garbage is pressed tight with almost no air. *Why:* Microbes are everywhere, also in dumps. But without air only the slow anaerobic microbes can work. William Rathje's team found newspapers they could still read decades after they were buried.
10. (mcq) Dumps often catch fire. Which gas, made by microbes that live without air, helps the fires start?  
   Options: Methane / Oxygen / Water vapour  
   **Answer:** Methane. *Why:* Anaerobic microbes in the dump make methane, which burns easily. That is one reason fires like the one at Brahmapuram, near Kochi, in 2023 are so hard to put out.
11. (mcq) How can you tell that compost in a pot is ready to use?  
   Options: It is dark and crumbly, smells like damp earth, and you cannot see the peels / It is wet and slimy / It smells sour  
   **Answer:** It is dark and crumbly, smells like damp earth, and you cannot see the peels. *Why:* Ready compost looks like dark soil and smells earthy. Slime and a sour smell mean it needs more browns and air, and more time.
12. (mcq) What should go on top of each day's peels in a compost pot?  
   Options: About twice as much browns, such as torn paper or dry leaves / Water, to keep them wet / A plastic sheet  
   **Answer:** About twice as much browns, such as torn paper or dry leaves. *Why:* Browns soak up the wet, make air spaces and hide the peels from flies. Water would make the pot too wet, and plastic would keep air out.

## Applications
- Families in flats turn their peels into compost for potted plants on the balcony.
- Farmers spread compost and rotted cow dung on fields to feed the soil.
- Plant nurseries sell compost and vermicompost in bags for gardens and pots.
- City compost plants turn tonnes of sorted wet waste into compost every day.
- Biogas plants let anaerobic microbes break down wet waste and dung in closed tanks. They make methane on purpose and burn it for cooking or to run vehicles.
- Indore turns sorted wet waste into gas that runs city buses.
- Engineers pipe methane out of some landfills, so it can be burned safely instead of building up and catching fire.
- Old dumps in Indian cities, such as Perungudi in Chennai, are being dug up and sorted, so the land can be used again. This is called biomining.
- Temples and markets compost flowers and banana leaves instead of sending them to dumps.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of microbes live in one spoonful of compost?** Scientists can now read the genes of the microbes in a sample, and they keep finding kinds nobody has named. Most of them have never been grown in a lab, so nobody knows what most of them do.
- **How do the microbes in a compost pot decide who does what, and when?** Bacteria send chemical signals to each other, and different kinds take over as the pot changes. Scientists are only starting to understand how this teamwork is arranged.
- **What is the best way for a big city like Chennai or Bengaluru to deal with all its wet waste?** Home composting, neighbourhood composting, big plants and biogas all have strengths and problems. Cities are still trying them out and comparing results.
- **How long will the oldest dumps keep making methane?** It depends on what is buried, how wet it is and how it is packed. Scientists measure it, but every dump is different, and many are decades old.

## Connected maths
- Kavya weighed her family's peels on a kitchen scale for three days: 400 grams on Monday, 300 grams on Tuesday and 500 grams on Wednesday. How much was that altogether? Is it more or less than 1 kilogram? **1,200 grams, which is 1 kilogram and 200 grams. That is more than 1 kilogram.**. 400 + 300 = 700. 700 + 500 = 1,200 grams. 1,000 grams make 1 kilogram, so 1,200 grams is 1 kilogram and 200 grams. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)
- Here are made-up smell scores from the paper jar test, from 0 (no smell) to 5 (very bad). Jar A: day 3 is 2, day 5 is 3, day 7 is 4. Jar B: day 3 is 1, day 5 is 1, day 7 is 1. Draw a bar chart for day 7. What pattern do you see across the days, and what do you predict for day 9? **On day 7, jar A's bar is 4 and jar B's is 1. Jar A's smell grows by 1 every two days, so it may be about 5 on day 9. Jar B stays at 1.**. Jar A went 2, 3, 4: up by 1 each check. If that goes on, day 9 would be 5, the highest score. Jar B stayed the same, so we predict 1. A prediction is a careful guess from a pattern. It can be wrong, so we check it. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- For every cup of peels, you add 2 cups of browns. Today the family has 4 cups of peels. How many cups of browns do they need? If they only have 5 cups of browns, how many cups of peels can they cover? **8 cups of browns. With only 5 cups of browns they can cover 2 cups of peels, with 1 cup of browns left over.**. 1 cup of peels needs 2 cups of browns, so 4 cups need 4 groups of 2, which is 8. With 5 cups of browns: 2 cups of peels use 4 cups of browns, and 1 cup is left. That is not enough for a third cup of peels. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
