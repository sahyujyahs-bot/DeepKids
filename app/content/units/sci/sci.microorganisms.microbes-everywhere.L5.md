# The microbes who live on you and in you: many lands on one body
`sci.microorganisms.microbes-everywhere.L5`

**Summary.** The bacteria between your toes are different from the bacteria on your forearm, though both places are your own skin. Why? In this unit you swab three places on your skin and grow what you find on potato slices. You play a dice game that shows how the microbes who live in you keep harmful newcomers out. You also find out why the same microbe can help in one place and harm in another.

**Intro.** *Your forearm and the gap between your toes are less than a metre apart, and both are covered by your own skin. Yet different microbes live in each. How can one body be so many different homes?* A microbe is a living being too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria are the commonest microbes on you. About 38 trillion bacteria live on and in a grown-up, and nearly all of them are harmless or helpful. They do not live evenly spread. A dry forearm, an oily forehead, a damp toe gap, a mouth and a large intestine each give different food, water and air. So each holds a different community. In this unit you test that yourself. You rub clean cotton buds on three places on your skin and grow what they pick up. You play Hold the Space, a dice game about how the microbes who already live in a gut keep a harmful newcomer out. By the end you can explain why most microbes do you no harm. You can also explain when a harmless microbe becomes a harmful one.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what residents and visitors are, and name the main places on and in a body where microbes live.
1. By the end I can explain why different parts of one body hold different microbes, using water, oil, acid and oxygen.
2. By the end I can run a fair swab test on potato slices, and say what the test can and cannot show.
3. By the end I can use a dice model to explain how residents keep a harmful newcomer out.
4. By the end I can explain why most microbes are harmless or helpful, and why the same microbe can help in one place and harm in another.
5. By the end I can say where a baby's microbes come from, and how food, people, pets and medicines change the community.
6. By the end I can say what scientists still argue about or do not know about the microbes who live with us.

## Terms
- **Microbe**: A living being too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria, yeasts and many other tiny beings are microbes. *e.g.* One full stop on this page could hold thousands of bacteria side by side.
- **Bacteria**: Microbes made of one small cell each. One of them is called a bacterium. They are the commonest living beings on and in your body. *e.g.* The bacteria in your large intestine break down plant fibre that your own cells leave whole.
- **Resident**: A microbe who lives and multiplies on a body for a long time. Residents live in the pores of the skin, in the mouth and in the gut. *e.g.* Soap and water lift off dirt, but the residents deep in your skin pores stay and grow back within hours.
- **Visitor**: A microbe who lands on a body from soil, food, a door handle or another person, and stays only a short time. *e.g.* Soil bacteria picked up on your fingers in a flower pot wash off with soap.
- **Community**: All the different living beings who share one place and affect each other. *e.g.* The bacteria, yeasts and tiny mites on a grown-up's forehead make up one community.
- **Microbiome**: The whole community of microbes who live on and in one body, or in one part of a body. *e.g.* Your gut microbiome is the community of microbes in your stomach and intestines.
- **Habitat**: The place where a living being lives, with the food, water, air and warmth found there. *e.g.* The gap between two toes is a warm, damp habitat. The forearm is a dry one.
- **Sebum**: The oil that skin makes in tiny glands beside each hair. It keeps skin soft and is food for some bacteria and yeasts. *e.g.* A grown-up's forehead and nose feel slightly oily by evening because of sebum.
- **Swab**: A clean cotton bud rubbed on a surface to pick up the microbes there. To swab is to rub with one. *e.g.* In the Three Lands Test you swab your forearm, your forehead and a toe gap.
- **Colony**: A spot you can see, made of millions of microbes who all grew from one or a few cells that landed there. *e.g.* One cream-coloured dot on a potato slice is a colony.
- **Anaerobe**: A microbe who lives without oxygen. For many anaerobes, oxygen is a poison. *e.g.* Most bacteria in the large intestine are anaerobes, so they cannot be grown on a slice left in the air.
- **Mucus**: A slippery layer that the body makes to cover its wet inner surfaces, such as the nose and the gut wall. *e.g.* Gut bacteria live in and on the mucus that lines the large intestine, and some eat it.
- **Holding the space**: The way residents keep newcomers out by already using the room and the food, and by making substances that newcomers dislike. Scientists call it colonisation resistance. *e.g.* In the Hold the Space game, a newcomer who lands on a square with a resident finds no room.
- **Germ**: An everyday word for a microbe who makes someone ill. The science word is pathogen. *e.g.* The bacterium who causes cholera is a germ. The bacteria who set curd are not.
- **Opportunist**: A microbe who lives harmlessly in one part of a body but causes illness when she reaches another part, or when the body's defences are low. *e.g.* A gut bacterium who gets into the bladder can cause a painful infection there.
- **Antibiotic**: A medicine that kills bacteria or stops them multiplying. An antibiotic does not work on viruses. *e.g.* An antibiotic for an ear infection also kills many harmless bacteria in the gut.
- **Succession**: The way a community in a new place changes in steps, as the first settlers are joined and replaced by others. *e.g.* A baby's gut is settled first by a few kinds of bacteria, and by many more kinds after the first solid food.
- **Archaea**: Single-celled microbes who look like bacteria but belong to a separate great branch of life. *e.g.* An archaeon in the human gut uses up hydrogen gas and makes methane.
- **Quorum sensing**: The way bacteria sense how many of their own kind are near. Each one gives out a signal substance, and all of them change what they do when the signal is strong. *e.g.* Light-making bacteria in a squid start to glow only when enough of them are crowded together.
- **Reading genes**: Finding out who lives in a sample by reading the DNA in the sample, without growing anything. *e.g.* Reading genes found many kinds of gut bacteria that nobody had ever grown on a dish.

## Explanations
### Residents and visitors: who lives on a body · *defined*
**Definition:** A resident is a microbe who lives and multiplies on or in a body for months or years. A visitor is a microbe who lands there and is gone within hours or days. All of a body's microbes together are called the microbiome.

Every healthy person, dog, crow and earthworm carries microbes. A grown-up human carries about 38 trillion bacteria, which is about as many as the body's own cells. All of them together weigh about 200 grams, the weight of a small mango. They are light because a bacterium is far smaller than a human cell. Besides bacteria there are yeasts, archaea and viruses, and on most grown-ups some tiny mites as well. Residents hold on. Skin residents live deep in the pores, where a wash does not reach, and they spread back over the skin within hours. Visitors come from soil, food, door handles and other people. Most visitors find the place too dry, too acid or too crowded, and they die or are washed off. Why does this matter? Because hand washing is aimed at visitors. Soap lifts off the visitors, among whom the germs are found, and the residents remain.
- e.g. After you repot a plant, soil bacteria are visitors on your fingers. Soap and water remove most of them.
- e.g. The bacteria in the pores of your forearm are residents. They were there last month and will be there next month.
- e.g. A dog who lives in your home leaves visitors on your skin every day. Some of them may settle.
### Your skin is three kinds of land: dry, damp and oily · *defined*
**Definition:** Skin is not the same everywhere. Some parts are dry, some are damp, and some are oily. Each kind of skin holds a different community of microbes.

In 2009 Elizabeth Grice, Julia Segre and their team in the United States swabbed 20 places on the skin of 10 healthy people. They read the genes of the bacteria in each swab. They found that the places fell into three groups. Oily places, such as the forehead and the sides of the nose, were home mostly to bacteria who eat skin oil. Damp places, such as the armpit, the elbow crease and the toe gaps, held bacteria who need water. Dry places, such as the forearm and the palm, held a mixed crowd, with many kinds but few of each. Why would this be so? A microbe needs water and food. Sweat gives water and salts. Oil glands give sebum. A dry forearm gives little of either, and flakes of dead skin fall off all day and carry bacteria away with them. The team wrote that a hairy, damp underarm and a smooth, dry forearm are as different for microbes as a rainforest and a desert. The same year, Elizabeth Costello and her team compared up to 27 places on several people. The place on the body told them more about the community than the person did.
- e.g. A grown-up's forehead is oily, and the commonest bacteria there live deep in the oil pores.
- e.g. The gaps between your toes are warm and damp inside socks and shoes, and they hold many bacteria and many kinds of fungi.
- e.g. Your forearm is dry. Fewer bacteria live there, but they are of many different kinds.
### Children's skin and grown-ups' skin hold different residents · *defined*
**Definition:** The skin community changes as a person grows. Skin makes much more oil from about the age of 10 to 13, and the oil-eating bacteria become common only then.

The bacterium who is commonest on a grown-up's forehead is called Cutibacterium acnes. They live deep in the hair pores, where there is little oxygen, and they eat sebum. On the skin of healthy children who have not yet reached their teens, they are hard to find at all. The reason is food. A child's oil glands make little sebum. In the early teens the glands begin to make much more, and the oil-eaters multiply. In 2012 Julia Oh and her team compared children and grown-ups aged 2 to 40. The children carried different bacteria from the grown-ups, most clearly inside the nose. Sweat changes too. Sweat on a child's forehead is mostly water and salt. From the teens, glands in the armpit add other substances, which armpit bacteria turn into smelly ones. So a body is a different habitat at different ages. That is one reason why your forehead swab may grow less than a grown-up's would.
- e.g. Sweat has no smell when it is fresh. Bacteria in a grown-up's armpit change substances in the sweat into smelly ones.
- e.g. Foot bacteria make the cheesy smell of shoes worn all day. Relatives of the same bacteria give some strong cheeses their smell.
- e.g. A baby, a ten-year-old and a grandmother in one home each carry a different skin community.
### Why the potato slice shows only some of them · *defined*
**Definition:** A potato slice in the air grows only the microbes who can eat potato, live in oxygen and multiply quickly. Many residents of the body need something else, so they never show up as spots.

Each spot on your slice is a colony. It began as one cell, or a few, who landed there and multiplied until millions made a dot you can see. A cell who cannot use potato or who needs a place without oxygen never makes a dot. The oil-eating bacteria of the forehead are like that. So are most bacteria of the large intestine, for whom oxygen is a poison. This means a slice with few spots does not prove that few microbes lived on that skin. It shows how many of the kind who like potato and air were there. Scientists met the same problem. For about a hundred years they knew only the microbes who grew on their dishes. Today they read the genes in a swab instead, which finds the ones who will not grow. The newer method has limits too. Genes show who is there, but not always who is alive or what they are doing.
- e.g. A swab from an oily forehead may grow few spots on potato, although millions of oil-eaters live in the pores there.
- e.g. In 1999 scientists compared the two methods on plaque from below one man's gums. Growing found 28 kinds. Reading genes found 59.
- e.g. About three in ten of the kinds of microbes known from the human mouth have never been grown in a laboratory.
### The mouth: several habitats within one hand's width · *defined*
**Definition:** The mouth has hard teeth, a rough tongue, smooth cheeks and narrow gaps under the gums. Each surface holds a different community.

More than 500 kinds of microbes are known from the human mouth. They do not mix evenly. The top of the tongue is a rough carpet with deep folds. Bacteria who need little oxygen live at the bottom of the folds. A tooth is the only hard surface in the body that is never shed, so a film can build up on a tooth for days. The cheeks shed their top cells all the time, and each cell carries bacteria away as you swallow. Saliva washes through all the time. A microbe who cannot hold on is swallowed and meets the acid of the stomach. In 2016 Jessica Mark Welch and her team coloured each kind of bacterium in tooth film with a different glowing dye. Under the microscope they saw order. Long thread-shaped bacteria stood up from the tooth like the spines of a hedgehog. Other kinds sat at the tips, in the open, and kinds who avoid oxygen sat deep inside. Those who make a waste substance sat beside those who eat that substance. Even a film too thin to see clearly has its own neighbourhoods.
- e.g. Your tongue feels rough because of many tiny bumps. The folds between them shelter bacteria.
- e.g. The film on a tooth is thickest along the gum, where the tongue and the brush reach least.
- e.g. Bacteria on the cheek are swallowed as the cheek cells they sit on come loose.
### From the stomach to the large intestine: acid, speed and no oxygen · *defined*
**Definition:** The food tube changes along its length. The stomach is very acid. The small intestine moves fast. The large intestine is slow and has no oxygen. So the number of microbes rises from a few thousand in a millilitre in the stomach to about a hundred billion in the large intestine.

Follow a mouthful down. Saliva holds about a billion bacteria in each millilitre, and you swallow them all day. Stomach acid kills most of them. A millilitre of stomach contents holds only a few thousand bacteria. The upper small intestine is also thinly settled, because food rushes through, and bile, a digestive juice from the liver, harms many bacteria. Lower down the flow slows and the numbers climb. In the large intestine, the leftovers stay for a day or more. It is warm, wet, full of plant fibre and has no oxygen. About a hundred billion bacteria live in each millilitre there. So nearly all of your 38 trillion bacteria are in the large intestine. The whole of your skin holds perhaps a hundred billion, which is less than one part in three hundred. Most of the gut residents are anaerobes. They break down fibre that your own body leaves whole, and the cells of the gut wall use what the bacteria leave over. Archaea live there too. One kind uses up the hydrogen gas that the fibre-eaters give off, and makes methane.
- e.g. One bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, lives in the stomach. She burrows into the mucus on the stomach wall and makes a substance that weakens the acid around her.
- e.g. Nearly all of the 38 trillion bacteria in a grown-up are in the large intestine.
- e.g. Much of the gas a person passes is made by gut microbes as they break down fibre.
### Places with few microbes, or none · *defined*
**Definition:** Healthy blood, muscle and brain have no microbes living in them. Some places once thought to be free of microbes, such as the lungs, hold small numbers.

Your skin and the lining of your gut are walls. Microbes live on the outer side of the walls. On the inner side, in the blood and the organs, defence cells remove any microbe who gets through. That is why a bacterium who is harmless on the skin can be dangerous in a deep cut. Some places are not as simple as the old books said. Textbooks used to say that healthy lungs have no bacteria. Reading genes showed that healthy lungs hold small, changing numbers, breathed in from the mouth and cleared again. Urine in the bladder was also called free of microbes. In 2014 a team in the United States used better ways of growing. They found living bacteria in most of the urine samples they tested, from women with and without bladder trouble. Whether an unborn baby meets any microbes in the womb is still argued about. Most scientists now think that a healthy womb has none, and that earlier findings were probably traces picked up during the testing.
- e.g. A splinter pushes skin bacteria through the wall, so the place may turn red and sore.
- e.g. Doctors clean the skin before an injection, so that the needle does not carry skin residents into the body.
- e.g. The inside of a healthy muscle has no microbes at all.
### A newborn is new land: where your microbes came from · *defined*
**Definition:** A baby meets her first microbes at birth. Over about three years the gut community changes in steps, from a few kinds to many. This step-by-step change is called succession.

In 2010 Maria Gloria Dominguez-Bello and her team swabbed mothers and their babies within minutes of birth. They found something striking. The mothers had different communities on the skin, in the mouth and in the birth passage. Each newborn carried nearly the same community all over the body. So the lands of the body sort the microbes afterwards. Babies born through the birth passage first carried bacteria from there. Babies born by an operation first carried bacteria like those of skin. Later studies found that these differences become smaller as the child grows, and babies born either way grow up healthy. Then comes milk. Mother's milk holds special sugars that a baby cannot digest at all. They are the third largest solid part of the milk, after milk sugar and fat. Why would a mother make food her baby cannot use? Because the sugars are food for certain bacteria, called Bifidobacterium, in the baby's gut. The first solid food brings fibre, and fibre-eating bacteria move in. A study of 903 children found three steps. The community changes fast until about 14 months. It settles slowly until about 30 months. After that it stays steady.
- e.g. In many Indian families a baby's first rice is a ceremony: choroonu in Kerala, annaprashan in Bengal. That spoonful is also the first rice for the baby's gut bacteria.
- e.g. A baby who puts a toy, a toe and the floor into her mouth is meeting visitors from the home.
- e.g. By about three years old, a child's gut community looks much like a grown-up's.
### Holding the space: how residents keep newcomers out · *defined*
**Definition:** A harmful microbe who arrives on a body must find room and food. Residents already use both, and some make substances that stop newcomers. This is called colonisation resistance, or holding the space.

Think of a full bus. A new passenger finds no seat. Residents hold the space in three ways. First, they cover the surface, so a newcomer finds nowhere to hold on. Second, they eat the food, so little is left. Third, many of them make substances that harm other microbes. In 2016 a team in Germany studied a bacterium who lives in some people's noses, called Staphylococcus lugdunensis. She makes a substance that kills Staphylococcus aureus, a bacterium who can cause serious wound infections. In the team's hospital samples, people who carried the first bacterium rarely carried the second. In 2017 Teruaki Nakatsuji and his team found skin residents who make substances that kill the same harmful bacterium. People with the skin illness eczema had few of these residents. The gut shows the idea most clearly. A long course of antibiotics can kill so many gut residents that one tough bacterium, called Clostridioides difficile, multiplies and causes severe diarrhoea. In 2013 doctors in the Netherlands treated such patients by putting gut bacteria from a healthy person into their intestine. It cured 13 of 16 patients at the first try. An antibiotic alone cured 4 of 13.
- e.g. In the Hold the Space game, the same dice rolls give a newcomer almost no squares on a full board and many squares on a nearly empty one.
- e.g. Bacteria in the birth passage make acid, and the acid keeps most other microbes from settling.
- e.g. After a course of antibiotics some people get a sore white coating in the mouth. A yeast who was already there has multiplied, because the bacteria around the yeast were killed.
### Most microbes are harmless: counting the kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** Of all the kinds of microbes on Earth, only a small number can make a human ill. Most kinds never live on us at all, and most of those who do cause no harm.

In 2001 three scientists went through the medical books and listed every kind of living being and virus known to make humans ill. They counted 1,415 kinds. Of these, 538 were bacteria, 307 were fungi, 217 were viruses and prions, 66 were single-celled beings called protozoa, and 287 were worms. Now compare. More than 500 kinds of microbes live in human mouths alone. About a thousand kinds of bacteria have been found on healthy human skin. Soil and sea hold far more kinds, and most have no name yet. So the germs are a short list beside a very long one. Why are so few harmful? To make someone ill, a microbe must get past the skin or gut wall, find food there, and survive the body's defences. Most microbes are built for soil, water or a leaf, and a warm human body is the wrong place for them. But harmless does not mean helpful. Many microbes on us seem to do nothing for us. They simply live there.
- e.g. The bacteria who set curd, raise idli batter and rot a fallen leaf never make a healthy person ill.
- e.g. A spoon of garden soil holds billions of bacteria, and almost none of them can live in a human.
- e.g. Of the microbes who do live on us, a few are helpers, a few are sometimes harmful, and many are passengers who neither help nor harm.
### Helper, passenger or trouble: the same microbe in a different place · *defined*
**Definition:** Many microbes are not simply good or bad. The same bacterium can be harmless in one part of the body and cause illness in another part. Such a microbe is called an opportunist.

Take the gut bacterium Escherichia coli. In the large intestine most kinds of this bacterium live quietly. If the same bacterium reaches the bladder, she can multiply there and cause a painful infection. She is the commonest cause of bladder infections. Or take Staphylococcus aureus. About one person in four carries this bacterium in the nose and never knows. In a deep wound or in the blood, the same bacterium is dangerous. Three things decide what happens. The first is the place. The second is the number, as when a film on a tooth grows thick. The third is the state of the body, as when someone is very ill or very old. This changes how we should think about germs. A few microbes, such as the cholera bacterium, harm us wherever they grow in us. For many others, 'germ' describes a microbe in the wrong place or at the wrong time. This is also why the walls of the body matter so much. Clean hands before eating, and a washed and covered cut, keep microbes in the places where they do no harm.
- e.g. Gut bacteria that leak into the belly through a burst appendix cause a dangerous infection, so doctors operate quickly.
- e.g. A skin bacterium who is harmless on the arm can cause illness when she grows on a plastic tube put into a hospital patient's vein.
- e.g. The bacteria who make acid on teeth do little harm in a thin film. In a thick film fed with sweets all day, they cause holes.
### What an antibiotic does to the residents · *defined*
**Definition:** An antibiotic kills bacteria. A medicine swallowed for an infection in the ear or chest also reaches the gut, and kills many residents there. Most of them grow back within weeks, and a few may not.

In 2008 Les Dethlefsen and his team studied three healthy grown-ups who took an antibiotic for five days. They read the genes of the gut bacteria before, during and after. About a third of the kinds of bacteria became rarer or disappeared from the samples. Four weeks after the medicine ended, each person's community looked close to how it had started. But a few kinds had still not come back after six months. Does this mean antibiotics are bad? No. Antibiotics have saved many millions of lives, and an infection that needs one must be treated. It means an antibiotic is a strong tool with a cost, to be used when a doctor says it is needed. It does nothing against colds and flu, which are caused by viruses. Taking one for a cold harms the residents and does not touch the virus. How best to help the residents grow back is not settled. In one study in 2018, people who took a probiotic after antibiotics got their own community back more slowly than people who took nothing.
- e.g. Loose stools during a course of antibiotics are common, because many gut residents have been killed.
- e.g. A doctor who says a cold needs no antibiotic is protecting the child's residents as well as the medicine's power.
- e.g. An antibiotic cream on a small skin infection reaches far fewer residents than a tablet that travels through the whole gut.
### People who live together share microbes, and so do their dogs · *defined*
**Definition:** Microbes pass between bodies all day by touch, by shared things and through the air. People in one home carry more of the same skin microbes than strangers do.

In 2013 Se Jin Song and her team sampled the skin, mouths and guts of 60 families, some with children and some with dogs. People in one home shared more microbes with each other than with people from other homes. The sharing was strongest on the skin. Grown-ups who had a dog shared more skin microbes with each other. They also shared more with their own dog than with other dogs. In 2014 a team in the Netherlands found that partners have more similar tongue communities than strangers do. They also measured that one ten-second kiss passes about 80 million bacteria. In 2015 James Meadow and his team sat people one at a time in a cleaned chamber and sampled the air. A person gives off more than a million tiny particles every hour, many of them carrying bacteria. The team could tell an occupied chamber from an empty one within four hours. They could even tell some people apart by the cloud alone. Do the shared microbes settle, or are they only passing? The scientists said plainly that they do not know yet.
- e.g. Your skin community is more like your sister's than like that of a child in another city.
- e.g. A family dog who sleeps on the sofa carries skin microbes from one family member to the next.
- e.g. When you leave a room, some of your bacteria stay behind on the chair and in the dust.
### Other animals and their partners · *defined*
**Definition:** Every animal who has been studied carries microbes, and many animals depend on particular ones. Some young animals take their microbes from their elders on purpose.

No animal makes the tools to break down the tough walls of plant cells. Plant-eaters depend on gut microbes who do. A cow keeps hers in a large first stomach chamber, the rumen. A termite keeps hers in the hind gut, and they digest the wood she eats. An elephant calf is born without the microbes who break down plants. Elephant calves eat the fresh dung of their mothers and other elders. People who raise orphan calves say this helps a healthy community settle in the gut. They give the calves dung from healthy grown-up elephants. One of the best-studied partnerships is in the sea off Hawaii. The bobtail squid is about 3 centimetres long. She has a pouch on her underside filled with bacteria who make light. At night the glow from below hides her shadow from fish hunting beneath her. Margaret McFall-Ngai and Edward Ruby have studied her for more than thirty years. A newly hatched squid has no bacteria. Within about half a day she has gathered the right kind from seawater, where that kind is rare among millions of others. Every dawn she pushes most of them out, and the rest multiply through the day.
- e.g. A cow chewing cud is breaking grass into small pieces for the microbes in her rumen.
- e.g. A termite can live on wood only because of the microbes in her gut.
- e.g. A bobtail squid without her light-making bacteria casts a shadow on a moonlit night.
### Bacteria sense their neighbours and act together · *defined*
**Definition:** Bacteria sense what is around them and change what they do. Many kinds also sense how many of their own kind are near, and act as a group once there are enough. This is called quorum sensing.

Bacteria sense and decide. A bacterium has sensors in her outer skin for sugar, acid, oxygen and many other things, and she swims towards what she needs. Bacteria also keep track of each other. In 1970 Kenneth Nealson, Terry Platt and Woodland Hastings were studying light-making sea bacteria. A few bacteria in a flask stayed dark. Once the flask was crowded, all of them lit up together. Each bacterium gives out a little of a signal substance. When many are near, the signal is strong, and each one switches on the genes for making light. One bacterium alone would waste her food by glowing. A crowd in a squid's pouch makes a useful lamp. Bonnie Bassler later showed that many kinds of bacteria talk this way. Some use one signal for their own kind and another that is shared between kinds. The bacteria on your teeth and in your gut use such signals too. Some scientists prefer other words. They say that a bacterium responds to substances by fixed chemistry, and that words such as 'decide' and 'talk' belong to animals with brains. Both sides agree on what the bacteria do.
- e.g. The light-making bacteria in a bobtail squid glow only when they are crowded together in her pouch.
- e.g. Bacteria in the film on a tooth change their behaviour once the film is thick.
- e.g. A bacterium in the gut swims towards a sugar and away from an acid.
### Animals have always lived among bacteria · *defined*
**Definition:** Bacteria and archaea lived on Earth for more than two billion years before the first animals. So no animal has ever lived in a world without microbes.

The oldest signs of life on Earth are about three and a half billion years old, and they are of microbes. The first animals appeared less than one billion years ago, in seas already full of bacteria. By then bacteria had changed the whole planet. The oxygen in the air was first made by bacteria who used sunlight, long before there were plants. Every animal body since then has grown up with bacteria on every surface. In 2013 Margaret McFall-Ngai and many other biologists wrote that this should change how animals are studied. An animal's gut, skin and defences took shape among bacteria. The closest case is inside your own cells. Each cell holds small parts called mitochondria, which release energy from food. They have their own DNA, and it is like the DNA of bacteria. Lynn Margulis argued in 1967 that mitochondria are the descendants of bacteria who came to live inside larger cells about two billion years ago. Her paper was turned down by about fifteen journals before one printed it. The evidence from DNA later showed she was right. So the story of bodies and bacteria living together goes back to the beginning of bodies.
- e.g. Every cell in your body holds mitochondria, whose ancestors were free-living bacteria.
- e.g. A cow, a termite and an elephant each depend on gut microbes to break down plants.
- e.g. A human baby, an elephant calf and a squid hatchling each gather microbes in the first hours and days of life.
### Is the stomach bacterium an enemy or an old companion? · *debated*
**Definition:** Helicobacter pylori is a bacterium who lives in the stomach of at least half of all people. She causes ulcers and stomach cancer in some of them. Scientists disagree about whether she also does some good.

Until the 1980s doctors thought no bacterium could live in stomach acid. They blamed stomach ulcers, which are open sores in the stomach wall, on worry and spicy food. In 1982 Robin Warren and Barry Marshall in Australia grew a spiral bacterium from the stomachs of ulcer patients. Few doctors believed them. In 1984 Marshall swallowed a broth of the bacteria. Within days he was vomiting, and a camera showed his stomach lining was inflamed. The two men received the Nobel Prize in 2005. Today ulcers are cured with antibiotics. Yet most carriers never fall ill. A study of the bacterium's genes in 2007 showed that she has travelled with humans since before people left Africa, about 58,000 years ago. She is now disappearing from many countries.
- e.g. A person with an ulcer who carries the bacterium is treated with antibiotics and is usually cured.
- e.g. Most people who carry the bacterium never know, because they never fall ill.
- e.g. In many countries far fewer children carry the bacterium today than their grandparents did as children.
- **Barry Marshall, Robin Warren and most stomach doctors:** The bacterium causes most stomach ulcers and raises the chance of stomach cancer. A person who is found to carry her and has stomach trouble should be treated. Losing her is a gain for health.
- **Martin Blaser, an American doctor and microbiologist:** People carried this bacterium for tens of thousands of years, and she changes how the stomach makes acid. In a study of 7,663 American adults in 2007, carriers of one type of the bacterium were less likely to have had asthma. Her disappearance may have costs that we are only beginning to see.
- **Scientists who are cautious about both claims:** The asthma finding is a link between two things, which is not proof that one causes the other. Children who carry the bacterium may differ in other ways, such as growing up in larger families. More careful studies are needed before anyone is left untreated.

## Mostly agreed
- Every healthy human carries trillions of microbes, mostly bacteria, and most of them are in the large intestine. *How we know:* In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo worked out the numbers organ by organ. They found about 38 trillion bacteria in a grown-up, nearly all in the large intestine, weighing about 200 grams in total.
- Different parts of one body hold different communities, and the place matters more than the person. *How we know:* Grice and Segre's team swabbed 20 skin places on 10 people in 2009. Costello's team compared up to 27 body places on several people over months in the same year. Both read the genes of the bacteria.
- Growing microbes on a dish misses most kinds. Reading genes finds many more. *How we know:* In 1999 Kroes, Lepp and Relman found 28 kinds by growing and 59 by reading genes in one sample of tooth film. In 2005 Eckburg's team found that most of the bacteria in the gut belonged to kinds never grown before.
- Residents make it harder for harmful microbes to settle. *How we know:* After antibiotics thin the gut residents, Clostridioides difficile can take over. In a trial in the Netherlands in 2013, putting back a healthy person's gut bacteria cured 13 of 16 patients at the first try.
- Only a small share of all kinds of microbes can make humans ill. *How we know:* A count in 2001 listed 1,415 kinds of living beings and viruses known to make humans ill, of which 538 were bacteria. More than 500 kinds of microbes live in human mouths alone.
- A child's gut community is built in steps over about the first three years. *How we know:* The TEDDY study followed 903 children from 3 to 46 months of age and read the genes in more than 12,000 stool samples. Whether the child was getting mother's milk was the strongest influence found.
- People who live together share skin microbes, and dogs add to the sharing. *How we know:* Song's team sampled 60 families in 2013. Household members shared more microbes than people from different homes, most of all on the skin.

## How we found out
**James Staley and Allan Konopka. Carl Woese. Ian Kroes, Paul Lepp and David Relman at Stanford University. Paul Eckburg, Elisabeth Bik and their team. The Human Microbiome Project., The puzzle was named in 1985. The tooth film study was in 1999, the gut study in 2005, and the Human Microbiome Project's main report in 2012..** For about a hundred years, scientists listed the microbes of the human body in one way. They wiped a swab on a dish of food jelly and studied whatever grew. By the 1980s there was a puzzle. Under a microscope a sample showed far more cells than ever grew on dishes. In 1985 James Staley and Allan Konopka named this the great plate count anomaly. Meanwhile, in the 1970s, Carl Woese had shown that every living cell carries one gene that works like a name tag. Scientists began to read that gene straight from a sample, without growing anything. In 1999 Ian Kroes, Paul Lepp and David Relman tried both methods on the film from below one man's gums. Growing found 28 kinds of bacteria. Reading genes found 59, and only about a quarter of those had also grown. In 2005 Paul Eckburg, Elisabeth Bik and their team did the same for the gut. Most of the bacteria they found belonged to kinds that had never been grown. In 2012 the Human Microbiome Project published gene readings from many places on the bodies of healthy volunteers.  
*What people believed before:* That the microbes who grow on a laboratory dish are the full list of who lives on us. Reading genes showed that dishes miss most kinds, because many microbes need food or air that a dish does not give.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A person and their microbes are one living unit, and should be studied as one.** (Biologists who use the word holobiont for a host together with all the microbes living on that host; *debated*)  
  For: No animal lives without microbes. Microbes shape how the gut and the defences grow, and some pass from mother to child.  
  Against: Nancy Moran and Daniel Sloan pointed out in 2015 that most residents are picked up from the surroundings and live for themselves. Microbes in one body also compete with each other.
- **Healthy lungs, bladders and other inner organs are free of microbes.** (Medical textbooks for most of the 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: Standard ways of growing bacteria found nothing in samples from these organs in healthy people.  
  Against: Reading genes found small communities in healthy lungs. In 2014 better ways of growing found living bacteria in most urine samples tested, including samples from healthy women.
- **The bacterium Helicobacter pylori may protect some children from asthma.** (Martin Blaser and Yu Chen, in a study published in 2007; *debated*)  
  For: Among 7,663 adults, those carrying one type of the bacterium were less likely to have had asthma, most of all asthma that began in childhood.  
  Against: The study found a link, which is not proof of a cause. The bacterium also causes ulcers and stomach cancer, so doctors do not leave her untreated in people who are ill.
- **Antibacterial soap protects a healthy family better than plain soap.** (Many advertisements for handwash; *rejected*)  
  For: The added chemicals do kill bacteria in laboratory tests.  
  Against: In 2016 the United States Food and Drug Administration removed 19 such chemicals from home handwashes. Makers had not shown that the products were safe for daily use over many years, or that they worked better than plain soap and water.
- **Taking a probiotic after antibiotics helps the gut residents return.** (Many doctors and chemists, who suggest probiotic capsules or drinks with antibiotics; *debated*)  
  For: Probiotics lower the chance of loose stools during antibiotics in some studies.  
  Against: In a study at the Weizmann Institute in 2018, people who took a probiotic after antibiotics regained their own gut community more slowly than people who took nothing.
- **City life is making our gut communities poorer in kinds.** (Justin and Erica Sonnenburg and other scientists who compare people living in different ways; *debated*)  
  For: The Hadza people of Tanzania, who hunt and gather their food, carry gut bacteria that have become rare or are missing in people in cities.  
  Against: Nobody has shown that carrying fewer kinds causes illness. People differ in food, medicines, water and much else at the same time.

## Roots
We have swabbed our own skin and learned who lives on and in a body. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves, about life and about the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Three Lands Test, swabs from your forearm, your forehead and between your toes grew different numbers and kinds of spots. All three places are your own skin. Scientists who read microbe genes find that the place on the body decides the community more than the person does. A newborn carries nearly the same microbes all over, and the places then sort them. So to a microbe your body is many lands, each with different weather.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'my body' as if a body were one thing and one place. For the beings who live on a body, a dry forearm and a damp underarm are as unlike as a desert and a rainforest. What we count as one is a whole country to them.  
*Evidence:* Grice and Segre's team swabbed 20 skin places on 10 people in 2009 and found that dry, damp and oily places each had their own community. Costello's team found in the same year that the body place decided the community more than the person did. Dominguez-Bello's team found in 2010 that newborns carry nearly the same community all over. If your own slices came out differently from what you expected, write down what you got and test again.

- *who am i:* You are someone, and you are also somewhere. Others are born, eat and die in the lands of your skin and gut, and most of them never leave.
- *what is life:* No animal has ever lived alone. Animals appeared in seas that bacteria had already filled for more than two billion years, and every animal body since has carried them.
- *what is this world:* Water, food and air decide who lives in a forest and who lives in a desert. The same three things decide who lives in your toe gap and who lives on your forearm.

All life on Earth descends from the same first cells. Some of their descendants stayed as single cells. Others, much later, became bodies made of trillions of cells. Then the two came together again, because the single cells settled on the bodies. So when bacteria settle on a newborn, two branches of one very old family are meeting. The body gives land, and the bacteria become part of how the body lives.

- Science (debated): Whether a host and all their microbes should be counted as one unit that evolves together is argued about among biologists.

## Story: The Clean Corner
*Madhi, her grandmother Chikki, the calf Thumbi and the old elephant Valli are made up, and so is the camp. How fast the calf gets better is imagined. These facts are real. Forest camps in the Nilgiris of Tamil Nadu raise orphaned elephant calves by hand. Bomman and Bellie, the carers shown in the film The Elephant Whisperers, belong to the Kattunayakan community. An elephant has no tools of her own for breaking down grass and leaves, and depends on the microbes in her gut. Elephant calves eat the dung of their mothers and other elders. A handbook for people who raise orphaned Asian elephants says this helps a healthy community of microbes to settle in the gut. It tells carers to give calves dung from healthy grown-up elephants.*

Madhi was eleven the summer she came to live at the elephant camp by the river. Her grandmother Chikki had worked there for thirty years. She had raised four orphan calves, and people said that no calf in her care had ever died.

The fifth calf had come in March. Men cutting bamboo found her beside a dry stream bed, three months old and alone. Nobody knew what had happened to her herd. Chikki named her Thumbi, the dragonfly, because she was never still.

Madhi was given one job. 'Keep her shed clean,' said Chikki. 'A calf without a mother falls ill easily.'

Madhi was good at clean. Every morning she carried out the old straw and laid down new. She scrubbed the milk bottles with ash and hot water and stood them in the sun. She swept the yard in front of the shed until the earth showed the lines of her broom.

Beside Thumbi's shed stood Valli, an old cow elephant who had worked in the forest all her life. Valli dropped her dung where she stood, in great round balls, many times a day. Madhi swept those up too. She was quick about it. No ball lay on the ground long enough to draw a fly.

For a while all went well. Thumbi drank her milk and chased the camp dogs. Then, in her sixth month, Chikki began to give her green food, as a herd would have done. Tender grass came first, then banana stem, then leaves.

Thumbi ate all of it. And all of it came out again, loose and sour, with the blades of grass still green and whole. Her belly swelled tight as a drum. She stood with her head low. In two weeks the bones of her hips began to show.

Chikki changed the grass. She stopped the banana stem. She sat up at night with her hand on the calf's belly. Nothing helped.

Madhi swept harder. Somewhere, she was sure, she had missed some dirt.

One thing made her cross. Whenever Valli dropped a fresh ball, Thumbi stretched her small trunk through the rails towards it. 'Chi! Dirty!' Madhi would say. She would push the trunk back and whisk the ball away. Thumbi would squeal and reach again. It became a race between the trunk and the broom, and the broom always won.

One morning Madhi overslept. When she ran out, the sun was already on the yard. Valli had dropped a ball beside the rails an hour before, and Thumbi had it. She had broken it open with her foot. She was putting the pieces into her mouth one at a time, with great care, in a way she had never eaten her grass.

Madhi's stomach turned. She ran for her grandmother. 'She is eating Valli's dung! I was late. It is my fault. Now she will be worse.'

Chikki came, wiping her hands on her sari. She looked at the calf for a long time. She did not hurry.

'How long has she been asking for it?' she said.

'Asking?'

'Reaching, like that.'

Madhi thought. 'Since she began on grass. Every day. I never let her.'

Chikki sat down on the step slowly, as if something heavy had been put in her lap. 'Thirty years,' she said, 'and I did not see it. My other four were never kept so clean.'

She picked a blade of grass and held it out. 'Can you eat this?'

'No.'

'Nor can she. Nor can Valli. No elephant can break grass down by herself. In Valli's belly live others, too small to see, more of them than there are leaves in this forest. They break the grass, and Valli lives on what they leave over. A calf is born without them. Milk she can manage alone. But grass?' She tapped Madhi's broom with the blade. 'In the forest a calf gets them from her mother's dung, and her aunts' dung. She eats it in the weeks when she starts on leaves. Nobody teaches her. She knows what she is missing.'

Madhi looked at her yard. It was the cleanest yard in the camp. Every morning she had swept away the one thing the calf needed.

'I thought dung was only dirt,' she said.

'To us it is dirt,' said Chikki. 'To her it is seed for an empty field.'

They let Thumbi finish. The camp's animal doctor came and checked Valli's dung, to be sure the old elephant carried no illness. After that Madhi kept one corner by the rails unswept. Each morning she chose a fresh ball from Valli and put it there herself, as carefully as she chose bananas.

It did not happen in a day. For a week nothing changed. Then the sour smell went. Then the swelling went down. One morning in the third week Madhi found Thumbi's dung lying in the straw in firm round balls, small copies of Valli's. She broke one open with a stick. There was no green blade in it anywhere. There were only fine pale threads, worked down by someone to almost nothing.

That evening Chikki fed Madhi her supper by hand, as she had when Madhi was small. She rolled the rice and rasam into a ball with her fingers. Madhi watched the fingers.

'Ajji,' she said, 'who gave me mine?'

Her grandmother put the ball of rice into her mouth. 'Who do you think?' she said.

**For the parent:** The calf was not eating dirt. She was gathering the microbes that every elephant needs to break down grass. The place Madhi kept cleanest was the place that was missing what the calf needed most.

- Ask: Thumbi's shed was very clean, yet she could not digest grass. What was missing?
- Ask: Where do you think your own gut microbes came from?
- Ask: When is cleaning something a help, and when could it take away something that is needed?

### e1 · The Three Lands Test: which part of your skin grows the most spots? (45 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Your forearm, your forehead and the gap between your toes are all your own skin. Do they carry the same number and the same kinds of microbes who can grow on potato?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which slice will grow the most spots: the forearm slice, the forehead slice or the toe gap slice? Which will grow the fewest? Will the slice that no skin touched stay clear? Write each guess and the reason for it.
- **fair test:** All four slices come from the same boiled potato and are the same thickness. Each gets one new cotton bud, wetted in the same cooled boiled water. Each place on the skin gets the same ten rubs, and each slice gets the same five lines. All four bags lie side by side in the same warm, dark place. Only one thing differs: the place on the skin that the bud touched. The fourth slice gets a wet bud that touched no skin. It shows what grows from the potato, the water and the air alone.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils and cuts the potato. Once the bags are sealed, never open them again, and never sniff them. A spot holds millions of microbes, and growing them makes far more than your skin ever carries. Anyone with asthma should not handle bags with fuzzy mould. At the end a grown-up puts the sealed bags in the dustbin and everyone washes hands with soap. Do not swab a cut, a rash, the inside of the nose or the mouth.
- **record:** Fill in the table each day. On day 4, draw the four slices as four circles, with every spot in its real colour and size. Under the drawing write three sentences. I think: which place carried the most microbes that grow on potato. I saw: the number of spots on each slice. Because: your reason, using what you know about dry, oily and damp skin. If a slice is covered all over, write 'covered' and say what share of the slice is covered.
- **results table:** Slice | What the skin there feels like | Spots on day 2 | Spots on day 3 | Spots on day 4 | Colours and look of the spots

(22 min); day 2: Look through the bags without opening them. Count the spots on each slice and write the numbers down. (5 min); day 3: Count again. Write down the colours, and whether the spots are shiny, dull or fuzzy. (5 min); day 4: Count one last time and draw the four slices. Say what you think, what you saw and why. A grown-up throws the sealed bags away. Wash hands. (13 min)

  - Note: Boiling kills almost every microbe in the potato, so the slices start nearly empty of life. - **s3 (4 min)**

  - Ask: Why do we need a slice that no skin touches? (good: To see what grows without any skin microbes.; So we can compare the others with it.)
  - Note: Every guess is fine if a reason comes with it. Many children guess that the forehead or the toes will win because they seem dirtier. Write the reason down in their words.

- **s7 (10 min) · grown-up needed**

  - Ask: The forehead slice may have few spots. Does that prove that few microbes live on a forehead? (good: No. It shows only the ones who can grow on potato in air.; No. Some microbes need oil or a place with no oxygen.)
  - Ask: What would you change to make the test fairer or to learn more? (good: Swab each place twice, on two slices.; Test a grown-up's forehead too.; Swab the same size of skin each time.)
  - Note: The usual result is that the toe gap slice grows the most spots and the forearm slice far fewer. The forehead varies, most of all in children.   - Check: A slice has only two spots. What does that tell you for certain? → **Few microbes who grow on potato in air were on the bud**
### e2 · Hold the Space: can a newcomer settle in a full gut? (35 min)
- **question:** A harmful microbe lands in a gut. Does it matter how many residents are already living there?
- **predict:** Guess first. After eight rounds, how many of the 36 squares will the newcomers hold on the full grid? How many on the nearly empty grid? Write both numbers and the reason for your guess.
- **fair test:** Both kinds of game use the same grid, the same dice and the same three rules, for the same eight rounds. Only one thing differs: how many residents are on the grid at the start. Each kind of game is played three times, because dice are chancy and one game can be a fluke.
- **record:** Write the score of each of the six games in the table. Then work out the average number of newcomers for the three full-gut games and for the three after-antibiotic games. Draw two bars, one for each average. Under the chart write: I think, I saw, because.
- **results table:** Game | Residents at the start | Newcomers after 8 rounds | Residents after 8 rounds | Empty squares left
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rule a grid of 6 rows and 6 columns. 2. Number the rows 1 to 6 down the side and the columns 1 to 6 along the top. 3. Count out the rice grains and the beans.  
  *Parent says:* This grid is a small patch of gut wall. Each square has room for one microbe. Rice grains are residents. Beans are harmful newcomers.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a grid with 6 rows and 6 columns. Number the rows and columns. Rice grains are residents. Beans are newcomers.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the three rules of a round aloud, from the rules card below. 2. Explain the two kinds of game. 3. Ask for the two guesses and the reasons. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* A round has three parts. A newcomer arrives. The newcomers multiply. The residents multiply. How many squares will the newcomers hold after eight rounds, in each kind of game? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the three rules. Guess how many squares the newcomers will win on a full grid. Guess again for a nearly empty grid. Write your reasons.
  - Note: The rules of a round. One: roll two dice. The first gives the row and the second the column. If that square is empty, put a bean there. If someone is there, the newcomer finds no room and is washed away. Two: every bean that was on the grid before this round adds one new bean on an empty square touching its own by a side, if there is one. Three: the residents add two rice grains, each on an empty square that touches a rice grain by a side.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set up the full gut: rice on every square except the six on the slanting line from the top left corner to the bottom right. 2. Play eight rounds. Your child chooses where the two new rice grains go each round. 3. Count the beans, the rice grains and the empty squares. Write them in the table. 4. Clear the grid and play twice more.  
  *Parent says:* This gut is full. Thirty residents are at home and six squares are empty. Play eight rounds and count who holds what.  
  *Child mode:* Put rice on all squares except the six slanting ones. Play eight rounds. Count the beans at the end. Play three games.
  - Ask: Why did most newcomers find no room? (good: Almost every square already had a resident.; The residents filled the empty squares quickly.)
  - Note: On the full grid a newcomer lands on an empty square only about one time in six at first. The residents fill the gaps within three rounds. Most games end with no beans or one bean.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set up the gut after a long course of antibiotics: rice only on the six slanting squares. 2. Play eight rounds with the same rules. Your child again chooses where the residents multiply. 3. If the grid fills up before round eight, stop and count. 4. Write the numbers in the table. Play twice more.  
  *Parent says:* Now an antibiotic has killed most of the residents. Only six are left. The rules are the same. Can you keep the newcomers out this time?  
  *Child mode:* Put rice only on the six slanting squares. Play eight rounds with the same rules. Count the beans. Play three games.
  - Ask: You chose where the residents multiplied. Why could you not block the newcomers this time? (good: There were too many empty squares.; The beans multiplied faster than I could block them.)
  - Note: On the nearly empty grid the first bean usually lands in round one or two, and the beans then spread fast. Most games end with 9 to 20 beans. Let your child try hard to block them, because finding that it cannot be done is the point.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child works out the average number of beans for each kind of game. 2. They draw two bars. 3. Ask for the claim, the evidence and the reason. 4. Then ask what the model leaves out.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? Which numbers show it? Why does a full grid protect itself? And what does a real gut have that our paper does not?  
  *Child mode:* Work out the average for each kind of game. Draw two bars. Say what you think, what you saw and why.
  - Ask: What does a real gut have that this model leaves out? (good: Residents also make substances that harm newcomers.; The body's defence cells.; Food, which residents use up.; Many different kinds of residents.)
  - Note: A model keeps one idea and drops the rest. This one keeps only room. Real residents also use up the food and make substances that stop newcomers, and the gut wall has its own defences. A real course of antibiotics also leaves far more than six residents in every 36.
  - Check: In this model, why do newcomers do badly on the full grid? → **Almost no square is empty**

### c1 · Test the Claim: design a fair test for a germ-killing handwash (35 min)

## Worksheets
### Who lives where on a body (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a resident microbe?  
   Options: One who lives and multiplies on a body / One who always causes illness / One who washes off with soap / One found only in soil  
   **Answer:** One who lives and multiplies on a body. *Why:* A resident lives and multiplies on or in a body for months or years. Most residents cause no illness, and soap does not remove those deep in the pores.
2. (fill) A microbe who lands on your skin and is gone within hours is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['visitor']. *Why:* A visitor comes from soil, food, a handle or another person, and does not stay. Hand washing is aimed at visitors.
3. (sort) Sort these places by the kind of skin they have.  
   **Answer:** forearm → Dry; palm → Dry; forehead → Oily; side of the nose → Oily; armpit → Damp; toe gap → Damp. *Why:* The forearm and palm have little water or oil. The forehead and nose have many oil glands. The armpit and toe gaps stay damp with sweat.
4. (truefalse) Your forearm and your toe gap carry the same community of microbes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The forearm is dry and the toe gap is damp, and each suits different microbes. The place on the body decides the community more than the person does.
5. (match) Match each land of the body to what it gives a microbe.  
   **Answer:** Forehead → Skin oil; Toe gap → Water from sweat; Stomach → Strong acid; Large intestine → Fibre and no oxygen; Forearm → Little water or oil. *Why:* Each land offers different food, water and air. That is why each holds a different community.
6. (mcq) Where do most of a body's bacteria live?  
   Options: On the skin / In the mouth / In the large intestine / In the blood  
   **Answer:** In the large intestine. *Why:* Nearly all of a grown-up's 38 trillion bacteria are in the large intestine, which is warm, wet, slow and full of fibre. Healthy blood has none. Choosing the skin is common, because skin is the part we see.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the fewest bacteria to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['A millilitre of healthy blood', 'A millilitre of stomach contents', 'A millilitre of saliva', 'A millilitre of large intestine contents']. *Why:* Healthy blood has none. Stomach contents hold a few thousand, because of the acid. Saliva holds about a billion. Large intestine contents hold about a hundred billion.
8. (truefalse) A potato slice with no spots proves that the swabbed skin had no microbes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A spot grows only from a microbe who can eat potato and live in air. Many skin residents need oil, or a place without oxygen, and never show up.
9. (mcq) In the Three Lands Test, why is one slice wiped with a bud that touched no skin?  
   Options: To use up the spare slice / To show what grows without skin microbes / To keep the other slices clean / To make the test longer  
   **Answer:** To show what grows without skin microbes. *Why:* That slice shows what comes from the potato, the water and the air alone. The other slices are compared with it. Without it, you could not tell which spots came from skin.
10. (mcq) Washing hands with soap at home mostly removes which microbes?  
   Options: Residents deep in the pores / Visitors sitting on the surface / Bacteria in the gut / All microbes on the body  
   **Answer:** Visitors sitting on the surface. *Why:* Soap lifts off the visitors on the surface, among whom germs are found. The residents in the pores stay and spread back within hours.
11. (fill) The oil that skin makes beside each hair is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['sebum']. *Why:* Sebum keeps skin soft, and it is food for the oil-eating bacteria and yeasts of the face and scalp.
12. (write) Explain why few bacteria live in the stomach but very many live in the large intestine.  
   **Answer:** The stomach is very acid, and the acid kills most bacteria that are swallowed. The large intestine is not acid. It is warm, wet and slow, and it is full of plant fibre for bacteria to eat.. *Why:* A strong answer names what each place is like for a microbe: acid in one, food and time in the other.
13. (write) In the Three Lands Test, why must every cotton bud get the same ten rubs?  
   **Answer:** So that the only thing that differs between the slices is the place on the skin. If one bud got more rubs, more microbes might be picked up there, and the test would not be fair.. *Why:* A strong answer says that only one thing may differ in a fair test, and names that thing.
14. (draw) Draw an outline of a body. Colour the oily, dry and damp skin in three colours, and add a key.  
   **Answer:** Oily: forehead, nose, scalp, upper back. Damp: armpits, elbow creases, backs of the knees, toe gaps. Dry: forearms, palms, shins.. *Why:* A strong drawing has a key and puts at least two places in each colour.
15. (perspective) Are the microbes on your skin part of you?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. The girl is right that some residents protect the skin. The biologist is right that they are separate beings. The doctor is right that the place matters.. *Why:* Each view is true from where it stands. A strong answer says what 'part of you' means before deciding.
### Holding the space, and how microbes arrive (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What does 'holding the space' mean?  
   Options: Residents leave newcomers no room or food / Residents hold on to the skin / The body keeps microbes out of blood / Microbes rest between meals  
   **Answer:** Residents leave newcomers no room or food. *Why:* Residents already cover the surface and eat the food, and some make substances that stop newcomers. So a harmful microbe who arrives finds it hard to settle.
2. (mcq) In Hold the Space, why do newcomers win many squares after the antibiotic?  
   Options: The dice change / Newcomers become stronger / Most squares are empty / Residents stop multiplying  
   **Answer:** Most squares are empty. *Why:* The rules and the dice stay the same. Only the number of residents at the start differs, and empty squares are what a newcomer needs.
3. (truefalse) An antibiotic kills only the harmful bacteria.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An antibiotic cannot tell a germ from a resident. In one study, five days of an antibiotic made about a third of the kinds of gut bacteria rarer.
4. (truefalse) An antibiotic cures a cold.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A cold is caused by a virus, and antibiotics do not work on viruses. Taking one for a cold harms the residents and leaves the virus alone.
5. (order) Put these stages of a baby's gut in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['No microbes before birth', 'The same few kinds all over at birth', 'Milk-sugar eaters become the biggest group', 'Fibre-eaters arrive with solid food', 'A steady community at about three years']. *Why:* The gut is settled in steps. Each new food feeds new kinds, and the community becomes steady at about three years.
6. (match) Match each thing to what scientists found.  
   **Answer:** A dog in the home → More shared skin microbes; A ten-second kiss → About 80 million bacteria passed; One hour sitting in a room → Over a million particles given off; Mother's milk → Sugars that feed a baby's bacteria. *Why:* Microbes pass between bodies by touch, through the air and with food. Each of these was measured in a real study.
7. (odd) Three of these are ways residents keep newcomers out. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Using up the food / Covering the surface / Making harmful substances / Making the skin thicker  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Residents use the food, cover the surface and make substances that stop newcomers. Microbes do not make skin thicker.
8. (count) In one game of Hold the Space, newcomers hold 18 of the 36 squares. What percent is that?  
   **Answer:** 50. *Why:* 18 is half of 36, and one half is 50 percent.
9. (fill) The step-by-step change of a community in a new place is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['succession']. *Why:* Succession happens on bare rock, in a new pond and in a newborn's gut. The first settlers are joined and replaced by others.
10. (sort) Sort these microbes into visitors and residents.  
   **Answer:** Soil bacteria on fingers after repotting → Visitor; Oil-eaters deep in forehead pores → Resident; Mouth bacteria just swallowed into the stomach → Visitor; Fibre-eaters in the large intestine → Resident; Bacteria picked up from a door handle → Visitor; Thread-shaped bacteria in tooth film → Resident. *Why:* A resident is in a place that suits her and stays there. A visitor is built for somewhere else, or is in a place that will soon kill or remove her.
11. (write) A child takes an antibiotic for five days. Explain what happens to the gut residents during the medicine and after it.  
   **Answer:** During the medicine, many kinds of gut bacteria are killed or become rare. After it ends, most of them multiply again, and the community is close to how it was within about four weeks. A few kinds may take much longer to return.. *Why:* A strong answer has both parts: the loss during the medicine and the return afterwards.
12. (write) Hold the Space is a model. Name two things a real gut has that the model leaves out.  
   **Answer:** Any two of these. Real residents use up the food. They make substances that stop newcomers. The body has defence cells. A real gut holds hundreds of kinds of residents, not one.. *Why:* A strong answer shows that a model keeps one idea and drops others. This model keeps only room.
13. (label) Label these four stages of a baby's gut with what the microbes are doing.  
   **Answer:** ['The day of birth: the first few kinds arrive from the mother and other people', 'Milk only: milk-sugar eaters are often the biggest group', 'First solid food: fibre-eaters and many new kinds settle', "Age three: the community is steady, like a grown-up's"]. *Why:* Each stage follows from a change in food and in what the child touches.
14. (perspective) A healthy child has a cold. Should she take an antibiotic 'to be safe'?  
   **Answer:** The doctor is right about the science: antibiotics do not work on viruses, and they harm residents. The grandmother's plan is sensible care. The uncle's wish to protect the child is kind, but the medicine would not do what he hopes.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the wish to help from whether the medicine can help.
15. (mcq) Why does mother's milk hold sugars that a baby cannot digest?  
   Options: By mistake / They feed the baby's gut bacteria / They make the milk sweeter / They kill all microbes  
   **Answer:** They feed the baby's gut bacteria. *Why:* The sugars pass through the baby's stomach untouched and feed Bifidobacterium in the gut. Choosing 'by mistake' misses that these sugars are the third largest solid part of the milk.
### Germ or neighbour? Evidence and open questions (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A bacterium is harmless in the gut and harmful in the bladder. What is different?  
   Options: The kind of bacterium / The place she is in / Her age / Her size  
   **Answer:** The place she is in. *Why:* She is the same kind of bacterium in both. In the bladder she is in a place where she can multiply and cause illness.
2. (mcq) What is an opportunist?  
   Options: A microbe who is always harmful / A microbe who never harms anyone / A microbe harmless in one place, harmful in another / A microbe who lives only in soil  
   **Answer:** A microbe harmless in one place, harmful in another. *Why:* An opportunist causes illness only when she reaches the wrong place or when the body's defences are low. Many residents are opportunists.
3. (mcq) Growing found 28 kinds of bacteria in tooth film. Reading genes found 59. What does this show?  
   Options: Reading genes makes mistakes / Growing misses many kinds / Tooth film has few bacteria / The two methods agree  
   **Answer:** Growing misses many kinds. *Why:* Many bacteria will not grow on a laboratory dish, because they need food or air that the dish does not give. Reading genes finds them anyway.
4. (mcq) Carriers of a stomach bacterium have asthma less often. What can we say for certain?  
   Options: The bacterium prevents asthma / The two are linked, cause unknown / Asthma kills the bacterium / The study must be wrong  
   **Answer:** The two are linked, cause unknown. *Why:* A link between two things does not show that one causes the other. Carriers may differ from other people in other ways as well. Choosing 'prevents asthma' jumps from a link to a cause.
5. (truefalse) Most kinds of microbes on Earth can make a person ill.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A count in 2001 listed 538 kinds of bacteria that make people ill. Human mouths alone hold more than 500 kinds, and soil and sea hold far more.
6. (truefalse) Healthy blood has many resident bacteria.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Healthy blood, muscle and brain have no microbes living in them. Defence cells remove any who get through the walls of skin and gut.
7. (match) Match each change to what follows from it.  
   **Answer:** Long antibiotics kill gut residents → A tough bacterium takes over; A thick tooth film gets sweets all day → Acid makes holes; A splinter pushes skin bacteria in → A red, sore spot; Gut bacteria reach the bladder → A painful infection; Feet stay damp in closed shoes → Fungi multiply and skin itches. *Why:* In each case the microbes were already there. 8. (count) A count listed 1,415 kinds of germs. Of these, 538 were bacteria. How many were not bacteria?  
   **Answer:** 877. *Why:* 1,415 take away 538 is 877. The rest were fungi, viruses, single-celled beings called protozoa, and worms.
9. (fill) Bacteria sense how many of their own kind are near. This is called ____ sensing.  
   **Answer:** ['quorum']. *Why:* Each bacterium gives out a signal substance. When the signal is strong, all of them change what they do, such as making light.

   **Answer:** Body places hold different communities → Agreed; Residents make it harder for germs to settle → Agreed; Most kinds of microbes are not germs → Agreed; The stomach bacterium protects against asthma → Still argued; Probiotics help after antibiotics → Still argued; A host and their microbes are one being → Still argued. *Why:* The first three have been measured many times. The last three have evidence on both sides, or depend on what the words mean.

   **Answer:** Any three. About 38 trillion bacteria live in a healthy grown-up. Human mouths hold more than 500 kinds. Only 538 kinds of bacteria were on a list of those that make people ill. Gut bacteria break down fibre for us. Skin residents make substances that kill harmful bacteria.. *Why:* A strong answer uses numbers or named examples, and does not only say that bacteria are good.
12. (write) Fresh sweat has no smell. Hours later a sock smells. Explain what happened in between.  
   **Answer:** Bacteria living on the foot fed on substances in the sweat and multiplied in the warm, damp sock. They gave off smelly substances. The smell comes from what the bacteria make.. *Why:* A strong answer names the bacteria, the damp place and the time they needed.

   Options: They are made by the baby's body / From the mother and people who hold the baby / From the baby's first solid food / From the air only  
   **Answer:** From the mother and people who hold the baby. *Why:* A baby meets her first microbes at birth, from her mother and from everyone and everything that touches her. A body does not make microbes: every microbe comes from another microbe.
11. (mcq) Which change brings many new kinds of microbes into a baby's gut?  
   Options: Learning to walk / Starting solid food / Growing teeth / Sleeping longer  
   **Answer:** Starting solid food. *Why:* Solid food brings fibre and other new foods for microbes, and new kinds settle. Milk alone feeds only a few kinds.
12. (mcq) People who live in one home share microbes most on which part of the body?  
   Options: The gut / The mouth / The skin / The blood  
   **Answer:** The skin. *Why:* Skin touches people, pets and furniture directly all day. A study of 60 families found the strongest sharing on the skin.
13. (mcq) Which of these is still argued about by scientists?  
   Options: Whether skin has microbes / Whether probiotics help after antibiotics / Whether the stomach is acid / Whether bacteria multiply  
   **Answer:** Whether probiotics help after antibiotics. *Why:* One study found that a probiotic slowed the return of a person's own gut community after antibiotics. Other studies find some benefits. The other three are settled.

## Applications
- Doctors treat severe gut infections that follow antibiotics by putting back gut bacteria from a healthy person.
- Hospitals ask staff to clean their hands between patients, to remove visitors picked up from one person before touching the next.
- Doctors test which germ is causing an illness, so that they can choose a medicine that harms fewer residents.
- Scientists look for new antibiotics among the substances that residents of the nose and skin make against their rivals.
- Skin doctors are testing creams of protective skin bacteria for people with eczema.
- People who raise orphaned elephant calves give them dung from healthy grown-up elephants, so that the calves gain gut microbes.
- Dentists advise brushing to keep the film on teeth thin, and they do not try to remove every bacterium from the mouth.
- Police scientists are studying whether the microbes a person leaves on objects could show who touched them.

## Open questions
- **Do the microbes we pick up from family, pets and furniture settle on us, or do they only pass through?** Studies can show that people in one home carry the same kinds. They cannot yet tell a microbe who has settled from one who landed an hour ago.
- **What do most of our residents do for us, if anything?** A few helpers are well studied. For most of the hundreds of kinds on a body, nobody has tested what happens when they are missing.
- **Why does the stomach bacterium cause ulcers in some carriers and no illness in most?** The bacterium comes in different types, people differ, and food and other microbes may matter. No one can yet tell in advance who will fall ill.
- **What is the best way to help gut residents return after antibiotics?** Time alone brings most of them back. In one study a probiotic slowed the return. Larger and longer studies have not been done.
- **Are people in cities losing kinds of gut microbes, and does it matter for health?** People who hunt and gather carry kinds that are rare in cities. But nobody has shown that carrying fewer kinds causes any illness.
- **What is it like to be a bacterium who senses her neighbours and acts with them?** Scientists can measure the signals and what the bacteria do next. Nobody has a way to find out what, if anything, a bacterium experiences.

## Connected maths
- In one family's Three Lands Test, the toe gap slice grew 36 spots, the forearm slice 9 and the No skin slice 1. How many times as many spots grew on the toe gap slice as on the forearm slice? Then take 1 spot away from each skin slice, for what grew without skin, and work it out again. **4 times. After taking 1 away from each, it is 35 and 8, which is a little more than 4 times.**. 36 divided by 9 is 4. After taking away the No skin spot, 35 divided by 8 is 4 with 3 left over, which is about 4.4. The answer hardly changes, because the No skin slice grew so few. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Hold the Space, three full-gut games ended with 0, 1 and 0 newcomers. Three after-antibiotic games ended with 14, 17 and 11. Find the average for each kind of game. Why is an average of three games better than one game? **The full-gut average is one third of a newcomer, or about 0.3. The after-antibiotic average is 14.**. 0 + 1 + 0 = 1, and 1 divided by 3 is one third. 14 + 17 + 11 = 42, and 42 divided by 3 is 14. Dice are chancy, so one game can come out unusually high or low. An average evens that out. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In an after-antibiotic game of Hold the Space, one newcomer lands in round 1. Each newcomer adds one more every round, if there is room. How many newcomers would there be after rounds 2, 3, 4 and 5? In real games the count is usually lower. Why? **2, 4, 8 and 16. Real games fall behind because some newcomers have no empty square beside them.**. The number doubles each round: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. On the grid, a newcomer in a corner or hemmed in by others has no empty square that touches hers, so she cannot add one. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A grown-up's skin holds about 100 billion bacteria. The whole body holds about 38 trillion. One trillion is 1,000 billion. About how many times as many bacteria are in the whole body as on the skin? **About 380 times as many.**. 38 trillion is 38,000 billion. 38,000 billion divided by 100 billion is 380. So the skin holds less than one part in three hundred of the body's bacteria. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- A millilitre of stomach contents holds about 10,000 bacteria. A millilitre of saliva holds about 1,000,000,000. A millilitre of large intestine contents holds about 100,000,000,000. How many times as many are in the large intestine as in saliva? How many zeros does each number have? **100 times as many. The numbers have 4, 9 and 11 zeros.**. The large intestine number has two more zeros than the saliva number, and each extra zero means ten times as many. Ten times ten is 100. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In Count the Kinds, 538 of the 1,415 kinds of germs were bacteria. Is that more or less than one third? Viruses and prions made up 217 kinds. Is that more or less than one sixth? **538 is a little more than one third. 217 is a little less than one sixth.**. One third of 1,415 is about 472, and 538 is more than that. One sixth of 1,415 is about 236, and 217 is less than that. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
