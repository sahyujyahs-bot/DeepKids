# Particles: where sugar goes when it dissolves
`sci.states.particles.L3`

**Summary.** Stir a spoon of sugar into water, and the grains vanish. Yet the water tastes sweet, and it weighs just as much as the water and sugar did before. The sugar has broken into particles far too small to see, and they have spread all through the water. Everything around us is made of particles like these: sugar, water, air, a steel tumbler and you. In this unit your child weighs dissolved sugar on a home-made balance, races a kalkandu lump against powdered sugar, and asks ants to find sugar that nobody can see.

**Intro.** *Drop a spoon of sugar into a clear glass of water and stir. In a minute the grains are gone, and the water looks just like plain water. Is the sugar still in there? If it is, where is it?* Most people say the sugar has 'melted' or 'gone'. Neither is right. In this unit you find out what really happens. You test it three ways: by tasting, by weighing, and by asking ants. You also find out that sugar, water and everything else are made of tiny pieces called particles, and why some sugar dissolves faster than other sugar.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that everything around me, solid, liquid or gas, is made of particles far too small to see.
1. I can explain what happens when sugar dissolves: water particles pull sugar particles off the grain, one by one, and they spread through the water.
2. I can show with a balance and by tasting that dissolved sugar is still there, and still weighs the same.
3. I can run a fair test to find whether a lump, grains or powder of sugar dissolves fastest, and explain why.
4. I can tell dissolving from melting, and give an example of each.
5. I can tell how people found out that a dissolved thing is still there, and the old idea that it stops existing.

## Terms
- **Particle**: A very tiny piece that a material is made of. Particles are far too small to see, even with a magnifying glass. *e.g.* A single grain of sugar is made of a huge number of sugar particles, packed together.
- **Dissolve**: When a solid breaks up into its particles in a liquid and spreads through it, we say the solid dissolves. You can no longer see it, but it is still there. *e.g.* Sugar dissolves in tea. Salt dissolves in rasam.
- **Solution**: The clear mixture you get when something dissolves in a liquid. *e.g.* Sugar water is a solution. So is salty water.
- **Crystal**: A solid whose particles are stacked in a neat, repeating pattern. Crystals often have flat sides and sharp corners. *e.g.* Each grain of sugar is a small crystal. Kalkandu, or rock sugar, is made of big sugar crystals.
- **Melt**: When a solid gets hot enough to turn into a liquid on its own. Melting needs heat, not water. *e.g.* Ghee melts in a warm pan. Ice melts on a hot day.
- **Molecule**: The scientists' name for the smallest particle of a substance like sugar or water. A molecule is made of even smaller atoms joined together. *e.g.* One sugar molecule is the smallest piece of sugar there can be.
- **Balance**: A tool that compares how heavy two things are. The heavier side goes down. *e.g.* A shopkeeper's pan balance weighs vegetables against iron weights.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To see which sugar dissolves fastest, use the same water, the same glasses and the same stirring for each.
- **Model**: Something we make or draw to help us think about a thing we cannot see or hold. *e.g.* Beans on a plate can be a model of the particles in a sugar grain.

## Explanations
### Everything is made of particles · *defined*
**Definition:** Every material, solid, liquid or gas, is made of particles that are far too small to see. Different materials are made of different particles.

A sugar particle is about a millionth of a millimetre across. Even the best ordinary microscope cannot show one. In a solid, the particles are packed close and hold their places, so a solid keeps its shape. In a liquid, they are close but slide past each other, so a liquid flows. In a gas, like air, they are far apart and fly about. Particles never stop moving, even in a stone.
- e.g. A grain of sugar holds roughly a million million million sugar particles. That is a 1 with 18 zeros.
- e.g. Air is made of particles too. You feel them push when the wind blows.
- e.g. Your body is made of particles: water, sugars, fats and many more.
### How sugar dissolves · *defined*
**Definition:** When sugar dissolves, water particles attract the sugar particles on the outside of a grain and pull them away, one by one. The sugar particles then spread out among the water particles.

Water particles bump and tug at a sugar grain from every side. They can only reach the particles on its outside. As the outside particles leave, the next ones are uncovered, so the grain gets smaller and smaller until it is gone. Each sugar particle is now surrounded by water particles. It is far too small to see, but it is still sugar. That is why the water tastes sweet.
- e.g. Sugar in a glass of nimbu pani dissolves in about a minute if you stir it.
- e.g. Salt dissolves in sambar, so you taste it everywhere but cannot see it.
### Dissolved sugar still weighs the same · *defined*
**Definition:** When something dissolves, nothing is lost. The solution weighs exactly as much as the liquid and the solid did before.

Every sugar particle that left the grain is still in the bottle. So the weight cannot change. This is true for every kind of change in a closed bottle, even melting and burning: the total weight stays the same. Scientists call this the conservation of mass. Mass means how much stuff there is.
- e.g. 100 grams of water and 16 grams of sugar make 116 grams of sugar water.
- e.g. An ice cube in a closed box weighs the same after it melts.
### Dissolving is not melting · *defined*
**Definition:** Melting is a solid turning into a liquid because it is heated. Dissolving is a solid breaking up into its particles inside a liquid.

People often say sugar 'melts' in tea. It does not. Sugar dissolves even in cold water, with no heat at all. To melt sugar, you have to heat it in a dry pan to about 186 degrees Celsius, much hotter than boiling water. Then it turns into a thick liquid that becomes caramel. Melting needs only one substance. Dissolving always needs two: one that dissolves and one that it dissolves in.
- e.g. Ice melts into water on a hot day. Nothing else is needed.
- e.g. Sugar dissolves in cold water from the fridge. No heat is needed.
### Why some sugar dissolves faster · *defined*
**Definition:** Sugar dissolves faster when it is crushed small, when it is stirred, and when the water is warm.

Water can only pull particles off the outside of a piece. Crush a lump into powder, and the same sugar has far more outside, so many more particles leave at once. Stirring helps because the sweet water next to the sugar is carried away, and fresh water takes its place. Warm water helps because its particles move faster and tug harder.
- e.g. Powdered sugar vanishes in seconds. A lump of kalkandu can take many minutes.
- e.g. Jaggery is grated or crushed before it goes into panakam, the South Indian jaggery drink.
### Particles move, but spread slowly on their own · *defined*
**Definition:** Particles in a liquid are always moving and bumping. This slowly spreads dissolved particles out, even with no stirring. Scientists call this spreading diffusion.

Diffusion through water is very slow. Sugar left unstirred at the bottom of a tall glass can take many days to spread to the top on its own. So the top stays plain while the bottom is very sweet. Stirring spreads it in seconds. In air, smells spread faster, because air moves about and its particles have more space.
- e.g. Unstirred sugar in tea makes the last sip the sweetest.
- e.g. The smell of jasmine flowers spreads across a room in a few minutes, carried by moving air.
### What is inside a particle? · *idea*
A sugar particle is made of 45 atoms joined together: 12 of carbon, 22 of hydrogen and 11 of oxygen. Atoms are made of even smaller particles. Physicists now describe those smallest particles as ripples in fields that fill all of space. Nobody knows yet whether there is anything deeper still. That is why this card says 'idea'.
- e.g. A water particle is made of 3 atoms: 2 of hydrogen and 1 of oxygen.
- e.g. Sugar and the paper in a book are made of the same three kinds of atom, joined in different ways.

## Mostly agreed
- Everything is made of particles far too small to see. *How we know:* Scientists first worked it out from how gases and dissolved things behave. Since the 1980s, special microscopes have made pictures of single atoms.
- A dissolved substance is still there, as particles spread through the liquid. *How we know:* The solution tastes of it, weighs more by exactly its weight, and gives it back when the liquid dries up.
- In a closed container, the total weight stays the same through dissolving, melting and chemical changes. *How we know:* Chemists have weighed this carefully since the 1700s, and it has always held.
- Crushing, stirring and warming make a solid dissolve faster. *How we know:* Anyone can test it at home with sugar or salt.

## How we found out
**Daniel Sennert, a German doctor, and later Robert Boyle, an English scientist, The early 1600s (Sennert), and later in the 1600s (Boyle).** For about 2,000 years, many teachers in Europe followed the Greek thinker Aristotle. They taught that when two things truly mix, each stops being itself. A new thing takes the place of both. So silver dissolved in a liquid would no longer be silver. In the early 1600s, Daniel Sennert, a German doctor who taught at Wittenberg, tested this. He dissolved silver in aqua fortis, a strong acid that eats into metal. The silver disappeared. Then he added another substance to the liquid, and the silver came out again as a fine powder. When he heated the powder, he had ordinary silver once more. Sennert said the silver had never stopped being silver. It had only broken into particles too small to see, and they had come together again. Later in the 1600s, the English scientist Robert Boyle used tests like this to argue that everything is made of tiny particles. Today we know they were right.  
*What people believed before:* When two things truly mix, each stops being itself, and a new thing takes the place of both. So dissolved silver would no longer be silver.

## Challenging perspectives
- **When sugar dissolves, it is gone.** (A very common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: You cannot see the sugar any more, and the water looks plain.  
  Against: The water tastes sweet, weighs more by exactly the weight of the sugar, and ants come to it. The sugar is still there, as particles too small to see.
- **Sugar melts in water.** (A very common way of speaking; *rejected*)  
  For: The grains seem to turn into liquid and run away, a bit like ice melting.  
  Against: Sugar dissolves even in ice-cold water. Melting sugar needs a dry pan at about 186 degrees Celsius. Dissolving needs water. Melting needs heat.
- **When two things truly mix, each stops being itself, and a new thing takes the place of both.** (Followers of the Greek thinker Aristotle, for about 2,000 years; *rejected*)  
  For: A solution looks like one new, even liquid. You cannot see two things in it.  
  Against: In the early 1600s, Daniel Sennert dissolved silver in a strong acid and got the same silver back. The silver had been there all along, as tiny particles.
- **Particles are not real. They are only a handy way of thinking.** (Some famous scientists around 1900, such as Wilhelm Ostwald and Ernst Mach; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody had ever seen a particle, and some said science should talk only about what can be measured.  
  Against: Experiments around 1908 measured how particles bump into specks in water, and worked out their size. Ostwald changed his mind. Today, microscopes can make pictures of single atoms.
- **Dissolved sugar weighs less, because it has become thin and spread out.** (A common guess before the balance test; *rejected*)  
  For: Spread out, the sugar seems lighter, like a cloud compared with a stone.  
  Against: Every particle is still in the bottle, and each one weighs just what it did. The balance stays level.

## Roots
We have learned that sugar breaks into particles in water and is still all there. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Stirred into water, your sugar vanished, yet the balance stayed level and every sip tasted sweet. The sugar had come apart into separate particles, far too small to see. Each of those particles is still sugar. A heap of sugar is many sugar particles packed together, and water can spread them out.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of sugar as the white grains in the dabba, so when the grains vanish we say the sugar is gone. But grains are only how sugar particles are packed. Spread out in water, it is all the same sugar.  
*Evidence:* In the hanger balance test, bottle A weighed the same after the sugar vanished. Every sip from the stirred bottle tasted sweet. Ants went to the clear sugar water drop and left the plain one. Crushed sugar powder still tasted sweet.

- *who am i:* You are made of particles too: water, sugars, fats and many more, far too small to see.
- *what is life:* Your blood carries a sugar called glucose dissolved in it, to give every cell energy. Ants drink sugar dissolved in nectar and sweet juices.
- *what is this world:* Everything around you, the air, the sea and the stone floor, is made of particles, and they never stop moving.

Before you stirred, the sugar was one heap you could hold in a spoon. In water it became countless particles spread through the whole bottle, and none was lost. Boil cane juice in a sugar mill, and the particles gather into grains again. The heap and the spread-out particles are the same sugar, arranged in two ways.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning he asks for the salt, but Shvetaketu cannot find it. 'Taste the water from the top, the middle and the bottom,' says Uddalaka. Each time it is salty. 'You cannot see the salt, but it is there,' he says. 'In the same way, the finest essence is in everything, and you are that.' (This is the tasting test from your unit, told more than 2,000 years ago. Your test also showed something the story leaves out: unstirred, dissolved sugar stays mostly at the bottom, so the water must be stirred to taste the same everywhere.)
- **Lucretius, On the Nature of Things, Book 1, about lines 265 to 328:** Lucretius, a Roman poet, said nature works through bodies too small to see. Wet clothes hung by the sea get damp, and dry again in the sun, but nobody sees the water come or go. A ring worn on a finger grows thinner over the years. (The camphor tablet and the drying clothes are the same kind of evidence. We see what particles do, not the particles themselves.)
- Science (settled): In a closed container, the total mass stays the same through dissolving, melting and chemical changes.
- Science (open): Whether there is a smallest thing, with nothing smaller inside it, nobody knows.
- Other view, Many chemists: The salt story in the Upanishad is a teaching about the self, not about chemistry. Dissolving shows only that sugar particles spread through water. It does not show that one hidden essence fills everything.
- Practice, Taste the hidden sugar (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. I'll put one drop of sugar water on your tongue. Notice the sweetness. Notice where it spreads. Now breathe slowly five times while it fades. The sugar has not gone. It is in you now, spread out as particles. Open your eyes.
- Ask: When the sugar spread through the water, was it one thing or many things?
- Ask: If something is too small for anyone to see, how can we be sure it is there?
- Ask: The ants found sugar you could not see. What else might be around you that only other beings notice?

## Story: The Sour Glass at Chithi's Stall
*Kavya, her aunt Meenakshi, the stall and the customer are made up. Juice stalls in India often keep a bottle of sugar syrup, and unstirred syrup really does stay at the bottom of a glass.*

Every summer holiday, Kavya helped at her Chithi's juice stall near the bus stand in Madurai. Chithi means aunt, and Kavya's Chithi was called Meenakshi. Her stall sold nimbu pani, sugarcane juice and sweet lime juice. It had a steel counter, a big glass jar of lemons, and a tall bottle of thick, clear sugar syrup.

Kavya was nine. Her job was to squeeze the lemons, pour in the syrup, fill the glass with cold water and hand it over. It was May, and the queue never ended. Bus drivers, students, a grandmother with a basket of jasmine flowers. Everyone was hot and thirsty.

That afternoon a bus conductor came to the counter. He wore a khaki uniform and a leather bag across his chest, and he looked tired. 'One nimbu pani,' he said, 'and hurry, my bus leaves in five minutes.'

Kavya squeezed half a lemon into a glass. She poured in a long stream of syrup. She filled the glass slowly with water from the cold steel can and handed it over with a straw. Then she turned to the next customer.

'Hey!' The conductor held up his glass. 'You forgot the sugar. This is sour as anything.'

'I put it in,' said Kavya. 'I poured the syrup myself.'

The conductor held the glass up to the light. 'Where? I see only water and lemon pips. There is no sugar in here.'

Kavya looked at the glass too. He was right that she could not see any sugar. The water was clear all the way down. But she had poured the syrup. She was sure of it. So where had it gone?

Then she noticed something. Near the bottom of the glass, the water looked a little different. It shimmered, the way air shimmers above a hot road. It was like a clear, wobbly layer, sitting under the rest.

Kavya also noticed the ants. A line of small black ants was marching along the counter to a sticky ring where an earlier glass had stood. They were not going to the wet ring of the water can. They were going only to the ring where syrup had dripped. They could find the sugar even in a ring she could hardly see.

'Wait,' said Kavya. 'Please don't drink from the top. Give me one second.'

She took a clean straw. She put her finger tight over its top end and pushed it straight down to the very bottom of his glass. Then she lifted her finger. A little water rushed up into the straw. She pressed her finger down again, lifted the straw out, and let the drops fall onto a clean steel spoon.

'Taste this,' she said. 'This is from the bottom.'

The conductor tasted it. His eyebrows went up. 'That is sweet. Very sweet.'

'The sugar is all at the bottom,' said Kavya. 'The syrup is heavy, so it sank, and I forgot to stir. The top is just lemon and water.' She took a long spoon and stirred the glass round and round, ten times. 'Now try it.'

He sipped. He smiled for the first time. 'Now that is nimbu pani.' Then he frowned. 'But I still can't see any sugar. Did you add more while I wasn't looking?'

'No,' said Kavya. 'It was in there all the time. You just can't see it.'

He laughed, paid, and ran for his bus, still drinking.

That night, Chithi and Kavya washed the glasses together. Kavya told her what had happened. 'Chithi, how can sugar be in the water if you can't see it? Where does it hide?'

Chithi dried her hands. 'It doesn't hide. It breaks up. A grain of sugar is made of tiny pieces called particles, far too small to see. The water pulls those particles apart and they spread out among the water's own particles. Each one is too small to see, but each one is still sugar.'

'So the syrup was sugar particles too?' said Kavya.

'Yes. Lots of them, packed into a little water. That makes syrup heavy, so it sinks if you don't stir. The particles do move about on their own, but very, very slowly. Left alone, they would take days to spread to the top.'

Kavya thought about the ants on the counter. 'The ants knew the sugar was there. They didn't need to see it.'

'Ants sense the world with their antennae,' said Chithi. 'They smell and taste with them. They found the particles that you couldn't see.'

The next morning, Kavya wrote a sign in big letters and stuck it on the syrup bottle: STIR TEN TIMES. Under it, smaller, she wrote: The sugar is always there. You just can't see it.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the conductor tasted the top of an unstirred glass. The sugar had already dissolved in the syrup, so it was invisible, but the syrup is denser than water and sank to the bottom. Sugar particles do spread on their own by diffusion, but through water this takes days, so the top stayed sour. The shimmering layer was the syrup bending light differently from the water above it. Kavya's straw trick took a sample from the bottom. Stirring spread the particles through the whole glass in seconds. The ants found a sugar ring that was almost invisible, because they sense sugar with their antennae.

- Ask: Why did the top of the conductor's glass taste sour, even though Kavya had poured in the syrup?
- Ask: How did Kavya take water from only the bottom of the glass?
- Ask: The conductor still could not see the sugar after stirring. How do you know it was there?
- Ask: The ants went to the syrup ring but not the water ring. What does that tell you about the ants?
- Ask: The conductor said there was no sugar because he could not see any. Is seeing a good way to decide if something is there?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Hanger Balance: does vanished sugar still weigh anything? (35 min)
- **question:** When sugar dissolves and we can't see it any more, is it still there? Does it still weigh anything?
- **predict:** Guess first. We will weigh a bottle of water with some sugar beside it. Then we will dissolve the sugar and weigh it again. Will it be lighter, the same, or heavier? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Everything that was on side A before stays on side A: the bottle, its cap, the water, the sugar and its paper. Nothing is added and nothing is taken away. The only change is that the sugar is dissolved instead of in grains. The bottle stays closed, so nothing can escape.
- **safety:** Only taste from clean spoons and clean bottles. Wipe up spilled sugar, or ants will find it.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the hanger before and after the sugar dissolved, with the pointer.
- **results table:** What we did | Where the pointer went | What bottle A looked like | How bottle A tasted
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang the coat hanger from a door handle, or from a broom handle across two chairs. 2. Tape the straw to the middle of the hanger so it points straight down. 3. Tape the paper on the door or a chair behind the straw tip. 4. Hang one bag from each end of the hanger.  
  *Parent says:* We are making a balance. If one side gets heavier, it goes down, and the straw swings the other way.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the hanger up. Tape the straw so it points down. Hang a bag on each end. The straw is your pointer.
  - Note: A plastic hanger with notches at each end works best, because the bags cannot slide. If you only have a wire hanger, tape the bag handles in place at the two ends. Keep the bags clear of the door so they swing freely.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour 1 small cup of water into each bottle and close the caps. Call them A and B. 2. Put 4 level teaspoons of sugar on a square of paper, and fold it into a packet. 3. Put bottle A and the sugar packet in the left bag. Put bottle B in the right bag.  
  *Parent says:* Bottle A has the sugar with it. So which side do you think goes down?  
  *Child mode:* Fill both bottles with one cup of water. Close them. Fold the sugar into a paper packet. Put bottle A and the sugar on the left. Bottle B on the right.
  - Note: Side A should go down, because it has the sugar too. 4 teaspoons of sugar weigh about 16 grams.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open bottle B and add water a teaspoon at a time. 2. After each spoon, close it and hang it back. 3. Stop when the straw points straight down again. 4. Draw a line on the paper where the straw tip is.  
  *Parent says:* Now we add water to bottle B until both sides weigh the same. When the straw points straight down, they balance.  
  *Child mode:* Add water to bottle B, one spoon at a time. Stop when the straw points straight down. Draw a line at the straw's tip.
  - Note: It may take 3 or 4 teaspoons. Now try this: lift the sugar packet out of bag A. Side A goes up, and the straw swings. Put it back. This shows your balance can feel 4 teaspoons of sugar.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to predict before anything changes.  
  *Parent says:* Now we will pour the sugar into bottle A and shake it until it disappears. Then everything goes back in bag A. Will side A go up, stay level, or go down? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will dissolve the sugar in bottle A. Then it goes back in the bag. Will side A go up, stay level or go down? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen, and why? (good: It will go up, because the sugar disappears.; It will stay level, because the sugar is still inside.; It will go down, because sugar water is heavier.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is good. Many children, and many grown-ups, guess that side A will go up. Don't say what will happen.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take bottle A and the packet out. 2. Roll the packet's paper into a funnel and pour every grain into the bottle. Tap the paper. 3. Close the cap tight and shake for about 2 minutes, until no grains are left. 4. Put the bottle and the empty paper back in bag A.  
  *Parent says:* Pour in every grain, and keep the paper. Now shake until you can't see any sugar at all.  
  *Child mode:* Pour all the sugar into bottle A. Keep the paper. Close the cap. Shake until the sugar is gone. Put it all back in the bag.
  - Note: If a few grains spill, put them back into the bag too, so nothing is lost. 4 teaspoons dissolve in a cup of water in 1 or 2 minutes of shaking.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let the hanger stop swinging. 2. Look at where the straw points.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the straw. Where does it point now?  
  *Child mode:* Wait for the hanger to stop. Where does the straw point now?
  - Note: With a kitchen scale, you can do the same thing in numbers: weigh bottle A with the packet beside it, then again after dissolving. The reading should not change.
  - Check: The sugar vanished, but the straw still pointed at the line. What does this show? → **The sugar is still in the bottle, and weighs just as much as before**
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour a few drops from each bottle onto a clean steel spoon, and taste them.  
  *Parent says:* We can't see the sugar. But can we taste it?  
  *Child mode:* Taste a drop from bottle A. Then one from bottle B. Can you taste the sugar?
  - Ask: The sugar water looks just like plain water. Why does it taste sweet? (good: The sugar is still in there.; Tiny sugar particles are spread through all the water.)
- **s8 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: dissolved sugar is still there. What I saw: the sugar vanished, but the balance stayed level, and the water tasted sweet. Why: the sugar broke into particles too small to see, and they spread through the water. Every particle was still in the bottle.
### e2 · Lump, Grains or Powder: which sugar dissolves first? (37 min)
- **question:** Does the size of the pieces change how fast sugar dissolves?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which will dissolve first in water: a lump of kalkandu, ordinary sugar grains, or powdered sugar? Which will be last? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same glass, the same amount of water from the same jug, and the same amount of sugar each time. Stir the same way: 5 slow stirs every 30 seconds. Only the size of the pieces changes. Water from one jug is all the same warmth.
- **safety:** If a grown-up powders the sugar in a mixie, the child stays clear of the blades. Crushing with a rolling pin is safe for children.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar for each kind of sugar, as tall as the number of minutes it took.
- **results table:** Kind of sugar | My guess (first, second or last) | Time until no pieces are left
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make powdered sugar: fold 2 teaspoons of sugar inside a sheet of paper and press hard with a rolling pin. 2. Look at the three kinds of sugar side by side. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* These are all sugar, in different sizes. Which one will dissolve first? Which last? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Crush some sugar with a rolling pin. Look at the lump, the grains and the powder. Which will dissolve first? Why?
  - Ask: Which will dissolve first, and why? (good: The powder, because its pieces are smallest.; The lump, because it is one piece.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour one small cup of water into each glass. 2. Measure 1 level teaspoon of each sugar. For the kalkandu, choose pieces that fill a teaspoon about as full as the sugar.  
  *Parent says:* Same water, same glasses, about the same amount of sugar. Only the size of the pieces is different.  
  *Child mode:* Pour one cup of water into each glass. Measure one teaspoon of each sugar.
  - Note: The kalkandu will not fill the spoon exactly. That is fine. If you have a kitchen scale, weigh about 4 grams of each instead.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tip the powdered sugar into glass 1 and start the timer. 2. Stir 5 times slowly every 30 seconds. 3. Stop the timer when no powder is left at the bottom. 4. Write the time.  
  *Parent says:* Go! Five slow stirs every 30 seconds. Shout stop when you can't see any powder.  
  *Child mode:* Tip in the powder. Start the timer. Stir 5 times every 30 seconds. Shout stop when it is gone!
  - Note: Powdered sugar usually goes in well under a minute.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same with the sugar grains in glass 2.  
  *Parent says:* Same again with the ordinary grains. Five slow stirs every 30 seconds.  
  *Child mode:* Now the grains. Same stirring. Shout stop when they are gone.
  - Note: Grains usually take 1 to 3 minutes. Look from the side, against a dark cloth, to see the last few.
- **s5 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do the same with the kalkandu in glass 3. 2. If pieces are still there after 15 minutes, stop and write 'more than 15 minutes'.  
  *Parent says:* Now the lump. This one may take a while. While we wait, watch the lump closely. Is it getting smaller from the outside, or from the middle?  
  *Child mode:* Now the kalkandu. Same stirring. Watch the lump. Does it shrink from the outside or the middle?
  - Ask: Is the lump shrinking from the outside or from the middle? (good: From the outside.; Its corners are getting round.)
  - Note: A pea-sized lump often takes 5 to 15 minutes. Its sharp corners go round first, because water reaches the corners from more sides.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the times together.  
  *Parent says:* Which was fastest? The same amount of sugar went in each glass. So why did the powder win?  
  *Child mode:* Which was fastest? All three had the same sugar. Why did the powder win?
  - Check: Why did the powdered sugar dissolve first? → **Its tiny pieces have much more outside, so water can pull off many particles at once**
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: smaller pieces dissolve faster. What I saw: the powder went in 30 seconds, the grains in 2 minutes, the kalkandu in 12 minutes. Why: small pieces have more outside, and water pulls particles off the outside.

## Worksheets
### Where Did the Sugar Go? (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Lakshmi drops a spoon of salt into a glass of water and stirs. The salt disappears. Which word says what happened?  
   Options: The salt melted / The salt dissolved / The salt evaporated  
   **Answer:** The salt dissolved. *Why:* Salt breaking up in water and spreading through it is dissolving. Melting needs heat. Evaporating is a liquid going into the air.
2. (mcq) Which of these is made of particles?  
   Options: Only a stone / Only water / A stone, water and air  
   **Answer:** A stone, water and air. *Why:* Solids, liquids and gases are all made of particles. Picking only the stone means thinking only hard things have pieces.
3. (mcq) Why can't we see sugar particles?  
   Options: They are far too small / They are see-through like glass / They hide at the bottom  
   **Answer:** They are far too small. *Why:* A sugar particle is about a millionth of a millimetre across, far too small for eyes or ordinary microscopes.
4. (mcq) In the hanger balance test, what happened to the straw pointer when the sugar dissolved?  
   Options: It swung towards bottle A / It swung towards bottle B / It stayed at the line  
   **Answer:** It stayed at the line. *Why:* The sugar was still in the bottle, so the weight did not change and the balance stayed level.
5. (mcq) A sugar grain has flat sides and sharp corners. What is a solid like this called?  
   Options: A crystal / A liquid / A solution  
   **Answer:** A crystal. *Why:* A crystal has its particles stacked in neat rows, which gives it flat sides. A solution is the mixture you get after dissolving.
6. (truefalse) When sugar dissolves in water, it is gone for ever.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The sugar is still there as particles. You can taste it and weigh it.
7. (truefalse) Sugar can dissolve in cold water.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It dissolves more slowly in cold water, but it does dissolve. No heat is needed.
8. (truefalse) Powdered sugar is a different substance from ordinary sugar.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is the same sugar, crushed into smaller pieces. It tastes the same.
9. (fill) The clear mixture you get when sugar dissolves in water is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['solution']. *Why:* Sugar water and salty water are solutions.
10. (fill) Sugar dissolves faster when it is crushed, when it is ____, and when the water is warm.  
   **Answer:** ['stirred']. *Why:* Stirring carries the sweet water away from the sugar, so fresh water can reach it.
11. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Particle → a very tiny piece that things are made of; Dissolve → break up and spread through a liquid; Melt → turn liquid because of heat; Crystal → a solid with particles in neat rows. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit.
12. (sort) Sort these. Is it dissolving or melting?  
   **Answer:** sugar in nimbu pani → Dissolving; ice on a hot day → Melting; salt in sambar → Dissolving; butter in a hot pan → Melting; jaggery in water → Dissolving; a candle near a flame → Melting. *Why:* Dissolving needs a liquid to break the solid up. Melting needs heat, and nothing else.
13. (write) Your friend says the sugar in her tea has disappeared. How could she find out if it is still there? Give one way.

   Options: Sugar sits at the bottom and never changes / Sugar slowly makes the water above it sweet, even with no stirring / A stone stays where you put it  
   **Answer:** Sugar slowly makes the water above it sweet, even with no stirring. *Why:* Over days, sugar particles keep moving and spread up through the water by themselves. A stone's particles move too, but they jiggle in place.

   Options: The ones in the middle / The ones on the outside / All of them at once

### Prove It With Particles (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Meenakshi puts a camphor tablet in an empty jar, closes the lid tight, and weighs the jar every day for a week. What happens to the weight of the closed jar?  
   Options: It goes down / It stays the same / It goes up  
   **Answer:** It stays the same. *Why:* Some camphor particles leave the tablet, but they stay in the air inside the closed jar. Nothing escapes, so the total weight stays the same.
2. (mcq) Anand puts 4 teaspoons of sugar into a full glass of water. Will the water spill over the top?  
   Options: No, the sugar takes up no space at all / A little may spill, because the sugar particles still take up some space / It will spill a whole cupful  
   **Answer:** A little may spill, because the sugar particles still take up some space. *Why:* Sugar particles are still there, so they take up some room. The level rises a little. A heap of sugar on a spoon also has air between the grains, so the rise is less than the size of the heap.
3. (mcq) Which test would best show that dissolved salt is still in water, without tasting?  
   Options: Looking at the water closely / Letting a few drops dry on a clean steel plate / Smelling the water  
   **Answer:** Letting a few drops dry on a clean steel plate. *Why:* When the water goes into the air, the salt particles stay behind and gather into white grains again. Looking cannot work, because the particles are too small to see.
4. (mcq) In the old days, some scientists said particles were not real because nobody could see one. What is the problem with that reason?  
   Options: Nothing, it is a good reason / Many real things are too small to see, and we can find them in other ways / Particles are big enough to see  
   **Answer:** Many real things are too small to see, and we can find them in other ways. *Why:* Tasting, weighing and the ants all found sugar we could not see. Not being able to see something does not show it is not there.
5. (truefalse) If you could keep cutting a grain of sugar in half, you would always get smaller sugar.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. After about 60 cuts, each halving the amount, you would reach one sugar particle. Cut that, and you get atoms of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, which are not sugar.
6. (fill) The rule that the total weight stays the same in a closed bottle, whatever changes happen inside, is called the conservation of ____.  
   **Answer:** ['mass']. *Why:* Mass means how much stuff there is. It is never lost, only moved or changed.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the slowest to dissolve to the fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['A kalkandu lump in cold water, not stirred', 'Sugar grains in cold water, stirred', 'Powdered sugar in warm water, stirred']. *Why:* Big pieces, cold water and no stirring are slowest. Small pieces, warm water and stirring are fastest.
8. (write) Ravi did the hanger balance test, but he did not put the empty sugar paper back in the bag. Side A went up a tiny bit. He says, 'Dissolved sugar weighs less!' What went wrong?  
   **Answer:** He took the paper out, so side A lost the paper's weight. That is why it went up. To be fair, everything that was on side A before must go back.. *Why:* A strong answer spots that the test was not fair: something was taken away.
9. (write) Sugar dissolves in water, but sand does not. What do you think is different about how water particles treat sugar particles and sand particles?  
   **Answer:** Water particles attract sugar particles strongly enough to pull them off the grain. Sand particles hold on to each other more tightly than water can pull, so they stay together.. *Why:* A strong answer says dissolving depends on a pull between the water and the solid, and is a good guess, even if not every detail is known.
10. (write) Your body gets energy from a sugar called glucose, carried in your blood. Why is it useful that this sugar is dissolved, and not in grains?  
   **Answer:** Dissolved sugar is spread out as tiny particles, so the blood can carry it everywhere and it can get into every cell. Grains would be too big and would block thin tubes.. *Why:* A strong answer links dissolving to how easily tiny particles can travel and get into cells.
11. (write) A grain of sugar holds roughly a million million million particles. Why does it still look like one solid grain, and not a cloud of dots?  
   **Answer:** The particles are packed tightly and hold on to each other in neat rows. They are far too small for our eyes to see one by one, so we only see the whole grain.. *Why:* A strong answer mentions that the particles are packed close and are too small to see apart.
12. (write) Many people say sugar 'melts' in tea. Plan a test to show it is dissolving, not melting.  
   **Answer:** Put sugar in a glass of cold water from the fridge and stir. It still disappears, with no heat. Then put sugar in a dry steel spoon on a warm day: it does not turn liquid. So water is needed, not heat.. *Why:* A strong answer tests sugar in cold water, where it cannot be melting, and keeps the test fair.
13. (write) In the camphor project, the jar tablet hardly shrank. Arjun says this shows camphor in the jar stopped moving. Is he right? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. The camphor particles in the jar keep leaving the tablet, but just as many land back on it, because they can't escape. So the tablet stays the same size, even though its particles are moving all the time.. *Why:* A strong answer separates 'nothing changes that we can see' from 'nothing is moving'.
14. (draw) Draw the hanger balance three times: with the sugar packet beside bottle A, with the packet lifted out, and after the sugar dissolved. Show the straw each time.  
   **Answer:** First drawing: straw at the line. Second: side A higher, straw swung. Third: straw back at the line.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the balance could feel the sugar, and that dissolving did not change the weight.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about how we know particles are real.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is everything around you made of?  
   Options: Particles only in solids, not in water or air / Particles far too small to see, in solids, liquids and gases / Grains you can see with a magnifying glass  
   **Answer:** Particles far too small to see, in solids, liquids and gases. *Why:* Solids, liquids and gases are all made of particles far too small to see. Picking the first means thinking water and air are smooth all the way down, with no pieces. Picking the last confuses grains, which are huge heaps of particles, with particles.
2. (mcq) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, stirs sugar into water and the grains disappear. Where is the sugar now?  
   Options: It has been destroyed / Spread through the water as tiny sugar particles / It has turned into water  
   **Answer:** Spread through the water as tiny sugar particles. *Why:* The sugar broke into particles too small to see, and they spread among the water particles. Picking 'destroyed' is the most common guess, because we trust our eyes. Picking 'turned into water' misses that the water tastes sweet: the sugar is still sugar.
3. (mcq) On a kitchen scale, a closed bottle of water and a packet of sugar weigh 120 grams together. Riya, a girl in Class 3, pours the sugar into the bottle, closes it and shakes it until the sugar is gone. She puts the bottle and the empty packet back on the scale. What does the scale show?  
   Options: Less than 120 grams / 120 grams / More than 120 grams  
   **Answer:** 120 grams. *Why:* Every sugar particle is still in the bottle, so the weight stays the same. Picking 'less' means thinking dissolved sugar weighs nothing. Picking 'more' is a guess that sugar water is heavier than water and sugar apart, but nothing was added.
4. (mcq) In the hanger balance test, why did we first lift the sugar packet out of the bag and watch the straw?  
   Options: To check that the balance could feel the weight of the sugar / To let the sugar cool down / To make the test go faster  
   **Answer:** To check that the balance could feel the weight of the sugar. *Why:* If the balance moved when the packet was lifted out, we knew it could feel the sugar. So when it did not move after dissolving, we knew the sugar had not lost any weight.
5. (mcq) Which dissolves fastest in a glass of water?  
   Options: A lump of kalkandu / Ordinary sugar grains / Powdered sugar  
   **Answer:** Powdered sugar. *Why:* Powder has the most outside, so water can pull off many particles at once. Picking kalkandu means thinking a bigger piece holds more and so goes faster, but it has the least outside.
6. (mcq) Meera, a girl in Class 3, wants to test whether stirring makes sugar dissolve faster. What should she keep the same?  
   Options: Nothing, it doesn't matter / The water, the glass and the amount of sugar / Only the glass  
   **Answer:** The water, the glass and the amount of sugar. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes, here the stirring. Everything else stays the same, so she knows the stirring made the difference.
7. (mcq) Which of these is melting, not dissolving?  
   Options: Salt disappearing in rasam / Ghee turning runny in a warm pan / Sugar disappearing in cold milk  
   **Answer:** Ghee turning runny in a warm pan. *Why:* Ghee turns liquid because it is heated, with nothing else added. That is melting. Salt in rasam and sugar in cold milk are dissolving: they break up in a liquid, with no heat needed.
8. (mcq) Arjun, a boy in Class 3, says, 'Sugar melts in tea because the tea is hot.' What is wrong with this?  
   Options: Nothing, he is right / Sugar dissolves even in cold water, so it is dissolving, not melting / Sugar never goes into tea  
   **Answer:** Sugar dissolves even in cold water, so it is dissolving, not melting. *Why:* Sugar dissolves in ice-cold water too. Melting sugar needs a dry pan at about 186 degrees Celsius. Hot tea only makes dissolving faster.
9. (mcq) Long ago, many teachers said that when two things truly mix, each stops being itself. What did Daniel Sennert, a doctor in the 1600s, do to show this was wrong?  
   Options: He dissolved silver in a strong acid and got the same silver back / He looked at silver particles with a microscope / He weighed the air  
   **Answer:** He dissolved silver in a strong acid and got the same silver back. *Why:* If the silver had stopped existing, he could not have got it back. So it must have been there all along, as tiny particles. Microscopes of his time could not show particles.
10. (mcq) Sugar was left at the bottom of a tall glass with no stirring. After 30 minutes, how did the top taste?  
   Options: Plain, because sugar particles spread very slowly by themselves / Very sweet, because sugar rises / Salty  
   **Answer:** Plain, because sugar particles spread very slowly by themselves. *Why:* Sugar particles do move by themselves, but through water this spreading is very slow. It could take days to reach the top. Picking 'sugar rises' is a fair guess, but sweet water is heavier, so it stays at the bottom.
11. (mcq) Ants went to a drop of sugar water and left a drop of plain water alone. The drops looked the same. How did the ants know?  
   Options: They sensed the sugar particles with their antennae / They saw the sugar / They guessed  
   **Answer:** They sensed the sugar particles with their antennae. *Why:* Ants smell and taste with their antennae. When an ant touches the sugar water, she picks up the sugar particles and knows it is food. Nobody can see dissolved sugar.
12. (mcq) A camphor tablet on an open saucer gets smaller every day, and the room smells of camphor. What is happening?  
   Options: The camphor is being destroyed / Camphor particles are leaving the tablet and going into the air / The camphor is melting into the saucer  
   **Answer:** Camphor particles are leaving the tablet and going into the air. *Why:* Camphor particles fly off into the air, and some reach your nose, so you smell it. The saucer stays dry, so it is not melting. Nothing is destroyed.

## Applications
- ORS, the oral rehydration drink given for diarrhoea, is salt and sugar dissolved in clean water. It has saved millions of lives.
- Sugar mills boil sugarcane juice until the water leaves and the sugar particles gather into crystals again.
- Syrups, sherbets, coffee and payasam all depend on sugar dissolving.
- Many medicines are dissolved in water or in syrup, so the body can take them in quickly.
- Washing powder dissolves in water, so it can reach the dirt in every thread of the cloth.
- Water purifiers that use RO remove the salts dissolved in tap or borewell water.

## Spotted around us
- Sugar disappearing into filter coffee or tea, and the last sip being sweetest when nobody stirred.
- Salt in rasam or sambar: you can't see it, but one taste tells you if there is enough.
- Syrup bottles at juice stalls and sugarcane juice carts.
- The smell of camphor in a cupboard or a pooja shelf, from particles leaving the tablets.
- The salt pans of Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu, where sea water dries in the sun and the dissolved salt comes back as white crystals.
- Ants marching to a sticky spot on the kitchen platform where a drop of tea or juice dried.

## Open questions
- **Is there a smallest thing, with nothing smaller inside it?** Sugar particles are made of atoms, and atoms of smaller particles. Physicists do not know if there is a last, smallest level.
- **Why do some things dissolve in water and others not, exactly?** Chemists understand the main reasons, but working out from scratch how much of a new substance will dissolve is still very hard. They often have to test it.
- **What happens at the very moment a single particle leaves a crystal?** It happens in far less than a millionth of a second. Scientists study it with computer models, and they are still learning the details.
- **How do ants tell sugar from other things so quickly with their antennae?** Scientists know ants have many sensing cells on their antennae, but how an ant puts the signals together to choose food is still being studied.
