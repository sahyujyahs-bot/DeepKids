# How seeds travel: wings and wind, water, animals and people, and why people save seeds
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.seed-dispersal.L5`

**Summary.** A tree cannot walk, yet young trees of its kind appear across a valley and on far islands. How do its seeds get there? In this lesson you will test paper winged seeds in the wind of a fan, see where 30 drops of one spinner land, and trace where ten foods came from.

**Intro.** *A plate of idli and sambar looks as South Indian as food can be. But the tomato and the chilli in the sambar came from the Americas, about 500 years ago. The tamarind came from Africa, long before that. The onion came from Central Asia. None of these plants can walk. So how did their seeds reach your kitchen?* Every seed has to leave the parent plant somehow. Some seeds have wings. Some float. Some are swallowed, and some are thrown. Some ride with people. In this lesson you first read the shapes of seeds from your kitchen, and guess how each one travels. Then you build three paper fliers and test them in front of a fan. The result tells you what a wing really does. After that you follow the longest journeys of all, and meet the people who save seeds.

## Terms
- **Seed dispersal**: Seed dispersal is the movement of seeds away from the parent plant. To disperse means to spread out in different directions. *e.g.* The seeds of a peepal tree are dispersed by birds, who eat the figs and drop the seeds far away.
- **Fruit**: A fruit is the part of a plant that grows from the ovary of a flower and holds the seeds. Many fruits are built to help the seeds travel. *e.g.* A drumstick, a coconut, a tamarind pod and a chilli are all fruits.
- **Competition**: Competition happens when living beings need the same thing and there is not enough for all. Plants compete for light, water and space. *e.g.* A hundred mango seedlings under one mango tree compete for the little light that reaches the ground.
- **Air resistance (drag)**: Air resistance is the push of air against anything that moves through the air. Scientists also call this push drag. A wider and faster thing meets more drag. *e.g.* A flat sheet of paper falls slowly because of drag. The same sheet crushed into a ball falls fast.
- **Steady fall speed**: The steady fall speed is the speed at which a falling thing stops speeding up. At this speed, the drag pushing up is equal to the weight pulling down. Scientists call this speed terminal velocity. *e.g.* A spinning mahogany seed reaches a steady fall speed of about one metre each second.
- **Samara**: A samara is a dry fruit with a thin, papery wing. The wing grew from the wall of the fruit. *e.g.* The flat, round fruit of the Indian elm is a samara. So is the spinning fruit of a maple tree.
- **Autorotation**: Autorotation is spinning that is caused only by falling through the air. No engine or muscle turns the wing. *e.g.* A mahogany seed starts to spin after falling a short way, and then comes down slowly like a small helicopter.
- **Pappus**: A pappus is a tuft of fine hairs on top of a small dry fruit. The hairs act like a parachute. *e.g.* The fluffy white balls on the roadside weed tridax are made of hundreds of fruits, each with a pappus.
- **Vortex**: A vortex is air or water that spins round and round, like the water going down a drain. *e.g.* A small vortex of air sits on top of a spinning maple seed and helps to hold the seed up.
- **Seed shadow**: A seed shadow is the pattern of places where the seeds of one plant land. Most seeds land near the parent, and a few land far away. *e.g.* Under and around one neem tree the ground is thick with fallen seeds. Fifty metres away there are only a few.
- **Long-distance dispersal**: Long-distance dispersal is the rare journey of a seed that travels very much farther than most seeds of that plant. *e.g.* A coconut that floats from one island to another has made a long-distance journey.
- **Frugivore**: A frugivore is an animal who eats mostly fruit. Many frugivores swallow seeds and drop them somewhere else. *e.g.* Hornbills, bulbuls, barbets, fruit bats and civets are frugivores.
- **Elaiosome**: An elaiosome is a small, oily food parcel on the outside of some seeds. Ants carry the whole seed home to eat the parcel. *e.g.* A castor seed has a pale bump at one end. That bump is an elaiosome.
- **Explosive dispersal**: In explosive dispersal the fruit itself throws the seeds. The drying or swelling fruit stores energy like a bent spring, and then lets go suddenly. *e.g.* A ripe balsam pod curls up at a touch and flings the seeds out.
- **Propagule**: A propagule is any part of a plant that can leave the parent and grow into a new plant. A seed is one kind of propagule. *e.g.* A mangrove tree drops long green seedlings that have already sprouted. Each one is a propagule.
- **Domestication**: Domestication is the slow change of a wild plant or animal into one that lives with people and depends on them. People cause the change by choosing which ones to grow or breed. *e.g.* Farmed paddy was domesticated from wild rice over thousands of years.
- **Shattering**: Shattering is the way a wild grass drops ripe grains from the stalk, one by one. Farmed grains have mostly lost this habit. *e.g.* Wild rice shatters, so the grain falls into the water. Farmed paddy holds the grain until someone threshes the stalks.
- **Variety (landrace)**: A variety is one kind within a crop, such as one kind of rice. A landrace is an old variety that farmers in one place have grown and saved for many generations. *e.g.* Mappillai samba, kattuyanam and navara are old rice landraces of Tamil Nadu and Kerala.
- **Seed bank**: A seed bank is a place where seeds of many kinds and varieties are kept alive for the future. Most seed banks keep the seeds dry and cold. *e.g.* The National Genebank in New Delhi keeps more than four lakh seed samples.
- **Invasive species**: An invasive species is a living being who was carried to a new place, spreads fast there, and crowds out the beings who lived there before. *e.g.* Lantana was brought to India as a garden plant about 200 years ago. Now lantana covers large parts of many forests.

## Explanations
### Why seeds leave home · *defined*
**Definition:** Seed dispersal is the movement of seeds away from the parent plant. A seed that lands away from the parent often has a better chance to live.

A plant makes many seeds. If all of them fell straight down, they would sprout in a heap under the parent. There are three reasons why that is a poor start. The first is competition. The seedlings would need the same light, water and space as each other and as the parent, and the parent's leaves already shade the ground. The second is enemies. The insects and moulds that feed on one kind of plant gather where that plant is common. Seedlings near the parent meet more of them. Two scientists, Daniel Janzen and Joseph Connell, proposed this around 1970. Studies in many forests have since found that seedlings close to a parent of the same kind die more often. It is not true for every tree. The third reason is change. A place that is good today may flood, burn or dry out. A plant whose seeds reach many places is more likely to have some young who live. Leaving home is also risky. Most seeds that travel land somewhere bad. Dispersal is worth it only because a few land somewhere good.
- e.g. Under a big mango tree you may find dozens of mango seedlings after the rains. Almost none of them become trees.
- e.g. A banyan seed dropped by a bird on a far wall has no banyan above to shade them.
- e.g. A groundnut plant is an exception. The plant pushes the young pods into the soil right beside the parent.
### Reading a fruit: the shape shows how the seeds travel · *defined*
**Definition:** A fruit is the part of a plant that holds the seeds. The shape, weight and covering of a fruit are evidence of how the seeds usually leave.

You can often work out how a seed travels by looking closely. A thin wing or a tuft of hair means wind. The seed is light and has a wide surface for the air to push on. A thick, light, waterproof husk full of air means water. Soft, sweet, brightly coloured flesh round hard seeds means that an animal eats the fruit. The animal digests the flesh and drops the seeds. Hooks or sticky gum mean that the fruit clings to fur, feathers or clothes. A dry pod that curls or snaps open means that the plant throws the seeds. A small oily bump on a seed is food for ants, who carry the seed home. Many plants use two ways, one after the other. A castor pod throws the seeds, and then ants carry them farther. A shape is good evidence, but it is not proof. To be sure, scientists watch what really happens to the seeds.
- e.g. A drumstick seed has three papery wings, so the wind can carry them.
- e.g. A coconut has a thick husk of fibres with air between them, so the coconut floats.
- e.g. Spear grass seeds have sharp points that catch in socks and in the fur of dogs and cattle.
- e.g. A ripe tomato is soft, red and juicy, with small slippery seeds that pass through an animal unharmed.
### How a wing slows a fall · *defined*
**Definition:** Air resistance, or drag, is the push of air against a moving thing. A falling seed speeds up until the drag pushing up is equal to the weight pulling down. From then on the seed falls at a steady speed.

When a seed is let go, the Earth pulls the seed down. This pull is the seed's weight. At first the seed falls faster and faster. But a falling thing has to push air out of the way, and the air pushes back. That push is drag. Drag gets bigger as the speed gets bigger. So there comes a speed at which the drag is as big as the weight. The two pushes cancel, and the seed stops speeding up. This is the steady fall speed. A wing changes this speed. A wing adds a lot of surface and very little weight. With more surface, the air pushes back harder at any speed. So the drag matches the weight at a much lower speed. A packed seed with no wing may fall at 5 metres each second or more. A winged seed of the same weight may fall at 1 metre each second. Scientists compare seeds by how much weight each square centimetre of wing must hold up. They call this the wing loading. A lower wing loading gives a slower fall.
- e.g. A flat sheet of paper and the same sheet crushed into a ball weigh the same. The flat sheet falls far more slowly.
- e.g. A mahogany seed with the wing broken off drops like a small stone.
- e.g. A person under an open parachute falls at about 5 metres each second, slow enough to land safely.
### The spinning seed: a wing that makes a small whirl of air · *defined*
**Definition:** Autorotation is spinning caused only by falling through the air. A spinning winged seed falls more slowly than the same seed would fall without spinning.

A mahogany seed has one long wing with the heavy seed at one end. After falling a short way, the seed starts to spin. The heavy end stays near the centre, and the wing sweeps round like one blade of a helicopter. Why does spinning help? As the wing sweeps round, it meets far more air each second than a wing that only drops. In 2009, David Lentink and his team studied model maple seeds in a tank of oil. They found a small whirl of air sitting on top of the front edge of the spinning wing. A whirl like this is called a leading-edge vortex. The air pressure inside the whirl is low, so the air below the wing pushes the wing up harder. This whirl lets the wing make much more lift than a wing of that size otherwise could. Insects, bats and hummingbirds get extra lift from the same kind of whirl when they hover. The seed has no muscles. The fall itself turns the wing.
- e.g. Mahogany trees along many city roads in India drop one-winged seeds that spin all the way down.
- e.g. Sal fruits in the forests of central and eastern India have wings that grew from the sepals of the flower.
- e.g. The fruit of the vine called hiptage, or madhavi lata, has three wings and spins as it falls.
### The parachute of hairs · *defined*
**Definition:** A pappus is a tuft of fine hairs on a small dry fruit. The hairs slow the fall, as a parachute does.

Look at the fluffy white ball on a tridax plant by the roadside. Each tiny fruit has a ring of feathery hairs on top. A dandelion fruit has about 100 hairs. The hairs cover less than a tenth of the circle they spread across. The rest is gaps. You might expect the air to rush through the gaps. It does not. Each hair is so thin that the air close to it is slowed down. The slowed air round one hair overlaps with the slowed air round the next. So the tuft acts almost like a solid disc, but weighs far less. In 2018, Cathal Cummins, Naomi Nakayama and their team in Edinburgh filmed dandelion fruits in a small wind tunnel. They found a ring-shaped whirl of air floating just above the hairs. Nobody had seen a whirl of that kind before. It adds to the drag and keeps the fruit steady. A dandelion fruit falls at only about 30 to 40 centimetres each second.
- e.g. Tridax, a small daisy with a white and yellow flower, grows on almost every roadside in India.
- e.g. A ripe pod of calotropis, called erukku or aak, splits and lets out flat brown seeds with long silky hairs.
- e.g. The floss of the silk cotton tree drifts across towns in the hot weeks before the monsoon, carrying the seeds.
### The wind does the carrying · *defined*
**Definition:** A winged or tufted seed has no way to push forward. The moving air carries the seed sideways while the seed falls. The longer the fall lasts and the faster the wind, the farther the seed goes.

People say that winged seeds fly. But a seed has no engine and no muscles. If you drop a winged seed in still air, the seed comes straight down. All the sideways travel comes from the wind. A wing helps in two ways, and both come from the same cause. The wing gives the air a lot to push on, compared with the weight. First, the seed falls slowly, so the wind has longer to carry the seed. Second, the wind sweeps a broad, light seed up to the wind's own speed very quickly. A rough rule follows. Distance is about the time in the air multiplied by the speed of the wind. A seed that falls for 20 seconds in a wind of 5 metres each second lands about 100 metres away. So three things make a seed go far: a slow fall, a strong wind, and a high start. This is one reason why tall trees can use wings well. Rising air matters too. On a hot afternoon, air rises from warm ground. A few seeds are caught in rising air and lifted high above the trees. Those few may travel many kilometres.
- e.g. A seed let go from 20 metres falls for 20 seconds at a steady 1 metre each second. From 2 metres the fall lasts only 2 seconds.
- e.g. Orchid seeds are as fine as dust. They fall so slowly that the wind can carry them for hundreds of kilometres.
- e.g. Many trees with winged seeds drop them in the dry, windy season, when the leaves are off and the wind passes through.
### The seed shadow: most land near, a few go far · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed shadow is the pattern of places where the seeds of one plant land. In almost every plant, most seeds land close to the parent and a few land far away.

Drop the same paper spinner many times in the same wind, and the landing places differ. The wind is never perfectly steady. For a real tree the spread is much wider. Most seeds leave on an ordinary breeze and land within a few tree-heights. A few leave in a strong gust, or are lifted by rising air, and go very far. If you counted the seeds at each distance, the count would be high near the tree. It would drop quickly and then tail off slowly over a long distance. The far end of this pattern is very hard to measure, because one seed in ten thousand is nearly impossible to find. Yet those rare seeds matter most for where a kind of plant can live. Here is the evidence. About 12,000 years ago the ice that covered northern Europe melted. Oak trees then spread north by about 1,000 kilometres in a few thousand years. An acorn falls only a few metres from the oak. In 1899 the British botanist Clement Reid worked out that falling acorns alone would need about a million years to cover that distance. The answer is rare long trips. Birds called jays carry acorns for kilometres and bury them.
- e.g. Under a rain tree the ground is covered in pods. A hundred metres away you find only one or two.
- e.g. A new island of sand in a river grows grass and bushes within a few years, from seeds that came on wind, water and birds.
- e.g. Peepal seedlings sprout on high walls and temple towers, far from any peepal tree.
### Carried by water · *defined*
**Definition:** Some fruits and seeds float, and are carried by rivers, floods and the sea. A floating fruit has trapped air and a covering that keeps water out.

A coconut is built for the sea. The outer skin is smooth and waterproof. Under the skin is a thick husk of fibres with air between them, so the whole fruit floats high. Inside the hard shell, the seed has food and water for the journey. In one small test of 15 coconuts, most were still able to sprout after floating in sea water for 110 days. In that time a slow ocean current can carry a coconut a few thousand kilometres. Mangrove trees do something different. The seed sprouts while still hanging on the mother tree, and grows into a long green seedling. This is called vivipary, which means live birth. The seedling then drops into the water and floats away on the tide, often lying flat at first. Later the root end takes in water and sinks, and the seedling roots in the mud. Fresh water carries seeds too. Lotus fruits and the seeds of many riverbank trees float downstream in the monsoon.
- e.g. Coconut palms line the coasts of Kerala, Lakshadweep and the Andaman Islands.
- e.g. At Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu and in the Sundarbans of Bengal, mangrove seedlings hang from the trees like green pencils.
- e.g. The Indian almond tree, common on beaches and city roads, has a corky fruit that floats in the sea.
### Carried by animals · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants are dispersed by animals. Some seeds are carried inside an animal who ate the fruit. Others are carried on the outside, or carried away to be stored as food.

A sweet fruit is a payment. The plant spends sugar on the flesh, and the animal who eats the flesh carries the seeds away. The seeds are hard or slippery, so many pass through the gut whole. They land in a dropping, which is a small heap of manure. Unripe fruit is green, hard and sour, because the seeds inside are not ready. Chillies show how exact this can be. In 2001, Joshua Tewksbury and Gary Nabhan tested wild chillies. Birds ate the hot fruit freely, and the seeds passed through them and sprouted well. Mice and rats tasted the heat and left the fruit alone. When they did eat mild chillies, they chewed the seeds, and no seeds sprouted. Birds do not sense the hot chemical, which is called capsaicin. So the heat keeps seed-chewers away and lets seed-carriers eat. Large animals carry large seeds. In the forests of Arunachal Pradesh, the scientist Aparajita Datta followed hornbills for years. She showed that hornbills swallow big fruits whole and drop the seeds far away, often kilometres from the parent tree. Elephants carry mango, jackfruit and wood apple seeds, and leave them in a great heap of dung. Other seeds ride outside, with hooks that catch in fur. Ants carry seeds that have an oily food parcel.
- e.g. A bulbul eats lantana berries and drops the seeds from a perch somewhere else.
- e.g. Flying foxes, the big fruit bats, carry figs and Indian almonds away from the tree and drop the seeds below their feeding perch.
- e.g. Flowerpeckers eat the sticky berries of a plant that grows on mango branches. They wipe the gluey seed onto another branch, where the seed sprouts.
- e.g. A squirrel buries nuts to eat later and forgets some. The forgotten ones can sprout.
### Thrown by the plant · *defined*
**Definition:** In explosive dispersal the fruit throws the seeds. The fruit stores energy as it dries or swells, in the way a bent spring stores energy, and then lets go all at once.

A ripe balsam pod is under strain. The outer layer of the pod wall is swollen with water and is trying to get longer. The inner layer holds it back. The five strips of the wall are joined along weak seams. A touch breaks a seam. Each strip then curls up in a few thousandths of a second and flings the seeds out. Other pods store energy by drying. The wall of a bauhinia pod has two layers of stiff fibres. The fibres in one layer run at a slant to the fibres in the other. When the pod dries, each layer shrinks in a different direction. The two halves cannot both shrink flat, so they twist into spirals. Shahaf Armon and her team in Jerusalem worked this out in 2011, using strips of stretched rubber. The pods of the rubber tree split with a loud crack and throw the seeds more than ten metres. Throwing is a short trip compared with wind or birds. But throwing costs the plant no sweet flesh, and needs no partner.
- e.g. Balsam is grown in many Indian gardens. Children press the ripe pods to make them pop.
- e.g. The dry pods of the cracker plant, a small purple-flowered weed, burst when a raindrop wets them.
- e.g. Castor capsules dry and snap open in the sun, and the seeds fly out.
### Big seed or far seed: a plant has one budget · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant has a limited amount of food to spend on seeds. The plant can make a few large seeds with a big food store, or very many small seeds with almost none.

A large seed gives the seedling a strong start. The seedling can grow tall roots and leaves in shade or in dry soil before the food runs out. But a large seed is heavy. Wind cannot lift a seed like that. Only a large animal or water can carry one. A small seed can be carried by a breath of wind, and the plant can make thousands. But each seedling has almost nothing to live on, and must land in just the right place. So there is no best size. The largest seed in the world is the coco de mer, a palm of the Seychelles islands. One seed can weigh more than 15 kilograms. The fresh fruit sinks in the sea, and these palms grow wild on only two islands. The smallest seeds belong to orchids. One orchid pod can hold more than a million seeds. An orchid seed has no food store at all. The seed can only sprout if a fungus in the soil feeds the seedling at first.
- e.g. One coconut palm makes about 50 to 100 large seeds in a year.
- e.g. One tridax plant makes thousands of tiny seeds in a year.
- e.g. A mango seedling can live for weeks in deep shade on the food in the large seed.
### People as seed carriers · *defined*
**Definition:** People carry seeds farther and faster than wind, water or any other animal. People carry crops on purpose, and weeds by accident. Domestication has changed some crops so much that they can no longer disperse without us.

Humans are animals who carry seeds, as hornbills and elephants do. But we carry them across oceans. After 1492, ships began to sail between the Americas and the rest of the world. Portuguese ships reached India in 1498. Within about a hundred years they had brought chilli, and later potato, tomato, maize, groundnut, papaya, guava, cashew, pineapple and tapioca. Before that, Indian cooks made food hot with black pepper and long pepper. Seeds went the other way too. Pepper, mango and sugarcane left Asia for the rest of the world. We carry seeds by accident as well. Seeds ride in grain sacks, in soil, on tyres and on shoes. The weed parthenium probably reached India mixed in wheat that came by ship from America in the 1950s. Tridax, the roadside daisy, also came from the Americas. Some of these newcomers spread fast and crowd out the plants who lived there before. They are called invasive species. The oldest change is in our crops. Wild rice shatters, which means the ripe grains drop off one by one. Early farmers could only harvest the grains that stayed on the stalk. Those were the grains they sowed the next year. After many generations, almost every plant in the field held on to the grain. Farmed paddy, wheat and maize now wait for people to thresh and sow them. People became part of the life cycle of these plants.
- e.g. The story goes that a Sufi saint named Baba Budan carried seven coffee beans from Yemen to the hills of Chikkamagaluru, around 1670.
- e.g. Ragi, the finger millet of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, was first farmed in East Africa. Ragi probably reached India more than 3,000 years ago.
- e.g. A maize cob is wrapped so tightly in leaves that the kernels have no way out unless someone opens the cob.
### People as seed savers · *defined*
**Definition:** A crop is not one kind of plant. A crop is thousands of varieties, each a little different. Saving seeds means keeping those varieties alive, in fields or in seed banks.

For thousands of years every farmer saved seed from each harvest to sow the next year. In each valley the crop slowly changed to suit that soil and weather. India once had many thousands of rice varieties. Some stand in deep flood water. Some grow in salty soil near the sea. Some ripen in 60 days. In the last hundred years, most farmers moved to a few high-yield varieties, and many old ones were lost. Why does that matter? If every plant in a field is nearly the same, one disease can kill them all. In Ireland in the 1840s, most people grew one variety of potato. A disease called blight destroyed the crop year after year, and about a million people died. Variety is also where cures are found. In the 1970s a virus called grassy stunt was destroying rice across Asia. Scientists tested more than 6,000 kinds of rice from a seed bank. Only one could resist the virus. That one was a wild rice collected in Uttar Pradesh. Breeders crossed that wild rice with farmed rice, and made new varieties that resist the virus. There are two ways to save seeds. A seed bank keeps dry seeds in the cold, where they stay alive for decades. The Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway is inside a frozen mountain and holds more than a million samples. The other way is to keep sowing. Farmers such as Rahibai Popere in Maharashtra grow old varieties every year and share the seed. A variety that is sown every year keeps changing slowly. In each year, the plants that cope best with that year's weather and pests leave the most seed. Seeds in a vault stay as they were.
- e.g. Nikolai Vavilov, a Russian scientist, collected seeds on five continents and built the first great seed bank, in Leningrad.
- e.g. At the yearly paddy festival started by Nel Jayaraman in Tamil Nadu, a farmer takes old rice seed home and brings back more the next year.
- e.g. In Telangana, women of the Deccan Development Society keep village seed banks of millets and pulses in clay pots.
### Does a plant decide when to let a seed go? · *debated*
A plant has some control over dispersal. Seeds do not all fall as soon as they are ripe. The stalk that holds a winged seed is strong enough to hold in a light breeze. It breaks in a strong, dry gust. So more seeds leave in exactly the kind of wind that carries them far. The scientist David Greene measured this on trees in 2005. A dandelion closes the tuft of hairs in damp air and opens it wide in dry air. In 2022, Madeleine Seale and her team showed how. A ring of tissue at the base of the hairs swells with water and pulls the hairs shut. Damp days usually have little wind, so the seeds stay at home on days when they would not go far. Plants sense, and they act on what they sense. Scientists agree about what happens. They do not agree about what to call it.
- e.g. A dandelion or tridax head is a wide ball on a dry afternoon and a closed brush on a wet morning.
- e.g. Pine cones open their scales in dry weather and close them in wet weather.
- e.g. Many trees with winged fruits hold them for weeks and lose most of them on a few windy days.
- **Scientists who study how plants sense and respond:** The plant senses dry air and strong wind, and lets the seeds go then. That is a decision. A decision does not need a brain. It needs sensing, and an action that depends on what was sensed.
- **Many plant scientists:** The stalk snaps when the wind's pull is stronger than the stalk. The hairs close because wet tissue swells. These are simple physical events. They say the word 'decide' should be kept for animals with brains.
- **A middle view:** The timing was shaped over a very long time, because plants that let go in good winds left more young. Whether you call the result a decision depends on what you mean by the word.

## Mostly agreed
- A winged or tufted seed is carried by moving air. The wing or tuft slows the fall, and gives no forward push. *How we know:* In still air, winged seeds and paper models of them fall straight down. In wind tunnels the sideways distance grows with the wind speed and with the time of the fall.
- A spinning winged seed gets extra lift from a whirl of air on the front edge of the wing. *How we know:* David Lentink's team saw this whirl on model maple seeds in a tank of oil in 2009. They then found the same whirl around real spinning seeds in a wind tunnel.
- Most seeds land close to the parent plant, and a few travel very far. *How we know:* Scientists set out trays to catch seeds at different distances from trees. The trays near the tree catch the most by far. Plants also reach new islands and bare ground, which only far-travelling seeds could do.
- Many seeds survive being eaten, and sprout after passing through an animal. *How we know:* Seeds picked out of the droppings of birds, bats, civets and elephants sprout when they are sown. Charles Darwin grew plants from seeds that he took from bird droppings.
- Chilli, tomato, potato, maize and groundnut came to India from the Americas within the last 500 years. *How we know:* The wild relatives of these crops grow only in the Americas. Indian writings from before 1500 do not mention them. Old remains of these crops from before that time are found only in the Americas.
- A crop with many varieties is safer from disease than a crop with one. *How we know:* The potato blight of the 1840s in Ireland destroyed a crop that was nearly all one variety. In the 1970s, one wild rice from Uttar Pradesh gave the resistance that saved rice crops across Asia from grassy stunt virus.

## How we found out
**Charles Darwin, an English naturalist (1809 to 1882), with questions and doubts from his friend the botanist Joseph Hooker, Darwin did the salt water tests in 1855 and 1856. He described them in his book On the Origin of Species in 1859..** In the 1850s, botanists had a puzzle. The same kinds of plants grew on islands and on mainlands hundreds of kilometres apart. Most botanists were sure that sea water kills seeds. So Edward Forbes and others said that land must once have joined those places, and had since sunk under the sea. Charles Darwin doubted it. Nobody had tested the seeds. In 1855, at his home in England, he filled jars with salt water and put in seeds of 87 kinds of plants. After 28 days he sowed them. Seeds of 64 kinds sprouted. A few kinds still sprouted after 137 days. Then Darwin found a problem. Most fresh seeds sink within days. So he tried dried branches with ripe fruits on them, as a flood might carry. Many floated for weeks. He looked up the speed of Atlantic currents and worked out that seeds could drift about 1,500 kilometres. He also grew 537 seedlings from three spoonfuls of pond mud, of the kind that sticks to a duck's feet. No sunken land was needed.  
*What people believed before:* That sea water quickly kills seeds, so plants could only reach islands across land that has since sunk. Darwin showed that the seeds of most kinds he tested were still alive after four weeks in salt water, and that dried fruits can float for weeks.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Winged seeds fly, in the way that birds do.** (A common way of speaking, found in many children's books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Winged seeds do travel through the air, and some go a long way.  
  Against: A bird pushes air backwards and down with muscles, and so moves forward. A seed has nothing to push with. In still air a winged seed only comes down slowly. The wind does all the sideways carrying.
- **Chilli, tomato and potato have always been part of Indian food.** (A common belief, because these foods are in almost every Indian kitchen today.; *rejected*)  
  For: They have been grown in India for many generations, and many Indian varieties exist nowhere else.  
  Against: All three came from the Americas after 1498. Before that, Indian cooks used black pepper, long pepper, ginger and mustard for heat. A food can be truly part of a place and still have come from somewhere else.
- **Seeds are safest in a frozen seed bank.** (Many governments and crop scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: Dry seeds kept at 18 degrees below zero stay alive for decades. A seed bank can hold a million samples in one building, safe from drought and war. Seeds from the Svalbard vault were used to rebuild a collection that was lost in the war in Syria.  
  Against: Farmers and scientists such as Debal Deb say that frozen seeds stop changing, while the weather, pests and diseases outside keep changing. They also say that the knowledge of how to grow and cook each variety lives with farmers, and is not stored in a vault. Most people who work with seeds now say that both ways are needed.
- **People should move trees to cooler places, because the climate is warming faster than seeds can travel.** (Some forest scientists. They call this assisted migration.; *debated*)  
  For: Many trees disperse seeds only a short way in each generation, and a tree takes many years to make seeds. The places where they can live are moving faster than that.  
  Against: People have moved plants with good intentions before, and some became invasive species. Lantana was brought to India as a garden flower. A moved plant can behave in unexpected ways in a new place.
- **Some large fruits were shaped for giant animals who are now gone.** (Daniel Janzen and Paul Martin, in 1982.; *debated*)  
  For: Some trees in the Americas make large, tough fruits that no wild animal living there now can swallow. The fruits rot under the tree. Giant ground sloths and elephant-like animals lived there until about 12,000 years ago.  
  Against: It is very hard to test what an animal who no longer lives used to eat. Some of these trees are also dispersed by water, by rodents or by people. Many scientists accept the idea for a few trees and doubt it for others.

## Roots
In this lesson you tested paper fliers in the wind of a fan, and followed seeds across seas and kitchens. Here we ask what those journeys show about plants, about the world and about us.

**Deep truth.** In the Fan Lane Test, the packed strip and the spinner were the same paper with the same clip. The fan blew equally on both. The spinner landed far away, because the wings gave the moving air more to push on and more time to push. So a winged seed does not fly. The wing only slows the fall, and the wind does all the carrying.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say that seeds fly and that plants spread, as if they went by their own power. We think that to travel, a being must move themself. A plant travels by being ready for something else that is already moving.  
*Evidence:* In the Fan Lane Test, the design that took longest to fall in still air also landed farthest in the wind. With the fan off, every design landed at your feet. Nothing in the paper pushed forward.

- *who am i:* Your body was built from food. Much of that food came from plants whose seeds crossed oceans in ships and pockets. You are partly made of long journeys that other people and other beings made.
- *what is life:* A plant stays in one spot for a whole life. But each seed is a chance to live somewhere else. So a kind of plant keeps moving across the land, one generation at a time.
- *what is this world:* Wind, rivers, sea currents, birds, bats, ants, elephants and people all carry seeds. The map of where each plant lives was drawn by everything that moves.

A tree is one being, rooted in one place. From that one tree come thousands of seeds, and they go out in every direction. The tree does not do the moving. The Sun warms the air and makes the wind, the rain fills the river, and a bird is hungry. Each of them takes a seed somewhere new. So the one tree becomes many trees only with the help of the whole world around them. And the forest that grows up feeds the birds and holds the rain in return.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** “किमत्र पश्यसीति । न किंचन भगव इति / तं होवाच यं वै सोम्यैतमणिमानं न निभालयस एतस्य वै सोम्यैषोऽणिम्न एवं महान्यग्रोधस्तिष्ठति” Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to bring a fruit of the banyan and break it open. Inside are tiny seeds. He asks him to break one seed, and the boy says he sees nothing inside. Uddalaka says that the great banyan tree grows from that fine essence which he cannot see. (A banyan seed is smaller than a mustard seed, small enough to pass through a bird and be dropped on a far wall. The teaching and the science both look at how something so small can hold a whole tree. The Upanishad is speaking about the self and the world, not about botany.)
- **The Gospel of Matthew 13:3-8, the parable of the sower:** A sower scatters seed. Some falls on the path, and birds eat that seed. Some falls on rock and withers. Some falls among thorns and is choked. Some falls on good soil and gives a large harvest. (This is a seed shadow, told two thousand years ago. Most seeds land in the wrong place, and the few that land well make up for all the rest. The parable is about people who hear a teaching, but the picture of the seeds is exact.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on interbeing:** Thich Nhat Hanh, a Vietnamese Buddhist teacher, wrote that if you look deeply at a sheet of paper you can see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. (Look at a chilli in the same way. In the chilli there is a bird who carried the seed, a ship that crossed an ocean, and a farmer who saved the seed each year for 500 years.)
- Science (settled): A wind-dispersed seed has no source of forward push. The distance travelled comes from the wind speed, the release height and the fall speed.
- Science (debated): Many plants let seeds go mostly in dry, gusty weather, when seeds travel farthest. Whether this should be called a decision is argued about.
- Science (settled): Farmed rice, wheat and maize have lost the ability to scatter their own seeds, and depend on people to sow them.
- Other view, Many plant scientists, and the Charvaka thinkers of ancient India: On this view, a seed on the wind is matter moved by matter. The wing is a shape that happened to leave more young, and was passed on. Dry air and a strong gust are enough to explain when a seed lets go. They would say that words such as 'ready' and 'help' are ours, and that we put them into the story.
- Practice, Let go and watch (3 min): Stand up and hold your paper spinner as high as you can. Take one slow breath in. As you breathe out, open your fingers. Do not throw and do not push. Watch the spinner all the way to the floor. Notice that you did nothing after letting go. Do this three times. Then sit down, and breathe in and out slowly three times. Feel the air move in and out. That is the same air that carried the spinner.
- Ask: A seed goes where the wind goes. When have you been carried somewhere good by something you did not plan?
- Ask: The tomato in your sambar had ancestors in South America. Where does a food come from: where it grows now, or where it began?
- Ask: A tree lets go of ten thousand seeds and never finds out which one lived. Could you make something and let it go like that?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A winged seed has no say in where the wind goes. Is the seed travelling, or being moved? (hint: Think about a person on a bus, and a leaf in a river. What is the difference, if any?)
- *deep:* A mother tree makes ten thousand seeds and perhaps one becomes a tree. Were the others wasted? (hint: Think about who ate them, and what would happen if the tree made only one seed.)
- *deep:* Farmed paddy needs people to sow the seed. People need paddy to eat. Who domesticated whom? (hint: Think about how many paddy plants there were before farming, and how many there are now.)
- *big:* The chilli came to India 500 years ago. Is the chilli an Indian plant now? How long does a plant need to belong? (hint: Think about tamarind, which came thousands of years ago, and lantana, which came 200 years ago.)
- *big:* A company can own a new variety of seed. Farmers bred the old varieties over thousands of years. Who should own seeds? (hint: Think about who did the work, who needs the seed, and what happens if only a few people own all of it.)
- *big:* A seed vault keeps seeds for a future nobody can see. What else should people keep safe for those who are not born yet? (hint: Think about languages, forests, songs and clean water.)

## Story: The Black Rock
*Tunga, Kiran, Doddamma and their village are made up, and so is the fire. These things are real. The honne tree, also called Indian kino or vengai, grows in the forests of Uttara Kannada in Karnataka. Each pod is flat and round, about as wide as a biscuit, with one seed in the middle and a papery wing all round. The pods ripen in the dry season and are carried by the wind. Hill slopes in these forests do burn in the dry months, and wind-carried seeds are among the first to arrive on burnt ground. Doddamma means a mother's elder sister in Kannada.*

The fire came in February, on the far side of the stream. It took one night to climb the hill from the water to the ridge. In the morning the slope across from Tunga's house was black. By March it was the colour of a brick, with nothing on it but stones.

"It will be green again," said Doddamma. She was Tunga's mother's elder sister, and she had walked that forest as a watcher for nineteen years. "Grass in two years. Trees later."

"How much later?"

"After my time, perhaps." She said it without any sadness, and went on shelling beans.

Tunga did not want to wait until after anyone's time. On their own side of the stream stood the honne, the tallest tree in the village, older than the house. In March the pods were ripe. Each one was flat and brown and no bigger than a biscuit, with a hard bump in the middle where the seed lay, and a papery frill all the way round.

She filled a basket with them. Then she took her cousin Kiran, who was seven and came whether he was asked or not, and went down to the bank.

Nobody could cross. The stream ran in a deep cut there, and Doddamma had forbidden the burnt hill in any case. The ground was loose, she said, and a slope with no roots in it slides.

So Tunga threw. She threw the first pod as hard as she could. It went up, turned on its edge, and fluttered down three steps from her feet.

Kiran laughed and threw one like a cricket ball. His came down two steps away.

They threw until their shoulders ached. The bank around them was brown with pods, and not one had reached the water.

"They are too light," said Kiran. "Make them heavy." He pressed five pods into a lump of wet clay from the bank and threw that. It sailed over the stream and hit the red slope with a smack.

They made twenty clay balls before dark, and nineteen of them crossed.

The next morning Tunga looked at the hill for a long time. She could see every ball, because each had left a pale mark. The marks made one crooked line along the very bottom of the slope, just above the water. That was as far as a boy's arm could send a lump of clay. Above the line, the hill went up and up, higher than ten temple towers, and there was nothing on it at all.

In the afternoon she sat under the honne with the empty basket and was cross with everything.

The wind came up the valley, as it did every afternoon in March, hot and steady from behind her. High in the tree, something let go.

She watched it because there was nothing else to watch. It was a pod from the topmost branch, and it did not drop. It turned over and over, slowly, and as it turned it slid away sideways on the wind. It was still in the air when it passed over the cowshed. It came down in the lane.

Tunga got up and walked from the trunk to the lane, counting. Forty-one steps.

Then she walked back and looked under the tree. Around the roots, the pods from the low branches lay in a heap, one on top of another.

The same pods. The same wind. She looked up at the top branch, and then at the lowest one, which she could nearly touch.

The tree had not thrown anything. The tree had only let go, from high up, when the wind was blowing. And the pod had taken a long time to come down.

That was the whole of it, she thought. A long time. A pod she threw was on the ground again in one breath. The pod from the top had stayed up while she could have counted to twelve, and the wind had been carrying it for all twelve.

Above the house the goat path climbed to the Black Rock, a ledge that stuck out over the valley. It was three times as high as the honne. From there the burnt hill lay across from you and a little below.

The next day she carried a full basket up, with Kiran behind her complaining about his legs. They reached the ledge at noon. The air was dead still. She held one pod out and let it go, and it tumbled straight down onto the rocks under their feet.

"See?" said Kiran. "You have to throw it."

"No. We have to wait."

They ate their rotti. They watched a kite circle. A little after three, the dry grass at the edge of the ledge began to lean.

Tunga laid one pod flat on her open palm and lifted her hand above her head. She did nothing else. The wind took the pod off her hand.

It went out over the stream, turning. It kept going. It grew small, and then smaller, a brown fleck against the red, and she lost sight of it before it ever came down.

For once Kiran said nothing. Then he said, "Give me some."

"Don't throw."

"I know," he said, and opened his hand.

They let the whole basket go, a few pods at a time, whenever the grass leaned. Some dropped into the stream. Some swung round and landed in their own trees. Most of them they could not follow at all.

Doddamma heard about it at supper. "Most will be eaten," she said. "Beetles. Mice. Most of the rest will dry up on the stones."

"I know."

"Then why carry them all that way?"

"The tree does the same," said Tunga.

Doddamma looked out at the honne for a while. Then she went back to her plate.

The rains came in June. By August the far hill had a green haze on it, and from the ledge Tunga could not tell what was grass and what was not.

The next March, Doddamma crossed the stream lower down, on her rounds, and climbed the burnt hill with a stick. She came home at dusk and put a leaf on the table. It was a small leaf made of several leaflets, and each leaflet had a tiny notch at the tip.

"Honne," she said. "High up, near the ridge. No ball of clay ever got there."

"How many?"

"I found four. I did not look everywhere." She sat down. "They may be yours. They may be the old tree's own. The wind does not sign its work."

Tunga tried to remember how many pods the basket had held. Hundreds. She pressed the leaf between two pages of her science notebook. Then she went outside to see whether the grass had started to lean.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Tunga tries to get seeds across the stream by throwing harder, and then by making them heavier. Both fail, because strength and weight are the wrong things to change. A honne pod travels because the papery wing makes the fall slow, and the wind carries the pod for as long as the fall lasts. So what matters is how high the pod starts and whether the wind is blowing. The clay balls crossed the stream, but they fell fast, so they reached only the bottom of the hill. The pods released from the high ledge in the afternoon wind reached the top. The story never says the words drag or fall speed. Let your child find the idea in the pod from the top branch.

- Ask: Tunga threw a pod as hard as she could and it landed three steps away. Why did throwing hard not help?
- Ask: The clay balls crossed the stream. Why was Tunga still not happy with them?
- Ask: The pod from the top branch landed forty-one steps away, and the pods from the low branches lay in a heap. What made the difference?
- Ask: On the ledge at noon, the pod fell straight down. What were they waiting for, and why?
- Ask: Doddamma said that most of the seeds would be eaten or dry up. Was it still worth doing? What would you have done?

### e1 · The Fan Lane Test: same paper, same weight, three shapes. Which travels farthest in the same wind? (45 min)
- **question:** Three fliers weigh the same and meet the same wind. Does the shape change how far they travel? Does the one that falls slowest also travel farthest?
- **predict:** Guess before any drop. Which flier will take longest to fall in still air? Which will land farthest from the fan, and about how far? Why do you think so? Write your guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** All three fliers are cut from the same sheet, to the same size, and each carries one paper clip of the same size. So they weigh the same, and only the shape differs. Each one is let go from the same spot, with no push, in the same wind. You repeat every drop, because moving air is never perfectly steady. A fan's wind is narrow and gets weaker farther away. So this is a model of a windy day, and not a copy of one.
- **safety:** Keep fingers, hair and paper away from the fan blades. A grown-up holds the fliers up for the 2-metre drops. Nobody stands on a chair alone.
- **record:** Fill in one row of the table for each flier. Work out the mean fall time and the mean distance. Lay out a thread for each mean distance, or draw a bar chart of the three. Then write three sentences: what I thought would happen, what I saw, and why I think it happened.
- **results table:** Flier | Fall time in still air: tries 1, 2 and 3 (seconds) | Mean fall time (seconds) | Distance in the fan lane: tries 1 to 5 (cm) | Mean distance (cm) | Farthest landing (cm)
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut three strips from one sheet. Each strip is 21 centimetres long and 5 centimetres wide. 2. Long wings: cut a slit 9 centimetres long down the middle of one end. Just below the slit, cut in 1.5 centimetres from each side. Fold the two sides of the lower part in, to make a narrow stem. Fold the end of the stem up by 2 centimetres and slide a paper clip on. Fold one wing towards you and one away. 3. Short wings: make a second spinner in the same way. Then fold each wing back on itself, so each wing is half as long. Press the folds hard. 4. Packed: fold the third strip in half three times. Hold the packet shut with a paper clip.  
  *Parent says:* We are making three paper models of seeds. Each has the same paper and the same clip. What is the same about them? What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Cut three strips of the same size. Make one spinner with long wings. Make one with short wings. Fold the third strip into a small packet. Put one clip on each.
  - Ask: All three have the same paper and the same clip. So what can we say about their weight? (good: They weigh the same.; Only the shape is different.)
  - Note: The paper clip stands for the seed, and the paper for the fruit round the seed. Check that the two wings of each spinner are folded opposite ways, like a letter T seen from the side.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the three fliers in a row. 2. Ask the questions below. 3. Write down each guess and each reason. Do not say who is right.  
  *Parent says:* Which one will take longest to fall? Which will land farthest from the fan? About how far? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your three fliers. Which will fall slowest? Which will land farthest from the fan? Say why. Write your guesses.
  - Ask: Will the fan push harder on one of them? Why? (good: Yes, on the long wings, because they are wider.; No, the fan blows the same on all of them.; The heavy-looking packet will not be moved much.)
  - Note: Many children say the packed strip is heavier. It is not. Do not correct this now. The question at the end comes back to it.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off every fan and shut the windows. 2. Mark a height of 2 metres on a door frame. 3. A grown-up holds each flier at the mark and lets go. 4. Your child starts the stopwatch at the letting go and stops it at the landing. 5. Do three drops for each flier and write every time.  
  *Parent says:* First we test in still air. I will let go at 2 metres. Start the stopwatch when I let go. Stop it when the flier lands.  
  *Child mode:* All fans are off. A grown-up drops each flier from 2 metres. You time the fall. Do three drops for each. Write every time.
  - Ask: Where did each flier land in still air: under the hand, or somewhere else? (good: Right under the hand.; Very close to the wall. None of them went sideways.)
  - Note: The packed strip falls in about 0.6 seconds, which is hard to time by hand. A hand-timed fall can easily be 0.2 seconds out. That is why you take three tries. The long wings usually take 1.5 to 2 seconds. If a spinner flutters and does not spin, check the wing folds. If neither spinner spins, add a second clip to all three fliers.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the fan on a table at one end of the clear floor. Set it to full speed. Stop it turning from side to side. 2. Hold a hand 50 centimetres in front of the fan, level with the middle of the fan. Put a tape mark on the floor right under your hand. This is the start mark. 3. Let each flier go from that spot, 5 times each. Do not push. 4. A helper watches where the flier first touches the floor, and marks the spot. 5. Measure from the start mark to each landing spot.  
  *Parent says:* Now the wind. Hold the flier in front of the fan and just open your fingers. I will mark where it first touches the floor.  
  *Child mode:* Switch on the fan. Hold a flier in the wind. Open your fingers. Do not push. Mark where the flier lands. Measure from the start mark. Do 5 drops for each.
  - Ask: The landing spots of one flier are not all the same. Why not? (good: The wind is not perfectly steady.; I may have let go a little differently each time.)
  - Note: In the fan's wind a spinner may tumble at first and spin only later. Count every drop anyway. The packed strip usually lands within about 50 centimetres. The long wings often land 1 to 2 metres away, and sometimes more. Measure to where the flier first touched down, not to where the flier slid to.
  - Check: In the Fan Lane Test, why is every flier let go from the same spot, with no push? → **So that only the shape differs**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each flier, add the three fall times and divide by 3. 2. Add the five distances and divide by 5. 3. Write the fliers in order of fall time. Write them in order of distance. 4. Cut a piece of thread as long as each mean distance. Lay the three threads side by side.  
  *Parent says:* The mean is the typical result: add the numbers, then divide by how many there are. Work out the mean fall time and the mean distance for each flier. Put them in order both ways. Are the two orders the same?  
  *Child mode:* Find the mean fall time for each flier. Find the mean distance. Put the fliers in order of time. Put them in order of distance. Are the orders the same?
  - Note: A worked example: distances of 150, 190, 160, 210 and 140 centimetres add up to 850. Then 850 ÷ 5 = 170 centimetres. A calculator is fine.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch the fan off. Drop the long-wing spinner once more from the same spot, and see where it lands. 2. Ask your child what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why. 3. Only then read the note below together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Then say why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Switch the fan off. Drop the long wings again. Where do they land now? Say what you thought. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Ask: All three weigh the same, and the fan blew the same on all three. So why did they land in different places? (good: The wings give the air more to push on.; The long wings fell slowly, so the wind had longer to carry them.; The packed strip fell before the wind could move it far.)
  - Ask: With the fan off, the long wings landed at the start mark. So what do the wings do, and what does the wind do? (good: The wings slow the fall.; The wind does the carrying.)
  - Note: Most families find that the flier with the longest fall time also travels farthest. Write down what you really found, even if it differs. The reason has two parts with one cause. A wing gives the air a wide surface to push on, for the same weight. So the fall is slow, and the wind has more time to carry the flier. The wind also brings a broad, light flier up to the wind's own speed quickly. The wing itself gives no forward push. With the fan off, every flier lands at your feet.
  - Think: A tree is ten times as tall as your drop. If the wind blew all the way down, about how much farther would a spinner from the treetop go?
### e2 · The Seed Shadow Test: where do 30 drops of the same spinner land? (25 min)
- **question:** When the same spinner is let go 30 times in the same wind, do all 30 land in the same place? If not, what is the shape of the pattern?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. Will all 30 drops land in about the same place? Point to where you expect the 30 landing spots to be. Why do you think so? Write your reason.
- **fair test:** The same spinner is used for all 30 drops. The same person lets go, from the same spot, with no push. The fan stays at one speed and points one way. Nothing is changed on purpose, so any spread in the landings comes from the small changes in the moving air and in the letting go.
- **safety:** Keep fingers, hair and paper away from the fan blades.
- **record:** Make a tally for six bands: 0 to 50 centimetres, 50 to 100, 100 to 150, 150 to 200, 200 to 250, and beyond 250. Count the landings in each band. Build or draw a bar chart with one bar for each band. Write which band had the most landings, and how far the farthest drop went.
- **results table:** Distance band | Tally of landings | Number of landings out of 30
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set up the fan and the start mark as in the Fan Lane Test: fan on full speed, start mark on the floor 50 centimetres in front of it. 2. Mark a line across the floor every 50 centimetres, up to 250 centimetres. 3. Ask for the prediction and the reason. Your child points to where on the floor they expect the 30 landings to be.  
  *Parent says:* We will let the same spinner go 30 times. Nothing will change. Will all 30 land in the same place? Point to where you think they will land. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Mark a line every 50 centimetres. You will drop one spinner 30 times. Will all the drops land together? Point to where you expect them to land. Say why.
  - Ask: If nothing is changed, why might the landings still differ? (good: The wind from a fan wobbles a little.; My fingers do not let go in exactly the same way.)
  - Note: Some children expect all 30 in one spot, because nothing is changed. Others expect an even spread. Do not correct either guess.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lets the spinner go from the start spot, with no push. 2. A helper calls out the band where the spinner first touches the floor. 3. Make one tally mark for that band. 4. Do this 30 times.  
  *Parent says:* Just open your fingers each time. I will call the band. You make the tally mark.  
  *Child mode:* Let the spinner go. Do not push. See which band the spinner lands in. Make a tally mark. Do this 30 times.
  - Note: If the spinner hits a wall or a chair, do that drop again. If the wings go soft, press the folds flat again. Thirty drops take about ten minutes.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the tally marks in each band. 2. Build a bar for each band: put one dal grain or coin in a column for each landing. 3. Find the band with the most landings, and the farthest landing of all.  
  *Parent says:* Count each band and build the bars. Which band has the most? How far did the farthest drop go?  
  *Child mode:* Count your tally marks. Build a bar for each band. Use one dal grain or coin for each landing. Which band has the most? How far was the farthest drop?
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they expected, what the chart shows, and why. 2. Then read the note below together. 3. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you expected. Say what the chart shows. Why do you think the drops did not all land together?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you expected. Say what your chart shows. Say why the drops spread out.
  - Ask: Suppose only the ground beyond 200 centimetres is good for growing. How many of your 30 seeds reached that ground? (good: Only two or three.; None this time, but one came close.)
  - Ask: A tree lets go ten thousand seeds, not thirty. What does that do to the number that go very far? (good: Many more of them will go very far.; Even a rare far trip will happen many times.)
  - Note: Most families find the landings bunched in two or three bands, with a few drops well short or well beyond. A fan is much steadier than the wind outdoors, so the spread in a real seed shadow is far wider. To see this, do 30 more drops with the fan turning from side to side. Now the wind comes in gusts, and some drops get almost none.
  - Check: A tree's seed shadow is the pattern of where the tree's seeds land. What does a seed shadow usually look like? → **Most seeds near, a few far**
  - Think: The farthest drop is rare. Why might that one seed matter more to the tree than the twenty that landed together?
### e3 · Darwin's Jar: do seeds still sprout after a day and a night in sea water? (35 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Does a day and a night in sea water kill seeds? Which kitchen seeds and fruits could float across water at all?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. Out of 10 moong seeds soaked in salt water, how many will sprout? Out of 10 soaked in plain water, how many? Why do you think so? Write both numbers and your reasons.
- **fair test:** Both lots of seeds come from the same packet. Both soak for the same time, and then sit on the same kind of damp cloth in the same place. Only one thing differs: salt in the soaking water. Ten seeds in each lot is a small number, so a difference of one or two seeds could be chance.
- **safety:** Nobody drinks the salt water. Throw away the seeds soaked in salt water at the end, and do not eat them.
- **record:** Fill in the table for both lots on day 3 and day 4. Write which things floated in plain water and which floated in salt water. Then write three sentences: what I thought would happen, what I saw, and why I think it happened.
- **results table:** Lot | Seeds at the start | Sprouted on day 3 | Sprouted on day 4 | What the sprouts look like
- **schedule:** day 1: Predict. Do the float check. Put 10 moong in salt water and 10 in plain water. Label both. (15 min); day 2: After about 24 hours, rinse both lots well. Put each lot on a damp cloth on a labelled plate. (8 min); day 3: Count the seeds with a root showing in each lot. Keep the cloths damp. (4 min); day 4: Count again. Write a conclusion. (8 min)
- **s1 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir one and a half level teaspoons of salt into one tumbler of water. Label it SALT. Label the other tumbler PLAIN. 2. Float check: drop the groundnut, chilli, cardamom, coriander, rajma and coir into the plain water. Write which float. Then try them in the salt water. 3. Take them out. Ask for the prediction. 4. Put 10 moong seeds in each tumbler.  
  *Parent says:* This salt water is about as salty as the sea. Which of these will float? Now the moong. How many of 10 will sprout after a day in sea water? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make salt water in one tumbler. Test which things float. Then put 10 moong in each tumbler. Guess how many will sprout. Say why.
  - Ask: What do the floaters have that the sinkers do not? (good: Air inside.; A dry, light covering.; A shell with a gap between the shell and the seed.)
  - Note: Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in each litre. A level teaspoon of salt is about 5 grams, so one and a half teaspoons in 200 millilitres is close. Groundnuts in the shell, dry chillies, cardamom pods, coriander and coir usually float. Rajma and moong sink. A thing that only just sinks in plain water may float in salt water, because salt water is denser.
  - Wait: Leave both tumblers for about 24 hours
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour away the water from both tumblers. 2. Rinse each lot of seeds three times in fresh water. 3. Spread each lot on a damp cloth on a plate. Label the plates. 4. Look closely at both lots and write what you notice.  
  *Parent says:* Rinse both lots well. Look at them side by side. Do the two lots look the same? What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Pour the water away. Rinse both lots three times. Put each lot on a damp cloth. Label the plates. Do they look the same?
  - Ask: Which lot of seeds has swollen more? Why might that be? (good: The plain water lot is fatter.; Perhaps salt water does not go into a seed so easily.)
  - Note: The seeds from plain water are usually much more swollen. Water moves into a seed less easily from salt water. Keep both cloths damp but not flooded. Keep the plates out of direct sun.
  - Wait: Look again tomorrow
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the seeds in each lot that have a root showing. 2. Write both numbers. 3. Sprinkle water on both cloths.  
  *Parent says:* Count the seeds with a root showing in each lot. Which lot is ahead?  
  *Child mode:* Count the seeds with a root in each lot. Write the numbers. Sprinkle water on both cloths.
  - Wait: Count once more tomorrow
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the sprouted seeds in each lot again. 2. Ask your child what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why. 3. Then read the note below together.  
  *Parent says:* Count once more. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Did the sea water kill the seeds?  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouts again. Say what you thought. Say what you saw. Did the salt water kill the seeds?
  - Ask: Moong seeds sink. So even if they live through sea water, could moong cross a sea alone? What else would a seed need? (good: No, they would sink to the bottom.; The seed would need to float, or ride on something that floats.)
  - Note: We expect that many of the salt water seeds still sprout, often a day later than the others. But this test has not been tried in every kitchen, so write down what you really find. If none of the salt lot sprout, that is a real result too. Darwin soaked seeds for 28 days, and most kinds still sprouted. He then met the same problem as you: most seeds sink. So he tried whole dried branches with ripe fruits, and many of those floated for weeks.
  - Think: Darwin's friends were sure that sea water kills seeds. Nobody had tried it. What else do people say that nobody has tested?

### a1 · Read the Shape: how would these kitchen seeds travel with no person to carry them? (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting. Wash hands after touching the chilli, and keep fingers away from eyes.
- **record:** Make a table with five columns: the fruit or seed, my guess, the drop test, the float test, and how I now think the seeds travel. Look at the drumstick seed's wings. Draw them, or tell your grown-up what you see.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the fruits and seeds on a plate. 2. Your child looks at each one closely, without cutting. 3. Ask the questions below and write each guess with its reason.  
  *Parent says:* No plant can walk. Imagine no person carries these seeds. How would each one get away from the parent plant? What makes you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each fruit closely. No person will carry these seeds. How would each one travel? Say what makes you think so.
  - Ask: Which of these do you think the wind could carry? What would a wind seed need? (good: None of them, they are all too heavy.; A wind seed would need to be very light.; It would need a wing or fluff.)
  - Note: Take any guess that comes with a reason. Children often say none of these can go by wind, because the whole fruits are heavy. The drumstick seed inside will surprise them.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**

Drop each one from above your head and watch the fall. Put each one in the tumbler of water. Rub each one on a sleeve and see what clings. 3. Write what you see.

  - Ask: The drumstick seed has three thin wings. What do you think they are for? (good: To catch the wind.; To make the seed fall slowly.)
  - Note: In a young cooking drumstick the wings are soft, white and damp, and the seed will not fall slowly. Dry one seed in the sun for a day and try again. In a ripe brown pod the wings are papery. Coconut husk and groundnut shells float. Tamarind seeds and tomato seeds sink.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the test results with the first guesses. 2. Look at the table of what each shape shows. 3. Ask the questions below and the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which guesses do you still agree with? Which have you changed? What was your evidence?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your guesses again. Which ones would you change now? Say what you saw that changed your mind.
  - Ask: The groundnut ripened under the soil, beside the parent. Is that a bad plan for a plant? Why, or why not? (good: The seeds are hidden from birds.; The place was good enough for the parent.; It could be bad, because the young are crowded.)
  - Ask: All of these are crops. Who carries their seeds today? (good: People.; Farmers and shopkeepers, and us.)
  - Note: The shape is good evidence, but it is not proof. Scientists check by watching what really happens to the seeds. The groundnut shows that not every plant sends the seeds far.
  - Check: A seed is small and light, with a thin papery wing. What is the best evidence that the wind carries seeds like this? → **The seed falls slowly when dropped**
### a2 · The Curl Test: how can a dry pod throw seeds with no muscles? (20 min, 3 days)
- **record:** Take a photo of the paper strip curled on the water, or draw it, and mark the wet side. Put the day 1 and day 3 photos of the pod half side by side. Write one sentence saying what drying did to the shape.
- **schedule:** day 1: Do the paper strip test. Split the pods, take out the seeds, draw one pod half, and put the halves on a sunny windowsill. (15 min); day 3: Look at the dried pod halves. Draw one again and compare the two drawings. (5 min)
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what will happen when a paper strip is laid flat on water. Write the guess and the reason. 2. Lay one strip gently on the water, so that only the lower side gets wet. 3. Watch for ten seconds. 4. Do it again with a new strip.  
  *Parent says:* What will this strip do when one side gets wet? Why do you think so? Now lay it flat on the water and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Guess what a paper strip will do on water. Say why. Lay the strip flat on the water. Watch for ten seconds.
  - Ask: The strip curled up off the water. Which side got longer, the wet side or the dry side? (good: The wet side. It is on the outside of the curve.; The wet side swelled.)
  - Note: The strip curls upward within a few seconds. Paper fibres swell when wet, so the wet side grows a little longer than the dry side. A strip with one long side and one short side has to bend. After a minute the water soaks through, and the strip goes flat again.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Split each pod along the seam and take out the seeds. 2. Your child takes a photo of one pod half, or draws it. 3. Ask what shape the halves will have when they are dry, and why. 4. Put the halves on a plate on a sunny windowsill.  
  *Parent says:* A pod wall has layers, like the two sides of the paper strip. What will happen to these halves as they dry? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Split the pods. Take out the seeds. Take a photo of one pod half, or draw it. Guess what shape the halves will have when dry. Put them in the sun.
  - Ask: If one layer of the pod wall shrinks more than the other as it dries, what must the wall do? (good: Bend or curl, like the paper.; Twist.)
  - Note: The seeds can go into dinner. Do not explain yet what will happen.
  - Wait: Leave the pod halves in the sun for 2 days
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the dry halves. 2. Your child takes a photo of the same half again and puts it beside the first photo. 3. Ask what they see and why they think it happened. Then read the note below together.  
  *Parent says:* What shape are the halves now? Why do you think a flat pod wall dried into that shape?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the dry pod halves. Photograph the same half again. Put the two photos side by side. What changed? Say why you think that happened.
  - Ask: On the plant, the two halves are joined along a seam while they dry. What happens when the seam finally gives way? (good: The halves spring apart and twist.; The seeds are flicked out.)
  - Note: Many pod halves twist into a loose spiral, and some only curl a little. Write down what yours did. The wall of a bean pod has two layers of stiff fibres that lie at a slant to each other. As they dry, each layer shrinks in a different direction, so the wall twists. Shop beans may twist less than wild ones. For thousands of years farmers have chosen plants whose pods stay shut, so that the seeds do not spill before the harvest.
  - Check: A dry pod bursts and throws the seeds. Where did the energy for the throw come from? → **Strain built up as the pod dried**

### a5 · The Variety Count: how many kinds of rice, banana and mango does your family know? (25 min)

  - Note: A variety is one kind within a crop. Ponni, sona masuri, basmati, matta and idli rice are varieties of rice. Poovan, nendran, rasthali and robusta are bananas. Banganapalli, alphonso, neelam and langra are mangoes.

### c1 · The Slow Fall Challenge: design a paper seed that takes the longest to fall 2 metres (30 min)
- **problem:** From one small piece of paper, build a model seed that carries a paper clip and falls 2 metres as slowly as possible.
- **your turn:** Design your own paper seed and predict the fall time. Test it three times and find the mean. Did it beat the long-wing spinner? Then let it go in front of the fan. Did the slowest faller also travel farthest?

That is about a quarter of a notebook page.; The model must carry one paper clip, which stands for the seed.; You may use scissors, 20 centimetres of sticky tape, and four pieces of thread, each 15 centimetres long.; The model is let go from 2 metres by opening the fingers. No throwing and no pushing.

- **creative:** A spinner with three wings, like a drumstick seed or the fruit of the hiptage vine.; A single curved wing with the paper clip at one end, like a mahogany seed.; A wide, thin wing swept back at the tips, like the gliding seed of the Javan cucumber vine.; A paper disc cut into a fringe of thin strips all round, like the hairs on a tridax fruit.
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look up the long-wing spinner's mean fall time from the Fan Lane Test, or time it now. 2. Ask your child what makes a thing fall slowly. 3. Your child draws a design, or tells you the plan, and writes a predicted fall time.  
  *Parent says:* The long-wing spinner took nearly 2 seconds to fall 2 metres. What made the fall slow? Draw a design, or tell me your plan, that could be even slower. How long will yours take?  
  *Child mode:* What makes a thing fall slowly? Draw your own paper seed, or tell your plan. Guess how long your seed will take to fall 2 metres.
  - Ask: You cannot change the weight of the paper clip. So what can you change? (good: How wide the paper spreads.; The shape, so that it spins or glides.; Where the clip sits.)
- **s2 (13 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Child mode:* Build your paper seed. Try one drop. Watch what happens. Change one thing. Try again.

  - Note: Engineers copy these shapes. Around 1905, Igo Etrich in Austria built gliders shaped like the gliding seed of the Javan cucumber vine. In 2021 and 2022, two teams in the United States built tiny sensors shaped like spinning seeds and dandelion fruits. The sensors are dropped from the air and drift down slowly over a wide area.
### c2 · The Hitch-hiker Challenge: design a seed that rides on a sock, and gets off again (30 min)
- **problem:** Build a model seed round a rajma bean that catches on a passing sock, stays on while you walk, and can be pulled off by one firm rub.
- **your turn:** Design your own hitch-hiker and predict how many steps it will stay on. Test it. Then explain how your seed would get off the animal in the end. A seed that never comes off never reaches the soil.
- **constraints:** The rajma bean is the seed. The bean must stay whole and must be inside or on the model.; The model must be no longer than your thumb.; It must catch when a sock brushes past once. Nobody may press it on by hand.; It must stay on for 20 steps, and come off with one firm rub against a table leg.
- **general:** Hooks: thin strips of paper or tape with the tips bent over, stuck all round the bean.; Spikes: broom bristles taped to the bean so that they point outwards.; Stickiness: a ring of tape round the bean with the sticky side facing out.
- **creative:** Barbs that all point backwards, so that the seed slides into the wool easily but will not slide out.; A great many very small hooks in place of a few big ones, as on a burr.; A seed with sharp points on every side, so that one point always faces up, like the thorny fruit called nerunjil or gokhru.; A long springy tail that wraps round a thread of the sock.
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask how a seed could hold on to fur with no hands. 2. Your child draws a design round a rajma bean, or tells you the plan. 3. They predict how many steps it will stay on.  
  *Parent says:* Some seeds travel on the fur of animals. How could a seed hold on? Draw your design, or tell me your plan. How many steps will it stay on?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your design round a rajma bean, or tell your plan. Guess how many steps it will stay on.
  - Ask: Wool is made of many thin loops and hairs. What shape catches in a loop? (good: A hook.; A barb that points backwards.; Something sticky.)
- **s2 (13 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child builds the model. 2. Lay it on the floor. Brush the socked foot past once. 3. If it catches, walk 20 steps. 4. Change one thing and test again.  
  *Parent says:* Build it and lay it on the floor. Brush your sock past once. Did it catch? Walk 20 steps. Change one thing and try again.  
  *Child mode:* Build your seed. Put the seed on the floor. Brush your sock past once. Walk 20 steps. Change one thing and try again.
  - Note: A grown-up handles the broom bristles and toothpicks if they are sharp. Most first models bounce off the sock. Smaller hooks, and more of them, usually work better than a few big ones.

## Worksheets
### Wings, Water and Carriers (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (match) Match each feature of a fruit or seed to the right carrier.  
   **Answer:** A thin papery wing → wind; A thick husk full of air → the sea; Sweet, bright flesh → an animal who eats fruit; Tiny hooks → an animal's fur; A small oily food parcel → ants; A pod that curls as it dries → the parent plant's own pod. *Why:* Each feature solves one problem: staying in the air, staying afloat, being eaten, or holding on. A strong answer says what the feature does, as well as who carries the seed.
2. (mcq) Why does a seedling often do badly right under the parent plant?  
   Options: The parent's leaves shade the ground / The soil there has no water at all / Seeds need wind to sprout / The parent pushes seedlings away  
   **Answer:** The parent's leaves shade the ground. *Why:* A seedling under the parent must share light, water and space with a much bigger plant. Enemies of that kind of plant also gather there. Choosing 'seeds need wind to sprout' mixes up travelling with sprouting.
3. (fill) The push of air against a moving thing is called air resistance, or ____.  
   **Answer:** ['drag']. *Why:* Drag pushes against anything that moves through air. It gets bigger as the thing goes faster and as the thing gets wider.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a winged seed pushes forward through the air with the wing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A seed has no muscles and no engine. The wing slows the fall, and the wind carries the seed sideways. In still air a winged seed comes straight down.
5. (sort) Sort these by what usually carries them: wind, water or animals.  
   **Answer:** tridax fruit → Wind; coconut → Water; mango → Animals; mahogany seed → Wind; mangrove seedling → Water; banyan fig → Animals; drumstick seed → Wind; spear grass seed → Animals. *Why:* Wings and hairs suit wind. Trapped air and a waterproof coat suit water. Flesh to eat, or hooks to cling, suit animals. A strong answer names the feature for one in each group.
6. (label) Draw a paper spinner falling. Label: wing, paper clip, weight arrow, drag arrow, direction of spin.  
   **Answer:** ['Wing: the two flat blades at the top', 'Paper clip: at the bottom of the stem, standing for the seed', 'Weight arrow: points straight down', 'Drag arrow: points straight up, from the wings', 'Direction of spin: a curved arrow round the stem']. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the weight arrow and the drag arrow the same length, because at a steady fall speed the two are equal.
7. (mcq) In the Fan Lane Test, three fliers weigh the same. Which one usually lands farthest from the fan?  
   Options: The long-wing spinner / The short-wing spinner / The packed strip / All land together  
   **Answer:** The long-wing spinner. *Why:* The long wings give the air the most to push on, so the fall is slowest and the wind carries that flier longest. Choosing 'all land together' means thinking that only weight matters.
8. (order) A bulbul eats a berry. Put what happens to the seed in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The bulbul swallows the berry whole', 'The soft flesh is digested', 'The bulbul flies to another perch', 'The seed leaves in a dropping', 'The seed sprouts in a new place']. *Why:* The flesh is the bird's food, and the hard seed passes through whole. The flying in between is what moves the seed. A strong answer says why the seed is not digested.
9. (write) The long-wing spinner and the packed strip weighed the same. Explain why the spinner landed farther from the fan.  
   **Answer:** The wings gave the air a wide surface to push on. So the spinner fell slowly, and the fan's wind had more time to carry the spinner sideways. The packed strip fell fast and reached the floor before the wind could move it far.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the wind did the carrying, and that the wing only made the fall slower. It does not say that the wing pushed the spinner forward.
10. (odd) Three of these are carried by the wind. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Tridax fruit / Orchid seed / Calotropis seed / Coconut  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Tridax and calotropis have hairs, and orchid seeds are as fine as dust, so wind carries all three. A coconut is far too heavy for wind, and floats on the sea.
11. (truefalse) True or false: most of the seeds of a tree land close to that tree.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Most seeds leave on an ordinary breeze, or simply drop. Only a few meet a strong gust, a flood or a far-flying animal. The pattern of landings is called the seed shadow.
12. (mcq) A fruit on the plant is green, hard and sour. What does that tell an animal?  
   Options: The seeds are not ready yet / The fruit is poisonous for ever / The fruit has no seeds / The plant is ill  
   **Answer:** The seeds are not ready yet. *Why:* An unripe fruit keeps eaters away until the seeds inside are ready to travel. Then the fruit turns soft, sweet and bright. Choosing 'poisonous for ever' misses that the same fruit will change when ripe.
13. (fill) Chilli, tomato and potato were carried to India by people from the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['Americas', 'America', 'South America', 'Central and South America']. *Why:* These crops were first farmed in Central and South America. Ships brought them to India after 1498.
14. (write) Give two reasons why farmers should keep growing many varieties of one crop.  
   **Answer:** First, if a disease or pest attacks, some varieties may resist it, so not everything is lost. Second, different varieties suit different places and weather, such as floods, salty soil or dry years.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two different reasons. Taste, special uses and festivals are also good reasons.
15. (mcq) What does a seed bank do?  
   Options: Keeps many kinds of seeds alive / Sells seeds at the lowest price / Turns old seeds into new kinds / Stops seeds from ever sprouting  
   **Answer:** Keeps many kinds of seeds alive. *Why:* A seed bank stores dry seeds, usually in the cold, so that they stay alive for decades. Choosing 'stops seeds from ever sprouting' misses the point: the seeds are kept so that they can be grown again.
16. (draw) Draw a tree from above. Add 20 dots to show where 20 of the tree's seeds land.  
   **Answer:** Most of the dots are close to the tree. A few are farther away, and one or two are far away, perhaps to one side where the wind blew.. *Why:* A strong drawing does not spread the dots evenly. It shows many near and few far, which is the usual shape of a seed shadow.
### Fall Speed, Far Trips and Farmed Seeds (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A seed falls for 10 seconds in a wind of 4 metres each second. How far sideways does the seed go?  
   Options: 40 metres / 14 metres / 4 metres / 400 metres  
   **Answer:** 40 metres. *Why:* Distance is about the time in the air multiplied by the wind speed. 10 × 4 = 40 metres. Choosing 14 means adding the numbers when they should be multiplied.
2. (write) Half of a seed's wing is torn off. What happens to the fall speed and to the distance travelled? Say why.  
   **Answer:** The seed falls faster, because a smaller wing gives the air less to push on. So the seed spends less time in the air, and the wind carries the seed a shorter distance.. *Why:* A strong answer links three things in a chain: less wing, faster fall, less time for the wind to carry.
3. (sort) Sort these: does it help a wind-carried seed go far, or not?  
   **Answer:** a slow fall → Helps the seed go far; a tall parent tree → Helps the seed go far; a strong, dry wind → Helps the seed go far; a heavy food store → Does not help; leaving on a calm, damp day → Does not help; a thick, heavy shell → Does not help. *Why:* A wind seed goes far when the fall is slow, the start is high and the wind is strong. Extra weight makes the fall faster. A strong answer explains one from each group.
4. (fill) A falling seed stops speeding up when the drag is equal to the seed's ____.  
   **Answer:** ['weight']. *Why:* Weight pulls down and drag pushes up. When they are equal, they cancel, and the seed falls at a steady speed.
5. (mcq) Why do birds eat hot chillies that mice and rats avoid?  
   Options: Birds do not sense the hot chemical / Birds like pain / Birds have no tongue / Chillies are only hot at night  
   **Answer:** Birds do not sense the hot chemical. *Why:* The hot chemical, capsaicin, burns the mouths of mammals. Birds do not sense it. Birds also swallow the seeds whole, while mice chew them. Choosing 'birds like pain' gives the bird a feeling that the bird does not have here.
6. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how wild rice became farmed paddy.  
   **Answer:** ['Wild rice drops ripe grains one by one', 'People gather the grains still on the stalks', 'People sow some of those grains', 'Each year more plants hold on to their grain', 'Farmed paddy holds every grain until threshed']. *Why:* Nobody planned the change. People could only harvest grains that had not fallen, so those were the ones that got sown. A strong answer says why the falling grains were left out.
7. (truefalse) True or false: a coconut in the husk sinks in sea water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The thick husk is full of air between the fibres, and the skin keeps water out. A coconut can float for more than three months.
8. (mcq) The tuft of hairs on a dandelion fruit is mostly gaps. Why does the tuft still slow the fall so well?  
   Options: Air is slowed around every thin hair / The hairs flap like wings / The gaps are filled with glue / The hairs are very heavy  
   **Answer:** Air is slowed around every thin hair. *Why:* Air close to each thin hair is slowed down. The slowed air round one hair overlaps with that of the next, so the tuft acts almost like a solid disc. Choosing 'flap like wings' gives the fruit muscles.
9. (write) A mangrove seed sprouts while still on the mother tree. How might that help in tidal mud?  
   **Answer:** The seedling drops with a root end ready to grow. So the seedling can take hold of the mud quickly, before the next tide washes the seedling away. A seedling that does not root can float off and try somewhere else.. *Why:* A strong answer names the problem, which is that the tide moves everything twice a day, and then says how a ready-grown seedling solves that problem.
10. (odd) Three of these crops came to India from the Americas. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Chilli / Tomato / Potato / Black pepper  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Black pepper is a vine of the Western Ghats and has grown in India for thousands of years. Chilli, tomato and potato came by ship after 1498.
11. (match) Match each way of travelling to one risk that goes with it.  
   **Answer:** On the wind → most seeds land in a poor place; On the sea → the seed may never reach land; Inside an animal → the seed may be chewed and killed; On an animal's fur → the seed may never drop off; Thrown by the pod → the seed goes only a few metres. *Why:* Every way of travelling has a cost. A strong answer can add the gain for each one too, such as a long distance or a heap of manure.
12. (mcq) The same spinner is let go 30 times in front of a fan. Why do the landings spread out?  
   Options: Moving air is never perfectly steady / The spinner gets heavier each time / The floor tilts / The spinner aims at different spots  
   **Answer:** Moving air is never perfectly steady. *Why:* Even a fan gives small gusts and lulls, and no two releases are exactly alike. Outdoors the wind changes far more. Choosing 'aims' gives the paper a plan.
13. (write) Acorns fall a few metres from an oak. Yet oaks once spread 1,000 kilometres in a few thousand years. Explain how.  
   **Answer:** A few acorns travel much farther than the rest. Birds such as jays carry acorns for kilometres and bury them, and forget some. Those rare long trips, repeated over many years, moved the oaks far faster than falling acorns could.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the usual short trip cannot explain the spread, and that rare long trips can.
14. (truefalse) True or false: farmed maize can scatter seeds without any help from people.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The kernels are fixed to the cob, and the cob is wrapped in tight leaves. Unless someone opens the cob and sows the kernels, they have no way to spread.
15. (perspective) A hill slope has burnt bare. Two people suggest how to bring the trees back.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees the trade-off. Planting fast foreign trees covers the soil quickly, but those trees may spread and crowd out local plants. Waiting for wind and birds brings the local plants back, but slowly, and rain may wash soil away in the meantime. Many foresters now plant or protect local trees first.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. It depends on how steep the slope is, how close the nearest forest is, and how long people can wait.
16. (draw) Design a fruit for a plant that lives on a riverbank. Label the features that help the seeds travel by water.  
   **Answer:** The fruit has a light covering with air spaces, a waterproof skin and a tough coat round the seed. It may be shaped to float upright or to lodge in mud.. *Why:* A strong drawing labels at least two features and says what each does: one for floating, and one for keeping the seed dry or safe.
### How Seed Scientists Argue (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (perspective) Two people disagree about the best way to keep old rice varieties alive.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that each way protects against a different danger. A cold seed bank protects against war, drought and neglect, and holds huge numbers. Growing in fields keeps the varieties changing with the weather and pests, and keeps the knowledge of how to grow and cook them. Most seed scientists and seed savers now say that both are needed.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. The best answers name one thing that each way can do and the other cannot.
2. (perspective) The climate is warming. Should people carry the seeds of wild trees to cooler places?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer weighs the two risks against each other. If people do nothing, some trees may die out, because their seeds travel too slowly. If people move them, a tree may spread too well in the new place, or bring a disease with it. A careful answer may suggest moving trees only short distances, and watching them closely.. *Why:* Scientists really do disagree about this. A good answer gives a reason and admits a risk.
3. (write) You find a fruit covered in tiny hooks. Is that proof that animals carry it? How would you check?  
   **Answer:** No. The hooks are good evidence, but not proof. To check, I would watch the plant and see what happens to the fruits. I could look at the fur of animals that pass, or drag a cloth through the plants and see if the fruits catch.. *Why:* A strong answer separates evidence from proof, and names a way to watch what really happens.
4. (write) Plan a fair test: does a higher start carry a spinner farther? Say what you change, keep and measure.  
   **Answer:** I change only the height of the start. I keep the same spinner, the same wind and the same way of letting go. I measure how far from the start mark the spinner lands, several times at each height. I need a wind that blows over the whole fall, such as a steady breeze outdoors, because a fan's wind is too narrow.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing, repeats each drop, and notices that a fan cannot give wind over a tall drop.
5. (mcq) With the same food, a plant can make 20 big seeds or 20,000 tiny ones. Which statement is true?  
   Options: Each way suits different places / Big seeds are always better / Tiny seeds are always better / The number of seeds never matters  
   **Answer:** Each way suits different places. *Why:* A big seed gives a seedling a strong start in shade or dry soil, but few are made and they do not go far. Tiny seeds go far and are many, but each needs a perfect spot. Choosing 'always better' misses the cost on the other side.
6. (write) A dandelion shuts the hairs in damp air and opens them in dry air. Is that deciding? Argue both sides.  
   **Answer:** For: the plant senses how damp the air is and acts differently because of it, which is what deciding means. Against: wet tissue swells and pulls the hairs shut, and some scientists say that the word 'decide' should be kept for animals with brains.. *Why:* A strong answer gives both sides fairly and says what the argument turns on: what we mean by the word 'decide'.
7. (mcq) Seed A falls at 1 metre each second and seed B at 4, from one tree. Which is true?  
   Options: A goes about 4 times as far / B goes about 4 times as far / Both go the same distance / A goes about 20 times as far  
   **Answer:** A goes about 4 times as far. *Why:* From a tree 20 metres tall, seed A is in the air for 20 seconds and seed B for 5 seconds. The wind carries A for four times as long, so A goes about four times as far. Choosing B means thinking that a faster fall is a faster journey.
8. (truefalse) True or false: one seed in ten thousand that travels very far is too rare to matter to a kind of plant.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A tree makes many thousands of seeds each year, so rare trips happen again and again. Those few seeds are how a kind of plant reaches new islands, new valleys and ground left bare by a fire.
9. (mcq) Seedlings close to a parent tree of the same kind often die more than seedlings far away. What is one main reason?  
   Options: Enemies of that kind gather there / The parent tree poisons them on purpose / Seedlings need to be lonely / The ground near a tree is always dry  
   **Answer:** Enemies of that kind gather there. *Why:* The enemies of one kind of plant are most common where that plant is common, which is round the parent. Seedlings there also get little light. Choosing 'poisons them on purpose' gives a reason that scientists have not found for most trees.
10. (match) Match each observation to what it is evidence for.  
   **Answer:** With the fan off, a spinner lands at your feet → a wing gives no forward push; Seeds from bird droppings sprout → seeds can survive being eaten; Wild relatives of chilli grow only in the Americas → chilli came from the Americas; Seeds sprout after four weeks in salt water → seeds could cross a sea alive; Trays near a tree catch the most seeds → most seeds land near the parent. *Why:* A claim in science rests on something that someone saw or measured. A strong answer can say why each observation points to its claim and not to another.
11. (sort) Sort these: is it settled, or do scientists still argue about it?  
   **Answer:** The wind carries winged seeds → Settled; Chilli came from the Americas → Settled; Many seeds survive an animal's gut → Settled; Whether a plant's timing counts as deciding → Still argued about; Whether to move trees to cooler places → Still argued about; Whether giant animals now gone shaped some fruits → Still argued about. *Why:* Science has both. A strong answer can say what kind of evidence would be needed to settle one of the open ones.
12. (write) Farmed paddy no longer scatters grain. For the paddy plant, is that a loss or a gain? Give your reason.  
   **Answer:** It can be argued both ways. It is a loss, because paddy now depends wholly on people and would die out without them. It is a gain, because people sow paddy on a huge part of the Earth's land, and there are far more paddy plants than there ever were wild rice plants.. *Why:* A strong answer picks a side or holds both, and gives a reason drawn from what the plant gets and what the plant gives up.
13. (draw) Draw the bar chart you expect for a real tree's seed shadow, from 0 to 500 metres. Mark the far seeds.  
   **Answer:** The bars are tall near 0 metres and drop quickly. Beyond about 100 metres the bars are very low, but they do not reach zero. A few seeds are marked far to the right.. *Why:* A strong drawing has a long, low tail to the right. It marks that tail as the part that is hardest to measure and matters most for reaching new places.
14. (mcq) Lantana was brought to India as a garden flower and now covers forests. What does this show about moving plants?  
   Options: A moved plant can behave in unexpected ways / Every foreign plant is harmful / Garden plants never spread / Birds refuse to eat foreign berries  
   **Answer:** A moved plant can behave in unexpected ways. *Why:* Lantana found birds to carry the berries and few enemies to eat the leaves. Nobody expected that. But chilli and tomato were moved too, and did no such harm. Choosing 'every foreign plant is harmful' goes further than the evidence.
15. (write) A seed takes 30 seconds to fall. The wind blows at 3 metres each second. How far does the seed go?  
   **Answer:** The seed goes 30 × 3 = 90 metres. Now suppose rising air holds the seed up for one minute more. That adds 60 × 3 = 180 metres, and the total is 270 metres, three times as far.. *Why:* A strong answer multiplies the time by the wind speed. A very strong answer adds that anything which keeps the seed up longer, such as rising air, adds a great deal of distance.
16. (truefalse) True or false: the shape of a fruit is proof of how the seeds travel.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A shape is good evidence, but scientists check by watching what really happens. Some fruits travel in two ways, and some shapes may have suited animals who are no longer alive.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a reason why a seed often does better away from the parent?  
   Options: Less competition for light and water / Seeds dislike their parents / Far soil is always richer / The parent stops making seeds  
   **Answer:** Less competition for light and water. *Why:* Away from the parent, a seedling shares light, water and space with fewer plants of the same kind, and meets fewer of their enemies. Far soil is not always richer. Most seeds that travel land somewhere poor.
2. (mcq) A fruit has a waterproof skin and a thick, light husk full of air. How do the seeds most likely travel?  
   Options: By water / By wind / On an animal's fur / By being thrown  
   **Answer:** By water. *Why:* Trapped air makes the fruit float, and the waterproof skin keeps the seed dry. That is the coconut's design. The fruit is far too heavy for wind.
3. (mcq) A seed carries a small oily food parcel on the outside. Who most likely carries this seed?  
   Options: Ants / The sea / The wind / Elephants  
   **Answer:** Ants. *Why:* Ants carry the whole seed to the nest, eat the parcel, and leave the seed in their rubbish heap. The parcel is too small to pay a large animal.
4. (mcq) Two seeds weigh the same, and one has a wing. Why does the winged seed fall more slowly?  
   Options: The wing meets more air resistance / The wing makes the seed lighter / The wing flaps / Gravity pulls the winged seed less  
   **Answer:** The wing meets more air resistance. *Why:* The wing adds surface and almost no weight. With more surface, drag matches the weight at a lower speed. Gravity pulls both seeds equally, because they weigh the same.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a falling seed keeps getting faster all the way to the ground.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Drag grows as the speed grows. When drag equals weight, the seed stops speeding up and falls at a steady speed. A winged seed reaches that speed within a metre or two.
6. (mcq) On a day with no wind at all, where does a winged seed from a tall tree land?  
   Options: Almost straight below / Far away, because of the wing / Higher up the tree / In the next field  
   **Answer:** Almost straight below. *Why:* A wing gives no forward push. With no moving air, the seed only comes down slowly. Choosing 'far away' treats the wing as an engine.
7. (mcq) Which change would carry a winged seed the farthest?  
   Options: A higher start in a stronger wind / A heavier seed / A shorter wing / Leaving on a calm day  
   **Answer:** A higher start in a stronger wind. *Why:* Distance is about the time in the air multiplied by the wind speed. A higher start gives more time, and a stronger wind gives more speed. A heavier seed or a shorter wing makes the fall faster.
8. (mcq) In a wind test, why do you let each flier go five times and take the mean?  
   Options: One drop could be a fluke / The paper needs to warm up / Five is a lucky number / The fan tires after one drop  
   **Answer:** One drop could be a fluke. *Why:* Moving air is never perfectly steady, so single drops differ. The mean of several drops is a fairer picture than any one of them.
9. (mcq) Which of these describes a seed shadow?  
   Options: Many seeds near, and a few far away / The shade under a seed / Seeds that grow only in the dark / The same number of seeds at every distance  
   **Answer:** Many seeds near, and a few far away. *Why:* A seed shadow is the pattern of where a plant's seeds land. Most land near, and a few go far. Those few are how plants reach new places.
10. (mcq) Which of these shows that a crop has come to depend on people?  
   Options: Paddy holds the grain until threshed / Mango trees give shade / Coconuts float on the sea / Tridax grows by the road  
   **Answer:** Paddy holds the grain until threshed. *Why:* Wild rice drops ripe grains. Farmed paddy holds them, so the grain is sown only when a person sows it. Coconuts and tridax still travel without us.
11. (mcq) Which of these foods was carried to India from the Americas?  
   Options: Tomato / Mango / Black pepper / Turmeric  
   **Answer:** Tomato. *Why:* Tomato was first farmed in Central and South America and came to India by ship after 1498. Mango, pepper and turmeric are from South Asia.
12. (mcq) Why is a field planted with one single variety risky?  
   Options: One disease could kill the whole crop / The plants grow too tall / Birds refuse to visit / The seeds forget how to sprout  
   **Answer:** One disease could kill the whole crop. *Why:* Plants of one variety are nearly the same as each other. A disease that can attack one can attack them all. With many varieties, some are likely to resist.
13. (truefalse) True or false: seeds kept in a cold seed bank go on changing to suit the weather outside.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Seeds in a cold store stay as they were when they went in. Only plants that are grown year after year can change with the weather and pests. That is why many people say both ways of saving are needed.

## Applications
- Foresters collect winged seeds in the dry season and sow them on bare slopes, timing the work to the winds and the first rains.
- Engineers have built tiny sensors shaped like spinning seeds and dandelion fruits. Dropped from the air, they drift down slowly and spread over a wide area.
- Velcro, the fastener on shoes and bags, copies the tiny hooks of a burr.
- Early glider builders copied the wide, swept-back wing of the gliding seed of the Javan cucumber vine.
- Plant inspectors at ports and airports check grain, soil and luggage, to stop the seeds of new weeds from entering a country.
- Seed banks send stored seeds to farmers and plant breeders when a new disease appears, so that resistant varieties can be found.
- Conservation teams protect hornbills, fruit bats and elephants partly because many forest trees depend on them to carry seeds.
- Mangrove planters along the coasts of Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Bengal collect floating seedlings and push them into the mud, to protect the shore from storms.

- Lantana bushes with pink and orange flowers along a forest road, with bulbuls eating the dark berries.

## Open questions
- **How far do seeds really travel?** The seeds that go farthest are the rarest, perhaps one in many thousands. They are almost impossible to find. So the most important part of a seed shadow is the part scientists can measure least well.
- **Can trees move fast enough to keep up with a warming climate?** The places that suit each tree are shifting by kilometres in a human lifetime. Nobody knows which trees can keep up through rare long trips, and which will be left behind.
- **How does a plant sense that the wind is right for letting seeds go?** Scientists know that many plants release seeds mostly in dry gusts. For most plants they do not yet know which part senses the dryness and the pull, or how.
- **Which fruits were shaped by animals who are no longer alive?** Some large fruits have no carrier today. They may have been eaten by giant animals who died out thousands of years ago. It is very hard to test what those animals ate.
- **How many seeds do people carry by accident every day?** Seeds ride on tyres, shoes, clothes, ships and aeroplanes. A few studies have counted seeds in road tunnels and on boots, but nobody knows the total for the world.

## Connected maths
- In one Fan Lane Test, the long-wing spinner landed 150, 190, 160, 210 and 140 centimetres from the start mark. What was the mean distance? **170 centimetres.**. Add the five distances: 150 + 190 + 160 + 210 + 140 = 850. Divide by the number of drops: 850 ÷ 5 = 170 centimetres. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the same test, the long-wing spinner's mean distance was 170 centimetres and the packed strip's was 25 centimetres. About how many times as far did the spinner go? **About 7 times as far.**. 170 ÷ 25 = 6.8, which is close to 7. Both fliers weighed the same, so the shape alone made the spinner travel about 7 times as far. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A winged seed leaves the top of a tree 25 metres tall and falls at a steady 1 metre each second. The wind blows at 4 metres each second. About how far from the tree does the seed land? **About 100 metres.**. The fall takes 25 ÷ 1 = 25 seconds. In each second the wind carries the seed 4 metres. 25 × 4 = 100 metres. (queues `math.big-ideas.change-and-motion.L5`)
- Seed A falls at 1 metre each second and seed B at 5 metres each second. Both leave a branch 20 metres up, in a wind of 3 metres each second. How much farther does seed A travel? **48 metres farther.**. Seed A falls for 20 ÷ 1 = 20 seconds and travels 20 × 3 = 60 metres. Seed B falls for 20 ÷ 5 = 4 seconds and travels 4 × 3 = 12 metres. 60 − 12 = 48 metres. Seed A goes 5 times as far, because the fall lasts 5 times as long. (queues `math.maths-and-world.maths-and-science.L5`)
- In a Seed Shadow Test, 2 of 30 drops landed beyond 2 metres. A real tree makes 15,000 seeds. If the same share went beyond the usual distance, how many seeds would that be? **1,000 seeds.**. 2 out of 30 is the same as 1 out of 15. 15,000 ÷ 15 = 1,000. So a share that looks tiny in 30 drops becomes a thousand seeds for a real tree. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- Charles Darwin soaked the seeds of 87 kinds of plants in salt water for 28 days. Seeds of 64 kinds still sprouted. What percent of the kinds survived? **About 74 percent.**. 64 ÷ 87 = 0.735 and a little more. As a percent that is about 74. So nearly three kinds in every four survived four weeks in sea water. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A coconut can float for 110 days and still sprout. A sea current carries it at 1 kilometre each hour. How far could it go? The Lakshadweep islands are about 400 kilometres from Kochi. Is that within reach? **About 2,640 kilometres. Yes, Lakshadweep is well within reach.**. In one day the current carries the coconut 24 × 1 = 24 kilometres. In 110 days that is 110 × 24 = 2,640 kilometres. That is more than six times the distance from Kochi to Lakshadweep. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L5`)
- In Seed Keeper, 7 of your 10 tomato seeds sprouted. You want 21 tomato plants. About how many seeds should you sow? **30 seeds.**. 7 out of 10 seeds are alive. To get 21 plants you need 3 lots of 7, because 21 ÷ 7 = 3. So you sow 3 lots of 10, which is 30 seeds. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)

## Warm-up
