# Weight, mass and the normal force: what pulls a thing down and what holds it up
`sci.gravity.weight-and-mass.L5`

**Summary.** On Jupiter you would weigh about two and a half times as much as on Earth. But you could not stand anywhere to feel it, because Jupiter has no solid ground. Weight is the pull of a world's gravity on a thing, in newtons. Mass is the amount of matter in the thing, in kilograms, and it stays the same on every world. A surface holds a thing up with a normal force, and that force is not always as big as the weight. Your child squashes dough in a pile of books to find where the push is biggest. They also work out weights on other worlds, and draw gravity, normal force and applied force as arrows.

**Intro.** *Put eight books in a pile on a table. Which book is pushed up the hardest by what lies under it: the top book, a middle book or the bottom book? Say what you think, and why.* It is hard to tell by looking, because nothing in the pile moves. In the Dough Stack experiment, you put soft dough at three levels of the pile. Then you see which dough is squashed the most. First, in Be the Table, your own hand holds up a book and feels what a table has to do. In this unit you find out what pulls a thing down, what holds it up, and how hard each one acts. You also find out why your weight would change on another world while your mass would not.

## Terms
- **Mass**: Mass is the amount of matter in a thing. It is measured in kilograms and grams. The mass of a thing is the same on every world. *e.g.* A 1 kilogram packet of rice has a mass of 1 kilogram in Kochi, on the Moon and on Mars.
- **Weight**: Weight is the pull of gravity on a thing. It is a force, so it is measured in newtons. The weight of a thing changes from world to world. *e.g.* A 1 kilogram packet of rice weighs about 10 newtons on Earth and about 1.6 newtons on the Moon.
- **Newton**: A newton is the unit of force. On Earth, gravity pulls 100 grams with a force of about 1 newton. *e.g.* A small apple, or 100 millilitres of water, weighs about 1 newton on Earth.
- **Gravity**: Gravity is the force with which every mass attracts every other mass. The Earth's gravity pulls everything towards the centre of the Earth. *e.g.* A dropped spoon falls towards the centre of the Earth, whether you drop it in Chennai or in Shillong.
- **Strength of gravity**: The strength of gravity at a place is the pull on each kilogram there, in newtons for each kilogram. It is about 9.8 on Earth and 1.6 on the Moon. *e.g.* On Mars the strength of gravity is about 3.7 newtons for each kilogram, so a 10 kilogram bag weighs 37 newtons.
- **Non-contact force**: A non-contact force acts between things that do not touch. Gravity is a non-contact force. *e.g.* The Earth pulls a mango while the mango is still falling through the air.
- **Contact force**: A contact force acts only while two things touch. The normal force and applied forces are contact forces. *e.g.* The push of your hand on a door stops the moment your hand leaves the door.
- **Normal force**: The normal force is the push of a surface on a thing that presses against it. It points straight out from the surface. *e.g.* A table pushes up on a steel tumbler with a normal force. A wall pushes sideways on a hand that leans on it.
- **At right angles**: Two lines are at right angles when they meet like the corner of a book. In maths, 'normal' means at right angles. That is where the normal force gets its name. *e.g.* On a sloping board, the normal force leans with the board. It still points straight out of the board's surface.
- **Applied force**: An applied force is a push or pull on a thing by someone or something that touches it. A person, an animal or a machine can be the one who pushes or pulls. *e.g.* A bullock pulling a cart, a hand pressing a rolling pin and a crane lifting a beam all apply forces.
- **Force diagram**: A force diagram is a simple drawing of one thing, with an arrow for each force on it. Each arrow points the way the force acts. A bigger force gets a longer arrow. *e.g.* A book on a table gets two arrows of equal length. Gravity points down and the normal force points up.
- **Balanced forces**: Forces on a thing are balanced when they cancel each other. A thing that stays still has balanced forces on it. *e.g.* A bucket standing on the floor is still. The floor's push up is as big as the pull of gravity down.
- **Load**: A load is the weight that a surface or a support has to hold up. *e.g.* The bottom book in a pile of eight carries a load of seven books.
- **Compress**: To compress a thing is to squash it, so that its parts are pushed closer together. A compressed thing pushes back. *e.g.* A sofa cushion is compressed when you sit on it, and it pushes up on you.
- **Inertia**: Inertia is how hard it is to change the movement of a thing. More mass means more inertia. Inertia does not depend on gravity. *e.g.* A full water can is harder to start swinging than an empty one, and harder to stop.
- **Gas giant**: A gas giant is a very large planet made mostly of hydrogen and helium. It has no solid surface to stand on. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants. *e.g.* A spacecraft that drops into Jupiter keeps sinking through thicker and thicker gas.

## Explanations
### Mass and weight are different · *defined*
**Definition:** Mass is the amount of matter in a thing, in kilograms. Weight is the pull of gravity on that thing, in newtons.

People often use the two words for the same thing, and shops call the mass printed on a packet its 'net weight'. In science they are two different quantities. Mass belongs to the thing. A packet of rice has the same grains, and so the same mass, wherever you take it. Weight is a force between the thing and a world. It depends on how strongly that world pulls. To find a weight, multiply the mass by the strength of gravity. On Earth that strength is about 9.8 newtons for each kilogram, and for quick sums we use 10. So 1 kilogram weighs about 10 newtons, and 100 grams weighs about 1 newton. Mass also shows up in a second way. A thing with more mass is harder to start moving and harder to stop. This is called inertia, and it is the same on every world.
- e.g. A 3 kilogram school bag weighs about 30 newtons on Earth. On the Moon its mass is still 3 kilograms, and it weighs about 5 newtons.
- e.g. In Hit the Bottles, a string held up each bottle's weight. The full bottle was still much harder to hit away than the nearly empty one.
- e.g. An Apollo astronaut with suit and backpack had a mass of about 150 kilograms. On the Moon he felt light, but he was still slow to start, stop and turn.
### Gravity pulls towards the centre of the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Gravity is a non-contact force. The Earth's gravity pulls every thing towards the centre of the Earth, whether the thing is still or moving.

Gravity needs no touching. It pulls a book on a table, a book in your hand and a book in mid-air equally hard. Nothing can switch it off, and no wall or roof blocks it. 'Down' means towards the centre of the Earth. So down in Chennai and down in Peru, on the other side of the Earth, point in nearly opposite ways. In a force diagram, the gravity arrow starts at the middle of the thing and points straight down. Gravity pulls both ways. The Earth pulls you, and you pull the Earth just as hard. The Earth has so much mass that your pull does not move it in any way we can measure.
- e.g. A plumb line, a stone on a thread, hangs along the line that points to the centre of the Earth. Masons use one to build a wall straight up.
- e.g. A ball thrown upward slows down, stops and comes back. Gravity pulled it down all the way up and all the way down.
- e.g. The Moon is 384,000 kilometres away, and the Earth's gravity still pulls it. That pull keeps the Moon in its orbit.
### Weight on the Moon and the planets · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing's weight on any world is its mass multiplied by the strength of gravity there, in newtons for each kilogram.

The strength of gravity is about 1.6 on the Moon. It is 3.7 on Mercury and on Mars, and 8.9 on Venus and on Uranus. It is 9.8 on Earth, 10.4 on Saturn, 11.2 on Neptune and about 25 on Jupiter. For the four giant planets, the number is measured at the cloud tops, because they have no ground. So a child with a mass of 30 kilograms weighs about 300 newtons on Earth. The same child weighs 48 newtons on the Moon, 111 on Mars and 750 on Jupiter. The child has not changed at all. On the Moon the child could jump about six times as high. A thrown ball would also fly about six times as far. Catching that ball would feel the same as on Earth, because its mass is the same.
- e.g. Pragyan, the rover that Chandrayaan-3 carried to the Moon in 2023, has a mass of about 26 kilograms. It weighs about 260 newtons on Earth and about 42 newtons on the Moon.
- e.g. A 1 litre bottle of water weighs about 10 newtons on Earth, 3.7 newtons on Mars and 25 newtons on Jupiter.
- e.g. A two-pan balance gives the same answer on every world, because it compares two masses. A spring scale does not, because it measures a pull.
### A bigger world does not always pull harder · *defined*
**Definition:** The strength of gravity at the surface of a world depends on two things: the mass of the world, and how far the surface is from its centre.

More mass makes the pull stronger. More distance from the centre makes it weaker. At twice the distance, the pull is one quarter as strong. At three times the distance, it is one ninth. In the Spreading Square, the light from a small torch covered 4 times the area at twice the distance. The pull of a world spreads out round it in the same way. This is a model, and Newton showed in 1687 that gravity follows it. To compare a world with the Earth, divide its mass by its width multiplied by its width. Use the Earth as 1 for both. Saturn has 95 times the mass of the Earth, so you might expect a crushing pull. But Saturn is also about 9.5 times as wide, so its cloud tops are very far from its centre. 9.5 times 9.5 is about 90, and 95 divided by 90 is just over 1. So the pull at Saturn's cloud tops is only a little stronger than on Earth. Uranus has 14.5 times the Earth's mass and is 4 times as wide. 14.5 divided by 16 is about 0.9, so Uranus pulls a little less than the Earth.
- e.g. In the Spreading Square, the lit square at 90 centimetres had 9 times the area of the one at 30. At 3 times the distance, each part got one ninth of the light.
- e.g. Jupiter has 318 times the Earth's mass and is about 11.2 times as wide. 318 divided by 125 is about 2.5, so its pull is about two and a half times the Earth's.
- e.g. Mercury and Mars both have a strength of gravity of 3.7. Mars is bigger and has more mass, but its surface is further from its centre.
- e.g. On a high mountain you are a little further from the Earth's centre. So you weigh very slightly less than at the seashore.
### The normal force: the push of a surface · *defined*
**Definition:** The normal force is the push of a surface on a thing that presses against it. It is a contact force, and it points straight out from the surface, at right angles to it.

A book on a table does not fall, though gravity pulls it all the time. So something must push it up. That push is the normal force from the table. Every surface can give one: a floor, a chair, a palm, a wall, the ground. The word 'normal' here is a maths word that means at right angles. It comes from 'norma', the Latin name for a carpenter's square. On a level table the normal force points straight up. On a sloping board it leans with the board. A wall gives a normal force that points sideways, into your hand when you lean on it. A surface can only push. It cannot pull a thing towards itself.
- e.g. You sit on a chair. The seat pushes up on you, and the floor pushes up on your feet. Both are normal forces.
- e.g. A ladder leans on a wall. The wall pushes the top of the ladder sideways, straight out from the wall.
- e.g. A ball in mid-air touches no surface, so no normal force acts on it. Only gravity and the air act on the ball.
### How a surface pushes back by the right amount · *defined*
**Definition:** A surface is compressed or bent by a load, and that makes it push back. It gives way until its push is as big as the force pressing on it.

Sit on a rope cot and the ropes dip. Stretched ropes pull back, like the rubber band in a catapult. The more they dip, the harder they push you up. They stop dipping when their push equals your weight. A heavier person makes them dip further. A cushion, a mattress and a sponge do the same, and you can see it. A table and a stone floor do it too, but they give way far too little for you to see. Underneath, every solid is made of atoms that hold each other at a set distance. Press them closer and they push back with electric forces, the way a compressed spring does. So no surface has to be told how heavy a thing is. The thing sinks in until the forces balance. If the load is too big for the material, the surface breaks.
- e.g. In the Dough Stack, the dough under more books was squashed wider. More load pressed it further before it stopped.
- e.g. A bathroom scale has a stiff metal part inside that bends very slightly when you stand on it. The scale measures that bending.
- e.g. A thin shelf sags under a row of heavy books. A thick shelf sags too, by an amount too small to see.
### The normal force is not always as big as the weight · *defined*
**Definition:** A normal force is as big as it needs to be to stop a thing sinking into the surface. That can be more than the thing's weight, less than it, or nothing.

For one book lying alone on a level table, the normal force equals the book's weight. Many books stop there, but it is only the simplest case. Press down on the book with your hand, and the table must push up against the weight and your push together. Lift the book a little with a string, and the table has less to do. Put the book at the bottom of a pile, and the table pushes it up with the weight of the whole pile. Put the book on a slope, and the normal force is smaller than the weight. The slope only stops the book sinking into it. Friction stops the book sliding down. When you jump, you push down hard on the floor, and the floor pushes you up with more than your weight. A runner's foot gets a push from the ground of two to three times the runner's weight at each step.
- e.g. In the Dough Stack, the table pushed up on the bottom book with the weight of all eight books.
- e.g. In Lift, Press and Tilt, a packet on a kitchen scale read less when a string lifted it. It read more when a finger pressed it down.
- e.g. In Lift, Press and Tilt, the packet read less and less as the board under the scale was tilted more steeply.
- e.g. You hang from a bar with your feet off the floor. The floor's normal force on you is zero.
### Applied forces · *defined*
**Definition:** An applied force is a push or a pull that a person, another animal or a machine puts on a thing. It is a contact force.

You apply a force when you lift a bag or press a rolling pin. You also do it when you pull a drawer, kick a ball or push a swing. A bullock applies a force to a cart, and a crane applies one to a beam. An applied force can point any way: up, down or sideways. It starts when the touching starts and stops when the touching stops. A ball that has left your hand has no force from the hand on it any more. It keeps moving because moving things keep moving. Gravity then curves its path down, and the air slows it. Applied forces also change normal forces. Press down on a table and the floor under the table legs pushes up harder. Lift one end of a bench and the floor under that end pushes less.
- e.g. A person pressing dosa batter out of a ladle, a potter pressing clay and a tailor pulling thread all apply forces.
- e.g. In Be the Table, a grown-up pressed down on the books, and your palm had to push up harder.
- e.g. A cricket ball flying to the boundary has two forces on it: gravity and air resistance. The bat's push ended when the ball left the bat.
### Force diagrams and balanced forces · *defined*
**Definition:** A force diagram shows one thing and every force on it as an arrow. If the thing stays still, the forces on it are balanced.

Draw the thing as a simple box. Draw one arrow for each force, starting from the box and pointing the way the force acts. Make a bigger force a longer arrow, and label each arrow. Only forces on the thing go in the diagram, never the forces it puts on other things. Then check the balance. For a thing that stays still, the up arrows together must be as long as the down arrows together. A 1 kilogram packet on a table has gravity of 10 newtons down and a normal force of 10 newtons up. If a string pulls the packet up with 3 newtons, the normal force shrinks to 7 newtons. 7 and 3 up still balance 10 down. If the forces do not balance, the thing speeds up, slows down or turns.
- e.g. A bag held still in your hand: gravity points down, and the applied force from your hand points up, with the same length.
- e.g. The bottom book of a pile: gravity and the push of the books above point down. One long normal force arrow points up.
- e.g. A ball at the top of a throw: one arrow only, gravity pointing down. So the ball does not stay there.
### Loads are passed down, all the way into the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Each surface in a pile holds up everything above it. So the load grows towards the bottom, and in the end the Earth holds it.

The top book of a pile presses on the second. The second presses on the third with its own weight and the top book's weight. So the load is passed down and grows at each level. The table carries the whole pile and passes that load through its legs to the floor. The floor passes it to the walls and pillars, and they pass it to the ground. The same thing happens inside the Earth. Rock deep down carries all the rock above it. At the centre of the Earth the squeeze is about 3.6 million times the push of the air around you. This sets a limit on mountains. A mountain that is too tall crushes the rock at its own base. On Mars a rock weighs much less, and that is one reason Mars has taller mountains. Its volcano Olympus Mons is about 22 kilometres high, two and a half times the height of Mount Everest. Weight also makes big worlds round. In a world wider than several hundred kilometres, the load is big enough to crush any tall lump down.
- e.g. In the Dough Stack, the dough under eight books was squashed wider than the dough under one book.
- e.g. In the Load on the Pillar, a ground-floor pillar held up six floors. It carried about six times the load of a pillar under the roof.
- e.g. Small asteroids are lumpy, like potatoes. Their weight is too small to crush rock into a ball.
### Worlds with nothing to stand on · *defined*
**Definition:** Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, and Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. None of them has a solid surface, so nothing there gives a normal force to stand on.

Jupiter is mostly hydrogen and helium. What we see in photographs is the top of its clouds. Below the clouds the gas gets thicker, hotter and more squeezed. Deep down the hydrogen is pressed into a liquid. A person there would have weight, because Jupiter's gravity would pull hard. But nothing would push back, so the person would keep sinking. A scale needs both forces. It can only show the push between you and a surface. That is why we give a giant planet's strength of gravity at its cloud tops. The Sun has no surface either. Its strength of gravity at the top of its glowing gas is about 274 newtons for each kilogram.
- e.g. In 1995 the Galileo probe dropped into Jupiter on a parachute. It sank for about an hour and never met a surface.
- e.g. Saturn has less mass in each litre than water has. A piece of Saturn would float in a big enough sea.
- e.g. A person floating in a pool has weight, but the pool floor gives no normal force. The water holds the person up with upthrust.
### Why does mass do two jobs? · *idea*
**Definition:** Mass sets how hard gravity pulls a thing. Mass also sets how hard the thing is to speed up. Scientists have ideas about why the two always match, but no agreed answer.

The two jobs could have been set by two different amounts. A stone might have had a lot of 'gravity mass' and only a little inertia. But every test finds that the two are equal. That is why a heavy and a light thing fall together when no air is in the way. Isaac Newton checked it in the 1680s with two pendulums. He filled their boxes with gold, lead, glass, sand, salt, wood, water and wheat. All of them swung in step, to within one part in a thousand. In 2022 a French satellite called MICROSCOPE reported a far finer test. Two metal cylinders fell round the Earth together, equal to about one part in a thousand million million.
- e.g. A full bottle is pulled ten times as hard as a bottle one tenth full. It is also ten times as hard to speed up. So both fall together.
- e.g. On the Moon in 1971, the astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon's feather. They landed together.
- **Isaac Newton, 1687:** The two kinds of mass are found to be equal by experiment. Newton gave no reason for it.
- **Albert Einstein, 1915:** Gravity is not a pull on each thing. Mass bends space and time, and every falling thing follows the same bent path. So all things must fall alike.
- **Many physicists today:** Einstein's idea fits every test so far. But it has not been joined with the physics of the very small. So the match may yet fail at some very fine level.

## Mostly agreed
- Mass stays the same on every world, and weight changes with the strength of gravity. *How we know:* The rocks that Apollo astronauts weighed on the Moon were weighed again on Earth. Each weight was about six times bigger, and each mass was unchanged.
- The Earth's gravity pulls everything towards the centre of the Earth. *How we know:* Plumb lines hang towards the centre at every place on Earth, and people stand upright all round the globe.
- A surface pushes back on anything that presses on it, with a force at right angles to the surface. *How we know:* Scales and force sensors measure this push directly. Engineers use it to design every floor, bridge and chair.
- Solid surfaces give way a little under every load, even when we cannot see it. *How we know:* Fine instruments measure the tiny bending of floors, bridges and steel beams. A digital scale works by measuring such bending.
- A thing that stays still has balanced forces on it. *How we know:* This follows from Newton's laws of motion, published in 1687. Every building that stands is a test of it.
- Jupiter and Saturn have no solid surface. *How we know:* Their average density is far too low for solid rock. In 1995 the Galileo probe sank into Jupiter for about an hour and met only gas.

## How we found out
**The scientists of the International Bureau of Weights and Measures near Paris, who compared the lumps, and Bryan Kibble, who invented the balance, The lump was chosen in 1889. It was compared with its copies from 1988 to 1992 and in 2014. The kilogram was redefined on 20 May 2019..** In 1889, a small cylinder of platinum and iridium was chosen to be the kilogram. It is about as tall and as wide as a plum, and it had been made in 1879. It is kept in a locked vault near Paris, and people call it Le Grand K. Copies were made and sent to many countries. India's copy is kept at the National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi. Everyone believed that the master lump never changed. A kilogram was the mass of that lump, so it could not be wrong. From 1988 to 1992, and again in 2014, scientists compared the master with its copies. The copies had drifted away from it by about 50 micrograms in a hundred years. That is about the mass of a grain of fine dust. No one could say which lump had changed, because each was only measured against the others. So on 20 May 2019, the kilogram was defined through a number of nature, the Planck constant. The new method uses a Kibble balance, invented by Bryan Kibble in 1975. It balances the weight of a mass against an electric force. Because weight depends on gravity, the balance must also measure the strength of gravity at its place very exactly.  
*What people believed before:* That a master lump of metal never changes, and so can define the kilogram for ever. The comparisons showed the copies and the master drifting apart by about 50 micrograms in a hundred years.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The normal force on a thing is always equal to its weight.** (Many simple school diagrams; *rejected*)  
  For: It is true for one thing resting alone on a level surface, which is the picture most books draw.  
  Against: In the Dough Stack, the table pushed the bottom book up with the weight of eight books. On a slope the normal force is smaller than the weight. In a jump it is bigger.
- **A table cannot push, because it is not alive and has no muscles.** (A common first idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A table does not move or get tired, and we cannot see it doing anything.  
  Against: A book on a table stays still though gravity pulls it. So an upward force must act on the book. Soft surfaces show the cause: they are compressed and push back. Hard surfaces are compressed too, by an amount too small to see.
- **A thrown ball carries the force of the throw along with it.** (John Philoponus in the 500s and Jean Buridan in the 1300s, and most people's first idea; *rejected*)  
  For: The ball keeps moving after it leaves the hand, so it seems that something from the hand must still be pushing it.  
  Against: An applied force is a contact force, so it ends when the hand lets go. A moving thing keeps moving with no push at all. Gravity and the air are the only forces on the ball in flight.
- **A planet with more mass always has stronger gravity at its surface.** (A popular belief; *rejected*)  
  For: More mass does make a stronger pull, and Jupiter, the planet with the most mass, has the strongest pull.  
  Against: The distance from the centre matters too. Saturn has 95 times the Earth's mass. But its cloud tops are so far from its centre that the pull there is about the same as on Earth. Uranus pulls less than the Earth.
- **'Weight' should mean what a scale shows, and not the pull of gravity.** (Some physics teachers and textbook writers; *debated*)  
  For: A scale is how we measure weight in real life. With this meaning, an astronaut in orbit really is weightless, which matches what the astronaut feels.  
  Against: Most textbooks keep 'weight' for the pull of gravity, which is nearly as strong in orbit as on the ground. They use 'normal force' or 'apparent weight' for what the scale shows.
- **Gravity is not really a force.** (Albert Einstein, in his general theory of relativity of 1915; *accepted*)  
  For: Einstein's theory says that mass bends space and time, and that falling things simply follow the bend. It predicts the bending of starlight and the timing of satellite clocks correctly.  
  Against: For everything in this unit, treating gravity as a force gives the right answers. Engineers and space agencies still use Newton's force for almost all their sums.

## Roots
In this unit you squashed dough under a pile of books and saw the load grow towards the bottom. Here we ask what that says about how things stay up, and about us.

**Deep truth.** In the Dough Stack, the dough under eight books was squashed far wider than the dough under one book. Every book rests on the one below, so each level carries everything above it. The bottom book is held up by the table, the table by the floor, and the floor by the Earth. So no book in a pile holds itself up.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a table stands or a book sits, as if each held itself up. Everything that stays up is held by something else, and that is held by something else again.  
*Evidence:* In the Dough Stack, the bottom ball carried the weight of all eight books and the top ball carried one. In Lift, Press and Tilt, the scale pushed up exactly as hard as the packet and the finger pressed on it. In the Load on the Pillar, a ground-floor pillar carried every floor above it.

- *who am i:* You stand because the floor pushes up on you, and the floor is held up by the building and the ground. The whole Earth under you holds you up.
- *what is life:* Every animal that stands, sits or walks is held up by the ground. A tree stands because her roots and trunk push on the ground, and the ground pushes back.
- *what is this world:* In a pile of books, a building or a planet, each part rests on another. The weight of everything above is passed down, and in the end the Earth holds it.

A book, a table, a floor, a pillar and the ground look like separate things. In the Dough Stack and the Load on the Pillar, they work as one chain. Each link pushes up on the link above with the weight that all the links above give it. At the bottom is the Earth, which pulls the whole pile down and also holds it up. Many sages taught that nothing stands alone. A pile of books is one exact case, and you can draw it with arrows.

- **Atharva Veda 12.1.12 (the hymn to the Earth):** The Earth is my mother, and I am her child. (The verse speaks of the Earth as the one who holds us. In this unit, the Earth is the last link in every chain that holds something up. The verse is a statement of faith and care, and the unit does not test it.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 26 (Burton Watson's translation):** A person uses only the small patch of ground under her feet. But if the earth around her were dug away down to the Yellow Springs, that small patch would be no use to her. (A pillar or a foot seems to need only the ground it touches. But the ground there is held up by all the ground round it. The passage says this as a lesson about usefulness, not about weight.)
- Science (settled): A surface pushes back on anything that presses on it, because the atoms of the two touching things repel each other. This push is the normal force.
- Science (open): Nobody knows why the mass that sets a thing's weight is exactly the mass that makes it hard to move.
- Other view, Many physicists: Saying that a thing is held up by something is our way of adding up forces between atoms. For them there is no chain of holders, only a very large number of pushes between neighbouring atoms.
- Practice, Feel your whole weight go down (3 min): Stand still in bare feet. Close your eyes. Feel the soles of your feet pressing on the floor. The floor pushes back with exactly your weight. Think of the floor resting on walls or pillars, and those on the ground. Take five slow breaths, and feel that whole chain holding you. Then open your eyes.
- Ask: The bottom book carries the most, and nobody sees it. What in your home or town works like the bottom book?
- Ask: The Earth holds up the pile, and the pile pulls on the Earth too. Is the Earth holding the pile, or are they holding each other?
- Ask: If no book holds itself up, is it right to say that a book stands on a table?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The Earth pulls a book down, and the Earth, through the table and the floor, holds the book up. Is the Earth doing one thing or two? (hint: Follow the load down from the book, level by level. Where does it end?)
- *deep:* Your weight changes from world to world, and your mass does not. Which of the two tells more about you? (hint: Think about what you would still have with no world near you at all.)

## Story: The Slow Start
*A made-up story. Revathi Chithi, Thenu, Karthik and the cable test are imagined. These things are real. The rover Pragyan has six wheels, a mass of about 26 kilograms, and travelled on the Moon at about 1 centimetre each second. It was carried by Chandrayaan-3, which landed on 23 August 2023. ISRO tested its rover on a copy of Moon soil made from rock from Tamil Nadu. Space agencies do use cables and counterweights to make a machine feel as light on Earth as it would on the Moon. The Moon's strength of gravity is about 1.6 newtons for each kilogram, about one sixth of the Earth's.*

Thenu was ten, and it was her first time inside the satellite centre in Bengaluru. It was Family Day. Her aunt Revathi worked here, and she had promised to show Thenu the rover.

The test hall was as big as a school ground. Half of the floor was a long grey tray, filled with rock powder. "It comes from hills in Tamil Nadu," said Revathi Chithi. "We ground it very fine, so that it behaves like the soil on the Moon."

At the edge of the tray stood a small machine on six wheels, about the size of a big suitcase. Thenu bent down to look at it. "This is Pragyan," said Chithi. "Twenty-six kilograms. It is going to the Moon on Chandrayaan-3."

A young engineer called Karthik was kneeling beside it with a laptop. He had joined the team two months ago. "On the Moon it will weigh six times less," he told Thenu happily. "Twenty-six kilograms will feel like four, as light as a school bag. It will run even better there than here. Watch."

He pressed a key. The six wheels began to turn, and the rover rolled out across the grey powder, with a neat line of tracks behind it. "See?" said Karthik.

Thenu frowned. "But here it is six times heavier than on the Moon," she said. "So how do you know it will run the same way there?"

Karthik opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"That is the right question," said Chithi. "We cannot take the Moon to Bengaluru. So we take the weight away." She pointed up. A strong cable hung from a crane in the roof, with a counterweight on its other end. "This cable will hold up five sixths of the rover's weight. The ground will carry only one sixth, as it will on the Moon."

They rolled the rover onto a flat parcel scale in the floor, and Thenu read it. "Twenty-six kilograms." Then they clipped on the cable and lifted a little. Thenu read the scale again. "Four kilograms," she said.

"The scale shows how hard the ground pushes up," said Chithi. "With the cable helping, the ground pushes only a sixth as hard. A wheel grips by pressing on the ground. A sixth of the press gives a sixth of the grip."

Karthik gave the wheels the same full power as before. This time they spun at once. The powder flew up behind them in grey sprays, and a small hill of dust grew behind each wheel. The rover hardly moved.

"Stop," said Chithi. Karthik stopped it. He looked puzzled. "I thought less weight would make everything easier. It must be the motors. I will turn up the power."

"That will make it worse," said Chithi.

"Easier for some things," she went on. "It is easier to lift. But look at this." She put her hand against the rover, with the cable holding it up, and pushed. It moved slowly. She pushed harder, and it moved a little faster. "Thenu, you try."

Thenu pushed. The rover felt solid and slow, like a full steel almirah on a smooth floor. "It is heavy," she said. "But you said it only weighs four kilograms now."

"Its weight is only a sixth. Its mass is the same," said Chithi. "Mass is how much of it there is, and that is the same on every world. Mass is what makes it hard to start moving. So the motors must still start twenty-six kilograms of rover. But the wheels have only a sixth of the grip to push with."

Karthik sat back on his heels. "So the push from the motors is as big as before, and the grip is smaller. That is why the wheels spin."

"Then what do we change?" asked Thenu.

"We cannot change the Moon," said Chithi, smiling. "We change the start. A wheel will hold, if the push grows slowly enough."

Karthik typed a new line. The motors began to turn very gently, and the push grew a little more every second. The six wheels bit into the powder. The rover rolled forward, slowly, and left a neat line of tracks behind it.

"There," said Chithi. "Slow is the speed at which a wheel holds."

Months later, Thenu sat in front of the television at home on the evening of the landing. Karthik had sent her a message. The lander stood on the Moon, and a ramp came down. Then Pragyan rolled out onto the grey soil.

"It is so slow," said her little brother.

"About one centimetre each second," said Thenu. "That is how fast its wheels can hold the ground."

**For the parent:** For the parent: on the Moon the rover has the same mass but only about one sixth of the weight. So the ground pushes up on its wheels one sixth as hard, and the wheels grip less. The motors still have to move all 26 kilograms of mass, because mass is what makes a thing hard to start moving. Grip is lower and the mass is the same, so a hard start makes the wheels spin. A slow start keeps the wheels holding. A thing that is lighter is not always easier to move.

- Ask: The ground pushed up on each wheel less in the cable test. Why did the wheels grip less?
- Ask: The rover weighs six times less on the Moon. Does its mass change too?
- Ask: Why did a harder push from the motors make the wheels spin more?
- Ask: How did a slow start help the wheels grip?
- Ask: Where have you seen a wheel or a foot slip when someone starts too fast?

### e3 · The Spreading Square: why does a pull get weaker with distance? (30 min)
- **question:** When the screen is twice as far from the light, how much bigger is the lit square? And how bright is it, compared with before?
- **predict:** Guess first. At twice the distance, the lit square could be twice as wide, twice the area or four times the area. Which will it be? Write your guess and your reason. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep the card with the square hole 10 centimetres from the light every time. Change only the distance from the light to the screen. Measure each lit square twice, from the same pencil marks, because the edges are a little fuzzy.
- **safety:** Do not look straight into the light. A grown-up cuts the square hole. Do the test in a room with a curtain, in the evening, with one grown-up beside you.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out each area by multiplying the side by itself. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Distance from light to screen (cm) | Side of the lit square (cm) | Area of the lit square (square cm) | Times the area at 30 cm
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts a 2 centimetre square hole in the middle of the card. 2. Tape the squared paper flat on a wall. 3. Rest the light on a pile of books so it shines at the paper. 4. Stand the card so that its hole is 10 centimetres from the light. 5. Make the room dark.  
  *Parent says:* The light spreads out in all directions. The card with the square hole lets only a square of light through. We will see how big that square is on the screen.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts a square hole in the card. Tape up the paper. Stand the card 10 cm from the light.
  - Note: A phone torch works well, because its light comes from one small point. A torch with a mirror behind the bulb makes a beam that does not spread in the same way. Fix the card so that it cannot move, with sticky tape on a book.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to look at the card and the wall. 2. Ask for a guess about the lit square at twice the distance. 3. Write the guess and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Suppose the lit square is 6 centimetres wide when the screen is 30 centimetres away. How big will it be at 60 centimetres? Twice as wide, twice the area, or four times the area? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how big the square will be at twice the distance. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The square gets wider and also taller, so both sides double.; It doubles in width, and so it must be more than double in area.)
  - Note: Most children say 'twice as big'. Do not correct this. The sides of the square double, but the area is width times height.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the screen 30 centimetres from the light. 2. Mark the 4 corners of the lit square with a pencil. 3. Measure the side of the square. 4. Move the screen to 60 centimetres and mark the corners. 5. Move it to 90 centimetres and mark the corners. 6. Write the three sides in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Measure from the light to the screen with the tape. Mark the four corners of the lit square each time. Measure its side.  
  *Child mode:* Put the screen at 30 cm. Mark the lit square. Do the same at 60 and 90 cm.
  - Note: The edges are fuzzy, so mark where the light is clearly bright. At 30, 60 and 90 centimetres the sides should be about 6, 12 and 18 centimetres. Measure each side twice. If the squares are not square, the card is leaning.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Multiply each side by itself to get the area. 2. Divide each area by the area at 30 centimetres. 3. Write the answers in the table. 4. Look at how bright the square is at each distance.  
  *Parent says:* Work out the area of each square. Then work out how many times bigger each one is than the first. Look at the brightness too.  
  *Child mode:* Multiply each side by itself. Divide by the first area. Write down the answers.
  - Note: Expect the area to be about 4 times bigger at 60 centimetres and about 9 times bigger at 90. The light on the screen should be dimmer in the larger squares, because the same light covers more paper.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for the three sentences. 2. Ask them to say how bright the light is at 60 centimetres, compared with 30. 3. Ask them how this might show what happens to the pull of a world. 4. Only then read the explanation together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. If the same light covers 4 times the area, how bright is each part?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why. How bright is each part?
  - Ask: At twice the distance, how bright is each part of the square? (good: One quarter as bright, because the same light covers 4 times the area.)
  - Ask: The pull of a world also spreads out in all directions. What does that suggest for a thing twice as far from the centre? (good: Its pull would be one quarter as strong. At three times the distance, one ninth.)
  - Note: This is a model. Light and gravity are different things, but anything that spreads out evenly from a point follows the same sums. At twice the distance, the area of a sphere round the point is 4 times as big. Newton showed in 1687 that gravity follows this rule. Do not say that gravity is made of light.
  - Check: In the Spreading Square, the screen moved from 30 to 90 centimetres. The distance was 3 times as big. How much bigger was the lit area? → **9 times**

   **Answer:** A box for the middle book, with an arrow down for gravity of 1 unit, an arrow down for the top book's push of 1 unit, and an arrow up for the bottom book's push of 2 units.. *Why:* A strong drawing has two down arrows and one up arrow. 12. (mcq) The Earth pulls an apple with a force of 1 newton. How hard does the apple pull the Earth?  
   Options: Almost nothing / 1 newton / 10 newtons / 1 newton, only while it falls  
   **Answer:** 1 newton. *Why:* Forces come in pairs. The apple pulls the Earth with the same force of 1 newton. The Earth is so massive that this pull moves it by too little to measure.
13. (write) Many people think the normal force always equals the weight. Describe a home test that shows this is wrong.  
   **Answer:** Put a packet on a kitchen scale and press down on it with a finger. The scale reads more than the packet's weight, so the table's push is bigger than the weight. Lifting the packet with a string makes the reading smaller.. *Why:* A strong answer names a test with a scale, a finger or a string, and says what the reading does. It says why that shows the normal force can change.
14. (order) Put these in order of how hard the floor pushes on a girl, from the smallest to the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['She hangs from a bar with her feet off the floor', 'She stands on a board tilted up at one end', 'She stands still on a level floor', 'She lands hard from a jump']. *Why:* Hanging gives a push of zero. A tilted board gives less than her weight. A level floor gives exactly her weight. A hard landing gives more than her weight.
15. (count) A pillar holds up 60 square metres of floor, each with a mass of 1,000 kilograms. Find the load in newtons.  
   **Answer:** 600000. *Why:* 60 times 1,000 is 60,000 kilograms. Each kilogram weighs about 10 newtons, so 60,000 times 10 is 600,000 newtons.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is the same on every world?  
   Options: Your weight / Your mass / The reading of a spring scale / The pull of gravity on you  
   **Answer:** Your mass. *Why:* Mass is the amount of matter in you, and you take it with you to every world. The other three change with the strength of gravity.
2. (mcq) A bag has a mass of 4 kilograms. About what does it weigh on the Earth?  
   Options: 4 newtons / 40 newtons / 400 newtons / 0.4 newtons  
   **Answer:** 40 newtons. *Why:* Each kilogram weighs about 10 newtons on the Earth. So 4 kilograms weigh about 40 newtons.
3. (mcq) A dog has a mass of 12 kilograms. About what does she weigh on the Moon, where each kilogram weighs 1.6 newtons?  
   Options: About 19 newtons / About 120 newtons / About 7.5 newtons / About 12 newtons  
   **Answer:** About 19 newtons. *Why:* Multiply the mass by the strength of gravity. 12 times 1.6 is 19.2 newtons. Choosing 120 uses the Earth's number instead of the Moon's.
4. (mcq) Why is the pull at Saturn's cloud tops only a little more than the Earth's?  
   Options: Its cloud tops are far from its centre / Gas has no weight / Saturn is very cold / Saturn spins fast  
   **Answer:** Its cloud tops are far from its centre. *Why:* Saturn has 95 times the Earth's mass. But its cloud tops are about 9.5 times as far from its centre. Distance counts twice, so it cancels most of the extra mass.
5. (mcq) What is the normal force?  
   Options: The push of a surface on a thing pressing on it / The pull of gravity on a thing / A push from a hand / The force that slows a sliding thing  
   **Answer:** The push of a surface on a thing pressing on it. *Why:* The normal force is the push of a surface on a thing that presses on it. It points straight out from the surface. Gravity is a different force, and friction slows sliding.
6. (mcq) A packet rests on a kitchen scale. A string lifts it so the scale reads less. What happens to the packet's weight?  
   Options: It gets smaller / It stays the same / It gets bigger / It becomes zero  
   **Answer:** It stays the same. *Why:* Gravity pulls the packet just as hard as before. The reading is smaller because the string carries part of the weight, so the scale pushes less.
7. (mcq) Which of these is an applied force?  
   Options: The Earth pulling a mango / A hand pushing a swing / A magnet pulling a pin / A falling mango  
   **Answer:** A hand pushing a swing. *Why:* An applied force is a push or pull put on a thing by touching it. A hand on a swing does this. The Earth and a magnet act across a gap.
8. (mcq) A ball leaves your hand and flies up. Which of these is true?  
   Options: The push of your hand fades away slowly / The push of your hand ended when the ball left it / The ball carries the same push as before / The hand pulls the ball back  
   **Answer:** The push of your hand ended when the ball left it. *Why:* An applied force is a contact force, so it ends when the touching ends. After that, only gravity and the air act on the ball.
9. (mcq) A book rests on a table. How long is the normal force arrow, compared with the gravity arrow?  
   Options: The same length, pointing up / Longer, pointing up / Shorter, pointing up / The same length, pointing down  
   **Answer:** The same length, pointing up. *Why:* The book is still, so the forces on it are balanced. The table pushes up exactly as hard as gravity pulls down.
10. (mcq) A 5 newton book is pressed down with 3 newtons. How long is the table's arrow, at 1 centimetre for each newton?  
   Options: 2 centimetres / 3 centimetres / 5 centimetres / 8 centimetres  
   **Answer:** 8 centimetres. *Why:* The table must push up against the weight and the press together. 5 plus 3 is 8 newtons, so the arrow is 8 centimetres.
11. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not know?  
   Options: Why the mass that pulls is the same as the mass that resists / Whether the Moon has gravity / Whether mass exists / How to measure mass  
   **Answer:** Why the mass that pulls is the same as the mass that resists. *Why:* Every test finds that a thing's mass sets its weight and how hard it is to speed up. The two always match. Nobody has a final reason why.
12. (mcq) Why is the bottom storey of a tall building built strongest?  
   Options: It carries the weight of every floor above it / It is heavier than the other floors / It is nearest to the Sun / It has the most people  
   **Answer:** It carries the weight of every floor above it. *Why:* Each storey holds up everything above it, so the load grows towards the bottom.

## Applications
- Engineers add up the load on every pillar, beam and foundation of a building, from the roof down to the ground. The lowest parts carry the most.
- Bathroom scales, kitchen scales and baby scales measure the push between a thing and the scale, and show it as a mass. They are set for the strength of gravity on Earth.
- Space agencies, such as ISRO, work out the weight of a rover or lander on the Moon and Mars from its mass. They test wheels with cables that take weight away.
- Lorries, buses and trains are weighed on weighbridges and the load is limited, so that roads and bridges are not overloaded.
- A lift has a limit in kilograms. When it starts to rise, its floor and cable must push and pull harder than the weight.
- Athletes and dancers use the push of the ground. A jumper presses down on the ground and is pushed up with more than her weight.
- Springboards, cots, mattresses and shoe soles are made soft so that they are squashed more and push back gently. This spreads the push and protects the body.
- Geologists and surveyors measure small changes in the strength of gravity to find heavy rock or empty caves underground.

## Open questions
- **Why is the mass that decides a thing's weight exactly the mass that makes it hard to move?** Every test finds that the two match, to about one part in a thousand million million. Einstein's theory builds the match in, but nobody knows why nature is made that way.
- **Why do the smallest particles have the masses they do?** The Higgs field gives some particles their mass, and it was found in 2012. But nobody knows why each particle's mass is the number it is. Most of the mass of a proton also comes from the energy of the forces inside it.
- **Is gravity carried by tiny particles, as the other forces are?** Such a particle is called a graviton. Nobody has found one. Nobody has yet joined the physics of gravity with the physics of the very small.
- **What is the mass we cannot see in galaxies, which scientists call dark matter?** Stars at the edges of galaxies move as if there were much more mass than we can see. Nobody has found out what this mass is made of.
- **What is inside Jupiter?** The Juno spacecraft measured Jupiter's gravity and found that the middle is probably not a small solid core. It may be a large, fuzzy region of rock and ice mixed into hydrogen. Scientists are still working on it.

## Connected maths
- A packet reads 1,240 grams on a kitchen scale. What is its weight in newtons? Round to one decimal place. **12.4 newtons.**. 100 grams weighs about 1 newton. So divide 1,240 by 100, which is 12.4. The packet weighs about 12.4 newtons. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L5`)
- The Moon rover has a mass of 26 kilograms. Each kilogram weighs 9.8 newtons on the Earth and 1.62 newtons on the Moon. How many times smaller is its weight on the Moon? **About 6 times smaller.**. On the Earth, 26 times 9.8 is 254.8 newtons. On the Moon, 26 times 1.62 is 42.12 newtons. 254.8 divided by 42.12 is about 6. The rover weighs about 6 times less. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- In a made-up Dough Stack result, the dough under 1 book was 26 millimetres wide, and under 8 books it was 47. The load grew 8 times. How many times wider was the dough? What does that say? **About 1.8 times wider. The width did not grow 8 times.**. 47 divided by 26 is about 1.8. The load grew 8 times, but the width grew only 1.8 times. So the width is not in proportion to the load. A wider ball also has more dough to push back, so it can carry more load without spreading as fast. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the Spreading Square, the sides of the lit square were 6, 12 and 18 centimetres. Work out the area of each square. How many times bigger is each area than the first? **36, 144 and 324 square centimetres. So the areas are 1, 4 and 9 times the first.**. The area of a square is the side times itself. 6 times 6 is 36. 12 times 12 is 144. 18 times 18 is 324. 144 divided by 36 is 4, and 324 divided by 36 is 9. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A planet has the same mass as the Earth but is 3 times as wide. By what number must the pull be divided? What if it were 5 times as wide? **By 9 for 3 times as wide, and by 25 for 5 times as wide.**. The pull is divided by the width times the width. 3 times 3 is 9. 5 times 5 is 25. So a planet 5 times as wide, with the same mass, would pull 25 times less. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- A pack may weigh at most 50 newtons. On Mars each kilogram weighs 3.7 newtons. What is the biggest whole number of kilograms you can carry? **13 kilograms.**. 50 divided by 3.7 is about 13.5. You cannot carry half a kilogram over the limit, so round down to 13 kilograms. Check: 13 times 3.7 is 48.1 newtons, which is under 50. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L5`)
- A pillar holds up 18 square metres of floor on each of 5 floors. Each square metre of one floor has a mass of about 1,000 kilograms. What is the weight above the pillar, in newtons? **900,000 newtons.**. 18 times 5 is 90 square metres. 90 times 1,000 is 90,000 kilograms. 90,000 times 10 is 900,000 newtons. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- A packet marked 1 kilogram reads 1,012 grams on a kitchen scale. By what percentage is the reading bigger than the mark? What could cause the difference? **About 1.2 per cent. The packet may hold a little extra, or the scale may be a little off.**. The reading is 12 grams over 1,000 grams. 12 divided by 1,000 is 0.012, which is 1.2 per cent. Packs are often filled a little over, and a scale can have a small error. Checking the scale with a known mass tells you which. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L5`)
