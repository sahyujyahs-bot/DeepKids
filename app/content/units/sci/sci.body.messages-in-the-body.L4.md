# Nerves and hormones: the body's fast messages and slow messages
`sci.body.messages-in-the-body.L4`

**Summary.** Your body does some things before you decide, and sends some messages through the blood. How fast are they? In this unit you will test what your body does before you choose, time a hormone message with water, and see what makes your mouth water with a lemon.

**Intro.** *You step on a sharp plastic brick in the dark. Your foot lifts before you can say 'ow'. You did not choose to lift it. So who told your leg to move?* A nerve carried the message. It went from your foot to your spinal cord, which runs inside your backbone. The spinal cord sent a message straight back to your leg muscles. Your brain heard about it a moment later. A quick answer like this is called a reflex. Your body also sends slow messages. They are chemicals called hormones, and they travel in your blood. A hormone told your bones to grow this year. In this unit you time a slow message with two tumblers of water. You also test a reflex with drops of lemon juice.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a nerve signal is, and how it passes from one nerve cell to the next.
1. I can say what a hormone is, and how it reaches the parts of the body.
2. I can compare nerve messages and hormone messages: how fast, how far and how long.
3. I can explain what a reflex is, and trace a reflex through the spinal cord.
4. I can explain why a hormone reaches every cell but changes only some of them.
5. I can time a message in my own body with a fair test, and say what my results show.
6. I can give examples of nerves and hormones working together.
7. I can tell how scientists found out that some messages travel in the blood.

## Terms
- **Neuron (nerve cell)**: A cell that carries messages. Most neurons have one long thin fibre, like a thread. *e.g.* One neuron runs from your lower back to your big toe. It is about as long as your leg.
- **Nerve**: A bundle of many nerve fibres wrapped together, like the wires inside a cable. *e.g.* A nerve runs behind your elbow. When you bump it, your little finger tingles.
- **Nerve signal**: A tiny electrical change that travels along a nerve fibre from one end to the other. *e.g.* When a mosquito lands on your arm, a nerve signal travels from the skin to your spinal cord.
- **Ion**: An atom that carries a small electric charge. Sodium and potassium ions carry nerve signals. *e.g.* Table salt in water breaks up into sodium ions and chloride ions.
- **Synapse**: The tiny gap where a neuron passes a message on to the next cell. Chemicals carry the message across. *e.g.* A message from your fingertip crosses at least two synapses before it reaches your brain.
- **Spinal cord**: A thick rope of neurons inside your backbone. It links your brain to the rest of your body. *e.g.* Run a finger down the bumps of your backbone. The spinal cord is inside those bones.
- **Reflex**: A quick answer that your body gives without waiting for you to decide. *e.g.* You blink when something flies at your eye. Your mouth waters when sour juice touches your tongue.
- **Reflex arc**: The path that a reflex message takes. It runs from a sensor to the spinal cord or brain stem, and out to a muscle or gland. *e.g.* Your fingertip touches a hot pot. A signal runs up an arm nerve to the spinal cord. Then it runs back down an arm nerve to an arm muscle. That whole path is one reflex arc.
- **Hormone**: A chemical message that a gland puts into the blood. The blood carries it to the whole body. *e.g.* Growth hormone tells your bones to grow longer.
- **Gland**: A group of cells that makes something and sends it out. Some glands make hormones, and some make sweat, tears or saliva. *e.g.* The pancreas is a gland behind your stomach. It makes the hormone insulin.
- **Receptor**: A tiny part on or in a cell that one kind of hormone fits, as a key fits a lock. *e.g.* A heart muscle cell has receptors for adrenaline. A hair has no receptors, because the part of a hair that you can see is not alive.
- **Target cell**: A cell that has the receptor for a hormone. Only target cells answer that hormone. *e.g.* The cells of the kidney tubes are target cells for the hormone ADH.
- **Adrenaline**: A hormone from two small glands above the kidneys. It gets the body ready for sudden hard work. *e.g.* After a sudden fright, adrenaline keeps your heart beating hard for some minutes.
- **ADH**: A hormone that tells the kidneys to save water. It is released by a small gland under the brain. *e.g.* On a hot day you have more ADH in your blood. So you make less urine, and the urine is darker.

## Explanations
### The body has two ways to send a message · *defined*
**Definition:** A nerve message is a signal that travels along nerve fibres to one place. A hormone message is a chemical that travels in the blood to the whole body.

Your body is made of trillions of cells, and they must work together. To do that, they send messages. Nerves are the fast way. A nerve message travels along a fibre, and it arrives in a fraction of a second. It goes only where that fibre goes, so it can reach one muscle and no other. Hormones are the slow way. A gland puts a chemical into the blood. The heart pumps the blood round the whole body in about one minute. So a hormone reaches every part, and its effect can last for minutes, hours or years. The body uses nerves for jobs that must be quick and exact. It uses hormones for jobs that are slow, long or spread over the whole body.
- e.g. Nerve: you feel a tap on your shoulder and turn your head at once.
- e.g. Nerve: your fingers move over the keys of a harmonium.
- e.g. Hormone: your bones grow a few centimetres longer each year.
- e.g. Hormone: after a meal, sugar moves out of your blood and into your muscles.
### A nerve signal is a wave of electrical change moving along a fibre · *defined*
**Definition:** A nerve signal is a short electrical change that moves along a nerve fibre. It is made by ions crossing the skin of the fibre.

A nerve fibre is a long, thin part of a neuron. Salty liquid lies inside it and outside it. The salt is there as ions, which are atoms with a small electric charge. When a signal starts, tiny gates open in the skin of the fibre. Sodium ions rush in at that spot. This makes the gates just ahead open too, and so the change moves along. The ions themselves hardly travel. The change is what travels, as a fall travels along a row of dominoes. The fastest nerve fibres carry a signal at about 100 metres per second. The slowest carry it at about 1 metre per second. Electricity in a copper wire is millions of times faster. After each signal, tiny pumps push the ions back, and this uses energy from food.
- e.g. A row of dominoes falls from one end to the other, but each domino stays in place.
- e.g. Fast fibres tell you where your foot touched the floor.
- e.g. Slow fibres carry a dull ache, which is why an ache arrives after the first sharp pain.
### At a synapse, a chemical carries the message across a gap · *defined*
**Definition:** A synapse is the tiny gap between a neuron and the next cell. A chemical crosses the gap and starts a signal on the other side.

Neurons do not join up like soldered wires. At the end of each fibre there is a gap, thousands of times thinner than a hair. An electrical signal does not jump that gap. Instead, the end of the fibre lets out a tiny puff of a chemical. The chemical drifts across in about one thousandth of a second. On the far side it fits into receptors and starts a new signal. So every nerve message is partly electrical and partly chemical. Each synapse adds a small delay. A path with few synapses is faster than a path with many. A synapse is also a place where a message can be made stronger, made weaker or stopped.
- e.g. Where a nerve fibre meets a muscle, the chemical tells the muscle to shorten.
- e.g. A dentist's injection stops signals in a nerve, so the tooth's message never reaches the brain.
- e.g. Learning a skill changes how strongly some synapses pass messages on.
### A reflex takes the short path · *defined*
**Definition:** A reflex is a quick answer that the body gives without waiting for a decision. Its message takes a short path called a reflex arc.

Suppose your fingers touch a hot pot. Sensors in the skin start a signal. It travels up a nerve in your arm to your spinal cord. Inside the spinal cord it crosses one or two synapses. Then a signal goes straight back down another fibre to the muscle that bends your elbow. Your hand pulls away in a fraction of a second. At the same time, the spinal cord sends the news up to your brain. That path has more synapses, so the feeling of pain comes a moment after your hand has moved. Some reflexes pass through the brain stem, the lowest part of the brain. Blinking, sneezing and mouth-watering are like this. Your brain can change some reflexes a little. You can hold a hot cup of tea for a second longer if you must.
- e.g. Your foot lifts off a sharp stone before you feel the pain.
- e.g. You blink when a paper ball flies at your face.
- e.g. Your mouth waters when sour lemon juice touches your tongue.
- e.g. A doctor taps below your kneecap and your leg kicks.
### A hormone is a chemical message in the blood · *defined*
**Definition:** A hormone is a chemical that a gland puts into the blood. It changes what certain cells do, often far away from the gland.

Glands that make hormones have no tubes. They put their hormones straight into the blood that flows through them. The blood carries each hormone to every part of the body. A hormone works in very small amounts. The adrenaline in your blood is about as dilute as one drop in a large swimming pool. A hormone message is slow to start, because the gland must release it and the blood must carry it. It is also slow to stop. The hormone stays in the blood until the liver and kidneys break it down or remove it. Adrenaline is cleared in a few minutes. Other hormones last for hours. The gland can keep on releasing a hormone for years, as it does with growth hormone.
- e.g. Insulin from the pancreas tells cells to take sugar in from the blood.
- e.g. ADH from a gland under the brain tells the kidneys to save water.
- e.g. Growth hormone from the same gland tells bones to grow longer.
- e.g. Thyroid hormone from a gland in the neck sets how fast cells use energy.
### Only cells with the right receptor answer a hormone · *defined*
**Definition:** A receptor is a part of a cell that one kind of hormone fits. A cell answers a hormone only if the cell has the receptor for that hormone.

A hormone in the blood passes almost every cell in the body. Most cells take no notice. A cell answers only if they have a receptor that the hormone fits, as a key fits a lock. Cells with the receptor are called target cells. Kidney tube cells have receptors for ADH, so ADH changes what the kidneys do. One hormone can also give different orders to different cells. Adrenaline fits receptors on heart cells, liver cells and the muscle cells round your airways. The heart beats harder. The liver lets sugar out into the blood. The airways widen. Each kind of cell does their own job when the same key turns their lock.
- e.g. A radio programme goes out everywhere, but only a radio tuned to that station plays it.
- e.g. Some asthma inhalers hold a medicine that fits the adrenaline receptors of the airways.
- e.g. A person whose cells lack a working receptor does not respond to that hormone, however much is in the blood.
### Fast and brief, or slow and lasting · *defined*
**Definition:** Nerve messages are fast, go to one place and stop quickly. Hormone messages are slower, go everywhere and last longer.

Compare the two kinds of message in three ways. The first is speed. A nerve message arrives in a fraction of a second. A hormone takes from seconds to hours to show its effect. The second is where the message goes. A nerve fibre ends at one place, so its message is exact. A hormone goes wherever blood goes. The third is how long the message lasts. A nerve message ends when the signals stop. A hormone goes on working until the body has cleared it from the blood. Each way suits different jobs. Catching a ball needs speed and one exact muscle. Growing needs a steady message to many kinds of cells for years. No body could do both jobs well with only one kind of message.
- e.g. Pulling a hand off a hot tawa: nerves.
- e.g. Growing 6 centimetres in a year: hormones.
- e.g. Keeping the right amount of water in the blood all day: hormones.
- e.g. Writing your name: nerves.
### Nerves and hormones work together · *defined*
**Definition:** Many things the body does use both kinds of message. Often a nerve message starts the answer and a hormone keeps it going.

A dog barks suddenly behind you. Nerves act first. You jump and turn within a fraction of a second. Other nerves run to the two adrenal glands above your kidneys. Within seconds these glands put adrenaline into your blood. Your heart beats hard and your liver lets out sugar. The dog walks away, but the adrenaline is still in your blood. Your heart goes on pounding for some minutes. The brain also links the two systems directly. A small part of the brain called the hypothalamus checks the blood. Just below it hangs the pituitary gland, the size of a pea. The hypothalamus tells the pituitary which hormones to release. So the nervous system and the hormone system are two parts of one message system.
- e.g. A sudden fright: nerves make you jump, and adrenaline keeps your heart pounding.
- e.g. Evening: your eyes tell your brain that the light is fading. A gland in the brain then releases the sleep hormone melatonin.
- e.g. A drink of water: sensors in the brain notice the change in the blood. The pituitary gland then releases less ADH.
### Plants send messages without nerves · *debated*
Plants have no neurons. Yet plants sense, decide and send messages through their bodies. They use hormones. A hormone called auxin gathers on the shady side of a shoot, and the shoot bends towards the light. A ripe banana gives off a gas hormone that tells nearby fruit to ripen. Plants also send electrical signals. Touch a touch-me-not, and an electrical signal travels along the leaf at about 2 centimetres per second. The leaflets fold as it passes. In 2018 scientists filmed a signal spreading from a bitten leaf to other leaves within a few minutes. Scientists agree on these findings. They disagree about what to call them.
- e.g. A touch-me-not folds their leaflets one pair after another, as the signal passes along.
- e.g. A money plant in a dim room bends their shoots towards the window.
- e.g. A raw mango ripens sooner in a bag with a ripe banana.
- **Plant scientists such as Jagadish Chandra Bose, and later Stefano Mancuso and Frantisek Baluska:** Plants carry electrical signals and use some of the same chemicals that animal neurons use. Bose measured such signals in Kolkata more than a hundred years ago. These scientists say plants have their own kind of signalling system, and they study it under the name plant neurobiology.
- **Plant scientists such as Amedeo Alpi and more than 30 others, writing in 2007:** Plants have no neurons and no synapses, so the word 'neurobiology' misleads. On this view the signals are real, but they are automatic responses. They are better described in the plant's own terms.
### Where the message systems came from · *idea*
Chemical messages are far older than nerves. Bacteria give off chemicals that other bacteria sense and act on. They were doing this billions of years before any animal lived. Sponges are animals with no neurons at all. Their cells still signal to each other with chemicals, and a sponge can squeeze their whole body slowly. Jellyfish have a net of neurons spread through the body, with no brain. Animals with a head and a tail came later, and their neurons gathered into a brain and a cord. Scientists think the first neurons appeared more than 600 million years ago. Nobody knows exactly how. One idea is that neurons began as ordinary cells that sent chemical messages to their neighbours. Over time some of them grew long fibres. The chemical was then let out at the far end of the fibre, right next to its target. If so, a synapse works like a hormone message that has been made very small and very private. The chemical goes only to the one cell next to it.
- e.g. Bacteria in a group sense how many neighbours they have by the chemicals around them.
- e.g. A sponge has no nerves, yet they can slowly squeeze water out of their body.
- e.g. An octopus has about two thirds of their neurons in their arms.

## Mostly agreed
- A nerve signal takes time to travel, and it is far slower than electricity in a wire. *How we know:* In 1850 Hermann von Helmholtz timed a signal along a frog's nerve. It travelled at about 27 metres per second.
- A nerve signal is made by ions crossing the skin of the nerve fibre. *How we know:* In 1952 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley measured the ions crossing the giant nerve fibre of a squid. This fibre is up to 1 millimetre thick, so a fine wire can be put inside it.
- Reflexes can run through the spinal cord without the brain. *How we know:* Charles Sherrington studied reflexes for many years and described their paths in a book in 1906. People whose spinal cord is cut off from the brain by an injury still have a knee kick.
- Some messages travel in the blood and not along nerves. *How we know:* In 1902 William Bayliss and Ernest Starling cut every nerve to a piece of gut. Acid in that piece still made the pancreas pour out juice.
- Hormones work in very small amounts, and those amounts can be measured. *How we know:* In 1959 Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson found a way to measure insulin in a small sample of blood. Yalow, a physicist, won a Nobel Prize for it in 1977.
- A hormone acts only on cells that have a receptor for it. *How we know:* In the 1970s Robert Lefkowitz's team tagged a hormone with a radioactive label. They found the receptors it stuck to on cells, and later worked out what the receptor is made of.

## How we found out
**William Bayliss and Ernest Starling at University College London. The idea they tested came from Ivan Pavlov's laboratory in St Petersburg., The test was done on 16 January 1902. Starling first used the word hormone in lectures in 1905..** Around 1900 the Russian scientist Ivan Pavlov was the world's leading expert on digestion. He had shown that nerves make the mouth water and make the stomach pour out juice. When food leaves the stomach, the pancreas pours out juice too. Pavlov was sure that nerves carried this message as well. In London, on 16 January 1902, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling tested the idea. They worked on a dog who had been put into a deep sleep with a drug. They cut every nerve to one loop of the dog's gut and left only the blood vessels. Then they put weak acid into the loop, as the stomach does. The pancreas still poured out juice. No nerve was left, so the message had to be travelling in the blood. Next they scraped some lining from the gut, mixed it with acid and injected the liquid into a vein. The pancreas answered again. They named the chemical secretin. In 1905 Starling named all such chemical messengers hormones, from a Greek word that means 'to set in motion'.  
*What people believed before:* That every message in the body travels along nerves. Bayliss and Starling cut all the nerves to a loop of gut. Its message still reached the pancreas, so it had travelled in the blood.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A nerve signal is electricity flowing along a nerve, just as it flows along a copper wire.** (Many people, since Luigi Galvani made a frog's leg twitch with electricity in the 1780s.; *rejected*)  
  For: A nerve signal is an electrical change, and instruments can pick it up through the skin.  
  Against: Helmholtz measured about 27 metres per second in a frog's nerve. Electricity in a wire is millions of times faster. In a wire, tiny particles called electrons flow along it. In a nerve, ions cross the skin of the fibre at one spot after another.
- **The brain controls everything the body does.** (A common belief, and many older school books.; *rejected*)  
  For: The brain does take part in almost everything, and it can change many body actions.  
  Against: A reflex runs through the spinal cord and starts before the brain hears of it. The gut has its own net of neurons and moves food along without orders from the brain. Glands such as the pancreas sense the blood and answer directly.
- **All the body's messages travel along nerves.** (Ivan Pavlov and most scientists before 1902.; *rejected*)  
  For: Pavlov had shown that nerves control saliva and stomach juice.  
  Against: Bayliss and Starling cut the nerves to a piece of gut. Its message still reached the pancreas through the blood.
- **Nerves and hormones are really one message system.** (Berta Scharrer and Ernst Scharrer, from 1928 onwards.; *accepted*)  
  For: The Scharrers found neurons that release hormones into the blood, in fish, insects and mammals. The brain's hypothalamus is made of such cells. Adrenaline is a hormone, and a close relative of it carries messages across synapses.  
  Against: For years most scientists did not accept that a neuron could act as a gland. The two kinds of message still differ in speed and in where they go, so the two names stay useful.
- **Plants have something like a nervous system.** (Scientists who named a new field 'plant neurobiology' in 2006.; *debated*)  
  For: Plants carry electrical signals from leaf to leaf. They sense, remember and decide.  
  Against: Plants have no neurons and no synapses. Many plant scientists accept the signals but reject the name.
- **In an emergency, adrenaline can give a person the strength to lift a car.** (Newspaper stories from many countries.; *debated*)  
  For: Adrenaline sends more blood and sugar to the muscles. A person in danger may also ignore pain and use muscles harder than they usually dare.  
  Against: Nobody has measured such strength in a test, because it would be wrong to frighten people that badly. The stories cannot be checked.

## Roots
In this unit you timed a slow message with two tumblers of water. You watched a fast one start with drops of lemon juice. Here we ask what these messages say about who is in charge of a body.

**Deep truth.** In the Lemon Test, your mouth watered within seconds of the juice touching your tongue. In the Water Message Test, your kidneys answered the drink many minutes later. So the parts of your body get news at different times. There is no single moment when all of you knows something.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture ourselves as one knower in the head. This knower learns each thing at one moment and then tells the body. But the saliva glands, the spinal cord and the kidneys each receive messages and answer on their own time.  
*Evidence:* In the Lemon Test, saliva came within a second or two, before any decision. In the Water Message Test, the kidneys usually answered 30 to 60 minutes after the drink. A hand leaves a hot pot before the brain feels the pain. In 1902, a loop of gut with every nerve cut still sent its message to the pancreas.

- *who am i:* You are many parts that keep each other informed. Some parts of you answer before the thinking part has heard the news.
- *what is life:* Every living being sends messages inside their own body. Plants and bacteria send theirs with chemicals and electrical signals, without any neurons.
- *what is this world:* The sodium that carries your nerve signals is the same sodium as in sea salt. Your messages are made from the ordinary matter of the Earth.

You began as one cell. That cell divided again and again, and the cells became different from each other: skin, nerve, kidney and bone. Messages are what keep those trillions of cells working as one body. Many traditions teach that everything comes from one source and stays joined to it. In a body you can see one way this is true. The many parts stay one body because they never stop sending each other news.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.1.6 to 5.1.15:** “सा ह वागुच्चक्राम / सा संवत्सरं प्रोष्य पर्येत्योवाच / अथ ह प्राण उच्चिक्रमिषन्स यथा सुहयः पड्वीशशङ्कून्संखिदेदेवमितरान्प्राणान्समखिदत् / भगवन्नेधि / त्वं नः श्रेष्ठोऽसि” Speech, sight, hearing and the mind quarrel over who is the greatest. Each leaves the body for a year, and the body lives on without them. When the breath begins to leave, all the others are pulled up with it. They beg the breath to stay. (The story is about the breath, and it knows nothing of nerves or hormones. Yet it pictures a body as many powers that depend on each other, with none of them ruling alone. This unit found the same thing in the way the parts send and answer messages.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2, 'Discussion on Making All Things Equal':** “百骸九竅六藏，賅而存焉，吾誰與爲親？ / 汝皆悅之乎？ / 其有私焉？ / 如是，皆有爲臣妾乎？ / 其臣妾不足以相治乎？ / 其遞相爲君臣乎？” Zhuangzi lists the hundred joints, the nine openings and the six organs of his body. He asks which of them he should feel closest to. He asks whether they are all servants, or whether they take turns as ruler. He leaves the question open. (This is the question that a reflex raises. Your spinal cord moved your hand, your kidneys answered the water, and your brain was told afterwards. Zhuangzi's question about which part rules is still a fair one.)
- Science (settled): The gut has its own net of several hundred million neurons. It moves food along by its own reflexes, even when its nerves to the brain are cut. Bayliss and Starling showed this in 1899.
- Science (debated): In 1983 Benjamin Libet recorded brain activity before people moved a finger. It began a fraction of a second before they said they had decided. Scientists still argue about what this means for how we decide.
- Other view, Most neuroscientists today: On this view, knowing happens only in the brain. A spinal cord that moves a hand and a kidney that lets water out are responding, and they know nothing. So there is one moment when you know about the hot pot. It is the moment when the signals reach your brain and you feel the pain.
- Practice, Messages already arriving (3 min): Sit still together and close your eyes. For a minute, notice the messages that are reaching you from inside. Is your stomach full or empty? Is your bladder full? Can you feel your heartbeat? Are you sleepy or awake? Now open your eyes. Which of those messages did you ask for?
- Ask: In the Lemon Test your mouth watered before you chose anything. Was that you doing it?
- Ask: In the Water Message Test your kidneys answered long after you drank. During that wait, did all of you know about the water?
- Ask: If each part of your body gets the news at a different time, when do you know something?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your hand pulls back from a hot pot before you feel pain. Who decided to move it? (hint: The message turned round in your spinal cord. Is your spinal cord part of 'you'?)
- *deep:* A hormone passes every cell, but only cells with the receptor answer. Is the hormone still a message to the cells that do not answer? (hint: Think of a letter written in a language you cannot read. Is it a message for you?)
- *deep:* After a fright your heart pounds for minutes. Is the fear in your brain, in your blood, or in both? (hint: In 1884 the thinker William James asked whether we feel afraid because our heart pounds.)
- *big:* Your kidneys, your heart and your gut each answer messages without asking your brain. Where in your body are you? (hint: There may be more than one good answer.)
- *big:* Hormones were found by tests on dogs. In one test, the dog was put to sleep with a drug and did not wake up again. The finding has helped millions of people. Was the test right? (hint: People argued about this in London at the time. Laws on such tests are much stricter now.)
- *big:* A plant has no nerves, yet a bitten leaf sends a signal to the other leaves. What does a body need in order to send a message? (hint: Think about a sender, a path and a receiver that can answer.)

## Story: The Last Line of the Kolam
*Yazhini, Malar Chithi and their Pongal morning are made up. These facts are real. A hand pulls back from a hot thing by a reflex through the spinal cord. The feeling of pain comes a moment later. A fright makes the adrenal glands above the kidneys put adrenaline into the blood. Adrenaline makes the heart beat hard and the hands tremble, and the body clears it in some minutes. Cool running water is the right first aid for a small burn.*

On the morning of Pongal, Yazhini had one job. It was the last line of the kolam.

Her aunt, Malar Chithi, had drawn the rest before sunrise. White lines of rice flour looped round a grid of dots, across the whole courtyard of the house in Madurai. In the middle stood three bricks, a wood fire and a new clay pot of milk and rice. Only the border was left.

"The border is yours," Chithi had said. "One line, all the way round, without lifting your hand."

Yazhini had practised that line on the back step for a week. Now she took a pinch of flour between her thumb and two fingers. She let it run out in a thin white thread, the way Chithi did.

She was halfway along the first side when the pot hissed. A tongue of milk foam was climbing over the rim. It was too early. Nobody was ready to shout. Yazhini did not stop to think. She put out her left hand to steady the pot.

Her hand came back before she knew it had gone. She was already sitting on the ground, holding her fingers against her chest, when the pain arrived. It came like a late guest, a whole moment after the hand had left the pot.

"Show me." Chithi was beside her. She was a nurse at the big hospital, and she looked at burns every week. Two fingertips were pink. She led Yazhini to the tap by the back door and held the fingers under the running water. "Not ice. Just water. We stay here until I say."

The water was cool, and the pain grew small. But something else was wrong. Yazhini's heart was banging. Her knees felt loose. She held out her good right hand, the kolam hand. It trembled like a leaf on the neem tree.

"I am not scared," she said. "It is over. Why am I shaking?"

"Because two messengers went out," said Chithi, "and only one has come home."

She turned Yazhini's wet hand over. "The first one ran along your nerves. Finger to backbone, backbone to arm. Pull! It did not wait to ask your head. It was there and back before the pain reached you. That messenger has finished."

"And the second?"

"The second is not a runner. It is a pinch of something poured into your blood, from two small glands that sit above your kidneys. It is called adrenaline. Your blood has carried it to every part of you. It told your heart to beat hard. It told your liver to put sugar into your blood. It made your muscles ready to run from a fire." She smiled. "Nobody has told it yet that the fire is only a Pongal pot."

"Then tell it to stop."

"Nobody can call it back. A nerve message stops when the nerve stops. What is in the blood stays there until your body has broken it down. That takes some minutes." She put the fingers back under the tap. "Keep them here. By the time they are cool, your hand will be yours again."

From the courtyard came her grandfather's voice. "The milk is rising! Where is the border?"

Yazhini pulled her hand out of the water. "I can do it now." She ran back, took a pinch of flour, and began. The line came out like the track of a beetle. It shook left and right, thick and thin. After one hand's length she stopped and stared at it.

Chithi did not scold. She walked Yazhini back to the tap and put the burnt fingers under the water again.

So Yazhini waited. It was the hardest part of the morning. The foam was climbing in the pot. Her cousins were gathering with steel plates and spoons to bang. She counted her breaths, slow in and slower out, the way Chithi showed her.

Somewhere between sixty and seventy she noticed that her heart had gone quiet. She had not felt it stop banging. It had only, at some point, stopped.

She held out her right hand. It lay on the air as still as a plate on a shelf.

"Now," said Chithi.

Yazhini knelt at the place where the shaky line ended. She did not rub it out. She took her pinch of flour and went on from there. The white thread ran out smooth and even, round the first corner, along the long side, round the second. She joined the end to the beginning just as the milk rose over the rim and ran down the side of the pot.

"Pongalo Pongal!" shouted the cousins, and the spoons crashed on the plates.

Yazhini jumped at the noise. Then she laughed, and felt her heart begin to knock again.

"There it goes," she said. "The second messenger."

"Give it a few minutes," said Chithi, and went to stir the pot.

That evening the border was still there. One hand's length of it shook like a beetle's track, and all the rest ran straight. Yazhini's grandfather wanted to know who had spoiled the corner.

"Nobody spoiled it," said Yazhini. "That part was drawn while the message was still in my blood."

**For the parent:** Yazhini's hand pulled back from the pot at once, because a nerve message is fast and ends quickly. Her hand went on shaking for minutes, because adrenaline stays in the blood until the body breaks it down. She could draw a steady line only after the slow message had faded.

- Ask: Why did Yazhini's hand move before she felt the pain?
- Ask: Why could nobody make the shaking stop at once?
- Ask: When has your heart gone on pounding after a fright was over?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Water Message Test: how long does a hormone message take? (27 min, 2 days)
- **question:** After you drink 2 tumblers of water, how many minutes pass before your kidneys send the extra water out? What happens on a day when you take only 4 sips?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After 2 tumblers of water, how many minutes will pass before you need the toilet? Will it be different after only 4 sips? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Do both days at the same time of day, at least one hour after a meal or a big drink. Start each day by emptying your bladder in the toilet. Use the same tumbler on both days. Sit and do quiet things during the wait on both days. Do not eat or drink anything else until the test ends. Only one thing changes: 4 sips of water on day 1, and 2 tumblers on day 2.
- **record:** Draw a table with two rows, one for each day. Write the drink, the minutes until you needed the toilet, the colour of the pee and your slow count. If you did not need the toilet in 90 minutes, write 'more than 90'.
- **results table:** Day and drink | Minutes until I needed the toilet | Colour of the pee | Slow count while peeing
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Empty your bladder, take 4 sips of water, and check every 15 minutes for up to 90 minutes. (12 min); day 2: At the same time of day, empty your bladder, drink 2 tumblers of water, and check every 15 minutes. Then compare the two days. (15 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the tumbler. Explain the two days. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* On one day you will take 4 sips of water. On the next day you will drink 2 tumblers. How many minutes after the 2 tumblers will you need the toilet? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will drink 2 tumblers of water. When will you need the toilet? Why?
  - Ask: What has to happen inside you between the drink and the toilet? (good: The water goes to my stomach.; It gets into my blood.; My kidneys take it out.; It fills my bladder.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children guess 5 minutes, because they picture the water running straight through. Choose a time when your child last ate or drank a lot more than an hour ago.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. Your child empties their bladder in the toilet. They take 4 sips of water. Write down the time. Draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* First go to the toilet, so that we start empty. Now take 4 sips of water and no more. I am writing down the time.  
  *Child mode:* Go to the toilet first. Then take 4 sips of water. Write the time.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set a timer for every 15 minutes, for up to 90 minutes. Each time, your child rates how much they need the toilet, from 0 to 3. When they need to go, they go, and they count slowly while they pee. Write the minutes, the colour and the count.  
  *Parent says:* How much do you need the toilet now? Say 0 for not at all, 1 for a little, 2 for clearly and 3 for now. If it reaches 2 or 3, go, and count slowly while you pee.  
  *Child mode:* Every 15 minutes, rate your need from 0 to 3. Count slowly while you pee.
  - Note: Your child can read, draw or do the term cards during the wait, but not run about. On most days there is little or no need within 90 minutes after 4 sips. Write 'more than 90' and stop. The slow count is a rough measure of how much pee there was.
  - Wait: Check every 15 minutes for up to 90 minutes
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2, at the same time of day. Your child empties their bladder. They drink 2 tumblers of water within 5 minutes. Write down the time when the second tumbler is finished.  
  *Parent says:* Go to the toilet first, as you did before. Now drink both tumblers of water. Take up to 5 minutes. I am writing down the time.  
  *Child mode:* Go to the toilet first. Then drink 2 tumblers of water. Write the time.
  - Note: Two tumblers is a safe amount. More water does not make a better test, so keep to two.
  - Wait: Do this the next day, at the same time of day
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Check every 15 minutes as on day 1. When your child needs the toilet, write the minutes, the colour and the slow count. Go on checking until 90 minutes have passed, because there may be a second visit.  
  *Parent says:* How much do you need the toilet now, from 0 to 3? When you go, look at the colour and count slowly. Then come back, because we go on checking.  
  *Child mode:* Rate your need every 15 minutes. When you go, check the colour and count.
  - Note: On most days the need arrives 30 to 60 minutes after the drink. There is more pee than usual and it is pale. On a hot day, or after a lot of play, the body keeps more of the water. Then the wait is longer. Write down what really happens.
  - Check: In the Water Message Test, why do you empty your bladder before each drink? → **So both days start the same**
  - Wait: Check every 15 minutes for 90 minutes
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the two rows of the table side by side. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at both days. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Your tongue felt the water at once. Why did your kidneys answer so much later?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Your tongue felt the water in less than a second. Why did the kidneys take so long? (good: The water had to get into the blood first.; The message to the kidneys is slow.; The message goes by blood, not by a nerve.)
  - Ask: Why was the pee paler on the day with 2 tumblers? (good: There was more water in it.; The kidneys let the extra water out.)
  - Note: The reason has several steps. The water passes from the gut into the blood, which takes roughly 10 to 20 minutes. Sensors in the brain notice that the blood is a little more watery. A gland under the brain then releases less of the hormone ADH. ADH tells the kidneys to save water. The ADH already in the blood fades slowly. About half of it is gone every 15 minutes. Only then do the kidneys let the extra water out. No nerve carries this order to the kidneys. The last step is a nerve message: the full bladder tells the brain.
  - Think: On a hot afternoon after a game, would the Water Message Test take a longer or a shorter time? Why?
### e2 · The Lemon Test: what makes your mouth water? (24 min)
- **question:** Does your mouth make more saliva when lemon juice touches your tongue? Does it also happen when you only think about a lemon, or only smell one?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will make your mouth water most: thinking about a lemon, smelling a lemon, or tasting lemon juice? Will thinking alone do anything? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Count swallows for exactly 2 minutes in every round. Keep your lips closed, and swallow only when your mouth feels full. The same person counts every time. Rest for 1 minute and take one sip of water between rounds. The rounds go from the weakest to the strongest, because the taste of lemon stays in the mouth. The first round uses 3 drops of plain water, to show how often you swallow anyway. Only one thing changes: what reaches your tongue, your nose or your thoughts.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the lemon with the knife.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows: water drops, thinking, smelling and tasting. Write the number of swallows in 2 minutes for each round.
- **results table:** Round | What happened | Swallows in 2 minutes
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep the lemon out of sight. Explain the four rounds. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will count how often you swallow in 2 minutes. You will think about a lemon, smell one and taste one. Which will make your mouth water most? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which will make your mouth water most: thinking, smelling or tasting? Why?
  - Ask: Do you think only thinking about a lemon will make your mouth water? Why? (good: Yes, my mouth waters when I think of sour things.; No, nothing is touching my tongue.; A little, but less than the real juice.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most people are sure that a thought alone makes the mouth water a lot. The test checks that belief.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Practise. Your child sits still with lips closed and raises a finger at each swallow. Count for 1 minute. Then draw the results table with four rows.  
  *Parent says:* Keep your lips closed. Swallow only when your mouth feels full, and raise a finger each time. I will count. This minute is only for practice.  
  *Child mode:* Keep your lips closed. Raise a finger each time you swallow.
  - Note: You can also watch the bump at the front of the throat. It rises with each swallow. One or two swallows a minute is usual at rest.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 1. Put 3 drops of plain water on the middle of your child's tongue with a teaspoon. Count swallows for 2 minutes. Write the number. Rest for 1 minute.  
  *Parent says:* Round 1. Here are 3 drops of plain water. Lips closed. I am counting for 2 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Round 1. Three drops of water. Lips closed. Raise a finger for each swallow.
  - Check: Why does the Lemon Test begin with 3 drops of plain water? → **To show how often I swallow anyway**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 2. Your child closes their eyes. Read the words in the note slowly. Count swallows for 2 minutes from the first word. Write the number. Rest for 1 minute with one sip of water.  
  *Parent says:* Round 2. Close your eyes and listen. I will describe a lemon. Keep counting your swallows with your finger.  
  *Child mode:* Round 2. Close your eyes. Think about the lemon. Raise a finger for each swallow.
  - Note: Read this slowly: 'Picture a bright yellow lemon. I cut it in half. Juice runs out on the plate. You pick up one half. You bite into it. The sour juice fills your mouth.' Then stay quiet until the 2 minutes end.
- **s5 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Round 3. Cut the lemon in half. Your child closes their eyes. Hold one half about 3 centimetres under their nose for 2 minutes. Count swallows. Write the number. Rest for 1 minute with one sip of water.  
  *Parent says:* Round 3. Eyes closed. I am holding a cut lemon under your nose. Breathe through your nose. Do not touch the lemon.  
  *Child mode:* Round 3. Eyes closed. Smell the lemon. Raise a finger for each swallow.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 4. Squeeze a little juice into the second teaspoon. Put 3 drops on the middle of your child's tongue. Count swallows for 2 minutes. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Round 4. Here are 3 drops of lemon juice. Lips closed. I am counting for 2 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Round 4. Three drops of lemon juice. Lips closed. Raise a finger for each swallow.
  - Note: Ask your child to notice how soon the mouth begins to water. It usually starts within a second or two.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the four numbers together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your four counts. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Did you decide to make saliva in round 4?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Which rounds were clearly above the water round? (good: The lemon juice round.; Only the taste, the others were about the same.; Smelling was a little more.)
  - Ask: How do you think the message got from your tongue to the glands that make saliva? (good: By nerves, because it was so fast.; It went to my brain and back.; It was a reflex.)
  - Note: Most people swallow about twice as often after the lemon juice as after the water. Thinking and smelling often add little or nothing. Write down what you really found. The reason: sensors on the tongue detect the acid in the juice. A nerve signal goes to the brain stem, the lowest part of the brain. A signal comes straight back along other nerves to the glands that make saliva. This is a reflex, and it starts within a second or two. The mouth can also learn to water at a smell or a thought. In careful tests that effect is small, and some people show none.
  - Think: Saliva starts in about a second. What does that speed tell you about the messenger?

## Activities
### a1 · Did I Decide That? Two things your body does before you choose (10 min)
- **record:** A table with three rows: clap, sole of the foot, and 'touch your nose'. For each, write how many times out of 5 the body answered, and whether you chose to do it.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the table with three rows. Explain the three tests. Ask which actions your child thinks they will choose.  
  *Parent says:* I will clap near your face, stroke your foot and ask you to touch your nose. Which of your answers will you choose? Which will just happen?  
  *Child mode:* Draw three rows: clap, foot, nose. Which answers will you choose?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit facing your child. At a surprise moment, clap your hands once, about 30 centimetres in front of their face. Watch their eyes. Do this 5 times, with gaps of different lengths. Count the blinks.  
  *Parent says:* Look at my nose and keep your eyes open. I will clap when you do not expect it. Did you choose to blink just then?  
  *Child mode:* Keep your eyes open. A helper claps near your face. Did you blink?
  - Note: Clap in front of the face, never beside an ear. Most children blink at the first claps. The blink may fade by the fifth clap, because the body learns that the clap is harmless.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child sits with one bare foot on your lap and looks away. Stroke the sole firmly from heel to toes with the blunt end of the pencil. Do this 5 times. Watch the toes and the leg.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the wall and not at your foot. I will stroke the sole of your foot. Tell me what your toes and your leg did.  
  *Child mode:* Look away. A helper strokes the sole of your foot. What do your toes do?
  - Note: The toes usually curl and the foot pulls back. A firm stroke works better than a light one, which only tickles.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say 'touch your nose' 5 times, with gaps. Your child may do it or not, as they like. Fill in the table. Then talk about the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Now I will say 'touch your nose' 5 times. Do it or not, as you choose. How was this different from the clap and the foot?  
  *Child mode:* A helper says 'touch your nose'. Do it or not. How is this different?
  - Ask: What was the same about the blink and the foot pulling back? (good: I did not choose them.; They were very fast.; They happened the same way each time.; They protect my eye and my foot.)
  - Check: What do we call a quick answer that the body gives without waiting for a decision? → **A reflex**
### a2 · Squeeze Chain: time a nerve message through two bodies (12 min)
- **record:** Write the time for 20 squeezes on each of three tries. Circle the middle time. Divide it by 20 to find the time for one pass.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the chain. Ask your child to guess how many seconds 20 squeezes will take, and why. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* I will squeeze your hand. When you feel it, squeeze back. Then I squeeze again. How many seconds will 20 squeezes take? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A squeeze goes back and forth 20 times. How many seconds will it take?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit facing each other and hold one hand. Both close your eyes. Practise 10 squeezes back and forth. Each squeeze is short and firm.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes closed. Squeeze only when you feel my squeeze, and never before it. Short and firm. Let us practise ten.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Squeeze back only when you feel a squeeze.
  - Note: With three or more people, sit in a line and hold hands. The squeeze travels down the line and back again.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start the stopwatch with your free hand as you give the first squeeze. Count your own squeezes silently. Stop the watch when you feel your child's tenth squeeze. That makes 20 squeezes. Do this three times and write each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now we time it. Twenty squeezes, ten from each of us. Eyes closed. I start now.  
  *Child mode:* Eyes closed. Twenty squeezes, ten from each of you. A helper times it.
  - Note: Most pairs take 4 to 6 seconds. A time under 3 seconds means you both fell into a beat and squeezed before you felt the squeeze. Try again more slowly.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Circle the middle time of the three. Divide it by 20 to find the time for one pass. Talk about the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Take the middle time and divide it by 20. That is the time for one pass. Where did the message travel inside you during that time?

### Nerves, hormones and reflexes (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What carries a nerve message from your fingertip to your spinal cord?  
   Options: Nerve fibres / Blood / Air in the lungs / Bones  
   **Answer:** Nerve fibres. *Why:* A nerve message is a signal that travels along nerve fibres. Blood carries hormones, which are the other kind of message.
2. (mcq) What carries a hormone from a gland to the cells it changes?  
   Options: The blood / Nerve fibres / The bones / The skin  
   **Answer:** The blood. *Why:* A gland puts the hormone into the blood, and the heart pumps the blood round the body. If you chose nerve fibres, you were thinking of the fast kind of message.
3. (fill) A quick answer that the body gives without waiting for a decision is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['reflex']. *Why:* A reflex is fast and needs no decision. Blinking at a clap and pulling a hand from a hot pot are reflexes.
4. (truefalse) A hormone message usually arrives faster than a nerve message.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A nerve message arrives in a fraction of a second. A hormone must be released and carried by the blood, and its effect takes from seconds to hours.
5. (match) Match each hormone to what it tells the body to do.  
   **Answer:** Growth hormone → Bones, grow longer; Insulin → Cells, take in sugar; Adrenaline → Heart, beat hard; ADH → Kidneys, save water. *Why:* Each hormone fits the receptors of its own target cells. So each one gives a different order to a different part of the body.
6. (sort) Sort these. Is each message carried mainly by nerves or by a hormone?  
   **Answer:** blinking at a clap → Nerves; growing taller → A hormone; kicking a ball → Nerves; pale pee after a lot of water → A hormone; feeling a mosquito land → Nerves; sugar leaving the blood after a meal → A hormone. *Why:* Ask how fast it happens and where. A quick answer in one place is the work of nerves. A slow change across the body is the work of a hormone.
7. (order) Put these steps in order, to show what happens when a fingertip touches a hot pot.  
   **Answer:** ['The fingertip touches the hot pot', 'A signal travels up the arm nerve', 'The signal crosses a synapse in the spinal cord', 'A signal travels down to the arm muscle', 'The hand pulls away', 'The brain feels the pain']. *Why:* The message turns round in the spinal cord, so the hand moves first. The news reaches the brain by a longer path, so the pain is felt last.
8. (mcq) In the Water Message Test, both days started at the same time of day. Why?  
   Options: So only the drink was different / So the water was colder / So the clock was easier to read / So the kidneys were resting  
   **Answer:** So only the drink was different. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. The body handles water differently in the morning and at night. Keeping the time the same leaves the drink as the only difference.
9. (fill) The tiny gap between one neuron and the next cell is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['synapse']. *Why:* At a synapse a chemical carries the message across the gap. Each synapse adds a small delay.
10. (truefalse) Your brain feels the pain before your hand leaves a hot pot.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The hand is pulled away by a reflex through the spinal cord. The message to the brain takes a longer path, so the pain is felt a moment later.
11. (mcq) Where does the message turn round when a hand pulls back from something hot?  
   Options: In the spinal cord / In the heart / In the fingertip / In the stomach  
   **Answer:** In the spinal cord. *Why:* The signal goes in to the spinal cord and crosses one or two synapses. Then it comes straight back out to the arm muscle.
12. (write) Explain in two sentences why growing taller is a job for a hormone and not for a nerve.  
   **Answer:** Growing is slow and goes on for years in bones all over the body. A hormone in the blood reaches all those bones and can keep working for a long time.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the job is slow, long and spread across the body. It also says that blood reaches every part.
13. (write) In the Water Message Test, your tongue felt the water at once. Why did your kidneys answer much later?  
   **Answer:** The tongue's message went by nerves, which are fast. The water had to pass into the blood first. Then the hormone ADH had to fall before the kidneys let the water out.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a hormone in the blood tells the kidneys, and no nerve does. It also says that each step takes minutes.
14. (draw) Draw an arm and a backbone. Draw a reflex path from a fingertip. Label the spinal cord and the muscle.  
   **Answer:** One arrow from the fingertip along the arm to the spinal cord inside the backbone. A second arrow from the spinal cord back out to the muscle of the upper arm.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the message going in and coming out again at the spinal cord. It does not go up to the brain first.
15. (mcq) Why does adrenaline change the heart but not a fingernail?  
   Options: The nail has no receptors for it / The nail is too far away / Blood never carries adrenaline / The nail is too hard  
   **Answer:** The nail has no receptors for it. *Why:* A hormone changes only cells that have its receptor. Heart muscle cells have receptors for adrenaline. The tip of a nail is not made of living cells, so it has none.

3. (mcq) Which of these happens fastest?  
   Options: A reflex / Growing one centimetre / Feeling sleepy at night / Pee turning pale after a drink  
   **Answer:** A reflex. *Why:* A reflex is carried by nerves and is over in a fraction of a second. The other three are the work of hormones and take minutes, hours or months.
4. (mcq) You step on a sharp stone and your foot lifts at once. Where did the message turn round?  
   Options: In the spinal cord / In the stomach / In the heart / In the stone  
   **Answer:** In the spinal cord. *Why:* The signal went up a nerve to the spinal cord and straight back down to the leg muscles. The brain heard about it a moment later.
5. (mcq) What must a cell have in order to answer a hormone?  
   Options: A receptor that the hormone fits / A nerve fibre of its own / A place near the gland / A hard outer shell  
   **Answer:** A receptor that the hormone fits. *Why:* A hormone fits its receptor as a key fits a lock. Being near the gland does not matter, because the blood carries the hormone everywhere.
6. (mcq) You run the Water Message Test on two days. What must be the same on both days?  
   Options: Time of day and an empty bladder / The amount of water drunk / The colour of the tumbler / The weather report on television  
   **Answer:** Time of day and an empty bladder. *Why:* The amount of water is the one thing that changes. Everything else that could change the result must stay the same. That includes the time of day and how full the bladder is.
7. (mcq) A sudden bang makes you jump, and then your heart pounds for minutes. Which messengers were at work?  
   Options: Nerves first, then a hormone / A hormone first, then nerves / Only nerves / Only hormones  
   **Answer:** Nerves first, then a hormone. *Why:* The jump is fast, so nerves carried it. The pounding goes on after the bang is over, because adrenaline is still in the blood.
8. (mcq) How did scientists first show that some messages travel in the blood?  
   Options: A message arrived with the nerves cut / They asked patients how they felt / They weighed the blood / They cooled the nerves with ice  
   **Answer:** A message arrived with the nerves cut. *Why:* In 1902 Bayliss and Starling cut every nerve to a piece of gut. Acid in that piece still made the pancreas pour out juice, so the message had gone by the blood.
9. (mcq) What crosses the gap at a synapse?  
   Options: A chemical / A drop of blood / A spark you can see / A tiny bone  
   **Answer:** A chemical. *Why:* The end of the nerve fibre lets out a chemical. It drifts across the gap and starts a new signal on the other side.
10. (mcq) Why can a hormone message last longer than a nerve message?  
   Options: The hormone stays in the blood longer / Hormones are heavier than nerves / Nerves get tired after one signal / Blood moves more slowly at night  
   **Answer:** The hormone stays in the blood longer. *Why:* A nerve message ends when the signals stop. A hormone goes on working until the liver and kidneys have cleared it from the blood.
11. (mcq) Your hand leaves a hot pot before you feel pain. Why does the pain come later?  
   Options: The path to the brain has more synapses / Pain travels in the blood / The brain is asleep / The pot cools down  
   **Answer:** The path to the brain has more synapses. *Why:* The reflex turns round in the spinal cord after one or two synapses. The message to the brain passes more synapses, so it arrives a moment later.
12. (mcq) After a meal the pancreas releases insulin. How does the insulin reach a muscle cell in the leg?  
   Options: The blood carries it there / A nerve carries it there / It moves through the bones / It is made in the leg  
   **Answer:** The blood carries it there. *Why:* The pancreas puts insulin into the blood. The heart pumps that blood to every part of the body, including the leg muscles.

## Applications
- A dentist's injection blocks the gates in a nerve fibre for an hour or two. No signal can pass, so the tooth's message never reaches the brain.
- People with one kind of diabetes make no insulin. They inject insulin, a hormone given as a medicine, so that their cells can take in sugar.
- A person with a severe allergy may carry an adrenaline injection. Adrenaline widens the airways and keeps the blood pressure up until help comes.
- A doctor taps below the kneecap with a small rubber hammer. A normal kick shows that the reflex path through the spinal cord is working.
- Salt sold for eating in India has iodine added. The thyroid gland in the neck needs iodine to make its hormone.
- A robot on Mars is given 'reflexes'. A radio message from Earth takes from about 3 to 22 minutes to arrive. So the robot must avoid rocks without waiting for orders.
- Fruit sellers ripen bananas with a gas that is a plant hormone. Gardeners dip cuttings in a powder of another plant hormone to make roots grow.

## Spotted around us
- A pressure cooker whistles suddenly and everyone jumps. A minute later, hearts are still beating fast.
- Your mouth waters at the pani puri stall when the sour water is poured.
- A house gecko drops their tail when caught, and the tail goes on wriggling. Nerve circuits inside the tail keep it moving for some minutes.
- The word 'iodised' on a packet of salt in the kitchen.
- A touch-me-not by a path folding their leaflets, one pair after another, after a goat brushes past.
- An older cousin who has grown taller than their mother within a year or two.

## Open questions
- **How do billions of nerve signals add up to one thought or one feeling?** Scientists can record signals from single neurons and from whole brains. Nobody can yet explain how the signals become what a being feels.
- **How many hormones does a body make?** New hormones are still being found. A hormone from fat cells was found in 1994, and one from the stomach in 1999. Fat, bone and muscle are now known to act as glands.
- **Do the microbes in the gut send messages that change how we feel?** Gut microbes make chemicals that reach the blood and the gut's nerves. In mice these change behaviour. In people the evidence is still thin.
- **How does a plant carry a fast signal from leaf to leaf without nerves?** The signals were filmed in 2018, and they use a chemical that neurons also use. Scientists are still working out which cells carry them and how.
- **Can a spinal cord that has been cut be helped to carry messages again?** Nerve fibres in the spinal cord hardly grow back by themselves. Since 2018 a few people with injuries have walked again with the help of electrical signals from a small device. It does not yet work for everyone.

## Connected maths
- In the Squeeze Chain, 20 squeezes took 5 seconds. What fraction of a second did one pass take? **One quarter of a second.**. 5 seconds shared among 20 passes is 5 divided by 20. In 1 second there were 4 passes, so each pass took one quarter of a second. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A nerve pass in the Squeeze Chain took a quarter of a second. The Water Message Test took 40 minutes. How many times longer was the water message? **9,600 times longer.**. 40 minutes is 40 times 60, which is 2,400 seconds. Each second holds 4 quarter-seconds. 2,400 times 4 is 9,600. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In Wool Paths, a reflex path was 100 centimetres and the brain path was 150 centimetres. A fast nerve signal covers 50 centimetres in one hundredth of a second. How much time does the extra length cost? Is that enough to explain why a reflex is quicker? **One hundredth of a second. No, that is too little to explain it.**. 150 minus 100 is 50 centimetres. The signal covers that in one hundredth of a second. A chosen action is slower by much more than that. So the main saving is in synapses and deciding, not in distance. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Here are swallows counted in 2 minutes in the Lemon Test. Water: 2. Thinking: 2. Smelling: 3. Lemon juice: 6. Which rounds are clearly above the water round? Could the one extra swallow for smelling be chance? **Only the lemon juice round is clearly above. Yes, one extra swallow could easily be chance.**. 6 is three times 2, which is a big difference. 3 is only one more than 2. A count of swallows goes up and down by one from try to try anyway. To be sure about smelling, the round would have to be repeated several times. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A fast nerve signal travels 50 metres in one second. In Wool Paths, a child's path from fingertip to brain is about 1 metre. A blue whale's path from tail to brain is about 25 metres. How long does each signal take? **The child's signal takes one fiftieth of a second. The whale's signal takes half a second.**. 50 metres take 1 second, so 1 metre takes one fiftieth of a second. 25 metres is half of 50 metres, so the whale's signal takes half of a second. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Suppose a gland stops releasing ADH when you drink. The ADH left in the blood halves about every 15 minutes. It starts at 16 units. How much is left after 15, 30 and 45 minutes? When is only a quarter left? **8 units, then 4 units, then 2 units. A quarter is left after 30 minutes.**. Half of 16 is 8. Half of 8 is 4. Half of 4 is 2. A quarter of 16 is 4, and that is reached after two halvings, which is 30 minutes. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
