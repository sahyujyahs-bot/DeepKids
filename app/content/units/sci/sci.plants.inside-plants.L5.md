# Inside a stem: two sets of pipes, growth rings and tissues
`sci.plants.inside-plants.L5`

**Summary.** Most of a tree trunk is made of dead cells, yet the tree is alive. Take away one thin strip of bark all the way round the trunk, and the whole tree slowly dies. Why? Most plants have two sets of pipes. Xylem pipes are dead and hollow, and they carry water up from the roots. Phloem pipes are alive, and they carry sugar from the leaves to every part that needs food. In a tree the phloem lies just under the bark, and a thin layer called the cambium adds new wood every year, which makes the growth rings. Your child maps these pipes with dye in a root, a stem and a leaf stalk, reads a tree's rings, and learns how skin, packing and pipes work together as tissues.

**Intro.** *Peel a banana slowly. Long pale strings stay stuck to the fruit. They run from the stalk end to the tip. What are these strings for?* Look for the same kind of string in a leaf stalk and on a peeled orange. In the first activity, String Hunt, you pull them out and compare them. Then, in Pipe Maps, you use coloured water to test what at least some of the strings do.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what a tissue is, and name a plant's three kinds: skin, packing and transport.
1. By the end I can tell xylem from phloem: what each carries, which way, and whether its cells are alive.
2. By the end I can show where the pipes sit in a root, a stem and a leaf stalk, and say why.
3. By the end I can explain 'source to sink', and why food in the phloem can travel up or down.
4. By the end I can explain how a trunk gets thicker, and why losing a ring of bark kills a tree.
5. By the end I can read growth rings, and say when rings can mislead us.
6. By the end I can plan a fair test with dye, and give my claim, evidence and reasoning.
7. By the end I can say which parts of a tree are living and which are dead, and what the dead cells do.

## Terms
- **Cell**: A cell is the smallest living unit of a body. Every plant and animal is built from cells. A plant cell has a stiff wall around the outside. *e.g.* One thin slice of onion skin is a sheet of hundreds of cells, each like a tiny room.
- **Tissue**: A tissue is a group of cells of the same kind that do one job together. *e.g.* The see-through skin you can peel from a leaf is a tissue. So is the bundle of pipes in a banana string.
- **Epidermis (say it: ep-i-DER-mis)**: The epidermis is the skin tissue of a plant. It is usually one cell thick. It keeps water in and germs out. *e.g.* The shiny surface of a mango leaf is the epidermis, covered with a thin layer of wax.
- **Ground tissue (packing tissue)**: Ground tissue fills the space between the skin and the pipes. Its cells store food, make food in green parts, and help hold the plant up. *e.g.* The soft flesh of a potato is ground tissue packed with stored starch.
- **Vascular tissue (say it: VAS-kyu-lar)**: Vascular tissue is the transport tissue of a plant. It is made of two kinds of pipes, xylem and phloem. *e.g.* The veins of a leaf are vascular tissue. So are the strings on a peeled banana.
- **Vascular bundle**: A vascular bundle is a strand of xylem and phloem pipes running side by side, often with strong fibres round them. *e.g.* Each coloured dot you see when you cut a dyed stem across is one vascular bundle.
- **Xylem (say it: ZY-lum)**: Xylem is the tissue that carries water and dissolved minerals up from the roots. Its pipes are made of dead, hollow cells with hard walls. *e.g.* The wood of a teak door is old xylem.
- **Vessel**: A vessel is one long xylem pipe. It forms when a column of cells die and their end walls break down, so the cells join into one tube. *e.g.* The tiny holes you can see on the cut end of a piece of teak are vessels.
- **Lignin (say it: LIG-nin)**: Lignin is a hard, waterproof substance that plants add to some cell walls. It makes wood stiff. *e.g.* A coconut shell is very hard because the walls of its cells are full of lignin.
- **Phloem (say it: FLOW-um)**: Phloem is the tissue that carries sugar from where it is made or stored to where it is needed. Its pipes are made of living cells. *e.g.* The sweet neera that drips from a cut coconut flower stalk is phloem sap.
- **Sieve tube**: A sieve tube is one phloem pipe. It is a line of living cells. The wall between two cells has many holes, like a tea strainer, so sap can pass. *e.g.* An aphid pushes her mouth tube into one single sieve tube to feed.
- **Companion cell**: A companion cell is a small living cell beside each sieve tube cell. The companion cell keeps the sieve tube cell alive, because the sieve tube cell has no nucleus. *e.g.* Each sieve tube cell in a mango twig has a companion cell alongside.
- **Sap**: Sap is the liquid that moves inside a plant's pipes. Xylem sap is mostly water with minerals. Phloem sap is water with a lot of sugar. *e.g.* Sugarcane juice is sweet because phloem sap delivered sugar to the stem, where it is stored.
- **Source**: A source is a part of a plant that gives sugar to the phloem. It either makes the sugar or lets go of stored food. *e.g.* A mango leaf in sunlight is a source. A sprouting potato is a source too.
- **Sink**: A sink is a part of a plant that takes sugar from the phloem, to use or to store. *e.g.* A swelling mango fruit, a growing root tip and a new leaf bud are all sinks.
- **Translocation (say it: trans-lo-KAY-shun)**: Translocation is the movement of sugar and other food through the phloem, from sources to sinks. *e.g.* At night, sugar made in a tapioca leaf during the day is translocated down to the fat roots.
- **Meristem (say it: MER-i-stem)**: A meristem is a place in a plant where cells keep dividing to make new cells. Plants grow only from their meristems. *e.g.* The very tip of a money plant shoot is a meristem. New leaves come from there.
- **Cambium (say it: KAM-bee-um)**: The cambium is a meristem shaped like a thin sleeve between the wood and the bark. It adds new xylem on the inside and new phloem on the outside. *e.g.* When you peel fresh bark off a twig, the wet, slippery surface is where the cambium was.
- **Wood**: Wood is xylem that the cambium has made, year after year. Most of its cells are dead. *e.g.* A rosewood chair, a pine packing crate and a neem twig are all wood.
- **Bark**: Bark is everything outside the cambium. The inner bark is living phloem. The outer bark is dead cork that protects the tree. *e.g.* The thick, cracked skin of a neem trunk is outer bark. Cinnamon sticks are dried inner bark.
- **Growth ring**: A growth ring is the layer of wood a tree adds in one growing season. Rings show up because wood made early in the season looks different from wood made late. *e.g.* Count the rings on a teak stump from the centre to the bark to learn about how many years the tree lived.
- **Sapwood and heartwood**: Sapwood is the pale outer wood whose pipes still carry water. Heartwood is the older, darker wood in the middle, whose pipes are blocked. *e.g.* In a cut rosewood log, the pale rim is sapwood and the dark purple-brown middle is heartwood.
- **Girdling**: Girdling means removing or squeezing the bark in a complete ring round a stem. It cuts the phloem, so sugar cannot pass. *e.g.* A wire tied tightly round a young tree and left for years can girdle the tree.
- **Dendrochronology (say it: den-dro-kron-OL-o-jee)**: Dendrochronology is the science of working out dates and past weather from tree rings. *e.g.* Scientists in India have used the rings of old teak trees in Kerala to study monsoons of the past 500 years.

## Explanations
### Tissues: cells that do one job together · *defined*
**Definition:** A tissue is a group of cells of the same kind doing one job together. A plant has three kinds of tissue: skin tissue on the outside, transport tissue that carries water and food, and ground tissue that fills the rest.

A plant is not one lump of the same stuff. Cut any stem across and you see different areas. The outside is a skin, called the epidermis. It is usually one cell thick, and it is covered in wax so the plant does not dry out. Inside are bundles of pipes, the vascular tissue. Everything else is ground tissue, which you can think of as packing. But the packing is busy. In a green stem, the packing cells make food. In a potato or a carrot, they store food. Some packing cells grow thick, hard walls and become fibres that hold the plant up. Why does a plant need tissues at all? A single cell can only do so much. A cell that is a good pipe is a bad store, and a cell that is a good skin is a bad pipe. So cells take different jobs and depend on each other, the way the people in a town do.
- e.g. The waxy skin of a brinjal is epidermis, the flesh is ground tissue, and the faint lines running through the flesh are vascular bundles.
- e.g. Jute fibre, grown in West Bengal and Assam, is bundles of strong fibre cells from the stem of the jute plant.
- e.g. The gritty bits in a pear or a chikoo are tiny groups of cells with very thick, hard walls.
### Two sets of pipes in every vein · *defined*
**Definition:** Every vein of a plant holds two kinds of pipe side by side. Xylem carries water and minerals up from the roots. Phloem carries sugar from the leaves and stores to wherever food is needed.

In the Pipe Maps test, the dye showed up as dots. Each dot is a vascular bundle. The dye stains only the xylem, because xylem carries the water you gave. Right beside every dyed dot lies a patch of phloem that the dye does not show. In a stem, the phloem is on the side of the bundle nearer the skin, and the xylem is nearer the middle. Why two sets, and not one? Because the two loads travel in different directions and in different ways. Water goes one way, from root to leaf, and most of it then leaves the plant as vapour. Sugar starts in the leaves and must reach roots, fruits and buds, which may be above or below. One pipe could not do both jobs at once.
- e.g. The strings on a peeled banana are vascular bundles. They carried water and sugar into the growing fruit.
- e.g. The white threads on a peeled orange are vascular bundles too.
- e.g. A dried ridge gourd, sold as a loofah scrubber, is the tough net of vascular bundles and fibres left when the soft flesh has gone.
### Xylem: pipes made of dead cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Xylem pipes are built from cells that have died and emptied. Their walls are hardened with lignin. Water is pulled up through them from the roots to the leaves.

A young xylem cell is alive. First the cell grows long. Then the cell builds a thick wall and stiffens the wall with lignin, often in rings or spirals. Then the cell breaks down everything inside, and dies. In a vessel, the end walls dissolve too, so a column of dead cells becomes one open tube. This death is not an accident. The cell's own genes switch it on at the right time. Why must the cell die? Because a living cell is full of jelly-like contents that would block the flow. Only an empty tube lets water through easily. The hard walls matter too. Water in the xylem is pulled from above, as you learned in the unit on how water climbs. A soft tube would be squashed flat by that pull, the way a paper straw flattens when you suck too hard. Lignin keeps the pipe open. The same hard walls hold the plant up, so xylem does two jobs: carrying and supporting.
- e.g. A wooden rolling pin is made of millions of dead xylem cells. They carried water once, and now they give the wood its strength.
- e.g. Conifers such as deodar have narrower xylem cells, called tracheids, which pass water to each other through small pits in their side walls.
- e.g. The spiral threads you sometimes see when you snap a lotus stem gently and pull the pieces apart are the wall thickenings of xylem cells.
### Phloem: pipes made of living cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Phloem pipes are called sieve tubes. They are lines of living cells joined by end walls full of holes. They carry phloem sap, which is water with a lot of sugar dissolved in it.

Phloem cells stay alive, but they change a great deal. A sieve tube cell gets rid of the nucleus and most other inner parts, so that sap can flow through. A cell with no nucleus cannot keep going alone for long. So each sieve tube cell has a partner alongside, a companion cell, which makes what both cells need. Phloem sap is as sweet as a soft drink, or sweeter. Often between a tenth and a quarter of it is sugar. The sugar is mostly sucrose, the same sugar as in your kitchen. The sap also carries building materials for proteins, and signals from one part of the plant to another. Why must these cells stay alive when xylem cells die? Because loading sugar into the pipe and taking it out again is work that only a living cell membrane can do. Phloem sap moves more slowly than water in the xylem, usually about half a metre to one metre in an hour. This is simplified. Phloem tissue also holds fibre cells and storage cells, not only sieve tubes.
- e.g. Neera and toddy are phloem sap tapped from the cut flower stalks of coconut, palmyra and date palms. Palm jaggery is made by boiling this sap down.
- e.g. Aphids, the tiny green or black insects on tender shoots of bean and hibiscus plants, feed on phloem sap.
- e.g. Cinnamon is the dried inner bark of a tree, and inner bark is phloem tissue.
### From source to sink: which way the food goes · *defined*
**Definition:** Phloem sap flows from a source to a sink. A source is any part that puts sugar into the phloem. A sink is any part that takes sugar out. So the flow can be upward or downward, and it can change with the season.

Xylem has one direction, which is up. Phloem has no fixed direction. A leaf in the sun is a source. Roots, fruits, flowers and growing tips are sinks, because they cannot make enough food for themselves. So sugar from one mango leaf may go up to a fruit and down to the roots at the same time, through different sieve tubes. A part can also change sides. A young leaf is a sink while it grows, and becomes a source once it is big. A carrot root is a sink all through the first season, filling up with food. Next season the same root becomes a source, and sends food up to build the flower stalk. A potato does the same when it sprouts in a dark cupboard. There is no light there, so every bit of the new shoot is built from food that travels out of the potato. People once thought food sap only went down. It goes wherever the sinks are.
- e.g. When a mango tree is full of growing fruit, the fruit is the strongest sink, and the tree makes fewer new leaves.
- e.g. A teak tree drops all their leaves in the dry season. The new leaves are built with food sent up from stores in the trunk and roots.
- e.g. Tapioca farmers in Kerala and Tamil Nadu harvest the fat roots, which are sinks packed with starch.
### What pushes the phloem sap along? · *idea*
The best explanation so far is called pressure flow. Ernst Münch, a German plant scientist, proposed it in 1930. At a source, cells load sugar into the sieve tubes. Water moves toward the place where it is held more tightly, and sugary sap holds water tightly, as you learned with osmosis. So water flows in from the xylem next door, and the pressure in the sieve tube rises. At a sink, cells take the sugar out, water leaves, and the pressure drops. Sap flows from high pressure to low pressure, the way toothpaste moves when you squeeze one end of the tube. The evidence is good. When an aphid's mouth tube is cut off while still stuck in a sieve tube, sap keeps oozing out of the cut end for hours, so the sap really is under pressure. In 2016, a team led by Michael Knoblauch measured pressures and pipe sizes in vines and tall trees and found they fit the idea. But the measurements are very hard to make, because a sieve tube seals itself the moment it is hurt. So scientists treat pressure flow as the leading idea, well supported, with details still being tested.
- e.g. Cut the tip off a full tube of toothpaste and squeeze the far end. The paste moves because the pressure is higher where you squeeze.
- e.g. An aphid does not need to suck. The pressure in the sieve tube pushes sap into her.
- e.g. Ants gather round aphids to drink honeydew, the sugary liquid that aphids pass out because they take in more sugar than they can use.
- **Ernst Münch and most plant scientists today:** Sugar loading at the source draws water in and raises the pressure. The pressure difference between source and sink pushes the sap along.
- **Scientists who doubted it:** In very tall trees the sieve tubes are long and narrow, and they asked whether pressure alone could push sap that far. Recent measurements suggest it can, but few trees have been measured.
### Where the pipes sit: centre, ring or arc · *defined*
**Definition:** In a root, the pipes are gathered in the centre. In the stem of most leafy plants, the bundles stand in a ring near the outside. In a leaf stalk, they lie in an arc.

The Pipe Maps test showed three different patterns. Why would one plant arrange the same pipes in three ways? Think about what each part has to bear. Xylem is the strongest tissue a young plant has, so where xylem sits decides where the part is strong. A root is pulled. When wind rocks the plant, the roots are tugged like ropes, and a rope is strongest with its strong strands together in the middle. A stem is bent. When a rod bends, the outside is stretched and squeezed the most, and the middle hardly at all. So strong material does the most good near the outside. That is why a hollow steel pipe makes a good cycle frame. A leaf stalk has to hold up a leaf blade and let it twist in the wind without snapping. An arc of bundles, like a gutter, does both. In grasses, palms, bananas and their relatives, the stem bundles are scattered all through the stem. This group of plants is called the monocots. The plants with a ring of bundles are mostly dicots. This reason is how engineers and botanists explain the patterns. It fits well, but the plant's history matters too.
- e.g. In a carrot, the paler core in the middle is xylem. That is where the dye appeared.
- e.g. A cut piece of sugarcane shows bundles scattered all over the cut face as small dots. Sugarcane is a grass, and a monocot.
- e.g. A celery stalk or a cauliflower leaf rib shows its bundles in a curved row. Each is a leaf stalk, not a stem.
### Meristems: where a plant makes new cells · *defined*
**Definition:** A meristem is a region where cells keep dividing. A plant grows longer from meristems at the tips of shoots and roots. A plant grows thicker from a meristem inside the stem, called the cambium.

A grown plant cell is boxed in by a stiff wall and usually stops dividing. So a plant keeps special groups of small, thin-walled cells that stay young and go on dividing. These are the meristems. The ones at the tips make shoots and roots longer. This explains a famous puzzle. If you hammer a nail into a tree trunk one metre above the ground, where is the nail ten years later, when the tree is much taller? The nail is still one metre above the ground. The trunk below the tip does not stretch. All the new height was added at the top. You grow differently. Your whole body grows until you are an adult, and then stops. A plant keeps their meristems for life, so a tree that is 500 years old still has tips as young as a seedling's.
- e.g. Pinch the tip off a tulsi shoot and that shoot stops getting longer. Side buds lower down then wake and grow.
- e.g. Grass keeps a meristem at the bottom of each leaf, so a lawn keeps growing after it is cut or grazed.
- e.g. A coconut palm has one growing tip at the top. If that tip is destroyed, the palm cannot make another.
### The cambium: how a trunk gets thicker · *defined*
**Definition:** The cambium is a thin sleeve of dividing cells between the wood and the bark. Each year it adds new xylem on its inner side and new phloem on its outer side. That is how a stem becomes a trunk.

The cambium is only a few cells thick. When one of its cells divides, one of the two new cells stays in the cambium. The other becomes a xylem cell on the inside, or a phloem cell on the outside. The cambium makes much more xylem than phloem. Xylem cells are hard, so they stay where they are, year after year. This is why a trunk is nearly all wood. As the wood grows, the cambium and the bark are pushed outward. Old phloem is soft, so it gets crushed, and the outer bark stretches and cracks. That is why the bark of an old neem or tamarind is deeply furrowed. So the living, working phloem of even a huge tree is a thin layer just under the bark. This is why girdling kills. If a ring of bark is removed all the way round, the phloem is cut. Water still rises in the wood, so the leaves stay green. But no sugar reaches the roots. When the roots have used up their stores, they die, and then the whole tree dies.
- e.g. A wire or nylon rope tied tightly round a young trunk and forgotten can slowly girdle the tree as the trunk thickens.
- e.g. Rubber tappers in Kerala cut only part of the way round the trunk, in a sloping line. They never cut a full ring.
- e.g. Gardeners make new plants by 'gootee', or air layering. They take a ring of bark off a branch. Food collects above the ring, and roots grow there.
### Growth rings: how wood records the seasons · *defined*
**Definition:** A growth ring is the wood added in one growing season. Where a tree has one growing season each year, the tree adds one ring each year.

At the start of a growing season, a tree has plenty of water and is opening new leaves. The cambium then makes wide xylem cells with thin walls, to carry a lot of water. This pale wood is called early wood. Late in the season, growth slows, and the cambium makes narrow cells with thick walls. This darker wood is called late wood. Then growth stops for the cold season or the dry season. When it starts again, wide pale cells lie right next to last season's narrow dark ones. That sharp line is what you see as a ring. In a good year, with enough rain and warmth, the ring is wide. In a drought, the ring is narrow. So a trunk keeps a record of every year the tree has lived. But rings can mislead. In a place that is warm and wet all year, a tree may grow without stopping and show no clear rings. A dry spell in the middle of the rains can make an extra, false ring. In a very bad year a tree may add no ring at all on one side. So scientists never trust one tree. They compare many.
- e.g. Teak grows in forests with a long dry season, so teak makes one clear ring each year.
- e.g. Deodar and pine in the Himalaya stop growing in winter, so they make one clear ring each year too.
- e.g. Many trees of the wet evergreen forests of the Western Ghats and the Andaman Islands have faint rings, or none that can be counted.
### Reading rings: a calendar kept by trees · *defined*
**Definition:** Dendrochronology is the science of using tree rings to find dates and to learn about past weather. It works by matching the pattern of wide and narrow rings between trees.

All the trees of one kind in one region live through the same wet and dry years. So they share the same pattern of wide and narrow rings, like a barcode. A scientist can match the inner rings of a living tree with the outer rings of an old beam from a house, and then match that beam with an even older log. In this way the calendar is carried back far beyond the age of any living tree. This matching is called cross-dating. Scientists do not need to cut a tree down. They drill out a core of wood as thin as a pencil, and the tree seals the hole. Andrew Douglass, an American astronomer, built the method in the early 1900s. In 1929 he used it to date the wooden beams of ancient villages in the American Southwest. Florence Hawley was one of the first scientists trained in the method, and she carried it to new regions of North America in the 1930s. In India, scientists at the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology in Pune and the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences in Lucknow read teak and deodar rings. From them they work out how strong the monsoon was in years long before anyone kept rain records.
- e.g. Teak rings from Kerala give a record of good and poor monsoon years that goes back about 500 years.
- e.g. Deodar trees in the western Himalaya give ring records more than a thousand years long.
- e.g. A bristlecone pine in California, named Methuselah, has more than 4,800 rings. This pine is one of the oldest known living trees.
### Sapwood, heartwood and bark: which parts are alive · *defined*
**Definition:** In a tree trunk, the outer wood, called sapwood, carries water. The inner wood, called heartwood, no longer does. The inner bark is living phloem, and the outer bark is dead cork.

Xylem pipes do not last for ever. After some years, air gets into them or they are plugged, and they stop carrying water. The tree then fills this old wood with resins and other substances that slow down rot. It turns darker and becomes heartwood. Every cell in heartwood is dead. Heartwood still helps to hold the tree up. In sapwood, the pipes are dead too, but thin sheets of living cells run through it and store food. So where is the living tree? The living tree is in the leaves, buds and root tips, in the cambium and inner bark, and in those living cells of the sapwood. In a great banyan or tamarind, that is a thin living layer wrapped round a core of dead wood. This explains why an old tree with a hollow trunk can stand green and healthy. Fungi have eaten the dead heartwood, but the living layer and the working sapwood are whole. Outer bark is made by a second cambium, the cork cambium. Cork cells fill with a waxy substance and die. They keep water in, and keep out insects, fungi and the heat of small fires.
- e.g. Old tamarind and banyan trees often have hollow trunks big enough for a child to stand in, and still bear leaves and fruit every year.
- e.g. Sandalwood oil comes from the heartwood of the sandalwood tree, which grows in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. Bottle corks and cork notice boards are the thick outer bark of the cork oak, peeled off without cutting into the living layer.
### Palms and bamboo: tall plants without rings · *defined*
**Definition:** Palms, bamboos and other monocots have no cambium sleeve. Their stems do not add a new layer of wood each year, so they have no growth rings.

A coconut palm's trunk is about as thick near the top as near the bottom. A young palm first spends some years growing wide at ground level. After that, the trunk grows up but hardly grows thicker. Its strength comes from hundreds of vascular bundles scattered through the trunk, each wrapped in hard fibres. Because there is no cambium, a palm cannot heal a deep cut in the trunk by growing new wood over it, as a mango tree can. A cut all the way round the surface does not usually kill a palm, though, because the phloem is spread all through the trunk and not gathered in a layer under the bark. Bamboo is a giant grass. A bamboo shoot comes out of the ground already at full width, and reaches full height in a few months. To guess the age of a coconut palm, people count the ring-shaped scars left by fallen leaves. A grown palm drops roughly a dozen leaves a year, so the count gives only a rough age.
- e.g. Coconut wood has no rings. The cut end of a palm trunk shows dark dots, which are the scattered bundles.
- e.g. Bamboo scaffolding on building sites is strong for its weight because its hard fibres are packed near the outside of a hollow tube.
- e.g. A banana 'trunk' is not a stem at all. It is a roll of leaf stalks wrapped tightly round each other.
### Pipes changed the world: how plants became tall · *defined*
**Definition:** The first land plants had no pipes and stayed a few centimetres tall. Plants with xylem and phloem appeared more than 420 million years ago. Wood appeared about 400 million years ago, and after that came forests.

A moss has no true xylem, so water has to soak from cell to cell. That is slow, so a moss stays small and lives in damp places. Fossils show that the first plants with pipes were thin green stalks with no leaves. Once plants could stiffen their pipes with lignin, they could stand taller and reach more light than their neighbours. Then some plants gained a cambium and began to make wood. By about 385 million years ago there were forests of real trees. This changed the planet. Roots broke rock into soil. Leaves took carbon dioxide out of the air. In the great swamps of that time, dead trunks piled up without rotting away, and were buried. Over millions of years, much of that buried wood became coal. Why did so much wood escape rotting? One idea is that few living things could yet digest lignin. Another is that the swamps were so waterlogged that wood rotted very slowly, whatever lived there. Scientists still argue about this. So the coal that still fuels many of India's power stations is mostly made from the stems, bark and wood of ancient plants. How tall can pipes carry a tree? The tallest tree known today is a coast redwood about 116 metres high. In 2004, George Koch and his team climbed the tallest redwoods and measured how hard it was for water to reach the top. They estimated a limit of about 122 to 130 metres. Nobody is sure the limit is right.
- e.g. Moss on a wet wall in the monsoon is only a few centimetres tall, because moss has no stiff pipes.
- e.g. The coal mined at Jharia in Jharkhand and Singareni in Telangana formed from plants that lived more than 250 million years ago.
- e.g. Fossil tree trunks about 20 million years old, turned to stone, can be seen at the National Fossil Wood Park at Tiruvakkarai in Tamil Nadu.
### Do the pipes carry messages too? · *debated*
Phloem carries more than food. Plants sense, remember and decide, and to do so their parts must tell each other things. Some of these messages are now known to travel in the phloem. A leaf senses how long the days and nights are. When they reach the right length, the leaf makes a small protein that travels in the phloem to the shoot tip, and the tip then starts making flowers. Scientists searched for this flowering signal for seventy years and found it in 2007. When a caterpillar bites one leaf, a signal races through the veins to far-off leaves in a minute or two, and those leaves begin making defence chemicals. In 2018, Masatsugu Toyota and his team filmed this as waves of light in a plant made to glow. Jagadish Chandra Bose in Kolkata measured electric signals in plants a hundred years ago. In 1926 he wrote that the phloem carries them, and that this tissue works like a nerve. Scientists agree the signals are real. They disagree about what to call them.
- e.g. A spinach plant flowers when days grow long, because the leaves send a flowering signal to the tips through the phloem.
- e.g. Touch one leaflet of a touch-me-not plant firmly and the folding spreads along the whole leaf, as a signal travels from part to part.
- e.g. A bitten tomato leaf sets off defences in leaves the caterpillar has not reached yet.
- **Jagadish Chandra Bose, and today Stefano Mancuso, František Baluška and others:** Plants have a signalling system that does the work of a nervous system. They sense, pass on signals, remember and choose. The phloem is one of its main pathways.
- **Lincoln Taiz and many other plant scientists:** The signals are real, but plants have no nerve cells and no brain. Words like 'nerve' and 'think' belong to animals and mislead when used for plants. The responses can be explained by chemistry.
- **Scientists in between:** Plants plainly process information in their own way. It is better to study how they do it than to argue about whether animal words fit.

## Mostly agreed
- Plants with veins have two transport tissues. Xylem carries water and minerals upward. Phloem carries sugar from sources to sinks. *How we know:* Dye in water shows up only in the xylem. Sugar marked with traceable carbon, fed to one leaf, is later found in the phloem and in roots, fruits and young leaves.
- Working xylem pipes are dead, hollow cells. Working phloem pipes are living cells. *How we know:* Under a microscope, xylem vessels are empty tubes with thick walls. Sieve tubes have a living lining, and they stop carrying sap when the cells are killed with heat, while the xylem beside them goes on carrying water.
- A tree grows taller only at the tips and thicker only from the cambium. *How we know:* Marks and nails on trunks stay at the same height for decades. Thin slices of a twig show the layer of dividing cells between wood and bark.
- Removing a complete ring of bark kills most trees, because it cuts the phloem. *How we know:* This has been repeated for 350 years. The bark swells above the ring, the leaves stay green at first, and the roots starve. Palms and other monocots are the exception, because their phloem is not in one layer.
- Where the year has one growing season, trees add one ring a year, and ring width follows the weather. *How we know:* Trees of known age, planted on a known date, have the right number of rings. Ring patterns match between trees of the same region, and match rain records where records exist.
- Most of the wood in a tree trunk is dead, and the tree lives in a thin layer round that wood. *How we know:* Stains that colour only living cells mark the inner bark, the cambium and scattered cells in the sapwood. They leave the heartwood blank. Hollow trees go on living.

## How we found out
**Marcello Malpighi, an Italian doctor. Theodor Hartig, a German forester. John Kennedy and Thomas Mittler, insect scientists working in England., The 1670s (Malpighi's ring of bark), 1837 (Hartig's sieve tubes), 1953 (the aphid's mouth tube), and the 1940s and 1950s (traceable carbon)..** About 350 years ago, the Italian doctor Marcello Malpighi cut a ring of bark off a branch. The bark swelled above the cut. He reasoned that food made in the leaves travels down in the bark. For the next 250 years, most books said that food sap only sinks, from the leaves to the roots. In 1837 the German forester Theodor Hartig found the food pipes themselves, the sieve tubes. But nobody could collect clean sap from one tube, because a tube seals itself when cut. Then scientists noticed that aphids already tap single tubes. In 1953, John Kennedy and Thomas Mittler in England cut through an aphid's mouth tube while she was feeding. Sap went on dripping from the cut tube for hours. So the sap was under pressure, and it was full of sugar. In the same years, other scientists gave single leaves carbon dioxide made with a kind of carbon that can be traced. The marked sugar turned up in the roots below. It also turned up in young leaves and fruits above. So food does not only sink. Food goes to wherever the plant is using or storing it.  
*What people believed before:* That food sap travels only downward, from the leaves to the roots. Traceable carbon showed sugar from one leaf moving up to fruits and young leaves as well as down to roots.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Sap goes round a plant the way blood goes round a body.** (Several naturalists in the 1600s, after William Harvey showed that blood circulates; *rejected*)  
  For: Liquid does move both up and down in a plant, and a little water does pass from the phloem back to the xylem.  
  Against: A plant has no heart and no closed loop. Xylem sap and phloem sap are two different liquids in two separate sets of pipes. Most of the water that goes up leaves through the leaves and never comes back down. Stephen Hales argued this in 1727 from his measurements.
- **A tree trunk grows upward, so a mark on the trunk rises as the tree gets taller.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Trees do get taller every year, and low branches on old trees are often missing, which makes it look as if they moved up.  
  Against: A trunk gets longer only at the tip. Nails, signs and carved names stay at the same height for the whole life of the tree. Low branches seem to rise because the old ones die and drop off.
- **To find a tree's age, just count the rings. One ring is always one year.** (Many books and nature guides; *debated*)  
  For: It works well for teak, pine, deodar and other trees that have one clear growing season each year.  
  Against: Some tropical trees make no countable rings. A dry spell in mid-season can add a false ring, and a very bad year can leave no ring. Scientists check by matching many trees.
- **The heartwood is dead, so it is useless to the tree.** (A common way of speaking about old trees; *debated*)  
  For: Heartwood carries no water and stores no food. Hollow trees can live for centuries without it.  
  Against: Heartwood adds stiffness, and it is filled with substances that slow rot, which protects the trunk. Some engineers who study trees argue that a hollow trunk with a thick enough shell is nearly as strong as a solid one. Others find that hollow trees fail more often in storms.
- **The phloem is the nervous system of a plant.** (Jagadish Chandra Bose in 1926, and some plant scientists today; *minority*)  
  For: Electric signals and chemical signals do travel through the phloem, quickly and over long distances. Plants use them to respond to wounds, touch and day length.  
  Against: Plants have no nerve cells, no synapses and no brain. Many plant scientists say that calling phloem a nervous system stretches the word too far. They agree that the signals are real.

## Roots
We have learned that a plant has dead pipes for water and living pipes for food, and that a tree trunk is mostly dead wood inside a thin living layer. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Pipe Maps test, dye climbed through xylem pipes. Each pipe is a column of cells that built hard walls, then died and emptied. Water can pass only because those cells are dead. So a living tree depends on dead cells, and most of a trunk is made of them. In a tree, dying at the right time is one of the jobs a cell can have.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort everything into 'alive' or 'dead', and we think a living body must be alive all the way through. We also think a cell that dies has failed. A tree shows that neither is true. A xylem cell's own genes take the cell apart at the right moment, and the emptied cell then works for the tree for years.  
*Evidence:* In Pipe Maps and in Bark or Wood, the dye travelled only in the xylem, and working xylem pipes are dead cells. A hollow tamarind tree still bears leaves and fruit. Yet a tree whose dead wood is whole dies if one thin ring of living bark is removed.

- *who am i:* The top layer of your skin, your hair and your nails are dead cells that your body made and keeps for protection. So you, too, are a living body that depends on dead cells.
- *what is life:* A living body is not alive in every part. Living cells and dead cells work together in a tree, and the tree needs both.
- *what is this world:* A wooden door, a page of your notebook and a jute bag are made of cell walls that plants built. Much of what people build with was built first by living cells.

A whole tree grows from one cell, the fertilised egg cell inside a seed. That cell divides, and all the cells that follow carry the same instructions. Yet some become skin, some become packing, some become living phloem, and some die to become xylem. In the cambium, a single thin layer makes wood on one side and bark on the other. So one cell becomes the many tissues of a tree, and the many tissues work as one body.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.11:** Uddalaka shows his son a great tree. If someone cuts the tree at the root, in the middle or at the top, sap runs out, yet the tree lives. But if life leaves one branch, that branch dries up. If life leaves the whole tree, the whole tree dries up. (Uddalaka saw what this unit shows: sap runs through every part of a tree, and one part can die while the rest lives. He used the tree to ask what it is that leaves when a living being dies. Science can describe the pipes and the cells. That question is still open.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 11:** Clay is shaped into a pot, but the empty space inside is what makes the pot useful. Doors and windows are cut into a wall, and the empty space is what makes the room useful. (A xylem pipe is useful to the tree because the pipe is empty. The cell had to clear out everything inside before water could pass.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on 'interbeing':** Look deeply into a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in it. Without them the paper could not be here. (Paper is made from wood, and wood is xylem cells. The page really is made of the pipes that once carried rain water up a tree.)
- Science (settled): The death of a xylem cell is switched on by the cell's own genes, at a set stage of growth. Biologists call this programmed cell death.
- Science (settled): Every cell of a tree comes from one first cell and carries nearly the same DNA. Cells become different tissues by using different parts of it.
- Science (open): How each new cell beside the cambium comes to be wood or phloem is only partly understood. Signals from neighbouring cells are part of the answer.
- Other view, Many biologists: A xylem cell does not die 'for' the tree. Its death is a chain of chemical steps set off by its genes, and words like 'job' are our way of telling the story. Some also say the dead wood is not part of the living tree at all. They see it as something the tree has made, as a snail makes a shell.
- Practice, A hand on wood (3 min): Let's find something made of bare wood, like a door or a table. Put your palm flat on it and close your eyes. Breathe slowly five times. Every line you can feel was made by living cells, and each cell built a wall and then died. Water once rose through this wood to leaves in the sun. Now touch your own hair. Those are cells your body made, and they are dead too. They are still part of you.
- Ask: Is the dead wood inside a living tree part of the tree, or something the tree made?
- Ask: Your hair and nails are dead cells. Are they part of you?
- Ask: A xylem cell works only after dying. Does that make dying a part of living?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Most of a tree trunk is dead cells. So where does the living tree begin and end? (hint: Think of a hollow tamarind tree that still bears fruit. Then think of your own hair and nails.)
- *deep:* A cambium cell makes wood on one side and bark on the other. How could one cell tell which side is which? (hint: What might be different on the two sides: the squeeze, the signals from neighbours, the food arriving?)
- *big:* A tree keeps a mark of every dry year in their rings. Is that a kind of memory? (hint: What does a memory need? Does it need someone to read it? Do the tree's later rings depend on the earlier years?)
- *big:* A xylem cell works only after dying. Can you think of anything else that does its job only after it has ended? (hint: Think of a seed coat, a snail's shell, a spoken word, a fallen leaf feeding the soil.)
- *big:* You stop growing when you are an adult. A tree keeps growing at the tips for life. Why might animals and plants differ in this? (hint: An animal moves about to find food. A plant stays in one place and must grow toward what they need.)
- *deep:* If a rope slowly girdles a tree and nobody notices for years, how could the tree's own body tell the story afterwards? (hint: Where would the trunk be fatter? What might the rings look like above and below the rope?)

## Story: The Clothes Wire
*A made-up story. Ammu, her family and the mango tree are imagined. The science is real. A wire left round a trunk can slowly cut off the bark as the tree thickens. A tree choked like this often flowers heavily before weakening, and the trunk swells above the wire. Rubber tappers in Kerala really do tap before sunrise, and they cut only part of the way round the trunk.*

The mango tree behind Ammu's house had never flowered like this. In January every twig ended in a spray of tiny flowers. The tree stood over the yard like a yellow cloud, and the bees were loud from morning to dusk.

'A blessing,' said Thankamma from next door, looking over the fence. 'You will be giving away mangoes by the basket.'

By April there were no baskets. The mangoes had set in hundreds, small and hard as marbles. Then they dropped, a few every night. Ammu's little brother Unni collected them in a bucket and counted them. He got to two hundred and gave up.

In May the leaves began to go wrong. They were smaller than last year's, and pale, and the tree was thin enough to see the sky through.

'Water her,' said Thankamma. So Ammu and Unni carried buckets every evening. Nothing changed. 'Cow dung,' said Thankamma. Amma dug in two baskets of it. Nothing changed.

'She is drinking,' Amma said one evening, with her hand on a low branch. 'Look. Not one leaf is drooping. A thirsty tree droops.' She frowned at the tree for a while. 'I don't know what she wants.'

Amma knew trees. She tapped rubber on the Kuruvilla estate, three hundred trees before sunrise, and in the holidays Ammu went with her to carry the bucket.

They left at five, with lamps strapped to their heads. In the dark the rubber trees stood in rows, each with a sloping cut in the bark and a coconut shell hanging below it. Amma set her knife in yesterday's cut and drew it down along the slope. A ribbon of bark as thin as a fingernail curled away. White latex beaded on the fresh edge and began to run down toward the shell.

Ammu had watched this a thousand times. That morning she asked about it. 'Why do you stop halfway round? If you went all the way round, you would get twice as much.'

'For one season,' said Amma. She did not stop walking. 'Then you would have a dead tree.'

'Why?'

'The leaves make the tree's food. It comes down to the roots just here, under the bark.' She tapped the trunk with the back of her knife. 'Not in the wood. The wood carries water up. If you cut the bark the whole way round, the food has no road down. The top goes on looking fine. It has water, it has sun. And all that time the roots are starving in the ground. By the time the leaves tell you, it is too late.' She set her knife to the next tree. 'So we always leave her a road.'

They were home by nine. Amma slept. Ammu washed the school uniforms and carried them out to the line.

The clothes wire ran from a hook on the house to the mango tree. It went once round the trunk and was twisted to itself. It had been there since before Ammu was born. She threw Unni's shirt over the wire and reached to pull it straight. Her hand came to rest on the trunk.

Above the wire, the trunk bulged. It pushed out over the wire in a thick lip, like dough rising over the edge of a tin. Below the wire, the trunk was narrower, and the bark there looked dry.

She stood still with her hand on the lip. She thought of the food with no road down, and of the top that went on looking fine.

She walked round the tree with one finger on the wire. On the far side the wire was gone. The bark had closed over it, and only a seam showed where it lay inside. But near the twisted knot, the wire stood a little away from the trunk. Under the knot, a strip of bark as wide as her palm ran straight down, smooth and unbroken.

She woke Amma, which was not allowed.

Amma looked at the trunk for a long time without saying anything. Then she fetched the pliers. She cut the wire at the knot and pulled, and a hand's length came free. The rest would not move. She cut again where the wire went into the bark, and left the buried part where it was. 'If I dig that out, I do the wire's work for it,' she said.

She laid her hand flat on the smooth strip under the knot. 'This is all she has been living on. This one road.'

'Will she get better?' said Unni.

'I don't know,' said Amma. 'Ask her next year.'

The rains came in June. One wet afternoon Ammu went out under an umbrella and looked at the groove where the wire had been. Along its upper edge the bark had a new rim, rounded and tight. She scratched the rim gently with her thumbnail. Under the brown it was green.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the tree had plenty of water, because water travels in the wood, and the wire did not reach the wood. But a tree's food travels down in the phloem, just under the bark. The wire squeezed the bark shut nearly all the way round, so sugar piled up above it and the roots went hungry. That is why the tree flowered so heavily, why the trunk bulged above the wire, and why watering did not help.

- Ask: The leaves were not drooping, so the tree had enough water. What was the tree short of, and in which part?
- Ask: Why did the trunk bulge above the wire and not below it?
- Ask: Why did Amma leave the buried part of the wire inside the tree?
- Ask: A rubber tapper cuts only part of the way round the trunk. What does that leave for the tree?
- Ask: What would you look for on a tree near your home to check that nothing is choking the bark?

### e2 · Bark or Wood: which part of a twig carries the water? (35 min, 2 days)
- **question:** A woody twig has bark on the outside, wood inside and a soft centre. Which of these carries water up to the leaves?
- **predict:** Guess first. When a twig stands in coloured water, where will the colour travel: in the bark, in the wood, in the soft centre, or in all three? Draw your guess on a circle. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use one twig, so the bark, the wood and the centre all get the same water for the same time. Compare them at the same height. Look only above the level of the coloured water.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts and peels. Snip the thorns off a rose stem first. Food colour stains, so work on newspaper.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each height. Draw the cut face of the twig and colour the part that the dye reached. Write your claim, your evidence and your reasoning.
- **results table:** Height above the water line (cm) | Inner side of the bark: coloured or not? | Surface of the wood: coloured or not? | Soft centre: coloured or not?
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess and draw. Stand the twig in coloured water. Leave it for a day. (10 min); day 2: Peel windows in the bark at three heights, look at the bark and the wood, cut the twig across, and say what you found. (25 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the cut end of the twig together. 2. Find the bark, the wood and the soft centre. 3. Ask your child to draw the cut face and colour in their guess. 4. Ask for the reason.  
  *Parent says:* This twig has bark, wood and a soft centre. Which part do you think carries water up? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the cut end. Find the bark, the wood and the centre. Colour in your guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The bark, because it is wet and green underneath.; The centre, because it is soft like a sponge.; The wood, because trees are mostly wood.)
  - Note: Each of these guesses is reasonable. Do not say which is right.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir food colour into 3 centimetres of water until it is as dark as ink. 2. Hold the bottom of the twig under water in a bowl and cut off 2 centimetres. 3. Stand the twig in the coloured water at once. 4. Put it in a bright, airy place.  
  *Parent says:* We cut under water so that no air gets into the pipes. Now we leave the twig to drink for a day.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to cut the end under water. Stand the twig in the dark water. Leave it for one day.
  - Note: Keep the leaves on. If the leaves hang limp after an hour, cut the end again under water.
  - Wait: Leave it for 1 day
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the twig out and wipe it. 2. Measure 5 centimetres up from the water line. 3. There, peel off a window of bark about 2 centimetres long. 4. Look at the inner side of the peeled bark. 5. Look at the surface of the wood under it. 6. Write what you see in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the inside of the bark, and then at the wood under it. Which one has the colour?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to peel a small window of bark. Look inside the bark. Look at the wood. Which is coloured?
  - Ask: What do you notice? (good: The wood has coloured lines on it.; The bark is green or white inside, with no dye.; The bark peels off easily and the wood is slippery.)
  - Note: The bark of a fresh twig slips off easily. The wet, slippery surface is where the cambium was. Expect coloured streaks on the wood and clean bark.
- **s4 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Peel another window at 10 centimetres and one at 15 centimetres. 2. Fill in the table for each. 3. Cut the twig straight across at 5 centimetres. 4. Look at the cut face with the magnifying glass.  
  *Parent says:* How high did the colour climb? And on the cut face, is the colour in the bark, the wood or the centre?  
  *Child mode:* Look at two more windows higher up. Then ask a grown-up to cut across. Where is the colour?
  - Note: On the cut face the colour is in the wood, often strongest in the outer part of the wood. The soft centre, called the pith, and the bark stay uncoloured.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. 2. Then ask what the bark might carry, if it does not carry the water. 3. Read the explanation card 'Two sets of pipes in every vein' together.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find, what did you see, and why do you think so? And if the bark carries no water, what could it be carrying?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found and what you saw. If bark carries no water, what could it carry?
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. Claim: the wood carries the water. Evidence: at 5, 10 and 15 centimetres the wood was coloured and the bark was not. Reasoning: the wood is made of xylem pipes. The second question leads to phloem, the food pipes in the inner bark.
  - Check: In Bark or Wood, the colour was in the wood and not in the bark. What does this show? → **Water travels up in the wood**

### a6 · Build the Two Pipes: a paper model of xylem and phloem (20 min)

### c1 · Tap Without Killing: design a rubber tapper's cut (25 min)
- **problem:** Wrap a sheet of paper round a bottle to make a trunk. Draw 24 lines from top to bottom. Each line is a strand of bark that carries food down and latex. Design one cut that crosses as many lines as you can, while at least 12 lines stay whole from top to bottom.

- **general:** Cut a sloping line half the way round the trunk. This is what rubber tappers in Kerala do.; Cut a V shape on one side, with the cup at the point of the V.; Cut a short sloping line a quarter of the way round, and accept less latex.
- **creative:** Plan for several years: tap one half of the trunk while the bark on the other half grows back.; Shave off only a very thin strip from the same cut each day, as real tappers do, so that little bark is used up.; Try two short cuts at different heights, and check whether more than 12 lines are now cut.

- **s3 (9 min)**

## Worksheets
### Pipes, tissues and rings (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which tissue carries water up from the roots?  
   Options: Phloem / Xylem / Epidermis / Cambium  
   **Answer:** Xylem. *Why:* Xylem carries water and minerals upward. Choosing phloem mixes up the two pipes: phloem carries sugar.
2. (mcq) Which pipes are made of living cells?  
   Options: Xylem vessels / Sieve tubes in the phloem / Both kinds / Neither kind  
   **Answer:** Sieve tubes in the phloem. *Why:* Sieve tubes are living cells. Xylem vessels are dead and empty when they work. Choosing 'both' is a fair guess, because we expect every part of a living plant to be alive.
3. (truefalse) True or false: food in the phloem can only travel downward.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Phloem carries sugar from a source to a sink, and the sink may be above, like a fruit or a young leaf.
4. (fill) A group of cells of the same kind doing one job together is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['tissue']. *Why:* A tissue is a team of similar cells. Skin, packing and transport are the three kinds of plant tissue.
5. (match) Match each tissue to its job.  
   **Answer:** Epidermis → keeps water in and germs out; Xylem → carries water up; Phloem → carries sugar to where it is needed; Ground tissue → fills the space and stores food; Cambium → makes new wood and new bark. *Why:* Each tissue has one main job. A strong answer can point to each one on a drawing of a cut stem.
6. (sort) Sort these plant parts: is each one a source or a sink?  
   **Answer:** a mango leaf in sunshine → Source; a growing mango fruit → Sink; a root tip → Sink; a sprouting potato → Source; a flower bud → Sink; a tapioca root filling with starch → Sink. *Why:* A source puts sugar into the phloem. A sink takes sugar out. The sprouting potato is a source, because stored food is leaving the potato.
7. (write) In a dye test, only small dots on a stem's cut face were coloured. What is the uncoloured part doing?  
   **Answer:** The uncoloured part is mostly ground tissue, which stores food, makes food if it is green, and helps hold the stem up. There is also skin on the outside, and phloem beside each dot, which the dye does not show.. *Why:* A strong answer names at least one other tissue and its job, and does not say the uncoloured part is 'nothing'.
8. (draw) Draw the cut face of a leafy stem. Show and label the skin, a ring of pipe bundles and the packing tissue.  
   **Answer:** A circle with a thin outer skin, a ring of small dots near the edge, and the rest filled in as packing.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the bundles in a ring near the outside, not in the centre and not filling the whole face.
9. (mcq) Where are the water pipes in a carrot root?  
   Options: In a ring near the skin / In the core at the centre / Scattered everywhere / A carrot has none  
   **Answer:** In the core at the centre. *Why:* In a root the xylem is in the centre. In Pipe Maps the dye showed up in the carrot's pale core. A ring near the skin is the pattern of a stem.
10. (order) Put these layers of a tree trunk in order, from the centre outward.  
   **Answer:** ['Heartwood', 'Sapwood', 'Cambium', 'Inner bark (phloem)', 'Outer bark (cork)']. *Why:* The oldest wood is in the centre. The cambium lies between the wood and the bark, with the living phloem just outside it.
11. (truefalse) True or false: a nail hammered into a tree trunk rises as the tree grows taller.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A tree grows taller only at the tips. The trunk below does not stretch, so the nail stays at the same height.
12. (fill) The thin layer between the wood and the bark that makes new wood is the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['cambium']. *Why:* The cambium is a sleeve of dividing cells. It adds xylem on the inside and phloem on the outside.
13. (label) Draw a tree stump seen from above, with five rings. Then label the bark, one growth ring and the centre.  
   **Answer:** ['Bark round the outside', 'One growth ring: one band of wood', 'Centre: the oldest wood']. *Why:* The centre is the oldest part, and the newest ring lies just under the bark.
14. (mcq) A teak stump has one very narrow ring among wide ones. What is the most likely cause?  
   Options: A year with little rain / A year with two summers / Someone painted the tree / A bird nested in the tree  
   **Answer:** A year with little rain. *Why:* A narrow ring means the tree grew little that year. In teak the usual cause is a poor monsoon. Insects or a fire can do it too.
15. (write) Why does a tree die if a ring of bark is removed all the way round the trunk?  
   **Answer:** The inner bark holds the phloem, which carries sugar from the leaves down to the roots. With the bark cut all the way round, no sugar reaches the roots. The roots use up their stores and die, and then the tree cannot take in water.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the bark carries, where it was going, and why the leaves stay green at first: water still rises in the wood.
### Why the pipes are built this way (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why must a xylem cell die before carrying water?  
   Options: Dead cells weigh less / An empty tube lets water through / Dead cells make sugar / To save food  
   **Answer:** An empty tube lets water through. *Why:* A living cell is full of contents that would block the flow. Only an empty tube lets water pass easily. The death is switched on by the cell's own genes.
2. (write) A sieve tube cell has no nucleus. How does it stay alive?  
   **Answer:** Each sieve tube cell has a companion cell alongside. The companion cell has a nucleus and makes what both cells need. The two are joined by tiny channels.. *Why:* A strong answer names the companion cell and says why the sieve tube cell gave up the nucleus: to leave room for sap to flow.
3. (truefalse) True or false: when a stem stands in coloured water, the dye stains the phloem.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The dye travels with the water, so it stains the xylem. The phloem lies beside each coloured dot and stays unstained.
4. (fill) A part of a plant that takes sugar out of the phloem is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['sink']. *Why:* Fruits, root tips, buds and stores are sinks. A part that gives sugar to the phloem is a source.
5. (sort) Sort these plants: are the pipe bundles in the stem in a ring, or scattered?  
   **Answer:** mango → Ring; sugarcane → Scattered; coconut palm → Scattered; hibiscus → Ring; maize → Scattered; tulsi → Ring; bamboo → Scattered; neem → Ring. *Why:* Grasses, palms and bamboo are monocots, with scattered bundles and straight leaf veins. Mango, hibiscus, tulsi and neem have a ring of bundles and net veins.
6. (mcq) A stem bends in the wind. Where does strong tissue help it most?  
   Options: In the very centre / Near the outside / Only at the bottom / Only in the leaves  
   **Answer:** Near the outside. *Why:* When a rod bends, the outside is stretched and squeezed most. So strong tissue near the outside resists bending best. A root is pulled, not bent, so its strong tissue is in the centre.
7. (draw) Draw one vascular bundle from a stem. Label the xylem and the phloem, and show which side faces the skin.  
   **Answer:** A small oval with larger pipes on the inner side labelled xylem, and smaller cells on the outer side labelled phloem, with an arrow to the skin.. *Why:* In a stem the phloem is on the outer side of each bundle and the xylem on the inner side.
8. (perspective) An old tamarind tree has a hollow trunk but is full of leaves and fruit. Two neighbours disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the heartwood was dead already, and the living cambium, phloem and sapwood are whole, so the tree is alive and feeding. It also grants that a hollow trunk can be weaker in a storm, so the tree should be checked for safety.. *Why:* Both have a point. Being alive and being safe near a house are two different questions.
9. (order) Put the life of a xylem cell in order.  
   **Answer:** ['A new cell forms on the inner side of the cambium', 'The cell grows long', 'The cell builds a thick wall with lignin', 'The cell breaks down everything inside and dies', 'The empty cell carries water']. *Why:* The wall must be built while the cell is alive. The cell begins work as a pipe only after dying and emptying.
10. (write) A young leaf is a sink. Later the same leaf becomes a source. Explain what changes.  
   **Answer:** A young leaf is small and still growing, so it uses more sugar than it can make. Sugar comes in through the phloem. When the leaf is full size, it makes more sugar than it uses, and sends the extra out through the phloem.. *Why:* A strong answer compares how much sugar the leaf makes with how much it uses.
11. (match) Match each thing people use to the plant tissue it comes from.  
   **Answer:** Jute fibre → strong fibre cells in the stem's phloem; Cinnamon stick → inner bark; Teak plank → xylem; Bottle cork → outer bark; Palm jaggery → phloem sap, boiled down. *Why:* People use every tissue of a plant. A strong answer can say what job each tissue did for the plant.
12. (mcq) A tight nylon rope has girdled a tree for years. Where is the trunk fatter?  
   Options: Just above the rope / Just below the rope / The same on both sides  
   **Answer:** Just above the rope. *Why:* Sugar coming down in the phloem is held up above the rope, so the trunk grows more there. Below the rope the trunk is starved and stays thin.
13. (odd) Three of these are made of dead cells when they do their job. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Xylem vessel / Cork / Heartwood / Sieve tube  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A sieve tube is alive when it works. Xylem vessels, cork and heartwood are all dead cells that still do a job for the tree.
14. (write) Why can nobody find the age of a coconut palm by counting rings?  
   **Answer:** A coconut palm has no cambium, so the trunk does not add a new layer of wood each year. The pipe bundles are scattered through the trunk, and there are no rings to count.. *Why:* A strong answer names the missing cambium. It may add that leaf scars give a rough age.
15. (mcq) The early wood in a growth ring looks pale. Why?  
   Options: Its cells are wide, with thin walls / Its cells are narrow, with thick walls / It is full of sugar / It has been dead for longer  
   **Answer:** Its cells are wide, with thin walls. *Why:* Early in the season the tree makes wide pipes with thin walls, so there is more empty space and less wall. Late wood has narrow cells with thick walls, so it looks darker.
### Evidence and arguments about the pipes (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) In early spring, sweet sap is tapped from the wood of sugar maples. Why is that surprising? Suggest an explanation.  
   **Answer:** It is surprising because wood is xylem, which usually carries water with very little sugar. In early spring the maple has no leaves yet. The tree turns starch stored in the wood into sugar and sends it up in the xylem sap to the buds.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that the rule 'xylem carries water, phloem carries sugar' has exceptions, and reasons from the season: no leaves, so no working source in the crown.
2. (perspective) Scientists agree that signals travel through the phloem. They disagree about what to call this.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence: signals from a bitten leaf reach far leaves within minutes, and a touch-me-not folds leaflets far from the touch. It also grants that plants have no nerve cells or brain. It may say the plant does the same job in a different way.. *Why:* Scientists agree on the measurements and disagree on the words. Plants sense, signal and respond through their whole body.
3. (mcq) An aphid's mouth tube is cut while she feeds, and sap keeps dripping from it. What does this show?  
   Options: Phloem sap is under pressure / Aphids pump the sap out / Xylem pipes leak / The plant is dying  
   **Answer:** Phloem sap is under pressure. *Why:* The aphid is gone, so nothing is sucking. The sap must be pushed out by pressure inside the sieve tube. This is strong evidence for pressure flow.
4. (write) At a leaf, sugar is loaded into a sieve tube. Explain why water then moves into the sieve tube.  
   **Answer:** Water moves toward the place where it is held more tightly. Sugary sap holds water tightly. So water moves by osmosis from the xylem next door into the sieve tube. This raises the pressure there.. *Why:* A strong answer uses osmosis, and links the extra water to higher pressure that pushes the sap along.
5. (truefalse) True or false: every tree in the world makes exactly one ring each year.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A tree makes one clear ring a year only where growth stops once a year. Some tropical trees make no clear rings, and odd weather can add a false ring or leave one out.
6. (mcq) Why do scientists compare the rings of many trees before trusting a date?  
   Options: One tree can have false or missing rings / Old trees are hard to find / Rings fade in sunlight / Every tree has a different pattern  
   **Answer:** One tree can have false or missing rings. *Why:* A single tree may skip a ring in a very bad year or add a false one. A pattern shared by many trees is far more trustworthy. Trees of one region share their pattern, which is what makes matching possible.
7. (write) A friend says dye soaks up a stem like ink into chalk. Give two observations that argue against this.  
   **Answer:** First, only small dots on the cut face are coloured, not the whole face. Second, when the stem is cut along its length, the colour runs in thin lines. Both fit pipes, and neither fits soaking.. *Why:* A strong answer uses observations, not just the word 'xylem'. It may add that a leafy stem takes up colour faster than a bare one.
8. (fill) Wood is hard and stiff because its cell walls contain ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lignin']. *Why:* Lignin hardens and waterproofs the walls of xylem cells. It keeps the pipes open and holds the tree up.
9. (sort) Sort these cells. Is each one living or dead while at work?  
   **Answer:** sieve tube cell → Living; xylem vessel cell → Dead; companion cell → Living; cork cell → Dead; cambium cell → Living; heartwood cell → Dead. *Why:* Loading sugar and dividing need living cells. Carrying water, protecting and holding up are done by dead cells with strong walls.
10. (mcq) Why could the first land plants, without xylem, not grow tall?  
   Options: They could not lift water or stand stiff / There was not enough sunlight / The air had no carbon dioxide / They had no seeds  
   **Answer:** They could not lift water or stand stiff. *Why:* Without stiff pipes, water must soak slowly from cell to cell, and nothing hard holds the plant up. Xylem solved both problems at once.
11. (order) Put the steps of dating an old beam by its rings in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Take a thin core from a living tree of the same region', 'Measure the width of every ring in the core and the beam', 'Find where the two patterns of wide and narrow rings match', "Count from a ring of known year to the beam's last ring"]. *Why:* The living tree gives known years, because the outermost ring is this year's. Matching the patterns carries those years across to the beam.
12. (write) Much of the world's coal formed from ancient trees. Give one idea for why so much wood was buried without rotting.  
   **Answer:** One idea: lignin is very hard to digest, and when the first forests grew, few living things could break it down. Another idea: the swamps were waterlogged, and wood under water rots very slowly. Either way, fallen trunks piled up, were buried, and slowly became coal.. *Why:* Either idea earns full marks. A strong answer links a property of wood or of the swamp to what happened to the whole Earth, and says scientists have not settled it.
13. (match) Match each observation to what it shows.  
   **Answer:** Leaves stay green above a ring of removed bark → water travels in the wood; The bark swells above the ring → food travels down in the bark; Sap drips from a cut aphid mouth tube → phloem sap is under pressure; A nail in a trunk stays at the same height → a tree grows taller only at the tips; One ring is much narrower than the rest → the tree grew little that year. *Why:* Each of these is evidence. A strong answer can say what a different result would have meant.
14. (odd) Three of these are sinks. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A growing fruit / A root tip / A flower bud / A leaf in full sun  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A leaf in full sun makes more sugar than it uses, so it is a source. The other three take sugar from the phloem.
15. (label) Draw a wedge of trunk from centre to bark. Label heartwood, sapwood, cambium, inner bark and outer bark. Shade the living parts.  
   **Answer:** ['Heartwood: centre, dead', 'Sapwood: outer wood, dead pipes with some living cells', 'Cambium: thin living layer', 'Inner bark: living phloem', 'Outer bark: dead cork']. *Why:* The shaded living parts are the cambium and the inner bark, with scattered living cells in the sapwood. The heartwood and the outer bark are dead.

   Options: One root hair cell / The skin of a leaf / A whole mango tree / A grain of sugar  
   **Answer:** The skin of a leaf. *Why:* The skin of a leaf is many cells of one kind doing one job, which makes it a tissue. One cell is too little, and a whole tree is many tissues and organs together.

   Options: Its pipes are dead and hollow / It carries sugar to wherever it is needed / It carries food only downward / It carries only water  
   **Answer:** It carries sugar to wherever it is needed. *Why:* Phloem carries sugar from sources to sinks, up or down. Its pipes are living cells. 'Only downward' is the old idea that tracing experiments showed to be wrong.
4. (mcq) The cut face of a stem shows bundles scattered all over it. Which plant is this most likely to be?  
   Options: Maize / Mango / Tulsi / Hibiscus  
   **Answer:** Maize. *Why:* Maize is a grass, and grasses are monocots with scattered bundles. Mango, tulsi and hibiscus have their bundles in a ring.

   Options: A root is pulled, like a rope / The centre is the warmest part / Soil pushes them inward / There is no room elsewhere  
   **Answer:** A root is pulled, like a rope. *Why:* Wind on the plant tugs the roots like ropes. A rope is strongest with its strong strands together in the middle. A stem is bent, so its strong tissue sits near the outside.

   Options: From the soil / From stores in the trunk and roots / From the air / From rain water  
   **Answer:** From stores in the trunk and roots. *Why:* With no leaves, nothing is making sugar. The stores in the trunk and roots become sources, and the phloem carries their food up to the buds.

   Options: Water cannot rise / Sugar cannot reach the roots / The wire poisons the tree / The leaves get too much sun  
   **Answer:** Sugar cannot reach the roots. *Why:* The wire chokes the phloem in the inner bark, so sugar cannot get down to the roots. Water still rises in the wood, which is why the leaves stay green.
8. (mcq) Where is the cambium in a tree trunk?  
   Options: In the very centre / Between the wood and the bark / In the outer bark / Only in the leaves  
   **Answer:** Between the wood and the bark. *Why:* The cambium is a thin sleeve between wood and bark. It adds xylem inward and phloem outward, which makes the trunk thicker.

   **Answer:** The tree lived about 40 years. *Why:* Where growth stops once a year, a tree adds one ring a year. So 40 rings mean about 40 years. Rings say nothing about height.
10. (mcq) Why can ring counting fail for a tree in a forest that is warm and wet all year?  
   Options: Growth never stops, so no clear lines form / The wood is too hard to cut / Such trees have no xylem / The rings are too wide to see  
   **Answer:** Growth never stops, so no clear lines form. *Why:* A ring line forms where growth stops and starts again. A tree that grows all year may leave no clear line to count.

## Applications
- Timber: every wooden door, bed and boat is xylem. Carpenters cut along the grain because the long cells lie that way.
- Paper is made by breaking wood into its separate xylem cells and pressing them into a sheet.
- Natural rubber comes from latex tubes in the inner bark of rubber trees. Most of India's rubber is tapped in Kerala.
- Neera, toddy and palm jaggery come from phloem sap. Kamala Sohonie, the first Indian woman to earn a PhD in science, studied neera and found it a nourishing drink.
- Grafting joins a branch of one mango variety to the roots of another. It works only if the two cambium layers touch and grow together.
- Gardeners use gootee, or air layering, to make new guava, litchi and pomegranate plants from branches.
- Tree rings tell scientists how strong the monsoon was in years before anyone measured rain. This helps them judge how unusual today's droughts and floods are.
- Engineers have built water filters from short pieces of pine sapwood. The tiny holes between xylem cells hold back bacteria. Trials have been run with families in north India.

## Open questions
- **How does each new cell beside the cambium come to be wood or phloem?** Scientists have found some of the signals that pass between the cells. But nobody can yet predict, cell by cell, what the cambium will make or how much.
- **How do sieve tube cells stay alive for years with no nucleus?** In palms, the same sieve tubes seem to work for many decades. The companion cells must supply them, but how they keep them going for so long is not known.
- **Is pressure flow the whole story of how phloem sap moves in the tallest trees?** Sieve tubes seal themselves when touched, so pressures inside them have been measured in only a few plants.
- **How do plants use the signals that travel through their veins?** Electric and chemical signals have been recorded moving through the phloem. What a distant leaf does with each kind of signal is only beginning to be worked out.
- **Why do some tropical trees make yearly rings while their neighbours in the same forest do not?** It seems to depend on how each kind of tree responds to dry spells, floods and leaf fall. For most tropical trees, nobody has checked.
- **Is there a greatest height that a tree's pipes allow?** One estimate is about 122 to 130 metres. It rests on measurements from a few redwoods, and other scientists think the limit is set by something else.
- **Why was so much wood buried without rotting in the age of the coal swamps?** Some scientists point to lignin, which few living things could digest then. Others point to waterlogged ground and sinking land. The evidence does not yet settle it.

- This table is made of wood. Were its cells alive when the tree was standing?
- A hollow tree has lost the middle of the trunk. How can the tree still be alive?
- Sugar is made in the leaves. How does it get into a mango?
- Why do you think the rubber tapper's cut goes only half the way round?
- Look at this stump. Which ring was made in the year you were born?
- Why doesn't a coconut trunk get fatter every year, as a mango trunk does?
- Where on this plant is the newest cell? Where is the oldest?

## Connected maths
- In Pipe Maps, you draw the cut face of a stem on squared paper. The face covers 80 small squares, and the coloured dots cover 8. What fraction of the face is coloured? What fraction is not? **One tenth is coloured. Nine tenths is not.**. 8 out of 80 is 8/80. Divide top and bottom by 8 to get 1/10. The rest is 1 - 1/10 = 9/10. So nine tenths of the stem is other tissue: packing, skin and phloem. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- A teak log is 30 centimetres across. Its rings are 3 millimetres wide on average. About how old was the tree? **About 50 years.**. Rings are counted from the centre to the bark, so use the radius, not the full width. The radius is 30 ÷ 2 = 15 centimetres, which is 150 millimetres. 150 ÷ 3 = 50 rings. Using the full 30 centimetres would count every ring twice and give 100. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- In Read a Tree's Diary, the 12 ring widths are 6, 5, 6, 2, 5, 4, 1, 2, 5, 6, 4 and 5 millimetres. What is the mean ring width? **4.25 millimetres.**. Add the widths: 6 + 5 + 6 + 2 + 5 + 4 + 1 + 2 + 5 + 6 + 4 + 5 = 51. Divide by the number of rings: 51 ÷ 12 = 4.25. So the mean is 4.25 millimetres. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the made-up teak table, 1,000 mm of rain gave a 1 mm ring, 1,300 mm gave 2 mm, 1,900 mm gave 4 mm and 2,200 mm gave 5 mm. Predict the ring for a year with 1,600 mm of rain. **About 3 millimetres.**. Look for the pattern. From 1,000 to 1,300 is 300 mm more rain, and the ring is 1 mm wider. From 1,900 to 2,200 is 300 more, and again 1 mm wider. So each extra 300 mm adds about 1 mm. 1,600 is 300 more than 1,300, so the ring is about 2 + 1 = 3 mm. A real tree would not follow the pattern this neatly. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A tree adds a ring 4 millimetres wide every year. How much wider is the trunk, measured straight across, after 10 years? **8 centimetres wider.**. A ring goes all the way round, so it adds 4 mm on one side of the trunk and 4 mm on the opposite side. That is 8 mm wider each year. In 10 years: 8 × 10 = 80 mm, which is 8 centimetres. Answering 4 centimetres counts only one side. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L5`)
- Suppose phloem sap moves 1 metre in an hour and xylem sap moves 6 metres in an hour. In a coconut palm 18 metres tall, how long does each take to travel the whole height? **Phloem sap takes 18 hours. Xylem sap takes 3 hours.**. Time is distance divided by speed. Phloem: 18 ÷ 1 = 18 hours. Xylem: 18 ÷ 6 = 3 hours. So sugar made at midday reaches the roots only the next morning. These speeds are example numbers. Real speeds change with the plant and the weather. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)

## Links
- [Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research, University of Arizona](https://ltrr.arizona.edu/) · interactive: For the parent: the laboratory that Andrew Douglass founded, with pictures of tree cores and how they are read.
- [Dendrochronology (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrochronology) · interactive: For the parent: how ring dating works, with photographs of cores and matched patterns.
- [Phloem (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phloem) · interactive: For the parent: diagrams of sieve tubes and companion cells, and the girdling experiment.
- [BBC Bitesize](https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize) · interactive: Search for 'xylem and phloem' for short, clear revision pages with diagrams.
- [National Fossil Wood Park, Tiruvakkarai]() · place · near Villupuram, Tamil Nadu: Tree trunks about 20 million years old, turned to stone, lying in the open. In some you can still see the grain of the wood.
- [Forest Research Institute museums]() · place · Dehradun, Uttarakhand: The timber museum shows a slice of a deodar tree that lived for about 700 years, with dates marked on the rings.
- [Lalbagh Botanical Garden]() · place · Bengaluru, Karnataka: Very old, wide trees to walk round, and a fossil tree trunk on display.
- [Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences](https://www.bsip.res.in) · place · Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh: Scientists here read the rings of Himalayan trees. The museum shows plant fossils.
- [A timber yard, a carpenter's shop or a fresh tree stump near you]() · place · your own town: Ask to look at the cut ends of logs. Count rings and look for the darker heartwood.

## Warm-up
- Which pipes carry water up a plant? → Xylem.
- Which pipes carry sugar? → Phloem.
- Which pipes are made of dead cells? → Xylem pipes.
- What do we call a part of a plant that takes sugar from the phloem? → A sink.
- Which thin layer makes a tree trunk thicker? → The cambium.
- What is wood made of? → Old xylem.
- A nail is in a tree trunk. The tree grows taller. Does the nail rise? → No. A tree grows taller only at the tips.
- Why does a coconut palm have no growth rings? → A palm has no cambium.
