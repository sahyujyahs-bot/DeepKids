# Metabolism: how your cells break food down, build you up and keep you warm
`sci.body.energy-from-food.L5`

**Summary.** A tumbler of warm water left on the table is cool within the hour. Your body has stayed warm for years, day and night, with no stove inside it. Where does the warmth come from? In this lesson you feed living yeast four different foods and measure the gas they make. You find out what a cell does with food: it breaks some of it down for energy, and uses that energy to build and repair the body. Heat escapes at every step, and that is why you are warm, and a little warmer after a meal.

**Intro.** *Put your palm on your cheek. It is warm. It was warm while you slept last night, and it will be warm tomorrow. A tumbler of warm water goes cool in an hour. So why do you never go cool?* Say what you think, and why. Then try the first activity, Warmer Than the Room. You will set a tumbler of warm water beside you and see which of you stays warm. After that you will feed yeast, who are tiny living beings, and see what they do with food. By the end you will know where your warmth comes from. You will also know why a meal makes you feel warmer.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what metabolism is, and tell breaking down (catabolism) from building up (anabolism).
1. By the end I can explain why my cells can build only with energy from breaking food down, and say what ATP does.

4. By the end I can explain how the body stores energy as glycogen and fat, and when it uses each store.
5. By the end I can say where the atoms of my food end up: built into my body, or breathed out.

7. By the end I can compare how fast different animals use energy, and say why a small animal must eat so often.

## Terms
- **Metabolism**: Metabolism is all the chemical changes that go on inside the cells of a living being. Some changes break molecules down, and others build new molecules. *e.g.* Right now your cells are breaking down sugar from your last meal and building new skin, both at once.
- **Molecule**: A molecule is a group of atoms joined together. Sugar, water, fat and protein are all made of molecules. *e.g.* One molecule of glucose has 6 carbon atoms, 12 hydrogen atoms and 6 oxygen atoms.
- **Catabolism**: Catabolism is the part of metabolism that breaks large molecules into smaller ones. Breaking them down releases energy. *e.g.* Your muscle cells break glucose down into carbon dioxide and water, and get energy to move.
- **Anabolism**: Anabolism is the part of metabolism that joins small molecules into larger ones. Building them up costs energy. *e.g.* Your body joins amino acids from dal into the protein of a new muscle fibre.
- **Glucose**: Glucose is a simple sugar. It is the main fuel that blood carries to your cells. *e.g.* The starch in rice and idli is cut into glucose in your gut, and the glucose passes into your blood.
- **ATP**: ATP is a small molecule that carries energy inside a cell. Its full name is adenosine triphosphate. A cell loads ATP with energy when it breaks food down, and spends ATP wherever it needs energy. *e.g.* A muscle fibre spends ATP each time it shortens. A cell also spends ATP to join amino acids into protein.
- **Cellular respiration**: Cellular respiration is the way a cell breaks glucose or fat down with oxygen. It gives carbon dioxide, water and energy. It is not the same as breathing, which only moves air in and out. *e.g.* You breathe faster when you run, because your muscle cells are using more oxygen for cellular respiration.
- **Fermentation**: Fermentation is a way of getting energy from sugar without oxygen. It breaks the sugar only part of the way, so it gives much less energy. *e.g.* Yeast in idli or bread dough ferment sugar and give off carbon dioxide gas, which makes the dough rise.
- **Mitochondrion**: A mitochondrion is a small part inside a cell where food is broken down with oxygen. The plural is mitochondria. Most of a cell's ATP is loaded there. *e.g.* A heart muscle cell is packed with mitochondria, because the heart never rests.
- **Metabolic rate**: Metabolic rate is how fast a body uses energy. It can be given in kilocalories a day or in watts. *e.g.* Your metabolic rate goes up many times when you run, and falls again when you sit.
- **Basal metabolic rate**: Basal metabolic rate is the energy a body uses while lying awake, at rest, in a comfortable room, long after a meal. It is the cost of only staying alive. *e.g.* Even when you lie still, your heart, brain, liver and kidneys keep working and using energy.
- **Thermic effect of food**: The thermic effect of food is the extra energy a body uses to digest, absorb and store a meal. That extra energy ends up as heat. *e.g.* For an hour or more after a big lunch your body gives off a little more heat than before it.
- **Glycogen**: Glycogen is a store of glucose. It is made of many glucose units joined in a branching chain, and it is kept in the liver and the muscles. *e.g.* During the night your liver breaks glycogen back into glucose, so your brain has fuel while you sleep.
- **Body fat**: Body fat is the body's large, long-lasting store of energy. It is kept in fat cells under the skin and around the organs. *e.g.* A bar-headed goose builds up body fat before flying over the Himalayas, and uses it on the way.
- **Brown fat**: Brown fat is a special kind of fat whose cells break fuel down only to make heat. Babies have a good deal of it, and adults have a little. *e.g.* A newborn baby cannot shiver well, so brown fat near the neck and back helps keep the baby warm.
- **Turnover**: Turnover is the steady breaking down and rebuilding of the body's own molecules. The body looks the same, but its material keeps being replaced. *e.g.* A red blood cell lasts about four months. Then it is taken apart, and a new one takes its place.
- **Lactate**: Lactate is a small molecule that muscle cells make when they break glucose down very fast. It is a fuel. The heart burns it, and the liver builds it back into glucose. *e.g.* After a hard sprint your blood carries extra lactate from your leg muscles to your liver.
- **Watt**: A watt is a unit for how fast energy is used or given out. One watt is one joule of energy every second. *e.g.* A resting ten-year-old gives out heat at about 55 watts, a little less than an old 60 watt bulb.
- **Torpor**: Torpor is a state in which an animal lets their body cool and their metabolic rate fall very low for some hours, to save energy. *e.g.* Some bats and hummingbirds go into torpor when they rest, because their small bodies would otherwise run out of fuel.

## Explanations
### Metabolism: the chemistry that keeps a cell alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Metabolism is all the chemical changes inside the cells of a living being. It has two parts. Catabolism breaks molecules down, and anabolism builds molecules up.

Digestion happens in the gut, which is a tube. Metabolism happens later, inside your cells, after the digested food has crossed into the blood. A cell takes in small molecules such as glucose, fatty acids and amino acids. Then it does one of two things with each of them. It can break the molecule down further and take the energy. Or it can join the molecule to others and build something the body needs. Thousands of different changes go on in one cell at the same time. Each change is helped by its own enzyme. An enzyme is a protein that makes one chemical change happen quickly at body warmth. Every living cell has a metabolism: yours, a neem tree's, a yeast cell's and a bacterium's. When metabolism stops for good, the cell has died.
- e.g. Glucose from your breakfast idli is broken down in a leg muscle cell, and the muscle uses the energy to climb stairs.
- e.g. Amino acids from sambar dal are joined into the protein of a new skin cell where you grazed your knee.
- e.g. In the Yeast Fuel Test, yeast cells break sugar down, and you catch the gas they give off in a balloon.
### Catabolism: breaking down releases energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Catabolism is the breaking of large molecules into smaller ones. It releases energy that was stored in the food molecule.

A glucose molecule holds energy in the way its atoms are joined. You saw this energy in Class 4, when a burning groundnut warmed water. A cell does not burn glucose in one go. It takes the molecule apart in more than twenty small steps, and each step has its own enzyme. In the end the carbon atoms leave as carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen atoms join oxygen and leave as water. This whole path is cellular respiration, and most of it happens in the mitochondria. Why so many steps? A single big step would let all the energy out as heat at once. Small steps let the cell catch part of the energy each time, in ATP. Fat and protein can be broken down for energy too. Fat gives the most energy for each gram.
- e.g. Cells in your heart break down fat and lactate all day to keep the heart beating.
- e.g. When you run for a bus, your leg muscles break glucose down many times faster than when you sit.
- e.g. A camel's hump is fat. Over days without food, the camel's cells break that fat down for energy.
### Anabolism: building up costs energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Anabolism is the joining of small molecules into larger ones. It costs energy. In your body that energy comes from catabolism.

Your body is built from the same kinds of small molecules that digestion gives you. Amino acids are joined into proteins, such as the fibres of muscle and the enzymes in every cell. Glucose units are joined into glycogen. Fatty acids are joined into fats and into the thin skin around each cell. None of these joins happens by itself. Each one needs energy, and the cell pays with ATP. So building depends on breaking. A child who is growing does a great deal of anabolism. An adult who has stopped growing still does a great deal, because the body rebuilds itself all the time. Plants do anabolism too. A rice plant joins carbon dioxide and water into glucose, using the energy of light, and then joins glucose into starch.
- e.g. After you graze your knee, cells at the edge of the cut build new protein and new cells to close it.
- e.g. At night, when you are growing, your bones add new material at their growing ends.
- e.g. In Grown in the Dark, a moong seedling builds a root and a shoot with energy from breaking down the food stored in the seed.
### ATP carries energy from the breaking to the building · *defined*
**Definition:** ATP is a small molecule that carries energy inside a cell. Catabolism loads it with energy, and anabolism and every other job in the cell spend it.

Breaking down and building up happen in different places in a cell, and at different moments. Something has to carry the energy from one to the other. That carrier is ATP. When a cell breaks glucose down, it uses the energy to load ATP. When the cell needs energy, it spends an ATP. The spent molecule is called ADP, and it goes back to be loaded again. The same ATP molecule is loaded and spent again and again, many hundreds of times a day. Teachers often say that an adult loads and spends about their own body weight of ATP each day. That number is a rough estimate, not a measurement. ATP was found in 1929. Yellapragada SubbaRow, who was born in Bhimavaram in Andhra Pradesh, and Cyrus Fiske found it in the United States. Karl Lohmann found it in Germany in the same year. All known living cells use ATP.
- e.g. A nerve cell in your brain spends ATP to pump charged atoms across its skin, so that it is ready to send the next signal.
- e.g. A yeast cell loads ATP by fermenting sugar, and spends ATP to grow and divide.
- e.g. A firefly spends ATP to make light.
### Why every step gives off heat · *defined*
**Definition:** No change of energy from one form to another is complete. Each time a cell loads ATP or spends ATP, part of the energy escapes as heat.

When a cell breaks glucose down with oxygen, it catches roughly 35 to 40 parts in every 100 of the energy in ATP. The other 60 or more parts become heat at once. Then the ATP is spent. Whatever it is spent on, that energy also ends as heat inside you, unless you do work on something outside your body or store the energy in new body material. A beating heart pushes blood, and the blood rubs against the vessel walls and warms them. So a body at rest gives out, as heat, all the energy it uses. For a resting ten-year-old that is about 55 watts. For a resting adult it is about 80 watts. Your body cannot switch this heat off. Most of it is not made on purpose. It comes with being alive. Shivering and brown fat are the body's two ways of making extra heat on purpose. Mammals and birds keep much of it in with fur, feathers and fat, and so stay warm.
- e.g. A crowded bus feels warm even on a cool morning, because every passenger gives out heat all the time.
- e.g. Your phone grows warm when it works hard, for the same reason: no machine passes energy on completely.
- e.g. In Warmer Than the Room, the tumbler of water goes cool and you stay warm, because you keep making heat and the water does not.
### Why you feel warm after eating · *defined*
**Definition:** After a meal your body uses extra energy to digest the food, carry it across the gut wall and store it. This extra use is called the thermic effect of food, and it ends as extra heat.

A meal makes work for your cells. Gut muscles squeeze the food along. Gland cells build and send out enzymes. Cells in the gut wall spend ATP to pull glucose and amino acids into the blood. Liver and muscle cells spend ATP to join glucose into glycogen and to build proteins. All this spending gives off heat. For a mixed meal, the extra energy used is about one tenth of the energy in the meal. Protein costs the most to handle, carbohydrate less, and fat the least. These shares are rough, because they are hard to measure. Your body temperature hardly changes after a meal. The brain notices the extra heat and sends more blood to the skin, so the heat leaves. That is why your skin, more than your insides, feels warmer. Three other things can add to the feeling. Hot food and drink carry their own heat into you. Chilli contains capsaicin, which switches on the nerve endings that sense heat, so you feel hot although nothing has warmed up. And your heart beats a little faster after a meal, to send more blood to the gut.
- e.g. After a wedding lunch on a banana leaf, many people feel warm and sleepy for an hour or two.
- e.g. A Burmese python may eat a meal a quarter of their own weight or more. Their metabolic rate can then rise to as much as 44 times its resting level.
- e.g. In the Lunch Test you count your pulse and take your temperature before and after a meal.
### Basal metabolic rate: what a resting body spends energy on · *defined*
**Definition:** Basal metabolic rate is the energy a body uses while lying awake and at rest, in a comfortable room, many hours after a meal. For most people it is well over half of all the energy they use in a day.

A resting body is busy. The heart beats, the kidneys clean the blood, the liver runs hundreds of chemical changes, and the brain never switches off. In an adult, four organs do more than half of this resting work: the liver, the brain, the heart and the kidneys. Together they are only about one twentieth of the body's weight. The brain is about 2 parts in 100 of an adult's weight, and uses about 20 parts in 100 of the resting energy. Muscles at rest use little for their size, but there is so much muscle that it adds up to about a fifth. In a child the brain's share is far bigger. A study in 2014 worked out that a child's brain, at four to five years old, uses glucose at a rate equal to about two thirds of the body's resting energy. The same study found that the body grows most slowly at the ages when the brain uses most. Basal metabolic rate depends mostly on body size and on how much of the body is muscle and organs.
- e.g. While you sleep, your body still uses energy at nearly its basal rate.
- e.g. In Twenty Beans you guess where a resting body spends its energy, and then compare with the measurements.
- e.g. A person with fever has a higher metabolic rate, because chemical changes go faster in a warmer body.
### Energy stores: glycogen and fat · *defined*
**Definition:** The body stores spare energy in two main forms. Glycogen is a small, quick store of glucose in the liver and the muscles. Fat is a large, slow store in fat cells.

You eat a few times a day, but your cells need fuel every second. Stores fill the gaps. After a meal, the liver and the muscles join spare glucose into glycogen. This is anabolism. Between meals and at night, the liver breaks glycogen back into glucose and lets it out into the blood. This is catabolism. An adult's liver holds about 100 grams of glycogen, and the muscles hold about 400 grams. Muscle glycogen is used only by the muscle that holds it. Glycogen holds water around it, so it is heavy for the energy it gives. Fat holds no water and gives more than twice the energy for each gram, so the body keeps its big store as fat. Gerty Cori and Carl Cori worked out how the body builds glycogen and breaks it down. In 1929 they also described what is now called the Cori cycle. A hard-working muscle breaks glucose down to lactate. The blood carries the lactate to the liver, and the liver builds it back into glucose. So lactate is a fuel being passed on. It is not a poison.
- e.g. A marathon runner who runs out of glycogen suddenly feels very weak. Runners call this 'hitting the wall'.
- e.g. A Himalayan brown bear eats through summer and autumn, and lives on body fat while sleeping through the winter.
- e.g. During an overnight fast your liver glycogen keeps the glucose in your blood steady until breakfast.
### Turnover: your body is rebuilt all the time · *defined*
**Definition:** Turnover is the steady breaking down and rebuilding of the body's own molecules and cells. It goes on in everybody, including adults who are no longer growing.

It is natural to think that food is fuel and the body is the engine, and that the engine's parts stay the same for years. Tests with labelled atoms in the 1930s showed that this is wrong. The body takes its own proteins and fats apart all the time, and builds new ones from a mix of old pieces and new food. An adult breaks down and rebuilds about 300 grams of protein every day. That is three or four times the protein the adult eats. A red blood cell lasts about four months. The cells that line the small intestine last only a few days. Why would a body do this? Proteins get damaged, and a damaged enzyme works badly. Rebuilding keeps the parts fresh. It also lets the body change quickly, by building more of one protein and less of another. Not everything is replaced. Most nerve cells in the brain last a lifetime, and so does the hard enamel of your teeth.
- e.g. The skin you can see today is not the skin you had two months ago. The outer cells have been shed and replaced.
- e.g. A broken arm in a cast gets thinner in a few weeks. Muscle protein is still being broken down, and less is being rebuilt.
- e.g. A healing bone is first joined by rough new bone, which is then taken apart and rebuilt into its proper shape.
### Where the atoms of your food go · *defined*
**Definition:** Atoms are never destroyed in the body. The atoms of food that is broken down for energy leave mostly as carbon dioxide in the breath and as water.

Glucose and fat are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms. When a cell breaks them down fully, each carbon atom leaves joined to oxygen, as carbon dioxide. Your blood carries that gas to your lungs, and you breathe it out. The hydrogen atoms end up in water, which leaves in urine, sweat and breath. So when a person's body uses up 10 kilograms of stored fat, about 8.4 kilograms of it leave through the lungs as carbon dioxide. The other 1.6 kilograms leave as water. Two scientists in Australia, Ruben Meerman and Andrew Brown, worked this out in 2014. They also found that most doctors they asked got it wrong. Many thought the fat turned into energy or heat. But atoms cannot turn into energy in a body. The energy comes from the way the atoms were joined, and the atoms themselves go on. Protein also contains nitrogen. The liver puts that nitrogen into urea, and the kidneys send the urea out in urine.
- e.g. You weigh a little less in the morning than at bedtime. Part of the difference is carbon you breathed out in the night, and part is water.
- e.g. A carbon atom you breathe out today may be taken in by a tulsi leaf tomorrow and built into sugar.
- e.g. A candle gets shorter as it burns, because its wax leaves as carbon dioxide and water vapour.
### With oxygen and without: respiration and fermentation · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell can break glucose down fully, using oxygen, or only part of the way, without oxygen. The first way is cellular respiration. The second is fermentation, and it gives much less energy.

The first part of breaking down glucose is a path of ten steps called glycolysis. It needs no oxygen, and it loads only a little ATP. Nearly every living cell has this same path, from bacteria to yeast to you. That is strong evidence that all life shares one ancestor. After glycolysis there is a choice. With oxygen, your mitochondria break the pieces down all the way to carbon dioxide and water. This loads about fifteen times as much ATP as glycolysis alone. Without oxygen, a yeast cell turns the pieces into alcohol and carbon dioxide. That is fermentation. Most of the energy stays in the alcohol, so the yeast must use a lot of sugar. Your own muscle cells do something similar in a hard sprint. They turn the pieces into lactate, which other cells burn later. In the Yeast Fuel Test the bottles are closed with balloons, so the yeast soon have little oxygen and they ferment.
- e.g. Idli and dosa batter rises overnight because bacteria and wild yeasts in it ferment sugars and give off carbon dioxide.
- e.g. A 100 metre sprinter gets much of her energy without oxygen for those few seconds, and breathes hard afterwards.
- e.g. Yeast given air and stirred well use oxygen and grow faster on the same sugar.
### Metabolic rate in other animals, and in a flower · *defined*
**Definition:** Different living beings use energy at very different rates. For each kilogram of body, a small warm animal uses energy much faster than a large one.

A small body has a lot of skin for its weight, so it loses heat quickly. To stay warm, each gram of a small mammal or bird must make more heat. The Etruscan shrew weighs about 2 grams and is one of the smallest mammals. They eat one and a half to two times their own weight in food every day. An elephant eats far more in total, but far less for each kilogram of body. Animals who do not keep a steady warmth, such as snakes, frogs and fish, need much less food. A python can go weeks between meals. Some small animals save fuel by going into torpor when they rest. Honey bees keep warm together. In cold weather the bees pack into a ball and shiver their flight muscles without moving their wings. The heat comes from the honey they eat. Some plants make heat too. The flower of the sacred lotus keeps itself at about 30 to 35 degrees Celsius for a few days, even when the air is as cool as 10 degrees. Lotus cells do this by breaking down stored food very fast.
- e.g. A sunbird at a hibiscus must feed again and again through the day, because a small warm body uses fuel quickly.
- e.g. A rock python lying still after a meal is not being lazy. Their body is working hard on the meal.
- e.g. On a cool morning, a lotus flower in a temple tank can be several degrees warmer than the air around it.
### Why does metabolic rate change with body size in the way it does? · *debated*
An elephant weighs about a million times as much as a small shrew, but uses far less than a million times the energy. In 1932 Max Kleiber measured many mammals. He found that when body weight goes up 10,000 times, metabolic rate goes up only about 1,000 times. This pattern is called Kleiber's law. Scientists agree on the pattern in a rough way. They do not agree on why it happens, or on how exact it is.
- e.g. A mouse's heart beats about 600 times a minute, and an elephant's about 30 times.
- e.g. A gram of shrew uses energy many times faster than a gram of elephant.
- **Max Rubner (1883) and others:** A body loses heat through its skin. A big body has less skin for its weight, so it needs less heat for each kilogram. This explains part of the pattern, but the numbers do not fit it exactly.
- **Geoffrey West, James Brown and Brian Enquist (1997):** Every cell is fed through branching tubes, such as blood vessels. In a bigger body the tubes are longer, and that limits how fast fuel can be delivered to each cell. Their sums give the pattern Kleiber found.
- **Other researchers:** There is no single law. Careful measurements give different patterns for small animals and large ones, and for resting and active animals. So there may be several causes working together.
### Does a more active person always use more energy in a day? · *debated*
It seems obvious that someone who moves more uses more energy in total. For a single day that is true. Over months, the measurements are less clear, and scientists disagree about what they mean. Moving your body is good for your heart, muscles, bones and mood, whichever side turns out to be right.
- e.g. A child who plays football for an hour uses more energy in that hour than a child who reads.
- **Herman Pontzer and colleagues (2012):** They measured the Hadza people of Tanzania, who walk many kilometres a day to find food. For their body size, the Hadza used about the same energy each day as office workers in cities. Pontzer thinks the body keeps its daily total within limits, and saves on other jobs when a person moves a lot.
- **Other physiologists:** They point out that the result depends on how body size is allowed for. Many studies show that energy use does rise with activity. They think any saving is small, and that it appears only at very high levels of activity.
### Which came first when life began: metabolism or genes? · *idea*
Every cell today has both a metabolism and genes, and each needs the other. Genes carry the plans for enzymes, and enzymes are needed to copy genes. So which came first, about four billion years ago? Nobody knows. Some scientists think simple chains of chemical changes began first, perhaps in warm, mineral-rich vents on the sea floor, and that genes came later. Others think molecules that could copy themselves came first, and that metabolism grew up around them. These are ideas, and tests are still going on.
- e.g. The ten steps of glycolysis are found in nearly all living beings, so that path must be very old.
- e.g. Some of the steps of metabolism can be made to happen in a laboratory with only water, iron and simple molecules, with no enzymes at all.

## Mostly agreed
- Every living cell has a metabolism. It breaks some molecules down for energy and builds others up. *How we know:* Wherever biologists have looked, from bacteria to banyan trees to people, they have found cells taking in molecules, changing them and giving off waste and heat.
- Energy is neither made nor lost in a living body. The energy in the food a body uses equals the heat it gives out, plus the work it does, plus what it stores. *How we know:* In the 1890s Wilbur Atwater and Edward Rosa kept people for days inside a sealed room that measured all their heat. The energy going in and the energy coming out matched very closely.
- A body's own molecules are broken down and rebuilt all the time, even in an adult who is not growing. *How we know:* From 1935, Rudolf Schoenheimer and David Rittenberg fed animals food made with labelled atoms. The labels soon turned up in the animals' own fat and protein.
- All known living cells carry energy in the same molecule, ATP. *How we know:* ATP was found in muscle in 1929. Since then it has been found in every kind of cell that has been tested.
- Nearly all living beings break glucose down by the same first ten steps. *How we know:* The enzymes for these steps have been found in bacteria, yeast, plants and animals, and they are built in closely matching ways.
- Fat that the body uses up leaves mostly through the lungs, as carbon dioxide. *How we know:* Counting the atoms in a fat molecule shows where each must go. Tests with labelled atoms agree with the count.

## How we found out
**Rudolf Schoenheimer and David Rittenberg, with Harold Urey, who found deuterium, 1932 (deuterium found), 1935 (the first tests with labelled fat) and 1942 (Schoenheimer's book, The Dynamic State of Body Constituents).** A hundred years ago, most scientists pictured the body as an engine. Food was the fuel. The body burned it straight away, and the engine's own parts stayed in place until they wore out. Nobody could test this, because an atom from food looks the same as an atom already in the body. Then, in 1932, Harold Urey found deuterium. It is a heavy kind of hydrogen, and it can be told apart from ordinary hydrogen. Rudolf Schoenheimer had left Germany in 1933 and worked at Columbia University in New York. With David Rittenberg he made fat that carried deuterium as a label, and in 1935 they fed it to mice. If the old idea was right, the mice would burn the fat and the label would leave. Instead, most of the label turned up in the body fat of the mice. Later they labelled amino acids with a heavy kind of nitrogen and fed them to adult rats. The label appeared in the rats' own proteins, though the rats were not growing. So the body was taking itself apart and rebuilding itself all the time.  
*What people believed before:* That the body is like an engine: food is burned straight away as fuel, and the body's own material stays in place for years. Labelled atoms showed that food atoms are built into the body within days, and that the body's own material is broken down and rebuilt all the time.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Food is fuel and the body is an engine. The fuel is burned, and the engine's own parts stay the same for years.** (Most scientists before the 1930s; *rejected*)  
  For: It fits what we see. A grown person looks the same from month to month, and food clearly gives energy.  
  Against: Labelled atoms from food turn up in body fat and body protein within days, even in adult animals who are not growing. The body is taken apart and rebuilt all the time.
- **Body heat is only waste.** (A common way of speaking; *debated*)  
  For: Heat escapes at every step of metabolism whether the body wants it or not. A lizard's body makes heat too, though far less, and it is not enough to keep the lizard warm.  
  Against: Mammals and birds depend on this heat to stay at a steady warmth. Brown fat cells break fuel down only to make heat. So the same heat is waste in one body and a need in another.
- **Lactic acid builds up in tired muscles and causes the soreness you feel the next day.** (Many coaches and older books; *rejected*)  
  For: Muscles do make more lactate during hard exercise, and hard exercise is followed by soreness.  
  Against: Lactate is cleared from the blood within about an hour, long before the soreness starts. It is a fuel that the heart burns and the liver builds back into glucose. Next-day soreness comes from tiny damage to muscle fibres and the repair that follows.
- **A growing body makes order out of disorder, so living beings break the rule that things get more mixed up over time.** (A puzzle that physicists raised, and Erwin Schrödinger wrote about in 1944; *rejected*)  
  For: A baby builds a neat, working body out of mashed food. That looks like order appearing from disorder.  
  Against: A body builds its order only by giving out heat and simple waste molecules, such as carbon dioxide. These spread out and add more disorder outside the body than the order built inside. So the rule holds.
- **Mitochondria were once free-living bacteria, which came to live inside larger cells.** (Lynn Margulis, in 1967; *accepted*)  
  For: Mitochondria have their own small ring of genes, like bacteria. They divide by themselves, and they are the size of bacteria.  
  Against: At first most biologists did not accept it, and about fifteen journals turned her paper down. Later, the genes of mitochondria were read and found to match those of one group of bacteria.

## Roots
We have learned that cells break food down for energy, build the body up with that energy, and give off heat at every step. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Yeast Fuel Test, the yeast who were given sugar made gas and froth, and the yeast with no food made almost none. Like yeast, your cells can build only with energy they get by breaking food down. Each time, part of that energy escapes as heat. So your body is built by breaking food apart, and your warmth shows that this is happening now.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of breaking down and building up as opposites, and we call one decay and the other growth. In your cells they are two halves of one exchange, and the building cannot happen without the breaking. We also think the body has a heater somewhere. Nearly all of its heat is simply what escapes each time a cell passes energy on.  
*Evidence:* In the Yeast Fuel Test, only the bottles where yeast could break a food down filled their balloons. In Grown in the Dark, a moong seedling built a root and a shoot while the food store in the seed leaves shrank. In Warmer Than the Room, the water cooled and you did not.

- *who am i:* The material of your body is changed for new material from food all the time. What lasts is the way you are built, and the life that keeps rebuilding it.
- *what is life:* To be alive is to break down and build up at the same time, without stopping. A yeast cell does it, a lotus does it, and you do it.
- *what is this world:* The energy that warms your cheek came to the Earth as sunlight. A plant stored it, you used it, and it leaves you as heat. You are one place it passes through.

Sunlight is one kind of energy. A rice plant stores it in starch. Your cells break the starch down, and the same energy becomes a heartbeat, a thought, a new patch of skin and the warmth of your hand. Then it all leaves you as heat. Awareness, the ground that everything rises from, shows itself here as one energy taking many forms in turn. None of the energy is used up. It is only passed on.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2:** “अन्नं ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात् / अन्नाद्ध्येव खल्विमानि भूतानि जायन्ते / अन्नेन जातानि जीवन्ति / अन्नं प्रयन्त्यभिसंविशन्तीति” Bhrigu's father tells him to find out for himself what all beings come from. Bhrigu thinks hard, and his first answer is food. Beings are born from food, they live by food, and when they die they go back into food. His father sends him to think further. (The science of metabolism says the same about the body. It is built from food, kept alive by food, and returns to the food of other beings. The Upanishad treats this as a true first answer, and then asks what lies beyond it.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment B30 (Diels-Kranz):** “κόσμον τόνδε, τὸν αὐτὸν ἁπάντων, οὔτε τις θεῶν οὔτε ἀνθρώπων ἐποίησεν, / ἀλλ’ ἦν ἀεὶ καὶ ἔστιν καὶ ἔσται πῦρ ἀείζωον / ἁπτόμενον μέτρα καὶ ἀποσβεννύμενον μέτρα” The Greek thinker Heraclitus said that this world was made by no god and no person. It always was and will be an ever-living fire, being kindled in measures and going out in measures. (A living body is like this in a plain way. Breaking down and building up go on together, in measured amounts, and the body keeps its form only while both continue.)
- **Khuddakapatha 4 (The Boy's Questions):** In this short Buddhist text a young novice is asked, 'What is one?' The answer given is that all beings are kept going by food. (Every being with a metabolism must take something in from outside. No living being keeps going on what is inside them alone, so each depends on others.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or destroyed in a living body. What goes in as food comes out as heat, work or stored material (measured by Atwater and Rosa in the 1890s).
- Science (settled): The molecules of a body are broken down and rebuilt all the time, so the body's material is always changing while its form stays (Schoenheimer, 1930s).
- Science (debated): Life may have begun as a simple metabolism, a chain of chemical changes in warm rock, before there were any genes. Other scientists think genes came first.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India, as reported by Madhava in the Sarvadarshanasamgraha: The Charvakas said that awareness is not the ground of anything. It appears when the elements come together in a body. In the same way, the power to make a person drunk appears when grain and a starter are mixed and left to ferment, though neither had that power alone. On this view, a mind is something a metabolism produces, and it ends when the body ends.
- Practice, Feel your own warmth (3 min): Sit still and rub nothing. Hold your open palms close to your cheeks without touching them. Wait, and feel the warmth that crosses the gap. This heat is leaving you all the time. It came from the food you ate today. Before that it was sunlight on a field. Now breathe out slowly onto your palm. That breath carries carbon atoms from the same food. Sit for five slow breaths and notice the warmth and the breath leaving.
- Ask: Your body is built by breaking food apart. Is breaking down a bad thing, a good thing, or neither?
- Ask: The material of your body keeps changing. What is it that stays?
- Ask: The warmth of your hand was once sunlight. Where will that warmth be an hour from now?

## Story: The Kid Who Would Not Warm
*Thenmozhi, Kathir, Paatti, the doctor and the goats are made up. These facts are real. Villages around Kotagiri in the Nilgiris get frost on some January nights. A newborn goat kid is born wet and loses heat fast. The kid has a small store of brown fat, which makes heat for the first hours, and needs the mother's first milk, called colostrum, soon after birth. People who keep goats are told to dry a chilled kid, warm the kid, and feed warm colostrum once the kid can swallow. A kid whose mouth feels cold must be warmed before feeding, and needs a vet.*

The frost came on the night Paatti was away.

She had gone down to Coonoor for a wedding. Thenmozhi, who was eleven, was in charge of the house, her brother Kathir and the goats. The house stood above the tea slopes near Kotagiri. Kathir was seven and did not think he needed anyone in charge.

At two in the morning Karuppi began to call from the shed. Thenmozhi woke at once. She knew that sound. Karuppi was having her kids.

By the time the two children reached the shed with the lantern, the first kid was already out. She was black like her mother, and she was trying to stand on four legs that would not agree with one another. Karuppi was licking her hard.

"There is another," said Thenmozhi.

The second kid lay behind Karuppi in the straw. She was smaller, and brown, and still wet. She did not try to stand. Karuppi had not turned round to her yet.

Kathir touched the small kid's ear. "She is cold, Akka. She is as cold as the tap."

"Then we warm her," said Thenmozhi.

Kathir ran to the house and came back with Paatti's brown shawl. He wrapped the kid in it. They waited. Outside, the grass was turning white.

After ten minutes the kid was no warmer. Kathir fetched a blanket, and then a second blanket. Now the kid was a small face in a great heap of wool. Her eyes were half shut.

"More," said Kathir. "We need more blankets. Blankets make you warm."

Thenmozhi was not listening. She was looking at a steel tumbler on the shed step. An hour ago, before she slept, she had filled it with hot water for herself. Then she had wrapped it in her muffler and forgotten it. She picked it up. The muffler was still tight around it. The water inside was cold.

She held the tumbler and thought.

The muffler had not kept the water hot. It had only made the water cool more slowly. Wool makes no heat. Wool keeps in heat that something else is making. She herself was warm inside her sweater, because something in her was making heat all night. The tumbler had nothing in it making heat. So it had gone cold, muffler or no muffler.

She looked at the kid in the heap of blankets.

"Kathir," she said, "a blanket is no use unless there is a fire under it. Her fire is nearly out."

"Goats do not have fires."

"Everybody has one. Yours is burning your dinner right now. That is why your hands are warm. She has never had a dinner. She was born with a little fuel, and the cold is using it up."

Paatti had the vet's number written on the wall by the door. Dr Revathy answered on the sixth ring, with sleep in her voice, and woke up fast. "Is the kid wet? Dry her first. Rub her hard with a dry cloth. A wet coat steals heat. Then put your finger in her mouth. Tell me if her mouth is warm or cold."

Thenmozhi rubbed the kid with an old towel until the brown coat stood up in tufts. She slid a finger into the small mouth. It was warm. Not very warm, but warm. The kid's tongue curled weakly round her finger.

"Warm. And she is sucking my finger."

"Good. Then she can swallow. She needs her mother's first milk, and she needs it now. Milk a little into a clean tumbler and give it with a spoon, slowly, a few drops at a time. Keep her against your own body under the blanket while you do it. Call me again in half an hour."

Karuppi did not like being milked by a child at two in the morning. But Thenmozhi had done it before, and she got half a tumbler of the first milk. It was thick and yellow, more like thin payasam than milk. She sat down in the straw with the kid inside her sweater and the blankets over them both. Kathir held the tumbler.

A few drops went in. The kid swallowed. A few more.

"Now you teach me," said Kathir. "Why milk? Why not just you? You are warm."

"I am helping. My heat is going into her. But I cannot hold her for ever. She has to make her own. For that she needs fuel inside her, and this is fuel."

They gave the milk one small spoon at a time. It took a long while. The lantern hissed. The frost thickened on the tea bushes below the shed.

Then the kid began to shake.

Kathir nearly dropped the tumbler. "Akka! She is getting worse!"

Thenmozhi put her hand on the small ribs. The shaking was fast and steady, and under it the kid's skin was warmer than before.

"No," she said slowly. "She is shivering. She could not even shiver before. Her muscles are working very fast, and the work makes heat. She is burning her dinner, Kathir. Her first one."

When they called Dr Revathy again, the kid was trying to climb out of the sweater. The doctor laughed. "Put her by her mother and let her find the milk herself. I will come up in the morning to look at them both."

The brown kid stood, fell, and stood again. She pushed under Karuppi, next to her black sister, and found what she wanted. Her tail began to flick.

Kathir folded Paatti's shawl. He looked at it for a moment.

"It did help a bit," he said.

"It helped," said Thenmozhi. "Once there was something under it to keep."

**For the parent:** For the parent: a blanket, a shawl or a muffler makes no heat. It only slows the heat leaving. A living body is warm because its cells break food down all the time, and heat escapes at every step. The newborn kid had almost no fuel, so wrapping alone could not warm her. Drying her, lending her warmth and feeding her gave her own cells something to break down. Her shivering was her muscles using that fuel to make heat.

- Ask: A warm tumbler wrapped in wool still goes cold. A child wrapped in wool stays warm. What is the difference between them?
- Ask: Why can a newborn animal get cold much faster than a grown one?
- Ask: Shivering looks like a bad sign. Why can it be a good one?
- Ask: What three things warmed the kid, and what did each one do?
- Ask: Where does your own warmth come from on a cold night?

### g1 · Break or Build? (20 min)
- **rules:** Put the two labels on the table, BREAK DOWN on the left and BUILD UP on the right. Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile, question side up.; The first player reads the top card aloud. On a count of three, every player points at one of the two labels.; Turn the card over and read the answer. Each player who pointed at the right label scores 1 point.; Put the card under the right label, and pass the pile to the next reader.; The game ends when the pile is used up. The player with the most points wins.; Playing alone: sort all 24 cards into two piles without looking at the backs. Then turn them over and count how many you got right. Play again another day and try to beat your score.
- **Printable cards: 24 body cards to cut out (the answer is on the back):** After lunch, your liver joins glucose units into glycogen. (back: Build up. Small units are joined into a bigger store. The liver pays for it with ATP.); At three in the night, your liver turns glycogen into glucose. (back: Break down. The store is cut back into glucose, which goes into the blood for the brain.); A muscle cell turns glucose into carbon dioxide and water while you run. (back: Break down. This is cellular respiration. It loads ATP and gives off heat.); New skin grows over a grazed knee. (back: Build up. Cells join amino acids into new proteins and make new cells.); Yeast turn sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. (back: Break down. This is fermentation. The yeast get a little energy from each sugar molecule.); A hen's body makes the protein of an egg white from the grain she ate. (back: Build up. Amino acids from her food are joined into new protein.); A camel uses up the fat in their hump on a long walk. (back: Break down. The fat is broken down to carbon dioxide and water, for energy.); A baby's bones grow longer. (back: Build up. New bone material is added at the growing ends. Growth is anabolism.); In a sprouting moong seed, stored starch is cut into sugar. (back: Break down. The seedling breaks down the store to get sugar and energy.); A sprouting moong seed makes a new root. (back: Build up. The seedling joins small molecules into the walls and proteins of new cells.); A rice plant joins carbon dioxide and water into glucose in sunlight. (back: Build up. This is photosynthesis. The energy for the building comes from light.); An old red blood cell is taken apart in the spleen. (back: Break down. The cell lasted about four months. Its iron is saved and used again.); Your bone marrow makes about two million new red blood cells every second. (back: Build up. Building new cells needs amino acids, iron and a great deal of ATP.); Brown fat cells in a newborn baby break down fat to make heat. (back: Break down. Here the energy is not caught in ATP. Nearly all of it becomes heat.); A bar-headed goose lays down body fat before flying over the Himalayas. (back: Build up. Spare food is built into fat, a store for the journey.); A spider makes silk for a web. (back: Build up. Silk is a protein. The spider joins amino acids to make it.); A mushroom's threads take in sugar from a rotting leaf and use it for energy. (back: Break down. The fungus breaks the sugar down, as your cells do.); A cow's body makes milk fat and milk sugar for her calf. (back: Build up. Small molecules from her food are joined into the fat, sugar and protein of milk.); An arm kept in a plaster cast for six weeks gets thinner. (back: Break down. Muscle protein is always being broken down. In an unused muscle, less is rebuilt.); A weightlifter's muscle fibres get thicker over months of training. (back: Build up. Training makes muscle cells build more protein than they break down.); In your stomach, enzymes cut the protein of dal into shorter chains. (back: Break down. This is digestion, in the gut. It happens before metabolism in the cells, but it is breaking down too.); A cell copies all its DNA before it divides. (back: Build up. The cell joins millions of small units into two new strands, and spends ATP on each join.); A firefly uses up fuel to load the ATP that powers their light. (back: Break down. Breaking down fuel loads the ATP. Spending the ATP makes the light.); A Himalayan brown bear lives on body fat through the winter sleep. (back: Break down. The bear's cells break down stored fat for energy for months.)

### e1 · The Yeast Fuel Test: which foods can a yeast cell break down for energy? (40 min)
- **question:** Which foods can yeast break down for energy: sugar, atta, a food you choose, or nothing at all? How can you tell from a balloon?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which bottle will have the biggest balloon after 30 minutes: no food, sugar, atta or your own food? Which will have no gas at all? Write your guesses and the reason for each.

Start all four bottles within two minutes of each other, and keep them side by side. Bottle A has yeast and no food. It shows what yeast do with nothing to eat.
- **safety:** A grown-up mixes the warm water. Do not taste the mixtures, and pour them down the sink afterwards. Balloons can choke a small child, so keep them away from children under three.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all four bottles. Draw a bar chart of the four balloons at 30 minutes. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Bottle and food | My guess: big, small or none | Froth at 10 minutes: none, a little or a lot | Balloon at 20 minutes (cm round) | Balloon at 30 minutes (cm round)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wash and label the four bottles A, B, C and D. 2. Blow up each balloon once and let the air out, so it stretches easily. 3. Put one level teaspoon of dry yeast into each bottle, using the paper funnel. 4. Let your child look at the yeast grains and smell them.  
  *Parent says:* These grains are dry yeast. Each grain holds a great many living cells, and they are resting. Water will wake them. What do you think they will need after that?  
  *Child mode:* Label four bottles. Stretch four balloons. Put one teaspoon of yeast in each bottle. Look at the grains. Smell them.
  - Note: Dry yeast keeps for months in a closed packet. If the packet is old or has been open for weeks, buy a new one. Any small bottles work if all four are the same.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put no food in bottle A. 2. Put two level teaspoons of sugar in B, and two of atta in C. 3. Your child chooses the food for D and adds two level teaspoons. 4. Ask for a guess and a reason for each bottle, and write them in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Bottle A gets yeast and no food. B gets sugar, C gets atta, and D is your choice. Which balloon will grow biggest? Which will stay flat? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Add no food to A. Add sugar to B and atta to C. Choose a food for D. Guess what each balloon will do. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do we need bottle A, which has yeast and no food? (good: To see what yeast do without food.; So we know that any extra gas in the other bottles comes from the food.)
  - Note: Take any guess with a reason. Many children expect atta to do as well as sugar, because both are energy foods. Do not correct this now. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up mixes hot and cold water until it feels just warm on the wrist. 2. Pour 100 millilitres into each bottle. 3. Hold a thumb over each top and swirl for ten seconds. 4. Stretch a balloon over each neck and note the time.  
  *Parent says:* The water must be warm, not hot. Hot water kills yeast. Let's fill all four quickly, so they start together.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up for warm water. Pour 100 ml into each bottle. Swirl each one. Fit the balloons. Note the time.
  - Note: Water at about 35 to 40 degrees Celsius is right. It should feel pleasantly warm, never hot. If you have no measuring cup, use half a 200 millilitre tumbler for each bottle.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the bottles side by side, away from a fan. 2. At 10 minutes, look for froth and bubbles and write what you see. 3. Let your child hold each bottle and smell near the neck. 4. While you wait, read the two cards on catabolism and anabolism together.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely at each bottle. Where do you see bubbles rising? Does any balloon stand up? What do you think the bubbles are?  
  *Child mode:* Wait ten minutes. Look for froth and bubbles. Write what you see. Smell near each bottle.
  - Ask: What do you notice, and what do you wonder? (good: The sugar bottle is frothing and its balloon is standing up.; I wonder why bottle A has no bubbles when the yeast are the same.)
  - Note: By 10 minutes the sugar bottle usually has a layer of froth and a balloon that stands upright. If it has no froth at all by 15 minutes, the yeast was old or the water too hot. Start again with new yeast.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At 20 minutes, wrap the string once round the widest part of each balloon. 2. Lay the string on the ruler and write the length. 3. Write 0 for a balloon that hangs limp. 4. Do the same at 30 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* We measure round the widest part of each balloon. A limp balloon scores zero. Let's measure all four now, and again in ten minutes.  
  *Child mode:* At 20 minutes, measure round each balloon with string. Write it down. Do it again at 30 minutes.
  - Note: The usual result: sugar gives a firm balloon, and jaggery or honey do nearly as well. Atta gives a small balloon or only a little froth. No food, salt and oil give almost nothing. Milk powder gives little, because yeast have no enzyme for milk sugar.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. 3. Only then, read the cards on catabolism and on respiration and fermentation.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Which foods could the yeast break down? What is your evidence? (good: Sugar, because that balloon grew most.; Bottle A had the same yeast and water and made no gas, so the gas came from breaking down the food.)
  - Ask: Atta is an energy food for us. Why did the yeast make so little gas from it? (good: Atta is mostly starch, which is long chains of sugar units.; Yeast have no enzyme to cut starch, so they can use only the little sugar that is in the atta.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses bottle A as evidence. The explanation: a yeast cell breaks sugar down to get energy, and carbon dioxide is what is left over. With little oxygen in the bottle, the yeast ferment the sugar, so alcohol is left over too. Your own cells break the same sugar down fully, with oxygen.
  - Check: Bottle A had yeast and warm water but no food, and its balloon stayed flat. What does that show? → **The gas comes from yeast breaking down food**

### e2 · The Lunch Test: what changes in your body after a meal? (30 min, 2 days)

## Worksheets
### Metabolism: breaking down, building up and heat (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is metabolism?  
   Options: The squeezing of food along the gut / All the chemical changes inside living cells / Breathing air in and out / The speed at which you eat  
   **Answer:** All the chemical changes inside living cells. *Why:* Metabolism is every chemical change in a cell, both breaking down and building up. Choosing 'the gut' mixes it up with digestion, which happens before the food enters the cells.
2. (sort) Sort these into catabolism (breaking down) and anabolism (building up).  
   **Answer:** glucose is turned into carbon dioxide and water → Catabolism; amino acids are joined into muscle protein → Anabolism; glycogen is cut into glucose → Catabolism; glucose units are joined into glycogen → Anabolism; body fat is used on a long walk → Catabolism; a bone grows longer → Anabolism. *Why:* Cutting glycogen, fat or glucose into smaller pieces is catabolism. Joining small units into glycogen, protein or bone is anabolism.
3. (fill) Building molecules up costs energy. The small molecule that carries this energy inside a cell is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ATP']. *Why:* ATP is loaded with energy when food is broken down, and spent wherever the cell needs energy. 'Glucose' is the fuel, not the carrier.
4. (truefalse) True or false: your body stops using energy when you lie completely still.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The heart, brain, liver and kidneys work all the time. The energy a resting body uses is its basal metabolic rate.
5. (mcq) A tumbler of warm water goes cool in an hour, and you do not. Why?  
   Options: Your cells keep making heat from food / Your skin stops all heat leaving / The room heats you but not the water / Water cannot hold heat  
   **Answer:** Your cells keep making heat from food. *Why:* You lose heat to the room all the time, just as the water does. Your cells replace it by breaking down food. Choosing 'skin stops heat' is the blanket mistake: skin only slows the heat.
6. (write) In the Yeast Fuel Test, why did every bottle get the same yeast and the same water?  
   **Answer:** So that the food was the only thing that changed. Then any difference between the balloons must come from the food.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a fair test changes one thing only, and names that thing: the food.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Catabolism → breaking large molecules into smaller ones; Anabolism → joining small molecules into larger ones; Glycogen → a store of glucose in the liver and muscles; Metabolic rate → how fast a body uses energy; Fermentation → getting energy from sugar without oxygen. *Why:* These words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
8. (mcq) Which gas filled the balloons in the Yeast Fuel Test?  
   Options: Oxygen / Steam / Ordinary air from the room / Carbon dioxide  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide. *Why:* Yeast give off carbon dioxide when they break sugar down. Choosing 'oxygen' mixes yeast up with a plant in sunlight, which gives off oxygen.
9. (order) Put these in order, from a meal to the heat of your skin.  
   **Answer:** ['Starch in rice is cut into glucose in the gut', 'Glucose crosses into the blood', 'A cell breaks glucose down and loads ATP', 'The cell spends the ATP on its work', 'The energy leaves the body as heat']. *Why:* Digestion comes first, then the blood carries glucose to the cells. The cells break it down, spend the ATP, and the energy ends as heat.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a blanket makes heat.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A blanket only slows the heat leaving a body. The heat is made by the body's cells as they break down food.
11. (mcq) Where does the body keep its large, long-lasting store of energy?  
   Options: In the blood / In the stomach / In the bones / In body fat  
   **Answer:** In body fat. *Why:* Fat holds the most energy for each gram. Glycogen in the liver and muscles is a smaller, quicker store. The blood carries only a little fuel at any moment.
12. (draw) Draw a yeast cell with sugar going in. Show two things that come out, and label them.  
   **Answer:** A cell with an arrow of sugar going in, and arrows of carbon dioxide and alcohol coming out. A heat arrow or an ATP label is a bonus.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the sugar is changed into something else. A drawing where the sugar simply vanishes misses that atoms are never lost.
13. (fill) After a meal the body uses extra energy to digest and store the food. This is called the ____ effect of food.  
   **Answer:** ['thermic']. *Why:* The thermic effect of food is the extra energy used to handle a meal. It ends as heat, which is one reason you feel warmer after eating.
14. (write) A grown-up no longer grows. Give one reason their body still needs to build new molecules every day.  
   **Answer:** The body breaks down and rebuilds its own proteins and cells all the time. Red blood cells, skin and the gut lining wear out and are replaced.. *Why:* A strong answer names turnover, or gives an example such as skin, blood cells or a healing cut.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about the heat a body gives off.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses facts from the unit, such as heat escaping at every step, or mammals and birds needing it to stay warm. It chooses one view and gives a fair point from another.. *Why:* All three have part of it. The heat cannot be avoided, mammals and birds depend on it, and it shows that the cells are at work.
### Stores, budgets and where the atoms go (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) After a meal, where do most of the atoms of the fuel your cells burned end up?  
   Options: In the toilet / They turn into energy / In the air, as carbon dioxide you breathed out / They stay in the stomach  
   **Answer:** In the air, as carbon dioxide you breathed out. *Why:* The carbon atoms leave as carbon dioxide in your breath, and the hydrogen atoms leave in water. Choosing 'turn into energy' is a common mistake: atoms are not turned into energy in a body.
2. (truefalse) True or false: lactate from a hard sprint is a waste that the body throws away.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Lactate is a fuel. The heart burns it, and the liver builds it back into glucose. Gerty Cori and Carl Cori described this cycle in 1929.
3. (count) In Twenty Beans, the brain, liver, heart and kidneys use 4, 4, 2 and 2 beans. How many is that altogether?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 4 + 4 + 2 + 2 = 12. So these four organs use 12 of the 20 beans, which is more than half.
4. (mcq) Why does the liver break down glycogen at night?  
   Options: To make you sleepy / To keep glucose in the blood while you are not eating / To cool the body / To store more fat  
   **Answer:** To keep glucose in the blood while you are not eating. *Why:* Your brain needs glucose all night, and you are not eating. The liver lets glucose out of its glycogen store to keep the blood level steady.
5. (order) Put these stores in the order a fasting body uses them, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['Glucose already in the blood', 'Glycogen in the liver', 'Body fat', 'Protein from the muscles']. *Why:* Blood glucose lasts minutes, and liver glycogen about a day. Then fat becomes the main fuel. The body breaks down much muscle protein only when little fat is left.
6. (write) In Grown in the Dark, the seed leaves shrank while the shoot grew. Explain why, using 'break down' and 'build up'.  
   **Answer:** The seedling broke down the starch and protein stored in the seed leaves. Some gave energy, and some of the pieces were built up into the new root and shoot.. *Why:* A strong answer links the two: the building was paid for, and supplied, by the breaking down.
7. (mcq) Which meal costs the body the most energy to digest and store, for the same energy eaten?  
   Options: One rich in fat / One rich in protein / One rich in sugar / They all cost the same  
   **Answer:** One rich in protein. *Why:* Protein costs the most to handle, carbohydrate less, and fat the least. Choosing 'the same' is reasonable if you have not met the thermic effect of food.
8. (fill) A cell that breaks glucose down without oxygen gets much ____ energy than a cell that uses oxygen.  
   **Answer:** ['less']. *Why:* Without oxygen the glucose is broken only part of the way, so most of its energy stays in the alcohol or lactate that is left.
9. (label) On a drawing of a body, label two places that store glycogen and one that stores fat.  
   **Answer:** ['Glycogen: the liver', 'Glycogen: the muscles', 'Fat: under the skin']. *Why:* Fat around the organs in the belly is also right for the fat store.
10. (truefalse) True or false: for each kilogram of body, a shrew uses energy faster than an elephant.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A small warm body loses heat quickly, so each gram of it must make more heat. An Etruscan shrew eats up to twice their own weight a day.
11. (write) In the Lunch Test, why must you measure at the same clock times on a day without lunch?  
   **Answer:** Pulse and temperature change a little through the day anyway. The second day shows those changes, so the rest must be due to the meal.. *Why:* A strong answer says the second day is there for comparison, and that it rules out the time of day as the cause.
12. (match) Match each living being to the way they manage their energy.  
   **Answer:** Honey bees in winter → pack together and shiver their flight muscles; A python → eats one huge meal and then rests for weeks; A Himalayan brown bear → lives on body fat through a winter sleep; A sacred lotus flower → breaks down stored food fast and warms itself; A newborn baby → makes heat in brown fat without shivering. *Why:* Each of these beings solves the same problem, staying alive on the fuel they have, in a different way.
13. (mcq) A person's body uses up 10 kilograms of stored fat. About how much of it leaves through the lungs?  
   Options: About 8 kilograms / About 1 kilogram / None of it / All 10 kilograms  
   **Answer:** About 8 kilograms. *Why:* About 8.4 kilograms leave as carbon dioxide, and the other 1.6 kilograms leave as water. The carbon atoms of the fat are breathed out.
14. (draw) Draw one glucose molecule entering a muscle cell. Show where its energy goes, using three arrows.  
   **Answer:** Arrows to ATP, to heat, and from the ATP to the muscle's work. The best drawings show the heat arrow thicker than the ATP arrow.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that only part of the energy is caught in ATP, and that the rest leaves as heat at once.
15. (perspective) Two people explain why a child's body puts on weight most slowly at about five years old.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the finding that the brain uses the most energy at about that age. It also notes that two things happening together does not prove that one causes the other.. *Why:* The first view fits the measurements. The second is a fair warning about what those measurements can prove.
### Metabolism across the living world (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) A flame and a cell both break fuel down with oxygen. Give two things a cell does that a flame does not.  
   **Answer:** A cell breaks the fuel down in many small steps and catches part of the energy in ATP. A cell uses that energy to build and repair itself.. *Why:* Other strong answers: a cell works at body warmth, a cell controls how fast it goes, and a cell makes copies of itself.
2. (mcq) Yeast, rice plants and people all break glucose down by the same first ten steps. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: It is a coincidence / They copied the steps from each other / It is the only way sugar can ever break / They share an ancestor who had those steps  
   **Answer:** They share an ancestor who had those steps. *Why:* The matching steps and matching enzymes are evidence that all three come from one early ancestor. Choosing 'the only way' is a fair thought, but some microbes break glucose down by other paths.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a growing child breaks the rule that things get more mixed up over time.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A body builds order inside only by giving out heat and waste, which add more disorder outside. Counting inside and outside together, the rule holds.
4. (count) A lunch gives 600 kilocalories. Digesting and storing it costs about one tenth. How many kilocalories is that?  
   **Answer:** 60. *Why:* One tenth of 600 is 600 ÷ 10 = 60. So about 60 kilocalories are spent on handling the meal, and they end as heat.
5. (mcq) Why does a cell break glucose down in many small steps and not in one big step?  
   Options: One step would take too long / Small steps use less glucose / To make more carbon dioxide / Small steps let it catch energy in ATP  
   **Answer:** Small steps let it catch energy in ATP. *Why:* One big step would let all the energy out as heat at once, as a flame does. Small steps let the cell load ATP along the way.
6. (write) The Hadza walk far every day, yet use about the same energy as office workers. Suggest one explanation.  
   **Answer:** The body may save energy on other jobs when a person is very active, so the daily total stays about the same.. *Why:* This is Herman Pontzer's idea, and scientists still argue about it. Another fair answer is that the result depends on how body size is allowed for.
7. (sort) Sort these by whether they raise or lower a body's metabolic rate.  
   **Answer:** running → Raises it; a large meal → Raises it; a fever → Raises it; deep sleep → Lowers it; torpor in a bat → Lowers it; shivering in the cold → Raises it. *Why:* Working muscles, a meal, fever and cold all make cells break down more fuel. Sleep, torpor and a winter sleep all slow metabolism down.
8. (draw) Draw a mitochondrion inside a cell. Show what goes in and what comes out.  
   **Answer:** A bean shape with a folded inner layer. Arrows in: pieces of glucose or fat, and oxygen. Arrows out: carbon dioxide, water, ATP and heat.. *Why:* A strong drawing has oxygen going in. Leaving out oxygen suggests a mix-up with fermentation, which needs none.
9. (mcq) In 1935, mice were fed fat that carried a label. Where did most of the label turn up?  
   Options: In the air they breathed out, at once / In their droppings / In the body fat of the mice / Nowhere, because it vanished  
   **Answer:** In the body fat of the mice. *Why:* Most of the labelled fat was built into the body's own fat stores before being used. This showed that the body's stores are always being changed for new material.
10. (fill) Lynn Margulis showed that mitochondria were once free-living ____.  
   **Answer:** ['bacteria']. *Why:* Mitochondria have their own ring of genes and divide by themselves, as bacteria do. Their genes match one group of bacteria.
11. (write) Kleiber found that an animal 10,000 times heavier uses only about 1,000 times the energy. Give one idea scientists have for why.  
   **Answer:** A big body has less skin for its weight, so it loses less heat for each kilogram. Another idea is that longer blood vessels limit how fast fuel reaches each cell.. *Why:* Either idea is a strong answer. The best answers add that scientists have not settled the question.
12. (order) Put these in order of how much energy each uses for one kilogram of body in a day, from most to least.  
   **Answer:** ['An Etruscan shrew', 'A ten-year-old child', 'An elephant', 'A python resting between meals']. *Why:* Small warm animals use the most for their weight. A python does not keep a steady warmth, so they use the least.
13. (truefalse) True or false: brown fat cells break down fuel mainly to load ATP.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Brown fat cells let the energy out as heat without loading much ATP. That is how a newborn baby keeps warm without shivering.
14. (perspective) Two scientists argue about how life began.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says what each view explains well, and admits that nobody knows yet. It may add that every cell today needs both.. *Why:* Both are real ideas held by working scientists, and neither has been shown to be right.
15. (mcq) A lotus flower stays near 32 degrees Celsius on a cool night. Where does the heat come from?  
   Options: Sunlight stored in the petals / The flower's cells breaking down stored food fast / Warm water in the pond / Insects sleeping inside it  
   **Answer:** The flower's cells breaking down stored food fast. *Why:* Lotus cells break down stored food very quickly, and let the energy out as heat. Choosing 'stored sunlight' is close in one way: the food was made with sunlight, but the heat comes from breaking it down.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is anabolism?  
   Options: Turning glucose into carbon dioxide / Joining amino acids into muscle protein / Cutting glycogen into glucose / Burning fat on a long walk  
   **Answer:** Joining amino acids into muscle protein. *Why:* Anabolism joins small molecules into larger ones. The other three break molecules down, so they are catabolism.
2. (mcq) Where do your cells get the energy to build new molecules?  
   Options: From breaking other molecules down / From the blanket around the body / From oxygen alone / From water  
   **Answer:** From breaking other molecules down. *Why:* Breaking food molecules down loads ATP, and building spends it. Oxygen helps to break food down, but oxygen is not the fuel.
3. (mcq) Why are you warm even when you sleep?  
   Options: The bed stores heat for you / Your heart heats the blood like a stove / You stop losing heat at night / Your cells break down food all the time, and heat escapes  
   **Answer:** Your cells break down food all the time, and heat escapes. *Why:* Every step of metabolism lets some energy escape as heat, and metabolism never stops. Choosing 'the bed' is the blanket mistake: bedding only slows the heat leaving.
4. (mcq) Why do you feel a little warmer after a meal?  
   Options: Food stays hot in the stomach for hours / Eating stops heat leaving the skin / The meal turns straight into heat / Digesting and storing food uses extra energy  
   **Answer:** Digesting and storing food uses extra energy. *Why:* The body spends extra ATP on digesting, absorbing and storing the meal, and that ends as heat. This is the thermic effect of food.
5. (mcq) In a resting adult, which uses more energy?  
   Options: All the muscles together / The skin / The brain, liver, heart and kidneys together / The bones  
   **Answer:** The brain, liver, heart and kidneys together. *Why:* Those four organs are about one twentieth of the body's weight and use more than half of its resting energy. Resting muscle uses little for its size.
6. (mcq) What is glycogen?  
   Options: A kind of body fat / A store of glucose in the liver and muscles / A gas that yeast give off / An enzyme in saliva  
   **Answer:** A store of glucose in the liver and muscles. *Why:* Glycogen is many glucose units joined in a branching chain. It is the body's quick, small store of fuel.
7. (mcq) When the body uses up stored fat, how does most of it leave?  
   Options: As heat / As carbon dioxide in the breath / In the urine as fat / As sweat only  
   **Answer:** As carbon dioxide in the breath. *Why:* The carbon atoms of the fat leave as carbon dioxide. Choosing 'as heat' mixes up energy with atoms. The energy leaves as heat, but the atoms leave as gas and water.
8. (mcq) In the Yeast Fuel Test, why did one bottle have yeast but no food?  
   Options: To show the gas comes from breaking down food / To save sugar / To give the yeast a rest / To make the test longer  
   **Answer:** To show the gas comes from breaking down food. *Why:* That bottle is the comparison. It had the same yeast and water, so any extra gas in the other bottles must come from the food.
9. (truefalse) True or false: an adult who has stopped growing no longer builds new body material.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The body breaks down and rebuilds its own proteins and cells all the time. An adult rebuilds about 300 grams of protein a day.
10. (mcq) Why must a shrew eat far more for their size than an elephant?  
   Options: Shrews are greedier / Elephants do not use energy / Shrew food has no energy / A small warm body loses heat fast  
   **Answer:** A small warm body loses heat fast. *Why:* A small body has a lot of skin for its weight, so it loses heat quickly and must make more heat for each gram.
11. (mcq) Yeast in a closed bottle get less energy from sugar than your muscle cells do. Why?  
   Options: Yeast are too small to use energy / The bottle is too cold / Sugar has less energy in water / Without oxygen, sugar is only partly broken down  
   **Answer:** Without oxygen, sugar is only partly broken down. *Why:* With little oxygen, yeast ferment sugar to alcohol and carbon dioxide. Most of the energy stays in the alcohol.

## Applications
- Bakers and idli makers control fermentation. They keep batter and dough warm so that yeast and bacteria break sugars down faster and give off more gas.
- Doctors measure a patient's metabolic rate from the oxygen they breathe in and the carbon dioxide they breathe out. This tells a hospital how much food a very ill person needs.
- People with diabetes measure the glucose in a drop of blood. The reading shows whether glucose is getting from the blood into the cells.
- Sports scientists plan what runners and cyclists eat before and during a long race, so that their glycogen stores do not run out.
- Nurses keep newborn babies warm against a parent's skin. This is called kangaroo care. A small baby loses heat fast and has little fuel to make more.
- Vets and farmers give a newborn calf, kid or lamb the mother's first milk within hours of birth, so that the young animal has fuel to make heat.
- Engineers who design the cooling for a cinema hall or a metro coach count the people, because each person adds about 80 to 100 watts of heat.
- A PET scanner in a hospital shows which parts of a body are using the most glucose. Doctors use it to study the brain and to find tumours.

## Spotted around us
- A vessel of idli batter that has risen to the rim by morning. Bacteria and wild yeasts broke down sugars overnight and gave off gas.
- A crowded bus or local train that feels warm on a cool morning, because every passenger gives out heat.
- A street dog curled into a tight ball on a cold night. A ball has less surface for heat to leave, so the dog needs less fuel.
- A sunbird visiting flower after flower without stopping. A small warm body must be fed again and again.
- A rock python or a rat snake lying still for days after a meal. Their body is working hard on it.
- Your own breath showing as mist on a cold morning. It carries water and carbon dioxide from your cells.
- A sparrow fluffed up into a ball on a wire in winter, holding in the heat that their cells make.
- Wedding guests who feel warm and sleepy after a big lunch on a banana leaf.

## Open questions
- **Why does metabolic rate rise more slowly than body weight, from a shrew to an elephant?** The pattern has been measured since 1932. Scientists have at least three explanations and have not agreed on one.
- **When a person becomes much more active, does their body save energy somewhere else?** Some measurements suggest that the daily total hardly rises. Others disagree, and it is hard to measure free-living people for months.
- **Did metabolism begin before genes, or genes before metabolism?** Nobody was there, and both ideas can explain some of the evidence. Laboratory tests of early-Earth chemistry are still going on.
- **Why do animals with a slower metabolism for their size often live longer?** The link is real across many mammals, but birds and bats break the pattern. Nobody knows the cause for certain.
- **How does a hibernating bear live for months on body fat without losing much muscle?** A person lying in bed that long would lose a lot of muscle. How the bear's cells keep rebuilding protein is still being worked out.

## Connected maths
- In a Yeast Fuel Test, the sugar balloon measured 12 centimetres round at 10 minutes, 18 at 20 minutes and 24 at 30 minutes. What would you predict for 40 minutes? Why can the pattern not go on for ever? **About 30 centimetres. The pattern must stop when the sugar runs out.**. The balloon grows by 6 centimetres every 10 minutes: 12, 18, 24. The next number in the pattern is 24 + 6 = 30. The gas comes from breaking sugar down, and the bottle has only two teaspoons of sugar. When that is used up, no more gas is made. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- The brain is about 2 parts in 100 of an adult's weight. It uses about 20 parts in 100 of the resting energy. For its weight, how many times the body's average does the brain use? **About 10 times the average.**. If every part used energy in step with its weight, 2 parts in 100 of the body would use 2 parts in 100 of the energy. The brain uses 20 parts. 20 ÷ 2 = 10. So each gram of brain uses about 10 times as much energy as an average gram of body. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A lunch gives 600 kilocalories. The body spends about one tenth of that on digesting and storing the meal. How much is spent, and how much is left for everything else? **About 60 kilocalories are spent, and about 540 are left.**. One tenth of 600 is 600 ÷ 10 = 60. Then 600 − 60 = 540. The 60 kilocalories end as heat within a few hours, which is one reason you feel warmer after a meal. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A resting ten-year-old gives out heat at about 55 watts. About how much heat do 30 resting children give out together? Is that more or less than a 1,500 watt room heater? **About 1,650 watts, a little more than the room heater.**. 30 × 55 = 1,650. A room heater gives 1,500 watts, and 1,650 is more than 1,500. So a classroom of 30 children warms the room about as much as one room heater left on all day. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- When a body uses up stored fat, 84 parts in every 100 leave as carbon dioxide in the breath. The rest leave as water. If 500 grams of fat are used up, how many grams leave in the breath as carbon dioxide? **420 grams.**. 84 parts in 100 of 500 grams is 500 × 84 ÷ 100 = 420 grams. The other 80 grams leave as water. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In a Lunch Test, Kavya's pulse counts before lunch were 76 and 80. Thirty minutes after lunch they were 86 and 90. What is the mean of each pair? By how many beats did her pulse rise? **The means are 78 and 88. Her pulse rose by 10 beats a minute.**. Before lunch: 76 + 80 = 156, and 156 ÷ 2 = 78. After lunch: 86 + 90 = 176, and 176 ÷ 2 = 88. The rise is 88 − 78 = 10 beats a minute. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is the word for all the chemical changes in a living cell? → Metabolism.
- Is joining amino acids into protein breaking down or building up? → Building up. It is anabolism, and it costs energy.
- Which small molecule carries energy inside every cell? → ATP.
- Does a blanket make heat? → No. It only slows the heat leaving. Your cells make the heat.
- Where does the body keep its quick store of glucose? → As glycogen, in the liver and the muscles.
- When a body uses up fat, how do most of the atoms leave? → As carbon dioxide in the breath.
- Which gas do yeast give off when they break sugar down? → Carbon dioxide.
- For their size, who uses energy faster: a shrew or an elephant? → The shrew. A small warm body loses heat fast.
