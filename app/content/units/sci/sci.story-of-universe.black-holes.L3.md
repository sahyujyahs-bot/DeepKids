# Black holes: where gravity is so strong that light cannot get out
`sci.story-of-universe.black-holes.L3`

**Summary.** A black hole is a place in space where gravity is so strong that nothing can get out, not even light. It forms when a huge amount of mass is squeezed into a tiny space, for example when a giant star burns out and its middle collapses. Because no light comes out, a black hole looks perfectly black. Far away, its pull is no stronger than that of a star of the same mass, so black holes do not suck things in from across space. Astronomers find them by watching stars swing round them and gas glow hot as it falls in. There is a huge one at the centre of our own galaxy.

**Intro.** *Black paper is the darkest thing in most homes. But a small hole in a closed cardboard box looks even darker than black paper. Why? The answer is also the reason a black hole is black.* In this unit you make a hole that is blacker than black paper, and find out why. Then you find out what a black hole is: a place where gravity holds on to everything, even light. You squeeze a slice of bread to see how a black hole forms, work out whether a black hole could swallow the Earth, and meet Andrea Ghez, who found the black hole at the centre of our galaxy without ever seeing it.

## Terms
- **Black hole**: A place in space where so much mass is squeezed into so small a space that its gravity lets nothing get out, not even light. *e.g.* There is a black hole about 4 million times as heavy as the Sun at the centre of our galaxy, the Milky Way.
- **Gravity**: The force that pulls things towards each other. Everything that has mass has gravity. The more mass, and the closer you are, the stronger the pull. *e.g.* Gravity brings a ball back down when you throw it up, and keeps the Earth going round the Sun.
- **Mass**: How much stuff something is made of. A big rock has more mass than a pebble. Mass is not the same as size: a squeezed thing keeps all its mass. *e.g.* A slice of bread squeezed into a tiny ball has the same mass as before. Only its size has changed.
- **Absorb**: To soak up. When a surface absorbs light, the light goes into it and does not come back out. The surface warms up a little. *e.g.* Black paper absorbs most of the light that falls on it. That is why it looks dark.
- **Reflect**: To bounce back. We see most things because light reflects off them into our eyes. *e.g.* White paper reflects most of the light that falls on it, so it looks bright.
- **Escape speed**: The speed something must go to get away from a planet or star and never fall back. The stronger the gravity, the higher the escape speed. *e.g.* To escape the Earth, a rocket must reach about 11 kilometres every second. To escape a black hole, you would need to go faster than light, and nothing can.
- **Event horizon**: The edge of a black hole. Anything that crosses it, even light, can never come back out. It is not a solid surface, only a boundary in space. *e.g.* If the Earth were squeezed into a black hole, its event horizon would be about as wide as a marble.
- **Speed of light**: How fast light travels: about 3 lakh kilometres every second. Nothing in the universe can go faster. *e.g.* In one second, light could go round the Earth about 7 times.
- **Orbit**: The path one thing takes as it goes round another, held by gravity. *e.g.* The Earth orbits the Sun once a year. A star called S2 orbits the black hole at the centre of our galaxy once every 16 years.
- **Light-year**: The distance light travels in one year. It is about 9 and a half lakh crore kilometres. Astronomers use it because space is so big. *e.g.* The nearest black hole we know of, called Gaia BH1, is about 1,560 light-years away.
- **Supermassive black hole**: A giant black hole, millions or billions of times as heavy as the Sun. Most big galaxies have one at their centre. *e.g.* The supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way is called Sagittarius A*, said 'Sagittarius A star'.

## Explanations
### What a black hole is · *defined*
**Definition:** A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from it.

Everything with mass has gravity. The Earth pulls you, the Sun pulls the Earth, and you even pull the Earth a tiny bit. Gravity gets stronger when there is more mass, and when you are closer to that mass. A black hole is what happens when a huge amount of mass is squeezed into a tiny space. Then you can get very close to all of that mass at once, and the pull there is enormous. Inside a certain edge, called the event horizon, the pull is so strong that even light cannot get out. A black hole is not a hole in the usual sense. It is not empty, and it is not a tunnel. It is a place where a great deal of mass is packed so tightly that it traps everything that comes too close.
- e.g. If the whole Earth were squeezed until it was about as wide as a marble, it would become a black hole.
- e.g. If the Sun were squeezed until it was about 6 kilometres wide, it would become a black hole. The Sun will never do this, because it is not heavy enough.
- e.g. The black hole at the centre of our galaxy is about 4 million times as heavy as the Sun.
### Why a black hole looks black · *defined*
**Definition:** We see a thing when light comes from it to our eyes. A black hole looks black because no light comes out of it.

Most things do not make their own light. We see them because light from the Sun or a lamp bounces off them into our eyes. White paper reflects most of the light that falls on it, so it looks bright. Black paper absorbs most of the light, but it still reflects a little. That little bit is why you can still see that it is paper. In the box test, light went into the small hole, bounced around inside, and was absorbed a little more at every bounce. Almost none found its way back out of the hole, so the hole looked blacker than black paper. A black hole is blacker still. Light that crosses its edge can never come back out at all.
- e.g. The pupil, the round black centre of your eye, is a hole into your eye. It looks black because light goes in and is absorbed inside.
- e.g. A deep well or a railway tunnel seen in bright daylight looks black inside, because very little light comes back out of it.
- e.g. Astronomers can see the area round a black hole, because gas there glows very hot. The black hole itself sends out no light.
### Escape speed: why light cannot get out · *defined*
**Definition:** Escape speed is the speed something needs to get away from a planet or star and never fall back.

Throw a ball up and gravity slows it down, stops it and brings it back. Throw it faster and it goes higher before it comes back. If you could throw it fast enough, it would slow down but never stop, and it would never come back. That speed is called escape speed. For the Earth it is about 11 kilometres every second, about 40,000 kilometres an hour. Rockets burn fuel for many minutes to reach it. A place with stronger gravity has a higher escape speed. At the edge of a black hole, the escape speed is the speed of light. Inside that edge it is more than the speed of light. Nothing can go faster than light, so nothing inside can get out.
- e.g. The Moon's escape speed is only about 2 and a half kilometres a second, because the Moon has much less mass than the Earth.
- e.g. The Sun's escape speed is about 620 kilometres a second.
- e.g. Light travels at about 3 lakh kilometres a second. It escapes the Earth and the Sun easily, but not a black hole.
### How a black hole forms · *defined*
**Definition:** A black hole forms when so much mass is squeezed into so small a space that gravity becomes strong enough to trap light.

A star is a huge ball of hot gas. Its own gravity is always trying to squeeze it smaller. The heat from the star's burning centre pushes back outwards, so the star stays the same size for millions or billions of years. When a very big star runs out of fuel, the push stops. Gravity wins, and the middle of the star collapses in less than a second. The outer layers can be blasted away in a huge explosion called a supernova. If the middle that is left is heavy enough, nothing can stop it shrinking, and it becomes a black hole. Only stars many times heavier than the Sun end this way. Scientists think a star must start at least about 20 times as heavy as the Sun, but they are still working out the exact limit.
- e.g. The Sun is too light to become a black hole. In about 5 billion years it will swell up, then shrink into a small, hot star called a white dwarf.
- e.g. The black hole in Cygnus X-1, about 7,000 light-years away, is about 20 times as heavy as the Sun. It formed from a giant star.
### Black holes do not suck things in from far away · *defined*
**Definition:** Far from a black hole, its gravity is the same as that of any star or planet with the same mass.

Many people think a black hole is like a vacuum cleaner that sucks in everything around it. It is not. How hard something pulls you depends on its mass and on how far you are from its middle. Squeezing a star into a black hole does not give it more mass. It only lets you get much closer to the middle. If the Sun were somehow swapped for a black hole of exactly the same mass, the Earth would keep going round it on the same path, once a year, as before. It would only get very dark and very cold, because a black hole gives no sunlight. Things fall into a black hole only if they come very close to it.
- e.g. Stars go round the black hole at the centre of our galaxy for millions of years without falling in, just as the Earth goes round the Sun.
- e.g. The nearest black hole we know of, Gaia BH1, has a star going round it once every 186 days, at a safe distance.
### How we find something we cannot see · *defined*
**Definition:** Astronomers find black holes by what they do to things nearby: stars that swing round them, and gas that glows as it falls in.

A black hole gives out no light, so no telescope can see it directly. But its gravity still pulls on everything nearby. When a star moves fast in a small circle round a spot where nothing can be seen, something very heavy must be there. From how fast the star moves and how small its circle is, astronomers work out how heavy the hidden thing is. Gas that falls towards a black hole speeds up, rubs against itself and gets so hot that it glows brightly. Two black holes that crash into each other shake space itself, and huge detectors called LIGO have picked up those shakes since 2015.
- e.g. Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel tracked stars racing round the centre of our galaxy for over 20 years and showed a black hole must be there.
- e.g. In 2019, astronomers using many radio telescopes across the world made the first picture of the glowing ring of gas round a black hole, in a galaxy called M87.
- e.g. India's AstroSat space telescope, launched in 2015, measures X-rays from hot gas falling towards black holes.
### How did the giant black holes in the centres of galaxies get so big? · *debated*
Small black holes form from dying giant stars. But the black holes at the centres of galaxies are millions or billions of times as heavy as the Sun. Astronomers have found some of these giants already in place less than a billion years after the universe began. Nobody is sure how they grew so big so fast.
- e.g. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, has found very heavy black holes in some of the earliest galaxies it can see.
- **Many astronomers:** They started as small black holes from the first stars. They grew by swallowing gas and by joining with other black holes.
- **Priyamvada Natarajan, an Indian-born astronomer at Yale University, and others:** Some started big. A huge cloud of gas may have collapsed straight into a heavy black hole, without becoming a star first. This would give them a head start.

## Mostly agreed
- Black holes are real. *How we know:* Stars have been tracked going round an unseen object at the centre of our galaxy, and LIGO has picked up the shaking of space when two black holes joined.
- Nothing that crosses the edge of a black hole can come back out, not even light. *How we know:* Einstein's theory of gravity, which has passed every test so far, predicts this. Karl Schwarzschild worked out the size of this edge in 1916.
- There is a black hole about 4 million times as heavy as the Sun at the centre of the Milky Way. *How we know:* The star S2 goes round it once every 16 years. Its speed and path give the mass. In 2022, a picture of the glowing gas round it was made.
- Black holes form when giant stars collapse at the end of their lives. *How we know:* Black holes found next to ordinary stars, like Cygnus X-1, are a few to a few tens of times as heavy as the Sun, the mass a collapsed giant star would leave.
- Far away, a black hole pulls no harder than any other thing of the same mass. *How we know:* Newton's and Einstein's laws of gravity both say this, and stars far from black holes move just as these laws predict.

## How we found out
**Andrea Ghez, an American astronomer at the University of California, Los Angeles, and Reinhard Genzel, a German astronomer at the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics, 1939 (Einstein's paper), 1992 and 1995 (the two teams begin), 2020 (Nobel Prize).** In 1939, Albert Einstein wrote a paper arguing that black holes could not form in nature. His own theory of gravity allowed them on paper, but he thought something would always stop a star being squeezed that small. Many scientists agreed. Even if black holes existed, they give out no light, so how could anyone find one? Two teams found a way. Reinhard Genzel, a German astronomer, began in 1992 with telescopes in Chile. Andrea Ghez, an American astronomer, began in 1995 with the giant Keck telescopes in Hawaii. Year after year, they photographed the stars at the very centre of our galaxy and watched them move. One star, called S2, raced round an empty-looking spot once every 16 years. From how fast and how tightly the stars swung round, the teams worked out how heavy the hidden thing was: about 4 million times as heavy as the Sun, packed into a space far too small to hold that many stars. Only a black hole fits. In 2020, Ghez and Genzel shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Roger Penrose. Ghez was only the fourth woman ever to win it.  
*What people believed before:* Einstein and many other scientists thought nature would never squeeze a star small enough to make a black hole. Ghez and Genzel tracked stars racing round the centre of our galaxy and showed that something 4 million times as heavy as the Sun is packed there, unseen: a black hole.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Black holes cannot really form in nature. Something will always stop a star being squeezed that small.** (Albert Einstein in 1939, and Arthur Eddington in the 1930s; *rejected*)  
  For: Black holes were so strange that many scientists thought nature must have a way to prevent them.  
  Against: Roger Penrose showed in 1965 that collapse cannot be stopped once it goes far enough. Since then, stars have been seen going round black holes.
- **A black hole sucks in everything around it, like a vacuum cleaner.** (A common idea in films and cartoons; *rejected*)  
  For: Things that come very close to a black hole do fall in and can never get out.  
  Against: Far away, a black hole pulls just like any star of the same mass. Stars go round black holes for millions of years without falling in.
- **Black holes slowly give off a very faint glow and shrink away over a very long time.** (Stephen Hawking, in 1974; *debated*)  
  For: His working joined the rules of the very small, quantum physics, with Einstein's gravity. Most physicists accept the working.  
  Against: The glow from real black holes would be far too faint to measure, so nobody has seen it.
- **Some giant black holes formed straight from collapsing gas clouds, without a star first.** (Priyamvada Natarajan and others; *debated*)  
  For: It explains how huge black holes appeared so early in the universe. The James Webb Space Telescope has found some that fit.  
  Against: Small black holes that grew fast might explain them too. More observations are needed.

## Roots
We have been learning that a black hole is a place where gravity is so strong that light cannot get out, so it looks perfectly black. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The hole in your box looked blacker than the black paper. Light went in through the hole and almost none came back out. We see black wherever light does not come back to our eyes. A black hole is black because its gravity lets no light come back at all.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of black as a colour that some things are made of, and of a black hole as a dark lump in space. Black is what we see when no light reaches our eyes from a place, and a black hole is a place that light cannot leave.  
*Evidence:* In the Blackest Black experiment, a hole into a closed box looked darker than black paper, and a smaller hole looked darker than a bigger one. Nothing about the hole was coloured black. Only the light that came back out was different.

- *who am i:* You know about the black hole at the centre of our galaxy only by thinking about what you see around it. Your mind can know a place that your eyes can never see.
- *what is life:* Life on Earth runs on sunlight. If the Sun were swapped for a black hole of the same mass, the Earth would keep going round, but plants would have no light to make their food.
- *what is this world:* The same gravity that brings your ball back down holds stars on their paths round the black hole at the centre of our galaxy, 26,000 light-years away.

Gravity is one pull that every bit of matter has. It holds your feet on the floor, and it gathered clouds of gas into the Sun and the stars. When a giant star burns out, the same pull squeezes its middle smaller and smaller. If nothing can stop the squeezing, a black hole forms. The gentle pull you feel when you jump and the pull that traps light are the same gravity, with far more mass in far less space.

- **Annambhatta's Tarkasangraha (17th century), the Nyaya school's example of inference:** The hill has fire, because the hill has smoke. Wherever there is smoke, there is fire, as in a kitchen. We do not see the fire, but we know it from the smoke. The Nyaya school called this kind of knowing anumana, inference. (Nobody sees the black hole at the centre of our galaxy. Astronomers know it from the stars racing round it, the way the Nyaya thinkers knew the fire from the smoke.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta (verses 3 and 7):** In the beginning, darkness was hidden by darkness. The hymn ends by asking where everything came from, and says that perhaps even the one who watches from the highest heaven does not know. (The inside of a black hole is a darkness nobody can look into. Scientists say honestly that they do not yet know what happens at its centre, as the hymn honestly asks rather than claims.)
- Science (settled): Black holes are real. Stars have been tracked going round one at the centre of our galaxy, and in 2015 LIGO picked up the shaking of space from two black holes joining.
- Science (idea): Stephen Hawking worked out in 1974 that black holes should give off a very faint glow and slowly shrink. Nobody has been able to see this yet.
- Science (open): What happens at the very centre of a black hole is not known. Our best theory of gravity gives impossible answers there.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: Only what we perceive directly can be trusted. Inference can mislead: smoke can rise from many things besides fire. On this view, a black hole that nobody can ever see is a guess, however good, and not knowledge.

## Story: The Hole in the Mysore Pak Box
*Nila, Karthik and Kamala Paati are made up. The science is real: a small hole into a closed box looks blacker than black paper, the Earth's escape speed is about 11 kilometres a second, and the nearest black hole known in 2026, Gaia BH1, is about 1,560 light-years away. The Earth squeezed into a black hole would be about 2 centimetres across.*

Nila was nine and her brother Karthik was seven. They lived on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Mysuru, with their Amma, their Appa and their grandmother, Kamala Paati. Paati had taught physics in a girls' school for thirty years. She still kept a box of chalk in her cupboard.

One evening Karthik came to Paati's room with a long face. He had watched a cartoon at his friend's house. In it, a black hole had swallowed a spaceship, then a planet, then the whole Earth. 'Paati,' he said, 'is a black hole coming to eat us?'

Paati put down her newspaper. 'First tell me,' she said. 'What does black mean?' Karthik pointed at his school shoes by the door. 'Those are black.' 'Good,' said Paati. 'Nila, bring me that empty Mysore pak box from the kitchen, and the black paper from your craft drawer.'

Paati glued black paper all over the lid of the sweet box. Then she put the lid on and pushed a pencil through the middle of it, making a small round hole. She held the box up near the tube light. 'Now look,' she said. 'Which is darker, the black paper or the hole?'

Karthik looked for a long time. 'The hole,' he said in surprise. 'The hole is blacker than the black paper!' Nila looked too. She could see the paper's tiny wrinkles and a faint shine on it. The hole had no shine at all. It looked like a little piece of night.

'We see things because light bounces off them into our eyes,' said Paati. 'Black paper soaks up most of the light, but it still sends a little back. That is how you can tell it is paper. Light that goes into the hole bounces around inside the box. Each time it bounces, more of it is soaked up. Almost none finds its way back out of that small hole.'

'So a black hole is a hole in space?' asked Karthik. 'No,' said Paati. 'That name is a bit of a trick. A black hole is not empty. It is a place where a huge amount of stuff is squeezed very tight. Its gravity is so strong that light falls in and can never climb back out. That is why it is black.'

She picked up Karthik's soft ball from the floor and tossed it gently up. It rose, stopped and came down into her hands. 'Gravity pulled it back,' she said. 'If I throw harder, it goes higher. If I could throw it about 11 kilometres every second, it would never come back. It would escape the Earth. Light goes much faster than that, so light escapes the Earth easily. But at the edge of a black hole, even light is not fast enough.'

Karthik frowned. 'How does stuff get squeezed so tight?' Paati went to the kitchen and came back with a slice of bread. She squeezed it in her fist, rolled it and squeezed it again, until it was a hard little ball the size of a marble. 'Same bread,' she said. 'Same amount of stuff. Only much smaller. If you could squeeze the whole Earth until it was as small as this ball, it would become a black hole. When a giant star burns out, its own gravity squeezes its middle like this, and some of them become black holes.'

'Then the Sun could become one and eat us!' said Karthik. 'The Sun is not heavy enough,' said Paati. 'Only stars many times heavier than the Sun end as black holes. And the nearest black hole we know of is about 1,560 light-years away. Light from that far away takes more than fifteen hundred years to reach us.'

Nila had been quiet, thinking. 'Paati,' she said slowly, 'suppose the Sun did turn into a black hole. Not bigger. Just the Sun, squeezed small. Would the Earth fall in?' Paati smiled. 'What do you think? Does squeezing the bread make it heavier?'

Nila looked at the bread ball. 'No. It weighs the same.' She thought some more. 'Then the Sun would weigh the same too. So it would pull the Earth just as hard as now. The Earth would keep going round it, the same as always.' She stopped. 'But it would be dark. And very cold.'

'Exactly,' said Paati. 'From far away, a black hole pulls no harder than a star of the same weight. It is not a vacuum cleaner. Things fall in only if they go very close. Stars go round the big black hole at the centre of our galaxy for millions of years, the way the Earth goes round the Sun.'

Karthik let out a long breath. 'So the cartoon was wrong.' 'The cartoon was exciting,' said Paati, 'and wrong.'

That night Karthik took the Mysore pak box to bed and put it on his shelf, with the hole facing him. 'That's my black hole,' he told Nila. 'Nothing gets out of it.' 'The smell of Mysore pak gets out of it,' said Nila. Karthik laughed and turned off the light.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a hole into a closed box looks blacker than black paper, because light that goes in is absorbed inside and almost none comes back out. A black hole is black for an even stronger reason: its gravity is so strong that light cannot climb out at all. Paati shows how squeezing changes size but not mass. Nila then works out the big idea herself: if the Sun were squeezed into a black hole of the same mass, it would pull the Earth just as hard as before, so the Earth would keep going round it. Black holes do not suck things in from far away.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Blackest Black: is a hole in a box darker than black paper? (30 min)
- **question:** Which looks darker: black paper, or a small hole into a closed box? Does the size of the hole, or the colour inside the box, change how dark it looks?
- **predict:** Before you test it, guess. Which will look darkest: the black paper, a small hole in the box, or a big hole? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Look at everything in the same light, from the same spot, one big step away. Change only one thing at a time: first the size of the hole, then the colour inside the box. Ask a second person to rank them too, because one pair of eyes can be fooled.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes and widens the hole, pushing the pencil and the scissors away from hands. Never look at the Sun, and keep the box out of direct sunshine.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw the lid, shading each part as dark as it looked.
- **results table:** What you look at | My rank (1 is darkest) | Second person's rank | What it looked like
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child a sheet of black paper and a sheet of white paper. 2. Show them the closed box. 3. Ask the guess question below. 4. Wait for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Black paper is about the darkest thing we have at home. Soon we will make a small hole in this box. Which do you think will look darker, the black paper or the hole? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Here is black paper. Here is a closed box. Soon it will have a hole. Which will look darker? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Which do you think will look darkest: black paper, a small hole in the box, or a big hole? Why? (good: The black paper, because it is black.; The hole, because the inside of the box is dark.; Any guess with a reason is good.)
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Most children and adults say the black paper. Do not explain yet. The test will show it.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Glue or tape black paper over the whole outside of the box lid. 2. Leave the inside of the box as it is. 3. Put the lid on. 4. A grown-up pushes a pencil through the middle of the lid to make a round hole, about 1 centimetre wide. 5. Tape a strip of white paper on the lid, next to the hole.  
  *Parent says:* Let's cover the lid with black paper. Now I will make a small hole with the pencil. The white strip is there so we can compare.  
  *Child mode:* Cover the lid with black paper. Put the lid on. A grown-up makes a small hole with a pencil. Stick white paper beside it.
  - Note: Push the pencil through from the outside, with the box resting on a table, never towards a hand. If the pencil hole is small, widen it with the tip of the scissors to about 1 centimetre, the width of a fingertip. Keep the box closed all through the test, so light gets in only through the hole.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the box in a bright room, or near a window in daytime, but not in direct sunshine. 2. Stand one big step away and look at the lid. 3. Rank the three: the white strip, the black paper and the hole. Number 1 is the darkest. 4. Ask another person to rank them too, without hearing your answer.  
  *Parent says:* Look carefully. Put them in order, from darkest to lightest. Now let's ask someone else, without telling them what you said.  
  *Child mode:* Stand one big step away. Look at the white paper, the black paper and the hole. Which is darkest? Number them. Ask someone else too.
  - Ask: Which looked darkest? What did the black paper look like, close up? (good: The hole was darkest.; I could see wrinkles and a little shine on the black paper.; The hole had no shine at all.)
  - Note: The hole should look clearly darker than the black paper, like a small piece of night. You can usually see the texture or a faint shine on black paper, but not in the hole. Write each person's ranking in the table.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up makes the hole bigger with scissors, until it is about 3 centimetres wide, the width of a bottle cap. 2. Put the lid back exactly as before. 3. Look from the same spot. Rank the white strip, the black paper and the big hole. 4. Is the big hole darker or lighter than the small hole was?  
  *Parent says:* Now we change one thing: the size of the hole. Do you think the bigger hole will look darker or less dark? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes the hole bigger. Look again from the same spot. Is the big hole as dark as the small one?
  - Note: Cut slowly round the hole with the scissors pointing away from you. The bigger hole usually looks less black than the small one. You may see the inside of the box faintly. It may still look darker than the black paper. Either result is fine: write what you see.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the box. 2. Line the inside with black paper or a black cloth, on the bottom and the sides. 3. Close it again. 4. Look at the big hole from the same spot and rank it again.  
  *Parent says:* Now the inside of the box is black too. Will the big hole look darker than before? Let's see.  
  *Child mode:* Line the inside of the box with black paper or cloth. Close it. Look at the big hole again. Is it darker now?
  - Note: With a black inside, each bounce soaks up more light, so the big hole should look darker than before. If you have a phone, take a photo of the lid. In the photo, the hole is usually the darkest spot of all.
  - Check: The small hole looked darker than black paper because: → **light went in and almost none came back out**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Then read the parent note aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why does it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a hole into a closed box looks blacker than black paper. What I saw: the small hole looked darkest, the big hole less dark, and a black inside made it darker again. Why: light that went into the hole was soaked up inside and almost none came back out. Now explain: we see black where no light comes back to our eyes. A black hole is black for the same reason, only stronger. Its gravity lets no light come back out at all.

## Worksheets
### Why Black Holes Are Black (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which word means to soak up light?  
   Options: reflect / absorb / escape  
   **Answer:** absorb. *Why:* Absorb means soak up. Reflect means bounce back. Black paper absorbs most of the light that falls on it.
2. (truefalse) A black hole is empty inside, like a hole in the ground.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A black hole holds a huge amount of mass, squeezed into a tiny space. The name only tells us it is black and that things can fall in.
3. (fill) The force that pulls things towards each other is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['gravity']. *Why:* Gravity pulls everything with mass towards everything else. In a black hole it is strong enough to trap light.
4. (write) In the Blackest Black experiment, which looked darker, the small hole or the black paper? Why?  
   **Answer:** The small hole. Black paper still sends back a little light. Light that went into the hole was soaked up inside the box, and almost none came back out.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the child's own result and says what happened to the light.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Absorb → soak up; Reflect → bounce back; Escape speed → the speed needed to get away for good; Event horizon → the edge of a black hole. *Why:* These four words are the ones you need to explain why a black hole is black and why nothing gets out.
6. (sort) Sort these. Does each one send a lot of light back to your eyes, or very little?  
   **Answer:** white paper → A lot of light; a steel plate → A lot of light; black paper → Very little light; a small hole into a closed box → Very little light; the pupil of your eye → Very little light; a mirror → A lot of light. *Why:* Things that look bright send a lot of light back. Things that look black send back very little. A hole into a box, or into your eye, sends back almost none.
7. (draw) Draw the box from the experiment, cut in half so we can see inside. Draw an arrow for light going into the hole and bouncing around inside.  
   **Answer:** A box with a hole in the lid, and an arrow entering the hole and zig-zagging between the walls, getting shorter or fainter at each bounce, without coming back out.. *Why:* The drawing shows the idea: light gets in, but each bounce soaks some up, so almost none finds the hole again.
8. (mcq) Lakshmi throws a ball up gently, then harder. The harder throw:  
   Options: goes higher before it comes back / comes back at once / stays in the air for ever  
   **Answer:** goes higher before it comes back. *Why:* The faster a thing goes up, the higher it gets before gravity stops it. Only at escape speed would it never come back.
9. (truefalse) Light is the fastest thing in the universe.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Light travels about 3 lakh kilometres a second. Nothing goes faster, which is why nothing can get out of a black hole.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: The Sun / A black hole / The Moon / A torch  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The Moon is the odd one out. The Sun and a torch make their own light, and a black hole traps light. The Moon only reflects sunlight. Other answers can be fine if the reason is good, for example 'a black hole, because it gives no light'.
11. (fill) To escape the Earth, a rocket must go about ____ kilometres every second.  
   **Answer:** ['11', 'eleven']. *Why:* The Earth's escape speed is about 11 kilometres a second. Rockets burn fuel for many minutes to reach it.
12. (label) Label the picture of a black hole: the event horizon, the hot glowing gas, and a star going round it.  
   **Answer:** The event horizon is the edge of the black circle. The hot glowing gas is the bright ring swirling round it. The star is the small dot further out, on its orbit.. *Why:* We cannot see a black hole itself. We know it is there from the gas and stars around it.
13. (order) Put the life of a giant star in order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Gravity pulls a cloud into a star. When a giant star runs out of fuel, nothing holds it up, so its middle collapses into a black hole.
14. (write) Your cousin says, 'If I go to space, a black hole will suck me in.' What would you tell him?  
   **Answer:** Only if you go very close to it. Far away, a black hole pulls like any star of the same mass. The nearest one we know of is about 1,560 light-years away.. *Why:* A strong answer corrects the vacuum cleaner idea and explains that the pull depends on mass and closeness.
15. (perspective) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, and her friend Arjun talk about the name 'black hole'.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with evidence. For Kavya: no light comes out, so it is black, and anything that crosses the edge falls in for good. For Arjun: a black hole is a huge mass squeezed small, not an empty space. Then it gives the other side its due.. *Why:* Both are partly right. The name describes what we see and what happens to things that fall in, but not what a black hole is made of.

   **Answer:** No. The Earth would have the same mass, so from the Moon's distance it would pull just as hard as now. The Moon would keep going round it, as before.. *Why:* A strong answer applies the Sun-swap idea to a new case: the pull from far away depends on mass, not size.
4. (truefalse) If you were very close to a small black hole, it would pull your feet much harder than your head.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Close to a small black hole, the pull grows very fast as you get closer, so the nearer end is pulled much harder. You would be stretched long and thin, like a strand of idiyappam. Scientists call this spaghettification. Far away, this stretching is far too small to feel.
5. (fill) Light travels about ____ lakh kilometres every second.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* About 3 lakh kilometres a second. Light could go round the Earth about 7 times in one second.
6. (match) Match each person to what they did.  
   **Answer:** Andrea Ghez → tracked stars round the centre of our galaxy; Albert Einstein → thought black holes could not form in nature; Karl Schwarzschild → worked out the size of a black hole's edge, in 1916; Priyamvada Natarajan → suggested some giant black holes formed straight from gas clouds. *Why:* Black holes were worked out on paper first, doubted, and then found by watching stars.
7. (draw) Draw what a watcher far away would see as a cloud of hot gas swirls into a black hole.  
   **Answer:** A black circle in the middle, with a bright ring or swirl of glowing gas round it. The black part has no light at all.. *Why:* The black hole itself sends no light, so only the glowing gas outside its edge can be seen.
8. (mcq) The Earth's escape speed is about 11 kilometres a second. A spacecraft is going 8 kilometres a second, straight up. What happens?  
   Options: It escapes the Earth for good / It slows down and falls back, or goes round the Earth / It turns into light  
   **Answer:** It slows down and falls back, or goes round the Earth. *Why:* 8 is less than 11, so the spacecraft cannot escape for good. Going straight up, it would slow and fall back. Satellites at about 8 kilometres a second go round the Earth instead of escaping.
9. (sort) Sort these. Is each one something scientists are sure about, or still arguing about?

What happens deep inside, and how the giants grew, are still open questions.
10. (order) Put these in order, from the lowest escape speed to the highest.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* More mass packed into a smaller space means stronger gravity at the surface, and a higher escape speed. A black hole's is the highest of all.
11. (label) Label the picture of a giant star near the end of its life: the burning middle, the outer layers, and the arrows for gravity pulling in.  
   **Answer:** The burning middle is the bright core. The outer layers are the huge glowing ball around it. The gravity arrows point inwards, towards the middle.. *Why:* While the middle burns, its heat pushes out against gravity. When it stops, gravity wins and the middle collapses.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a black hole?  
   Options: An empty hole in space / A place where gravity is so strong that not even light can get out / A star that has gone dark for the night  
   **Answer:** A place where gravity is so strong that not even light can get out. *Why:* A black hole is a place where a huge amount of mass is squeezed so tight that its gravity traps even light. Picking 'an empty hole' is a common mix-up from the name. A black hole is not empty at all.
2. (mcq) Why does a black hole look black?  
   Options: It is made of black rock / No light comes out of it / It is always in a shadow  
   **Answer:** No light comes out of it. *Why:* We see black where no light comes back to our eyes. A black hole's gravity lets no light out. It is not made of anything painted or coloured black.
3. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 3, makes a small hole in the black lid of a closed box. Which looks darkest?  
   Options: The black paper on the lid / The small hole / Both look exactly the same  
   **Answer:** The small hole. *Why:* The small hole looks darkest. Black paper sends back a little light. Light that goes into the hole is soaked up inside and almost none comes back out.
4. (mcq) A boy throws a ball straight up, harder each time. What happens?  
   Options: It goes higher each time before it falls back / It always goes to the same height / It falls back faster when he throws it gently  
   **Answer:** It goes higher each time before it falls back. *Why:* A faster throw goes higher before gravity stops it. At escape speed, about 11 kilometres a second for the Earth, it would never come back.
5. (mcq) Why can light not get out of a black hole?  
   Options: The black hole is too far away / The escape speed there is more than the speed of light / Light gets tired  
   **Answer:** The escape speed there is more than the speed of light. *Why:* To get out, you must go faster than the escape speed. Inside a black hole the escape speed is more than the speed of light, and nothing can go faster than light.
6. (mcq) How does a black hole usually form?  
   Options: A giant star runs out of fuel and its middle collapses / Two planets crash together / A hole is dug in space  
   **Answer:** A giant star runs out of fuel and its middle collapses. *Why:* When a giant star runs out of fuel, nothing holds it up against its own gravity. Its middle collapses, and if it is heavy enough it becomes a black hole.
7. (mcq) Farhan squeezes a slice of bread into a tiny ball. What has changed?  
   Options: Its mass, the amount of bread / Its size / Both its size and its mass  
   **Answer:** Its size. *Why:* Squeezing changes the size, not the amount of stuff. A black hole is a huge amount of mass squeezed into a tiny space, in the same way.
8. (mcq) Can the Sun become a black hole?  
   Options: Yes, very soon / No, it is not heavy enough / Yes, every star does  
   **Answer:** No, it is not heavy enough. *Why:* Only stars many times heavier than the Sun end as black holes. The Sun will end as a small, hot star called a white dwarf.
9. (mcq) If the Sun were swapped for a black hole with exactly the same mass, what would happen to the Earth?  
   Options: It would be sucked in / It would keep going round, but in the dark and cold / It would fly off into space  
   **Answer:** It would keep going round, but in the dark and cold. *Why:* Far away, the pull depends on the mass, and the mass is the same. So the Earth's path would not change. It would only lose the Sun's light and warmth.
10. (truefalse) A black hole sucks in everything in space, even things very far away.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Far from a black hole, its pull is like that of any star of the same mass. Only things that come very close fall in.
11. (mcq) How did Andrea Ghez find the black hole at the centre of our galaxy?  
   Options: She took a photo of the black hole itself / She watched stars move fast round a spot where nothing could be seen / She flew a spaceship there  
   **Answer:** She watched stars move fast round a spot where nothing could be seen. *Why:* A black hole gives no light. Ghez tracked stars for years. From how fast they swung round, she worked out that something about 4 million times as heavy as the Sun was hidden there.
12. (mcq) In 1939, what did Albert Einstein think about black holes?  
   Options: That one was about to hit the Earth / That they could not form in nature / That they were made of light  
   **Answer:** That they could not form in nature. *Why:* Einstein thought something in nature would always stop a star collapsing that far. Later evidence, like the stars going round the centre of our galaxy, showed that black holes are real.

## Applications
- Watching how stars move round unseen black holes taught astronomers how to weigh things they cannot see, and the same method is used to find planets round other stars.
- Einstein's theory of gravity, which predicts black holes, is also used to keep GPS clocks on satellites correct, so phone maps show the right place.
- The LIGO detectors that picked up colliding black holes measure lengths to far less than the width of an atom. India is building its own detector, LIGO-India, in Maharashtra.
- The computer methods used to make the first picture of the gas round a black hole, from many telescopes at once, are like methods used to build clearer pictures in medical scans.
- Coatings that trap light like the box hole, such as Vantablack, line telescopes and cameras so stray light does not spoil the picture.
- The study of hot gas falling into black holes helps scientists understand how matter behaves at temperatures far higher than any on Earth.

## Spotted around us
- The pupil of your eye, in a mirror. It is a hole into your eye, and it looks black because the light that goes in does not come back out.
- The opening of a deep village well or a temple tank's dark steps, seen from above on a sunny day.
- A railway tunnel on a ghat section, such as the hill railways of the Nilgiris or the Konkan line, looking black inside at noon.
- The windows of a tall building far away at noon, which often look dark even though the rooms inside are lit.
- A sky show about black holes at a planetarium, such as the Jawaharlal Nehru Planetarium in Bengaluru or the B. M. Birla Planetarium in Chennai.
- News about LIGO-India, the gravitational-wave detector being built near Aundha Nagnath in Hingoli district, Maharashtra.

## Open questions
- **What happens at the very centre of a black hole?** Einstein's theory of gravity gives impossible answers there, like an endless squeeze. Physicists think a new theory that joins gravity with quantum physics is needed, and nobody has one that works yet.
- **Do black holes slowly glow and shrink away?** Stephen Hawking worked out in 1974 that they should. But the glow from real black holes would be far too faint to measure, so nobody has checked it.
- **What happens to the information about things that fall in?** If a black hole shrinks away, it is unclear whether any trace of what fell in comes back out. Physicists have argued about this for 50 years.
- **How did the biggest black holes grow so big, so early?** Some were already millions of times as heavy as the Sun when the universe was young. Growing from small black holes may have been too slow. Direct collapse of gas clouds is one idea.
- **How many black holes are there in the Milky Way?** Most black holes have nothing near them to glow or swing round, so they are very hard to find. Estimates run into the millions, but only a few dozen have been found.

## Connected maths
- In the Blackest Black experiment, Revathi made a small square hole 1 centimetre wide and a big square hole 3 centimetres wide. Draw both on squared paper with 1 centimetre squares. How many squares does each hole cover? **The small hole covers 1 square. The big hole covers 9 squares.**. The big hole is 3 squares wide and 3 squares tall. 3 times 3 is 9. So the big hole lets in 9 times as much light, and gives the light a much bigger way back out. That helps explain why it looked less black. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L3`)
- The Moon's escape speed is about 2 and a half kilometres a second. The Earth's is about 11. How many Moon escape speeds add up to about the Earth's? Is it more or less than 4? **A little more than 4.**. 2 and a half plus 2 and a half is 5. Four of them make 10, and 11 is a little more than 10. So the Earth's escape speed is a little more than 4 times the Moon's. This is why rockets leaving the Moon need far less fuel. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- The star S2 goes round the black hole at the centre of our galaxy once every 16 years. It was closest to the black hole in 2002 and again in 2018. When will it be closest next? **In 2034.**. 2002 plus 16 is 2018. 2018 plus 16 is 2034. The years go up by 16 each time, because S2 takes 16 years to go round once. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Light travels about 3 lakh kilometres in one second. The Moon is about 3 lakh 84 thousand kilometres away. Does light from the Moon reach us in less than one second, or more? **A little more than one second.**. In one second, light goes 3 lakh kilometres. The Moon is 3 lakh 84 thousand kilometres away, which is more. So moonlight takes a little more than one second to reach us, about one and a quarter seconds. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- The light we see from the star going round Gaia BH1, the nearest black hole we know of, left the star about 1,560 years ago. If this year is 2026, in which year did that light set off? **In about the year 466.**. 2026 minus 1,560 is 466. So the light set off about 466 years after the year 1. That is about 10 years before the Indian astronomer Aryabhata was born, in 476. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- In the Hidden Partner activity, Ishita's torch went round 6 times in 30 seconds. How long did one round take? **5 seconds.**. 30 seconds shared equally among 6 rounds is 5 seconds for each round. 6 times 5 is 30. Astronomers do the same with stars: they time the rounds to find how long one orbit takes. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
