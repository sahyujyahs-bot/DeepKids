# Liquids up close: why water pushes harder the deeper you go
`sci.states.liquids-up-close.L4`

**Summary.** Poke three holes in a bottle, one high, one in the middle and one low, and fill it with water. The lowest hole squirts the farthest. A thin tube of water and a huge wide tank can push on their bottoms with exactly the same strength, if the water is equally deep. A tap on the top floor of a building runs slowly when the roof tank is nearly empty, though the tank is big. Your child tests where water pushes hardest, marks a level line along a wall with a hose, and finds out why a push on a liquid in a closed pipe goes everywhere. It explains rooftop tanks, dams, car brakes and the deep sea.

**Intro.** *Poke three holes in a plastic bottle, one near the top, one in the middle and one near the bottom. Fill the bottle with water. All three jets start at once. Which one will squirt the farthest?* Many people guess the top hole, because it is higher. The answer comes from something you cannot see: the push that water makes on everything it touches. In this unit you measure that push with a bottle, and with a hose that is longer than a room. You find out why the push grows with depth, why it does not depend on how much water there is, and why the water in a hose always settles to the same level. You also build a machine from two syringes, and see why the top-floor tap runs slowly and why a dam is thickest at the bottom.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Liquid**: A liquid keeps its size but takes the shape of its container. Its particles touch each other and slide past each other. *e.g.* Water takes the shape of a tumbler and keeps the same amount.
- **Force**: A force is a push or a pull. A force can start something moving, stop it or change its shape. *e.g.* You use a force when you push a door open.
- **Pressure**: Pressure is how hard something pushes on each small part of a surface. A big force on a tiny area makes a big pressure. *e.g.* A pin goes into a notice board easily, because all the push of your thumb is on its tiny point.
- **Depth**: Depth is how far down something is below the top of the water. *e.g.* A coin at the bottom of a bucket 30 centimetres deep is at a depth of 30 centimetres.
- **Weight**: Weight is the pull of gravity on something. The more stuff, the more weight. *e.g.* A full bucket of water weighs more than an empty one.
- **Jet**: A jet is a narrow stream of liquid that shoots out of a small hole under pressure. *e.g.* Water squirts as a jet from a hole in a bottle.
- **Level**: The level of a liquid is the height of its top surface. Water that can flow freely settles to the same level everywhere it is joined. *e.g.* The water in the spout of a kettle stands as high as the water inside the kettle.
- **Overhead tank**: An overhead tank is a big water tank high on a roof. It sends water down to the taps below by the push of its own water. *e.g.* Many homes in India have a plastic tank on the terrace.
- **Hydraulic**: Hydraulic means worked by a liquid in a closed pipe. A push at one end moves something at the other end. *e.g.* A car's brakes are hydraulic. Your foot pushes a liquid, and the liquid pushes the brakes.
- **Piston**: A piston is a plunger that fits tightly in a tube and moves in and out. The plunger of a syringe is a piston. *e.g.* The plunger of a syringe or a bicycle pump is a piston.
- **Dam**: A dam is a wall built across a river to hold water back. *e.g.* Mettur Dam holds back the Kaveri river in Tamil Nadu.
- **Incompressible**: Incompressible means that it cannot be squeezed into a smaller space. A liquid is very nearly incompressible, because its particles already touch. *e.g.* You cannot squeeze the water in a full closed bottle into a smaller space.
- **Air pressure**: Air pressure is the push of the air on everything. We live at the bottom of a deep sea of air, so it pushes on us all the time. *e.g.* Air pressure pushes the juice up a straw when you drink.
- **Siphon**: A siphon is a tube that carries a liquid up over a rim and down to a lower place, once it is full. *e.g.* A siphon can empty a water drum into a bucket on the floor.
- **Fair test**: A fair test changes only one thing and keeps everything else the same, so that you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To test the three holes, the bottle, the water and the stool stay the same, and only the height of the hole changes.
- **Blood pressure**: Blood pressure is the push of your blood on the walls of the tubes it flows through. Your heart makes it. *e.g.* A doctor measures blood pressure with a cuff round your arm.
- **Mason**: A mason is a builder who lays bricks and stones. Masons need walls and floors that are level. *e.g.* A mason uses a water level to check that two ends of a wall are at the same height.

## Explanations
### Liquids: particles that touch and slide · *defined*
**Definition:** In a liquid the particles touch their neighbours and slide past them. So a liquid flows, takes the shape of its container, keeps its size and cannot be squeezed into a smaller space.

The particles of a liquid are packed about as close as in a solid, but they are not locked in place. They slide and tumble over each other. This is why a liquid pours and fills the bottom of a container first. Because the particles are already touching, you cannot push them much closer, so a liquid keeps its size when it moves from one container to another. Pour a litre of water from a jug into a wide dish, and it is still a litre.
- e.g. A cup of water poured into a plate is still a cup of water.
- e.g. A sealed bottle full of water is very hard to squash.
- e.g. Milk, juice, oil and honey all flow, but at different speeds.
### Pressure: the push on each small bit of a surface · *defined*
**Definition:** Pressure is how hard something pushes on each small part of a surface. It depends on the force and on the area it is spread over.

Stand on one foot and then on two. Your weight is the same, but on one foot it is pressed on a smaller area, so the pressure on the floor is bigger. A pin has a tiny point, so your thumb pushes it into a board easily. A flat thumb on the board does nothing. Scientists measure pressure as force divided by area. A camel has wide feet, so the pressure on the sand is small and the camel does not sink. A nail on a bed of nails has the same force spread over hundreds of points.
- e.g. A sharp knife cuts better than a blunt one, because the edge is thin.
- e.g. A camel's wide feet spread its weight and stop it sinking into the sand.
- e.g. A tractor with wide tyres sinks less in a wet paddy field than a tractor with narrow tyres.
### Water pushes on everything it touches, in every direction · *defined*
**Definition:** Water pushes on every surface it touches. The push is sideways on a wall, upwards under a boat and downwards on the bottom. It is always at a right angle to the surface.

A liquid cannot hold its own shape, so its particles press against whatever holds them in. Every wall of a container feels this push. In a bottle with holes in the side, the jets leave straight out from the wall, and not downwards. If you hold your hand flat in water, you feel the water on every side. The push at one point is the same in every direction. This is why a tank wall bulges out under the water. It is also why a fish feels the push all over her body, and not only from above.
- e.g. The side of a full bucket bulges a little under the water.
- e.g. Jets leave a bottle at a right angle to the wall, whichever way the holes face.
- e.g. A boat is pushed up by the water under it.
### Pressure grows with depth · *defined*
**Definition:** The deeper you go in water, the bigger the push. The push at any depth is the weight of the water above, pressing on each small part.

Imagine a column of water standing on one square centimetre of the bottom. The deeper it is, the taller the column, and the more it weighs. So the push on that square centimetre is bigger. Every 10 metres of water adds about the push of the whole air above us. That push is about the weight of a one-kilogram bag on each square centimetre. So at 10 metres under water, the total push is about double what it is at the surface. At 20 metres it is about three times. This is why your ears hurt in the deep end of a swimming pool.
- e.g. The lowest hole in a bottle squirts the strongest jet.
- e.g. A swimmer feels a bigger push on the ears at 3 metres than at 1 metre.
- e.g. A water pipe at the bottom of a tall tank must be stronger than one at the top.
### Depth matters, not the amount or the shape · *defined*
**Definition:** The push at a depth in water depends only on how deep it is. It does not depend on how much water there is, or on the shape of the container.

A tall thin tube of water 1 metre deep and a wide lake 1 metre deep make the same push at 1 metre down. This surprises many people. The reason is that only the water straight above a point presses on it. The water to the side is held up by the tube wall, or by the water next to it, which pushes on it from the side with the same push. The lake presses on a much bigger area, so its total force on its bottom is larger. But the pressure, the push on each small bit, is the same. This is sometimes called the hydrostatic paradox.
- e.g. A puddle 5 centimetres deep and a swimming pool 5 centimetres deep push equally on the bottom, bit for bit.
- e.g. A thin tube 10 metres tall can burst a strong barrel at the bottom, as the story of Pascal tells.
- e.g. A tall narrow glass of juice pushes harder on its base than a shallow wide plate of juice.
### Water finds its own level · *defined*
**Definition:** Water that is free to flow in joined containers settles at the same height in all of them, whatever their shapes and widths.

Join a thin tube and a wide bucket with a hose, and the water stands at the same height in both. If one side were higher, the water at the bottom would be pushed harder on that side, and it would flow towards the lower side until the push was equal. Equal push at the same depth means the same height. Masons use a clear hose full of water to mark two spots on a wall at exactly the same height, even if they are many metres apart. A kettle's spout and body also show it, because the water in the spout stands as high as the water inside.
- e.g. The water in the spout of a kettle stands as high as the water in the body.
- e.g. A mason's water level hose marks two points at the same height on a long wall.
- e.g. The water in a house's pipes rises to the level of the water in the roof tank.
### Why the top-floor tap runs slowly · *defined*
**Definition:** The push that sends water out of a tap comes from the height of the water in the tank above it. A tap close below the tank gets only a small push, and a tap far below gets a big one.

A roof tank sends water down by the push of its own water. A tap one floor below the tank has only a little depth of water pushing on it. A tap on the ground floor, many metres lower, has a much bigger push. So when the water in the tank falls low, the top-floor tap suffers most. The ground-floor tap hardly notices. That is why top floors have weak taps in the morning, when the tank has been emptied. Pumps, taller tank stands and keeping the tank full can all help. Narrow pipes and many taps open at once also reduce the flow.
- e.g. A bucket of water held high drains faster through a tube than one held low.
- e.g. Houses at the bottom of a hill get stronger water pressure than houses at the top.
- e.g. A water tower is built high, so that taps far away still get a good push.
### Dams and pipes: stronger at the bottom · *defined*
**Definition:** The push of water is biggest at the bottom, so dams are thickest at the bottom, and pipes and tanks must be strongest there.

A wall holding back deep water feels a push that grows with depth. At the top it is small. Near the bottom it is huge. So a dam is built wide at the base and narrower at the top. The Kallanai, or Grand Anicut, is a stone weir across the Kaveri river in Tamil Nadu. It was built about two thousand years ago, and it is traditionally credited to the Chola king Karikalan. It is still in use today. Tall modern dams, such as the Tehri Dam in the Himalayas, which is about 260 metres tall, follow the rule that a dam is widest at the bottom.
- e.g. Mettur Dam in Tamil Nadu is much thicker at the bottom than at the top.
- e.g. A storage tank has thicker walls at the bottom than at the top.
- e.g. A tall water tank on legs has its outlet pipe at the bottom, where the push is the strongest.
### A push on trapped liquid goes everywhere · *defined*
**Definition:** When you push on a liquid trapped in a closed pipe, the extra pressure goes to every part of the liquid equally. This is Pascal's principle.

Fill a tube joined to two syringes with water, with no air. Push one plunger in, and the other one moves out. Your push has travelled through the water. If the second piston is much wider than the first, the same pressure pushes on a bigger area, so the force is much bigger. A piston with 100 times the area gets 100 times the force. This is how hydraulic machines work. Car brakes, the arm of an excavator and a barber's chair all use it. There is a catch. The big piston moves much less. A 100 times bigger force means a 100 times shorter move. Nothing is gained for free.
- e.g. A foot pushing a brake pedal makes a strong push at the wheel.
- e.g. An excavator's arm lifts tonnes of soil with a liquid pushed through tubes.
- e.g. A car jack lifts a heavy car with a few pumps of a handle.
### Why a liquid passes on a push but a bubble does not · *defined*
**Definition:** A liquid is very nearly incompressible, so it passes on a push almost at once. Air is easy to squeeze, so a bubble of air soaks up some of the push.

Water particles touch, so a push on one end moves the particles all the way along the pipe. If there is a bubble of air in the pipe, the bubble gets squashed first. Part of your push goes into squeezing the air, and the other end moves less. This is why a car's brakes feel soft and spongy if air gets into the brake fluid. Mechanics get rid of the air by letting a little fluid out of each wheel. They call this bleeding the brakes. The same thing happens with the syringes of a hydraulic arm, and that is why every bubble must be tapped out before you start.
- e.g. A spongy brake pedal can mean that there is air in the brake fluid.
- e.g. A hydraulic arm made of syringes with a bubble in the tube moves slowly and unevenly.
- e.g. A medicine syringe is held upright and tapped to get the air to the top before the dose is pushed.
### Pressure in the body and the deep sea · *defined*
**Definition:** Your blood is a liquid, so its pressure also depends on depth. A diver or a deep-sea animal feels a bigger and bigger push of water as she goes down.

When you stand, the blood in your feet is pushed down by the blood above, so the pressure in your feet is bigger than in your head. If you stand up too fast, the blood pools in your legs and you can feel dizzy. A giraffe's heart makes about twice as much blood pressure as ours, to push blood up the long neck to her head. In the sea, the push grows with every metre. At the deepest part of the ocean, about 11 kilometres down, the water pushes with more than a thousand times the push of the air at the surface. Animals that live there have bodies without air pockets to be crushed. Sperm whales dive more than a kilometre, and scientists think their lungs fold flat as they go down.
- e.g. Standing up quickly can make you feel dizzy for a moment.
- e.g. Your ears feel the push in the deep end of a swimming pool.
- e.g. A submarine's hull must be very strong to survive deep dives.
### How does a siphon work? · *debated*
A siphon is a tube that empties a container over its rim. Fill the tube with water, put one end under the water in a drum and the other end lower down outside, and the water keeps running out. Everyone agrees that the outlet must be lower than the water surface in the drum, and that gravity pulls the water down the longer side. Scientists argue about the part played by the air. In the textbook version, the push of the outside air on the water surface in the drum is what pushes the water up and over. In the newer version, the water in the tube behaves like a chain, and its particles hold on to each other, so the falling side pulls the rising side along. Some experiments have reported siphons running inside a vacuum chamber, which started the argument again.
- e.g. A water drum can be emptied into a bucket on the floor with a siphon.
- e.g. Fish tank owners siphon dirty water out of their tanks.
- e.g. A siphon stops working if air gets into the tube, because the chain of water is broken.
- **The air pressure view:** The push of the air on the surface of the water in the drum pushes the water up the tube and over the top. Without air pressure, the water could not rise. This is the version taught in many textbooks.
- **The chain view:** The water particles hold on to each other, so the falling water pulls the rest along, like a chain over a pulley. Some experiments reported that siphons keep running when the air is removed, which fits this view.

## Mostly agreed
- The pressure in a liquid grows steadily with depth, and at one depth it is the same in every direction. *How we know:* Pressure gauges lowered into lakes and oceans read bigger and bigger values with depth, and the numbers match the weight of the water above.
- At a given depth, the pressure does not depend on the shape of the container or on how much water is beside it. *How we know:* Vessels of many shapes and widths, filled to the same depth, give the same reading at the bottom.
- A change in pressure at one place in a closed liquid is passed on equally to every other place. *How we know:* Hydraulic presses, jacks and brakes work exactly as this rule predicts.
- Water that can flow freely settles to the same height in all connected containers. *How we know:* Builders have used water levels for thousands of years, and they agree with other tools.
- At the same depth, a denser liquid pushes harder than a lighter one, whatever its thickness. *How we know:* Measurements in salt water, fresh water, oil and mercury show that pressure follows the weight of the liquid, and not how runny it is.
- The air around us pushes on everything with about the weight of one kilogram on each square centimetre. *How we know:* Barometers have measured this push since the 1640s, and it falls as you climb a mountain.
- The pressure at the bottom of the deepest ocean trenches is more than a thousand times the pressure at the surface, and living things are found there. *How we know:* Instruments and sampling vehicles sent to the deepest trench have measured the pressure and found animals living at great depths.

## How we found out
**Simon Stevin, a Flemish engineer, and Blaise Pascal, a French thinker, 1586 (Stevin) and the 1640s to the 1650s (Pascal).** For a long time, people thought that more water must push harder at the bottom. A wide lake, they said, must press on its bed far harder than a bucket does. In 1586, a Flemish engineer named Simon Stevin reasoned and showed that this is not so. The push on the bottom depends on how deep the water is, and not on how much water there is or on the shape of the vessel. More than sixty years later, the French thinker Blaise Pascal went further. He showed that a push on a liquid in a closed container goes out equally in every direction. A famous story says that Pascal fixed a long thin tube to a sealed barrel of water and poured a few cups of water into the tube. The barrel is said to have burst. The story may be a legend, but the physics is right: a tall thin tube of water pushes on its bottom as much as a tall wide tank does.  
*What people believed before:* People thought that a bigger amount of water, or a wider container, must push harder at the bottom. Stevin and Pascal showed that only the depth matters, and that a push on a closed liquid goes everywhere equally.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A bigger amount of water pushes harder at the bottom, so a wide lake pushes harder than a thin tube.** (Many people, for a long time before 1586; *rejected*)  
  For: A lake is so heavy, and a tube of water holds so little. It seems clear that more water must push harder.  
  Against: The push at a depth depends on the depth only. A thin tube 1 metre deep and a lake 1 metre deep push equally on each bit of the bottom. The lake pushes on a bigger area, so its total force is larger, but the pressure is the same.
- **Water only pushes downwards, because of gravity.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Things fall down, and water flows down.  
  Against: Water pushes sideways on the walls of a bottle, upwards under a boat, and in every direction at once at a point. Jets leave a bottle sideways from the wall.
- **When you drink through a straw, you suck the liquid up.** (Most people, in everyday speech; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels as if your mouth pulls the juice upwards.  
  Against: When you draw in your breath, the air pressure in the straw falls. The air outside pushes down on the juice in the glass, and this push is what drives the juice up the straw. A straw with a hole in its side does not work, because air comes in through the hole.
- **A thick liquid pushes harder than a runny one at the same depth.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Honey is thick, and a jar of honey pushes harder on the bottom than a jar of water.  
  Against: Honey pushes harder because it is heavier for its size. Coconut oil is thicker than water, but lighter, and it pushes less. Pressure depends on the weight of the liquid, not on how runny it is.
- **Water from a tank can squirt higher than the water level in the tank.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: A jet leaving a low hole seems to have plenty of force to go very high.  
  Against: The jet cannot rise above the level of the water that feeds it, apart from small splashes. All the push comes from the height of that water. A fountain that goes higher needs a pump or a higher tank.
- **Nature hates empty space, so a pump can lift water to any height.** (Many scholars before the 1640s; *rejected*)  
  For: A pump sucks, and water follows to fill the space.  
  Against: Well-diggers found that pumps could not lift water higher than about 10 metres. In 1643, Evangelista Torricelli explained this by the push of the air. The air pushes the water up, and the push is only enough to hold up a column of about 10 metres.
- **A dam wall can be the same thickness all the way down.** (Some people; *rejected*)  
  For: A wall looks strong enough if it is strong enough at the top.  
  Against: The push of water grows with depth. At the bottom of a tall dam, it is far bigger than at the top. A wall must be much thicker there, or it would break.

## Roots
We have measured the push of water at different depths and watched water find its level in a hose. Now we ask what it means that a place in water is held only by the water above it, and what that says about us.

**Deep truth.** In the bottle test, the lowest hole squirted farthest, though all three holes met the same water. In the hose test, a thin tube and a wide bucket stood at the same height. A place in water is pushed only by the water above it. How much water lies around it makes no difference.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think more water means more push, so that a big tank beats a small one and a wide lake beats a thin tube. The tests show that depth matters, and the amount does not.  
*Evidence:* In the bottle test, the jets from the deeper holes landed farther. In the hose test, the water in the wide funnel and the thin tube stood at the same height. If your results differ, write them down as they are.

- *who am i:* Your blood is a liquid too. When you stand, the push in your feet is bigger than in your head, just as a lower hole in the bottle pushes harder than a higher one.
- *what is life:* A fish feels the push of the water all over her body, and it grows as she swims deeper. Scientists think deep-diving whales let their lungs fold flat as they go down.
- *what is this world:* The sea, the rain in the clouds and the water in a tank all follow the same rule. The deeper you go, the more water lies above you, and the more it pushes.

Gravity pulls on every bit of water, and the water below must hold up the water above. So the push grows with depth, and it is the same in every direction at one depth. A bottle, a dam and a hose all follow this one rule. Water settles to one level whenever it can flow freely, because only then is the push equal at equal depths.

- **Thirukkural 396, by Thiruvalluvar:** The deeper you dig in sandy ground, the more water springs up. In the same way, the more you learn, the more knowledge springs up in you. Scholars do not agree on the date of the Kural, and some place it about two thousand years ago. (The deeper the well, the stronger the push of the water at the bottom. Deeper means more push, which is the same rule you saw in the bottle test.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 66:** The rivers and the seas are lords of the valley streams because they are good at staying below them. So the streams flow to them. (Water always runs down to the lowest place, and the deepest water there pushes hardest. The sea is the lowest place, and all rivers find their way to it.)
- Science (settled): The pressure in a liquid grows with depth, and at one depth it is the same in every direction.
- Science (settled): A change of pressure at one place in a closed liquid is passed on equally to every other place.
- Science (debated): How a siphon works is still argued, between the push of the air and the pull of water on itself.
- Other view, Some engineers: Pressure is a number that you work out from the depth and the weight of the liquid. To say that a place in the water is held up by the water above it is a helpful picture, but it adds nothing to the maths, and it should not be read as a lesson about anything else.
- Practice, Down to the bottom of the tank (3 min): Close your eyes and breathe in slowly. Picture yourself standing at the bottom of a deep, clear tank. All the water above you rests on you gently. Breathe out slowly, and picture yourself floating up to the top, with the push getting smaller and smaller. Do it three times. Notice how your breath changes.
- Ask: If a place in water is pushed only by the water above it, what is pushing on a fish at the bottom of a pond?
- Ask: A thin tube and a huge lake push equally at the same depth. Which one is stronger?
- Ask: Your heart pushes blood up to your head against gravity. What would happen to the push in your feet if you lay down flat?

## Story: The Tap That Sighed
*A made-up story. Devika, her Ammamma, Mathew chettan, Mr Nair and the building are imagined. The science is real. A roof tank pushes water down the pipes by the depth of the water in it, and a nearly empty tank gives the taps just below it a weak push. Many other things also affect a tap, such as the width of the pipes and how many taps are open at once.*

Devika lived at the very top of Sea Breeze Apartments in Kochi. Her flat was on the fifth floor, just under the roof. She lived there with her grandmother, who she called Ammamma.

Every morning at seven o'clock, the whole building woke up and turned on its taps. Devika's tap did not roar. It sighed. A thin thread of water crept out, and filling one bucket took as long as singing three songs.

Her friend Anoop lived on the ground floor. When he opened his tap, the water leapt out and splashed his shirt. 'Your tap is so strong,' Devika told him. 'Mine is tired.'

On Sunday there was a meeting of all the families in the hall. Everyone spoke at once. Mr Nair, the secretary, banged on the table. 'The top floors need a bigger pump,' he said. 'It will cost eighty thousand rupees.'

Mathew chettan, the caretaker, scratched his head. He was a tall, quiet man who had looked after the building for twelve years. 'The tank on the roof holds five thousand litres,' he said. 'I fill it every night. It is a very big tank. I do not understand why the fifth floor gets so little.'

Devika was sitting on the stairs outside the hall, listening through the door. That week at school she had made a science project with a bottle full of water, with three holes in its side. She remembered something strange. When the bottle was full, the jets leapt out far. When it was half empty, the jets came out weak, though there was still plenty of water inside.

'It isn't how much water there is,' she thought. 'It's how high the water stands above the hole.'

She thought about the tank on the roof. At seven in the morning, after everyone had bathed and filled their buckets, the tank must be almost empty. And her tap was only a little way below it.

The next morning Devika asked Mathew chettan to climb to the roof with her at quarter past seven. He was surprised, but he came. They lifted the lid of the big black tank. At the bottom lay a shallow pool of water, no deeper than Devika's hand.

'See?' said Mathew chettan. 'There is water. Not much, but enough to flow.'

'It flows,' said Devika, 'but it doesn't push. The push on a pipe comes from the water standing above it. A tank can be huge, but if the water is only a hand deep, the push is small.'

She pointed down at the building. 'Anoop's tap is at the very bottom, many floors below the tank. There is a tall column of water above his pipe, even when the tank is nearly empty. So it hardly changes for him. My tap is just a little below the tank. When the tank is full, there is a whole metre of water above my pipe, and it pushes well. When it is nearly empty, there is almost nothing above it.'

Mathew chettan was quiet for a while. Then he laughed. 'In twelve years, nobody has explained my own tank to me. And a girl of nine has done it in three minutes.'

'Then we need a taller tank,' he said. 'Or a bigger pump.'

'Or we keep the tank deep at seven o'clock,' said Devika. 'The pump fills it at night, and by seven it is empty. If it ran again at quarter to six, there would be a lot of water in it when everyone wakes up.'

At the next meeting, Devika brought her bottle. She held it over a bucket in front of all the families and let the water jet from the three holes. 'Look at the lowest one,' she said. 'It's the strongest, because there is the most water above it.' Then she watched the water fall to half, and the jets shrank. 'Same bottle, same holes, same water. Only the depth changed.'

Mr Nair pushed up his glasses. 'So it is not the size of the tank. It is the depth of the water.' 'Yes, uncle,' said Devika.

Mathew chettan put a small timer on the pump switch, so that the pump ran again at quarter to six every morning. It cost a few hundred rupees, and not eighty thousand.

On Monday at seven o'clock, Devika turned her tap. A strong, steady stream rushed out into the bucket. Ammamma clapped her hands.

Anoop called her on the phone. 'Is your tap better?' 'Yes,' said Devika. 'Now the tank is deep in the morning.' There was a pause. 'And mine was always strong,' said Anoop, 'because I live at the bottom of the whole building's water.'

**For the parent:** The push of water out of a tap depends on how high the water stands above the tap, and not on how much water is in the tank. A deep tank pushes harder than a shallow one, even if the shallow one is wider.

- Ask: Why was the tap on the ground floor strong even when the roof tank was nearly empty?
- Ask: Why did the top-floor tap get weaker as the water in the roof tank went down?
- Ask: Devika's bottle jets got weaker as the bottle emptied. How did that help her understand the tank?
- Ask: What did the new timer change inside the roof tank at seven in the morning?
- Ask: Mr Nair thought a bigger pump was the answer. Why was the problem not about how big the tank was?

## Experiments
### e1 · Three Jets: how far does water squirt from holes at three heights? (45 min)
- **question:** A bottle has three holes of the same size, at three different heights. When the water squirts out, which hole sends its jet farthest, and which sends it the shortest distance?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which hole will squirt the farthest, the top one, the middle one or the bottom one? Which will squirt the shortest distance? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** You use one bottle, with three holes made by the same tool, so they are the same size. The water starts at the same line each time, and you keep it there by topping up from a jug. The bottle stands on the same stool each time, and you peel off all three tapes at the same moment. Only the height of the hole changes, so any difference in the jets comes from the height.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes, by pushing the compass point or nail away from the body into the bottle on a folded cloth. Wet floors are slippery. Wipe up water at once. Keep water away from electric sockets and switches.
- **record:** Fill in the table with the three results for each hole. Then draw the bottle with its three jets and show where each one landed.
- **results table:** Hole | Height of the hole above the base of the bottle (cm) | Depth of the hole below the water line (cm) | Where the jet landed, measured from the foot of the stool (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the bottle, the stool and the jug. Ask your child to guess, and to give a reason. Write the guess down. Do not correct it.  
  *Parent says:* We will make three holes, one high, one in the middle and one low. We fill the bottle with water. Which hole will squirt the farthest? Which will squirt the shortest distance? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three holes in a bottle. Which squirts the farthest? Why?
  - Ask: Which hole will squirt the farthest, and why? (good: The top hole, because it is highest.; The bottom hole, because it has the most water above it.; The middle hole, because it is in the middle.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is good. Write it at the top of the table.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark three spots in a vertical line on the bottle, at 4, 10 and 16 centimetres above the base. 2. A grown-up pushes the compass point or nail through each spot to make a hole. 3. Cover each hole with tape. 4. Mark a water line at 22 centimetres above the base.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up will make three small holes in a straight line. Then you cover each hole with tape on the outside. Then we mark the water line.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes three holes. Cover each with tape. Mark the water line.
  - Note: Make all three holes with the same tool, so they are the same size, about 2 millimetres. If the holes are different sizes, the test is not fair. If the straight part of your bottle is shorter than 22 centimetres, put the water line 2 centimetres below where the bottle starts to narrow, and work out each depth as the water line minus the height of the hole.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the stool in the bathroom, or on a balcony. 2. Lay the tape measure on the floor along a straight line from the foot of the stool. 3. Stand the bottle on the edge of the stool, with the holes facing the tape measure.  
  *Parent says:* Put the stool where water can drain away. Lay the tape measure on the floor, straight out from the stool. Stand the bottle on the edge, with the holes facing the tape.  
  *Child mode:* Put the stool in the bathroom. Lay the tape on the floor. Stand the bottle on it.
  - Note: The stool matters. If the bottle stands on the floor, the lowest jet has no room to fall, and lands short. A 40 centimetre stool gives every jet room to fall.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the bottle to the 22 centimetre line. 2. One person peels all three pieces of tape off at the same moment. 3. A second person pours from the jug to keep the water at the line. 4. A third person watches and marks where each jet first lands.  
  *Parent says:* Fill the bottle to the line. Peel all three tapes off together. Keep pouring to hold the water at the line. Watch where each jet lands.  
  *Child mode:* Peel the tapes off together. Watch where the jets land. Mark the spots.
  - Note: Wet marks on the newspaper show where each jet landed. Read the distance as quickly as you can. A phone video from the side also helps.
  - Wait: Look at the first five seconds of the jets. After that, the water level starts to fall.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read each landing spot on the tape measure. 2. Write the distances in the table. 3. Refill, re-tape and repeat twice more. 4. Pick the middle number for each hole.  
  *Parent says:* Read the distance for each jet. Write it in the table. Then do it two more times. Use the middle number for each hole.  
  *Child mode:* Read the three distances. Do it twice more. Use the middle number.
  - Note: If your results do not match, check that the holes are the same size, and that the water was kept at the line.
  - Check: The lowest hole squirted the farthest. Why? → **The water above the lowest hole is deepest, so the push is the biggest**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lower the water line by 10 centimetres, to 12 centimetres. 2. Peel off the tapes again and watch the jets. 3. Compare with the first time.  
  *Parent says:* Now we try a lower water line. Peel off the tapes again. Are the jets longer or shorter? What do you think this tells us about the water above?  
  *Child mode:* Lower the water line. Test again. Are the jets longer or shorter?
  - Ask: What happened to the jets when the water was lower, and why? (good: They were shorter, because there was less water above each hole.; They were weaker, because the push was smaller.; The push depends on the depth of the water.)
  - Note: The jets will be shorter, and the top hole may stop altogether if the water falls below it.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the push of water is bigger when the water is deeper. What I saw: the lowest hole squirted the farthest, and the jets got shorter when the water was lower. Why: the push comes from the weight of the water above, and deeper holes have more water above them.
  - Think: If the bottle were twice as wide but held the same depth of water, do you think the jets would be longer, shorter or the same? Why?
### e2 · Same Level: mark a level line on a wall with a hose (45 min)
- **question:** A clear hose full of water has one end at each of two spots on a wall, 3 metres apart. The water in the hose stands at some height at each end. Are the two heights the same? Does a wide funnel on one end change it? Does a bubble of air?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the two marks be at the same height from the floor? Will the water stand higher in the wide funnel or in the thin end of the hose? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** You use the same hose, filled with the same coloured water, for every test. The two ends are held against the same wall, 3 metres apart, and you wait for the water to stop moving before you mark it. You measure the height of each mark from the floor in the same way. Only one thing changes in each test: a plain end, a funnel on one end, or a bubble in the hose.
- **safety:** Keep water away from electric sockets and switches. Wipe up spills at once, because wet floors are slippery. Do not suck the hose to fill it. Fill it by dipping it in the bucket.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three tests. Then draw the wall, the hose, and the two marks, and write the height of each mark.
- **results table:** Test | Mark 1, height from the floor (cm) | Mark 2, height from the floor (cm) | Difference between the marks (mm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the hose, the funnel and the wall. Ask your child to guess, and to give a reason. Write the guess down. Do not correct it.  
  *Parent says:* We will fill a long hose with water and use it to mark two spots on a wall, 3 metres apart. Will the two marks be at the same height? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A hose full of water. Two marks, 3 metres apart. Same height? Why?
  - Ask: Will the two marks be at the same height from the floor, and why? (good: Yes, because the water in the hose stays level.; No, because the water will run to one end.; Yes, because both ends are open to the air.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a few drops of food colour in the bucket of water. 2. Push the whole hose under the water. 3. Hold it there until no bubbles come out of either end. 4. Pinch both ends with your thumbs and lift the hose out.  
  *Parent says:* Push the whole hose under the coloured water. Wait until no more bubbles come out. Keep both ends shut with your thumbs. Then lift it out.  
  *Child mode:* Push the whole hose under the water. Wait for no more bubbles.
  - Note: Look along the hose against the light. Any bubble you can see will spoil the test. If there is a bubble, put the hose back under the water and try again.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Each person holds one end of the hose upright against the wall, 3 metres apart. 2. Lift or lower the ends until the water stands a few centimetres below the top of the hose at each end. 3. Wait until the water stops moving. 4. Mark the water level on a piece of tape on the wall at each end.  
  *Parent says:* Each of you holds one end against the wall. Move the ends up or down until the water stands near the top of each one. Wait for it to settle. Then mark the wall at the water.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the hose ends against the wall. Wait for the water to settle. Mark it.
  - Note: Keep the ends of the hose upright, and keep the middle low. If the water runs out, lift your ends higher.
  - Wait: The water may bob up and down for a few seconds. Wait until it is still.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure the height of each mark from the floor with the tape measure. 2. Write the numbers in the table. 3. Work out the difference. 4. Ask your child what they notice.  
  *Parent says:* Measure each mark from the floor. Write the heights in the table. Are they the same? How close are they?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each mark from the floor. Are they the same height?
  - Note: A small difference of a few millimetres is normal. If the difference is big, check for a bubble. If the floor is uneven, the marks can be level while their heights from the floor are not the same.
  - Check: The two marks, 3 metres apart, are the same height from the floor. Why? → **The water in the hose settles at the same level at both ends**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fit the funnel on one end of the hose. 2. Pour a little coloured water into the funnel until it is part full. 3. Wait for the water to settle. 4. Compare the water level in the funnel with the level in the thin end, using the ruler.  
  *Parent says:* Put the wide funnel on one end. Pour in a little more water. Is the water higher in the wide funnel or in the thin end? Measure it.  
  *Child mode:* Put the funnel on one end. Compare the two water levels.
  - Ask: Is the water higher in the wide funnel or in the thin end, and why? (good: The same, because only the depth matters.; Higher in the wide funnel, because there is more water.; The same, because water finds its own level.)
  - Note: Many people guess that the wide funnel pushes harder because it holds more water. The levels will be nearly the same, within a few millimetres.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let a bubble of air about 5 centimetres long into the middle of the hose. 2. Repeat the marking at both ends. 3. Measure the height of each mark and write the difference in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now we let a bubble into the hose, in the middle. Mark the wall again at both ends. Are the two marks still at the same height?  
  *Child mode:* Let a bubble into the hose. Mark again. Are the marks still level?
  - Note: The marks will be different, sometimes by several centimetres. The bubble is squashed, and it holds back the push of the water. That is why masons check their hoses for bubbles.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: water in joined containers settles at the same level, whatever their width. What I saw: the two marks were within a few millimetres, the funnel made no difference, and the bubble spoiled it. Why: water at the same depth has the same push, so if one side were higher it would be pushed down until the push was equal.
  - Think: How could a mason use this hose to check that the tops of two walls in a house are at the same height?

### Pressure and Depth (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) What does the word pressure mean?  
   Options: How hard something pushes on each small part of a surface / How heavy something is / How hot something is  
   **Answer:** How hard something pushes on each small part of a surface. *Why:* Pressure is the push on each small bit of a surface. The same weight on a smaller area makes a bigger pressure. Picking 'how heavy' leaves out the area that the push is spread over.
2. (mcq) A swimmer swims from 1 metre deep to 3 metres deep. What happens to the push of the water on her?  
   Options: It gets bigger / It gets smaller / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It gets bigger. *Why:* There is more water above her at 3 metres, so the push is bigger. The deeper you go, the bigger the push.
3. (mcq) Where does the push of water at a depth come from?  
   Options: The weight of the water above it / The colour of the water / The wind on the surface  
   **Answer:** The weight of the water above it. *Why:* Gravity pulls on the water above a point, and the water below has to hold it up. That is the push. Colour and wind do not change it.
4. (mcq) A clear hose full of water has its two ends held against a wall, 3 metres apart. What do you expect about the water in the two ends?  
   Options: It stands at the same height in both ends / It stands higher in the end held lower / It stands higher in the end that is wider  
   **Answer:** It stands at the same height in both ends. *Why:* Water that can flow freely settles to the same level everywhere it is joined. If one end were higher, the push would move water until they were equal.
5. (mcq) Why is a dam wall thicker at the bottom than at the top?  
   Options: The push of the water is biggest at the bottom / It is easier to build that way / There is less water at the bottom  
   **Answer:** The push of the water is biggest at the bottom. *Why:* The push grows with depth, so the bottom of the wall feels the biggest push and must be the strongest. There is more water above the bottom, not less.
6. (mcq) Two syringes are joined by a tube of water. Why must there be no air in the tube?  
   Options: Air squashes and soaks up some of the push / Air makes the water heavy / Air makes the syringes hot  
   **Answer:** Air squashes and soaks up some of the push. *Why:* Water cannot be squeezed, so it passes the push along. A bubble of air gets squashed, and the far plunger moves less.
7. (truefalse) Water pushes only downwards.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Water pushes on every surface it touches, sideways on a wall, upwards under a boat and downwards on a bottom.
8. (truefalse) A tall thin tube of water and a wide tank of water, both 2 metres deep, push equally on each bit of the bottom.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The push at a depth depends only on how deep it is. The tank pushes on a bigger area, but each bit feels the same push.
9. (fill) How far down something is below the top of the water is called its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['depth']. *Why:* Depth is the distance below the surface. The push of water grows with it.
10. (fill) Water that is free to flow in joined containers settles at the same ____ in all of them.  
   **Answer:** ['level']. *Why:* The level is the height of the water's surface. In joined containers, the water settles to the same level.
11. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Pressure → the push on each small part of a surface; Depth → how far down something is below the top of the water; Piston → a plunger that fits tightly in a tube; Dam → a wall across a river to hold water back. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit.
12. (sort) Is the push of water bigger or smaller at each place, compared with a place 1 metre deep in a pond?  
   **Answer:** the bottom of a deep well → Bigger push; an ankle-deep puddle → Smaller push; a fish just under the surface → Smaller push; a diver 20 metres down in the sea → Bigger push; the bottom of a tall water tank → Bigger push; a splash in a toddler's bath → Smaller push. *Why:* The push grows with depth. Places deeper than 1 metre have a bigger push, and shallower places have a smaller push.
13. (order) A bottle has three holes at 4, 10 and 16 centimetres above its base. Put the jets in order from the weakest to the strongest.  
   **Answer:** ['The hole at 16 centimetres', 'The hole at 10 centimetres', 'The hole at 4 centimetres']. *Why:* The hole nearest the top has the least water above it, so it has the weakest jet. The lowest hole has the most water above it, so it has the strongest.
14. (write) A friend says water only pushes downwards. Describe one way to show her that water pushes sideways too.  
   **Answer:** Make holes in the side of a bottle and fill it with water. The water shoots out sideways, so the water pushes on the side wall.. *Why:* A strong answer names a test, such as holes in the side of a bottle, and says what it shows.
15. (write) A roof tank is nearly empty, and the tap just below it runs slowly. Explain why in your own words.  
   **Answer:** The push at the tap comes from the depth of the water above it. When the tank is nearly empty, there is little depth of water above the tap, so the push is small and the water runs slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer says the push depends on the depth of the water above the tap, and that the tank is shallow when nearly empty.
16. (draw) Draw a tall tank of water, seen from the side. Draw arrows to show the push on the side wall at three depths.  
   **Answer:** A tank with three arrows pointing outwards at the wall. The arrow near the top is short and the arrow near the bottom is the longest.. *Why:* A strong drawing has longer arrows at the greater depths, because the push grows with depth.
17. (truefalse) In a bottle with holes at different heights, the lowest hole gives the strongest jet because there is more water above it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The push grows with depth, and the lowest hole has the most water above it.

   **Answer:** The same pressure goes everywhere in the liquid, so a big piston gets a big force. But the big piston moves much less than the small one, so you give up distance.. *Why:* A strong answer says the pressure is the same, the force grows with the area, and the distance moved shrinks.
12. (write) Why must a submarine that dives very deep have very thick walls?  
   **Answer:** The push of the water grows with depth, and at great depths it is huge. The walls must be strong enough not to be crushed.. *Why:* A strong answer links depth to a bigger push, and says the hull must be strong.
13. (write) A friend says a siphon works because the air 'sucks' the water along. What would you tell her, and what do scientists still argue about?  
   **Answer:** The water is pushed by the air outside and pulled by gravity on the longer side. Scientists still argue about how much comes from the air pressure, and how much from the water pulling itself along.. *Why:* A strong answer says sucking is not a push, and mentions the argument between the air-pressure view and the chain view.
14. (draw) Draw a bottle with three jets from three holes. Show which jet is longest, and label which hole has the deepest water above it.  
   **Answer:** A bottle with three arcs. The jet from the lowest hole is longest. The lowest hole is labelled as having the deepest water above it.. *Why:* A strong drawing has the lowest hole giving the longest jet, and labels it as having the deepest water.
15. (draw) Draw two joined syringes with a small piston and a big piston. Show the push on each with arrows.  
   **Answer:** A small syringe and a big syringe joined by a tube. A short arrow on the small piston and a long arrow on the big piston.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a longer arrow on the big piston, because it gets a bigger force.
16. (perspective) Meena and Joseph disagree about a tall flat building where the top-floor tap is weak.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and gives a reason about pressure. For Meena, the push comes from the depth above the tap. For Joseph, a pump adds a push that does not depend on the tank. It also says that the other idea would work too.. *Why:* Both work, because both raise the push at the tap. A higher tank uses depth of water, and a pump adds its own push. ## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does the push of water at a point depend on?  
   Options: How deep the point is / How wide the container is / How much water is in the container  
   **Answer:** How deep the point is. *Why:* The push comes from the water straight above the point, so it depends on the depth only. The width and the amount of water do not change it.
2. (mcq) A boy dives deeper and deeper in a pool, and his ears start to hurt. Why?  
   Options: The push of the water on his ears gets bigger with depth / The water gets colder / The water gets lighter  
   **Answer:** The push of the water on his ears gets bigger with depth. *Why:* There is more water above him at greater depth, so it pushes harder on his ears.
3. (mcq) The water in the spout of a kettle and the water in its body stand at which heights?  
   Options: The same height / Higher in the spout / Higher in the body  
   **Answer:** The same height. *Why:* Water in joined containers settles at the same level, whatever their shapes.
4. (mcq) A closed pipe is full of liquid. You push on a piston at one end. What happens?  
   Options: The push goes to every part of the liquid / The push stays at the piston / The liquid disappears  
   **Answer:** The push goes to every part of the liquid. *Why:* A push on a trapped liquid is passed on equally to every part of it. This is why hydraulic machines work.
5. (mcq) Why is the hull of a deep-diving submarine very thick?  
   Options: The push of the water is huge at great depth / To make it heavy / To make it shine  
   **Answer:** The push of the water is huge at great depth. *Why:* The deeper the submarine goes, the bigger the push on the hull. The hull must be strong not to be crushed.
6. (mcq) A bubble of air in a hydraulic pipe makes the machine move in a spongy way. Why?  
   Options: Air is squashed and takes up some of the push / Air is hot / Air is heavy  
   **Answer:** Air is squashed and takes up some of the push. *Why:* Air is easy to squeeze, so a bubble absorbs some of the push. Liquids pass on a push because they are hard to squeeze.
7. (truefalse) A fish just under the surface feels the same push as a fish at the bottom of a deep lake.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The fish at the bottom has far more water above her, so she feels a much bigger push.
8. (mcq) Which of these will make a roof tank give a stronger push to the taps below?  
   Options: Making the water deeper above the taps / Painting the tank white / Making the pipe longer  
   **Answer:** Making the water deeper above the taps. *Why:* The push at a tap comes from the depth of the water above it. Colour does nothing, and a longer pipe usually slows the flow.
9. (mcq) When you drink through a straw, what pushes the drink up the straw?  
   Options: The air pressure on the drink in the glass / Your mouth, which pulls like a rope / The colour of the straw  
   **Answer:** The air pressure on the drink in the glass. *Why:* Drawing in your breath lowers the pressure in the straw. The air pushing on the drink in the glass is what drives it up.
10. (truefalse) A dam wall is built the same thickness all the way down.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The push grows with depth, so the wall is thickest at the bottom.
11. (mcq) Which pushes harder on the bottom of a jar at the same depth, honey or coconut oil?  
   Options: Honey, because it is heavier for its size / Coconut oil, because it is thicker / They push equally  
   **Answer:** Honey, because it is heavier for its size. *Why:* Pressure follows the weight of the liquid. Honey is denser than water, and coconut oil is lighter than water.
12. (mcq) A big piston has 10 times the area of a small piston, and the two are joined by a liquid. What do you gain and what do you lose?  
   Options: Ten times the force, but the big piston moves one tenth as far / Ten times the force and ten times the distance / The same force but more distance  
   **Answer:** Ten times the force, but the big piston moves one tenth as far. *Why:* The same pressure on a bigger area gives a bigger force. But the same amount of liquid moves the big piston a shorter way.

## Applications
- Overhead tanks on roofs send water down to taps by the push of their own water, and the higher the tank, the stronger the push.
- Hydraulic brakes in cars, autos and lorries pass a foot's push through a liquid to the wheels.
- Excavators such as JCBs lift tonnes of soil with liquid pushed along tubes to pistons.
- A barber's chair, a car jack and a garage lift all use a hydraulic piston to lift a load with a small push.
- Dams are built thickest at the bottom, where the push of the water is the biggest.
- Masons use a clear hose of water to mark a level line on a long wall.
- Water towers in towns are built high, so that taps far away still get a good push of water.
- Submarines and deep-sea research vehicles have thick, strong hulls to survive the huge push of the sea at depth.
- Blood pressure cuffs measure the push of blood in the arteries, which doctors use to check on health.
- Diving tables tell divers how long they can stay at each depth, because the push of the water changes the gases in their blood.
- Hydroelectric power stations use the push of deep water behind a dam to spin turbines.
- Drip irrigation tanks raised on a stand give a steady push to water in tubes across a field.

## Open questions
- **How does a siphon work: by air pressure, or by the water pulling itself along?** Experiments in recent years have reported siphons running in a vacuum. Scientists still argue about how much comes from each cause.
- **Why does water flow smoothly in some places and become a swirling, chaotic mess in others?** The way a fluid turns from smooth to turbulent is still not fully explained, and it is one of the big unsolved problems in physics.
- **How do deep-sea animals keep their bodies working under a push more than a thousand times that of the air?** Scientists have found that some deep-sea fish use special chemicals in their cells, but there is a lot more to learn about how they cope.
- **Is there liquid water in oceans under the ice of other worlds, and what is the pressure like there?** Some moons of Jupiter and Saturn seem to have oceans under their ice. Space missions are being planned to find out more.
- **Is the pressure at the very centre of the Earth really millions of times the surface air pressure?** Scientists work out the pressure from the weight of the rock above and from how earthquake waves travel. They have no way to measure it directly.
- **How did people thousands of years ago know how to build dams and step wells without knowing the rule of pressure?** Few written records tell us how the builders learned. Historians think they learned from many trials and failures.

## Connected maths
- The top jet landed 37 centimetres away, the middle jet 46 centimetres and the bottom jet 52 centimetres. How much farther did the bottom jet go than the top jet? How much farther than the middle jet? **15 centimetres farther than the top jet, and 6 centimetres farther than the middle jet.**. 52 take away 37 is 15. 52 take away 46 is 6. The bottom jet beat the top jet by 15 centimetres, and the middle jet by 6 centimetres. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- The bottom hole was 18 centimetres below the water line and the top hole was 6 centimetres below. How many times as deep is the bottom hole? The bottom jet landed 52 centimetres away and the top jet 37 centimetres away. Is the bottom jet 3 times as far? Why might that be? **3 times as deep. The bottom jet is only about 1.4 times as far.**. 18 divided by 6 is 3, so the bottom hole is 3 times as deep. 52 divided by 37 is about 1.4. The jet does not go 3 times as far. Three times the depth gives a faster jet, but the speed grows more slowly than the depth, and the jet also has to fall to the floor. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Three tries for the bottom hole landed at 50, 54 and 52 centimetres. What is the middle number? Why use the middle number and not the biggest? **52 centimetres. The biggest might be a fluke.**. Put the numbers in order: 50, 52, 54. The middle one is 52. One try can be a fluke, so the middle number is fairer than the biggest or smallest. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- The first mark was 101.2 centimetres above the floor and the second mark was 101.5 centimetres above the floor, 3 metres away. What is the difference in centimetres and in millimetres? How many millimetres is that for each metre of the wall? **0.3 centimetres, which is 3 millimetres. That is 1 millimetre for each metre.**. 101.5 take away 101.2 is 0.3 centimetres. There are 10 millimetres in a centimetre, so 0.3 centimetres is 3 millimetres. The wall is 3 metres long, and 3 divided by 3 is 1 millimetre in each metre. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)

How many litres in a minute does each give? **4 times as fast. The kitchen tap gives 5 litres a minute and the shower 1.25 litres a minute.**. 48 divided by 12 is 4. A minute has 60 seconds. 60 divided by 12 is 5 litres a minute. 60 divided by 48 is 1.25 litres a minute. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A small syringe has a piston with an area of 1 square centimetre. A bigger one has an area of 4 square centimetres. They are joined by water. You push the small piston with a force of 3 newtons. What force does the big piston get? If the small piston moves 8 centimetres, how far does the big one move? **12 newtons. The big piston moves 2 centimetres.**. The pressure is the same in both. Four times the area gives four times the force, so 3 times 4 is 12 newtons. The same amount of water moves a piston with four times the area one quarter as far, so 8 divided by 4 is 2 centimetres. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- A roof tank is 2 metres long, 1.5 metres wide and 1 metre deep. How many cubic metres does it hold? One cubic metre holds 1,000 litres. How many litres is that? How many litres are left? **3 cubic metres, which is 3,000 litres. 750 litres are left.**. 2 times 1.5 is 3, and 3 times 1 is 3 cubic metres. 3 times 1,000 is 3,000 litres. Three quarters of 3,000 is 2,250, so 3,000 take away 2,250 leaves 750 litres. Only a quarter is left, so the water is shallow. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Every 10 metres of water adds about one unit of push, and the air adds one unit. At 10 metres, the total push is 2 units. What is the total at 30 metres? How many times as deep is 30 metres as 10 metres? How many times as big is the total push? **4 units at 30 metres. 3 times as deep, but only 2 times the total push.**. At 30 metres the water adds 3 units, and the air adds 1, so the total is 4. 30 divided by 10 is 3 times as deep. 4 divided by 2 is 2 times the total. The air always adds one unit, so the total does not grow as fast as the depth. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)

How thick is the base in the drawing? **6 centimetres tall. 4 centimetres thick at the base.**. 30 divided by 5 is 6 centimetres. 20 divided by 5 is 4 centimetres.
