# Carbon: how one kind of atom builds sugar, fuel, plastic and living things
`sci.chemistry.carbon-chemistry.L4`

**Summary.** White sugar turns black when it is heated on a hot tawa. So do atta, cotton thread and paper, but salt stays white. The black solid is mostly carbon. Carbon is an element, and each carbon atom makes four bonds. Because of this, carbon atoms join into long chains, branches and rings. These chains and rings are the frame of sugar, cooking gas, petrol, plastic and every living body. Your child heats eight kitchen things to find which ones hold carbon chains. They also test three materials built from the same sugar ring: glucose, cornflour and cotton.

**Intro.** *Sugar is white, and a cotton thread is white. Heat either one on a hot tawa and it turns black. Where does the black come from?* Many things go black when they get too hot: a forgotten dosa, a roti left on the flame, milk that boils dry. Salt never does. In this unit you find out what the black solid is and why only some things leave it. You heat eight kitchen things side by side. You build molecules on paper with atoms that make one, two or four bonds. You also test glucose, cornflour and cotton, which are three materials built from one and the same ring of atoms.

## Terms
- **Atom**: The smallest piece of an element. Atoms are far too small to see. *e.g.* A grain of sugar holds many billions of atoms of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
- **Element**: A substance made of only one kind of atom. *e.g.* Carbon, oxygen, iron and gold are elements. Water is not, because it has two kinds of atom.
- **Carbon**: An element whose atoms each make four bonds. Charcoal, soot, pencil graphite and diamond are forms of carbon. *e.g.* The black on a burnt roti is mostly carbon.
- **Bond**: The hold that joins one atom to another atom. *e.g.* In a molecule of cooking gas, bonds hold carbon atoms to hydrogen atoms.
- **Molecule**: A group of atoms joined together by bonds. *e.g.* A molecule of water is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.
- **Carbon chain**: A row of carbon atoms, each bonded to the next. *e.g.* A molecule in petrol has a chain of about 8 carbon atoms. A molecule in candle wax has about 25.
- **Ring**: A chain of atoms whose two ends are joined, so that the atoms form a closed loop. *e.g.* A molecule of glucose is a ring of five carbon atoms and one oxygen atom.
- **Carbon skeleton**: The pattern in which the carbon atoms of a molecule are joined: straight, branched or in a ring. *e.g.* Five carbon atoms can be joined in a straight row, in a row with one branch, or as a cross.
- **Hydrocarbon**: A substance whose molecules are made of only carbon and hydrogen atoms. *e.g.* Cooking gas, petrol, diesel and candle wax are hydrocarbons.
- **Charring**: Turning black from heat. Heat drives the other atoms out as gases, and a solid that is mostly carbon stays behind. *e.g.* The bottom of a dosa chars when the tawa is too hot.
- **Charcoal**: The black solid left when wood is heated with very little air. It is mostly carbon. *e.g.* The ironing man's coal iron and many roadside corn stalls burn charcoal.
- **Glucose**: A simple sugar. Each molecule is one small ring with 6 carbon atoms in all. *e.g.* Glucose powder stirred into water is glucose. Your blood carries glucose to every cell.
- **Starch**: A food store made by plants. Each molecule is a long chain of glucose rings. *e.g.* Rice, potato, cornflour and atta are mostly starch.
- **Cellulose**: The material of plant cell walls. Each molecule is a long, straight chain of glucose rings. *e.g.* Cotton, paper and the hard part of wood are mostly cellulose.
- **Organic chemistry**: The study of substances built on chains and rings of carbon atoms. *e.g.* A chemist who designs a new medicine or a new plastic is doing organic chemistry.

## Explanations
### Carbon is an element · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbon is an element. Every carbon atom is the same kind of atom. Heating or mixing cannot break carbon into anything simpler.

Everything is made of atoms, and there are about a hundred kinds. A substance made of only one kind of atom is called an element. Carbon is one of the elements. You have already met carbon in several forms. Charcoal and the soot from a lamp are mostly carbon. The grey core of a pencil is graphite, which is carbon atoms joined in flat sheets. A diamond is carbon atoms joined in a network where every atom holds four others. Graphite is soft and diamond is very hard, yet both are pure carbon. The atoms are the same, and only the way they are joined is different. Carbon atoms also sit inside many substances that are not black at all. Sugar, cotton, oil, wax and plastic all hold carbon joined to other atoms. About 18 kilograms of every 100 kilograms of a human body is carbon. Only oxygen makes up more of you.
- e.g. Charcoal, soot, pencil graphite and diamond are all forms of carbon.
- e.g. White sugar holds carbon atoms joined to hydrogen and oxygen atoms.
- e.g. A child who weighs 30 kilograms holds more than 5 kilograms of carbon.
### Each carbon atom makes four bonds · *defined*
**Definition:** A bond is the hold between two atoms. A carbon atom makes four bonds, a hydrogen atom makes one, and an oxygen atom makes two.

Atoms join to each other by bonds. Each kind of atom makes a set number of bonds. A hydrogen atom makes one bond. An oxygen atom makes two, and a nitrogen atom makes three. A carbon atom makes four. In the activity One Bond or Four, you draw molecules with these rules. Two hydrogen atoms join, and then neither has a bond to spare, so the molecule ends as a pair. A carbon atom is different. One carbon atom can hold four hydrogen atoms. This molecule is called methane, and it is the main gas in piped cooking gas. A carbon atom can also use one bond to hold a second carbon atom and still have three bonds free. The second carbon atom can hold a third, and so on. There is a second reason too. The bond between two carbon atoms is strong, so a long row of carbon atoms does not fall apart easily. Rows of oxygen atoms do fall apart, which is why long oxygen chains are not found.
- e.g. Methane is one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms.
- e.g. Water is one oxygen atom bonded to two hydrogen atoms.
- e.g. In the gas propane, three carbon atoms in a row hold eight hydrogen atoms.
### Chains, branches and rings · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbon atoms can join in a straight chain, in a chain with branches, or in a closed ring. The pattern is called the carbon skeleton.

A carbon atom in the middle of a chain uses two bonds to hold its two neighbours. It still has two bonds left. Each of those can hold a hydrogen atom, or it can hold another carbon atom and start a branch. The two ends of a chain can also bond to each other and close into a ring. So carbon atoms can be arranged in a huge number of ways. In the activity Five Carbons, you find that five carbon atoms can be joined in three different skeletons. Each one is a real liquid with its own boiling point: 36, 28 and 10 degrees Celsius. All three have exactly the same atoms, 5 carbon and 12 hydrogen. Substances like these, with the same atoms joined in different patterns, are called isomers. Ten carbon atoms can make 75 different skeletons. Chemists have recorded more than 100 million different substances, and most of them are built on carbon chains or rings.
- e.g. Butane, a gas in the LPG cylinder, is a straight chain of four carbon atoms.
- e.g. Five carbon atoms make three different skeletons: a row, a row with one branch, and a cross.
- e.g. A glucose molecule holds a ring. So does the molecule that gives camphor its smell.
### Why some things turn black when they are heated · *defined*
**Definition:** Charring is turning black from heat. Heat drives hydrogen and oxygen out of a carbon-chain substance as gases. A black solid that is mostly carbon stays behind.

In the experiment The Black Inside, you heat eight things on a tawa. Sugar, atta, cotton thread, paper and dry onion skin turn black. Salt, baking soda and a steel coin do not. The five that turn black were all made by plants. Their molecules are chains and rings of carbon atoms with hydrogen and oxygen atoms attached. Strong heat breaks these molecules. The hydrogen and oxygen leave as water vapour and other gases, which is some of the smoke you see. Many carbon atoms stay behind, bonded to each other, as a black solid. This is called charring. Salt holds no carbon, so it has nothing black to leave. Baking soda is a puzzle, because it does hold carbon. But each of its carbon atoms sits alone, held by oxygen atoms. When baking soda is heated, that carbon leaves as carbon dioxide gas. So a black solid is a sign of carbon atoms that were joined in chains or rings.
- e.g. Milk that boils dry leaves a black crust on the pan.
- e.g. A roti held on the flame too long gets black spots.
- e.g. Salt heated on a tawa stays white, however long you wait.
### Fuels are carbon chains, and the length of the chain matters · *defined*
**Definition:** A hydrocarbon is a substance made only of carbon and hydrogen. Cooking gas, petrol, diesel and wax are hydrocarbons with chains of different lengths.

The gas in a kitchen pipe is mostly methane, with 1 carbon atom in each molecule. The LPG in a cylinder is propane and butane, with 3 and 4 carbon atoms. Petrol molecules have about 5 to 12. Kerosene has about 10 to 16, and diesel is a little longer. Candle wax has about 20 to 40. Short chains hold on to each other weakly, so the substance is a gas. Longer chains hold on to each other along their whole length. So the substance is a liquid, then a thick liquid, then a solid. You sort these in the game Chain Line. All these fuels come from petroleum and natural gas. Those formed from tiny sea plants and other sea creatures, buried for millions of years. Coal formed from buried swamp forests. When a hydrocarbon burns, its carbon joins with oxygen and becomes carbon dioxide, and its hydrogen becomes water. The carbon dioxide stays in the air and traps heat. That is why burning fuels is warming the Earth.
- e.g. Methane, with 1 carbon atom, boils at 162 degrees below zero.
- e.g. Octane in petrol, with 8 carbon atoms, is a liquid that boils at 126 degrees Celsius.
- e.g. Candle wax, with about 25 carbon atoms in a chain, is a solid in the room.
### Sugars are rings, and rings join into chains · *defined*
**Definition:** Glucose is a sugar whose molecule is one small ring. Starch and cellulose are long chains made of many glucose rings joined together.

A glucose molecule has 6 carbon atoms, 12 hydrogen atoms and 6 oxygen atoms. Five of the carbon atoms and one oxygen atom form a ring. Plants make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, using light. Then they join the rings together. Each time two rings join, one molecule of water is released. Rings joined one way make starch. A starch chain coils, and the plant packs the coils into tiny grains as a food store. Rings joined the other way make cellulose, where every second ring is turned over. A cellulose chain is straight, and straight chains lie side by side and hold each other tightly. That makes strong fibres, such as cotton. In the experiment One Ring, Three Materials, you test all three. Glucose dissolves and tastes sweet. Cornflour, which is starch, clouds cold water and thickens hot water. Cotton does neither. Table sugar is two rings joined: one glucose and one ring of a sugar called fructose.
- e.g. Glucose powder is single rings. It dissolves at once and tastes sweet.
- e.g. Rice and potato are mostly starch, a coiled chain of glucose rings.
- e.g. A cotton shirt is mostly cellulose, a straight chain of thousands of glucose rings.
### Plastics are very long carbon chains made by people · *defined*
**Definition:** A plastic is a material made of very long molecules. In most plastics, the frame of each molecule is a chain of carbon atoms.

The plastic of a milk packet is called polythene. Factories make it from a gas called ethene, which has 2 carbon atoms in each molecule. The ethene comes from petroleum or natural gas. In the factory, thousands of ethene molecules are joined end to end. The result is a chain with many thousands of carbon atoms. A polythene chain is built like candle wax, but it is hundreds of times longer. Long chains tangle and hold each other, so the plastic is tough and bends without breaking. Polyester, nylon and the plastic of water bottles also have carbon frames. Their chains hold a few oxygen or nitrogen atoms too. Plastics are light, cheap and waterproof. They are also a problem. Soil microbes have enzymes that cut the chains of wood and food. Almost none has an enzyme for a chain that people invented less than a hundred years ago. So plastic stays in the soil and the sea for a very long time.
- e.g. A milk packet and a carry bag are polythene.
- e.g. A water bottle is a plastic called PET. A polyester sari is the same plastic spun into thread.
- e.g. A nylon rope is a carbon chain with nitrogen atoms in it.
### Living bodies are built on carbon chains and rings · *defined*
**Definition:** The main substances of every living body are carbon-chain molecules: sugars, starch and cellulose, fats, proteins and DNA.

A fat molecule has three carbon chains, each usually 12 to 18 carbon atoms long. A protein is a long chain built from small carbon units called amino acids. Muscles, hair, silk and enzymes are proteins. DNA carries the instructions for building a body, and its long frame holds a carbon ring in every step. So a mango tree, a crow, a mushroom and a child are all built on carbon. Plants take their carbon from the air. A leaf takes in carbon dioxide and joins the carbon into glucose. Animals, including humans, cannot do this. We get our carbon by eating plants, or by eating other animals who ate plants. We give carbon back with every breath, as carbon dioxide. A tree may hold a carbon atom for a hundred years. Then the wood rots or burns, and the atom returns to the air. You follow one atom on such a journey in the game Carbon Trail.
- e.g. Coconut oil and ghee are fats, with three carbon chains in each molecule.
- e.g. The protein in dal and in egg is a chain of amino acids.
- e.g. A banyan tree built their trunk from carbon dioxide taken out of the air.
### Carbon atoms were made inside stars · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbon atoms are made in the hot centres of old stars. There, three helium atoms are pressed together into one carbon atom.

The first stars were made of hydrogen and helium, the two lightest elements. There was no carbon. Deep inside a star, hydrogen atoms are pressed together into helium, and this gives the star its light. When an old star runs low on hydrogen, the centre grows even hotter. Then three helium atoms can join into one carbon atom. This step is hard, because all three must meet at almost the same moment. In 1953 the astronomer Fred Hoyle worked out that the step needs carbon to have a special property. A team in California tested carbon and found the property he had predicted. When old stars die, they throw their carbon out into space. New stars and planets form from that dust and gas. In 1957 Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle showed how stars make nearly all the elements. So the carbon in sugar, in a pencil and in you is older than the Sun. It was made in stars that died before the Sun was born.
- e.g. The Sun is turning hydrogen into helium now. It will make carbon near the end of its life.
- e.g. Dust clouds in space hold carbon thrown out by old stars.
- e.g. In 2010, astronomers found balls of 60 carbon atoms in the gas around a dying star.
### Could living things be built on another element? · *debated*
Every living being found so far is built on carbon. Scientists have asked whether this must be so. Silicon is the usual other choice, because a silicon atom also makes four bonds. Sand and most rocks are silicon joined with oxygen. Nobody has found life on another world yet, so the question is open. Here are the main views.
- e.g. Silicon sits just below carbon in the table of elements, and makes four bonds.
- e.g. Sand is silicon bonded to oxygen in a hard network.
- e.g. Space telescopes find many carbon molecules in gas clouds, and few silicon chains.
- **Most chemists:** Carbon is the best builder by far. Bonds between silicon atoms are weaker, so long silicon chains break apart in water and in air. When carbon burns it makes a gas, which a body can breathe out. When silicon burns it makes a solid like sand.
- **Some astrobiologists, who study the chances of life on other worlds:** We know only one example of life, the life on Earth. In very cold places with no water, other chemistry might work. We should not rule out what we have not looked for.
- **Chemists who work with silicon:** People already make silicon chains with oxygen between the silicon atoms. These are the silicones in bathroom sealant and baking moulds. They are stable, but they form far fewer shapes than carbon does.

## Mostly agreed
- Carbon is an element, and charcoal, graphite and diamond are all forms of carbon. *How we know:* In 1797 Smithson Tennant burned a diamond in a closed vessel. It made the same amount of the same gas as an equal weight of charcoal.
- A carbon atom makes four bonds, and carbon atoms bond to each other in chains and rings. *How we know:* August Kekulé and Archibald Couper each proposed this in 1858. X-ray pictures of crystals later showed the atoms in exactly these positions.
- The substances of living things can be made from substances that were never alive. *How we know:* In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a substance found in urine, from a mineral salt. Chemists now make sugars, vitamins and medicines in the same way.
- Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose rings. *How we know:* When either one is broken down completely with acid, the only sugar that comes out is glucose.
- Petroleum, natural gas and coal formed from living things buried long ago. *How we know:* Coal holds the prints of leaves and bark. Oil holds molecules that are only made by living cells.
- Carbon atoms are made in stars. *How we know:* The light of old stars shows carbon in their outer layers. The carbon property that Fred Hoyle predicted in 1953 was found in a laboratory the same year.

## How we found out
**Friedrich Wöhler and Hermann Kolbe in Germany, August Kekulé, Archibald Couper, and Kathleen Lonsdale in England, Wöhler made urea in 1828. Kolbe made the acid of vinegar in 1845. Kekulé and Couper published in 1858, and Kekulé proposed the ring in 1865. Lonsdale's X-ray work was published in 1929..** Two hundred years ago, chemists sorted substances into two kinds. Mineral substances, like salt, came from rocks. Organic substances, like sugar and urea, came from living bodies. Most chemists thought that only a living body could make an organic substance, with the help of a life force. In 1828 the German chemist Friedrich Wöhler heated a mineral salt called ammonium cyanate. White crystals formed. He tested them and found they were urea, the same substance as in urine. He wrote to his teacher that he had made urea without needing a kidney. One result did not change every mind, but more followed. In 1845 Hermann Kolbe made the acid of vinegar starting from carbon. Then chemists asked why carbon made so many substances. In 1858 August Kekulé in Germany and Archibald Couper from Scotland each gave the same answer. A carbon atom makes four bonds, and carbon atoms bond to each other in chains. In 1865 Kekulé proposed that six carbon atoms could also close into a ring. In 1929 Kathleen Lonsdale, working in Leeds, used X-rays to show that this ring is real, flat and six-sided.  
*What people believed before:* That the substances of living things could be made only inside a living body, by a life force. Wöhler made urea from a mineral salt, and Kolbe made the acid of vinegar from carbon. So these substances follow the same chemistry as salt and rock.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The substances of living things can be made only by living things, because they need a life force.** (Most chemists before 1828, including the great Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius.; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody had ever made sugar, fat or urea in a laboratory. Substances from living things also burned and charred, while minerals did not.  
  Against: In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea from a mineral salt. In 1845 Hermann Kolbe made the acid of vinegar starting from carbon itself. No life force was needed.
- **Organic means natural and free of chemicals.** (Food labels, shops and everyday speech.; *debated*)  
  For: On a food label, organic has a real meaning. It means the crop was grown without factory-made pesticides and fertilisers.  
  Against: In chemistry, organic means built on carbon chains. Petrol and plastic are organic in this sense. Everything is made of chemicals, including water, mangoes and you. The word has two meanings.
- **Coal and oil come from dead dinosaurs.** (A common belief, helped by cartoons and toy packets.; *rejected*)  
  For: Coal and oil are called fossil fuels, and dinosaurs are the best-known fossils.  
  Against: Most coal formed from swamp forests about 300 million years ago, before the first dinosaurs. Oil formed mostly from tiny sea plants and other sea creatures that sank into mud.
- **Plastic has nothing in common with wood, cotton or food.** (A common belief.; *debated*)  
  For: Plastic is made in factories from petroleum. Plastic does not rot, and no person can live by eating plastic.  
  Against: Plastic, wood, cotton and food are all built on carbon chains, and the carbon in petroleum came from living things. The real difference is that few microbes have an enzyme that cuts plastic chains. In 2016 scientists in Japan found a bacterium who eats one kind of plastic.
- **Somewhere, life could be built on silicon in place of carbon.** (Some astrobiologists and many writers of science fiction.; *minority*)  
  For: A silicon atom makes four bonds, like a carbon atom. There is far more silicon than carbon in the rocks of the Earth.  
  Against: Silicon chains break easily and form far fewer shapes. Even on the Earth, with all that silicon, life chose carbon. No silicon life has been found.

## Roots
In this unit you found carbon inside sugar, cotton, paper, wood, fuel and plastic. Here we ask what it means that one kind of atom is the frame of so many things, including you.

**Deep truth.** In The Black Inside, sugar, atta, cotton thread and paper each left a black solid when heated. That black solid is mostly carbon. Each carbon atom makes four bonds, so carbon atoms join into long chains and rings. That is why one element can be the frame of food, cloth, fuel and every living body.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat food, cloth, fuel, plastic and living bodies as different kinds of matter. They share one frame, which is carbon atoms joined in chains and rings. What differs is the pattern of the joins.  
*Evidence:* In The Black Inside, five different things made by plants all left carbon, and salt left none. In Five Carbons, the same five atoms made three different skeletons. In One Ring, Three Materials, one ring made a sweet powder, a thickener and a fibre.

- *who am i:* About 18 of every 100 kilograms of your body is carbon. Each of those atoms was in your food, before that in the air, and long before that inside a star.
- *what is life:* Every living being we know builds their body on chains and rings of carbon. Plants take carbon from the air. Animals, including us, take it from plants and give it back with every breath.
- *what is this world:* The same carbon atom can be air, then a leaf, then a child, then air again. Sugar, wood, petrol and plastic are that one atom joined in different patterns.

A grain of sugar, a cotton thread and a milk packet look like three unrelated things. Heat them and each leaves the same element behind. Their carbon atoms were made in stars, drifted through space and became part of the Earth. Plants take those atoms from the air and build them into chains. Every animal borrows the chains for a while. Many sages taught that the many things of the world are forms of one. The science here says something plain and close to that. A very large part of what you see is one kind of atom, joined in different patterns.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that by knowing one lump of clay, everything made of clay is known. The pot and the plate are names for shapes, and the clay is what is there. (One element, carbon, is shaped into sugar, wood and fuel. The text is speaking of one reality behind all things, and it is not speaking of an element. The likeness is in the way of looking: ask what a thing is made of, beneath its name.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), chapter 1:** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you will see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. (In The Black Inside, paper left carbon. That carbon was taken from the air by a tree, with water that fell as rain. The teaching and the chemistry trace the same path.)
- **Primo Levi, The Periodic Table (1975), last chapter, 'Carbon':** Levi, a chemist, follows one carbon atom for hundreds of years. It leaves a rock and enters a leaf, a glass of wine and a man's body. It returns to the air many times. (This is the journey of the game Carbon Trail, told as a story. Levi says plainly that he made up the details. But every step he describes is one that carbon atoms really take.)
- Science (settled): Carbon atoms are made inside old stars and thrown into space when the stars die. The carbon in every living body on Earth came from such stars.
- Science (settled): All life found so far is built on carbon chains, and all of it shares one family tree.
- Science (open): Whether life must be built on carbon, or could be built on something else, is not known.
- Other view, Many chemists: Sharing an element says very little. A diamond, a plastic bag and a child all hold carbon, yet they have almost nothing else in common. What makes each thing what it is lies in the exact pattern of its atoms. On this view the differences are as real as the sameness, and matter more.
- Practice, One breath of carbon (3 min): Sit still and hold one hand in front of your mouth. Breathe out slowly and feel the warm air. That breath carries carbon atoms that were part of your food. Now look at a plant, or at a tree outside the window. Their leaves are taking carbon from the air right now. Breathe out five more times, slowly. Each time, think of where one of those atoms might go next.
- Ask: In The Black Inside, white sugar left black carbon. Was the sugar hiding the carbon, or is 'black' only how carbon looks when it is alone?
- Ask: A carbon atom was in the air, then in rice, then in you, and will be in the air again. At which moment was it yours?
- Ask: A diamond and the black on a dosa are the same atoms in different patterns. Which matters more for what a thing is: its atoms or its pattern?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A diamond and the black on a burnt roti are the same element. What makes them so different? (hint: Think about the same bricks built into a wall and thrown in a heap.)
- *deep:* Five carbon atoms and twelve hydrogen atoms can make three different liquids. Where is the difference kept? (hint: Nothing was added or taken away. Only the pattern of the joins changed.)
- *big:* A carbon atom in your hand may once have been in a tree, a fish or a dinosaur. Is that atom yours? (hint: You will breathe that atom out one day, and a leaf may take that atom in.)
- *big:* Sugar from a sugarcane and sugar made in a laboratory have the same molecules. Is one more natural than the other? (hint: Could any test tell the two apart? If not, what does 'natural' mean here?)
- *deep:* Plastic is made from carbon that living things gathered millions of years ago. Why can living things not take it back? (hint: Think about a lock and a key. Microbes have keys for old chains. Who has a key for a new one?)
- *big:* Burning petrol puts very old carbon into today's air. Who should decide how much we burn? (hint: The air is shared by people in every country and by other animals and plants. Children not yet born will share it too.)

## Story: The Pot That Came Out Red
*Thenmozhi, Kathir and Paati are made up, and so is this firing. These facts are real. Keeladi is a village near Madurai in Tamil Nadu. Archaeologists have dug up a town there that is more than 2,000 years old. Among the finds are bowls that are black inside and red outside, called black-and-red ware. Manamadurai, further down the Vaigai river, is a potters' town. Most archaeologists think such bowls were fired upside down, with plant matter packed inside. Where air could not reach, carbon from the smoke stayed in the clay. The iron in the clay also turned dark there. Where air could reach, the clay turned red.*

The bowl in the glass case at Keeladi was broken, and Thenmozhi could not stop looking at it.

It was red on the outside, like any pot in her grandmother's yard. But the inside was black, and so was a band around the rim. The black shone. The card beside it said the bowl was more than two thousand years old.

"Who painted the inside?" asked her cousin Kathir, who was six.

"Nobody," said Thenmozhi. "Paint would have washed off in two thousand years." She did not know how the black had got there. She only knew that she wanted to make one.

Paati's house in Manamadurai had a potter's wheel in the front room and a firing pit behind. Paati had made pots for fifty years. She gave Thenmozhi a lump of clay and asked no questions.

Thenmozhi pinched the clay into a small bowl and dried it for four days in the shade. On firing day Paati set it among her own pots. She packed straw and paddy husk around them and lit the heap in the evening. In the morning Thenmozhi lifted her bowl out with two sticks.

It was red. It was red inside and outside, from rim to foot.

"The fire makes clay red," said Paati. "It has always done so."

"Then I will put the black in myself," said Thenmozhi.

She made a second bowl. When it was dry she held a steel plate over the kitchen lamp until soot gathered on it. She rubbed the soot all over the inside of the bowl with her thumb. It went in black as night. Kathir clapped.

After the next firing, the second bowl was as red as the first. Not one grain of soot was left.

"Where did the soot go?" said Kathir. "Did the fire eat it?"

Thenmozhi had no answer. She sat down beside the firing pit with her chin on her knees. The heap had burnt down in the night. On top lay husk that had turned to soft ash, pale grey, almost white. It fell to powder when she touched it.

She pushed a stick into the heap, only to have something to do. The stick went down a hand's depth and turned up something dark.

She dug with both hands. Under the pale ash, where the husk had been packed tight against the ground, the husk was black. Every piece still had the shape of a husk. It was light and dry, and it left a black line across her palm.

She held white ash in one hand and black husk in the other.

"Same husk," she said slowly. "Same fire. Same night."

"The top got more fire," said Kathir.

"The top got more air," said Thenmozhi. She was thinking of the lamp. A lamp in the wind burned clean, but a lamp under a plate left soot. "On top, the black met the air and burnt away. Down here no air could reach it. So the black stayed."

She looked at her two red bowls. Their insides had been open to the air all night. Any black that touched them had burnt away, soot and all.

"Paati," she called. "Which way up did the old potters put their bowls?"

Paati was wedging clay and did not look up. But she was smiling. "Which way would you put them?"

The third bowl took four more days to dry. On firing day Thenmozhi filled it to the brim with paddy husk. She laid a bed of husk in the pit, turned the bowl over on to the bed and pressed down. Now the inside was shut in with the husk, and no air could get to it. The outside stood open to the fire.

"You are baking it upside down," said Kathir. "All the husk will fall out."

"The husk has nowhere to fall," said Thenmozhi. "And the air has no way in."

The fire burnt through the evening. Thenmozhi slept badly.

In the morning she lifted the bowl with the two sticks and turned it over. Black husk spilled out, each piece still husk-shaped.

The outside of the bowl was red. The inside was black, a deep, even black. A black band ran around the rim, exactly as wide as the part that had sat in the husk bed. She rubbed it with a cloth, and it began to shine.

Kathir put his whole hand inside and pulled it out clean. "It does not come off," he said.

"It is in the clay now," said Thenmozhi.

Paati turned the bowl in her hands for a long time. "My grandmother fired her lamp bowls this way," she said. "I could have told you on the first day. But then it would have been my bowl."

At breakfast, Kathir's dosa came off the tawa with one black edge. He held it up and looked at it. Then he looked at the black bowl on the shelf.

"Is it the same black?" he asked.

"Rice grew, and husk grew," said Thenmozhi. "I think the black is inside everything that grew. We only see it when the heat gets in and the air does not."

**For the parent:** The husk on top of the heap had air, and it burnt away to pale ash. The husk underneath had no air, and it stayed black. Things that grew hold carbon. Heat leaves that carbon behind as a black solid wherever air cannot reach it. In air, the carbon burns and goes into the air as a gas.

- Ask: The husk on top and the husk underneath were in the same fire. Why did one turn to pale ash and the other stay black?
- Ask: The soot rubbed inside the second bowl was gone after the firing. Where had the carbon gone?
- Ask: Kathir asked whether the black on his dosa was the same black as in the bowl. What would you tell him, and how could you test it?

### e1 · The Black Inside: which kitchen things turn black when heated? (30 min)
- **question:** Eight small things are heated side by side on a hot tawa for 8 minutes. Which ones turn black, and which ones do not? What do the ones that turn black have in common?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess for each of the eight things: will it turn black, or not? Then say what you think the black ones will have in common. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same small amount of each thing, about as much as one grain of chana. Put all eight on the same tawa, so they get the same heat for the same time. Keep the flame low to medium and do not change it. Look at 3 minutes and again at 8 minutes. Only one thing changes from heap to heap: what the heap is made of.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the heating and stays at the stove. Switch on the exhaust fan or open a window, because the sugar and the paper make smoke. Use only a tiny pinch of each thing. Salt may crackle and jump, so keep faces away. If the paper or thread starts to glow, press it flat with the spoon. Let everything cool for 5 minutes before anyone touches it.
- **record:** Draw a table with eight rows, one for each thing. Write where each thing comes from and its colour after 3 minutes and after 8 minutes. Write whether it leaves a black mark on paper.
- **results table:** The thing | Where it comes from | Colour after 3 minutes | Colour after 8 minutes | Does it leave a black mark on paper?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the eight things on the table. Ask your child to guess for each one, and to give a reason. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* We will heat these eight things on the tawa for 8 minutes. Which ones do you think will turn black? What will the black ones have in common? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the eight things. Which will turn black? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What do you think the black ones will have in common? (good: They are all food.; They all come from plants.; They are all things that can burn.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say that everything burns black. Others say that white things stay white.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover the cold tawa with foil. Help your child put a tiny heap of each thing on the foil. Make two rows of four, with gaps between. Draw a map on paper of which heap is where. Draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* Each heap must be tiny, about as big as one grain of chana. Why should all the heaps be the same size? Now draw a map, because the heaps will soon look different.  
  *Child mode:* Put eight tiny heaps on the foil. Keep gaps between them. Draw a map.
  - Note: Small heaps heat through quickly and make little smoke. Equal heaps keep the test fair. The foil keeps burnt sugar off your tawa.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Switch on the exhaust fan. Put the tawa on a low to medium flame. Your child stands one step back and watches. At 3 minutes, say the colour of each heap and write it down. At 8 minutes, do the same. Then switch off the flame.  
  *Parent says:* Watch each heap and tell me what you see. Which one changed first? Is anything rising from it? I will write the colours at 3 minutes and at 8 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Stand back and watch. Say what each heap does. A grown-up writes the colours.
  - Note: Sugar usually melts within 2 minutes, turns brown and then black. Thread, paper and onion skin go brown and then black. Atta goes dark brown and may need the full 8 minutes. Salt may crackle. The coin may get a faint yellow or blue tint, but it does not turn into black crumbs. If little has happened by 5 minutes, raise the flame slightly.
  - Check: In The Black Inside, why are all eight heaps heated on one tawa together? → **So each heap gets the same heat and time**
- **s4 (5 min)**

  *Parent says:* Everything is cool now. Rub each heap across the white paper. Which ones leave a black mark, like a pencil or a piece of charcoal?  
  *Child mode:* Rub each cool heap on white paper. Which ones leave a black mark?
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. Fill in where each thing comes from. Then read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Now look at where each thing comes from. What do the five black ones share?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What do the things that turned black have in common? (good: They all came from plants.; They were all made by living things.; They all have carbon in them.)
  - Ask: The sugar was white. Where was the black before you heated it? (good: It was inside the sugar, joined to other things.; It was there but it did not look black.)
  - Note: The five that turned black were all made by plants. Their molecules are chains and rings of carbon atoms, with hydrogen and oxygen atoms attached. Heat drives the hydrogen and oxygen out as water vapour and smoke. A black solid that is mostly carbon stays behind. Salt holds no carbon. Baking soda does hold carbon, but each carbon atom sits alone and leaves as carbon dioxide gas. Write down what you really saw, even if one heap only went brown.
  - Think: Milk is white, and it leaves a black crust when it boils dry. What does that tell you about milk?
### e2 · One Ring, Three Materials: glucose, cornflour and cotton (30 min)
- **question:** Glucose, cornflour and cotton are all built from the same small ring of atoms. Do they behave the same way in cold water, on the tongue and in hot water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. All three are built from one and the same ring. Which will dissolve in cold water? Which will taste sweet? Which will change in hot water? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use 2 level teaspoons of each material, or a ball of cotton about that size. Use 100 millilitres of water each time, which is half a small tumbler. Stir each tumbler for 1 minute. Heat each one for 3 minutes on the same low flame. Only one thing changes: the material.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the heating. The cornflour paste holds heat, so let it cool before anyone touches it. Taste only a tiny pinch. Do not swallow the cotton.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows: glucose, cornflour and cotton. For each one, write what happened in cold water, how it tasted, and what happened when it was heated.
- **results table:** The material | In cold water after 1 minute of stirring | Taste | After 3 minutes of heating
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three materials on the table. Tell your child that all three are built from the same small ring, called glucose. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* These three are built from the same ring of atoms, called glucose. In glucose powder the rings are single. In cornflour and cotton they are joined in long chains. Will they behave the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* All three are built from one ring. Will they behave the same? Why?
  - Ask: Which one do you think will taste sweet, and why? (good: Glucose, because it is a sugar.; All three, if they are made of the same ring.; Only the powder, because cotton is not food.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is a good start. Do not say yet which chain is which.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 100 millilitres of water into each tumbler. Add 2 level teaspoons of glucose to the first and 2 of cornflour to the second. Put the cotton ball in the third. Your child stirs each for 1 minute, then looks.  
  *Parent says:* Stir each one for a slow count of sixty. Now look through the water. Which material can you still see? Wait two minutes and look at the cornflour again.  
  *Child mode:* Stir each tumbler for one minute. Which material can you still see?
  - Note: The glucose dissolves and the water turns clear. The cornflour makes the water milky, and a white layer settles within a few minutes. The cotton gets wet and stays a ball.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child tastes a tiny pinch of dry glucose, then a tiny pinch of dry cornflour. Then they hold a clean wisp of cotton on the tongue for a moment and take it out. Write the tastes.  
  *Parent says:* Taste a tiny pinch of each powder. Then hold this clean wisp of cotton on your tongue and take it out again. Which ones taste sweet?  
  *Child mode:* Taste a pinch of each powder. Touch the cotton to your tongue. Which is sweet?
  - Check: Cornflour is made of glucose rings. Why does it not taste sweet? → **Its rings are joined in long chains**
- **s4 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Heat the glucose water in the small pan on a low flame for 3 minutes, stirring. Pour it back. Do the same with the cotton and its water. Stir the cornflour water well, then heat it last. Your child watches from one step back.  
  *Parent says:* I will heat each one for 3 minutes. Watch the spoon as I stir. Tell me the moment anything changes.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the pan from one step back. Which one changes when heated?
  - Note: The glucose water stays thin. The cotton does not change. The cornflour water turns thick and glossy quite suddenly, just before it boils. Heat the cornflour last, because it leaves the pan sticky.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. Then read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. The ring is the same in all three. So what made them behave so differently?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: The ring is the same in all three. What is different? (good: How many rings are joined.; The way the rings are joined.; One is single rings and two are long chains.)
  - Note: Glucose is single rings. They are small, so water molecules surround each one and carry it off. That is dissolving. They also fit the sweet sensors on the tongue. Starch is a coiled chain of hundreds or thousands of rings, packed into tiny grains. The grains are too big to dissolve in cold water. Hot water gets in between the chains, the grains swell and burst, and the loose chains tangle. That thickens the water. Cellulose is a straight chain in which every second ring is turned over. Straight chains lie side by side and hold each other tightly, so water cannot separate them.
  - Think: A cotton shirt is washed in water hundreds of times and does not dissolve. What would happen if cotton were single rings?

- **question:** One wooden toothpick is burnt in the open flame. Ten more are heated in the same flame, wrapped tightly in foil so that almost no air can reach them. What is left in each case?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. What will be left of the toothpick burnt in the open? What will be inside the foil packet: ash, wood that looks the same, or something else? Why do you think so?

- **record:** Draw a table with two rows: the toothpick burnt in the open, and the toothpicks in the foil packet. Write what was left, its colour, whether it kept its shape and whether it draws on paper.

### Carbon chains at home (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) How many bonds does one carbon atom make?  
   Options: Four / One / Two / Six  
   **Answer:** Four. *Why:* A carbon atom makes four bonds. That lets it hold two neighbours in a chain and still have two bonds free.
2. (truefalse) Charcoal and diamond are made of the same kind of atom.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both are carbon. In diamond every atom holds four others in a tight network. In charcoal the atoms are joined in a jumble.
3. (fill) A substance made of only one kind of atom is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['element']. *Why:* Carbon, oxygen and iron are elements. Sugar is not, because it holds three kinds of atom.
4. (sort) Sort these by what happens on a hot tawa. Does each one turn black or not?  
   **Answer:** sugar → Turns black; cotton thread → Turns black; paper → Turns black; salt → Does not turn black; a steel coin → Does not turn black. *Why:* Sugar, cotton and paper were made by plants and are built on carbon chains. Salt and steel hold no carbon chains.
5. (mcq) Sugar is white. Why does it turn black when it is heated strongly?  
   Options: Carbon is left behind / Dirt from the tawa sticks / Smoke paints it / The white burns away  
   **Answer:** Carbon is left behind. *Why:* Heat drives hydrogen and oxygen out of the sugar as water vapour and gases. The carbon atoms stay behind as a black solid.
6. (match) Match each fuel to the length of its carbon chain.  
   **Answer:** Piped cooking gas → 1 carbon atom; LPG in a cylinder → 3 or 4 carbon atoms; Petrol → About 8 carbon atoms; Candle wax → About 25 carbon atoms. *Why:* The shortest chains are gases. Longer chains are liquids, and the longest are solids.
7. (truefalse) A longer carbon chain makes a fuel thinner and quicker to boil.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Longer chains hold on to each other more strongly. So the fuel is thicker and boils at a higher temperature.
8. (fill) Rice, potato and cornflour are mostly ____, a long chain of glucose rings.  
   **Answer:** ['starch']. *Why:* Plants join glucose rings into starch and pack the starch away as a food store.
9. (mcq) Which of these is built from glucose rings joined in long, straight chains?  
   Options: Cotton / Salt / Steel / Glass  
   **Answer:** Cotton. *Why:* Cotton is mostly cellulose. Each cellulose molecule is a straight chain of thousands of glucose rings.
10. (order) Put these in order, from the shortest carbon chain to the longest.  
   **Answer:** ['Methane', 'Butane', 'Octane', 'Candle wax', 'Polythene']. *Why:* Methane has 1 carbon atom, butane 4, octane 8, wax about 25 and polythene many thousands.
11. (write) Salt and sugar are both white grains. Explain why only sugar leaves a black solid when heated.  
   **Answer:** Sugar molecules are built on carbon atoms joined in rings, with hydrogen and oxygen attached. Heat drives off the hydrogen and oxygen, and carbon is left. Salt holds no carbon, so nothing black can be left.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the black solid is carbon and that it was inside the sugar all along. It says that salt has no carbon.
12. (write) Glucose dissolves in cold water, but cotton does not. Both are built from the same ring. Explain the difference.  
   **Answer:** Glucose is single small rings, so water can surround each one and carry it away. In cotton the rings are joined in long straight chains that hold each other tightly. Water cannot pull them apart.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the ring is the same and the joining is different. It links small molecules to dissolving.
13. (draw) Draw a chain of 3 carbon atoms. Add hydrogen atoms until every carbon atom has 4 bonds.  
   **Answer:** Three C atoms in a row joined by two lines. The two end atoms each hold 3 H atoms, and the middle atom holds 2. That is 8 H atoms in all.. *Why:* A strong drawing gives every carbon atom exactly four lines and every hydrogen atom exactly one. This molecule is propane.
14. (perspective) Is a plastic bottle 'unnatural'?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that plastic and wood share a carbon frame. Another is that microbes have enzymes for wood but almost none for plastic.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses a fact about carbon chains or about microbes, and answers another view fairly.
15. (mcq) Where does a plant get the carbon to build wood?  
   Options: From carbon dioxide in the air / From the soil / From water / From sunlight  
   **Answer:** From carbon dioxide in the air. *Why:* A leaf takes carbon dioxide from the air and joins the carbon into glucose. Sunlight gives the energy, but light is not a material.
### Chains, rings and reasons (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) An atom makes only one bond. What is the biggest molecule that such atoms can build alone?  
   Options: A pair of two atoms / A chain of three / A ring of six / A chain of any length  
   **Answer:** A pair of two atoms. *Why:* Once two one-bond atoms join, neither has a bond left for a third. Hydrogen gas is made of such pairs.
2. (mcq) A carbon atom in the middle of a chain has two bonds left. What can they hold?  
   Options: Hydrogen atoms or a branch / Nothing at all / Only another chain end / Only oxygen atoms  
   **Answer:** Hydrogen atoms or a branch. *Why:* Each spare bond can hold a hydrogen atom, or it can hold another carbon atom and start a branch.
3. (truefalse) Baking soda holds carbon, so it turns black on a hot tawa.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each carbon atom in baking soda sits alone, held by oxygen atoms. When heated, it leaves as carbon dioxide gas. A black solid needs carbon atoms joined to each other.
4. (truefalse) Bending a chain of five carbon atoms makes a new substance.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bending changes no joins. A new substance needs the atoms to be joined in a different pattern, such as a branch.

   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* The two end atoms hold 3 each and the two middle atoms hold 2 each. 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 10. This is butane.
6. (count) Twenty glucose rings join into one chain. How many water molecules come out?  
   **Answer:** 19. *Why:* A chain of 20 rings has 19 joins, and each join gives off one water molecule.

   **Answer:** silk → A living being; paper → A living being; coconut oil → A living being; nylon rope → People, from petroleum; a PET water bottle → People, from petroleum; polyester cloth → People, from petroleum. *Why:* Silkworms make silk, trees make the fibres of paper and coconut palms make the oil. Nylon, PET and polyester are made in factories from petroleum.

10. (write) Wood heated inside tight foil turns to charcoal. Wood heated in the open turns to ash. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Inside the foil there is no air, so the carbon cannot burn and stays behind as charcoal. In the open, carbon joins with oxygen from the air and leaves as carbon dioxide gas. Only a little mineral ash is left.. *Why:* A strong answer names the one difference, which is air. It says where the carbon went in each case.
11. (write) Cornflour does not thicken cold water, but it thickens hot water. Explain what the heat does.  
   **Answer:** Starch chains are packed into tiny grains that cold water cannot enter. Hot water gets in between the chains. The grains swell and burst, and the loose chains tangle and hold the water.. *Why:* A strong answer speaks about long chains in grains, and says the hot water frees them to tangle.
12. (write) Many people think coal and oil are made from dinosaurs. What would you tell them, and what evidence would you give?  
   **Answer:** Most coal formed from swamp forests, long before the dinosaurs. Oil formed mostly from tiny sea plants and other sea creatures buried in mud. Coal still holds the prints of leaves and bark.. *Why:* A strong answer corrects the belief kindly and gives evidence, such as leaf prints in coal.
13. (label) Label the three skeletons that five carbon atoms can make.  
   **Answer:** ['Straight chain: five atoms in one row', 'Chain with one branch: four in a row, and one joined to the second atom', 'Cross: one atom in the middle, holding four others']. *Why:* These are the only three ways to join five carbon atoms without a ring. Each is a real liquid with its own boiling point.
14. (perspective) Should a food packet be allowed to say 'chemical-free'?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer notices that the word 'chemical' is used with two meanings. It may suggest a clearer label.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement about words. A strong answer separates what is true in chemistry from what the customer wants to know.
15. (mcq) Fuel gas from a cylinder burns. What becomes of its carbon atoms?  
   Options: They join oxygen as carbon dioxide / They vanish / They turn into heat / They stay in the burner  
   **Answer:** They join oxygen as carbon dioxide. *Why:* Atoms are never lost. Each carbon atom joins with oxygen and leaves as carbon dioxide gas. The heat comes from the new bonds forming.
### Carbon, from stars to plastic (challenge, 15 questions)

   Options: Diamond is pure carbon / Diamond holds water / Diamond is a kind of glass / Oxygen is made of carbon

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea from a mineral salt. Chemists now make sugars, vitamins and medicines.

   Options: Their atoms are joined in different patterns / They have different numbers of atoms / One has no carbon / They differ only in colour  
   **Answer:** Their atoms are joined in different patterns. *Why:* All three have 5 carbon and 12 hydrogen atoms. One is a straight chain, one has a branch and one is a cross.

## Applications
- Oil refineries separate petroleum by chain length. Short chains become cooking gas, middle chains become petrol and diesel, and the longest become wax and road tar.
- Medicines are carbon molecules built to an exact shape. Asima Chatterjee, a chemist in Kolkata, studied carbon molecules from Indian plants. She developed medicines from them for epilepsy and malaria.
- Charcoal is used in water filters. Its surface is full of tiny holes, and many unwanted molecules stick there.
- Cooks use starch chains to thicken food. A spoon of cornflour or rice flour thickens a soup or a payasam when it is heated.
- Carbon fibre is made of thin threads of carbon atoms joined in sheets. It is lighter than steel and very strong, so it is used in aircraft, racing cycles and rockets.
- Scientists read the age of old wood, cloth and bone from one rare kind of carbon atom. It slowly disappears after a living being dies.

## Spotted around us
- The black spots on a roti or a dosa that stayed on the heat too long. The black is mostly carbon.
- The red LPG cylinder in the kitchen. Inside it, chains of 3 and 4 carbon atoms are squeezed into a liquid.
- The charcoal glowing in an ironing man's iron box, or under roasting corn at a roadside stall.
- The number inside the triangle on the base of a plastic bottle or dabba. Each number names a different carbon chain.
- The label inside a shirt collar. Cotton is a carbon chain made by a plant, and polyester is a carbon chain made in a factory.
- A candle or a diya at Karthigai Deepam or Deepavali. The flame is carbon chains joining with oxygen from the air.

## Open questions
- **How did carbon molecules first come together into something alive?** Simple carbon molecules form easily, even in space. Nobody has yet shown every step from such molecules to a living cell.
- **Must living things be built on carbon?** Every living being found so far is carbon-based, but we have looked closely at only one planet.
- **How many different carbon molecules are possible?** The number is far greater than the number of stars we can see. Chemists have made only a tiny share, so most have never existed.
- **Can microbes learn to eat all our plastics?** In 2016 scientists in Japan found a bacterium who breaks down the plastic of water bottles. Most other plastics still have no known eater.
- **Where can we safely put the carbon dioxide from burning fuels?** Trees, soil, the sea and rock all take some up. Nobody yet knows how to store enough of it, cheaply and for long enough.

## Connected maths
- In One Bond or Four, a chain of 1 carbon atom held 4 hydrogen atoms. A chain of 2 held 6, and a chain of 3 held 8. How many hydrogen atoms will a chain of 4 hold? How many will a chain of 10 hold? **A chain of 4 holds 10 hydrogen atoms. A chain of 10 holds 22.**. Each extra carbon atom adds 2 hydrogen atoms: 4, 6, 8, 10. So the number is 2 for every carbon atom, plus 2 more for the two ends. For 10 carbon atoms, 10 times 2 is 20, and 20 plus 2 is 22. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In Five Carbons you found 3 skeletons for 5 carbon atoms. Chemists have counted the others: 4 atoms make 2 skeletons, 6 make 5 and 7 make 9. Do the numbers go up by the same amount each time? Will 8 atoms make more or fewer than 12? **No. The steps are 1, 2 and 4, so they keep growing. 8 atoms will make more than 12. The real number is 18.**. From 2 to 3 is a step of 1. From 3 to 5 is a step of 2. From 5 to 9 is a step of 4. The next step should be bigger than 4, so the answer is more than 13. It is 18. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In The Black Inside, 8 things were heated and 5 turned black. All 5 came from plants. The other 3 did not come from plants. Draw two circles: 'came from a plant' and 'turned black'. Where does each thing go? What do you notice? **Sugar, atta, cotton thread, paper and onion skin go in both circles. Salt, baking soda and the coin go in neither. No thing is in only one circle.**. Every thing that came from a plant turned black, and every thing that turned black came from a plant. So the two circles hold exactly the same five things. That is strong evidence that the two facts are linked. (queues `math.logic.sets-and-venn.L4`)
- Here are four boiling points from the game Chain Line. Methane boils at 162 degrees below zero and butane at 1 degree below zero. Pentane boils at 36 degrees and octane at 126 degrees. Which are gases on a 30 degree day in Chennai? Which would still be a gas at 10 degrees below zero in Ladakh? **On a 30 degree day, methane and butane are gases. At 10 degrees below zero, only methane is still a gas.**. A substance is a gas when the air is warmer than its boiling point. 30 is above 1 below zero and above 162 below zero, but below 36 and 126. 10 below zero is colder than 1 below zero, so butane turns liquid there. (queues `math.number-sense.negative-numbers.L4`)
- About 18 of every 100 kilograms of a human body is carbon. A child weighs 30 kilograms. How many kilograms of carbon does the child's body hold? **5.4 kilograms, which is 5 kilograms and 400 grams.**. For 100 kilograms the carbon is 18 kilograms, so for 10 kilograms it is 1.8 kilograms. 30 kilograms is 3 lots of 10, and 3 times 1.8 is 5.4. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In Foil Packet Charcoal, wood became charcoal and smoke. A charcoal maker heats 100 kilograms of dry wood in a closed kiln and takes out 25 kilograms of charcoal. What fraction of the wood's weight left as smoke and gas? **Three quarters. 75 kilograms left as smoke and gas.**. 100 take away 25 is 75. 75 out of 100 is the same as 3 out of 4. So three quarters of the weight left, and one quarter stayed as charcoal. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
