# Falling: why heavy and light things land together
`sci.gravity.falling.L3`

**Summary.** Drop a full bottle of water and a bottle with only a little water together, and they land with one thud. Gravity, the force that makes things fall, pulls harder on the full bottle, because it has more mass. But more mass also needs a bigger pull to speed up, and the two match exactly. So both fall in the same way. Only air resistance, the push of the air, slows light, spread-out things like feathers and flat paper. People believed heavy things fall faster for nearly two thousand years, until careful tests showed they do not. The unit then goes further: everything with mass attracts everything else, gravity bends light and slows clocks, and nobody yet knows what gravity is deep down.

**Intro.** *Hold a big potato in one hand and a small lemon in the other. Hold them at the same height. If you let go together, which will hit the floor first? Make your guess, and say why, before you try.* In 1971, an astronaut called David Scott stood on the Moon. He held a hammer in one hand and a falcon feather in the other. A camera was filming. Millions of people on Earth were watching. He let go of both at the same moment. On Earth, the feather would float down slowly and the hammer would thud down. On the Moon, there is no air. What do you think happened?

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. A force can start something moving, stop it, speed it up or change its direction. *e.g.* Kicking a football is a force. Gravity pulling a mango down from a tree is a force too.
- **Gravity**: The force that attracts every thing that has mass towards every other thing. The Earth has so much mass that its gravity is the one we feel. *e.g.* A mango falls from the tree to the ground. Gravity pulled it down.
- **Fall**: To move down towards the Earth because nothing is holding you up. *e.g.* When you let go of a ball, it falls.
- **Down**: Towards the middle of the Earth. It is not the bottom of a page. For people on the other side of the Earth, down points the other way from ours. *e.g.* In Chennai and in Buenos Aires, a dropped ball falls towards the middle of the Earth.
- **Mass**: The amount of matter in something, which means how much material it is made of. A steel tumbler has more mass than a paper cup of the same size. *e.g.* An elephant has more mass than a cat. A brick has more mass than a sponge.
- **Weight**: The force of gravity on something. A thing with more mass has more weight, because the Earth pulls it harder. *e.g.* A full water bottle has more weight than an empty one. You feel the difference when you hold them.
- **Air resistance**: The push of the air on something moving through it. It is a force, and it slows light, spread-out things the most. *e.g.* A flat sheet of paper floats down slowly. The same sheet, crumpled into a ball, drops fast.
- **Vacuum**: A space with no air in it at all. In a vacuum there is no air resistance. *e.g.* Space between the planets is almost a vacuum. Scientists can also pump the air out of a big chamber.
- **Speed up**: To go faster and faster. A falling thing speeds up every moment it falls. *e.g.* A ball dropped from a balcony is going much faster at the bottom than at the top.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what made the difference. *e.g.* Drop two bottles from the same height, at the same moment, onto the same floor. Only the amount of water inside is different.
- **Parachute**: A big, light sheet that catches the air. The air pushes up on it and slows the fall. *e.g.* Soldiers and skydivers jump from planes with parachutes. A plastic bag with a toy tied under it is a small parachute.
- **Free fall**: Falling with only gravity acting on you: nothing holding you up and almost no air in the way. Things in free fall feel weightless. *e.g.* For a moment at the top of a jump, you feel light. Astronauts in the space station are in free fall all the time.
- **Weightless**: Feeling no weight, because nothing is pushing up on you. Gravity is still pulling. *e.g.* Astronauts float in the space station. Gravity still pulls them, but they are falling, so the floor does not push on them.
- **Model**: A simple thing we make or draw to help us think about something real. A model always gets some things right and some things wrong. *e.g.* A globe is a model of the Earth. A stretched dupatta with a ball on it is a model of bent space.
- **Space-time**: Space and time, joined together. Einstein showed that mass bends space-time, a little like a heavy ball dents a stretched cloth. *e.g.* The Sun bends the space-time near it. The Earth moves along that bend, round and round the Sun.
- **Orbit**: The curved path of something that keeps falling round another thing and keeps missing it. *e.g.* The Moon orbits the Earth. The space station orbits the Earth about 16 times a day.
- **Lagrange point**: A place in space where a spacecraft can stay in step with two big bodies, such as the Sun and the Earth, as they move. *e.g.* India's Aditya-L1 spacecraft watches the Sun from near a Lagrange point, about 15 lakh kilometres from Earth.
- **Escape speed**: How fast something must go to get away from a planet's pull and never fall back. *e.g.* To escape the Earth, a rocket must reach about 11 kilometres every second. Your best jump leaves the ground at only 2 or 3 metres a second.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see. Every atom has mass, so every atom attracts every other atom. *e.g.* You, the Earth and the Moon are all made of atoms.
- **Energy**: What it takes to make something move, warm up or change. Energy never disappears. It only changes from one kind into another. *e.g.* A stone held up high has stored height energy. As it falls, that energy turns into movement.
- **Light**: What our eyes see. Light is made of tiny packets of energy called photons. Gravity bends its path too. *e.g.* Light from far stars bends a tiny bit as it passes close to the Sun.
- **Graviton**: A tiny particle of gravity that some scientists think might exist. Nobody has ever found one. *e.g.* Light comes in tiny packets called photons. Maybe gravity comes in tiny packets called gravitons. We don't know yet.

## Explanations
### What gravity is · *defined*
**Definition:** Gravity is the force that attracts things with mass towards each other. On Earth, it pulls everything towards the middle of the Earth.

Let go of anything, and it falls: a mango, a pencil, a raindrop, or you after a jump. Nothing has to touch it. Gravity acts across the air, and even across empty space. It never switches off. It pulls on you while you sleep, while you swim and while you fly in a plane. You only notice it when nothing is holding you up. The force of gravity on you is called your weight.
- e.g. A ball you throw up always comes back down.
- e.g. Water in a river flows downhill, towards the sea.
- e.g. Rain falls from clouds to the ground.
### Down means towards the middle of the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Down means towards the middle of the Earth, from wherever you are standing.

The Earth is a huge ball. People live all over it, on every side. A child in Argentina is standing almost upside down compared with you. But she does not fall off. For her, down is towards the middle of the Earth, just as it is for you. So 'down' is not one direction in space. It points to the middle of the Earth from wherever you are. Builders use a plumb line, a weight on a string, to find down. The string points almost exactly towards the middle of the Earth.
- e.g. A plumb line hangs straight down. Masons use it to build walls that stand straight.
- e.g. Imagine digging straight down from India, through the middle of the Earth. You would come out in the Pacific Ocean, west of South America. Nobody can really dig that deep.
### Heavy and light fall together · *defined*
**Definition:** If there is no air in the way, all things fall in the same way. Heavy and light things, dropped together from the same height, land together.

This is the great surprise of falling. Most people guess that a heavy thing falls faster. Try it with a full bottle of water and a bottle with only a little water, and they land together. Here is why. The Earth pulls the full bottle harder, because it has more mass. But more mass also makes a thing harder to speed up. Think of pushing a full trolley and an empty trolley. A bottle with twice the mass gets twice the pull, but it also needs twice the pull to speed up as fast. So the extra pull is used up exactly by the extra mass, and both bottles speed up in the same way. Only the air spoils this, for things that are very light and spread out.
- e.g. A full water bottle and a bottle with only a little water, dropped together, hit the floor together.
- e.g. A cricket ball and a tennis ball, dropped together from a balcony, land together.
- e.g. On the Moon, with no air, a hammer and a feather land together.
### Air slows some falling things · *defined*
**Definition:** Air resistance is the push of the air on something moving through it. It slows light, spread-out things much more than heavy, compact things.

Air is made of tiny particles, far too small to see. A falling thing has to push them out of the way, and they push back. That push is air resistance. A heavy, compact thing, like a stone, is hardly slowed by it. A light, spread-out thing, like a feather or a flat sheet of paper, is slowed a lot. Crumple the paper into a ball and it falls fast, though it is the same paper. The faster a thing falls, the harder the air pushes. In the end, the push of the air can become as big as the pull of gravity. Then the thing stops speeding up and falls at a steady speed. Scientists call this its terminal speed.
- e.g. A big raindrop falls at about 30 kilometres an hour, about as fast as a bicycle going downhill. Without air, a drop from a high cloud would hit the ground far harder.
- e.g. Seeds of the semal tree, the silk-cotton tree, float on fluff and can drift far.
- e.g. A skydiver falls at about 200 kilometres an hour, until the parachute opens and slows her to a gentle drift.
### Who first found that heavy and light fall together? · *debated*
Everyone now agrees on the answer. But who should get the credit? Many people found pieces of it, over two thousand years. Each one had to argue against what everyone believed.
- e.g. Aristotle said a stone ten times heavier falls ten times faster. People believed him for about 1,900 years.
- e.g. Galileo's books made the idea famous, but others had tested it before him.
- **Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,350 years ago:** Heavy things fall faster, in proportion to their weight. He reasoned about it, but did not test it carefully. For a long time almost everyone agreed with him.
- **John Philoponus, a scholar in Alexandria, Egypt, about 1,500 years ago:** He dropped two weights, one many times heavier, from the same height. The difference in time was very small. So Aristotle was wrong. Few people listened.
- **Simon Stevin and Jan Cornets de Groot, in Delft, in the Netherlands, in 1586:** They dropped two lead balls, one ten times heavier, from a church tower about 10 metres high. The two thuds on a board below sounded as one.
- **Galileo Galilei, in Italy, from about 1590 to 1638:** He gave the clearest argument. He slowed falling down with ramps to measure it. He wrote books that many people read. The famous Leaning Tower of Pisa story comes from his student. It may not have happened.
### Galileo's tied-together stones · *defined*
**Definition:** A thought experiment is an experiment you do only in your mind, by careful reasoning. Galileo used one to show that heavy and light things must fall together.

Galileo showed Aristotle was wrong without dropping anything at all. Imagine a heavy stone and a light stone. Aristotle says the heavy one falls fast and the light one falls slowly. Now tie them together with a string. The slow light stone should hold the heavy one back. So the pair should fall slower than the heavy stone alone. But the pair is heavier than the heavy stone alone. So, by Aristotle's rule, it should fall faster. It cannot be both slower and faster. So the rule must be wrong. Galileo wrote this in a book in 1638.
- e.g. Tie a heavy stone and a light stone together. Do they fall faster, slower, or the same?
- e.g. Einstein also loved thought experiments. He imagined riding on a beam of light.
### Falling things speed up · *defined*
**Definition:** A falling thing speeds up as it falls. Without air, it gains about 10 metres a second of speed every second.

A dropped ball does not fall at one steady speed. Every second, it goes faster. After 1 second, it has fallen about 5 metres. After 2 seconds, about 20 metres. After 3 seconds, about 45 metres. Galileo found this pattern. Falling was too fast to measure, so he slowed it down. He rolled balls down a long, gentle ramp. He timed them with a water clock, by weighing the water that ran out. The distances from the start went up as 1, 4, 9, 16, the square numbers. Where does the speed come from? A thing held up high has stored energy. As it falls, that stored energy turns into movement energy.
- e.g. Drop a ball from a step, then from a balcony. It hits much harder from higher up.
- e.g. Water falling over a high waterfall, like Jog Falls in Karnataka, hits the pool below with a roar.
### Everything pulls everything · *defined*
**Definition:** Every thing that has mass attracts every other thing that has mass. Bigger masses attract more strongly, and the force gets weaker with distance.

The Earth pulls you, and you pull the Earth just as hard. When you jump, the Earth moves up towards you a tiny bit. It is far too little to notice, because the Earth has so much more mass than you. You also attract the Moon, and your pencil attracts your eraser. Every atom in the universe attracts every other atom. Isaac Newton wrote this law in a book in 1687. He showed that the force that drops an apple is the same force that keeps the Moon going round the Earth. In 1798, Henry Cavendish measured the tiny force between big lead balls and small ones, in a building at his home in London. Gravity is a very weak force. A small fridge magnet can lift a paper clip against the gravity of the whole Earth.
- e.g. The Moon pulls on the sea and makes the tides.
- e.g. Two people sitting next to each other pull on each other, but far too weakly to feel.
- e.g. A fridge magnet holds up a paper clip, beating the gravity of the whole Earth.
### Gurutva: old Indian ideas about falling · *debated*
Long before Newton, thinkers in India asked why things fall. Their words still live in our languages. Guru means heavy in Sanskrit. A guru is a teacher with 'weight'. Gurutva means heaviness. Gurutvakarshan, the attraction of heaviness, is the Hindi word for gravity today. How much did these thinkers really know? Historians argue about it.
- e.g. Gurutvakarshan is the Hindi word for gravity.
- e.g. The English word gravity comes from the Latin gravis, which means heavy. So does grave, when it means serious.
- **Kanada, a thinker of the Vaisheshika school, more than 2,000 years ago:** Things fall because they have gurutva, heaviness, when nothing holds them up.
- **Brahmagupta, an astronomer, in 628 CE:** Bodies fall towards the Earth because it is the Earth's nature to attract them. In the same way, it is water's nature to flow.
- **Bhaskara II, an astronomer, in the 1100s:** The Earth has a power of attraction, and pulls heavy things to itself.
- **Most historians today:** These thinkers clearly said the Earth attracts things. But they did not say how strong the pull is, or show that the same pull holds the Moon. That was Newton's step. Some modern claims go further than the old books say.
### Einstein's picture: mass bends space and time · *defined*
**Definition:** General relativity is Einstein's theory of gravity. Mass and energy bend space and time together, and a falling thing moves along the straightest path it can through that bent space-time.

Newton described gravity as a force: the Earth pulls the Moon, and the Moon pulls the Earth. In 1915, Albert Einstein gave a different picture, called general relativity. Space and time are joined together, and scientists call them space-time. Mass bends space-time. In Einstein's picture, a falling thing is not being pushed or pulled at all. It moves along the straightest path it can, through bent space-time. That would explain why everything falls in the same way, heavy or light: the path belongs to the place, not to the thing. A heavy ball on a stretched cloth is a model of this. Like every model, it is wrong in places. The ball only dents the cloth because the Earth's gravity pulls it down, so the model uses gravity to explain gravity. And real space-time bends in every direction at once, not just downwards like a cloth.
- e.g. Put a heavy ball on a stretched dupatta. A marble rolled past it curves in towards the dent.
- e.g. The Earth goes round the Sun along the bend the Sun makes in space-time.
- **Isaac Newton, 1687:** Gravity is a force that attracts masses towards each other. His law works so well that engineers still use it to send spacecraft to the Moon and Mars.
- **Albert Einstein, 1915:** Gravity is the bending of space and time. This explains things Newton's law cannot, like the exact bending of light and clocks slowing near heavy things. For everyday falling, both give the same answers.
### Falling together feels like floating · *defined*
**Definition:** Things that fall together do not push on each other. So anything in free fall feels weightless, even though gravity is still pulling it.

Ask a grown-up to make small holes near the bottom of a plastic bottle, and fill it with water. Water squirts out. Now drop the bottle. While it falls, the squirting stops. The water and the bottle are falling together, so the water no longer presses on the holes. In 1907, Einstein imagined a person falling off a roof. While falling, he would feel no weight at all. Einstein called this the happiest thought of his life. Astronauts in the space station feel this all the time. Gravity up there is still about 9 tenths as strong as on the ground. But the station and the astronauts are falling round the Earth together. So nothing pushes on their feet, and they float.
- e.g. Water stops squirting from a falling bottle.
- e.g. Your tummy feels funny on a swing or a giant wheel as it starts to drop.
- e.g. Astronauts float in the space station, about 400 kilometres up, though gravity is still strong there.
### Can you jump into space? Escape speed · *defined*
**Definition:** Escape speed is how fast something must start out to get away from a planet's pull for ever. For the Earth, it is about 11 kilometres a second.

Jump as high as you can. You go up, slow down, stop and fall back. Jump faster, and you go higher before falling back. Could you ever jump so fast that you never come back? Yes, in theory. You would need to leave the ground at about 11 kilometres every second. That is far faster than a bullet. Scientists call it the escape speed. A child's good jump leaves the ground at only 2 or 3 metres a second. Rockets reach escape speed by burning fuel for many minutes. A small world has a small escape speed. On the Moon it is about 2.4 kilometres a second. A black hole has so much mass squeezed into so small a space that, inside its edge, the escape speed is faster than light. So even light cannot get out.
- e.g. India's Mangalyaan had to go faster than the Earth's escape speed to reach Mars. Chandrayaan did not need to, because the Moon is still held by the Earth's gravity.
- e.g. On a tiny asteroid, a hard jump could send you off into space.
### Places that stay in step: Lagrange points · *defined*
**Definition:** A Lagrange point is a place near two big bodies, like the Sun and the Earth, where a spacecraft can stay in step with them as they move.

Things closer to the Sun than the Earth normally go round the Sun faster than the Earth does. But about 15 lakh kilometres from the Earth, towards the Sun, there is a special place. There, the Earth's gravity pulls back on a spacecraft by just the right amount. The spacecraft goes round the Sun in exactly one year, the same as the Earth. So it stays between the Earth and the Sun all the time. This place is called the first Lagrange point. The Sun and the Earth have five such points. They are named after the mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who described them in 1772. Leonhard Euler had found three of them a few years earlier. India's Aditya-L1 was launched in September 2023 to watch the Sun from near the first Lagrange point. The James Webb Space Telescope works near the second one, on the far side of the Earth from the Sun. Spacecraft there still need small pushes from their engines now and then to stay in place.
- e.g. Aditya-L1, India's Sun-watching spacecraft, sits near the first Lagrange point.
- e.g. The James Webb Space Telescope works near the second Lagrange point.
### Light falls too · *defined*
**Definition:** Gravity bends the path of light. Heavy things like the Sun and galaxies bend the light passing near them.

Light has no mass at all. You might think gravity cannot touch it. But Einstein said light follows the bends in space-time too. So starlight passing close to the Sun should bend a little. In 1919, there was a total eclipse of the Sun. Astronomers led by Arthur Eddington and Frank Dyson photographed stars near the dark Sun. The stars seemed shifted, by about the amount Einstein predicted. Today, telescopes see whole galaxies bending the light of galaxies behind them. The far galaxies look stretched into arcs and rings. This is called gravitational lensing. The Indian-born astronomer Priyamvada Natarajan uses it to map dark matter, invisible matter we know only by its gravity.
- e.g. Starlight bends near the Sun, as photographed in 1919.
- e.g. Giant galaxies bend light like a lens, making rings and arcs in telescope pictures.
### Clocks tick slower lower down · *defined*
**Definition:** Time runs slower closer to a big mass. Scientists call this gravitational time dilation.

This is one of the strangest facts in science. Time itself runs a little slower closer to a big mass. A clock on the ground floor ticks very slightly slower than a clock on the roof. The difference is far too small to feel. Over a whole lifetime, it adds up to much less than a second. But today's best clocks can measure it. In 2010, scientists in the USA lifted one very precise clock just 33 centimetres higher than another. The higher clock ran faster, by the tiny amount Einstein's theory predicts. GPS satellites, which help maps on phones, fly about 20,000 kilometres up. Their clocks run fast compared with clocks on the ground. Engineers allow for this, or phone maps would drift off by kilometres every day.
- e.g. GPS satellite clocks are set to allow for the effect of gravity on time.
- e.g. Someone living on a mountain ages a tiny bit faster than someone by the sea. Over a lifetime, it is far less than a second.
### How living things know which way is down · *debated*
Living things feel gravity all the time. Inside your ears are tiny stones, made of the same material as chalk. They rest on tiny hairs. When you tip your head, the stones slide and bend the hairs, and your brain knows which way is down. Plants feel gravity too. A seed planted upside down still sends a root down and a shoot up. Charles Darwin and his son Francis studied this in 1880. How plants sense down is still being studied.
- e.g. Tilt your head and you know at once that you tilted. Your ear stones told you.
- e.g. A potted plant tipped over sideways bends the shoot back up within a day or two.
- e.g. A falling cat twists round to land on her feet.
- **The starch-grain idea, most plant scientists:** Root tip cells contain heavy grains of starch. They sink to the lowest side of each cell, like sand in a bottle. The plant feels where they settle and grows that way.
- **Other plant scientists:** Some plants with almost no starch grains still find down. So maybe the whole cell feels the weight of its own insides pressing down. Both ways may work together.
### What is gravity, deep down? Gravitons and the big unknown · *idea*
Scientists know how gravity behaves very, very well. They can land spacecraft on the Moon with it. But they do not know what gravity is, deep down. The force of electricity and magnetism is carried by tiny particles called photons, the particles of light. So maybe gravity is carried by tiny particles too. Scientists call them gravitons. Nobody has ever found one. They may be far too weak to catch. Einstein's bent space-time works beautifully for planets and stars. Quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things, works beautifully for atoms. But the two do not fit together. Inside a black hole, and at the very start of the universe, both are needed. That is one of the biggest puzzles in science.
- e.g. In 2015, the LIGO detectors in the USA caught ripples in space-time from two black holes crashing together. These gravitational waves were ripples of space-time itself, not single gravitons.
- e.g. Some physicists think gravity is not a basic force at all. It might come out of something deeper, the way warmth comes out of jiggling atoms.
- **Many physicists:** Gravity, like the other forces, is carried by tiny particles called gravitons. Nobody knows how to catch one.
- **Some other physicists:** Space and time themselves may be made of something smaller. Gravity might grow out of that. These ideas have not been tested yet.

## Mostly agreed
- On Earth, everything falls towards the middle of the Earth. *How we know:* A plumb line, a weight on a string, hangs straight down everywhere on Earth. In the 1800s, the Great Trigonometrical Survey measured plumb lines all across India. They pointed towards the middle of the Earth, with tiny tugs sideways towards the heavy Himalayas.
- Without air, heavy and light things fall in exactly the same way. *How we know:* In 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon, and they landed together. In 2014, a BBC team dropped a bowling ball and feathers in NASA's huge vacuum chamber in Ohio. They landed together. A French satellite called MICROSCOPE tested this in space from 2016 to 2018. Two metal cylinders fell the same way to about 1 part in a million billion.
- Falling things speed up, and air slows light, spread-out things. *How we know:* Galileo measured the speeding up with balls on ramps. You can see air resistance yourself: a flat sheet of paper falls slowly, but the same sheet crumpled falls fast.
- Everything with mass pulls on everything else. *How we know:* In 1798, Henry Cavendish measured the pull between big lead balls and small ones in a building at his home in London. Scientists still repeat this experiment today.
- Gravity bends light and slows clocks. *How we know:* Starlight was seen bending near the Sun during the 1919 eclipse. Very precise clocks run slower when they are lower down, and GPS satellites must be corrected for it every day.
- Astronauts float because they are falling, not because there is no gravity. *How we know:* The space station is about 400 kilometres up. Gravity there is still about 9 tenths as strong as on the ground. Scientists work this out from Newton's law, and the station's orbit matches it exactly.

## How we found out
**Aristotle (the old idea), John Philoponus, Simon Stevin with Jan Cornets de Groot, Galileo Galilei, and the astronaut David Scott, The 300s BCE (Aristotle), the 500s CE (Philoponus), 1586 (Stevin), 1638 (Galileo's book) and 1971 (the Moon drop).** For about two thousand years, most people believed what the Greek thinker Aristotle taught: a heavy thing falls faster than a light one, in proportion to its weight. It seemed obvious, because a stone does beat a leaf. About 1,500 years ago, John Philoponus, a scholar in Alexandria, dropped two weights from the same height, one many times heavier than the other. He wrote that the difference in time was very small. Few people took notice. In 1586, Simon Stevin and Jan Cornets de Groot dropped two lead balls, one ten times heavier, from a church tower in Delft, about 10 metres up. The two thuds on a board below sounded as one. Then Galileo Galilei slowed falling down by rolling balls along gentle ramps, so that he could measure it. In 1638 he wrote that, without air, all things would fall at the same speed. A leaf lags behind because the air slows it, not because it is light. In 1971, the astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the airless Moon, and they landed together.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle taught that heavier things fall faster, in proportion to their weight. Drops from the same height showed that heavy and light weights land almost together, and Galileo showed that the air, not lightness, slows things like leaves.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Heavy things fall faster than light things.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,350 years ago. Most children and many grown-ups still guess this.; *rejected*)  
  For: A stone does fall faster than a leaf or a feather. That is what we see every day.  
  Against: The leaf is slowed by the air, not by being light. Crumple a paper into a ball and it falls almost as fast as a stone. Without air, on the Moon, a hammer and a feather land together.
- **Galileo dropped two balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa, and proved Aristotle wrong in front of a crowd.** (Many storybooks and textbooks; *debated*)  
  For: Vincenzo Viviani, who worked with Galileo in his last years, wrote this story down about twelve years after Galileo died.  
  Against: Galileo never wrote about it himself, and nobody else at the time mentioned it. Many historians think it is a legend. We know for sure that Simon Stevin dropped lead balls from a church tower in Delft in 1586.
- **Isaac Newton discovered gravity when an apple fell on his head.** (A very popular story; *rejected*)  
  For: Newton himself told a friend, William Stukeley, that watching an apple fall in his garden set him thinking about gravity.  
  Against: Newton never said it hit his head. And people knew things fall long before Newton. His real discovery was that the pull that drops an apple also holds the Moon in the sky. He also worked out the law for it.
- **There is no gravity in space. That is why astronauts float.** (Many people, and some cartoons; *rejected*)  
  For: Astronauts float in the space station, and far out in space the pull of the Earth does get weaker.  
  Against: The space station is only about 400 kilometres up. Gravity there is still about 9 tenths as strong as on the ground. The astronauts float because they and the station are falling round the Earth together. Gravity also holds the Moon, the planets and whole galaxies together, far out in space.
- **Indian astronomers discovered gravity long before Newton.** (Some modern writers and websites; *debated*)  
  For: Brahmagupta wrote, in 628 CE, that the Earth attracts bodies the way water flows. Bhaskara II wrote of the Earth's power of attraction in the 1100s. These were real and clear ideas.  
  Against: They did not give a law for how strong the pull is. They did not show that the same pull keeps the Moon going round. Some claims online go further than what the old books really say.
- **Gravity is a force, like a push or a pull.** (Isaac Newton, and most everyday science; *debated*)  
  For: It works wonderfully. Engineers use Newton's law of gravity to send spacecraft to the Moon and Mars.  
  Against: In Einstein's theory, gravity is not a force at all. Mass bends space and time, and falling things move along the straightest paths through that bent space-time. This also explains the exact bending of light and the slowing of clocks, which Newton's law cannot. Physicists use both pictures, and Einstein's has passed every test so far.
- **In a vacuum, things float, because there is no air to hold them down.** (A common mix-up; *rejected*)  
  For: Films often show things floating around in empty space.  
  Against: Air does not hold things down. Air actually pushes up a little. In a vacuum chamber on Earth, a feather drops like a stone. Things only float in space because they are falling freely.

## Roots
We have been learning why things fall, and why heavy and light things land together. Now we ask what falling tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The Earth pulls a full bottle harder than a nearly empty one, because it has more mass. But more mass is also harder to speed up, like a full trolley. The extra pull and the extra mass cancel exactly, so both bottles land with one thud. With no air in the way, even a hammer and a feather land together, as astronauts showed on the Moon.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things into heavy and light, and expect heavy things to fall faster, because heaviness is what our hands feel most. Falling ignores that difference. Only the air slows some things more than others, and there shape matters as much as weight.  
*Evidence:* In the Drop Race, a full bottle and a bottle with a little water gave one thud, and so did ten coins and one coin. In Paper That Falls Like a Stone, a crumpled sheet beat a flat sheet of the same weight, and a flat sheet lying on a book fell with the book.

- *who am i:* The Earth pulls on every part of you, and you pull on the Earth just as hard.
- *what is life:* A seed planted upside down still sends a root down. Living things sense gravity and grow with it.
- *what is this world:* Without air, a hammer and a feather fall in exactly the same way. Gravity gives everything the same fall.

Gravity does not sort the world into heavy and light. In the same place, a hammer, a feather, a mango and a planet all fall in exactly the same way. Einstein took this seriously. Because every thing falls alike, he said the fall belongs to the shape of space and time, not to the thing that falls. This is a sign that the world is one whole, with one shape that everything in it shares.

- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.8.9:** The thinker Gargi asks what holds everything. Yajnavalkya answers that one unchanging reality holds the Sun and the Moon in their places. (Science names gravity as what keeps the Moon going round the Earth, and the Earth round the Sun. It still does not know what gravity is, deep down. The Upanishad does not describe gravity, but it wondered about the same holding.)
- **Vaisheshika Sutra 5.1.7:** Kanada, an Indian thinker, said that when nothing holds a thing up, it falls because of gurutva, its heaviness. (In the Drop Race, the bottles fell the moment the book was pulled away, with nothing holding them up. Kanada named that cause gurutva. Guru, a teacher, also means 'heavy'.)
- Science (settled): Without air, a heavy thing and a light thing fall together. An astronaut dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon in 1971, and they landed together. A satellite called MICROSCOPE found metals fall the same way to about 1 part in a million billion.
- Science (open): Einstein's idea, that mass bends space and time, has passed every test so far. But what gravity is deep down, and how it fits with quantum physics, nobody knows yet.
- Other view, Aristotle, the Greek thinker: Aristotle taught that heavy and light are two different natures. Earth and water move down towards their natural place, and fire and air move up. On his view, falling shows how things differ, not what they share.
- Practice, Heavy hand, light hand (3 min): Sit on the floor with a cushion in front of you. Hold a steel spoon in one hand and a crumpled paper ball in the other. Close your eyes and feel which hand is heavier. Now hold both over the cushion and open your hands together. Listen for the soft landings. Then put your hands in your lap and breathe slowly three times. Feel your heavy bones and your light hair, all held down by the same gravity.
- Ask: Gravity gives a hammer and a feather the same fall. Why do you think it does not treat them differently?
- Ask: If everything falls the same way, is the fall something the thing does, or something the place does?
- Ask: Your two hands felt a heavy thing and a light thing, but falling treated them the same. What else might feel different to us, yet be the same underneath?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If you dropped a ball down a tunnel right through the Earth, where would it stop? (hint: It speeds up all the way to the middle. Then what? Gravity pulls towards the middle from every side.)
- *deep:* Why does a heavy thing not fall faster, when the Earth pulls it harder? (hint: Think of pushing a full trolley and an empty one. Which is harder to get moving?)
- *deep:* Everything falls in the same way, heavy or light. Is the fall something the thing does, or something the place does? (hint: Einstein thought it belongs to the place. In his picture, the bend in space-time is the same for anything that passes through it.)
- *big:* People believed heavy things fall faster for about 1,900 years. Why did so few people test it? (hint: Is it easy to doubt something that every teacher and every book says?)
- *big:* Few people listened when John Philoponus disagreed with Aristotle. Should we trust an idea because a famous person said it, or because of evidence? (hint: What would you do if everyone in your class believed something you had tested and found wrong?)
- *big:* You attract the Earth, the Moon and every star. Is anything in the universe truly alone? (hint: Every atom in you attracts every atom everywhere, however far away.)
- *big:* Time runs a tiny bit slower downstairs than upstairs. So is there one true time for everyone? (hint: Two very precise clocks, one on a shelf and one on the floor, will slowly disagree.)
- *big:* Is gravity good or bad? It keeps us on the ground and holds the air on Earth. It also makes us fall and get hurt. (hint: Imagine a day with no gravity at all. What would happen to the air, the rain, the rivers and you?)
- *deep:* If light has no mass, why does gravity bend it? (hint: Maybe it is not the light that is pulled. Maybe the path through space is bent.)
- *deep:* A plant in the dark still knows which way is down. What might it be like to feel down without ears or eyes? (hint: The root cells have heavy grains that settle to the bottom. Plants feel what they sense.)
- *big:* The word guru means heavy. Why might a wise person be called heavy? What gives a person weight with others? (hint: Think of someone whose words you always listen to. What makes their words weigh more?)
- *big:* Most of the matter in the universe is invisible. We only know it is there by its gravity. How can we be sure something exists if we can never see it? (hint: You cannot see the wind, but you can see the leaves move.)

## Story: The Weigher's Daughter
*John Philoponus was a real scholar in Alexandria, about 1,500 years ago. He really wrote that two weights, one many times heavier, dropped from the same height, land with only a very small difference in time. Galileo really read his books, a thousand years later. Thekla, her father, the roof and the students are made up. Thekla's argument about tying the weights together was really written by Galileo, in 1638. The story gives it to her.*

My father weighed things for a living. In the great market of Alexandria, near the harbour where the lighthouse stood, he sat under a striped awning with his bronze balance and his wooden box of lead weights. Wheat from up the Nile, jars of olive oil, bolts of linen, black pepper that came on ships from India: everything that was sold crossed his scales first. Everyone trusted them. 'A weigher has only one thing to sell,' he told me. 'It is the truth.'

I was eleven, and I knew every weight in the box by feel. The biggest was as heavy as a small melon, and so cold in the mornings that it made my fingers ache. The smallest was as light as a date stone. The middle one was exactly a tenth of the biggest.

Most evenings, a thin man in a dusty cloak came and sat on the step beside our stall. His name was John. The students from the lecture halls called him Philoponus, which means the Lover of Work. He was a teacher of grammar, of words and sentences, not a famous philosopher. And he had very bad manners. He argued with Aristotle.

Aristotle had been dead for more than eight hundred years, and he was the greatest mind there had ever been. Everyone said so. Aristotle taught that heavy things fall faster than light things. A weight ten times heavier falls ten times faster. Everyone knew that. I knew it. I had seen a stone beat a leaf a thousand times.

'Have you?' said John, when I told him. 'Or have you seen the air hold up a leaf?'

One evening, he asked my father if he could borrow the biggest weight and the middle one. The big one was ten times heavier. 'And may I borrow your daughter, Thekla,' he said, 'for her ears?' My father laughed and said yes to both.

So I climbed the outside stairs of the grain store with the Lover of Work, holding a lead weight in each hand. The roof was flat and warm and white with pigeon droppings. Far below, in the empty yard, John had laid my father's big copper pan face down on the stones.

'Hold them out over the edge,' he said. 'Both at the same height. Let go of both at the same moment. If Aristotle is right, you will hear the big one strike the pan. Then you will hear the small one, a long time after. Count the time between, if you can.'

Down in the street, three students in white had stopped to watch. One of them pointed up at us and said something, and the others laughed. My face went hot. I did not want to be the girl who threw stones off roofs with a man everybody laughed at. I wanted, I am ashamed to say, for the big weight to win quickly, so that we could go down before anyone else saw.

I held my arms out. The weights pulled at my shoulders, the big one much harder than the small. I opened my hands.

Clang.

One clang. Or perhaps two, so close together that I could not pull them apart. It was like the sound of two drums struck by one drummer.

'I let go badly,' I said. 'The big one slipped.' John said nothing. He went down for the weights and brought them up again, slowly, because the stairs were steep and he was not young. I dropped them again. Clang. And again, holding my breath, watching my hands. Clang. Never once did I hear the long wait that Aristotle promised.

A pigeon feather lay on the roof by my foot. I picked it up and dropped it with the small weight. The weight rang on the pan. The feather wandered down on the evening breeze, rocking like a little boat, and settled on the stones long after.

'There,' I said. 'The light thing is slower.'

'The feather is slower,' said John. 'Is it because it is light, or because it is wide and soft, and the air pushes up on it? Try this.' He pulled a thread from his cloak and handed it to me. 'Aristotle says the small weight is slow. Tie it to the big one. What will it do?'

I tied them together. I thought about it, with the evening wind in my ears. The slow small weight should pull back on the big one, like a small brother dragging on your hand. So the pair should fall slower than the big weight alone. But the pair was heavier than the big weight alone. So, by Aristotle, it should fall faster.

'It cannot do both,' I said.

'No,' said John. 'It cannot.'

The students had gone. The sky over the harbour was turning the colour of a ripe pomegranate. I sat on the warm roof with the two weights in my lap, tied together, and I felt something I had never felt before. It was as if the floor of the world had tipped a little. The greatest mind there had ever been had been wrong about something a girl could test with her father's weights in one evening. Nobody had thought to try, for eight hundred years.

John wrote it in his book. He wrote that if you let fall two weights from the same height, one many times heavier, the difference in time is a very small one. Not many people believed him. His book sat on shelves for a thousand years.

Then, in a city called Pisa, a young teacher named Galileo found it, and read it, and wrote the name of John the Grammarian in his own notes. And he went out and rolled balls down ramps, and dropped things, and argued, and wrote books that the whole world read.

That night, before we closed the stall, my father lifted the big weight from my hands and set it back in its place in the box. 'Well?' he said. 'Which one fell faster?'

I thought of the single clang. 'Neither,' I said. 'They fell together. Only the air is unfair to feathers.'

My father looked at me for a long moment, the way he looked at a sack of wheat whose weight surprised him. Then he nodded, and wrote nothing down, and closed the box.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the story turns on the single clang. Heavy and light weights, dropped together, land together. The feather is slower not because it is light, but because the air pushes on it. Thekla's tied-weights puzzle is Galileo's real argument: if heavy things fell faster, a heavy and a light weight tied together would have to fall both slower and faster at once, which is impossible. The story also asks a quieter question. Should we trust an idea because a great person said it, or because we tested it? And who gets remembered: the famous philosopher, the mocked grammarian, or the girl who listened?

- Ask: Thekla, the weigher's daughter who tells the story, let go of two weights. What did she expect to hear? What did she really hear?
- Ask: Why did the feather land long after the small weight? Was it because the feather is light?
- Ask: Thekla tied the two weights together. Why did that show that Aristotle's rule could not be true?
- Ask: Thekla felt ashamed when the students laughed. Have you ever felt that way about an idea you believed?
- Ask: People believed Aristotle for about eight hundred years before John tested him. Why do you think nobody tried sooner?

### e4 · The Falling Ruler: catch it if you can (20 min)
- **question:** How far does a falling ruler drop before you can catch it? Is your writing hand quicker than your other hand?
- **predict:** Guess first. When someone lets go of a ruler between your fingers, how many centimetres will it fall before you catch it? Which hand will catch it sooner, and why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same ruler every time, with the 0 mark level with the top of your thumb. The helper lets go without warning. Change only one thing: the hand you catch with. Swap hands after every try, so that practice helps both hands the same. Do 3 tries with each hand, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Use the chart in step 4 to turn each distance into a time.
- **results table:** Hand | Try 1 (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rests their arm on the table, with their hand over the edge. 2. They open their thumb and first finger a little. 3. Hold the ruler at the top, hanging down between their thumb and finger. The 0 mark should be level with the top of the thumb.  
  *Parent says:* I'll hold the ruler. You keep your fingers open, ready. Don't look at my hand. Watch the ruler.  
  *Child mode:* Rest your arm on the table. Open your thumb and finger a little. The ruler hangs between them. Watch the ruler, not the helper.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let go of the ruler without any warning. 2. Your child catches it as fast as they can. 3. Read the number at the top of their thumb and write it down. 4. Do 6 tries in all, swapping hands each time: writing hand, other hand, writing hand, other hand, writing hand, other hand. Write each number in the row for that hand.  
  *Parent says:* Catch it as fast as you can! Now read the number at the top of your thumb. That's how far it fell. Now try your other hand.  
  *Child mode:* Catch it as fast as you can! Read the number at your thumb. Write it down. Swap hands each time, 6 tries in all.
  - Note: Wait a different time before each drop, so your child cannot guess when it will come. Most children catch it between 15 and 30 centimetres. If the ruler slips right through, write 'more than 30' and try again.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the middle value for each hand. Is one hand quicker?  
  *Parent says:* Which hand caught the ruler after the shorter fall? Was it quicker?  
  *Child mode:* Find the middle number for each hand. Which hand let the ruler fall less?
  - Note: With only 3 tries per hand, a small difference could be luck. Many children find no clear difference between their hands. That is a real result too.
  - Check: Why does the helper let go of the ruler without any warning? → **So you cannot guess the moment. Then the distance shows how quick you really are**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Use the chart to turn the distances into times.  
  *Parent says:* The ruler speeds up as it falls. In one tenth of a second, it falls 5 centimetres. In two tenths, 20 centimetres. So 20 centimetres means you took about two tenths of a second to catch it.  
  *Child mode:* The ruler speeds up as it falls. 5 cm means one tenth of a second. 20 cm means two tenths. How quick were you?
  - Note: Your eyes see the ruler move. A message runs along nerves to your brain. Your brain sends a message to your fingers. All of that takes time. That time is called reaction time.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: my writing hand is a little quicker. What I saw: the ruler fell 15 cm with my writing hand and 19 cm with my other hand. Why: the ruler always falls the same way, so a shorter fall means I caught it sooner.

### Falling Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Many people think gravity pulls only on heavy things. A girl lets go of a feather, and the feather drifts slowly down. What does that show?

   **Answer:** Gravity pulls the feather too. The air just slows the feather down. *Why:* The feather does come down, so something pulls it down. That is gravity, which pulls on everything with mass. The air pushes up on the wide, soft feather, so it falls slowly. Picking 'the wind' is a fair guess, but a feather falls indoors too, where there is no wind.
2. (draw) Draw the round Earth with four people standing on it: one on top, one at the bottom and one on each side. Draw an arrow from each person to show which way is down for them.  
   **Answer:** Four people round a circle, each with their feet on the Earth. Four arrows, all pointing to the middle of the circle.. *Why:* Down always means towards the middle of the Earth, wherever you stand. Nobody on the Earth is really upside down.

### Gravity Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) The Earth pulls a full bottle of water twice as hard as a half-full one. So why doesn't the full bottle fall faster?  
   Options: The full bottle also has twice the mass, so it is twice as hard to speed up / Gravity pulls everything with the same force / The air always balances it out / The full bottle does fall twice as fast  
   **Answer:** The full bottle also has twice the mass, so it is twice as hard to speed up. *Why:* Think of pushing a full trolley and an empty one. The full one needs a bigger push to speed up. The extra pull on the full bottle is used up exactly by its extra mass. So both speed up in the same way. Picking 'the same force' is a common mistake: the pull is bigger on the heavier bottle.
2. (perspective) Everyone now agrees that heavy and light things fall together, if the air does not get in the way. But who should get the credit? Historians argue about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a person and says what counts most: being first, doing a clear test, or explaining it so that everyone understood. It answers back fairly, for example that Galileo used Philoponus's idea, or that Philoponus's book went unread for a thousand years.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Science is built by many people, and credit often goes to the one who explains it best, not always the first.
3. (truefalse) Starlight passing close to the Sun goes perfectly straight, because light has no mass.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Light has no mass, but it still follows the bends in space and time near a big mass. In 1919, astronomers photographed stars near the Sun during an eclipse. The stars looked shifted, as Einstein had predicted.
4. (mcq) Two very exact clocks are set to the same time. One goes on a high shelf. One goes on the floor. After many years, which clock shows the later time?  
   Options: The shelf clock, very slightly / The floor clock, very slightly / They always stay exactly together  
   **Answer:** The shelf clock, very slightly. *Why:* Time runs a tiny bit slower closer to a big mass, like the Earth. So the floor clock ticks slower and falls behind. The difference is far too small to feel, but the best clocks can measure it. Picking 'exactly together' is what everyone thought before Einstein.
5. (write) GPS satellites, which help maps on phones, fly about 20,000 kilometres above the Earth. Their clocks run a little fast compared with clocks on the ground. What would happen to phone maps if engineers forgot about this?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the satellites' clocks would slowly drift ahead of ground clocks. The maps use exact times to work out where you are. So the maps would get more and more wrong, by kilometres every day.. *Why:* Clocks higher up, further from the Earth's mass, really do run faster. Engineers allow for it every day.
6. (mcq) How fast would you need to jump to leave the Earth for ever and never fall back?  
   Options: About 11 kilometres every second / About as fast as a good runner / No speed is ever enough  
   **Answer:** About 11 kilometres every second. *Why:* That is the Earth's escape speed, far faster than a bullet. Rockets reach it by burning fuel. Picking 'no speed is enough' misses that spacecraft really have left the Earth, like India's Mangalyaan, which went to Mars.
7. (mcq) A small magnet lifts a paper clip off the table, even though the whole Earth is pulling the clip down. What does that tell us?  
   Options: Gravity is a very weak force. It only feels strong because the Earth is so huge / Magnets switch gravity off / Paper clips have no mass / The magnet is heavier than the Earth  
   **Answer:** Gravity is a very weak force. It only feels strong because the Earth is so huge. *Why:* Magnetism is far stronger than gravity. Gravity feels strong only because the Earth has so much mass. Why gravity is so weak is still a mystery. Picking 'magnets switch gravity off' is a fair guess, but the clip still has weight while the magnet holds it.
8. (write) The space blanket makes a dent because the Earth's gravity pulls the heavy ball down. Why is that a problem, if we are using the blanket to explain gravity? Name one more way it is not like real space.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the model uses gravity to explain gravity, so it cannot show where gravity comes from. It also says one more difference, for example that real space bends in every direction at once, not just downwards like a sheet, or that time bends too.. *Why:* Every model is useful and also wrong in some way. Knowing how a model is wrong is part of using it well.
9. (write) Your arm got tired holding a bag still. A door handle held the same bag for an hour and never got tired. What does this tell you about the Earth holding the Moon?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says holding something still needs a force, but a force does not use up energy by itself. My arm got tired because muscles use energy just to stay tight. The Earth's gravity has held the Moon for billions of years without running down.. *Why:* A force is a push or a pull. Gravity is a force that never gets tired.
10. (fill) A bean seed is planted upside down in a jar. The seed sends a root out. Even upside down, the root turns and grows ____.  
   **Answer:** ['down', 'downwards']. *Why:* Plants feel gravity. Heavy grains in the tip of the root settle to the lowest side, and the root grows that way. Charles Darwin and his son Francis studied this in 1880. How plants sense down is still being studied.
11. (mcq) Many people think astronauts float because there is no gravity in space. But gravity at the space station is about 9 tenths as strong as on the ground. So why do astronauts float?  
   Options: They and the station are falling round the Earth together, so nothing pushes on their feet / The station has a machine that switches gravity off / They are lighter than air / The Sun pulls them up  
   **Answer:** They and the station are falling round the Earth together, so nothing pushes on their feet. *Why:* The station moves sideways so fast that it keeps falling round the Earth and missing it. The astronauts fall with it, so the floor never pushes on their feet. Picking 'no gravity' is the most common myth about space.
12. (write) A grown-up makes small holes near the bottom of a plastic bottle full of water, and water squirts out. Then she drops the bottle. While it falls, the squirting stops. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the water and the bottle are falling together. The water no longer presses down on the bottom of the bottle, so it does not push out of the holes. It is the same reason astronauts float.. *Why:* Things falling together do not press on each other. Einstein called this idea the happiest thought of his life.
13. (mcq) Imagine a tunnel straight through the Earth, with no air inside. A ball is dropped in. What happens?  
   Options: It speeds up to the middle, shoots past, just reaches the other side, then falls back / It stops at the middle and stays there / It falls out of the other side into space  
   **Answer:** It speeds up to the middle, shoots past, just reaches the other side, then falls back. *Why:* The ball speeds up all the way to the middle. Then it is moving so fast that it shoots past. After the middle, gravity pulls it back, so it slows and stops just as it reaches the other side. Then it swings back like a pendulum. Picking 'stops at the middle' forgets how fast it is going there.
14. (write) Vera Rubin, an American astronomer, found that stars at the edges of galaxies move too fast for what we can see. Scientists think an invisible 'dark matter' pulls on them. How can we know something is there if we can never see it?  
   **Answer:** A thoughtful answer says we can see what it does. It pulls on stars, the way you cannot see the wind but can see leaves move. A strong answer may add that scientists still argue about what it is.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Scientists still argue about dark matter.
15. (truefalse) Scientists can land spacecraft on the Moon using gravity, so they know exactly what gravity is, deep down.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Scientists know how gravity behaves very well. But nobody yet knows what it is, deep down. Some think it is carried by tiny particles called gravitons, but nobody has found one.
16. (draw) Draw Galileo's Ramp with the marble's place after beats 1, 2, 3 and 4. Show how the gaps between the marks change.  
   **Answer:** A sloping ramp with four marks. The gap from the start to the first mark is small, and each gap after that is bigger than the one before.. *Why:* The marble speeds up, so it covers more ground in each beat.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Why does a raindrop fall to the ground?  
   Options: Gravity pulls it towards the middle of the Earth / The cloud pushes it out / The wind blows it down  
   **Answer:** Gravity pulls it towards the middle of the Earth. *Why:* Gravity pulls everything with mass towards the middle of the Earth, even a tiny raindrop. Wind can blow raindrops sideways, but gravity brings them down.
2. (mcq) Does gravity pull on you while you are asleep?  
   Options: Yes. It never switches off / No. It stops when you lie still / Only at night  
   **Answer:** Yes. It never switches off. *Why:* Gravity acts all the time, on everything. It holds you on your bed while you sleep.
3. (mcq) In the Drop Race, you drop a stack of ten coins and one coin together from a book. What happens?  
   Options: They land together / The ten coins land long before / The one coin lands first  
   **Answer:** They land together. *Why:* Both are compact, so the air hardly slows them. Without much air in the way, heavy and light fall together.
4. (mcq) In a fair test of falling, what should you change?  
   Options: Only the thing you drop / The thing and the height / Everything  
   **Answer:** Only the thing you drop. *Why:* Change one thing and keep the rest the same. Then you know what caused any difference.
5. (mcq) A skydiver opens her big parachute. What happens next?  
   Options: She slows down, because the air pushes up on the parachute much more / She stops falling / She falls faster  
   **Answer:** She slows down, because the air pushes up on the parachute much more. *Why:* The wide parachute pushes against lots of air, and the air pushes back up. Gravity still pulls, so she keeps falling, just slowly.
6. (mcq) Which of these is slowed most by the air as it falls?  
   Options: A flat sheet of paper / A crumpled ball of the same paper / A steel coin  
   **Answer:** A flat sheet of paper. *Why:* The flat sheet is light and spread out, so it pushes against the most air for its weight.
7. (mcq) Who said, about 2,350 years ago, that heavy things fall faster?  
   Options: Aristotle / Galileo / Newton  
   **Answer:** Aristotle. *Why:* Aristotle, a Greek thinker, said it. For about 1,900 years, few people tested it carefully.
8. (mcq) Falling was too fast for Galileo to measure. How did he slow it down?  
   Options: He rolled balls down long, gentle ramps / He dropped things in water / He tied them to balloons  
   **Answer:** He rolled balls down long, gentle ramps. *Why:* A gentle ramp keeps the same speeding-up pattern, but slower. So he could time it with a water clock.
9. (mcq) Did Galileo really drop balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa?  
   Options: Historians are not sure. It may be a legend / Yes, everyone saw it and wrote about it / No, he lived nowhere near Pisa  
   **Answer:** Historians are not sure. It may be a legend. *Why:* The story comes from his student, written years later. Galileo himself never wrote about it.
10. (mcq) The Earth pulls on the Moon. Does the Moon pull on the Earth too?  
   Options: Yes, just as hard. The Moon's pull lifts the seas into tides / No. A small thing cannot pull a big thing / Only when the Moon is full  
   **Answer:** Yes, just as hard. The Moon's pull lifts the seas into tides. *Why:* Every pull between two things is shared, so the Moon pulls the Earth just as hard as the Earth pulls the Moon. The Earth has much more mass, so it hardly moves. But the Moon's pull is enough to lift the seas a little, and that makes the tides.
11. (mcq) In the space blanket, why did marbles roll towards the heavy ball?  
   Options: They rolled down into the dent it made in the sheet / The ball pulled them with a rope / The marbles were magnetic  
   **Answer:** They rolled down into the dent it made in the sheet. *Why:* The heavy ball bends the sheet. This is a model of Einstein's idea that mass bends space.
12. (mcq) You jump off a low wall. While you are in the air, how do you feel?  
   Options: Almost weightless, because you are falling / Twice as heavy / Exactly as you feel standing still  
   **Answer:** Almost weightless, because you are falling. *Why:* While falling, nothing pushes on your feet, so you feel almost no weight. Astronauts feel this all the time.
13. (mcq) A heavy wooden ruler and a light plastic ruler are dropped the same way. Which falls further in one tenth of a second?  
   Options: They fall the same distance, about 5 cm / The heavy one / The light one  
   **Answer:** They fall the same distance, about 5 cm. *Why:* Heavy and light things fall together when the air hardly slows them. Both rulers are thin and compact.
14. (mcq) What did astronomers see during the eclipse of 1919?  
   Options: Starlight bending as it passed the Sun / Stars falling out of the sky / The Sun stopping  
   **Answer:** Starlight bending as it passed the Sun. *Why:* The stars near the dark Sun looked shifted. This showed that gravity bends light, as Einstein said.
15. (mcq) How does your body know which way is down?  
   Options: Tiny stones in your ears slide and bend tiny hairs / Your eyes see the ground / Blood flows to your feet  
   **Answer:** Tiny stones in your ears slide and bend tiny hairs. *Why:* Inside your ears, tiny chalky stones rest on hairs. When you tip your head, they slide, and your brain knows which way is down, even with your eyes shut.
16. (mcq) A galaxy can bend the light of galaxies far behind it. What do telescopes see?  
   Options: The far galaxies stretched into arcs and rings / Nothing at all / The far galaxies turned upside down  
   **Answer:** The far galaxies stretched into arcs and rings. *Why:* Gravity bends light, so a big galaxy acts like a lens. Scientists call this gravitational lensing.

## Applications
- Masons and builders use a plumb line, called a sahul in many Indian languages, to build straight walls and pillars.
- Water tanks on rooftops use gravity to push water down to the taps, with no pump needed.
- Hydroelectric dams, like Bhakra Nangal and Idukki, use falling water to make electricity.
- Parachutes slow down skydivers, soldiers, supply drops and even spacecraft returning to Earth.
- Pendulum clocks keep time because gravity swings the pendulum at a steady beat.
- GPS satellite clocks are set to allow for gravity's effect on time, or phone maps would drift off by kilometres each day.
- ISRO launches rockets from Sriharikota, burning fuel to fight gravity and reach orbit.
- India's Aditya-L1 spacecraft studies the Sun from near a Lagrange point, where it stays in step with the Earth.
- Spacecraft use the pull of planets to speed up and change direction for free. This is called a gravity assist, or slingshot.
- Engineers test helmets, phone cases and car parts by dropping them from measured heights.
- Plants use air resistance to spread their seeds. Engineers copy winged seeds to design drones.
- Astronomers use galaxies as giant lenses, because gravity bends light, to see even further away.
- Scientists measure tiny differences in gravity to find heavy rock, water and oil deep underground.
- Doctors and space agencies study how bones and muscles weaken in free fall, to keep astronauts healthy.
- Very precise clocks can now measure height differences of a few centimetres, from how fast they tick.

## Open questions
- **What is gravity, deep down?** Scientists can predict exactly how gravity behaves. But nobody knows what it is made of, or why mass bends space and time at all.
- **Do gravitons exist?** Some physicists think gravity is carried by tiny particles called gravitons. They would be so weak that no machine we know how to build could catch one.
- **How do gravity and quantum physics fit together?** Einstein's theory works for planets and stars. Quantum physics works for atoms. Inside black holes, and at the very start of the universe, they disagree. Nobody has a theory that joins them.
- **Why is gravity so much weaker than the other forces?** A small magnet beats the whole Earth's pull on a paper clip. Nobody knows why gravity is so weak.
- **What is dark matter?** Galaxies spin as if there is much more matter in them than we can see. We know it only by its pull. Nobody has caught a particle of it, and a few scientists think our law of gravity is what needs changing.
- **Does gravity travel instantly, or does it take time?** Scientists now think gravity's changes travel at the speed of light. In 2017, gravitational waves and light from the same crash of two dead stars arrived almost together. But how gravity really 'travels' is still not fully understood.
- **Is anything ever completely free of gravity?** The pull of every star and galaxy reaches everywhere, getting weaker with distance but never quite zero. Even the emptiest parts of space feel a tiny pull.
- **Exactly how do plants sense which way is down?** Heavy starch grains in root tips play a big part. But plants with almost no starch grains can still find down, so scientists think there is more to it.

## Connected maths
- In the Parachute Test, the small parachute took 2 seconds to fall, the medium one 3 seconds and the large one 5 seconds. Look at the chart. How much longer did the large one take than the small one? And how many seconds did all three take, added together? **3 seconds longer. 10 seconds altogether.**. The large one took 5 seconds and the small one took 2 seconds. 5 take away 2 is 3. So the large one took 3 seconds longer. Altogether: 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 seconds. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- On Galileo's Ramp, the marble had gone 10 cm after 1 beat, 40 cm after 2 beats and 90 cm after 3 beats. Look at the chart. Spot the pattern. How far will it have gone after 4 beats? **160 cm.**. Divide each distance by 10: 1, 4, 9. These are 1 × 1, 2 × 2 and 3 × 3. They are called square numbers. The next one is 4 × 4 = 16. Then multiply back by 10: 16 × 10 = 160 cm. Another way: the gaps are 10, 30, 50. Each gap is 20 more than the last. So the next gap is 70. 90 + 70 = 160. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- A girl jumped three times. Her jumps were 22 cm, 27 cm and 25 cm high. Look at the chart. What was her best jump? How much higher was it than her lowest jump? **Her best jump was 27 cm. It was 5 cm higher than her lowest jump.**. Put the jumps in order: 22, 25, 27. The biggest is 27 cm. The smallest is 22 cm. 27 take away 22 is 5. So the best jump was 5 cm higher. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- In the Drop Race, a family did 12 drops. They heard one thud 10 times and two thuds 2 times. Make a tally chart. Then answer: what does the result tell you? **One thud: 10. Two thuds: 2. Most drops landed together, so heavy and light things fall together. The few two-thud drops were probably uneven starts.**. Draw tally marks in groups of five. One thud: two groups of five, so 10. Two thuds: two marks, so 2. 10 + 2 = 12, so every drop is counted. 10 out of 12 is most of them. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- In A Force That Never Gets Tired, you and a grown-up pull a dupatta, and nobody moves. You pull with a force of 4. How big must the grown-up's force be? Then you pull with a force of 6. What must the grown-up's force be now, to keep everything still? **4, and then 6.**. Everything stays still only when the two forces are equal and opposite. So your force of 4 needs a force of 4 from the grown-up: 4 = 4. When your force grows to 6, the grown-up's must also be 6. If the grown-up kept pulling with only 4, the dupatta would slide 2 towards your side, because 6 take away 4 is 2. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L3`)
