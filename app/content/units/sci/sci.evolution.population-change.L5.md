# Chance, mixing and new species: how whole populations change
`sci.evolution.population-change.L5`

**Summary.** In one forest in Odisha, many tigers have wide black stripes that almost hide the orange. Scientists have found no wild tigers like this anywhere else, and the dark coat does not seem to help them. So why is it common there? In this lesson you shake beans in a steel tumbler and watch a colour take over a small group by chance alone. You find out how a few travellers keep two groups alike. You also find out how groups that stop mixing become new species.

**Intro.** *Natural selection makes useful features common. But some features are common in one place and do no good at all. If nothing chose them, how did they spread?* You may already know natural selection. Members whose features suit the place leave more young, so those features spread. That is one way a population changes. This lesson is about three other ways. First you draw beans from a tumbler with your eyes shut, and find out what chance does to a small group. Then you compare a group of 8 with a group of 40. You pass a narrow bottleneck, and read the real case of the black tigers of Similipal. You open a bridge between two islands of beans and send one traveller across in each round. Last, you lay out paper chromosomes and find out how the wheat in a chapati became a new species in one step.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can explain genetic drift, using my results from the Lucky Tumbler Test.
1. By the end I can explain why chance changes a small population fast and a large one slowly.
2. By the end I can describe a bottleneck and the founder effect, with one real example of each.
3. By the end I can explain gene flow, and how a few travellers keep two populations alike.
4. By the end I can name four kinds of barrier that keep two species from mixing.
5. By the end I can explain how doubled chromosomes make a new plant species in one step.
6. By the end I can say what evidence shows that a common feature is useful, and not common by chance.

## Terms
- **Population**: A population is all the living beings of one kind who live in one place and can breed with each other. *e.g.* All the tigers of the Similipal forest in Odisha are one population. All the house crows of Kochi are another.
- **Gene version (allele)**: A gene is one instruction in DNA. Many genes come in more than one version. Scientists call each version an allele. *e.g.* In tigers, one gene shapes the stripes. One version gives narrow stripes. Another version gives wide stripes that run together.
- **Share (frequency)**: The share of a gene version is the fraction of all the copies in a population that are that version. Scientists call it the frequency. *e.g.* If 4 beans out of 8 are dark, the share of dark is 4 out of 8, which is one half.
- **Chance**: Something happens by chance when nothing about the members decides the result. Nobody can say beforehand which way it will go. *e.g.* When you draw beans with your eyes shut, colour plays no part in which beans you draw.
- **Genetic drift**: Genetic drift is a change in the shares of gene versions that happens by chance alone. No version is better than another, yet the shares move. *e.g.* In the Lucky Tumbler Test, nobody chooses by colour. The share of dark beans still moves up and down from round to round.
- **Fixed and lost**: A gene version is fixed when every member of the population has it. A version is lost when no member has it any more. *e.g.* When all 8 beans in the tumbler are dark, dark is fixed and pale is lost.
- **Bottleneck**: A bottleneck happens when a large population is cut down to a few survivors. The population that grows back comes only from those few. *e.g.* Hunters left about 20 northern elephant seals alive by 1892. All the seals of today descend from them.
- **Founder effect**: The founder effect happens when a few members start a new population. The new population has only the gene versions those few carried. *e.g.* If three cats reach an island and one has a bent tail, bent tails can become common there.
- **Inbreeding**: Inbreeding is breeding between close relatives. In a very small population, every possible mate is a relative. *e.g.* A tiger population of a few dozen, cut off from other forests, has only cousins to choose from.
- **Gene flow**: Gene flow is the movement of gene versions from one population to another. It happens when a member moves and has young in the new place. *e.g.* A young male tiger walks from Kanha to Pench and fathers cubs there. His gene versions have moved with him.
- **Migrant**: A migrant is a member who moves from one population to another and stays to breed. *e.g.* In the Bridge Test, the one bean that crosses to the other island in each round is a migrant.
- **Wildlife corridor**: A wildlife corridor is a strip of forest or wild land that joins two larger wild places. Animals use the corridor to move between them. *e.g.* Forest corridors up to about 200 kilometres long join the Kanha and Pench tiger reserves in central India.
- **Genetic rescue**: Genetic rescue means bringing a few members from another population into a small, inbred one. They bring gene versions the small group had lost. *e.g.* In 1995, eight female pumas from Texas were released among the last Florida panthers.
- **Species**: A species is a group of living beings who can breed with each other and have young who can also have young. This meaning works for most animals, though not for all of life. *e.g.* A horse and a donkey can have a mule, but a mule cannot have young. So horses and donkeys are two species.
- **Speciation**: Speciation is the forming of a new species. One species becomes two when two of its populations can no longer breed together. *e.g.* Snapping shrimps on the two sides of Panama were one species before the land rose between the two seas.
- **Reproductive barrier**: A reproductive barrier is anything that stops two groups from having young together who can breed. It may be a sea, a season, a song or the chromosomes. *e.g.* Two kinds of firefly can live in one field and never mix, because each answers only the flash pattern of their own kind.
- **Hybrid**: A hybrid is the young of parents from two different species or two very different populations. *e.g.* A mule is a hybrid of a female horse and a male donkey.
- **Chromosome doubling (polyploidy)**: Chromosome doubling happens when a cell copies all the chromosomes but does not divide. The new cell has twice as many sets. Scientists call a being with extra sets a polyploid. *e.g.* Bread wheat has six sets of chromosomes, which came from three different wild grasses.
- **Neutral change**: A neutral change is a change in DNA that makes no difference to how well a living being survives or has young. *e.g.* Many one-letter changes in DNA leave the protein exactly as before. Only chance decides whether such a change spreads.

## Explanations
### A population is counted in shares, not in single members · *defined*
**Definition:** A population changes when the shares of its gene versions change. A share is the fraction of all the copies that are one version.

One tiger never changes her stripes. So to find out how tigers change, scientists do not watch one tiger. They count the gene versions in a whole population. Think of one gene with two versions. Count every copy of that gene in the population, and find what fraction is each version. Those fractions are the shares. If the shares are the same in the next generation, the population has not changed for that gene. If the shares have moved, the population has evolved a little. In 1908 the English mathematician G. H. Hardy and the German doctor Wilhelm Weinberg each showed something important. The shares stay the same if four things hold. The population is very large. No version helps survival. Nobody arrives or leaves. No new mutation appears. Real populations break these rules all the time, so real shares keep moving. This lesson is about what moves them.
- e.g. In the Lucky Tumbler Test, you begin with 4 dark beans and 4 pale beans. The share of dark is 4 out of 8, which is one half.
- e.g. Among 12 tigers tested in Similipal, 58 of every 100 copies of the stripe gene were the wide-stripe version. Among 395 wild tigers from other forests, none were.
- e.g. A population can change a great deal in shares while looking the same from outside. Many gene versions change nothing that we can see.
### Genetic drift: change by chance · *defined*
**Definition:** Genetic drift is a change in the shares of gene versions that happens by chance. Nothing about the versions causes it.

In every generation, some members leave many young and some leave none. Sometimes the reason is a feature, and that is natural selection. But often the reason has nothing to do with any feature. A branch falls on one nest and not on the next. A flood takes the eggs on the low bank. One seed lands on rock and another on soil. Now ask why this changes the shares. The members who leave young are only a sample of the population, and a sample rarely matches the whole exactly. So the next generation begins with slightly different shares. Then the same thing happens again, starting from the new shares. Nothing pulls the shares back to where they began, because the population keeps no record of the old numbers. The small changes add up, the way a person wanders who takes each step in a direction picked by a coin. One more thing about the word chance. It does not mean that nothing caused the branch to fall. It means that the cause had nothing to do with the gene versions. The American biologist Sewall Wright worked out the mathematics of this in 1931.
- e.g. In Four From Eight, you draw 4 beans from 4 dark and 4 pale with your eyes shut. About half the time you do not get 2 and 2.
- e.g. In the Lucky Tumbler Test, the dark count on one island may go 4, 6, 6, 8. Nobody looked at colour, and dark still took over.
- e.g. In 1956 Peter Buri kept 107 small populations of fruit flies. Each began with two versions of an eye-colour gene in equal shares. After 19 generations, more than half the populations had lost one version or the other.
### Why chance is strong in a small group and weak in a large one · *defined*
**Definition:** The smaller a population is, the faster genetic drift changes its shares. In a very large population, drift is slow.

Toss a coin 4 times. Getting 3 heads would not surprise you, and that is three quarters heads. Now toss the coin 400 times. Getting 300 heads would be astonishing. In a big sample the lucky and unlucky results nearly cancel out. In a small sample they do not. A population works the same way. When only 4 members become parents, one lucky dark bean moves the share a long way. When 20 become parents, one lucky bean hardly shows. So a small island can lose a version within a few generations, while a large population keeps both for a very long time. What counts is the number who actually breed. Scientists call this the effective population size, and it is often far smaller than the number of animals you can count. In a herd where only a few males father all the calves, the breeding number is small even if the herd is large.
- e.g. In the Lucky Tumbler Test, most islands of 8 beans lose one colour within 10 rounds. A group of 40 beans almost never loses a colour in 5 rounds.
- e.g. A drop of pond water can hold millions of bacteria. In a population that size, drift moves the shares very slowly.
- e.g. The last wild Asiatic lions all live in and around one forest in Gujarat. A population in one small place is where drift works fastest.
### Drift removes variety, and chance does not bring it back · *defined*
**Definition:** When drift takes a gene version down to zero, that version is lost from the population. Only a new mutation or a migrant can bring it back.

Watch what happens at the ends of the Lucky Tumbler Test. While an island has both colours, the count can go up or down. Once the island is all dark, every survivor is dark and every young is dark. The count can never move again. So drift has two results that seem opposite. Different islands become different from each other, because each loses different versions. And each island becomes more alike inside, because versions keep dropping out. Variety is what natural selection works with. A population with few versions has less to draw on when the weather, the food or a disease changes. This is one reason a small, cut-off population is at risk even when every member looks healthy.
- e.g. In made-up results for four islands of 8 beans, two islands ended all dark, one ended all pale and one still had both colours after 10 rounds.
- e.g. Northern elephant seals now number more than 200,000. Their DNA still shows very little variety, because all of them descend from about 20 seals.
- e.g. In the Narrow Neck, 40 beans of 5 kinds pass through a bottleneck of 5 survivors. Most often only 3 kinds are left.
### Bottlenecks: a population squeezed through a few survivors · *defined*
**Definition:** A bottleneck is a sharp fall in the size of a population. The few survivors carry only part of the variety that the population had.

A storm, a disease, a drought or hunting can cut a population down to a handful. The survivors are a small sample, so they carry only some of the gene versions. Which versions get through is mostly chance. When the population grows back, its numbers may recover fully, but the lost versions do not return. A bottleneck also leaves relatives breeding with relatives. Many harmful gene versions do no damage when a body has one copy, and do damage only when a body has two. Relatives often carry the same rare version, so their young more often get two copies. That is why inbred populations tend to have more illness and fewer healthy young.
- e.g. People hunted northern elephant seals for the oil in their fat. By 1892 only about 20 were left. Today there are more than 200,000, all descended from those few.
- e.g. Around 1775 a typhoon struck Pingelap, a small island in the Pacific Ocean. About 20 people lived through it. Today roughly 1 islander in every 10 or 20 sees no colour at all. Elsewhere this condition is very rare.
- e.g. By the early 1990s fewer than 30 Florida panthers were left. Many had heart defects and bent tail tips, which are signs of inbreeding.
### The founder effect, and the black tigers of Similipal · *defined*
**Definition:** The founder effect is drift at the start of a new or cut-off population. A few founders carry a small sample of gene versions, and a rare version among them can become common.

Similipal is a tiger reserve in Odisha. Many of its tigers have black stripes so wide that they run together and cover much of the orange. In 2021, Vinay Sagar, Uma Ramakrishnan and their team at the National Centre for Biological Sciences in Bengaluru found the cause. It is a change of one letter in a gene called Taqpep. A tiger looks this way only with two copies of the changed version, one from each parent. In Similipal the changed version made up more than half of all the copies they found. Among 395 wild tigers from other forests, the team found no copies at all. Does the dark coat help a tiger in Similipal? The team found no sign that it does. The Similipal tigers are few, and they are cut off from other tiger forests. In such a group a rare version can become common by chance, helped by relatives breeding with relatives. This is the founder effect together with drift.
- e.g. In the Founders' Boat, 2 beans out of 50 on the mainland are speckled. When a speckled bean is among the 5 founders, speckled beans are at least 5 times more common on the island.
- e.g. The sample from Similipal was only 12 tigers. So the share of 58 in 100 is a rough figure, and the team said so.
- e.g. The team first traced the changed version through families of black tigers living in Indian zoos, where the parents of every cub are known.
### Gene flow: gene versions travel between populations · *defined*
**Definition:** Gene flow is the movement of gene versions from one population into another. It makes populations more alike.

Animals walk, fly and swim. Seeds ride on wind, water and fur. Pollen travels on bees and on the wind. Whenever a traveller has young in the new place, gene versions arrive with them. Gene flow does two things. It brings versions that the population lacked or had lost, so variety goes up. And it makes the two populations more alike, because each now holds some of the other's versions. So gene flow works against drift. Drift pulls populations apart, and gene flow pulls them back together. Notice that a traveller must breed to count. A tiger who crosses a road and is killed before fathering cubs moves no genes.
- e.g. In the Bridge Test, one island starts all dark and the other all pale. One bean crosses each way in every round. After 8 rounds both islands hold both colours.
- e.g. Sandeep Sharma and his team studied the DNA of tigers in four reserves of central India. Reserves still joined by forest corridors, such as Kanha and Pench, had kept their gene flow. Reserves that had lost their corridors had not.
- e.g. Wind carries pollen from one field of maize or bajra to the next. That is gene flow between fields.
### How little mixing is enough, and what genetic rescue can do · *defined*
**Definition:** A very small amount of gene flow is enough to stop two populations from drifting far apart. Bringing in a few migrants can restore a small population's lost variety.

You might think many travellers are needed to keep two populations alike. Sewall Wright's mathematics gave a surprising answer. About one breeding migrant in each generation is enough to keep most gene versions present in both populations. This is true for a herd of 50 and for a herd of 5,000. The reason is that drift is slow in a large group, so a large group needs less mixing to stay the same. In 1996 the biologists Scott Mills and Fred Allendorf checked this rule for wildlife managers. They advised between 1 and 10 migrants per generation, because real populations are messier than the mathematics. The same idea can save a population. In 1995, wildlife managers released eight female pumas from Texas among the last Florida panthers. Five of them had kittens. The panthers have since grown from fewer than 30 to somewhere between 120 and 230.
- e.g. In the Bridge Test, a single bean crossing each way was one eighth of each island. The two islands still came to look alike within about 8 rounds.
- e.g. After the Texas pumas arrived, Florida panther kittens with mixed parents survived better than kittens without.
- e.g. Rescue has a risk. If far too many migrants arrive, the gene versions special to the small population could be swamped. In Florida, studies found that this did not happen.
### Barriers that people make · *defined*
**Definition:** People can stop gene flow without meaning to. A highway, a canal, a town or a custom can divide one population into several small ones.

A four-lane highway is only a few metres wide, yet many animals will not cross, or die trying. So a road can split one forest population into two, and each half then drifts as a smaller group. National Highway 44 passes through the Pench tiger forests of central India. There the road was built with nine crossings beneath it for animals. Camera traps set by the Wildlife Institute of India in 2019 photographed 18 kinds of wild animals using them, including 11 different tigers. Customs can be barriers too, and humans are animals to whom this applies. In India, many communities have married only within the community for dozens of generations. In 2017 Nathan Nakatsuka, Kumarasamy Thangaraj of the Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology in Hyderabad, David Reich and their team studied the DNA of more than 2,800 people from over 260 groups. They found that 81 groups had each grown from a small number of founders. In such a group, a harmful gene version that is rare elsewhere can be common. Knowing this helps doctors look for those versions early.
- e.g. A railway line with fences on both sides can separate the frogs of one pond from the frogs of the next.
- e.g. The nine crossings under National Highway 44 are between 50 and 750 metres wide, so that even shy animals will use them.
- e.g. The 2017 study found no group that was a separate kind of human. It found small groups within one well-mixed species, each with a few versions made common by the founder effect.
### From two populations to two species: the barriers that keep them apart · *defined*
**Definition:** Two populations have become two species when barriers stop them from having young together who can breed. Scientists call this reproductive isolation.

While two populations exchange migrants, new gene versions spread through both, and they stay one species. Now suppose the gene flow stops, perhaps because a sea or a hot lowland lies between them. Each population then changes by itself, through its own mutations, its own drift and its own selection. No one change is large. But after many thousands of generations the two may differ in ways that matter for breeding. Scientists sort these barriers into two kinds. Some barriers act before mating. The two groups breed in different months, or live in different parts of the same place. Their songs, flashes or scents may differ, so that neither answers the other. Their flowers may fit different visitors. Other barriers act after mating. The hybrid young may die early, or be healthy but unable to have young. Most pairs of species are kept apart by several barriers at once. None of these barriers was built for the purpose. They are side effects of two populations changing separately.
- e.g. Time: two kinds of fly may breed in the same orchard weeks apart, so they never meet as adults.
- e.g. Signal: each kind of firefly flashes a pattern of their own. A female answers only the pattern of her kind.
- e.g. Visitor: one monkeyflower in North America is pink and visited by bees. A close relative is red and visited by hummingbirds. Pollen seldom passes between them.
- e.g. After mating: a horse has 64 chromosomes and a donkey has 62. Their mule has 63, an odd number that cannot be shared out evenly, so a mule cannot make working egg or sperm cells.
### A sea that became land: the snapping shrimps of Panama · *defined*
**Definition:** When a barrier forms at a known time, scientists can measure how far the separated populations have moved towards being separate species.

North and South America were once separate, with open sea between them. Slowly the land of Panama rose, and about 3 million years ago it closed the gap. Sea animals on the two sides could no longer meet. The American biologist Nancy Knowlton and her team studied snapping shrimps there, in work published in 1993. They found seven pairs of close relatives, with one member of each pair in the Pacific Ocean and the other in the Caribbean Sea. The team compared the DNA and proteins of each pair. They also put a male from one sea with a female from the other. Snapping shrimps accept a mate of their own kind and fight anyone else. The cross-sea couples fought more, and paired less, than couples from the same sea. So in about 3 million years, one species on each stretch of coast had become two. The pairs were not all equally different, which showed that the sea closed for some shrimps earlier than for others.
- e.g. The pairs that differed most in DNA were also the least willing to accept each other as mates.
- e.g. DNA changes that make no difference to survival pile up at a fairly steady rate. So the number of differences between two species tells roughly how long they have been apart. Scientists call this the molecular clock.
- e.g. The same event that split the sea animals joined the land animals. Animals from the two continents walked across the new land and met.
### When separated populations meet again · *defined*
**Definition:** If two populations that were apart come into contact, three things can happen. They can merge into one, stay fully separate, or form a hybrid zone where they meet.

Barriers do not last for ever. Seas fall, forests spread and rivers change course. What happens next depends on how far the two populations have changed. If they still breed freely and their hybrid young do well, gene flow starts again and the two slowly become one population. If they no longer breed together at all, they live side by side as two species. Often the result lies in between. The two breed where their ranges touch, but the hybrids do a little less well than either parent kind. Then the hybrids stay in a narrow band called a hybrid zone, and the two kinds stay different on each side. One more thing can happen there. If hybrids do poorly, members who choose mates of their own kind leave more young. So selection can make the two kinds choosier over time. Scientists call this reinforcement.
- e.g. Carrion crows are all black and hooded crows are grey and black. They meet in a narrow band across Europe and have hybrid young there. The band has stayed narrow for as long as people have mapped it.
- e.g. In the Bridge Test, two islands that had become different grew alike again once beans could cross. That is merging.
- e.g. Most people living today carry short pieces of Neanderthal DNA. Two human populations that had been apart for a very long time met, and had children together.
### A new species in one step: doubled chromosomes · *defined*
**Definition:** A new plant species can form in a single generation when a hybrid's chromosomes are doubled. The new plant can breed with others like themself, but not with either parent species.

Chromosomes are the long threads of DNA inside a cell, and they come in matching pairs. To make a pollen grain or an egg cell, a plant must separate each pair, so that each new cell gets one of every kind. Now think of a hybrid between two plant species. The hybrid has one set of chromosomes from each parent, and the two sets do not match. With no partners to pair with, the chromosomes are shared out unevenly. Most pollen and egg cells then lack some instructions, and the hybrid has few or no seeds. Sometimes a cell copies all the chromosomes and then fails to divide. Now every chromosome has an exact partner. A plant grown from that cell can make balanced pollen and eggs, and can set seed with others like themself. But a cross back to either parent gives young with unmatched chromosomes again. So the doubled plant is cut off from both parents at once. This is a new species, made in one step. It is common in plants and rare in animals. The description here is simplified, because real chromosomes sometimes pair in more complicated ways.
- e.g. Bread wheat, the wheat of atta and chapati, has 42 chromosomes in six sets. About 9,000 years ago a farmed wheat with 28 chromosomes crossed with a wild goat grass with 14, and the chromosomes doubled.
- e.g. Ragi, or finger millet, has four sets of chromosomes, which came from two different wild grasses.
- e.g. In 1950 the American botanist Marion Ownbey reported two new species of goatsbeard in the states of Washington and Idaho. Their parent species had been brought from Europe only decades before. Each new species had double the chromosomes of the parents.
- e.g. The bananas we eat have three sets of chromosomes. An odd number cannot be shared out evenly, which is why they have no seeds and are grown from shoots.
### Can a new species form with no wall at all? · *debated*
Most new animal species seem to begin with a barrier in space, such as a sea or a mountain range. Can one population split in two while its members still live in the same place? The best-known case is the apple maggot fly of North America. These flies once laid their eggs only in the fruit of wild hawthorn trees. Settlers planted apple trees, and around the middle of the 1800s some flies began to use apples. Apples ripen earlier than hawthorn fruit. Today the apple flies come out earlier in the year, mate on apples and lay on apples. The hawthorn flies do all of this later, on hawthorn. The two groups live in the same countryside and still exchange a few genes. They differ in the shares of several gene versions.
- e.g. The apple flies and hawthorn flies are not yet two species. Scientists call them host races, which means groups part of the way to being species.
- e.g. Chromosome doubling in plants is a case of a new species forming with no wall, and nobody disputes that one.
- **Ernst Mayr, a German-American biologist, writing from the 1940s onwards:** New animal species nearly always need a barrier in space. Without one, gene flow mixes the population again before any split can grow.
- **Guy Bush and Jeffrey Feder, American biologists who studied the apple maggot fly:** A new food plant can act as a barrier. If flies mate on the plant where they feed, and the two plants fruit at different times, the two groups mostly stop meeting.
- **Many biologists today:** Both happen, and the argument is about how often. Some also ask whether the apple flies had help from gene versions that arose long ago in separated populations.
### Chance or selection: which has shaped more of life? · *debated*
Everyone agrees that both drift and natural selection happen. The argument is about how much each has done. It matters most for DNA. When scientists compare the DNA of two species, they find thousands of differences. Are most of those differences useful ones that selection kept? Or did most of them spread by chance? The answer is not the same for every kind of change. For bodies and behaviour that fit a way of life, such as a beak or a wing, selection is the only known cause. For the letters of DNA, the picture is far less settled.
- e.g. Much of the DNA of animals and plants makes no protein. A changed letter there often changes nothing we can measure.
- e.g. Different three-letter DNA words can stand for the same building block of a protein. A change from one such word to another leaves the protein the same.
- **Ronald Fisher, an English mathematician and biologist, in the 1930s:** Populations in nature are large, so chance mostly cancels out. Natural selection decides nearly everything that lasts.
- **Motoo Kimura, a Japanese biologist, in 1968:** Most changes in DNA make no difference to survival. They spread or vanish by drift alone. Selection matters for the few changes that do make a difference. This is the neutral theory.
- **Tomoko Ohta, a Japanese biologist, in 1973:** Many changes are neither neutral nor clearly harmful. They are very slightly harmful. In a large population selection removes them. In a small population drift is stronger than such weak selection, and they can spread.
- **Some biologists who study whole genomes today:** Selection on one gene also drags along the DNA beside that gene. So selection may shape much more of the genome than the neutral theory allows.
### Humans: one well-mixed species · *defined*
**Definition:** All living humans belong to one species. Human populations have never been apart long enough, or completely enough, to become different kinds.

People have always walked, sailed, traded and married across distances. So gene flow has run through the whole human species for as long as our species has existed. In 1972 the American biologist Richard Lewontin measured how human variety is spread. He compared gene versions in populations around the world. He found that about 85 parts in 100 of all human variety can be found inside any one population. Only a small part is a difference between one region's people and another's. Later studies of whole genomes agree. Human groups differ in the shares of gene versions, and not in having different genes. One honest point must be added. With many thousands of DNA letters read together, a scientist can usually tell which part of the world most of a person's ancestors lived in. Small differences in shares, added up over many genes, make that possible. But there is no sharp line anywhere, and no group has a gene that makes them a separate kind.
- e.g. In Which Bowl?, three bowls hold dark and pale beans in slightly different shares. Shown one bean, you can hardly ever tell which bowl the bean came from.
- e.g. Skin colour changes little by little as you travel from the equator towards the poles. It follows sunlight, and tells almost nothing about a person's other genes.
- e.g. Two people from the same village can differ more in their DNA than one of them differs from someone on another continent.
### Following the idea further: atoms, the moving Earth and what nobody knows · *idea*
Follow drift down to the smallest scale. A new gene version begins as a mutation, and a mutation is a change in a few atoms of one DNA letter. Heat makes every atom jiggle. Now and then a letter is in the wrong shape at the moment the DNA is copied, and the wrong partner letter goes in. Radiation from rocks and from space also breaks DNA. In 1963 the Swedish physicist Per-Olov Löwdin proposed something further. A single proton, which is a particle inside the atom, might jump across a pair of DNA letters by a quantum effect called tunnelling, and so cause a mutation. This is an idea that has not been shown in living cells. Now follow gene flow up to the largest scale. The plates of the Earth's crust move a few centimetres a year. Over millions of years they open seas and close them, as in Panama. Ice ages lock water into ice and lower the sea, so that islands join the mainland and part again. So the list of species on Earth depends partly on the slow movement of rock and ice. Some questions are open. Nobody knows how many gene changes it usually takes to make two populations unable to breed. Nobody knows what share of the differences between species came by chance.
- e.g. Each time one of your cells divides, about 6 billion letter pairs of DNA are copied. A few mistakes get past the cell's checking, so every person is born with some dozens of new mutations.
- e.g. The land of Panama rose because one plate of the Earth's crust is sliding under another.
- **Per-Olov Löwdin, a Swedish physicist, in 1963:** Some mutations may begin when a proton tunnels from one DNA letter to its partner. This remains an idea. In physics, such an effect happens whether or not anyone is watching.
- **Most biologists:** Ordinary chemistry is enough to explain nearly all mutations: heat, copying slips, chemicals and radiation.

## Mostly agreed
- Chance alone can change the shares of gene versions in a population, and it has been measured. *How we know:* In 1956 Peter Buri kept 107 small populations of fruit flies for 19 generations. Each began with two versions of an eye-colour gene in equal shares. The shares wandered differently in every bottle, and more than half the bottles lost one version.
- Drift is faster in small populations than in large ones. *How we know:* The mathematics was worked out by Sewall Wright in 1931, and experiments with flies and bacteria give the results he predicted. You can check it at home with 8 beans and with 40 beans.
- A population that has passed through a bottleneck has less genetic variety, even after its numbers recover. *How we know:* Northern elephant seals fell to about 20 animals by 1892. A study of their DNA in 2024 compared old museum specimens with living seals. The living seals had far less variety.
- Gene flow makes populations more alike, and a little of it is enough. *How we know:* Tigers in central Indian reserves that are still joined by forest share more gene versions than tigers in reserves that have been cut off. Florida panthers regained variety after eight pumas were brought from Texas.
- New species form when populations stop exchanging genes for long enough. *How we know:* Seven pairs of snapping shrimp species sit on opposite sides of Panama, which closed about 3 million years ago. Members of a pair from opposite seas are far less willing to accept each other as mates.
- In plants, a new species can form in one generation by chromosome doubling. *How we know:* Scientists can count the chromosomes. Bread wheat has 42, in six sets that match three different wild grasses. Two new goatsbeard species appeared in North America within a few decades, and scientists have since remade them by crossing the parents.

## How we found out
**Sewall Wright, an American biologist. Peter Buri, who ran the fly experiment in the United States. Motoo Kimura and Tomoko Ohta, Japanese biologists., 1931 to 1973. The fly experiment was published in 1956..** In the 1930s Ronald Fisher, an English mathematician, argued that chance hardly matters in evolution. Populations are large, he said, so luck cancels out and natural selection decides nearly everything. Sewall Wright, an American biologist, had worked out in 1931 that chance could change the shares of gene versions in small groups. The two agreed on the mathematics. They disagreed about real animals. In 1956 Peter Buri tested the question with fruit flies. He set up 107 bottles. Each held 8 males and 8 females, and each began with two versions of an eye-colour gene in equal shares. In every generation he picked the 16 parents at random. After 19 generations the bottles looked nothing alike. More than half had lost one version or the other. Selection could not explain it, because the same version won in some bottles and lost in others. Later, Motoo Kimura in 1968 and Tomoko Ohta in 1973 showed that much of the change in DNA spreads in this way.  
*What people believed before:* That chance is too weak to change a population, so every difference between two populations must be there because it helped. Buri's bottles all had the same conditions, and still ended up different.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Every feature of a living being is there because it helps.** (A common habit of thought among biologists and everyone else. Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin argued against it in a well-known paper of 1979.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many features clearly do help, such as a deep beak for hard seeds. Asking 'what is this for?' has led to many discoveries.  
  Against: Buri's flies and the black tigers of Similipal show that a version can become common with no help from selection. Some features are side effects of others. A claim that a feature helps needs its own evidence.
- **Wild populations are so large that drift hardly matters.** (Ronald Fisher, an English mathematician and biologist, in the 1930s; *debated*)  
  For: In a truly huge population, chance effects nearly cancel out, and even a small advantage wins in the end.  
  Against: Many species live as small, partly separate groups. Bottlenecks happen. The number that breed is often far below the number alive.
- **Most of the DNA differences between species spread by chance, without helping or harming.** (Motoo Kimura, a Japanese biologist, in 1968. Tomoko Ohta added very slightly harmful changes in 1973.; *debated*)  
  For: DNA changes that leave a protein unchanged pile up faster than changes that alter the protein. That is what you would expect if most changes that spread are harmless ones.  
  Against: Some whole-genome studies find signs that selection has touched much of the DNA, at least in species with very large populations.
- **Animals cannot split into two species unless a sea, a mountain range or some other wall divides them.** (Ernst Mayr, a German-American biologist, from the 1940s; *debated*)  
  For: Nearly every well-studied pair of young animal species has a barrier in space in their history.  
  Against: Apple maggot flies are dividing in the same orchards, by food plant and season. Fishes in some small crater lakes appear to have split with no barrier.
- **Hybrids are dead ends and play no part in evolution.** (Many zoologists for much of the 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: Many hybrids, such as mules, cannot have young.  
  Against: Bread wheat, ragi and many other plants began as hybrids. Most people alive carry DNA from Neanderthals. Gene versions do sometimes pass between species through hybrids who can breed.
- **A rare population should always be kept pure, and never mixed with outsiders.** (Many conservation workers, especially before the 1990s; *debated*)  
  For: A local population may have features suited to the place. Too many outsiders could swamp those features.  
  Against: Florida panthers were dying out from inbreeding until eight pumas from Texas were added. In late 2024 two tigresses from Maharashtra were moved to Similipal to add variety there.

## Roots
We have learned how chance, mixing and separation change whole populations. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Lucky Tumbler Test, neither colour was better, and nobody looked at colour. Most islands still ended with a single colour, and different islands kept different ones. So a feature can be found in every member of a group without being of any use. Being common shows that a feature spread. It does not show why.  
*Habit it breaks:* We ask 'what is it for?' about every feature of a living being, and accept the first answer that sounds sensible. That habit treats 'common' as if it meant 'useful'. Some features are common only because of who happened to be the parents, long ago.  
*Evidence:* Three of four islands of 8 beans lost a colour within 10 rounds, with no hunter and no advantage. Peter Buri's fly bottles did the same in 1956. In Similipal, wide black stripes became common in a small, cut-off tiger population, and nobody has found that they help.

- *who am i:* Some of what you are helped your ancestors to live. Some of it is there because of who happened to meet, and which egg and sperm happened to join. Nobody can sort every part of you into one pile or the other.
- *what is life:* Life changes in two ways at once. Selection keeps what suits the place, and chance keeps whatever happened to be passed on. Mixing then shares both kinds of change around.
- *what is this world:* No population is a closed box. Tigers, pollen and people carry gene versions from place to place. Where the carrying stops for long enough, a new species begins.

All living beings come from one beginning. That one line became millions of species in a plain way: populations stopped mixing, and each went on changing separately. So the many kinds of life are one family whose branches lost touch with each other. Inside each species the mixing still goes on, and it shares every new version among the members. Much of that mixing is chosen. A young tiger decides to leave and walk, and a firefly decides which flash to answer.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 4 (the oak at the village shrine):** “散木也！以爲舟則沈，以爲棺槨則速腐，以爲器則速毀，以爲門戶則液樠，以爲柱則蠹，是不材之木也，无所可用，故能若是之壽。” A carpenter passes a huge old oak and calls the oak useless, because the wood is good for nothing. That night the oak speaks to him in a dream. The fruit trees are stripped and broken because they are useful, says the oak. Being useless is what let me grow old. (The carpenter judged the oak by what the wood could do for him. This lesson's science shows that a feature needs no use to be there. 'What is it for?' is our question, and nature does not always have an answer to it.)
- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), chapter 2:** Darwin wrote that he looked at the term species as one arbitrarily given, for the sake of convenience, to a set of individuals closely resembling each other. (Species form slowly, as populations stop mixing. So at any moment some groups are part of the way, like the apple flies and the hawthorn flies. The sharp line is in our word more than in the living beings.)
- **Milindapanha, Book 2, chapter 1 (the chariot):** The monk Nagasena asks King Milinda whether the axle is the chariot, or the wheels, or the pole. The king says no each time. 'Chariot', they agree, is a name for the parts working together. (No single tiger is 'the tiger', and no single gene is. A species is a name for members who keep breeding together. When they stop, the name stops fitting.)
- Science (settled): Chance alone changes the shares of gene versions in a population, and does so fastest in small populations.
- Science (settled): In drift, 'chance' means that the cause had nothing to do with the feature. Each death and each birth still had its own causes.
- Science (debated): Most of the DNA differences between species spread by chance, and not because they helped.
- Other view, Richard Dawkins, an English biologist, and others who stress adaptation: They agree that drift changes DNA letters. But they point out that an eye, a wing or a beak is far too complicated and costly to arise by luck. For anything like that, they say, selection is the only explanation we have. So 'what is it for?' remains the right first question about a body, as long as the answer is then tested.

- Ask: Can something about you matter if it has no use?

## Story: The Cats of the Thuruthu
*The island, Nila, Ammachi, Saramma, Thankamma and the cats are all made up, and so are the counts. These things are real. Vembanad Lake in Kerala has many small islands that can be reached only by boat, and 'thuruthu' is a Malayalam word for such an island. Coir rope is made from coconut husk, and rats do gnaw it. Bent or kinked tails in cats are inherited. A study of Asian cats in 2016 found that a kitten with a kinked tail always had at least one parent with a kinked tail.*

The ferry to the thuruthu was a long black canoe, and the woman who poled it never hurried. Nila sat in the middle with her school bag on her knees. Kochi was two buses behind her. Ahead was Ammachi's island, which had forty houses, one shop and no road.

A cat was waiting on the landing stone. She was the colour of milky tea, and her tail had a bend near the tip, like a finger beckoning.

By the end of the first week, Nila had noticed something. The cat at the shop had the same bend. So did the black cat who slept in Ammachi's woodpile, and the three kittens under the church steps. At home in Kochi, Nila fed four cats behind the flats, and every one of them had a tail as straight as a ruler.

"Why do the cats here have bent tails?" she asked Saramma, who sold fish from a basket.

"For the boats," said Saramma at once. "A cat on a canoe edge needs a hook to balance with."

She asked the teacher at the one-room school. "The lake wind," he said. "It blows from the west all year. Things grow the way the wind pushes them."

She asked her cousin Appu. "They get them shut in doors," said Appu, who had once shut his own finger in one.

That made three reasons, and Nila did not see how all three could be right. She took a notebook and walked the whole island, which took an hour. She counted twenty-three cats. Seventeen had the bend.

Then she tested the reasons, one at a time.

The kittens under the church steps were ten days old. Their eyes were barely open, and they had never met a door. Two of the three had the bend already. Nila crossed out Appu's reason.

A white cat near the coconut sheds had a straight tail. She had lived on the island all her life, in the same west wind. Nila crossed out the teacher's reason.

The boats took longer. For three evenings Nila sat on the landing stone and watched the cats come down for fish scraps. They walked along the canoe edges, bent tails and straight tails alike. In three evenings one cat fell in. She had a bend.

Nila crossed out the last reason and looked at her page. Seventeen out of twenty-three, and no reason left.

On the day before she went home, the ferry woman was mending a pole on the bank. Her name was Thankamma. She was older than Ammachi, and she had poled the canoe before the school was built.

"You have been counting our cats," Thankamma said. "Saramma told me. What did you find?"

"That the bend isn't for anything," said Nila. "But it's nearly everywhere. So there must be a reason I haven't thought of."

Thankamma pushed a peg into the pole with her thumb. "When my mother came here as a bride, there was not one cat on the thuruthu. There were rats. They ate the rice, and they chewed the coir rope the whole island lived on. So my mother went to the market in Alappuzha and came back with a fish basket. There were three kittens in it. Two sisters and a brother."

She held up one finger and crooked the top of it.

"The brother had a tail like this. We called him Kokki, the hook. Nobody chose him for it. He was what was in the basket."

Nila looked along the bank. The tea-coloured cat sat on the landing stone, washing a paw. Beyond her, a black cat was walking the edge of a canoe.

"All of them?" she said. "All twenty-three?"

"Where else would a cat come from? Cats do not swim the lake." Thankamma laughed. "If the basket had held a different brother, you would have had nothing to count."

Nila opened the notebook. Under the three crossed-out reasons she wrote one line: Three kittens in a basket, and one was Kokki.

It did not look like a reason. It looked like something that had happened once, on one day, and had never stopped mattering.

The next morning the canoe took her back across. The tea-coloured cat rode as far as the middle of the lake on the front of the boat, the way she always did. Then she turned round, and Nila could see the small bend near the tip of her tail.

**For the parent:** For the parent: this is the founder effect. Every cat on the island descends from three kittens, so the island's cats hold only the gene versions those three carried. One founder had a bent tail, which is inherited. In so small a group, chance alone could make his version common. The bend does not help a cat. Nila's three tests ruled out the useful-sounding reasons one by one, and that is how a scientist tells a useful feature from one that is common by chance. The cause was not something the bend does. It was who happened to arrive.

- Ask: Why might a feature be common in a small, cut-off group even if the feature does nothing?
- Ask: How could you test whether a feature helps the animals who have it?
- Ask: Suppose three different kittens had started a population. Would it look the same today?
- Ask: People gave three reasons at once, with no evidence. Why do we so quickly invent a reason for what we see?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Lucky Tumbler Test: when nobody chooses, do the colours stay half and half? (45 min)
- **question:** An island holds 8 beans, 4 dark and 4 pale. In every round, 4 beans picked with your eyes shut leave no young, and the other 4 each have one young. Nobody looks at colour. After 10 rounds, is the island still half dark and half pale? Do four islands end the same? Does a group of 40 behave like a group of 8?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After 10 rounds, how many of the 8 beans on one island will be dark? Will four islands that start the same end the same? Will a group of 40 change more, less or the same as a group of 8? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every island starts with 4 dark and 4 pale beans. The beans are the same kind, so fingers cannot tell them apart. The picker's eyes stay shut. In every round exactly half the beans are taken out, and every bean left gets one young of the same colour. Only chance differs between islands. For the last part, only the size of the group changes.
- **safety:** Dry beans are a choking risk for children under three. Pick up any that fall on the floor.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for the start and for rounds 1 to 10. Make one column for each island. Write the number of dark beans after every round. Add a last column for the group of 40, for rounds 1 to 5.
- **results table:** Round | Island A: dark beans out of 8 | Island B | Island C | Island D | Big group: dark beans out of 40
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the tumbler and the beans. 2. Explain one round, using the steps in the pictures. 3. Ask for the three guesses and a reason for each. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Nobody will look at colour in this test. After ten rounds, how many of the eight beans will be dark? Will four islands end the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many beans will be dark after 10 rounds. Will four islands end the same? Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think the colours will stay at 4 and 4, or move away from it? (good: Nobody is choosing, so it should stay half and half.; With eyes shut I might take more of one colour just by luck.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children, and most adults, say the island will stay close to 4 and 4 because nothing favours either colour.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Colour 40 beans all over with the marker. Leave them to dry for two minutes. 2. Put the dark beans in one small bowl and 40 pale beans in the other. 3. Draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* The dark beans and the pale beans must feel exactly the same. Why does that matter, if your eyes are shut?  
  *Child mode:* Colour 40 beans dark and let them dry. Put dark and pale in two bowls. Draw the table.
  - Note: If you have red and white karamani of the same size, use those and skip the colouring. Do not mix two kinds of bean that feel different, such as kala chana and kabuli chana. Fingers would learn to choose.

  *Parent says:* Island B starts exactly as Island A did. Do you expect the same numbers? Let's see.  
  *Child mode:* Start again with 4 dark and 4 pale. Do 10 rounds. Then do two more islands.
  - Note: One round takes about half a minute once you have the rhythm. A second person can run a second tumbler to save time. Expect about three of your four islands to end all dark or all pale. Which colour wins on each is as hard to call as a coin toss.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 20 dark and 20 pale beans in the deep bowl and cover it with the cloth. 2. Stir with a hand under the cloth. 3. With eyes shut, your child takes out 20 beans, counting aloud. 4. Sort the 20 beans left by colour and add a matching young for each. 5. Write the number of dark beans out of 40. Do 5 rounds.  
  *Parent says:* This group is five times bigger. Half still leave no young in every round. Will its dark share move as far as the small islands did?  
  *Child mode:* Put 20 dark and 20 pale in the bowl. Take out 20 with eyes shut. Double the rest. Do 5 rounds.
  - Note: To compare fairly, look at shares. On an island, 6 dark out of 8 is three quarters. In the big group, 22 out of 40 is just over half. The big group nearly always stays between 10 and 30 dark beans, and it does not lose a colour in 5 rounds.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a line chart. Put rounds 0 to 10 along the bottom and dark beans, 0 to 8, up the side. 2. Draw one line for each island, in four colours. 3. Mark where any line reaches 0 or 8.  
  *Parent says:* All four lines start at four. Draw each island's line. What happens to a line once it touches the top or the bottom?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line chart with one line for each island. All four start at 4.
  - Note: A line that reaches 0 or 8 stays there. That flat end is the most important thing on the chart.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table and the chart together. 2. Ask your child what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Was either colour better at anything?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: No colour was better. So why did some islands end with only one colour? (good: By luck, more of one colour were left in some rounds.; The next round started from the new numbers, so the luck added up.; Once a colour reached zero there was nobody to bring it back.)
  - Ask: Why did the group of 40 change less than the islands of 8? (good: With 20 parents, the lucky and unlucky ones nearly cancel.; One bean is a big part of 8 and a small part of 40.)
  - Note: The reason: the 4 beans left in each round are a small sample, and a small sample rarely matches the whole. So the share of dark moves a little in each round, and nothing moves it back. The changes add up until one colour is gone. This is genetic drift. It is change without selection. The model leaves things out. Real parents have different numbers of young. Real populations change size. New versions appear by mutation and arrive with migrants. If all four of your islands still have both colours after 10 rounds, that is unusual and also a fair result. Run five more rounds and watch.
  - Think: If a visitor saw only your all-dark island, what wrong story might they tell about dark beans?
### e2 · The Bridge Test: can one traveller in each round keep two islands alike? (30 min)
- **question:** East Island has 8 dark beans. West Island has 8 pale beans. In every round, one bean picked with your eyes shut crosses from each island to the other. Is one traveller out of 8 enough to make the islands alike? What happens when the bridge is closed again?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After 8 rounds with one bean crossing each way, how many dark beans will East Island have? How many will West Island have? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both islands always hold 8 beans. In every round, each island loses 4 beans picked with eyes shut, and the 4 left are doubled, as in the Lucky Tumbler Test. The only thing that changes between the parts of the test is whether a bean crosses the bridge.
- **safety:** Dry beans are a choking risk for children under three. Pick up any that fall on the floor.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for the start and for each round. Write the number of dark beans on each island, and the difference between the two numbers.
- **results table:** Round | Bridge open or closed | East Island: dark beans out of 8 | West Island: dark beans out of 8 | Difference
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 8 dark beans in one tumbler and 8 pale beans in the other. 2. Lay the paper strip between them. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* These islands are as different as they can be. One bean will cross each way in every round. After eight rounds, how many dark beans on each island? Why?  
  *Child mode:* One island is all dark. One is all pale. Guess the dark count on each after 8 rounds. Say why.
  - Note: Many children say one traveller is too few to matter, so East Island will still be nearly all dark. Write the guess down.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the paper bridge away. 2. Run two rounds on each island, as in the Lucky Tumbler Test. 3. Write the dark counts.  
  *Parent says:* The bridge is closed. Take out four and double the rest, on each island. Did anything change? Could it ever change?  
  *Child mode:* Close the bridge. Do 2 rounds on each island. Did anything change?
  - Note: Nothing can change. East Island has only dark beans to double, and West Island has only pale. Without mutation or a migrant, a lost colour stays lost.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the bridge back. 2. On each island, take out 4 beans with eyes shut and double the 4 left. 3. Shake both tumblers. With eyes shut, take one bean from each and swap them. 4. Write the dark count on each island and the difference. 5. Do 8 rounds.  
  *Parent says:* First the islands lose four and double the rest. Then one bean crosses each way. What is the difference between the islands now?  
  *Child mode:* Take out 4 on each island and double the rest. Swap one bean. Write both counts. Do 8 rounds.
  - Note: Count after the swap, so the number can be odd. The difference usually falls from 8 to about 2 within 8 rounds. Sometimes both islands end all dark or all pale together. That is the two islands drifting as one group of 16.
  - Check: In the Bridge Test, what does the bean that crosses to the other island stand for? → **An animal who moves and has young in the new place**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the bridge away again. 2. Run 5 more rounds on each island with no swap. 3. Write the counts and the difference.  
  *Parent says:* The bridge is closed again. The islands are now alike. Will they stay alike with no travellers?  
  *Child mode:* Close the bridge again. Do 5 more rounds. Do the islands stay alike?
  - Note: With no bean crossing, each island drifts in a direction of their own, as in the Lucky Tumbler Test. In many homes the difference grows again within 5 rounds. If it does not, look at whether either island has lost a colour.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the difference column for all three parts. 2. Ask your child what they guessed, what they saw and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you guessed, what you saw and why you think it happened. What would these islands need in order to become two species?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: One bean is only one eighth of an island. Why did it change so much? (good: The traveller was doubled in later rounds.; A new traveller arrived in every round, so they added up.; It brought a colour the island did not have at all.)
  - Ask: What must happen before two populations can become two species? (good: The travellers must stop.; They must stay apart for a very long time.)
  - Note: The reason: each traveller brings a version to the island that lacked it, and that version is then copied there. A new traveller arrives in every round. So the two islands move towards the same shares. This is gene flow. It works against drift. Sewall Wright showed that about one breeding migrant per generation is enough to keep two populations from drifting far apart. For two populations to become two species, gene flow must stop, or nearly stop, for a very long time.
  - Think: A highway is built between two forests. What has happened to the bridge?

### a3 · The Black Tigers of Similipal: three explanations and four clues (12 min)

### a4 · Three Grasses in One Chapati: a new species in one step (15 min)
- **safety:** Use child-safe scissors, or let a grown-up cut the strips.
- **record:** Three drawings of the plate: the hybrid, the doubled plant, and the cross back to a parent. Under each, write how many strips have no partner.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut 4 long and 4 short strips in red, and the same in blue. 2. Lay out Grass A: 2 long red and 2 short red. 3. Lay out Grass B: 2 long blue and 2 short blue. 4. Explain the pairing rule in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Each strip is a chromosome. In Grass A, every strip has an exact partner, the same colour and length. Is that true in Grass B too?  
  *Child mode:* Cut the strips. Make Grass A in red and Grass B in blue. Does every strip have a partner?
  - Note: The pairing rule: to make pollen or an egg cell, a plant lines up each chromosome with an exact partner and sends one of each pair into the new cell. So a pollen grain of Grass A carries 1 long red and 1 short red. A strip with no partner goes to one side or the other by chance.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the hybrid on the plate: 1 long red and 1 short red from Grass A, 1 long blue and 1 short blue from Grass B. 2. Ask your child to pair every strip with an exact partner. 3. Ask what this means for the hybrid's pollen.  
  *Parent says:* This is the hybrid seedling. Pair every strip with one of the same colour and length. Can you? What will happen when this plant tries to make pollen?  
  *Child mode:* Make the hybrid: one of each strip. Try to pair them. How many have no partner?
  - Note: No strip has a partner. The four strips are shared out by chance, so most pollen and egg cells miss a long or a short. Few or none work, and the hybrid sets almost no seed. A mule is in the same position, with 63 chromosomes.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Double the hybrid: add a second copy of every strip. 2. Ask your child to pair the strips now. 3. Then cross the doubled plant back to Grass A: put 1 of each of the four kinds of strip on the plate with 1 long red and 1 short red. Pair again.  
  *Parent says:* A cell copied every chromosome and did not divide. Pair the strips now. Then cross this new plant back with Grass A. Which strips are left without partners?  
  *Child mode:* Double every strip and pair them. Then cross back with Grass A. Which strips have no partner?
  - Note: After doubling, all 8 strips pair. In the cross back, the 2 blue strips have no partners, so those young set little seed. Now the real numbers. A farmed wheat with 28 chromosomes gives 14. A wild goat grass with 14 gives 7. Their hybrid has 21, and doubling gives 42, which is bread wheat. This happened about 9,000 years ago. The farmed wheat with 28 was made the same way, from two wild grasses, long before.
  - Check: A hybrid plant doubles all the chromosomes. Why is the new plant a new species? → **They breed with their own kind but not with either parent**

## Worksheets
### Chance in Small Groups (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is genetic drift?  
   Options: A change in shares caused by chance / A change caused by a useful feature / Animals moving to a new place / A copying mistake in DNA  
   **Answer:** A change in shares caused by chance. *Why:* Drift is change by chance alone. A useful feature spreading is natural selection, moving is gene flow, and a copying mistake is a mutation.
2. (fill) When every member of a population has the same gene version, that version is said to be ____.  
   **Answer:** ['fixed']. *Why:* A fixed version has a share of the whole population. The other versions of that gene are lost.
3. (truefalse) True or false: in genetic drift, the version that spreads is always the most useful one.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In drift, which version spreads is decided by chance. A useless version can spread, and a useful one can be lost.
4. (mcq) In which population does chance change the shares fastest?  
   Options: 8 members / 80 members / 800 members / 8,000 members  
   **Answer:** 8 members. *Why:* The smaller the group, the bigger the part each lucky or unlucky member plays. In large groups, luck nearly cancels out.
5. (write) An island of 8 beans became all pale, though nobody chose beans by colour. Explain how.  
   **Answer:** In each round, the beans that had young were a small sample picked by chance. By luck, more pale beans were left in some rounds. Each round started from the new numbers, so the changes added up. When dark reached zero, no dark bean was left to have young.. *Why:* A strong answer says three things: small samples differ from the whole, the changes add up, and a lost colour cannot return.
6. (order) Put these populations in order, from fastest drift to slowest.  
   **Answer:** ['10 tigers in a small forest', '500 deer in a park', '20,000 crows in a city', 'A million bacteria in a drop of water']. *Why:* Drift is fastest in the smallest group and slowest in the largest.
7. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Genetic drift → shares change by chance; Bottleneck → a population is cut to a few survivors; Founder effect → a few members start a new population; Gene flow → versions move between populations; Lost → no member has the version any more. *Why:* These five words name what chance and movement do to the shares of gene versions.
8. (truefalse) True or false: after a bottleneck, the lost gene versions come back once the numbers recover.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The new population descends only from the survivors. A lost version can return only by a new mutation or with a migrant.
9. (mcq) Forty seeds of five kinds pass a bottleneck of five survivors. Which kind is most likely to be lost?  
   Options: The rarest kind / The most common kind / The largest kind / The darkest kind  
   **Answer:** The rarest kind. *Why:* With only 2 seeds among 40, the rarest kind is seldom among 5 picked by chance. Size and colour play no part when the picking is blind.
10. (draw) Two islands of 8 beans both start with 4 dark. Draw a line chart of how their dark counts might change.  
   **Answer:** Two wobbly lines that both start at 4 and go different ways. A line that reaches 0 or 8 stays flat from there on.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the lines wandering differently, and shows that a line cannot leave 0 or 8.
11. (sort) Sort these cases: was the change caused by chance, or by natural selection?  
   **Answer:** An island of 8 beans goes all dark in a blind draw → Chance; Pale beans are caught first on a dark cloth → Natural selection; Seals descended from 20 survivors have little variety → Chance; Bacteria that survive a medicine multiply → Natural selection; Bent tails are common where three cats began a population → Chance; Finches with deep beaks survive a drought of hard seeds → Natural selection. *Why:* In selection the feature itself changes who has young. In drift the feature makes no difference, and the group is small.
12. (mcq) Northern elephant seals number over 200,000. Why does their DNA have so little variety?  
   Options: All descend from about 20 survivors / Seals do not have mutations / The sea is the same everywhere / They are all the same age  
   **Answer:** All descend from about 20 survivors. *Why:* Hunting cut the seals to about 20. Those few carried only part of the old variety, and everyone since has inherited from them.
13. (write) Why does chance change a group of 40 more slowly than a group of 8?  
   **Answer:** In a group of 40, twenty members become parents. A few lucky ones of one colour are balanced by lucky ones of the other colour, so the share moves little. In a group of 8, only four become parents, and one lucky bean moves the share a long way.. *Why:* A strong answer compares the size of the sample that becomes the parents, and says that luck cancels out in a big sample.
14. (odd) Three of these are cases of genetic drift. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Black tigers common in one small forest / Fly bottles that each lose a gene version / Deeper finch beaks after a drought / A rare illness common on a small island  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Deep beaks cracked the hard seeds, so those finches survived. That is natural selection. In the other three, chance in a small group did the work.
15. (write) A feature is found in every animal of one herd. How could you test whether the feature is useful?  
   **Answer:** Find a herd where some have the feature and some do not. Count how many young each kind raises, or how many survive, over several years. If those with the feature do no better, the feature may be common by chance.. *Why:* A strong answer asks for a count of survival or young, and says that being common is not evidence of being useful.
### Gene Flow and New Species (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is gene flow?  
   Options: Gene versions moving between populations / Genes changing by copying mistakes / Genes being lost in a small group / Blood moving round the body  
   **Answer:** Gene versions moving between populations. *Why:* Gene flow happens when a member moves to another population and has young there. Copying mistakes are mutations, and loss in a small group is drift.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a tiger who walks to a new forest but has no cubs there has moved genes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Gene versions arrive in the new population only when the traveller has young there.
3. (fill) A strip of forest that joins two larger wild places is called a wildlife ____.  
   **Answer:** ['corridor']. *Why:* Animals use a corridor to move between the two places, which keeps gene flow going.
4. (mcq) One island is all dark beans, another all pale. One bean crosses each way every round. What happens to the difference?  
   Options: It shrinks / It stays the same / It grows / It doubles every round  
   **Answer:** It shrinks. *Why:* Each traveller brings a colour the island lacked, and that colour is copied there. So the two islands move towards the same shares.
5. (write) One migrant is a tiny part of a population. Why can one migrant in each generation still matter so much?  
   **Answer:** The migrant may bring a version the population had lost or never had. That version is then passed on and copied. A new migrant arrives in every generation, so the effect adds up. Drift is slow in a large group, so little mixing is needed to balance it.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the migrant's version is inherited, and that migrants keep arriving.
6. (match) Match each barrier to a real example.  
   **Answer:** New land between two seas → snapping shrimps of Panama; Breeding at different times of year → apple flies and hawthorn flies; Different light signals → two kinds of firefly; Young who cannot have young → a horse and a donkey; Doubled chromosomes → bread wheat and wild grasses. *Why:* Each example shows a different way that two groups are kept from having young together.
7. (sort) Sort these barriers: do they act before mating, or after mating?  
   **Answer:** They breed in different months → Before mating; Their hybrid young cannot have young → After mating; Their songs differ → Before mating; Their hybrid young die early → After mating; A sea lies between them → Before mating; Different insects carry their pollen → Before mating. *Why:* Barriers before mating stop the two from meeting or choosing each other. Barriers after mating act on the hybrid young.
8. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how one species becomes two.  
   **Answer:** ['One population exchanges genes freely', 'A barrier stops the gene flow', 'Each group changes in different ways', 'The two groups can no longer breed together']. *Why:* Gene flow must stop first. Then separate mutations, drift and selection build up differences, until breeding together fails.
9. (mcq) A mule has 63 chromosomes. Why can a mule not have young?  
   Options: An odd number cannot be shared out evenly / 63 is too many for any animal / Mules do not live long enough / Mules never meet other mules  
   **Answer:** An odd number cannot be shared out evenly. *Why:* To make egg or sperm cells, chromosomes must pair up and separate. With 63, one is always left without a partner, so the cells do not get full sets.
10. (draw) Draw two forests with a highway between them. Add two different ways for animals to cross.  
   **Answer:** For example: the road raised on pillars so animals walk beneath, and a bridge of ropes or branches above the road for monkeys and squirrels. A tunnel for small animals also counts.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows crossings for different kinds of animal, and keeps the gap wide enough for shy ones.
11. (perspective) Similipal's tigers are few and cut off. Two people disagree about bringing in tigers from other forests.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives evidence for each side. Florida panthers recovered after eight pumas were brought in. But too many outsiders could swamp what is special in the local tigers. It may suggest bringing in a few and watching the results.. *Why:* Both worries are real. Most scientists favour a few migrants for a small, inbred population, with careful checking.
12. (truefalse) True or false: gene flow makes two populations more different from each other.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Gene flow carries each population's versions into the other, so they become more alike. Drift and separate selection make them more different.
13. (mcq) Eight pumas from Texas were released among the last Florida panthers. What did the pumas bring?  
   Options: Gene versions the panthers had lost / A new kind of food / A new mutation / Stronger muscles  
   **Answer:** Gene versions the panthers had lost. *Why:* The small panther population had lost much of its variety through drift and inbreeding. The pumas' young carried versions from a larger population.
14. (write) A new highway with no crossings cuts a forest in two. Predict what happens to the animals' genes over 100 years.  
   **Answer:** Each half is now a smaller population, so drift is faster. Each side loses some gene versions, and the two sides lose different ones. The two halves become less varied inside and more different from each other. Inbreeding may rise.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the halves are smaller, that drift speeds up, and that the two sides grow apart.
15. (odd) Three of these stop gene flow. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A fenced highway / A wide sea / A forest corridor / A hot lowland between cool hilltops  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A corridor joins two wild places and lets animals move between them. The other three keep populations apart.
### Doubled Chromosomes, Chance Against Selection, and One Human Species (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Bread wheat has 42 chromosomes, in sets of 7. How many sets is that?  
   Options: Six / Two / Four / Seven  
   **Answer:** Six. *Why:* 42 ÷ 7 = 6. The six sets came from three wild grasses, two sets from each.
2. (write) Explain why a hybrid of two plant species often sets no seed.  
   **Answer:** The hybrid has one set of chromosomes from each parent, and the two sets do not match. To make pollen and eggs, chromosomes must pair with exact partners. With no partners, they are shared out unevenly, so most pollen and egg cells lack some instructions.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the chromosomes cannot pair, and links pairing to making working pollen and eggs.
3. (draw) A hybrid has four chromosomes: long red, short red, long blue, short blue. Draw the cell after doubling, with partners paired.  
   **Answer:** Eight strips in four pairs: two long red together, two short red together, two long blue together, two short blue together.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that after doubling every chromosome has an exact partner.
4. (truefalse) True or false: chromosome doubling can make a new plant species in a single generation.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The doubled plant can breed with others like themself, but young from crosses back to either parent have unpartnered chromosomes.
5. (mcq) A wheat's egg cell carries 14 chromosomes. A goat grass's pollen carries 7. How many does their hybrid have?  
   Options: 21 / 14 / 7 / 42  
   **Answer:** 21. *Why:* 14 + 7 = 21. The hybrid has 21, which cannot pair evenly. Doubling gives 42, the number in bread wheat.
6. (fill) Motoo Kimura's idea that most DNA changes spread by chance is called the ____ theory.  
   **Answer:** ['neutral']. *Why:* A neutral change makes no difference to survival, so only chance decides whether it spreads.
7. (perspective) Two scientists disagree about what has shaped most of the DNA differences between species.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives evidence for each. Changes that leave a protein the same pile up fastest, which fits chance. But beaks and wings clearly fit their uses, which only selection explains. It may say each is right about a different kind of change.. *Why:* Both are partly right. Selection explains features that fit a way of life. For DNA letters, scientists still argue over the shares of chance and selection.
8. (mcq) Tomoko Ohta studied very slightly harmful gene versions. In which population can such a version spread?  
   Options: A small one, where drift is strong / A huge one, where selection is strong / Any, at the same rate / None, ever  
   **Answer:** A small one, where drift is strong. *Why:* Weak selection removes a slightly harmful version from a large population. In a small population chance is stronger than such weak selection.
9. (write) Black tigers are common in one small forest and absent elsewhere. Give two clues that point to chance rather than selection.  
   **Answer:** Any two: the population is small and cut off, which is where drift is fastest. Their DNA has little variety, a sign of a small isolated group. Scientists found no clear sign that the dark version was favoured. No wild tigers elsewhere carry the version.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the size and isolation of the population, and notes that nobody has shown dark tigers leaving more cubs.
10. (sort) Sort these by what they do to a population.  
   **Answer:** A DNA letter is miscopied → Mutation; Bacteria that survive a medicine multiply → Natural selection; A version vanishes from a group of 8 by luck → Genetic drift; Pollen blows in from the next field → Gene flow; A cell's chromosomes double → Mutation; A young tiger fathers cubs in a new forest → Gene flow; Twenty survivors refound a population → Genetic drift; Camouflaged prey are caught less often → Natural selection. *Why:* Mutation makes new versions. Selection and drift change the shares within one population. Gene flow moves versions between populations.
11. (truefalse) True or false: most human genetic variety can be found within any one population.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. About 85 parts in 100 of human variety is inside any one population. Human groups differ in shares of versions, and not in kind.
12. (write) Three bowls hold dark and pale beans in slightly different shares. Why can one bean not tell you the bowl?  
   **Answer:** Every bowl holds both colours. A dark bean is a little more likely to come from the bowl with more dark beans, but could easily come from any of the three. The bowls differ in shares, and one bean cannot show a share.. *Why:* A strong answer says that both colours are in every bowl, and that a share is a fact about a group and not about one member.
13. (mcq) Apple flies and hawthorn flies live in the same countryside. What mostly keeps them apart?  
   Options: Their fruits ripen at different times / A river between them / Different numbers of wings / They are different colours  
   **Answer:** Their fruits ripen at different times. *Why:* The flies mate on the fruit they use, and apples ripen weeks before hawthorn fruit. So most apple flies have finished mating before hawthorn flies come out.
14. (order) Put the history of bread wheat in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Two wild grasses cross', "Their hybrid's chromosomes double, giving a wheat with 28", 'That wheat crosses with a wild goat grass', "The new hybrid's chromosomes double, giving 42"]. *Why:* Bread wheat was made by crossing and doubling twice. Each doubling gave a plant that could set seed with their own kind.
15. (write) Is a species something real in nature, or a label people use? Give one piece of evidence for each side.  
   **Answer:** Real: a firefly answers only the flashes of her own kind, and a mule cannot have young, so nature itself keeps some groups apart. A label: apple flies and hawthorn flies are part of the way to being two species, and scientists disagree about where to draw such lines.. *Why:* A strong answer gives evidence for both sides. Species split slowly, so some lines are sharp and some are not.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Beans are drawn blind, and neither colour is better. One colour still takes over. What is this called?  
   Options: Genetic drift / Natural selection / Gene flow / Mutation  
   **Answer:** Genetic drift. *Why:* Change in shares by chance alone is genetic drift. Choosing natural selection would mean that one colour helped beans survive, and here neither did.
2. (mcq) Why does chance change a population of 8 faster than a population of 800?  
   Options: A small sample often differs from the whole / Small animals breed faster / Small groups have more mutations / Large groups do not have genes  
   **Answer:** A small sample often differs from the whole. *Why:* With few parents, a little luck moves the shares a long way. With many parents, lucky and unlucky members nearly cancel out.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a version lost by drift comes back by itself in a few generations.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Once no member carries a version, nobody can pass it on. Only a new mutation or a migrant can bring it back.
4. (mcq) A population is cut to 20 survivors and grows back to thousands. What is this event called?  
   Options: A bottleneck / A corridor / A hybrid zone / Chromosome doubling  
   **Answer:** A bottleneck. *Why:* A bottleneck squeezes a population through a few survivors. Their descendants carry only the versions those few had.
5. (mcq) Three cats start an island population. One has a bent tail. Bent tails become common. What is the simplest cause?  
   Options: The founder effect / Bent tails help on islands / The island bends tails / The cats wished for bent tails  
   **Answer:** The founder effect. *Why:* With three founders, one cat's version starts at a large share. No advantage is needed. Saying the bend helps would need evidence that bent-tailed cats raise more kittens.
6. (mcq) What does gene flow do to two populations?  
   Options: Makes them more alike / Makes them more different / Makes both smaller / Stops all mutations  
   **Answer:** Makes them more alike. *Why:* Travellers who breed carry each population's versions into the other. Choosing 'more different' describes drift, which gene flow works against.
7. (mcq) Roughly how many breeding migrants per generation keep two populations from drifting far apart?  
   Options: About one / About a hundred / Half the population / None are needed  
   **Answer:** About one. *Why:* About one migrant per generation is enough in the mathematics. Wildlife managers aim for between 1 and 10, because real populations are messier.
8. (truefalse) True or false: for two populations to become two species, gene flow between them must nearly stop.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. While versions keep passing between them, the two change together. Differences that block breeding build up only when they are apart.
9. (mcq) Which of these is a barrier that acts after two animals have mated?  
   Options: Their young cannot have young / They breed in different months / Their songs differ / A sea lies between them  
   **Answer:** Their young cannot have young. *Why:* A mule is healthy but cannot breed, so no genes pass on. The other three stop the two from meeting or choosing each other in the first place.
10. (mcq) How can a new plant species form in a single generation?  
   Options: A hybrid's chromosomes double / A plant grows very tall / A seed lands on an island / A plant is watered more  
   **Answer:** A hybrid's chromosomes double. *Why:* Doubling gives every chromosome a partner, so the plant sets seed with their own kind but not with either parent. Landing on an island starts a split that takes thousands of generations.
11. (mcq) A feature is common in one population. What would show that the feature is useful?  
   Options: Members with the feature raise more young / Nearly every member has the feature / The feature looks well designed / The feature is very old  
   **Answer:** Members with the feature raise more young. *Why:* Useful means leaving more young because of the feature, and that can be counted. Being common is not enough, because chance can make a feature common too.
12. (mcq) Human populations differ mainly in what?  
   Options: The shares of gene versions / Having completely different genes / The number of chromosomes / Being different species  
   **Answer:** The shares of gene versions. *Why:* All humans have the same genes, in versions found nearly everywhere. Populations differ in how common each version is, because gene flow has always joined them.

## Applications
- Wildlife managers plan forest corridors and road crossings so that tigers, elephants and smaller animals can keep exchanging genes.
- Managers move a few animals between cut-off populations to restore lost variety. Two tigresses were moved from Maharashtra to Similipal in late 2024 for this reason.
- Zoos keep records of every animal's parents and choose mates who are not close relatives. This slows the loss of gene versions in small zoo populations.
- Doctors use the founder effect to know which inherited illnesses to look for first in a community that grew from few founders.
- Seed banks store many varieties of rice, ragi and wheat. A crop grown from very few parent plants has passed a bottleneck, and one disease can then harm every plant.
- Plant breeders double chromosomes on purpose to make new crops. Triticale, a cross of wheat and rye, was made this way.
- Scientists count DNA differences that spread by chance to estimate how long ago two species separated. This is the molecular clock.

## Spotted around us
- Chapati, puri and bread are made from bread wheat, a plant with the chromosomes of three wild grasses.
- Ragi mudde, ragi dosa and ragi malt come from finger millet, which carries the chromosomes of two wild grasses.
- The bananas at the fruit stall have no hard seeds. They have three sets of chromosomes, which cannot be shared out evenly.
- The cats of one street or one fishing harbour often share a coat pattern, because many of them descend from the same few cats.
- Fireflies flashing over a paddy field or a village pond in the monsoon are signalling to their own kind.
- A highway raised on pillars through a forest, or a sign that says animals cross here, marks a place where people are keeping gene flow open.
- A mule carrying loads on a hill path is a hybrid of a horse and a donkey.
- Crickets calling on a warm evening are singing the song of their own species. Another species nearby sings at a different speed.

## Open questions
- **How much of the difference between species came by chance, and how much by selection?** For DNA letters, scientists still disagree. Kimura and Ohta showed that chance must do a great deal. Some studies of whole genomes suggest that selection touches more of the DNA than their theories allow.
- **How many gene changes does it take to make two populations unable to breed together?** In a few cases, such as some fruit flies, scientists have found single genes that make hybrids unable to breed. In most species nobody knows the number, or whether it is usually a few genes or many.
- **How often do animals split into two species with no barrier in space?** Apple maggot flies and some lake fishes suggest that this can happen. It is hard to prove that the two groups were never apart at any time in the past.
- **Does the dark coat help the tigers of Similipal at all?** The 2021 study found no clear sign of an advantage, but it could test only 12 wild tigers from Similipal. A small advantage or disadvantage would need many years of counting cubs.
- **Do any mutations begin with a quantum jump of a single particle in DNA?** Per-Olov Löwdin proposed this in 1963. Calculations have been made since, but nobody has shown it happening inside a living cell.

## Connected maths
- After round 2, Island A had 6 dark beans out of 8. The big group had 18 dark beans out of 40. Both started at one half. Which has moved further from one half? **Island A. Its dark share moved by one quarter. The big group's share moved by only one twentieth.**. 6 out of 8 is 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75. One half is 0.5, so the island moved by 0.25. 18 out of 40 is 18 ÷ 40 = 0.45, so the big group moved by 0.05. The island moved five times as far. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A tumbler holds 4 dark and 4 pale beans. There are 70 different sets of 4 beans you could draw. Exactly 36 of those sets have 2 dark and 2 pale. Is a 2 and 2 draw more or less likely than not? **Very slightly more likely than not: 36 chances in 70, which is just over one half.**. Half of 70 is 35. 36 is one more than 35, so the chance is just over one half. 36 ÷ 70 = 0.51 to two places. So in 10 draws you should expect about 5 to be 2 and 2, and about 5 to be something else. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- On the mainland, 2 beans in 50 are speckled. On one island, 1 of the 5 founders is speckled. How many times more common is the speckled kind on the island? **5 times more common.**. 2 out of 50 is the same share as 4 out of 100. 1 out of 5 is the same share as 20 out of 100. 20 ÷ 4 = 5. So the speckled kind starts 5 times more common on the island. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A farmed wheat has 28 chromosomes and a wild goat grass has 14. Each parent gives half. How many chromosomes does their hybrid have? How many after doubling? How many sets of 7 is that? **The hybrid has 21. After doubling there are 42, which is 6 sets of 7.**. Half of 28 is 14, and half of 14 is 7. 14 + 7 = 21. Doubling gives 21 × 2 = 42. 42 ÷ 7 = 6 sets. An odd number such as 21 cannot be shared into two equal halves, which is why the hybrid needs doubling to set seed. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L5`)
- In Similipal, 58 of every 100 copies of the stripe gene are the wide-stripe version. A tiger is black only with two such copies. If the copies came together by chance, what share of tigers would be black? Suppose the forest held 30 tigers: about how many would be black? **About one third of the tigers, so about 10 tigers out of 30.**. The chance that the first copy is the wide-stripe version is 0.58. The chance that the second is too is also 0.58. For both together, multiply: 0.58 × 0.58 = 0.34 to two places, which is about one third. One third of 30 is 10. The number 30 is made up for this sum. Real tigers do not pair up by chance alone, so the true share may differ. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- About 20 elephant seals were alive in 1892. Suppose the number doubled again and again. How many doublings does it take to pass 200,000? **14 doublings. After 13 doublings there are 163,840 seals, and after 14 there are 327,680.**. Keep doubling: 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640, 1,280, 2,560, 5,120, 10,240, 20,480, 40,960, 81,920, 163,840, 327,680. The thirteenth doubling gives 163,840, which is below 200,000. The fourteenth passes it. Thirteen or fourteen doublings in about 130 years is one doubling every 9 or 10 years. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- Of 16 bean islands, 12 ended with one colour lost. Of Peter Buri's 107 fly bottles, 58 ended with one version lost. Which is the bigger share? Give one reason the shares might differ. **The bean islands: 75 in 100, against about 54 in 100 for the flies. Each fly bottle had more parents than a bean island, so the flies drifted more slowly.**. 12 ÷ 16 = 0.75, which is 75 in 100. 58 ÷ 107 = 0.54 to two places, which is 54 in 100. A bean island had 4 parents in each round. A fly bottle had 16. Chance moves a larger group more slowly, so fewer bottles had lost a version, even after more generations. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is genetic drift? → A change in the shares of gene versions that happens by chance.
- Does drift change a small population or a large one faster? → A small one.
- What is a bottleneck? → A time when a population is cut down to a few survivors.
- What is gene flow? → Gene versions moving from one population to another, when a traveller has young there.
- Does gene flow make two populations more alike or more different? → More alike.
- Why can a mule not have young? → A mule has 63 chromosomes, an odd number that cannot be shared out evenly.
- How can a new plant species form in one generation? → A hybrid's chromosomes double.
- Being common shows that a feature spread. What does it not show? → Why the feature spread: whether by being useful or by chance.
