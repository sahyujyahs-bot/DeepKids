# Mixing coloured light: why a red torch and a green torch make a yellow wall
`sci.light.colour.L4`

**Summary.** Shine a red torch and a green torch on one white wall, and the wall looks yellow, though no yellow light is anywhere in the room. Hold a pencil in front, and its two shadows are not grey. One is red and one is green. Your child tests how coloured lights add up, how filters and inks take colours away, and why three kinds of colour cells in the eye are enough to build every colour on a phone screen. Nobody yet knows how three signals in the brain become the feeling of yellow.

**Intro.** *Switch off the room light. Shine a red torch on a white wall. Now add a green torch on the same spot. What colour is the spot now? Most people are sure they know. Most people are wrong.* Red light plus green light makes yellow. But red paint plus green paint makes a muddy brown. Lights and paints seem to follow opposite rules. In this unit you find out why. You make coloured shadows, stack coloured inks, and light up objects with coloured torches. You will see a coloured thing turn almost black, and a white wall turn yellow with no yellow light anywhere. Then you meet the real puzzle. The yellow is not in the room. So where is it?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can predict the colour made when two or three coloured lights shine on one white spot: red and green make yellow, red and blue make magenta, green and blue make cyan, and all three make white.
1. I can explain why a pencil's shadow turns red or green when two coloured lights shine on it.
2. I can explain that a filter, an ink or a paint takes colours away from white light, so mixing them makes the colour darker.
3. I can say why a coloured thing can look bright in one coloured light and almost black in another.
4. I can explain that most eyes have three kinds of colour-sensing cells, and that this is why three lights can make every colour on a screen.
5. I can explain why tiny dots of two colours blend into a third colour when I stand far enough away.
6. I can fill in a results table, compare it with my guess, and finish with claim, evidence and reasoning.
7. I can say what scientists agree about mixing colours, and what nobody knows yet.

## Terms
- **Additive mixing**: Mixing coloured lights by shining them on the same spot. Each light adds more light, so the mix is brighter than each one alone. *e.g.* A red torch and a green torch on one white wall make a yellow patch that is brighter than either torch alone.
- **Primary colours of light**: Red, green and blue. They are the three lights that can be mixed to make nearly every other colour we see. *e.g.* A phone screen makes all its colours from tiny red, green and blue dots.
- **Secondary colours of light**: The colours made by mixing two primary lights. Red and green make yellow. Green and blue make cyan. Red and blue make magenta. *e.g.* A stage lit by a green lamp and a blue lamp together glows a bright blue-green.
- **Cyan**: A bright blue-green, like the colour of a clear tropical sea. It is the colour you get from green light plus blue light. *e.g.* The blue-green ink in a colour printer is cyan.
- **Magenta**: A bright pink-purple. It is made by red light plus blue light, with no green. It is not in the rainbow. *e.g.* The flowers of a bougainvillea plant are often magenta.
- **Subtractive mixing**: Mixing filters, inks or paints. Each one takes some colours away from white light, so the mix is darker than each one alone. *e.g.* Yellow highlighter over blue highlighter makes green that is darker than either.
- **Filter**: A see-through sheet that lets some colours of light through and takes the rest away. *e.g.* Red cellophane lets red light through and takes most other colours away.
- **Dye**: A coloured substance that soaks into cloth, paper or hair. It takes some colours out of the light that falls on it and sends the rest back to your eyes. *e.g.* Indigo dye makes cloth look blue because it takes away most red and yellow light.
- **Cone cell**: One of the colour-sensing cells in the retina. Most people have three kinds, and each kind responds best to a different part of the rainbow. *e.g.* Your cone cells are working right now as you read this page in daylight.
- **Pixel**: One tiny spot of a screen picture. On most screens each pixel is a small group of one red, one green and one blue dot. *e.g.* If you look at a phone screen through a strong magnifier, you can see the tiny coloured dots of each pixel.
- **Coloured shadow**: A shadow that is not grey. It happens when two lights of different colours shine on a thing from different sides. *e.g.* Under a red lamp and a green lamp, a pencil makes one red shadow and one green shadow.
- **Complementary colours**: Two colours of light that make white when added together, such as red and cyan. In paint they are pairs that make a dull grey-brown. *e.g.* On the colour circle, yellow sits opposite blue.
- **Afterimage**: A ghost of a colour that you still see for a few seconds after you stop looking at something bright or strongly coloured. *e.g.* After staring at a bright green leaf, you may see a pink leaf shape on a white wall.
- **Optical mixing**: Colours blending in your eye because tiny dots of different colours are too small to tell apart. *e.g.* From across a room, close red and green dots look yellow-brown.
- **Halftone**: A way of printing a picture with tiny dots of a few inks. From far away, the dots blend into a picture with many colours. *e.g.* A colour newspaper photo is made of tiny dots when you look at it with a magnifying glass.
- **Colour vision difference**: A way of seeing colour that is different from most people's, often because one kind of cone cell works differently. It is also called colour blindness. *e.g.* Some people find it hard to tell a red tomato from a green one on a plate.
- **Black**: What we see when almost no light comes to the eye from a place. A black object takes in nearly all the light that falls on it. *e.g.* A black dosa tawa takes in nearly all the light that falls on it.

## Explanations
### Coloured lights add together · *defined*
**Definition:** When coloured lights shine on the same spot, their light adds up. Red plus green makes yellow, green plus blue makes cyan, red plus blue makes magenta, and all three together make white.

Take a white wall in a dim room. Shine a red torch on it, and the wall looks red. Add a green torch on the same spot, and the spot turns yellow. Add a blue torch too, and it turns white. Each torch adds more light, so the spot gets brighter each time. This is called additive mixing. The wall does not change. The light that reaches your eye changes. Stage lamps, phone screens and LED strip lights all use it. They need only red, green and blue lamps to make almost every colour.
- e.g. Red and green stage lamps lighting the same curtain make it look yellow.
- e.g. A strip of coloured LED lights in a shop window changes colour by changing how bright its red, green and blue lamps are.
- e.g. A phone screen shows white when its red, green and blue dots all shine together.
### Why red and green lights look yellow · *defined*
**Definition:** The eye has three kinds of colour cells. Red light plus green light excites them in nearly the same way as real yellow light does, so we see yellow.

Real yellow light has its own wavelength, between green and orange in the rainbow. A lemon lit by sunshine sends some of this light to your eyes. But the eye does not measure wavelengths. It has three kinds of cone cells, and each kind reports how much light it caught. Real yellow light makes the red-sensing and green-sensing cones respond a lot. A red torch and a green torch together make those same two kinds respond a lot too. The brain gets the same signals, so you see the same yellow. A machine that measures wavelengths could tell the two apart at once. Your eyes cannot.
- e.g. A phone screen shows yellow using only its red and green dots. Its yellow looks the same as a banana's yellow.
- e.g. A lemon lit by sunshine and a screen showing yellow look alike, though the light from each is very different.
- e.g. A device called a spectrometer splits light by wavelength, and it shows that the screen's yellow and the lemon's yellow are not the same.
### Coloured shadows · *defined*
**Definition:** When two lights of different colours shine on a thing from different sides, each shadow is lit by the other light, so each shadow has the colour of the other light.

A shadow is a place where a light is blocked. With one white torch, the shadow is dark, because no light reaches it. With a red torch and a green torch, the pencil blocks the red light on one side. Only green light reaches that patch, so the shadow looks green. On the other side the pencil blocks the green light. Only red reaches that patch, so the shadow looks red. Where both lights reach, the wall looks yellow. If you add a blue torch as well, the shadows turn cyan, magenta and yellow, and the lit wall looks white.
- e.g. On a stage lit from both sides by a red lamp and a blue lamp, a dancer's two shadows look blue and red.
- e.g. In a room lit by a tubelight and a warm yellowish lamp, shadows have a faint bluish or yellowish edge.
- e.g. Theatre lighting designers put coloured lamps on both sides of the stage on purpose, so that actors have coloured shadows.
### Filters take colours away · *defined*
**Definition:** A colour filter lets some colours of light through and takes the others away. Two filters together take away even more.

White light holds every colour. A red filter lets red light through and takes most of the other colours away. The filter does not make red light. It only decides what to let through. A green filter lets green through. Put a red filter and a green filter together, and almost no light gets past both. The light is mostly dark. A cyan filter lets green and blue through. A yellow filter lets red and green through. Put those two together, and only green gets through both. This is called subtractive mixing, because each layer subtracts, or takes away, some light.
- e.g. A yellow sheet over a cyan sheet in front of a torch gives a green patch.
- e.g. Red cellophane plus green cellophane in front of a torch gives a patch that is almost dark.
- e.g. Sunglasses with coloured lenses make everything look tinted, because they take some colours away.
### Inks, dyes and paints · *defined*
**Definition:** Inks, dyes and paints are made of tiny coloured grains or molecules. Each kind takes some colours out of the light and sends the rest back. Mixing them takes away more colours, so the mix is darker.

A yellow paint sends back red and green and takes away blue. A cyan paint sends back green and blue and takes away red. Mix them and only green is sent back by both, so you get green. But it is a darker green than either paint, because each paint took away some light. Cyan, magenta and yellow are the three colours that mix best for paint and ink. A colour printer uses these three inks and adds black ink. Mixing all three gives a dark muddy colour, not a true black, so black ink is added. Red, yellow and blue paints, as many children learn, also work but make a smaller range of colours.
- e.g. A colour printer makes all its pictures from cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink.
- e.g. Mixing many paints from a school box makes a dark brown.
- e.g. A pink highlighter over a yellow highlighter makes an orange-red.
### Why a thing can look bright in one light and dark in another · *defined*
**Definition:** A coloured thing sends back only the colours it does not take away. If those colours are missing from the light that falls on it, the thing looks dark.

A green leaf takes away most red and blue and sends back green. In sunlight, which holds every colour, it looks green. In a pure red light there is almost no green for the leaf to send back, so the leaf looks nearly black. A yellow cloth sends back red and green but takes away blue. In red light it looks bright red. In green light it looks bright green. In blue light it looks dark. A white thing sends back every colour, so it takes on whatever colour the light has. A black thing takes away nearly all of every colour, so it looks black in every light.
- e.g. A green leaf looks nearly black under a red bulb.
- e.g. A white sheet under a blue bulb looks blue.
- e.g. Under the orange street lights some cities use, a red car and a blue car can look much alike.
### Complementary colours and afterimages · *defined*
**Definition:** Two colours of light that make white together are complementary, such as red and cyan. After staring at one colour, you may see its complement for a few seconds.

Stare at a bright green shape for half a minute, then look at a white wall. You may see a pink shape. The cone cells that sense green have been working hard, and they tire. When you look at white, those cells answer a little less than usual, while the other kinds answer as usual. So the white looks a little pinker for a moment. Stare at a cyan stripe and you see a faint red one. This is an afterimage. It is made in the eye, not on the wall. It fades in a few seconds as the tired cells rest.
- e.g. After a bright camera flash, you may see a dark spot, then a coloured spot, for a few seconds.
- e.g. After staring at a blue-green stripe, you see a faint red-orange stripe on a white wall.
- e.g. Surgical gowns are often green or blue, which is said to rest eyes that have stared at red for a long time.
### Dots that blend · *defined*
**Definition:** When coloured dots are too small and too close to see separately, their light blends in your eye into one colour.

Your eye can only tell two dots apart if they are far enough apart. Closer than that, their light lands on the same cells and mixes. A colour newspaper photo has tiny dots of cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink. From a normal distance the dots blend, and you see a picture with every colour. A screen is the same. It has tiny red, green and blue dots. Painters have used this too. Georges Seurat, a French painter, built whole paintings from small dots of paint in the 1880s. Step back from a dot painting and the colours blend. Step close and they fall apart again.
- e.g. A colour newspaper picture is a pattern of tiny dots when you look at it with a magnifier.
- e.g. A kolam made with dots of two colours of rangoli powder looks like a third colour from a few steps away.
- e.g. A TV looks like a smooth picture from the sofa and like coloured dots from one centimetre away.
### The brain adjusts for the colour of light · *idea*
**Definition:** Your brain tries to guess the true colour of a thing, even when the light changes. Scientists call this colour constancy, and they are still working out exactly how it is done.

A white sheet looks white in sunlight. It also looks white under a yellowish bulb, though the light reaching your eye is much yellower. Your brain notices the colour of the light and takes it into account. It looks at the whole scene, not at one patch. This is why a sari in a shop may look one colour under the shop lights and a slightly different one when you carry it to the door. Cameras do a similar job with a setting called white balance. Nobody yet knows exactly how the brain does it. In 2015, a photo of a dress went around the world, and people argued about whether it was blue and black or white and gold. Their brains had guessed different lights.
- e.g. A white wall looks white in the morning and still looks white in the evening, though the light is yellower.
- e.g. A phone camera sometimes makes photos look too yellow indoors until its white balance catches up.
- e.g. A shopkeeper takes a sari to the door to show its colour in daylight.
### Different eyes, different colours · *defined*
**Definition:** People and animals have different numbers of cone cell types, so they see colour differently. Most people have three. Dogs have two. Many birds have four.

A dog has two kinds of cone cells, so a dog tells yellow and blue well, but mixes up red and green. Many birds have four kinds, and one of them senses ultraviolet. In many countries, about 1 boy or man in 12 and about 1 girl or woman in 200 has a colour vision difference. In most cases one of the three kinds of cone works differently, so some reds and greens look alike. It is not an illness. It is a different way of seeing. Even among people with three kinds of cone, the exact response of each kind varies a little, so no two people see colour in exactly the same way. A test with dotted number pictures, designed by Shinobu Ishihara in Japan more than 100 years ago, is still used to find out.
- e.g. A dog does not see a red ball on green grass as clearly as a human does.
- e.g. A person with red-green colour blindness may sort a red crayon with the brown ones.
- e.g. A peacock's feathers reflect some ultraviolet light, which peafowl can probably see and we cannot.
### Is yellow in the light or in us? · *debated*
A red torch and a green torch together look yellow, and a real yellow lamp looks yellow too. So is yellow a property of the light, or of the eye, or of the brain? People who study this give different answers. Everyone agrees on the measurements. The disagreement is about what we mean when we say a colour is there.
- e.g. A banana looks yellow in daylight and nearly black in blue light.
- e.g. A screen shows a yellow sunflower using only red and green dots.
- e.g. Two people may call the same wall yellow and still see it slightly differently.
- **Many physicists:** Colour is a name for a mix of wavelengths. Yellow light has its own wavelength. Red plus green is a different mix that happens to fool our eyes.
- **Many vision scientists:** Colour is what the eye and brain make from the light. The same signals make the same yellow, whatever the light was. So yellow is a result in us, caused by light.
- **Many philosophers:** Even if we know every wavelength and every signal, we may not know what yellow feels like. Nobody can check whether yours feels the same as mine.

## Mostly agreed
- Red, green and blue lights mixed together can make almost every colour, including white. *How we know:* Stage lamps, LED strips and screens do it every day, and people matching colours with three lights get very close.
- Most people have three kinds of cone cell, and a colour is built from comparing their three signals. *How we know:* Thomas Young guessed it in 1802. By 1980 scientists had measured the light that each kind of cone catches in human retinas.
- Filters, inks and paints take colours away, so mixing them makes things darker. *How we know:* Stacked cellophane sheets and layered highlighters get darker with each layer, and a lightmeter shows less light getting through.
- Different mixes of light can look the same to us. *How we know:* A spectrometer shows that a yellow lamp and a red-plus-green screen have different wavelengths, yet people cannot tell them apart.
- Colour vision differs between people and between animals. *How we know:* Colour tests find people who confuse red and green. Behaviour tests and measurements of retinas show that dogs have two kinds of cone and many birds have four.
- Coloured shadows happen whenever lights of different colours shine on a thing from different sides. *How we know:* Anyone can repeat the two torch test, and theatre lighting designers use coloured shadows on purpose.

## How we found out
**Thomas Young and James Clerk Maxwell, 1802 to 1861.** For a long time, painters taught that red, yellow and blue are the three basic colours, and that every other colour is a mix of them. In 1802, Thomas Young in England suggested something different for light. He guessed that the eye has only three kinds of detector, so three lights might be enough to make any colour. In the 1850s, James Clerk Maxwell, a Scottish scientist, tested the idea. He built a box of coloured lights and asked people to match colours by changing just three of them. Three were enough. In 1861, he showed a picture of a tartan ribbon. It was made from three black and white photographs, taken through red, green and blue filters, and then shone back through the same filters on one screen. The ribbon appeared in colour. For paints, the old rule is a useful guide. For light, the three that work best are red, green and blue.  
*What people believed before:* Red, yellow and blue are the only three basic colours for everything. Maxwell's tartan ribbon, made from three filtered pictures, showed that red, green and blue light can make the full range of colour.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Red, yellow and blue are the three basic colours that mix to make all the others.** (Many art teachers and school books for hundreds of years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Painters can make many colours from red, yellow and blue paints, and the idea is easy to teach.  
  Against: Cyan, magenta and yellow make a much wider range of colours when mixed as paint or ink, and printers use them. For light, red, green and blue work best. There is no single set of basic colours for everything.
- **Mixing colours always makes a darker colour.** (Many people who only ever mix paints.; *rejected*)  
  For: Every time you mix more paints, the colour does get darker.  
  Against: When you mix coloured lights, each light adds more light, so the mix gets brighter. Red and green light together are brighter than either.
- **A screen with red, green and blue can show every colour the eye can see.** (Many people, including many who sell screens.; *rejected*)  
  For: Screens show a huge range of colours, and nearly all the colours of everyday life.  
  Against: A pure green or blue-green from a laser or a rainbow is more intense than any screen can make. Screens can show most colours, but not all.
- **Brown is a colour of its own, like red or blue.** (Many young children.; *accepted*)  
  For: Brown looks as different from orange as green looks from blue, and we have a separate word for it.  
  Against: Vision scientists find that brown is dim orange or yellow seen next to brighter things. A patch of orange light on a dark wall looks orange, and the same colour in a bright room looks brown.
- **Black and white are not colours.** (Many art teachers.; *debated*)  
  For: Black means no light and white means all colours together, so they seem to lack colour.  
  Against: People use both as colours in everyday life, and vision scientists treat the brightness of a colour as part of the colour. Whether to call them colours is partly a choice of words.
- **Everyone sees colours exactly the same way.** (Many people who have never compared notes.; *rejected*)  
  For: Most people agree on the names of colours, and most people have three kinds of cone cell.  
  Against: About 1 boy or man in 12 sees some reds and greens differently. Even people with three kinds of cone have slightly different cones. Nobody can compare the feeling of colour from the inside.

## Roots
We have mixed coloured lights, stacked coloured inks and watched colours vanish and appear. Now we ask what these tests tell us about colour itself, and about the eye that sees it.

**Deep truth.** The wall lit by a red torch and a green torch looked yellow, yet no yellow light reached it. Your eye has only three kinds of colour sensor, so it reports red plus green as yellow. Very different mixes of light can feel exactly the same to us. So a colour is what your eye reports, not something the light has.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a lemon sends yellow light, as if each colour name were one fixed kind of light. But red plus green and real yellow are different light that look the same. A colour name tells us about the eye as much as about the light.  
*Evidence:* In the two torch test, the wall looked yellow where the red and green lights overlapped, though only a red torch and a green torch were on. If your wall looked orange or greenish, write that down honestly. Cheap cellophane is never perfect, and your result is still real evidence.

- *who am i:* Every colour you have ever seen came from just three signals in your eyes. You are partly made of how your own eyes answer the light.
- *what is life:* Different kinds of animals have different numbers of colour cells. A dog has two kinds, most people have three and many birds have four. Each kind of animal lives in a set of colours of their own.
- *what is this world:* The world sends out light of every wavelength. Each eye picks just a few signals from it. So the colours we know are a meeting between the world and an eye.

Light comes in a whole range of wavelengths. An eye sorts that range into only three signals, and everything we call colour is built from those three. That is why three lights can stand in for almost any mix. The red and green torches did not turn into yellow. Your eyes gave the same answer to them as to real yellow. So colour lives in the meeting of light and a living eye, and neither makes it alone.

- **Plotinus, Enneads 1.6.9:** Plotinus, a Greek thinker who lived about 1,800 years ago, wrote that no eye could ever see the Sun unless it had first become something like the Sun. (Our eyes are built to catch sunlight, which holds all the colours. Each kind of cone cell is tuned to a different part of it.)
- **Katha Upanishad 2.2.15:** The Katha Upanishad says that there the Sun does not shine, nor the Moon or the stars, nor this lightning. All things shine after that light, and by its light all this is lit. (Every colour in a room is lit by a light that comes first. Without light, a rose, a leaf and a wall have no colour to show.)
- **Tao Te Ching 12:** Laozi wrote that the five colours make the eyes blind. He meant that a mind dazzled by bright things stops seeing what is quiet underneath. (A brightly coloured stage can fool the eye. A red and a green lamp made a yellow that was not there.)
- Science (settled): Most people have three kinds of cone cell, and lights that excite the three kinds in the same way look the same. Thomas Young proposed this in 1802, and later experiments confirmed it.
- Science (open): How three signals in the brain become the feeling of a colour, such as yellow, is not known.
- Other view, Many physicists: Real yellow light and red-plus-green light are different things. One has a single wavelength and the other has two. A spectrometer tells them apart at once. So the colour is in the light, and the eye is just a poor instrument that can be fooled.
- Practice, Is white always white? (3 min): Sit near a window in the daytime with a plain sheet of white paper. Hold the paper up, and look at it for three slow breaths. Notice its colour. Now carry the paper under a yellowish lamp, and look for three more breaths. Notice its colour again. Last, ask yourself which colour the paper really is. Let that question sit in your mind while you breathe out slowly.
- Ask: The wall looked yellow but no yellow light touched it. Where is the yellow?
- Ask: A dog's world is built from two kinds of colour cell and yours from three. Is a dog's world smaller, or only different?
- Ask: If you could grow a fourth kind of colour cell, what do you think a new colour would be like?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A red torch and a green torch make a yellow wall, but there is no yellow light anywhere. Is the wall really yellow? (hint: Think about what reached your eye, and what your eye does with it.)
- *deep:* Mixing lights makes things brighter, and mixing paints makes things darker. Why would mixing do opposite things? (hint: Think about what each torch adds, and what each paint takes away.)
- *big:* A dog has two kinds of cone cell and you have three. Does a dog see fewer colours, or different colours? (hint: A dog is good at some things we are bad at. Think about why that might matter to a dog.)
- *deep:* Every colour on a phone screen is made from only three. Does that mean every colour in the world is made from three? (hint: Think about what the screen is copying, and what it is making from scratch.)
- *big:* A sari looks one colour in the shop and another at the door. Which one is its true colour? (hint: Think about who decides what the true colour is, and what the light does.)
- *big:* Hot stars look blue and cooler stars look red, but we call red a warm colour. Why does it feel backwards? (hint: Think about where the words warm and cool come from, and what your eye reports.)
- *deep:* If a picture is made of three kinds of dots, where is the picture: in the dots or in your head? (hint: Walk away from the picture until the dots vanish. What changed?)
- *big:* Can you imagine a colour you have never seen? What does your answer say about where colours live? (hint: Try it, and notice what you picture.)
- *deep:* Who decides which three colours are the primary colours: nature, our eyes, or us? (hint: Think about why the answer is different for lights and for paints.)
- *big:* A friend with red-green colour blindness sees a map differently from you. Who sees the map correctly? (hint: Think about what the map is for, and who made it.)

## Story: The Sunrise With No Yellow Bulb
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Red and green light shining on the same white surface look yellow. A green cloth lit only by red light looks almost black. Stage lamps in red, green and blue are used in many theatres. The school, the play, Ilakkiya, her aunt and everyone else in the story are made up.*

Ilakkiya was ten, and on the afternoon before Pongal she was the only person in the school hall who was not shouting.

The school was a small one, near Thanjavur, with paddy fields on three sides. Tomorrow it would put on a play called The Sun Comes to the Paddy. Twelve children were dressed as paddy plants, in green skirts and green scarves. One small boy was the Sun, in a bright yellow shawl. At the end of the play, the Sun was meant to rise slowly over the field, and the whole stage was meant to glow gold.

Ilakkiya's aunt ran a shop called Selvi Light and Sound Service. She hired out speakers and lamps for weddings and festivals. That week she had brought a big box of stage lamps to the school. There was a red lamp, a green lamp, a blue lamp and one fat yellow lamp for the Sun. Ilakkiya's job was to hold the ladder.

Then the yellow lamp went pop.

A thin line of smoke curled up from it, and the whole hall went quiet. Aunt Selvi flicked the switch twice. Nothing. "The spare is in my shop," she said slowly. "The shop is twelve kilometres away, and the last bus has gone."

The school electrician, Murugesan Anna, scratched his head. "No yellow lamp, no golden Sun," he said. "We must change the ending. The Sun will rise in plain white light, and that is that." The headmistress looked sad. The twelve paddy plants looked sadder. They had practised for a month.

"We do not need a yellow lamp," said Ilakkiya.

Murugesan Anna laughed, but kindly. "Child, the Sun is yellow."

Ilakkiya did not argue. She just remembered a kitchen wall. The week before, she had wrapped a red sweet wrapper over one torch and a green one over another, only to see what would happen. When she pointed both at the wall, the wall had turned a warm yellow. She had stared at it for a long time.

"Please switch on the red lamp and the green lamp," she said. "Only those two. Point both at the white curtain at the back."

Murugesan Anna did it, mostly to make her happy. The red lamp lit one round patch of the curtain red. The green lamp lit another patch green. And where the two patches overlapped, the curtain glowed a warm, golden yellow.

Murugesan Anna looked at the curtain. Then he looked at the lamps. Then he looked at the curtain again. "There is no yellow in those lamps," he said.

"Don't look at the lamps, Anna," said Ilakkiya. "Look at the curtain."

Aunt Selvi began to laugh so loudly that the paddy plants turned round. "I have sold lamps for twenty years," she said, "and I never once tried it!"

But a second trouble came at once. When Ilakkiya tested the red lamp alone, to see what dawn would look like, a girl in a green skirt shrieked. "Our skirts have gone black!" And it was true. In pure red light, the green cloth looked nearly black.

Ilakkiya thought hard. Green cloth could only send back the green light that fell on it. A red lamp had no green in it. So the skirts had nothing to send back. Then she smiled. "The play begins before sunrise," she said. "It should be dark. Let the field be dark at first!"

She wrote the plan on the back of her hand. First, only the red lamp. The field would be dark, and the Sun, in his yellow shawl, would look like a small red ball at the edge. Next, the green lamp. The curtain would turn yellow, the paddy would turn green again, and the Sun's shawl would shine gold. Last, the blue lamp, and the whole stage would blaze white, like noon.

On Pongal evening the hall was full. The lights dimmed. Only the red lamp glowed, and twelve dark shapes stood very still in a red field, with a small red Sun at the edge. Nobody breathed.

Then Murugesan Anna, who had been told to wait for Ilakkiya's nod, switched on the green lamp. A golden light spread across the stage. The paddy turned green, one skirt after another, as if the whole field were waking up. The Sun's shawl blazed gold. A moment later the blue lamp came on, and the whole stage shone as bright as noon. The audience clapped before the song had even started.

Afterwards, Murugesan Anna came to find her. "One question," he said. "There is no yellow in the lamps. There is no yellow in the curtain. It is only white cloth. So where did the yellow come from?"

Ilakkiya thought about it for a long time. "The red and the green both landed in the same place," she said slowly. "My eyes answered the way they would answer to yellow. I think the yellow was made inside us." She grinned. "But I don't know how. Ask me when I am eleven."

**For the parent:** For the parent: red light and green light landing on the same white curtain look yellow, because the eye has three kinds of colour cells, and red plus green excites them in nearly the same way as real yellow light does. So the stage did not need a yellow lamp. A green cloth lit by pure red light looks almost black, because the cloth has no green light to send back.

- Ask: A red lamp and a green lamp together lit a white curtain yellow. Where was the yellow made?
- Ask: A green cloth looks nearly black under a red lamp. What does that tell you about where a thing's colour comes from?
- Ask: If you had only a red, a green and a blue lamp, how would you light a pink sky at sunset?
- Ask: A white curtain can look red, yellow, green or white. What does the curtain itself do, and what does the light do?

## Experiments
### e1 · Two Torches, Two Shadows: what colour is a shadow? (30 min)
- **question:** When a red torch and a green torch shine on one white wall, what colour is the wall where they overlap? And what colour is each shadow of a pencil in front of the wall?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. What colour will the wall be where the red and green lights overlap? What colour will the pencil's shadows be? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same two torches, the same wall, the same pencil and the same dim room each time. Keep both torches between half a metre and one metre from the wall, and move them closer if the patches look faint. Change one thing at a time: first only red, then only green, then both. Look at each set-up for a full ten seconds, because your eyes need time to settle in a dim room. Safety: use LED or phone torches, because cellophane can melt on a hot bulb, and never shine a torch into anyone's eyes. Who does what: with one child, the child holds the red torch and the parent holds the green. With 2 to 4 children, one holds each torch, one holds the pencil and one is the recorder with the table. Swap jobs for each set-up. If you have only one phone, use a small LED torch for the second light.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the wall and the pencil's two shadows with coloured pencils. Write what you thought, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Lights on | Colour of the wall where the light lands | Colours of the pencil's shadows | Was my guess right? (yes or no)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two torches, the cellophane and the white wall. Ask for a guess and a reason for each question. Write the guesses at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will cover one torch with red and one with green. Then we shine both on the wall. What colour will the wall be where the two lights meet? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A red torch and a green torch. Both shine on one wall. What colour will it be? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think the wall will be that colour? (good: Red and green paint make brown, so it might be brown.; The two colours will mix.; It might look like a colour from the middle of the rainbow.)
  - Ask: What colour will the pencil's shadows be? (good: Black or grey, like all shadows.; One red and one green.; Dark.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children guess brown, because they know paints. That is a good guess, and the test will show if it holds. Write what they say, word for word.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fold the red cellophane in two. Fix it over the front of one torch with a rubber band. Do the same with the green cellophane on the other torch. Dim the room. Shine each torch alone on the wall.  
  *Parent says:* Cover each torch with cellophane. Keep the sheet tight and flat. Now shine each torch alone on the wall. What colour is each patch?  
  *Child mode:* Fix red on one torch. Fix green on the other. Shine each on the wall. What colours do you see?
  - Note: Dim the room by closing the curtains, ideally in the evening. If a patch is very faint, use one layer of cellophane, or move the torch closer to the wall. If a patch looks pale, add a second layer. Cheap cellophane lets a little white light through, so a patch can look slightly pale. That is fine.
  - Check: Why do we shine each torch alone on the wall before using both together? → **So we know what each light does alone, and can tell what is new when we use both**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the pencil upright in the clay, a hand's width from the wall. Switch on the red torch alone from about one metre away. Look at the shadow. Then switch on the green torch alone and look again.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the pencil. First only the red torch. What colour is the shadow? Now only the green. What colour is the shadow now?  
  *Child mode:* Stand the pencil near the wall. Shine the red torch. Look at the shadow. Now shine the green. Look again.
  - Note: With one torch, the shadow is dark, and the rest of the wall is the colour of the torch. The shadow may look dark red, dark green or almost black. Write what you really see. If the pencil's shadow is too thin to see, use a thick marker pen instead. This is your starting point for the next step.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Switch on both torches. Stand them about 40 centimetres apart and about one metre from the wall, so the two patches overlap. Look at the middle. Then look at the pencil's two shadows. Write the colours in the table. Then move one torch back a hand-span at a time, and write how the middle colour changes each time.  
  *Parent says:* Both torches now, side by side, so the patches overlap. What colour is the wall in the middle? How many shadows does the pencil have? What colour is each one?  
  *Child mode:* Switch on both torches. Make the patches overlap. What colour is the middle? What colour is each shadow?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the shadows? (good: There are two shadows.; One shadow is red and one is green.; The shadows are not black or grey.)
  - Note: Expect the overlap to look yellow, or orange-yellow, or sometimes pale green-yellow, depending on which torch is stronger. If it looks orange, the red is too strong, so move the red torch a little further back. If it looks green-yellow, move the green torch back. One shadow should look green and the other red. The shadows can be faint, so a darker room helps. The yellow will not be perfect. That is a real result.
  - Check: One of the pencil's shadows is green. Why? → **The pencil blocks the red torch there, but the green torch still lights that spot**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If you have a third torch, cover it with blue cellophane. Switch on all three so the patches overlap. Look at the middle and at the pencil's three shadows. With only two torches, swap the green one for blue and see what red and blue make.  
  *Parent says:* Now a third torch, with blue. Where all three meet, what colour is the wall? What colours are the three shadows?  
  *Child mode:* Add a blue torch. What colour is the middle? What colours are the shadows?
  - Note: Blue cellophane is dim, so use only one layer of blue and move the blue torch closer. Expect the middle to look white or pale. The three shadows should look cyan, magenta and yellow. With only two torches, red and blue give magenta. Green and blue give cyan. This step is a bonus. Skip it if your torches are weak.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now about mixing lights? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: red light and green light together look yellow. What I saw: the wall was yellow where the two patches overlapped, and the pencil had one red shadow and one green shadow. Why: each torch adds its light to the spot, and the eye gives the same answer to red plus green as it does to yellow.
### e2 · Highlighter Layers: do inks add colours or take them away? (27 min)
- **question:** When you colour one highlighter over another on white paper, do the colours add up like torch light, or do they take colours away?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. What colour will yellow over blue be? What colour will pink over yellow be? Will the crossing look brighter or darker than the single colours? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same three highlighters and the same kind of white paper every time. Colour each stripe with one slow stroke and the same pressure. Only the colours you put on top of each other change. At the end, check whether the order of the layers matters.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the nine crossing squares and colour each one in with coloured pencils. Write which crossings are darker than the single stripes.
- **results table:** Bottom layer | Top layer | My guess for the colour | The colour I saw | Lighter or darker than the single colours?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three highlighters and the paper. Ask for a guess and a reason for each question. Write the guesses at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will colour stripes across each other. What colour will yellow over blue be? What about pink over yellow? Will the crossings be brighter or darker? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three highlighters. We will draw stripes across each other. What colours will the crossings be? Why?
  - Ask: Will the crossings look brighter or darker than the single stripes? (good: Darker, like mixing paints.; Brighter, like mixing torches.; I am not sure.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Children who have just done the torch test often guess brighter. Others who know paints guess darker. Both are good guesses. Write what they say.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Use the ruler to draw three wide horizontal stripes across the first page: yellow, pink and blue. Leave a gap of one finger-width between them. Colour each with one slow stroke. Close each cap after use. Keep the pens away from your mouth and eyes.  
  *Parent says:* Draw three stripes across the page. Yellow, then pink, then blue. One slow stroke each. Leave a gap between them.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three stripes across. Yellow, pink, blue. Leave gaps.
  - Note: Use plain printer or copy paper, because highlighter ink bleeds through thin paper. Try each pen on a scrap first. One child: the parent can draw a second grid with the same pens, to compare. 2 to 4 children: share the three pens and pass them round, so each child draws a grid on their own page. Then compare grids. Do different hands give different colours?
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now draw three tall stripes down the page, from top to bottom, crossing the first ones. Use blue first, then yellow, then pink. Leave gaps between them. Use the same pressure each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now three tall stripes, crossing the first ones. Blue, yellow, pink. Same slow stroke as before.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three tall stripes down the page. Blue, yellow, pink. Cross the first ones.
  - Note: Wait a few seconds for the ink to dry before you look closely. The crossings of stripes of the same colour should look deeper than the single stripe. The crossings of different colours are the new ones.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the nine crossing squares in daylight or under a lamp. Colour each square in your table and write whether it is lighter or darker than the two single stripes.  
  *Parent says:* Look at each crossing. What colour is it? Is it brighter than the stripes, or darker?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the nine crossings. What colours do you see? Brighter or darker?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the crossings of two different colours? (good: They are new colours, like green or orange.; They are darker than the stripes.; Blue and yellow make green.)
  - Note: Expect yellow with blue to make green. Pink with yellow makes orange or orange-red. Pink with blue makes purple or violet. Crossings of the same colour look deeper. Your highlighters may give slightly different colours from these. Write what you really see. Yellow, pink and blue are close to the three inks used in colour printers, which are yellow, magenta and cyan.
  - Check: Where two different highlighter stripes cross, the square is darker than both stripes. Why? → **Each ink takes some colours of light away, so less light comes back from the crossing**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On the second sheet, colour a short yellow stripe. Colour a blue stripe across it. Then colour a pink stripe across the crossing. Look at the colour where all three layers meet. Then colour one patch with blue first and yellow second, and compare it with yellow first and blue second. Last, colour a yellow patch with one stroke, then two, then three. Write how the colour changes.  
  *Parent says:* Three layers now. What colour is the middle where all three meet? Then try blue first and yellow second. Is it the same green?  
  *Child mode:* Put all three colours on top of each other. What colour is it? Does the order change anything?
  - Note: Three layers usually give a dark brownish or greyish colour. The order often makes only a small difference. If you see a small difference, write it down as a real result. Different inks soak in at different rates.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now about mixing inks? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why it happened.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: inks take colours away, so mixing them makes the colour darker. What I saw: yellow over blue made a darker green, and all three layers made a dark colour. Why: each ink takes some colours out of the light, so less light comes back to my eye.

## Worksheets
### Mixing Light and Mixing Colours (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A red torch and a green torch shine on the same spot of a white wall. What colour does the spot look?  
   Options: Yellow / Brown / Dark green  
   **Answer:** Yellow. *Why:* Each torch adds its light to the spot. Red plus green looks yellow. Picking 'brown' is common, because red paint and green paint make brown. But torches add light, and paints take light away.
2. (mcq) A green torch and a blue torch shine on one spot of a white wall. What colour does the spot look?  
   Options: Cyan, a bright blue-green / Yellow / Magenta  
   **Answer:** Cyan, a bright blue-green. *Why:* Green plus blue makes cyan, a bright blue-green. Yellow is made by red plus green, and magenta is made by red plus blue.
3. (fill) Red light and blue light shining together make a bright pink-purple called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['magenta']. *Why:* Red plus blue light, with no green, makes magenta. It is not in the rainbow. Your eyes and brain make it.
4. (truefalse) When you mix coloured lights, the mix is brighter than each light alone.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each light adds more light to the spot. This is why red, green and blue lights together make white.
5. (truefalse) Red cellophane over a torch adds red light to the beam.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The cellophane makes no light. It only lets the red part of the light through and takes the other colours away.
6. (mcq) A teacher says that mixing more and more paints always makes a darker colour. Which sentence best explains why?  
   Options: Each paint takes some colours of light away, so less light comes back / Paints are made of darker stuff than light / Paint is heavy, so it sinks the colour  
   **Answer:** Each paint takes some colours of light away, so less light comes back. *Why:* Each paint takes some colours out of the light that falls on it. Mixing many paints takes away many colours, so little light comes back. Picking 'heavy' mixes up paint with weight. Colour is about light.
7. (sort) Sort these. Does each one show lights adding together, or colours being taken away?  
   **Answer:** a red lamp and a blue lamp lighting one curtain → Lights adding together; a yellow highlighter over a blue one → Colours taken away; a phone screen showing white → Lights adding together; red cellophane over a torch → Colours taken away; stirring all the paints into one pot → Colours taken away; a TV showing bright yellow → Lights adding together. *Why:* Lamps and screens send out light, and the lights add up. Cellophane, highlighters and paints do not make light. They take colours away from the light that falls on them.
8. (match) Match each mix of lights to the colour it makes on a white wall.  
   **Answer:** Red and green → yellow; Green and blue → cyan; Red and blue → magenta; Red, green and blue → white. *Why:* The three primary colours of light add up to make the other colours, and all three together make white.
9. (write) In the Two Torches test, one shadow of the pencil was green, not black. Explain in two or three sentences why it was green.  
   **Answer:** The pencil blocked the red torch at that spot. The green torch's light still reached it. So the shadow was lit by green light only, and it looked green.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a shadow is only dark where all the light is blocked. Where just one light is blocked, the other light still lands there.
10. (mcq) A green leaf is held under a pure red light. What does the leaf look like?  
   Options: Nearly black / Bright green / Bright red  
   **Answer:** Nearly black. *Why:* The leaf sends back only green light. A pure red light has almost no green in it, so the leaf has little to send back. Picking 'bright green' forgets that a thing can only show the colours that fall on it.
11. (fill) A yellow cloth looks bright in red light and in green light, but dark in ____ light.  
   **Answer:** ['blue']. *Why:* Yellow cloth sends back red and green, and takes away blue. In blue light it has almost nothing to send back.
12. (mcq) Colour printers use cyan, magenta and yellow ink, and one more. Which one?  
   Options: Black / White / Red  
   **Answer:** Black. *Why:* Mixing all three inks gives a dark muddy colour, not a true black. Black ink makes dark lines and shadows sharp. White comes from the paper.
13. (draw) Draw a red torch and a green torch shining on a white wall. Show where the wall looks red, where it looks green and where it looks yellow.  
   **Answer:** The red torch lights one round patch red. The green torch lights another patch green. Where the two patches overlap, the wall looks yellow.. *Why:* Each torch lights its own patch. Only where both reach does the wall add the colours together.
14. (order) Put these three stage looks in order, from the least light on the stage to the most.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Each lamp adds more light. One lamp gives the least, two give more, and three give the most. All three together look white.
15. (mcq) From far away, a square of thin red and green stripes looks yellowish-brown. Where does the mixing happen?  
   Options: In your eye / On the paper / Inside the pen  
   **Answer:** In your eye. *Why:* The stripes never mix on the paper. From far away, their light lands on the same cone cells in your eye and adds up there.
16. (write) A friend says, 'Red and green make brown, because that is what paints do.' Write two sentences to help your friend. What is different about lights?  
   **Answer:** Paints take colours away from the light, so mixing them makes things darker and browner. Lights add colours, so red and green lights together make yellow, which is brighter.. *Why:* A strong answer says the two kinds of mixing do opposite things: paints take away and lights add.
### Adding and Taking Away Colours (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A phone screen can show a bright yellow, but it has no yellow dots. How does it do it?  
   Options: It lights its red and green dots together / It uses a special yellow filter / It turns the blue dots black  
   **Answer:** It lights its red and green dots together. *Why:* Red and green light together look yellow to your eyes. A screen only needs red, green and blue dots to show nearly every colour.
2. (mcq) Cyan paint sends back green and blue light and takes away red. Yellow paint sends back red and green and takes away blue. Mixed together, which colour do both paints send back?  
   Options: Green / Red / Blue  
   **Answer:** Green. *Why:* Green is the only colour that both paints send back. Red is taken away by the cyan, and blue is taken away by the yellow. So the mix looks green.
3. (mcq) Why do colour printers add black ink to cyan, magenta and yellow?  
   Options: The three together make a muddy dark colour, not a deep black / Black ink is cheaper than the other colours / Black is a primary colour of paint  
   **Answer:** The three together make a muddy dark colour, not a deep black. *Why:* Cyan, magenta and yellow together take away most light, but not all. The result is a dark brownish grey. A separate black ink gives a true black and sharp letters.
4. (truefalse) A screen with red, green and blue dots can show every colour that the human eye can see.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Screens show a very large range of colours, but a pure green or blue-green, like the light of a laser, is stronger than any screen can make.
5. (fill) After staring at a green shape and looking at a white wall, you may see a pink ghost. This is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['afterimage']. *Why:* An afterimage is made in your eye. The cone cells that sensed green tired, so white looks a little pink for a moment.
6. (mcq) You stare at a red square, then look at a white wall and see a faint blue-green square. Where is that blue-green square?  
   Options: Only in your eyes and brain / On the wall / On the red square  
   **Answer:** Only in your eyes and brain. *Why:* The wall is plain white. The ghost square is made by tired cone cells in your eye. If you look at a different wall, it follows your eyes.
7. (sort) Sort these. Seen through red cellophane, does each ink on white paper seem to vanish, or look dark?  
   **Answer:** red ink → Seems to vanish; yellow ink → Seems to vanish; green ink → Looks dark; blue ink → Looks dark; black ink → Looks dark. *Why:* Red cellophane lets only red light through. The white paper, the red ink and the yellow ink all send back plenty of red, so they look alike. Green, blue and black inks send back almost no red, so they look dark.
8. (match) Match each filter to the colours of light that it lets through.  
   **Answer:** Red filter → red only; Cyan filter → green and blue; Yellow filter → red and green; Magenta filter → red and blue. *Why:* A filter lets through its own colours and takes the rest away. Cyan is green plus blue, yellow is red plus green, and magenta is red plus blue.
9. (write) Explain why red cellophane and green cellophane held together in front of a torch let almost no light through.  
   **Answer:** The red cellophane lets red light through and takes away the other colours, including green. The green cellophane then takes away the red light that got through. So almost no light gets past both sheets.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the light through the first sheet and then the second. It says what each sheet lets through and what it takes away.
10. (mcq) A navy cloth and a black cloth look alike under a warm yellowish bulb. Where would be the best place to tell them apart?  
   Options: By a window in daylight / Under a dimmer warm bulb / In the dark  
   **Answer:** By a window in daylight. *Why:* Daylight holds plenty of blue light, so the navy cloth has blue to send back and looks clearly blue. A warm bulb gives out little blue.
11. (draw) Draw a red, a green and a blue torch shining on one spot of a white wall. Label the colour in the middle. Label the colour where just two beams overlap.  
   **Answer:** The three beams overlap in the middle, which looks white. Red and green overlap as yellow. Green and blue overlap as cyan. Red and blue overlap as magenta.. *Why:* Each pair of lights makes a secondary colour, and all three together make white.
12. (perspective) Is yellow in the light, or in us? People disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses evidence. For example: the wall looked yellow with no yellow light, so the yellow was made by the eye. Or: the torches were different from real yellow, so a machine could tell the difference. It then gives the other side a fair point.. *Why:* Both are right about different things. Physicists measure the light, and vision scientists study the experience. How we name a colour depends on both.
13. (truefalse) Tiny dots of yellow and blue ink, seen from far away, blend into green.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. From far away, the light from the dots adds up in your eye and looks pale and greyish. Green appears only when yellow and blue are mixed as paints, one over the other.
14. (write) A cloth looks maroon in the shop and a brownish red at home. Give two reasons why its colour might look different.  
   **Answer:** The lamps in the shop and the light at home are not the same mix of colours, so the cloth has different light to send back. Also, my brain may adjust for the light differently in a bright shop and a dim room.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two reasons: the light is different, and the brain's adjustment is different. Colour depends on the light as well as on the cloth.
15. (mcq) A mix of red and green light looks the same as pure yellow light to your eyes. What does this tell us?  
   Options: Our eyes cannot tell apart different mixes that give the same three signals / Red and green light are really yellow / Yellow light does not exist  
   **Answer:** Our eyes cannot tell apart different mixes that give the same three signals. *Why:* Your eyes only report three signals. Different mixes of light can give the same three signals, and then they look the same to us.
16. (odd) Which is the odd one out? Yellow and blue paint stirred together, a yellow sheet over a cyan sheet, two highlighters crossing, red and green torches on a wall. Say why.  
   **Answer:** red and green torches on a wall. *Why:* The torches add light together. The other three take colours away from the light, so they get darker. Only the torches get brighter.
17. (write) A green leaf and a yellow flower are put under the same blue light. Which one looks brighter? Say why.  
   **Answer:** Both look dark. The leaf sends back green and the flower sends back red and green, and a blue light has almost none of either. Both have little to send back, so both look dark.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a thing can only send back colours that fall on it. A blue light has almost no red or green.
### Three Signals, Many Colours (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A scientist looks at a yellow lamp and at a screen showing yellow through an instrument that spreads light by wavelength. What does she find?  
   Options: Different wavelengths, even though they look alike / Exactly the same wavelengths / No light from the screen  
   **Answer:** Different wavelengths, even though they look alike. *Why:* The yellow lamp has light of one yellow wavelength. The screen has a red wavelength and a green wavelength, but no yellow. Our eyes cannot tell them apart, but the instrument can.
2. (write) Thomas Young guessed in 1802 that the eye has only three kinds of detector. Name two things you saw or did in this unit that fit his guess.  
   **Answer:** Red and green torches made a yellow wall, so two lights could stand in for one colour. Phone screens show every colour with only red, green and blue dots. If the eye had a separate detector for yellow, red plus green might not look yellow.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two pieces of evidence and says how each one fits the idea of three detectors.
3. (mcq) A dog has two kinds of cone cell. Which statement fits best?  
   Options: Some colours that differ to us may look alike to a dog / A dog sees only black and white / A dog sees more colours than we do  
   **Answer:** Some colours that differ to us may look alike to a dog. *Why:* Tests on dogs suggest they tell blue and yellow apart, but mix up red and green. With two signals instead of three, fewer kinds of difference can be told apart.
4. (mcq) Many birds have four kinds of cone cell. What could this let a bird do?  
   Options: Tell apart some mixes of light that look the same to us / See in total darkness / See only black and white  
   **Answer:** Tell apart some mixes of light that look the same to us. *Why:* A fourth kind gives a fourth signal to compare, so a bird can notice differences that give us the same three signals. Birds also see some ultraviolet.
5. (truefalse) A person with red-green colour blindness cannot see any colours.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most people with red-green colour blindness see many colours. They mix up some reds and greens, because one of their three kinds of cone cell works differently.
6. (fill) In 1861, a picture of a tartan ribbon was made from three black and white photographs, each taken through a red, a green or a blue ____.  
   **Answer:** ['filter']. *Why:* Each photograph recorded how much of one colour was in the ribbon. Shone back through the same filters on one screen, they made a colour picture.
7. (write) Georges Seurat painted with small dots of pure colour instead of mixing paints. Why might a painter choose dots?  
   **Answer:** From a distance, the dots mix in the eye by adding light, so the colours look brighter than the same paints mixed together. Mixed paints get darker because each takes away colours.. *Why:* A strong answer says that optical mixing adds light, while mixing paint takes colours away. Dots can keep colours brighter.
8. (sort) Sort these. Do scientists agree on it, or does nobody know yet?  
   **Answer:** Red and green light together look yellow. → Scientists agree; Filters take colours away from white light. → Scientists agree; Most people have three kinds of cone cell. → Scientists agree; What yellow feels like inside another person. → Nobody knows yet; Exactly how the brain turns cone signals into the feeling of a colour. → Nobody knows yet. *Why:* The first three have been tested many times. What colour feels like from the inside, and how signals become a feeling, are still open questions.
9. (mcq) A stage designer has red, green and blue lamps. She wants a pale pink glow. Which plan is best?  
   Options: A strong red lamp with weaker green and blue lamps / Only the blue lamp / Only the green lamp  
   **Answer:** A strong red lamp with weaker green and blue lamps. *Why:* Pink is red mixed with a little white. A strong red with weaker green and blue adds the white to the red. Only blue or only green would not give any red.
10. (write) Plan a fair test to find out whether the distance of the torches from the wall changes the colour made by a red torch and a green torch. Say what you change, what you keep the same, and what you look for.  
   **Answer:** I change the distance of the green torch from the wall: 50 centimetres, 1 metre and 2 metres. I keep the red torch, the wall, the room and the cellophane the same. I look at the colour of the overlap each time and write it down. I try each distance twice.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only one thing, keeps the rest the same, repeats the test and says what it will look at. A fainter green torch should make the overlap look more orange.
11. (perspective) What are the three primary colours? People give different answers.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that the right set depends on whether you are mixing light or taking colours away. It uses the torch test and the highlighter test as evidence.. *Why:* There is no single set of basic colours for everything. For lights, red, green and blue work best. For inks, cyan, magenta and yellow work best.
12. (mcq) Many people looked at the same photo of a dress and saw different colours. What best explains it?  
   Options: Their brains guessed different colours for the light that fell on the dress / Some people have no cone cells / The photo changed between looks  
   **Answer:** Their brains guessed different colours for the light that fell on the dress. *Why:* The brain tries to remove the colour of the light to find the true colour of a thing. In a photo with unclear light, brains can guess differently.
13. (mcq) A TV looks like a smooth picture from the sofa, but like separate coloured dots from one centimetre away. Why?  
   Options: From far away, the dots are too small and close for the eye to tell apart, so their light blends / The dots move apart when you step back / The TV changes its picture when you come near  
   **Answer:** From far away, the dots are too small and close for the eye to tell apart, so their light blends. *Why:* Your eye can only tell dots apart if they are far enough apart. Further away, their light lands on the same cone cells and adds up.
14. (draw) Draw a white wall lit by a red torch on the left and a blue torch on the right, with a pencil in front. Colour the lit wall and the two shadows.  
   **Answer:** The middle where both lights reach looks magenta. The shadow where the red light is blocked looks blue. The shadow where the blue light is blocked looks red.. *Why:* Each shadow takes the colour of the light that still reaches it.
15. (label) Label the drawing of the back of an eye. Show a cone cell that responds best to red, one to green and one to blue, and the nerve to the brain.  
   **Answer:** Three kinds of cone cells lie in the retina. They are labelled red, green and blue, and a nerve leaves the eye towards the brain.. *Why:* The brain compares the signals from the three kinds of cone cell and builds the colour we see.
16. (order) Put these in order from the fewest kinds of cone cell to the most.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* A dog usually has two kinds of cone cell. Most people have three. Many birds have four.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which three colours of light make white when they are shone together on one spot of a white wall?  
   Options: Red, green and blue / Red, yellow and blue / Cyan, magenta and yellow  
   **Answer:** Red, green and blue. *Why:* Red, green and blue lights add up to white. Red, yellow and blue are the old painters' set. Cyan, magenta and yellow are the three inks of a printer.
2. (mcq) A pencil stands in front of a wall lit by a red torch and a green torch. What colour is the shadow where the red light is blocked?  
   Options: Green / Red / Black  
   **Answer:** Green. *Why:* Where the red light is blocked, only the green light still reaches the wall, so the shadow looks green.
3. (mcq) A yellow sheet and a cyan sheet are held together in front of a white torch. What colour comes out?  
   Options: Green / White / Red  
   **Answer:** Green. *Why:* Yellow lets red and green through. Cyan lets green and blue through. Only green gets past both. Each sheet takes colours away.
4. (mcq) A blue shirt is lit by a pure red light. What does it look like?  
   Options: Nearly black / Bright blue / Bright red  
   **Answer:** Nearly black. *Why:* The shirt sends back blue light. A pure red light has almost no blue, so the shirt has little to send back.
5. (mcq) Why can just three lights make so many colours?  
   Options: Most eyes have three kinds of cone cell, and colour is built from their three signals / Three is a magic number in nature / The lights hide the other colours inside them  
   **Answer:** Most eyes have three kinds of cone cell, and colour is built from their three signals. *Why:* The eye turns all light into three signals. Lights that give the right three signals can look like any colour.
6. (mcq) A newspaper photo is made of tiny dots of four inks. Why does it look like a smooth picture from a metre away?  
   Options: The dots are too small and close to tell apart, so their light blends in the eye / The dots move together / The ink spreads out  
   **Answer:** The dots are too small and close to tell apart, so their light blends in the eye. *Why:* Your eye cannot tell tiny dots apart from far away. Their light lands on the same cone cells and adds up.
7. (mcq) Which sentence is the best reasoning for 'red and green torches make a yellow wall'?  
   Options: Each torch adds light to the wall, and the eye gives the same answer to red plus green as to yellow / Red and green paint make yellow / There was yellow hiding in the torches  
   **Answer:** Each torch adds light to the wall, and the eye gives the same answer to red plus green as to yellow. *Why:* Reasoning says why. It uses what lights do and what the eye does. It does not use paint, because paint takes colours away.
8. (mcq) Which of these do scientists not know yet?  
   Options: How three signals in the brain become the feeling of a colour / That filters take colours away / That red and green light look yellow together  
   **Answer:** How three signals in the brain become the feeling of a colour. *Why:* The first two were tested many times. How signals become a feeling is still an open question.
9. (mcq) Which two lights shining together make magenta?  
   Options: Red and blue / Red and green / Green and blue  
   **Answer:** Red and blue. *Why:* Red plus blue light makes magenta. Red plus green makes yellow. Green plus blue makes cyan.
10. (mcq) You stare at a bright green shape and then see a pink ghost on a white wall. What made the pink?  
   Options: Tired cone cells in your eye / A pink spot on the wall / The green shape glowing  
   **Answer:** Tired cone cells in your eye. *Why:* The cone cells that sense green tired while you stared. On white they answered less than usual, so the white looked a little pink.
11. (mcq) A yellow cloth is held in blue light. What does it look like?  
   Options: Dark / Bright yellow / Bright blue  
   **Answer:** Dark. *Why:* Yellow cloth sends back red and green, and a blue light has almost none of either.
12. (mcq) Cyan ink and yellow ink are printed on the same spot of a page. What colour does the spot look?  
   Options: Green / Red / White  
   **Answer:** Green. *Why:* Cyan ink takes away red and yellow ink takes away blue. Only green is left.

## Applications
- Phones, TVs and computer screens make every colour, and white, from tiny red, green and blue dots.
- Stage and film lighting designers use red, green and blue lamps and colour filters to set the mood of a scene.
- Colour printers make full-colour pictures from cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink.
- Boats show a red light on the left and a green light on the right at night, so that other boats can tell which way they are heading.
- Traffic signals use red, amber and green lights, so the colours are easy to tell apart from far away.
- Wedding and festival LED strip lights change colour by changing how bright their red, green and blue lamps are.
- Photographers and phone cameras use a setting called white balance, so that a white thing looks white under any lamp.
- Dyers and weavers in India check cloth colours in daylight, because lamps change how colours look.
- Colour vision tests help find people who need help with colour-coded jobs, such as wiring or reading maps.
- Painters such as Georges Seurat built whole pictures from small dots of colour that blend in the eye.
- Sunglasses and camera filters take away some light or some colours before the light reaches the eye or the sensor.
- Cross-stitch and weaving patterns put threads of different colours side by side, so that the colours mix in the eye from a distance.

## Spotted around us
- The tiny red, green and blue stripes on a phone or TV screen, when you look at it through a strong magnifying glass.
- Coloured shadows on the wall behind a stage at a wedding, where lamps of two colours shine from different sides.
- The dots in a colour newspaper photo, seen with a magnifier.
- The red and green side lights of a fishing boat at night, seen from the beach at Chennai or Kochi.
- The orange glow of some street lamps, where a red car and a blue car can look much alike.
- A kolam or rangoli made with dots of coloured powder, which seems to blend into a new colour from a few steps away.
- Festival strip lights on a house that slowly change from red to yellow to green to blue.
- A shopkeeper in a cloth market carrying a sari to the door to show its colour in daylight.
- A green leaf that looks nearly black under the red light of a bulb at a festival stall.

## Open questions
- **How do three signals from cone cells turn into the feeling of yellow?** Scientists can follow the signals from the eye into the brain. But nobody knows how those signals become the feeling of a colour. Some call this the hard problem.
- **Does your yellow feel the same as my yellow?** We can test whether two people sort colours the same way. We cannot yet find a way to compare what a colour feels like from the inside.
- **Could a person ever see a colour that nobody has seen before?** In the 1980s a few experiments reported that people saw colours such as reddish green when the eyes were tricked in a special way. Other scientists want these results repeated.
- **What do birds with four kinds of cone cell see that we cannot?** We can measure what their eyes respond to and test what they can tell apart. We cannot imagine the experience, and we cannot ask them.
- **How exactly does the brain take away the colour of the light, so that a white sheet looks white under a yellow lamp?** Scientists know the brain compares different parts of a scene. They are still working out how, and why some people guess differently, as with the famous photo of a dress.
- **Why do most people have three kinds of cone cell, and not two or four?** Scientists think three helped our early ancestors tell ripe fruit from leaves. But the full story of why we ended up with three is still being pieced together.
- **Do colours feel calm or excited in the same way for everyone?** Some studies find that people in many places link red with warmth and blue with calm. Others find that it changes from place to place and from culture to culture.

## Connected maths
- In the Eight Looks challenge, three torches are each on or off, which gives 8 settings. A school stage has four lamps instead of three, each on or off. How many settings does it have, counting all off? Explain how you worked it out. **16 settings.**. Three lamps give 8 settings. A fourth lamp can be on or off with each of those 8 settings. So there are 8 settings with it off and 8 with it on. 8 plus 8 is 16. Each new lamp doubles the settings. (queues `math.logic.counting-ways.L3`)
- In the Highlighter Layers grid there are 9 crossing squares. 3 of them are crossings of the same colour with itself. What fraction of the crossing squares are crossings of two different colours? **6 out of 9, which is the same as two thirds.**. There are 9 squares in all. 3 of them are the same colour twice. So 9 take away 3 is 6 squares with two different colours. 6 out of 9 can be shared by 3, which gives 2 out of 3. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A phone screen row is 1,000 pixels wide. Each pixel has one red, one green and one blue dot across. How many coloured dots are there along one row? **3,000 dots.**. Each pixel has 3 dots. There are 1,000 pixels. 1,000 times 3 is 3,000. So there are 3,000 coloured dots along one row. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the Wrong-Colour Flag test, Meera stared at 3 pictures for 30 seconds each, and rested her eyes for 20 seconds between them. How long did the whole test take? **130 seconds, which is 2 minutes and 10 seconds.**. Three stares of 30 seconds make 90 seconds. There are 2 rests between 3 pictures, and 2 times 20 is 40 seconds. 90 plus 40 is 130 seconds. 130 seconds is 2 minutes and 10 seconds. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- In one test, thin stripes blended into one colour when you stood 3 metres away. Stripes that were twice as wide blended at 6 metres. At what distance would stripes three times as wide blend? **9 metres.**. Twice as wide gave twice the distance, 6 metres. So the distance goes up in step with the width. Three times as wide means three times the distance. 3 times 3 is 9 metres. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Eight people did the Wrong-Colour Flag test. The chart shows how many saw the ghost flag clearly, faintly or not at all. How many people saw it at all? What fraction saw it clearly? **7 people saw it at all. 5 out of 8 saw it clearly.**. 5 saw it clearly and 2 saw it faintly. 5 plus 2 is 7. The 1 person who saw nothing is not counted. 5 of the 8 people saw it clearly, so the fraction is 5 out of 8. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- A designer says orange needs 3 units of red light for every 1 unit of green light. A big curtain gets 12 units of red light. How many units of green light does it need for the same orange? **4 units of green.**. For every 3 units of red there is 1 unit of green. 12 units of red is 4 groups of 3. So there are 4 groups of 1 unit of green, which is 4 units. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A torch gives 100 units of light. A yellow sheet lets half of the light through. A second yellow sheet lets half of what is left through. How many units reach the wall with one sheet, with two sheets and with three sheets? **50 units with one sheet, 25 units with two sheets and 12 and a half units with three sheets.**. Half of 100 is 50. Half of 50 is 25. Half of 25 is 12 and a half. Each sheet halves what is left, so the light gets weaker with every sheet. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
