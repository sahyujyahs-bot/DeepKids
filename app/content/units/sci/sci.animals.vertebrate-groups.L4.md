# Five groups, five worlds: how fish, frogs, snakes, birds and mammals sense the same place
`sci.animals.vertebrate-groups.L4`

**Summary.** Stand by a pond at dusk in Mysuru. A fish feels a falling insect through ripples on her skin. A python in the reeds finds a warm rat with pits on her lips. A pigeon sees colours you cannot, and a dog follows a smell that is hours old. All of them have a backbone, so all are vertebrates. Yet each group has a different sense that stands out, because senses are tools built for a way of life. In this unit you test your own eyes, skin and ears the way these animals use theirs, and ask whether they all live in the same world.

**Intro.** *A barn owl sits on a beam in a granary with every light switched off. She hears a rat nibble ragi on the floor, drops silently, and catches him. She never needed to see him. How can an animal hunt well in complete darkness, and what other senses do animals have that we do not?* People often say there are five senses: sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch. Animals with a backbone have those, and more. A fish has a line of tiny sensors along her sides that feel water move. Some snakes have pits that feel the heat of a warm body from a distance. Many birds see colours that people cannot see at all. In this unit you will meet the five groups of animals with a backbone again: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. This time you ask what each group senses best, and how you can test a little of it with your own body.

## Terms
- **Sense organ**: A part of the body, such as an eye, an ear or a nose, that picks up one kind of signal from the world. *e.g.* Your nose is the sense organ for smell.
- **Receptor**: A tiny cell inside a sense organ that reacts to one kind of signal, such as light, a smell or a push, and sends a message on. *e.g.* The receptors in your eye react to light.
- **Signal**: Anything in the world that a sense can pick up, such as light, sound, heat, a smell or a ripple. *e.g.* A ripple on the water is a signal for a fish.
- **Lateral line**: A line of tiny sensors along the sides of a fish. They feel water moving past the body. *e.g.* A fish in muddy water uses her lateral line to feel where another fish is.
- **Eardrum**: A thin, tight skin that shakes when sound reaches it. The shaking is passed to the inner ear. *e.g.* A frog has a round eardrum on each side of the head, with no ear flap.
- **Ear flap**: The part of the ear that sticks out from the head and collects sound. Its scientific name is the pinna. *e.g.* A fox turns her large ear flaps towards a sound.
- **Heat pit**: A small hollow in the face of some snakes that feels the heat given off by warm bodies. *e.g.* An Indian rock python has rows of heat pits along her lips.
- **Infrared**: Heat that travels as invisible rays from a warm body. People cannot see it, but skin can feel it. *e.g.* You feel infrared on your face when you stand near a lit diya.
- **Ultraviolet**: A kind of light that people cannot see. Many birds can see it. *e.g.* A bird may see a pattern in a flower or feather that is only in ultraviolet.
- **Cone**: A cell in the eye that senses colour. Different cones react to different colours. *e.g.* People have three kinds of cones, and many birds have four.
- **Field of view**: How much of the space around you your eyes can see at one time, measured in degrees. *e.g.* A pigeon sees almost all the way round. An owl sees only a narrow slice in front.
- **Binocular vision**: Seeing the same thing with two eyes at once. It lets the brain judge how far away something is. *e.g.* A cat uses both eyes to judge a jump onto a wall.
- **Echolocation**: Finding things by making a sound and listening to the echo that bounces back. *e.g.* A small bat calls out in the dark and hears the echo from a flying moth.
- **Infrasound**: Very low sound that people cannot hear, though they may feel it as a throb. *e.g.* Asian elephants call to each other with infrasound across a forest.
- **Prey**: An animal that other animals hunt and eat. *e.g.* A rat is prey for a barn owl.
- **Predator**: An animal that hunts and eats other animals. *e.g.* A barn owl is a predator of rats.

## Explanations
### What a sense is: a way to pick up one kind of signal · *defined*
**Definition:** A sense is a way a body picks up one kind of signal. Tiny cells called receptors react to the signal and send a message to the brain. Receptors are often gathered in a sense organ, such as an eye or an ear.

The world is full of signals. There is light, sound, smell, heat, touch and the movement of water or air. No receptor reacts to all of them. An eye reacts to light but not to smell. A nose reacts to tiny bits of a thing but not to sound. So every sense is a kind of question. The eye asks, where is the light? The ear asks, where is the shaking? All animals with a backbone have eyes, ears, a nose, taste and touch. But the five groups have developed some senses much further than others, and a few groups have senses that people do not have at all.
- e.g. A cat's whiskers react to a push from air or a wall.
- e.g. A frog's eardrum reacts to the shaking of sound.
- e.g. A fish's lateral line reacts to moving water.
### Fish: feeling water move with the lateral line · *defined*
**Definition:** A lateral line is a row of tiny sensors along a fish's head and sides. Each sensor has a bundle of hairs that bends when water moves past. The bending sends a message to the brain.

Water is thick, and it carries movement a long way. When another fish swims past, or an insect lands on the surface, the water moves. A fish with a lateral line feels that movement along her whole body, even in muddy or dark water. She can tell the direction and often how far. Many fish also hear through an inner ear that sits inside the head, with no ear opening. Their bodies are mostly water, so sound goes straight through. Fish smell with nostrils that take in water. Catfish such as magur have whiskers called barbels with taste cells, so they can taste the mud as they feel along it. Sharks and rays can even sense the very weak electricity made by the muscles of other animals.
- e.g. A rohu or catla in a pond turns towards a ripple without seeing what made it.
- e.g. A magur catfish tastes the muddy bottom with her whiskers.
- e.g. A shark can find a fish hidden in sand by the electricity of that fish's muscles.
### Amphibians: eardrums, thin skin and eyes for movement · *defined*
**Definition:** A frog has a round eardrum on each side of her head, behind the eye. Sound shakes the eardrum, and the shaking is passed to the inner ear.

A frog has no ear flap. Her eardrum sits flat on the skin, like the skin of a drum. Frogs hear each other's calls, and during the monsoon the calls of male frogs fill the night so that females can find them. A frog's eyes bulge on top of her head, so she can see above the water while most of her body stays hidden. In 1959 a team at MIT in the USA studied a frog's eye and found cells that react to small dark moving things, like flies. This is one reason a frog may ignore a dead fly that lies still, and snap at one that moves. Tadpoles have a lateral line like a fish. Many adult amphibians keep it only if they live in water. Frog skin is thin and wet, and also senses touch and some chemicals.
- e.g. The loud chorus of Indian bullfrogs in the first monsoon rains.
- e.g. A frog sits with only her eyes above the water.
- e.g. Tadpoles feel ripples with a lateral line, and then lose it as they leave the water.
### Reptiles: scent from the tongue, feeling the ground and sensing heat · *defined*
**Definition:** A heat pit is a small hollow on a snake's face with a thin membrane inside. It reacts to the infrared given off by warm bodies. Pit vipers, boas and pythons have them.

A snake has no outer ear and no eardrum on the outside. She feels the shaking of the ground through her jaw bone, and her inner ear picks it up. Her forked tongue flicks out, collects tiny bits of scent from the air and the ground, and wipes them onto two spots in the roof of her mouth. The two tips of the fork can gather scent from two sides at once, which may help her follow a trail. Some snakes can also sense heat. An Indian rock python has rows of small heat pits along her lips. Cobras and Russell's vipers do not have pits. Crocodiles have tiny dark dots on their jaws that feel ripples and pressure, so a mugger crocodile can feel a fish swim near without seeing the fish. Some geckos can see colour in light so dim that people see only grey.
- e.g. A python senses the heat of a rat in a dark storeroom.
- e.g. A cobra follows scent with her tongue.
- e.g. A mugger crocodile floats with her jaws just under the water, ready to feel a ripple.
### Birds: sharp eyes, extra colours and hearing · *defined*
**Definition:** Most birds have four kinds of colour cones in their eyes. People have three. The fourth kind lets a bird see ultraviolet light.

Sight is the main sense for most birds. Their eyes are large for the size of their head, and they see small details and fast movement very well. Because of the fourth cone, a bird sees colours and patterns that we do not. In 2005 a scientist named Mark Eaton checked the feathers of many birds. He found that in most species where males and females look the same to us, the two sexes look different in ultraviolet. Birds also hear well. A barn owl has a flat, heart-shaped face that works like a dish and gathers sound, and her two ears sit at slightly different heights. She can catch a mouse in a completely dark room by sound alone. Most birds do not smell strongly, but a few do. A turkey vulture can find food by smell.
- e.g. A male and a female of many bird kinds look alike to us, but look different to each other in ultraviolet.
- e.g. A barn owl catches a rat in a dark granary by sound.
- e.g. A crow notices a small movement on the road from far away.
### Mammals: smell, ears and whiskers, and sounds we cannot hear · *defined*
**Definition:** Mammals are the only animals with three tiny bones in the middle ear, which pass sound from the eardrum to the inner ear. Most mammals also have ear flaps that can collect sound.

Many mammals live by smell. A dog has many tens of times more smell receptors in her nose than a person has, and the part of her brain for smell is large. A cat or a deer turns her ear flaps one at a time towards a sound. Whiskers are long, stiff hairs with a rich supply of nerves at the base. They feel a push from air or from a wall in the dark. Harbour seals can follow the swirl left in the water by a fish that swam past about half a minute ago, using only their whiskers. Small insect-eating bats such as the Indian pipistrelle make sounds too high for people to hear, then listen to the echoes. In 1984 Katy Payne stood near elephants at a zoo in Oregon in the USA and felt a throbbing in the air that she could not hear. She later recorded Asian elephants calling with sounds too low for people to hear. Elephants also feel rumbles through the ground with their feet.
- e.g. A dog follows the smell of someone who walked past an hour ago.
- e.g. A bat finds a moth by the echo of her own call.
- e.g. An elephant listens to a far-off call through her feet and trunk.
### Wide view or deep view: where the eyes sit · *defined*
**Definition:** A field of view is how much space around you your eyes can see at once. Binocular vision is when both eyes see the same space, which helps judge how far away something is.

Eyes on the sides of the head see a very wide view. A pigeon sees about 340 degrees, almost all the way round. A horse also sees almost all the way round. That is useful to an animal that is hunted, because she can spot a hunter coming from almost any side. But the two eyes share very little space, so she does not judge distance well with both eyes together. Eyes at the front of the head, as in a person or an owl, see a narrower view, but the two eyes overlap in a large area. The brain compares the two pictures and works out how far away something is. That helps a hunter pounce, or an animal jump from branch to branch. A person sees about 200 degrees in total, and an owl somewhere near 110 degrees, because books give slightly different numbers. An owl cannot move her eyes in their sockets, so she turns her whole head, up to about 270 degrees.
- e.g. A pigeon on a wall notices a cat creeping up from behind.
- e.g. A cat judges the distance to a mouse before she jumps.
- e.g. A person catching a ball with one eye closed finds it much harder.
### Colour: who sees more, and who sees less · *defined*
**Definition:** Colour vision depends on how many kinds of cone an animal has. More kinds of cone usually means more colours can be told apart.

People have three kinds of cones, so we see red, green, blue and everything mixed from them. Most mammals have only two kinds. A dog is not colour-blind in the sense of seeing only black and white. A dog sees blues and yellows well, but cannot tell red from green, like some people. Many birds, fish, turtles and some lizards have four kinds, including one for ultraviolet. A goldfish can see ultraviolet light. So the same pond looks different to a dog, a person and a bird. Seeing more colours is not always better. Animals that hunt at night usually trade colour for the ability to see in dim light.
- e.g. A dog sees a red ball on green grass as a dull ball on yellowish grass.
- e.g. A pigeon tells apart colours that look the same to us.
- e.g. A gecko at night has to see in dim light.
### Senses people do not have: electric, heat and magnetic · *debated*
Some animals sense signals that people cannot sense at all. Sharks and rays feel the electricity that other animals' muscles make. Some snakes feel infrared heat. Many birds, turtles and fish seem to use the Earth's magnetic field to find their way. Scientists have shown this by changing the magnetic field around the animals and seeing them change direction. How the animals sense the field is still debated. Nobody has found the magnetic receptor with certainty. This is one of the open puzzles in biology.
- e.g. A shark finds a fish hidden under sand by the weak electricity the fish gives off.
- e.g. A python feels the heat of a rat in the dark.
- e.g. A migrating bird holds her course on a cloudy night.
- **Light-based idea (cryptochrome):** A protein in the eye, called cryptochrome, may react to the magnetic field when light hits it. Birds might then see the field as a pattern laid over what they see. Robins stopped finding their way when weak radio noise disturbed this effect.
- **Iron-based idea (magnetite):** Tiny pieces of the iron mineral magnetite in the body may act like a small compass needle. Cells with iron were once found in birds' beaks, but later work showed that many of them were white blood cells, not nerve cells.
- **Both together:** Some scientists think an animal might use one method for the compass direction and another for a map of where she is. Nobody knows yet.
### Why no animal senses everything · *idea*
Eyes, ears and the brain parts that read them use a lot of energy. A body that spends energy on a sense it hardly needs wastes food. Mexican tetras that live in dark caves have lost their eyes, and a study in 2015 worked out that keeping eyes would cost them a noticeable part of their daily energy. They have more sensors on their lateral lines instead. The Ganges river dolphin, called susu, lives in muddy water where eyes are little help. Her eyes have no working lens and tell only light from dark. She finds fish by sending out clicks and listening for the echoes. The Ganges river dolphin is India's national aquatic animal. So each animal's senses are a trade, and they fit the place where her body lives. This is an idea that most biologists accept, but the exact costs are still being measured.
- e.g. A cave fish with no eyes and a strong lateral line.
- e.g. A susu in the muddy Ganges, finding fish by echoes.
- e.g. A deer in the dark who hears and smells more than she sees.
### One pond, many worlds · *debated*
Put a fish, a pigeon, a snake and a person at the same pond. They have different senses, so they notice different things. The question is what this means. The German biologist Jakob von Uexküll wrote in 1934 that each animal lives in her own sensory world, which he called an umwelt. His famous example was a tick. A tick waits on a branch and reacts to three signals only: the smell of sweat acid, warmth of about body heat, and the touch of hairy skin. Nothing else about the world matters to her. Other scientists say there is just one world, and each animal gets a partial view of that world, as if looking through different windows. Which is it? We can compare what animals sense, but we cannot yet say what it is like to sense as a bat does.
- e.g. A dog that smells a whole story on a lamp post, while a person smells nothing.
- e.g. A bird that sees a feather pattern in ultraviolet that a person never sees.
- e.g. A tick that responds to only three things in the whole world.
- **Jakob von Uexküll, 1934:** Each animal lives inside her own sensory world, made of the signals her body can pick up and the things that matter to her. A tick and a person really do live in different worlds.
- **Many physicists and biologists today:** There is one real world. Every animal senses a part of it, like different windows onto one room. We can learn the rest of the room by using instruments, and by comparing what different animals notice.

## Mostly agreed
- All animals with a backbone sense light, sound or shaking, smell and taste, touch and balance. The five groups have developed some of these senses much further than others. *How we know:* Scientists have looked inside the bodies of fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals, and recorded messages from their nerves when each kind of signal is given.
- Fish feel moving water with the lateral line. *How we know:* When scientists switch off the lateral line with a mild chemical, fish find the source of a ripple much less well in the dark, while fish with a working lateral line find it easily.
- Some snakes sense the heat of warm bodies with heat pits. *How we know:* Scientists have blindfolded pit vipers such as rattlesnakes. The snakes still struck at a warm target, and did not strike at a cool one.
- A barn owl can find a mouse in total darkness using only sound. *How we know:* In 1971 Roger Payne tested barn owls in a pitch-dark room. They landed on a rustling piece of paper pulled across the floor. This also worked when there was no mouse, only the paper.
- Many bats find their way and their food by echolocation. *How we know:* In 1938 Donald Griffin and Robert Galambos used a microphone that could pick up very high sounds. They recorded bats calling, and showed that bats with their mouths taped shut crashed into obstacles.
- Most birds have four kinds of colour cone in their eyes, and can see ultraviolet light. *How we know:* The cones of many birds have been studied under the microscope, and birds respond in tests to patterns that exist only in ultraviolet.

## How we found out
**Lazzaro Spallanzani, Georges Cuvier, Donald Griffin and Robert Galambos, 1793 to 1938.** In 1793 an Italian priest and scientist named Lazzaro Spallanzani wanted to know how bats fly in the dark without hitting anything. People then believed that eyes were the only way to find a path through a room. So he covered the eyes of bats, and they flew around the room and avoided obstacles just as before. Next he blocked their ears, and the bats bumped into things. He concluded that the ears were doing the work of eyes. A famous French scientist, Georges Cuvier, did not believe it. He said that bats must feel the air with the skin of their wings. For nearly 150 years nobody could prove who was right. Then in 1938 two students, Donald Griffin and Robert Galambos, used a new microphone that could pick up very high sounds. It showed that bats make sounds too high for people to hear, and listen for the echoes.  
*What people believed before:* That eyes are the only way to find a path in the dark, so bats must be feeling the air with their skin.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Bats are blind.** (A very old saying, 'blind as a bat'.; *rejected*)  
  For: Bats fly easily in the dark, and many small bats use sound more than sight.  
  Against: All bats can see. Large fruit bats, such as the Indian flying fox, find ripe fruit with their eyes and their noses. Small bats that use echolocation can also see.
- **A snake stings with her tongue.** (Many people, because the tongue flicks like a tiny stinger.; *rejected*)  
  For: The forked tongue moves fast and looks sharp.  
  Against: The tongue is soft and harmless. It carries tiny bits of scent into the mouth. A snake bites with her teeth, and some snakes use fangs to put venom in.
- **Dogs see only in black and white.** (A popular belief in many places.; *rejected*)  
  For: Dogs do not seem to care about colour, and their eyes have fewer colour cones than ours.  
  Against: Dogs can tell blue from yellow. They have two kinds of cones. What they cannot do is tell red from green, like some people.
- **An owl can turn her head all the way round.** (Stories and cartoons.; *rejected*)  
  For: An owl turns her head so far that she can look straight behind her.  
  Against: An owl turns her head about 270 degrees, three-quarters of the way round. She has extra neck bones and special blood vessels that let her do this without cutting off blood to her brain.
- **A frog will starve beside a pile of dead flies, because she cannot see food that does not move.** (A claim often made after the 1959 frog eye study.; *minority*)  
  For: A frog's eye has cells that react most to small moving dark things, and a frog in a bare tank may ignore still food.  
  Against: A frog also uses smell, and some frogs learn to eat still food in captivity, especially if it is wiggled. The study showed what the eye reports, not everything a frog does.
- **Birds sense the Earth's magnetic field through their eyes.** (Some biologists, such as Thorsten Ritz and Klaus Schulten, and many later researchers.; *debated*)  
  For: Robins lose their direction when weak radio noise disturbs a chemical effect in the eye, and the effect needs light.  
  Against: No one has found the receptor with certainty, and some experiments point to iron-based sensors or to more than one method.

## Roots
We have seen that a fish, a snake, a bird and a bat each sense a different slice of the same place. Now we ask what that tells us about the world and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** A fish feels the pond as moving water. A python feels it as warm and cool. A pigeon sees colours we never see. So the world each of us notices is only the slice our body was built to catch.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the dark', 'the pond' or 'the colour of the feather' as if every animal met the same picture. But the dark is not dark to an owl who hears, and a feather has a pattern for birds that people never see.  
*Evidence:* In the wide-view test, most people see a little more than half the way round, while a pigeon sees almost all the way round. In the ripple test, most people feel a ripple only when it is close, while a fish feels one along her whole side. An owl catches a rat in a pitch-dark granary by sound alone.

- *who am i:* You are one animal among many, and your eyes, ears and skin show you only a slice of what is there.
- *what is life:* Every living being, from a tick to an elephant, listens to the world in her own way, and answers it.
- *what is this world:* It looks like one place that many bodies sense in many ways, and no body senses all of it.

All animals with a backbone began as small fish in the sea more than 500 million years ago. Each group kept its own senses and grew some of them very far. Yet all senses do one job. They let something in the world reach a body, and let the body answer. Every sense organ is a place where one part of nature picks up a signal from another part.

- **Kena Upanishad 1.2:** The Upanishad speaks of the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind, the speech of speech and the eye of the eye. It points to something behind each sense that does the sensing. (A fish, a bat and a bird all hear, feel and see in different ways. The teaching asks what is the same behind all those different ears and eyes.)
- **Udana 6.4, the blind men and the elephant:** In this old story from the Buddhist teachings, several blind men each touch a different part of an elephant. One feels a leg, another the tail, another an ear. Each describes a different animal, and each argues that he is right. (Each group of animals senses a different part of the same place. No body gets the whole elephant, and each is partly right.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 17 (Autumn Floods):** Two friends stand on a bridge over a river. Zhuangzi says the fish are happy. His friend Huizi says, you are not a fish, so how do you know? Zhuangzi answers, you are not me, so how do you know that I do not know? (We cannot sense a lateral line or a heat pit from the inside. The old question remains: how far can one being know what it is like to be another?)
- Science (settled): Each animal can pick up only some of the signals around her, and the signals differ from group to group. The biologist Jakob von Uexküll called each animal's sensory world her umwelt.
- Science (open): Nobody knows what it feels like to sense as a bat or a snake does. In 1974 the philosopher Thomas Nagel asked 'What is it like to be a bat?' to show how hard the question is.
- Other view, Many neuroscientists: A sense is a set of cells that react to a signal and pass it on. What matters is what the animal does with the signal. Asking what the bat feels adds nothing we can test, and may not be a good question.
- Practice, Listen like an owl (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Cup both hands behind your ears, with your fingers pointing forward. Listen for one minute. Count how many sounds you can find, and point to where each one comes from. Now take your hands away and listen again. Then open your eyes and look for each sound.
- Ask: If a pigeon could look at your street, what would she say you are missing?
- Ask: A python senses a rat as a warm glow. Is the rat warm, or bright, or smelly? Who decides?
- Ask: Is there a sense that no animal has found yet?
- Ask: When you and a dog smell the same lamp post, are you smelling the same thing?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A python senses a rat as a warm glow. A person senses the same rat as a shape and a smell. Which of them is sensing what the rat really is? (hint: Think about whether 'warm', 'brown' and 'smelly' belong to the rat, or to the meeting of a rat and a body.)
- *deep:* Hunted animals tend to have eyes on the sides of the head, and hunters have eyes at the front. What does each way of seeing gain, and what does it lose? (hint: Think about the wide view and the deep view that you tested.)
- *deep:* A lateral line works well in water but would work badly in air. Why? (hint: Think about how far a ripple travels in water, and how far a puff of air moves a feather.)
- *big:* The philosopher Thomas Nagel asked in 1974, 'What is it like to be a bat?' Could you ever find out? What would count as an answer? (hint: A bat can hear echoes, and you can only imagine them. Is imagining enough?)
- *big:* Cameras can record ultraviolet and heat that we cannot see. Do such cameras show the world as it really is? (hint: A camera also picks up only some signals. Who chooses which ones?)
- *big:* If you could borrow one sense from another animal for a day, which one would you pick, and what do you think you would find out? (hint: Think about a place you know well, such as your street, and what might be hiding in it.)
- *deep:* An owl turns her head, a deer turns her ears and a snake flicks her tongue. In each case, what is the animal doing, and what question is she asking the world? (hint: Each movement points a sense organ at a place. Notice which sense it is.)
- *big:* Dogs can be trained to sniff out some illnesses in people. What does that tell you about what a smell can carry? (hint: A smell is made of tiny bits of a thing. What else might be carried in those bits?)

## Story: The Owl in the Granary
*A made-up story. Kavya, Ajji and Farhan are imagined, though Mandya, in Karnataka, is a real place where ragi is grown. The science is real: barn owls hunt by sound, even in a pitch-dark room, as Roger Payne showed in 1971. Owl pellets really do hold the fur and bones of what the owl ate. In some places people believe that an owl in the house is a bad sign. In others the owl is a friend of the farmer.*

In the holidays, Kavya went to her grandmother's house near Mandya. Her cousin Farhan came from Hyderabad. It was the time after the ragi harvest, and the sacks stood in the store room behind the house, piled up to the beam.

Every morning, Ajji came out of the store room with her lips tight. 'The rats were here again,' she said. A sack in the corner had a hole, and a small heap of ragi lay on the floor like brown sand.

Ajji had her own plan. At night she left an oil lamp burning in the store room, because rats hate light. When she heard a rustle, she banged a steel plate with a spoon. The noise was so loud that the whole house jumped. The rats ran. But in the morning there was always another hole.

High on the beam above the sacks lived a white barn owl. She had a pale heart-shaped face and big dark eyes. Ajji did not like her at all. 'An owl in the house brings bad luck,' she said. 'My own grandmother told me so.' She waved a broom at the beam every evening. The owl only looked down at her, blinked, and sat still.

One morning, Kavya found grey lumps on the floor under the beam. They were as big as her thumb and dry like old felt. 'Dirt,' said Farhan. Kavya was not so sure. She pulled one apart with a stick, and inside she found fur, and then thin white bones. At the very end she found a tiny skull with two long front teeth. 'This is a rat,' she said slowly. 'The owl eats them. Ajji, she is working for you.'

Ajji sniffed. 'She may eat one now and then,' she said. 'But the rats come back every night.' Kavya thought about this all day. At night the lamp was burning and the plate was banging, so the owl could not be hunting. Maybe that was the problem.

'How does she catch them at all?' asked Farhan. 'It is pitch dark under the beam, except for the lamp.' 'With her eyes,' said Kavya. 'Owls have night eyes.' But then she stopped. 'If she can see in the dark, why does Ajji put a lamp?' And then she asked a better question. 'What if she does not need to see?'

The next evening, Kavya asked Ajji to put out the lamp. Ajji frowned for a long time. Then she agreed, as long as she herself would sit in the doorway with them. They sat on a mat, all three, with the door open only a hand-width. The store room was black. Kavya could not see her own fingers.

For a long time nothing happened. The wind moved in the coconut trees outside. Then there was a tiny sound, a dry scratch, like a fingernail on paper. A rat was working at a sack. Almost at once there was a soft whoosh of air, and then a squeak, and then nothing. Farhan snapped on his torch. The beam was empty. The owl was already back on her perch, with something in her claws.

'She did not even need the torch,' whispered Farhan. 'She did not need a lamp either,' said Kavya. 'She heard him.' Ajji said nothing, but she was staring up at the beam.

Kavya wanted to be sure. The next night, she tied a cloth bag to the end of a long string. She filled it with a handful of dry husks, so it would make a dry little rustle. She laid it in the far corner, and held the other end of the string at the doorway. The room was dark again. Ajji sat beside her. Nobody spoke.

Kavya gave the string one small pull. The husks whispered. Up on the beam, there was a tiny movement. Kavya pulled again. Then the white shape slid off the beam without a sound, and dropped. She landed right on the bag, and pinned it with her claws. Kavya heard her stand very still, as if waiting for the bag to move. It did not. There was no rat there, only husks and a cloth.

Farhan laughed out loud, so quietly that it was almost just air. The owl flew back to the beam. Ajji let out a long breath. 'She went for the sound,' she said. 'She never saw it.' 'And she turned her whole face to the sound before she dropped,' said Kavya. 'Her face is like a dish. It catches the sound and sends it to her ears.'

Ajji sat for a while in the dark. Then she said, 'My grandmother was a wise woman. But she did not sit in the dark and pull a string.' She said it gently, and Kavya did not laugh. 'Each of us sees what we see,' said Ajji. 'Tonight I have seen something new.'

That night, Ajji blew out the lamp for good. She opened the little window under the roof, so the owl could come and go. She put the steel plate back in the kitchen, where it belonged. A week later, the sacks were whole. A fortnight later, there were no new holes at all, only grey pellets on the floor, which Farhan swept up every morning with great seriousness.

On the last night of the holidays, Kavya sat on the mat and pressed both hands behind her ears, with her fingers pointing forward. The rustle of a leaf in the yard suddenly sounded bigger and closer. She took her hands away, and it shrank again. 'So that is why foxes have big ears,' she whispered. Up on the beam, the owl turned her face and looked at her.

**For the parent:** An owl does not need light to find a rat. She hears the rustle, turns her dish-shaped face towards it, and drops. In the dark, a lamp helps the rats more than it helps the owl.

- Ask: A barn owl in a dark room drops onto a rustling bag that has no rat in it. What does that tell you about how she finds prey?
- Ask: Why might loud banging near a barn owl make it harder for her to catch a rat, even if it makes the rats run?
- Ask: If you cup your hands behind your ears, a faint rustle sounds louder. What do your hands do, and which animals have ears that do the same job?

### g2 · Owl and Mice: catch a mouse by sound (15 min)
- **rules:** Mark a start line and a finish line on the floor, about 5 metres apart, with a chalk line or a strip of cloth.; One player is the owl. The owl sits on a low stool, or on a cushion on the floor, in the middle with eyes closed or a scarf over them, and holds a rolled newspaper for pointing.; The other players are mice.   - Think: How could a mouse cross a dry-leaf floor without being heard? What would an owl need to change?

## Experiments
### e1 · Wide View, Deep View: how far round can you see, and can you judge distance? (40 min)
- **question:** How far round can you see with two eyes, and with only one? And do two eyes help you judge how far away something is?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess two things. At which clock number will you first see the moving card as it comes round from behind you? And will you touch the pen top more often with two eyes or with one? Give a reason for each guess. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same person keeps their eyes on the same note on the wall every time, and the same helper waves the same card at the same height and speed. The circle, the chair and the distances do not change. Only the eye that is open changes. Each wide-view test is done twice, because one try can be a fluke. The pen test is done six times for each eye setting, with the pen in a new spot each time.
- **safety:** Use a pen with its cap on, never a sharp pencil, and keep it at arm's length from the face so nobody pokes an eye. Cover an eye with a cloth held gently or with the palm. Never press on the eye.
- **record:** Write down the clock number where your child first saw the card move, for each test. Multiply by 30 to get degrees. For the pen test, count how many times out of 6 the finger touched the cap with two eyes, and with one eye. Draw a bar chart of the best angle for each test, and a second bar chart of the pen hits.
- **results table:** Test | Clock number first seen: try 1 | Clock number first seen: try 2 | Angle in degrees (clock number times 30)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the setup. Ask for their two guesses and the reasons. Write them at the top of the results table.  
  *Parent says:* Today we test your eyes. A card will come round from behind you, and you say when you first see it move. Then you try to touch a pen with one finger. What do you think will happen, and why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We test your eyes today. Guess first. When will you see the card move? Will one eye see less? Why?
  - Ask: Why might covering one eye change what you can see? (good: One eye sees less.; The nose gets in the way.; Each eye sees a different side.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the chair in the middle of an open floor. Stick the note on the wall 2 metres straight ahead, at your child's eye height. Put the 7 markers in a half circle on the right side, 1 metre from the chair. Number 12 is straight ahead, 3 is straight to the right, and 6 is right behind.  
  *Parent says:* Sit here and look at the note on the wall. Keep your head still. The numbers on the floor are like a clock. Twelve is in front of you, three is on your right, and six is right behind you.  
  *Child mode:* Sit on the chair. Look at the note on the wall. Keep your head still.
  - Note: Paper cups or chappals make good markers. Put each number on a slip under its marker. The helper will stand beside each marker and hold the card at your child's eye height.
  - Check: Why must your child keep looking at the note on the wall? → **So only the card moves, and the eyes stay still**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test with both eyes open. The helper starts at marker 6, behind the chair, and holds the card at eye height. The helper waves the card gently up and down for 3 seconds. Then the helper moves to marker 5, waves again, and so on. The child says 'now' when they first see anything move. Write down the number. Do the whole test twice.  
  *Parent says:* I will wave the card behind you, then move round the circle. Say now, the moment you see anything move, even a blur. Keep looking at the note.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the note. Say now when you see the card move.
  - Ask: At which number did you first see it move? Could you see its colour straight away? (good: Around three or four.; I saw something move, but not what it was.; The colour came later.)
  - Note: Most people first notice movement around 3 or 4 on the clock, which is 90 to 120 degrees from straight ahead. Colour usually comes later, nearer the front. Your child's numbers may differ. Write down what you get.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to cover the left eye with the cloth. They keep looking at the note with the right eye. Test the right side twice, as before. Then they cover the right eye and you test the right side twice again. Write down each number.  
  *Parent says:* Cover your left eye gently. The card comes round on your right side again. Say now when you see it move. Then we cover the right eye and do it again.  
  *Child mode:* Cover one eye. Say now when the card moves. Then swap eyes.
  - Ask: With which eye covered did you see the card latest? Why do you think that happened? (good: With my right eye covered.; My nose was in the way.; The right eye looks out to the right.)
  - Note: With the right eye covered, the nose blocks the view on the right, so the card usually shows up much later, around 1 or 2. With the left eye covered, the right eye still sees almost as far as with both eyes. Press gently on the cloth and never on the eye.
  - Check: The card comes in from the right. Which eye can see it earliest? → **The right eye, because it looks out to the right**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn each clock number into degrees by multiplying by 30. Write the angle in the last column. Then double the best angle for both open eyes. This is a rough size of the whole view.  
  *Parent says:* Twelve is zero degrees, straight ahead. Each number is 30 degrees more. If you saw it at four, that is four times thirty, which is 120 degrees. What angle did you get with both eyes open?  
  *Child mode:* Multiply the clock number by 30. That is the angle. Write it down.
  - Note: The doubled number assumes the left side sees as far as the right. For most people it comes out between about 180 and 240 degrees in total. Do not worry if the number is different. Small angles in this test are normal because the card is small and slow.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit your child at a table. Stand the capped pen in clay on the table, about 40 centimetres in front of them. Your child holds one finger beside their ear. They swing the finger down and touch the cap in one smooth move. They must not feel for it. Do six tries with both eyes open. After each try, move the pen to a new spot 30, 40 or 50 centimetres away. Count how many touches.  
  *Parent says:* Hold one finger beside your ear. Look at the pen. Now swing your finger down and touch the top of the cap in one move. No feeling about. I will move the pen after each try.  
  *Child mode:* Hold a finger by your ear. Touch the pen cap in one move.
  - Note: A touch means the fingertip lands on the cap. A near miss counts as a miss. Move the pen to a new place each time so your child cannot predict where it will be.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now your child covers one eye. Do six more tries with the pen in new spots. Count how many touches. Compare the two counts.  
  *Parent says:* Cover one eye gently. Look at the pen with the other eye. Touch the top of the cap in one move, like before.  
  *Child mode:* Cover one eye. Touch the pen cap in one move.
  - Note: Most people touch the cap more often with two eyes. Your child's counts may be different, and that is fine. If one eye does as well as two, say so honestly. Some people judge distance well with one eye by using other clues, such as size.
  - Check: A child touches the cap 5 times out of 6 with two eyes, and 2 times out of 6 with one eye. What does this suggest? → **Two eyes help to judge distance**
- **s8 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw one bar chart of the best angle for each wide-view test. Draw a second bar chart of the pen touches. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then tell them: a pigeon sees about 340 degrees, and an owl about 110 degrees.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Now look at the numbers. A pigeon sees about 340 degrees, and an owl about 110. Which one needs to see behind, and which one needs to judge distance?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the bars. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says something like this: eyes on the sides see a wider view, and eyes at the front overlap and judge distance. If your child says the nose got in the way, that is a very good observation. Do not explain until they have said what they saw.
  - Think: A pigeon is hunted and an owl hunts. How do their eyes fit that?
### e2 · Ripple Skin: how far away can your hands feel a ripple? (35 min)
- **question:** A fish feels a ripple with her lateral line. How far away can your hands feel a ripple, and can you tell which side it came from?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Your hands are in still water and a ripple starts to your left or right. Will you tell the side correctly when it is close? Will you at 20 centimetres? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same helper makes every ripple with the same gentle dip of one fingertip. The water is still before each ripple. Your child's ears are covered, so only the skin can sense it. A coin toss decides left or right, so the helper cannot make a pattern. Only the distance changes. Each distance is tried 10 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Put the basin on a towel on a flat, low surface so it does not tip. Wipe up spills at once, because a wet floor is slippery. Keep electrical things, including headphones, away from the water. Cover the ears with ear muffs or a folded towel.
- **record:** For each distance, write how many of the 10 ripples your child located correctly. Guessing alone would give about 5. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each distance.
- **results table:** Distance from your hand | Right answers out of 10 | Score you would get by pure guessing | Better than guessing?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the basin. Ask for their guesses and reasons. Write them at the top of the results table.  
  *Parent says:* Fish have a line of tiny sensors along their sides that feel water move. Can your hands do anything like it? Will you tell which side a ripple came from? Will you at 20 centimetres? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Fish feel water move. Can your hands? Guess first. Will you tell left from right? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think your hands will feel when a ripple reaches them? (good: A tiny wobble.; Nothing at all when it is far.; A touch like a very light tickle.)
  - Note: Do not tell what a lateral line is yet. Let your child make their guess first. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the towel on a low, flat table or the floor. Put the basin on it with 4 centimetres of water. Your child kneels or sits beside it. They put both hands flat in the middle of the water, with fingers spread. They cover their ears and close their eyes. Cut a card strip 20 centimetres long. Mark it at 5 and 10 centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* Put both hands flat in the middle of the water. Keep them still, like a fish resting. Close your eyes, and we cover your ears, so you cannot hear or see anything. Only your skin will tell you.  
  *Child mode:* Put both hands in the water. Keep them still. Close your eyes.
  - Note: Wait until the water is completely still before every ripple. This takes about 10 seconds. If the child's hands touch the sides, move the basin so that the hands sit in the middle.
  - Check: Why do we cover the child's ears and eyes? → **So only the skin of the hands can sense the ripple**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start with 5 centimetres. Toss the coin. Heads means the left side, and tails means the right side. Hold the strip above the water with one end over the child's hand on that side. Dip one fingertip, 1 centimetre deep, at the other end. Quickly lift it out and keep still. Ask 'left or right?' Do 10 tries. Count the right answers.  
  *Parent says:* I will make one small ripple, then you say left or right. Wait until I say go. I make the ripple 5 centimetres from your hand.  
  *Child mode:* Feel for the ripple. Say left or right. Try ten times.
  - Note: Wait for still water between tries. Do not say if the answer is right until the end of the 10. Your child may get nearly all 10 right here. This is the closest distance.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now test 10 centimetres in the same way. Use the strip to measure from the child's hand. Toss the coin each time, and wait for still water. Do 10 tries and count the right answers.  
  *Parent says:* Now the ripple will start further away, 10 centimetres from your hand. Feel for it, and say left or right.  
  *Child mode:* Now the ripple is farther away. Say left or right.
  - Ask: Was it easier or harder than before? What did your hands feel? (good: Harder, the ripple was weaker.; I guessed more.; I felt a tiny wobble sometimes.)
  - Note: Expect the score to drop for most children, but it varies. Keep the same gentle dip, not a bigger one.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Last, test 20 centimetres. Use the strip again. Toss the coin each time and wait for still water. Do 10 tries and count the right answers.  
  *Parent says:* The last ripple will be further still, 20 centimetres from your hand. Feel for it, and say left or right. It is fine to guess.  
  *Child mode:* The ripple is far now. Say left or right. Guess if you must.
  - Note: If your child still gets 9 or 10, try 30 centimetres in a wider basin. If the score is near 5, that is real data. Say so.
  - Check: At 20 centimetres, your child gets 5 right out of 10. What does that mean? → **About what pure guessing gives, so the skin felt little at that distance**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the three scores in the table. Draw a bar chart. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then tell them how a lateral line works.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Now I will tell you what a fish has that you do not.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each distance. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: After your child's own words, say: a fish has a line of tiny sensors along each side with hairs that bend when water moves. She feels where another fish is, even in muddy or dark water. Your hands have no such sensors, but skin can still feel big ripples. A fish feels water movement only a short way, about one or two body lengths, so distance matters for her too.
  - Think: A fish has sensors along her whole side. What could she find that you could not?
### e3 · Cup Your Ears: do ear flaps help you hear? (30 min)

- **s1 (3 min)**

  *Child mode:* Cup your hands behind your ears. Say now when you hear the tick.
  - Check: Your child hears the tick from 120 centimetres with bare ears, and from 160 centimetres with cupped hands. What does this suggest? → **The cupped hands caught more sound**

  *Child mode:* Hold the cone to your ear. Say now when you hear the tick.
  - Note: The cone often does best of all. Make sure the child holds the small end gently to the ear and does not push it in.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the best distance for each setting. Draw a bar chart. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Which animal would use cupped ears like these?

  - Think: A fox hunts mice that squeak under the grass. How does her ear shape help?

  - Think: Your skin feels warmth, but a python feels it much more finely. What would she be able to do?

### a3 · Nose Trail: follow a smell on the floor with two nostrils, and with one (30 min)

## Worksheets
### Senses of the five groups (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which sense organ does a fish use to feel water moving along her sides?  
   Options: The lateral line / The eardrum / The heat pit / The ear flap  
   **Answer:** The lateral line. *Why:* The lateral line is a row of tiny sensors along a fish's sides. Hairs in it bend when water moves. An eardrum is for sound, a heat pit is for warmth, and an ear flap collects sound.
2. (truefalse) All animals with a backbone use exactly the same senses in exactly the same way.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. All five groups have eyes, ears, a nose and touch, but each group has developed some of them much further. Fish have a lateral line, some snakes have heat pits, and many birds see ultraviolet.
3. (fill) A small hollow in a snake's face that feels the heat of a warm body is called a heat ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pit', 'pits']. *Why:* A heat pit has a thin skin inside that reacts to infrared, the heat given off by warm bodies. Pit vipers, boas and pythons have them.
4. (match) Match each part to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Lateral line → feels moving water; Heat pit → feels the warmth of a body from a distance; Eardrum → shakes when sound reaches it; Cone → senses colour in the eye. *Why:* Each sense organ picks up one kind of signal. The lateral line feels water movement, the heat pit feels infrared, the eardrum shakes with sound, and a cone reacts to colour.
5. (mcq) Where does a frog's eardrum sit?  
   Options: Flat on the skin of her head, with no ear flap / Deep inside her head, behind an ear flap / At the end of her tail / Under her tongue  
   **Answer:** Flat on the skin of her head, with no ear flap. *Why:* A frog's eardrum is a round disc on the outside of her head, behind the eye. She has no ear flap. Mammals are the animals with ear flaps.
6. (sort) Sort each sense under the group that is best known for it.  
   **Answer:** lateral line → Fish; heat pits on the lips → Reptile; a fourth colour cone for ultraviolet → Bird; three tiny middle-ear bones → Mammal; a forked tongue gathering scent → Reptile; taste cells on whiskers → Fish. *Why:* These are the best-known examples. Some of these senses are shared. Many fish and reptiles also see ultraviolet, for example. But the lateral line, the three middle-ear bones and the forked tongue are best known in the groups shown.
7. (truefalse) A dog sees only in black and white.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dogs have two kinds of colour cones. They tell blue from yellow, but cannot tell red from green, like some people.
8. (mcq) Why do many hunted animals, such as pigeons and horses, have eyes on the sides of the head?  
   Options: Side eyes give a very wide view, so they can spot a hunter from almost any side / Side eyes help to judge distance / Side eyes let them see in the dark / Side eyes make the head lighter  
   **Answer:** Side eyes give a very wide view, so they can spot a hunter from almost any side. *Why:* Eyes on the sides see a very wide view. But the two eyes share very little space, so judging distance is harder. Judging distance is what front eyes do best.
9. (count) A pigeon sees about 340 degrees around her. A person sees about 200 degrees. How many more degrees does the pigeon see?  
   **Answer:** 140. *Why:* 340 take away 200 is 140. The pigeon sees 140 degrees more than a person.
10. (write) Revathi says: 'A python in a dark storeroom sees a rat with her eyes.' Explain what is wrong with this idea.  
   **Answer:** A python does not need light. Her heat pits feel the warmth of the rat. So she can find the rat in the dark without seeing it.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the python senses heat, not light, and names the heat pits. Saying 'she smells it' is partly true, since snakes smell with their tongues, but it leaves out the heat pits.
11. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Lateral line, heat pit, eardrum, claw.  
   **Answer:** The claw. The other three pick up a signal. A claw is for gripping or tearing.. *Why:* A lateral line, a heat pit and an eardrum are sense organs that pick up water movement, heat and sound. A claw does not pick up a signal.
12. (order) Put these in the right order for how a barn owl catches a rat in a dark room.  
   **Answer:** ['A rat rustles on the floor', "The sound reaches the owl's face and ears", 'The owl turns her face towards the sound', 'The owl drops onto the spot']. *Why:* First the rat makes a sound. The owl's face gathers it, she turns until the sound is in front, and then she drops. She does not need light.
13. (mcq) What does a small bat do to find a moth in the dark?  
   Options: Sends out high sounds and listens for the echoes / Smells the moth from far away / Feels the moth with her whiskers / Sees the moth in ultraviolet light  
   **Answer:** Sends out high sounds and listens for the echoes. *Why:* Echolocation means making a sound and listening to the echo that bounces back. The bat's calls are too high for people to hear.
14. (fill) Ears that stick out from the head and catch sound are called ear ____.  
   **Answer:** ['flaps', 'flap', 'pinnae', 'pinna']. *Why:* An ear flap, or pinna, collects sound and can often turn towards it. Mammals have ear flaps. Birds, frogs and snakes do not.
15. (draw) Draw a pond as a fish would sense it. Show where the lateral line is, and draw arrows for the ripples it feels.  
   **Answer:** A fish with a line along her side, and ripple lines reaching that line from an insect on the surface.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the lateral line along the side of the body, and ripple lines arriving at it from a falling insect.
16. (write) A scientist wants to find out if a fish can feel ripples with her lateral line alone. Why should she make sure the fish cannot see or hear what makes the ripple?  
   **Answer:** If the fish could see or hear the ripple, she might turn because of that. The scientist needs to know that only the lateral line did it.. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and blocks other explanations. A strong answer says the fish might be using eyes or ears, and these must be ruled out.
### Looking deeper at animal senses (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Why might a male and a female bird that look alike to us look different to each other?  
   Options: Birds see ultraviolet, and their feathers may differ in ultraviolet / Birds have heat pits that show warmth / Sparrows use a lateral line / Sparrows use echolocation  
   **Answer:** Birds see ultraviolet, and their feathers may differ in ultraviolet. *Why:* Most birds have a fourth kind of colour cone that senses ultraviolet. A feather can have a pattern in ultraviolet that people never see.
2. (mcq) An owl cannot move her eyes in their sockets. How does she look to the side?  
   Options: She turns her whole head / She rolls her eyes / She uses her heat pits / She does not look to the side  
   **Answer:** She turns her whole head. *Why:* An owl's eyes are fixed in tube-shaped sockets, so she turns her head instead, up to about 270 degrees.
3. (truefalse) Cobras find prey in the dark with heat pits.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Heat pits are found on pit vipers, boas and pythons. Cobras and Russell's vipers have none, so they use smell, touch and sight.
4. (fill) Sounds too low for people to hear are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['infrasound']. *Why:* Infrasound is sound below what people can hear. Asian elephants use it to call across a forest.
5. (order) Put these in the right order for how a fish senses an insect that falls on the water.  
   **Answer:** ['An insect falls onto the water', 'Ripples spread through the water', 'The hairs of the lateral line bend', 'The fish turns towards the ripple']. *Why:* The fall makes ripples, the ripples move the water along the fish's side, the hairs bend and send a message, and the fish turns.
6. (sort) Sort each animal by the kind of eyes that help her most.  
   **Answer:** pigeon → Wide view (side eyes); owl → Deep view (front eyes); horse → Wide view (side eyes); cat → Deep view (front eyes); rabbit → Wide view (side eyes); monkey → Deep view (front eyes). *Why:* Animals that are hunted, such as pigeons, horses and rabbits, tend to have side eyes for a wide view. Animals that hunt or leap, such as owls, cats and monkeys, tend to have front eyes for judging distance.
7. (mcq) A fish lives in a dark cave and has no eyes. How might she still find food?  
   Options: Her lateral line and other senses work without light / She grows new eyes at night / She uses heat pits / Fish do not need to find food  
   **Answer:** Her lateral line and other senses work without light. *Why:* Mexican cavefish have lost their eyes but have many more sensors on their lateral lines. Water movement tells them about food and walls.
8. (write) A friend says: 'Bats are blind.' Give two pieces of evidence that this is wrong.  
   **Answer:** All bats can see. A large fruit bat such as the Indian flying fox finds ripe fruit with her eyes and nose. Small bats can also see, and they use echoes as well.. *Why:* A strong answer says that bats can see, and gives an example such as the fruit bat. Echolocation is something extra that small bats use, and not a replacement for sight.
9. (match) Match each animal to a signal she picks up that a person's body does not.  
   **Answer:** Shark → weak electricity from other animals' muscles; Python → infrared heat from a warm body; Pigeon → ultraviolet light; Small bat → echoes of sounds too high for people to hear. *Why:* A shark finds hidden fish by electricity, a python by heat, a pigeon sees ultraviolet and a small bat uses high-pitched echoes.
10. (perspective) Meera and Imran watch a python in a dark storeroom find a warm rat.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side, gives a reason and names a test. For example, cover the python's eyes and see if she still strikes at a warm target but not a cool one.. *Why:* Imran's view fits the evidence from snakes with covered eyes. A good test removes one sense at a time and checks the result.
11. (mcq) According to most biologists, why does no animal have every sense?  
   Options: Senses cost energy, and each animal keeps the ones that fit her life / No animal wants more senses / Heavy senses make animals sink / Nobody has looked for more senses  
   **Answer:** Senses cost energy, and each animal keeps the ones that fit her life. *Why:* Eyes, ears and the brain parts that read them use a lot of energy. A sense that is hardly needed is a waste of food. This is a widely held idea, and scientists are still measuring the costs.
12. (truefalse) Scientists fully understand how birds sense the Earth's magnetic field.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Experiments show that birds use the magnetic field, but the receptor has not been found with certainty. There are two main ideas, one based on light and one based on iron.
13. (draw) Draw a top view of a pigeon and an owl. Shade how far round each one can see, and write the angle beside each shape.  
   **Answer:** A pigeon with a very wide shaded wedge of about 340 degrees, and an owl with a narrow wedge of about 110 degrees with a larger overlap in front.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the pigeon seeing almost all the way round, with a small overlap in front, and the owl seeing a narrow wedge that is mostly overlap.
14. (mcq) A harbour seal follows the swirl a fish left in the water. Which part does she use?  
   Options: Her whiskers / Her heat pits / Her lateral line / Her eardrum  
   **Answer:** Her whiskers. *Why:* A seal's whiskers have many nerves at the base. They feel the swirl left in the water by a swimming fish.
15. (count) A full circle is 360 degrees. A pigeon sees about 340 degrees. How many degrees does she not see?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* 360 take away 340 is 20. Only a small slice straight behind her is hidden.
16. (fill) A snake's forked tongue gathers tiny bits of ____ from the air and ground.  
   **Answer:** ['scent', 'smell', 'chemicals']. *Why:* The tongue carries scent into two spots in the roof of the mouth. The snake does not sting with it.
17. (write) A cat judges a jump onto a wall using both eyes. Explain how two eyes help her.  
   **Answer:** Each eye sees the wall from a slightly different place. Her brain compares the two pictures and works out how far away the wall is.. *Why:* A strong answer says the two pictures are different, and the brain uses the difference to judge distance.
### Think like a scientist about animal senses (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) Design a test to find out if a guppy can feel ripples without seeing them. Say what you would change, what you would keep the same and what you would measure.  
   **Answer:** Make ripples near the fish in a dark or covered tank, so she cannot see them. Change only the distance. Keep the size of the ripple the same. Count how often the fish turns towards it.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing (such as distance), keeps the others the same, and measures something countable, such as the turns. It also removes sight as a clue.
2. (count) A child is blindfolded and keeps both hands still in water. She says the side of a ripple correctly 9, 7 and 5 times out of 10 at 5, 10 and 20 centimetres. Guessing gives about 5. At how many centimetres is her score the same as guessing?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* At 5 centimetres she is right 9 times and at 10 centimetres 7 times, both more than 5. At 20 centimetres she scores 5, the same as guessing, so her skin could not feel the ripple that far away.
3. (mcq) A python in a dark storeroom has a warm rat on one side and a warm hot-water bottle on the other. What might she do?  
   Options: Strike at either, because both are warm / Ignore both / Strike only at the bottle / Strike only at the rat because she can see it  
   **Answer:** Strike at either, because both are warm. *Why:* Heat pits sense warmth, not whether the warm thing is food. In the dark, either one can fool her. This is why the experiments with warm targets work.
4. (truefalse) Because dogs see fewer colours than people, dogs are worse at everything to do with seeing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dogs see fewer colours, but they notice movement well and are thought to see well in dim light. Each animal's eyes fit her way of life.
5. (match) Match each animal to the clue that shows her senses fit her way of life.  
   **Answer:** Barn owl → a heart-shaped face that gathers sound; Pigeon → eyes on the sides of the head; Susu river dolphin → eyes with no lens, and clicks that make echoes; Cat → ear flaps that turn towards a sound. *Why:* Each sense fits a way of life. The owl hunts in the dark by sound, the pigeon needs a wide view, the susu lives in muddy water, and the cat hunts by hearing small sounds.
6. (mcq) Why does a barn owl catch a rat more easily when the rat walks on dry leaves?  
   Options: Dry leaves rustle, so the sound is louder and easier to hear / Dry leaves are warm / Dry leaves smell like rats / Dry leaves help the owl to see  
   **Answer:** Dry leaves rustle, so the sound is louder and easier to hear. *Why:* A louder, sharper sound is easier for the owl to locate. This is why a rat that freezes and stays quiet is safer.
7. (perspective) Asha and Karthik are talking about a tick on a branch. The tick reacts only to sweat smell, to body warmth and to hairy skin.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason. Asha's view is the idea of Jakob von Uexküll. Karthik's is a view held by many scientists today.. *Why:* Both views fit the facts. They differ in what 'world' means. This is a real debate, and nobody has settled it.
8. (write) Explain why a lateral line works well in water, but would be little use to a bird flying in air.  
   **Answer:** Water is thick and carries a push or ripple a long way, so hairs along the body bend. Air is thin, and a push from a moving animal fades fast, so there would be almost nothing to feel.. *Why:* A strong answer says that water carries movement much better than air. It is also true that birds rely on sight and hearing instead.
9. (sort) Sort each of these by whether a person's body can sense it without a tool.  
   **Answer:** a ripple of water on the hand → People can sense it; the heat of a diya on the cheek → People can sense it; ultraviolet light → People cannot sense it; the weak electricity of another animal's muscles → People cannot sense it; a bat's high clicks → People cannot sense it; a smell in the air → People can sense it. *Why:* People feel ripples, warmth and smells. Ultraviolet, the electricity of muscles and a bat's clicks are outside what our bodies can sense, though instruments can detect them.
10. (fill) Seeing the same thing with two eyes at once is called ____ vision.  
   **Answer:** ['binocular']. *Why:* Binocular vision is two eyes seeing the same space. The brain compares the two pictures to judge distance.
11. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Pigeon, horse, rabbit, owl.  
   **Answer:** The owl. Her eyes face forwards and give a deep view. The others have side eyes and a wide view.. *Why:* Pigeons, horses and rabbits are hunted and need a wide view. An owl hunts, and needs to judge distance.
12. (draw) Draw an owl's face from the front. Add arrows to show how the face works as a dish that gathers sound.  
   **Answer:** A heart-shaped face with arrows curving inwards from the sides towards the ears.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the flat face as a dish, with arrows of sound gathering into the ears.
13. (mcq) A scientist covers a snake's eyes and nostrils. The snake still strikes at a warm target but not at a cool one. What does this show?  
   Options: The snake can sense heat, not just light or smell / The snake is blind / The snake hears the target / Snakes never use their eyes  
   **Answer:** The snake can sense heat, not just light or smell. *Why:* With sight and smell blocked, the only difference left between the two targets is warmth. So the snake must sense warmth.
14. (write) A farmer says: 'Owls bring bad luck.' Write one fair question you could ask, and one way to test it, to find out whether owls help or harm the farmer.  
   **Answer:** How many rats are in the granary with and without an owl? Count the rat holes in the sacks for two weeks with an owl, and then compare with two weeks without one.. *Why:* A strong answer asks a question that can be tested and describes a fair comparison. It treats the farmer's belief with respect while looking for evidence.
15. (count) A clock's tick is heard from 120 centimetres with bare ears, and from 150 centimetres with a card cone at one ear. By how many centimetres did the cone help?  
   **Answer:** 30. *Why:* 150 take away 120 is 30. The cone let the tick be heard from 30 centimetres farther away.
16. (truefalse) Ear flaps are found on mammals, and not on any bird, frog or snake.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The outer ear flap, or pinna, belongs to mammals. Birds, frogs and snakes have an eardrum or none, but no ear flap.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does a receptor do?  
   Options: Reacts to one kind of signal and sends a message / Makes the signal / Stores food / Moves the body  
   **Answer:** Reacts to one kind of signal and sends a message. *Why:* A receptor is a tiny cell that reacts to one kind of signal, such as light, a smell or a push, and sends a message to the brain.
2. (mcq) Which animal feels moving water with a lateral line?  
   Options: A rohu fish / A cow / A crow / A python  
   **Answer:** A rohu fish. *Why:* Fish have a lateral line. Cows and crows have no such sensors, and a python feels heat with her pits.
3. (mcq) A python lies in the dark and finds a warm rat. Which part senses the rat?  
   Options: The heat pits on her lips / Her ear flaps / Her whiskers / Her eardrum  
   **Answer:** The heat pits on her lips. *Why:* Pythons have heat pits along their lips. They sense the infrared given off by a warm body. Pythons have no ear flaps or whiskers.
4. (mcq) Why does a pigeon see almost all the way round?  
   Options: Her eyes are on the sides of her head / Her eyes are at the front / She has four eyes / Her eyes move around inside her head  
   **Answer:** Her eyes are on the sides of her head. *Why:* Eyes on the sides of the head see a very wide view. This helps a hunted animal to spot danger from any side.
5. (mcq) Why do owls and cats judge distance well?  
   Options: Their eyes face forwards and overlap / Their eyes are small / Their eyes are on the sides / They have heat pits  
   **Answer:** Their eyes face forwards and overlap. *Why:* When two eyes see the same space, the brain compares the pictures and works out distance. Side eyes overlap very little.
6. (mcq) How does a cupped hand behind the ear help you hear?  
   Options: It catches more sound and guides it in / It makes the sound louder at the source / It blocks all other sounds / It warms the ear  
   **Answer:** It catches more sound and guides it in. *Why:* The hand works as a bigger ear flap. It collects more of the sound and points it into the ear.
7. (mcq) How does a barn owl catch a rat in a dark room?  
   Options: She hears the rat and turns her face towards the sound / She smells the rat / She feels the rat's heat / She sees ultraviolet light  
   **Answer:** She hears the rat and turns her face towards the sound. *Why:* Barn owls can find a rat in complete darkness using sound alone. Her flat face works like a dish that gathers sound.
8. (truefalse) Dogs see only in black and white.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dogs see blues and yellows, and cannot tell red from green.
9. (mcq) Which group of animals with a backbone has three tiny bones in the middle ear?  
   Options: Mammals / Birds / Reptiles / Fish  
   **Answer:** Mammals. *Why:* Only mammals have three middle-ear bones. Birds and reptiles have one.
10. (mcq) You test whether cupped hands help you hear a clock. What should stay the same in a fair test?  
   Options: The clock, the room and the way the helper walks / The position of the ears / Everything / Nothing  
   **Answer:** The clock, the room and the way the helper walks. *Why:* In a fair test, only one thing changes. Here, only the position of the ears should change. Everything else, such as the clock, the room and the walking, stays the same.
11. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not fully understand?  
   Options: How birds sense the Earth's magnetic field / That bats use echoes / That fish have a lateral line / That snakes smell with their tongue  
   **Answer:** How birds sense the Earth's magnetic field. *Why:* Birds seem to use the magnetic field, but the sensor has not been found with certainty. The other three are well shown.
12. (match) Match each group to a sense that stands out in it.  
   **Answer:** Fish → lateral line; Reptile → heat pits in some snakes; Bird → ultraviolet vision; Mammal → ear flaps and three middle-ear bones. *Why:* Each group is best known for these senses. Some are shared with other groups, but these are the clearest examples.

## Applications
- Thermal cameras, used by firefighters to find people in smoke, do something like what a python's heat pits do.
- Doctors use ultrasound scans of a baby before birth. The scan uses echoes, the way a bat does.
- Ships measure the depth of the sea with sonar, which is echolocation by machine.
- Sniffer dogs are trained to find lost people, and researchers are testing dogs for finding some illnesses.
- Scientists record bat calls with special microphones to count bats without catching them.
- Robots with whisker-like sensors can feel their way through dark places.
- Designers shape hearing dishes and microphones like an owl's face or a fox's ear, to catch faint sounds.
- Engineers copy the lateral line when they build sensors that feel water flow around a boat or under the sea.

## Open questions
- **How exactly do birds sense the Earth's magnetic field?** Experiments show that birds and some other animals use the field. But two ideas, one based on light and one on iron, are still debated, and the receptor has not been found with certainty.
- **What does a heat pit feel like to a snake? Is it a kind of seeing, a kind of feeling, or something else?** Scientists can measure how the pit reacts and how the brain handles it. Nobody can tell what the snake experiences.
- **How does a fish in a big school use her lateral line to swim so close to others without bumping?** Scientists have models and experiments, but they have not settled how much comes from the lateral line, how much from sight, and how much from simple rules.
- **Are there senses that animals have, but that scientists have not found yet?** New senses keep turning up. The heat pit and the electric sense were only found in the last two hundred years. Nobody knows how many more there are.
- **How does an animal's brain decide which signals to pay attention to, and which to ignore?** A body is flooded with signals. Scientists know that brains filter them, but they are still working out how.
- **Can anyone know what it is like to be a bat?** This is a question about experience, not measurement. The philosopher Thomas Nagel asked it in 1974, and it has not been settled.

## Connected maths
- A pigeon sees about 340 degrees, and a person about 200 degrees. An owl sees about 110 degrees. How many degrees more than the owl does a person see? How many more than a person does a pigeon see? **A person sees 90 degrees more than the owl. A pigeon sees 140 degrees more than a person.**. 200 take away 110 is 90. 340 take away 200 is 140. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- A full circle is 360 degrees. A pigeon sees about 340 degrees. What fraction of the full circle can the pigeon see? Write it as simply as you can. **17 out of 18, or 17/18.**. The pigeon sees 340 out of 360. Divide both numbers by 20. 340 divided by 20 is 17, and 360 divided by 20 is 18. So she sees 17 eighteenths of the circle. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the Cup Your Ears test, a child hears a tick from 120 centimetres with bare ears, and from 160 centimetres with cupped hands. By what fraction of 120 centimetres did the cupped hands help? **By one third.**. 160 take away 120 is 40. 40 out of 120 is 1 out of 3, because 120 divided by 40 is 3. So the cupped hands added a third. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In the Ripple Skin test, a coin toss decides left or right. If a child guesses every time, how many answers do you expect to be right out of 10? Out of 30? A child gets 9 right out of 10 at one distance. Is that likely to happen by guessing alone? **About 5 out of 10, and about 15 out of 30. Getting 9 out of 10 by guessing would happen only about 1 time in 100.**. A coin lands left half the time, so a guess is right half the time. Half of 10 is 5, and half of 30 is 15. Getting 9 or 10 right out of 10 by luck happens in about 11 of every 1,024 tries, which is about 1 in 100. So 9 out of 10 is strong evidence that the child really felt the ripple. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- About how many species are known in each group? Fish 36,000. Reptiles 12,000. Birds 11,000. Amphibians 8,700. Mammals 6,500. These are rough numbers. About how many times as many species of fish are there as mammals? **About 5 and a half times.**. 36,000 divided by 6,500 is a little more than 5. Five times 6,500 is 32,500, and 6 times is 39,000. So the answer is between 5 and 6, close to 5 and a half. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- An owl turns her head about 270 degrees. A right angle is 90 degrees. How many right angles is that? What fraction of a full turn? **3 right angles, which is three quarters of a full turn.**. 270 divided by 90 is 3. A full turn is 360 degrees, or 4 right angles. So 3 out of 4 right angles is three quarters. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- A bat sends out a call and hears the echo 0.01 seconds later. Sound travels about 340 metres in 1 second. About how far away is the moth? **About 1.7 metres.**. In 0.01 seconds, sound travels 340 times 0.01, which is 3.4 metres. That is the way there and back. So the moth is half of that away, which is 1.7 metres. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- Suppose a small bat catches 600 insects in a night. This is a made-up number for practice. About how many does she catch in a week of 7 nights? In 30 nights? **About 4,200 in a week, and about 18,000 in 30 nights.**. 600 times 7 is 4,200. 600 times 30 is 18,000. This is a made-up number, because real bats catch different amounts each night. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
