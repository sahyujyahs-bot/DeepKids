# Dissolving: what water takes, what oil takes, and where the fizz hides
`sci.states.dissolving.L4`

**Summary.** Turmeric stains a plastic box yellow and will not wash out with plain water, yet food colour vanishes into water at once. A grown-up who bites a hot chilli gets no help from a sip of water, but a spoon of curd cools the burn. A bottle of soda gushes and goes flat when it is warm, but stays fizzy when it is cold. In this unit your child tests which colours go into water and which go into oil, finds where the fizz hides in a cold bottle, and sees why salt makes ice colder. Then they follow one rule all the way from kitchen jars to the skin of every living cell: a thing dissolves best in a liquid that is like itself.

**Intro.** *Rub turmeric on a white plastic box and then wash it with plain water. The yellow stays. Now rub a little oil on the stain and wipe. The yellow comes off on the oil. Why does the colour leave in one liquid and not the other?* Water takes some things and not others. Here you ask which liquid takes which thing. You test turmeric, chilli and food colour in water and in oil. You open a cold soda and a warm soda and see where the fizz was hiding. You make kulfi in a bag, using salt on ice. By the end you can say why a grown-up should reach for curd, not water, after a hot chilli.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that water and oil each take different things, and test where a colour goes, to water, to oil or to both.
1. I can say why turmeric stains plastic and why oil or soap lifts it, using the idea that a thing dissolves best in a liquid that is like itself.
2. I can explain why chilli heat is eased by curd or milk better than by water.
3. I can show that gases dissolve in liquids, and that a warm drink holds less gas than a cold one.
4. I can sort mixtures into solution, suspension and colloid using three tests: look, torch and wait.
5. I can show that salt makes ice melt and the mixture get colder, and say what dissolved particles do to freezing.
6. I can explain why almost nothing is completely insoluble, and how rain dissolves limestone into caves.
7. I can tell how Joseph Priestley first made fizzy water, and what people used to believe about air.

## Terms
- **Solute**: The thing that dissolves. It is the part that seems to disappear into the liquid. *e.g.* In salt water, the salt is the solute.
- **Solvent**: The liquid that does the dissolving. Water is the solvent in most kitchen solutions. *e.g.* In sugar syrup, the water is the solvent. In turmeric oil, the oil is the solvent.
- **Concentration**: How much of the dissolved thing is in a certain amount of the liquid. More in the same liquid means a higher concentration. *e.g.* A tumbler of sea water holds about nine grams of salt. A tumbler with one pinch of salt has a much lower concentration.
- **Dilute**: To add more of the solvent so the dissolved thing is spread thinner. A diluted solution is weaker. *e.g.* Adding water to strong sherbet syrup dilutes it.
- **Mixture**: Two or more things put together without changing into something new. You can often get them apart again. *e.g.* Sand and salt mixed in a bowl is a mixture.
- **Suspension**: A mixture where bits you can see float in a liquid for a while and then settle to the bottom. *e.g.* Muddy water is a suspension. Leave it still and the mud sinks.
- **Colloid**: A mixture where bits too small to see float in a liquid and never settle. The liquid looks cloudy, and a torch beam shows up inside it. *e.g.* Milk is a colloid. Tiny drops of fat float in the water.
- **Emulsion**: A colloid made of tiny drops of one liquid spread through another liquid that they do not dissolve in. *e.g.* Milk is an emulsion of fat drops in water. So is the cloudy water after you shake oil with soap.
- **Molecule**: A group of atoms joined together that acts as one tiny piece. A water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. *e.g.* A water molecule is far too small to see. A drop of water holds countless of them.
- **Dissolved gas**: A gas that has spread through a liquid as separate molecules, so it makes no bubbles you can see. *e.g.* A sealed bottle of soda has carbon dioxide dissolved in it. The bubbles appear when you open the bottle.
- **Pressure**: How hard something pushes on each bit of a surface. Squeezed gas pushes hard on its container. *e.g.* A sealed soda bottle has a higher gas pressure inside than the air outside.
- **Fat-soluble**: Able to dissolve in oil or fat but hardly in water. *e.g.* The yellow colour in turmeric and the heat in chilli are fat-soluble.
- **Freezing point**: The temperature at which a liquid turns into a solid. Pure water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Sea water freezes at about minus 2 degrees Celsius, a little colder than fresh water.
- **Brine**: Very salty water. *e.g.* The salty water in a jar of lemon pickle is brine.
- **Pigment**: A coloured substance that gives colour to a thing. Some pigments dissolve in water and some in oil. *e.g.* Turmeric, beetroot and carrot all hold pigments.
- **Soluble in oil**: Able to dissolve in oil. Many oily things dissolve in oil but not in water. *e.g.* Camphor dissolves in coconut oil, which is why hair oils can carry its smell.

## Explanations
### Solute and solvent: who dissolves whom · *defined*
**Definition:** A solution has two parts. The solvent is the liquid that does the dissolving, and the solute is the thing that dissolves in it.

In salt water, water is the solvent and salt is the solute. In golden turmeric oil, oil is the solvent and the turmeric colour is the solute. The same thing can be a solute in one liquid and not dissolve at all in another. Sugar dissolves well in water and not at all in oil. Turmeric colour dissolves in oil and hardly in water. So you cannot ask only 'does it dissolve?'. You must ask 'dissolve in what?'. Water is the most common solvent in kitchens and in bodies, but it is not the only one. Nail polish remover is a solvent for nail polish, and petrol is a solvent for grease.
- e.g. Salt is the solute and water is the solvent in rasam.
- e.g. Carotene, the orange pigment of carrots, is the solute in the orange ghee of carrot halwa.
- e.g. Nail polish remover is the solvent that lifts nail polish, which water cannot do.
### How strong a solution is: concentration · *defined*
**Definition:** Concentration is how much dissolved thing there is in a certain amount of the liquid. More dissolved thing in the same liquid means a stronger solution.

Two glasses can both hold salt water and still taste very different. One teaspoon of salt in a big tumbler tastes only a little salty. One teaspoon in a small cup tastes very salty. The amount of salt is the same, but the salt is more crowded in the small cup. Scientists say the small cup has a higher concentration. They often write it as grams in each litre. Sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in every litre. A tumbler that holds a quarter of a litre therefore holds about nine grams of salt. If you add water, you dilute the solution. The amount of dissolved thing stays the same, but it spreads through more liquid, so each sip has less.
- e.g. Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in each litre.
- e.g. Sherbet syrup is a concentrated solution of sugar, and a tall glass of sherbet is a diluted one.
- e.g. A water purifier with a TDS meter shows how many milligrams of dissolved solids are in each litre of water.
### Three kinds of mixtures: solution, suspension and colloid · *defined*
**Definition:** A solution has particles too small to see and never settles. A suspension has bits you can see that settle. A colloid has tiny bits that never settle but scatter light.

Salt water is a solution. It is clear, and a torch beam hardly shows in it. Muddy water is a suspension. It is cloudy, a torch beam shows up in it, and the mud settles if you wait. Milk is a colloid. It is cloudy and a torch beam shows up in it, but the tiny fat drops never settle. So the torch and the clock together sort the three kinds. A beam of light shows inside a colloid because the floating bits are big enough to scatter light. The effect is named the Tyndall effect, after John Tyndall, a scientist who studied it in the 1800s. You see it when sunlight comes through the mist in a forest, or through the smoke of an agarbatti.
- e.g. Salt water, sugar syrup and rasam strained clear are solutions.
- e.g. Muddy puddle water and atta stirred in water are suspensions.
- e.g. Milk, fog, smoke from an agarbatti and jelly are colloids.
### Why water takes salt and sugar but not oil · *defined*
**Definition:** A water molecule has a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end. It holds on well to things that also have charged or sticky parts, and not at all to oily things that have none.

Water molecules are bent in shape, with the oxygen atom at one end and the two hydrogen atoms at the other. The oxygen end has a tiny negative charge and the hydrogen ends have a tiny positive charge. Salt is made of charged particles called ions, so water grips each ion and pulls it away from the grain. Sugar molecules have many spots that water can hold on to. Oil molecules have none of these charged or sticky spots, so water holds on to other water molecules instead and pushes the oil out into drops that join up and float. Chemists sum this up as 'like dissolves like'. Charged and sticky things dissolve in water, and oily things dissolve in oil.
- e.g. Salt and sugar dissolve in water because water can hold on to them.
- e.g. Coconut oil does not dissolve in water because it has no charged or sticky spots for water to hold.
- e.g. Camphor and wax dissolve in oil because they are oily too.
### Things that dissolve in oil · *defined*
**Definition:** Fat-soluble things dissolve in oil or fat and hardly in water. Many colours, smells and vitamins are fat-soluble.

The yellow in turmeric, the orange in carrots and the red in chilli powder all dissolve in oil much better than in water. That is why cooks fry spices in oil first, and why carrot halwa turns the ghee orange. It is also why turmeric stains a plastic box. Plastic is oily on the molecule scale, so the turmeric colour sticks to it, and plain water cannot pull it off. Oil can. Vitamins A, D, E and K are fat-soluble too, and vitamins B and C dissolve in water. So a little oil with vegetables helps your body take in the orange pigment carotene, which the body can turn into vitamin A.
- e.g. Frying turmeric and chilli in oil at the start of a tadka spreads their colour and heat through the dish.
- e.g. Carrot halwa turns orange because carotene dissolves in the ghee.
- e.g. A greasy hairband and a turmeric-stained box both clean better with oil or soap than with water.
### Why chilli burns and curd helps · *defined*
**Definition:** The hot part of a chilli is a fat-soluble chemical called capsaicin. It dissolves in fat and hardly in water, so water cannot wash it off, but milk fat and curd can.

When a grown-up bites a chilli, capsaicin sticks to the nerves in the mouth that sense heat. They send a 'hot' message even though nothing is actually hot. A sip of water spreads the capsaicin around but does not carry it away, because capsaicin does not dissolve in water. Milk, curd and buttermilk have fat in them, and capsaicin dissolves into that fat. Scientists also think a milk protein called casein grabs capsaicin, which may help too. Coconut, rice and bread soak up some as well. This is why Indian meals often end with curd rice, and why a spicy dish often comes with raita.
- e.g. Curd rice, raita and buttermilk soothe a spicy meal better than water.
- e.g. Washing chilli-cut fingers with oil or soap works better than water alone.
- e.g. Rice, bread and coconut also soak up some of the hot chemical.
### Soap has one end for water and one for oil · *defined*
**Definition:** A soap molecule has a head that likes water and a tail that likes oil. It sits between oil and water and lets them spread through each other in tiny drops.

A soap molecule is long, like a tadpole. Its head dissolves in water. Its tail dissolves in oil. When you wash a greasy plate, the tails push into the grease and the heads stick out into the water. Soon the grease is wrapped in tiny balls with the heads facing outwards, and the water can carry the balls away. The grease has not dissolved. It has been broken into very tiny drops that stay spread through the water. That kind of mixture is an emulsion. Milk and mayonnaise are emulsions too, held together by their own natural soap-like molecules.
- e.g. Dish soap lifts oil from a kadai and rinses off with water.
- e.g. Milk stays an even white because its fat drops are held apart by molecules with a water end.
- e.g. Hair shampoo lifts oil from hair in the same way.
### Gases dissolve in liquids too · *defined*
**Definition:** A gas can spread through a liquid as separate molecules. More gas dissolves when the liquid is cold and when the gas is squeezed to a higher pressure.

A sealed bottle of soda has carbon dioxide gas squeezed into the water at a pressure several times that of the air outside. Under that pressure, a lot of gas dissolves and no bubbles show. When you open the cap, the pressure drops and the water can no longer keep so much gas, so bubbles stream out. William Henry measured this in 1803. He found that the mass of gas that dissolves is in proportion to the pressure. Warmth works the other way. Warm water holds less gas, because the fast-moving gas molecules escape more easily. A warm soda gushes and goes flat quickly. A cold one stays fizzy. Fish need oxygen dissolved in water. Warm water holds less oxygen, which is why fish in a hot pond can gasp at the surface.
- e.g. A cold bottle of soda keeps its fizz longer than a warm one.
- e.g. Goli soda has a glass marble held in the neck by the pressure of the gas inside.
- e.g. Fish gulp at the surface of a hot, still pond because the warm water holds little oxygen.
### Salt makes ice melt, and makes the mixture colder · *defined*
**Definition:** Dissolved particles make it harder for water to freeze. So salt water freezes at a temperature below zero, and a mix of ice and salt gets much colder than plain ice.

Ice at zero degrees is always wetted by a thin film of liquid water. Water molecules are leaving the ice and joining it at the same speed. When you sprinkle salt, it dissolves in that film. The salt particles get in the way, so fewer water molecules can rejoin the ice, but the ice keeps melting. Melting takes heat, and it takes it from the mixture. So the mixture gets colder. Salty ice can reach about minus 20 degrees Celsius. That is cold enough to freeze milk and sugar into kulfi. Sea water freezes at about minus 2 degrees. In cold countries, salt is spread on roads for the same reason, so that ice melts even when the air is a little below zero.
- e.g. A kulfi seller packs ice and salt round the pot so that the kulfi freezes.
- e.g. Sea water stays liquid at zero degrees, when a freshwater lake has already frozen.
- e.g. Salt on an icy road melts the ice until the road gets colder than the salty water can handle.
### Cell skins: oil and water kept apart · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living cell is wrapped in a thin skin made of fat-like molecules with a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail. The skin forms by itself because the tails hide from water.

Inside you and outside you there is water. A cell needs a skin that keeps their inside different from their outside. The skin is made of molecules like soap, with a head that likes water and a tail that likes oil. In water they line up in a double layer, with the heads facing the water on both sides and the tails hidden in the middle. Nobody has to build it. In 1925, two scientists in Leiden, Evert Gorter and Francois Grendel, took the fats out of red blood cells and spread them out. They found that there was just enough to cover each cell twice. That was the first sign that a cell is wrapped in a double layer. So the same rule that sorts turmeric into oil also builds the walls of every living cell.
- e.g. Soap bubbles and cell skins both form from molecules with a water end and an oil end.
- e.g. A bacterium in curd, a leaf cell of a tulsi plant and your own cheek cell all have such a skin.
- e.g. The skin lets some things in and keeps others out.
### Almost nothing is completely insoluble · *defined*
**Definition:** Everything dissolves a very little in water, if you wait long enough. A thing we call insoluble just dissolves so little that we do not notice.

Chalk looks insoluble, but a tiny amount does dissolve. Even glass and rock dissolve a little. Rainwater carries a bit of carbon dioxide from the air, and that makes it able to dissolve limestone rock very slowly. Over hundreds of thousands of years, flowing rainwater can carve out huge caves. Some of the longest caves in India are in the limestone hills of Meghalaya. Where the dripping water evaporates, it leaves the dissolved limestone behind as stalactites hanging from the roof. So 'insoluble' is a word for 'dissolves too little for us to see in a short time'. Given enough time, water wears down mountains.
- e.g. Limestone caves in Meghalaya were carved by rainwater dissolving the rock.
- e.g. Stalactites form as dripping water leaves a little dissolved limestone behind each time.
- e.g. The white scale inside a kettle is dissolved minerals left behind by evaporating water.
### The sea is a giant glass of dissolved gas · *defined*
**Definition:** The ocean dissolves a large share of the carbon dioxide that people release, which slows the warming of the air but changes the sea.

Carbon dioxide gas from the air dissolves into sea water, as it does in soda. Scientists estimate that the ocean has taken in roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide that people have put into the air. The gas makes the sea water slightly more acidic. This matters for creatures with shells and for coral, which build their bodies from dissolved minerals. At the same time the sea is warming, and warmer water holds less gas. So scientists are measuring how much more carbon dioxide the sea can keep taking in. Nobody knows exactly how this will change over the next hundred years.
- e.g. Coral reefs off the Lakshadweep islands depend on sea water that holds the right minerals.
- e.g. A warm ocean holds less dissolved gas than a cold one, just as a warm soda does.
- e.g. Plankton in the sea take dissolved carbon dioxide and make food from it.

## Mostly agreed
- A thing dissolves best in a liquid that is like itself. Charged and sticky things dissolve in water, and oily things dissolve in oil. *How we know:* Salt and sugar dissolve in water but not in oil, while turmeric colour and carotene dissolve in oil and hardly in water. Chemists have measured thousands of cases.
- How strong a solution is depends on how much is dissolved in how much liquid, which scientists call its concentration. *How we know:* The same teaspoon of salt tastes weak in a big tumbler and strong in a small cup. Dilution makes the amount in each sip smaller.
- More gas dissolves in a liquid when the liquid is cold and when the gas is squeezed to a higher pressure. *How we know:* William Henry measured gas in water at different pressures in 1803. A cold soda stays fizzy and a warm soda gushes and goes flat.
- Dissolved particles lower the freezing point of water, so salt on ice makes the mixture colder than plain ice. *How we know:* Sea water freezes below zero. An ice and salt mixture can reach about minus 20 degrees Celsius and freeze kulfi.
- Almost nothing is completely insoluble. Everything dissolves a very little in water. *How we know:* Rainwater dissolves limestone and carves caves. White scale from kettles is dissolved minerals left behind when the water evaporates.
- A mixture can be sorted into solution, suspension or colloid by looking at it, shining a torch through it and waiting. *How we know:* Salt water stays clear, muddy water settles, and milk scatters a torch beam but never settles.

## How we found out
**Joseph Black, Joseph Priestley and William Henry, The 1750s, 1767 to 1772, and 1803.** For a long time, people thought air was one single thing. In the 1750s, Joseph Black, a Scottish doctor and chemist, heated chalky stones and weighed them. They lost weight, because a gas escaped, and the gas was not ordinary air. He called it fixed air. We now call it carbon dioxide. In 1767, Joseph Priestley, an English minister and scientist, lived next to a brewery in Leeds. Beer was fermenting in big vats, and fixed air lay in a heavy layer above them. Priestley set bowls of water above the vats. The water took in the gas and tasted pleasantly sparkling. In 1772 he published a way to push the gas into water in a bottle. In 1803 William Henry measured how much gas dissolves in water at different pressures. He found that more gas dissolves when the pressure is higher.  
*What people believed before:* People thought there was only one kind of air, and that gases could not be held in water. Black showed that chalk gives off a different gas, and Priestley and Henry showed that water can hold it, more when cold and under pressure.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Oil and water never mix.** (A common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: Shake oil and water in a bottle, and the oil soon floats back up as a layer.  
  Against: A drop of soap lets oil spread through water as tiny drops, as it does in milk. A very tiny amount of oil does dissolve in water. And oil mixes perfectly with other oil. Oil and water simply hold on to their own kind best.
- **Water is the universal solvent. It can dissolve anything.** (A phrase in many school books; *rejected*)  
  For: Water dissolves more things than any other common liquid, including salt, sugar, many acids and many minerals.  
  Against: Water does not dissolve oil, wax, plastic or gold in any amount you could see. Petrol, nail polish remover and oil are better solvents for many things. Water is a very good solvent for charged and sticky things, not for everything.
- **The fizz in soda is air.** (A common first idea; *rejected*)  
  For: The bubbles look like air bubbles.  
  Against: The gas in soda is carbon dioxide, which is squeezed into the water at the factory. Air does not dissolve much in water. When the cap is opened, the pressure falls and the dissolved carbon dioxide comes out as bubbles.
- **Salt melts ice by making heat.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Ice does melt when you sprinkle salt on it.  
  Against: The mixture of ice and salt gets colder, not warmer, so it cannot be making heat. Melting takes heat, and the ice and salt mixture takes it from its surroundings and from the milk in the bag, which is what freezes the kulfi.
- **Warm water holds more of everything, including gases.** (A natural guess after learning that warm water dissolves more sugar; *rejected*)  
  For: Warm water does dissolve more sugar, and more of many solids.  
  Against: Gases are the opposite. A warm soda holds less gas than a cold one, and warm pond water holds less oxygen than cool water. What warmth does depends on what is dissolving.
- **Milk is a solution, because it looks the same all through.** (A common first idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Milk looks smooth and even, like salt water.  
  Against: A torch beam shows up inside milk, which means there are tiny floating bits. Under a microscope you can see round drops of fat. Milk is a colloid and an emulsion, not a solution.

## Roots
We have tested which liquid takes which thing, and where gas hides in a soda. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In your test, food colour went into the water and turmeric colour went into the oil. Nothing forced them. Each colour went where it was held best. So a thing dissolves in what is most like itself.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'oil and water do not mix' as if the two simply refuse each other. But every colour in the test found a home in one of them. Mixing depends on which neighbours a thing is held by best.  
*Evidence:* In the Water or Oil test, food colour stayed in the water while turmeric and chilli colour moved into the oil. A drop of soap, which has a water end and an oil end, held on to both.

- *who am i:* Your body takes in vitamins A, D, E and K with fat, and vitamins B and C with water. You carry watery places and oily places at once.
- *what is life:* Every living cell is wrapped in a skin that forms by itself, because oily tails hide from water. Life is built on oil and water staying apart.
- *what is this world:* The sea dissolves gas from the air, as a soda does. Rain dissolves rock into caves. Our world is full of things dissolved in each other.

Water holds on to charged and sticky things such as salt and sugar. Oil holds on to oily things such as turmeric colour and carotene. Soap has one end for each, so it sits where the two meet. A cell skin, a soap bubble and a drop of milk all form at such a meeting place. Each thing goes to what is most like itself, and new shapes appear where different kinds meet.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka asked his son Shvetaketu to put a lump of salt in water and come back in the morning. The salt could not be seen. But when he sipped from the top, the middle and the bottom, each sip was salty. The Upanishad uses this to teach that the finest essence is everywhere, unseen, and says 'tat tvam asi', you are that. (The gas in a sealed soda is like that salt. You cannot see it, but it is spread through every drop, and you find it when the bottle is opened.)
- **Kabir, a poet-saint of north India in the 1400s, in a couplet sung in many collections:** Kabir says that everyone knows the drop merges into the ocean, but few know that the ocean also merges into the drop. (A drop of soap and the whole bowl of water take part in each other. In the Water or Oil test, the colour and the liquid both change what the other looks like.)
- Science (settled): A substance dissolves best in a liquid whose molecules hold it in a similar way. Charged and sticky things dissolve in water, and oily things in oil.
- Science (settled): The mass of a gas that dissolves in a liquid is in proportion to its pressure, and falls as the liquid warms.
- Science (open): How much more carbon dioxide the sea can keep taking in as it warms is still being measured.
- Other view, Many chemists: 'Like dissolves like' is only a rule of thumb, and it has many exceptions. The real reason a substance dissolves is that the mixture has less energy and more disorder than the separate parts. Talking about things finding their own kind is a story laid over that.
- Practice, One drop of colour (3 min): Fill a clear glass with water and let it go still. Add one drop of food colour. Breathe in slowly, and watch the colour open and spread. Breathe out slowly. Breathe in again, and watch the colour reach every part of the glass. Breathe out. Do this three times, then sit quietly and look at the even colour.
- Ask: Turmeric went into the oil and food colour went into the water. What do you think 'like' means when we say a thing dissolves in what is like itself?
- Ask: If your skin, your blood and the sea all hold dissolved things, where does 'you' end and the water around you begin?
- Ask: If almost nothing is totally insoluble, is anything really separate from the water around it?

## Story: The Goli Soda That Went Flat
*The story is made up. The science is real: gas dissolves less in warm water than in cold water, and a goli soda bottle is held shut by the pressure of the gas inside. Meera, her aunt and the cart are imagined.*

It was a May afternoon in Madurai, and the road shimmered with heat. Meera was nine. Every holiday, she helped her aunt, Selvi Athai, at her soda cart near the bus stand.

The cart held a crate of goli soda. Each glass bottle had a round marble stuck in its neck. When Athai pushed the marble down with a wooden opener, the bottle went pssst, and a stream of tiny bubbles rose to the top. Meera loved that sound.

Athai kept the bottles in a steel tub of water with blocks of ice. 'Cold soda is good soda,' she always said.

That Monday, the ice man did not come. By noon the water in the tub was as warm as tea, and the bottles bobbed in it, half under and half above.

A customer asked for a soda. Athai pushed the marble down. The bottle roared, and a fountain of foam shot over her hand and onto the road. The customer was left with half a glass. After two sips, the soda tasted flat and sweet, with no sparkle at all.

'Weak soda,' said the customer, and put the glass down. By evening, three more people had said the same thing.

Athai wiped her hands on her sari. 'Murugan has cheated me,' she said. Murugan was the man who brought the crates every week. 'He has put weak soda in my bottles. I will tell him not to come again.'

'Maybe it is the heat,' said Meera. 'It is the same soda.'

'The same soda cannot be strong one day and weak the next,' said Athai. 'That is what weak means.'

Her cousin Karthik, who was twelve, laughed. 'Heat cannot change soda. It is just sugar and water and gas.'

'Gas,' said Meera slowly. She looked at the foam drying on the road. Before the bottle was opened, there had been no bubbles at all. Where had all that gas been hiding?

That night she asked her aunt for two bottles from the same crate, with the same date stamped on the side. She took one to the neighbour's fridge. The other she left on the cart, in the sun, for an hour.

The next morning she set out two steel tumblers side by side. Karthik, who did not believe a word of it, held the opener.

First the cold bottle. Pssst. A thin silver thread of bubbles rose, calm and steady. Meera counted to sixty, and the bubbles were still coming.

Then the warm bottle. Pssst, WHOOSH. Foam climbed up the tumbler and spilled over the top. Within a minute the foam was gone, and the water lay still and flat.

Karthik stopped laughing. 'Same crate,' said Meera. 'Same maker. Same day. Only one of them was warm.'

She explained it the way she had worked it out. At the factory, gas is squeezed into cold water so hard that it spreads out in pieces too small to see. That squeeze is what pushes the marble up against the neck and keeps the bottle shut. In cold water, the water holds on to the gas well. In warm water, the gas slips out more easily. So a warm bottle is full of gas that is already trying to leave. When you open it, it all rushes out at once, and the soda is left flat.

Athai picked up the flat tumbler and sniffed it. 'So Murugan did not cheat me,' she said. 'The sun did.'

That afternoon Athai walked to the ice factory by the market and bought a block wrapped in a gunny sack. She put the tub in the shade of the cart's umbrella. She told every customer to open the bottle slowly and not to shake it.

On the way home, Karthik asked, 'Where does the gas go when the soda goes flat?'

'Into the air,' said Meera. 'Some goes into you, as a burp.'

Karthik laughed. This time Meera did too.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a gas can dissolve in water, spread out as molecules too small to see. A sealed soda holds gas at a high pressure, which squeezes a lot of it in, and cold water holds more gas than warm water. When the bottle is warm, the water holds less gas and more of it escapes as soon as the pressure drops, so the bottle foams over and goes flat. The soda was not weak. It was warm. The marble stays up because the pressure of the dissolved gas pushes it against the neck of the bottle.

- Ask: Why was there no bubble in the bottle before it was opened, though it was full of gas?
- Ask: Why did the warm bottle foam over and then go flat, while the cold bottle stayed fizzy?
- Ask: Athai thought Murugan had cheated her. What did Meera do to check, instead of just arguing?
- Ask: Why did Meera use two bottles from the same crate and not two from different shops?
- Ask: What did Athai do after that, and which part of the science did it use?

### e1 · Water or Oil? Which liquid takes the colour (40 min)

  *Parent says:* Here are three colours and two liquids. Where will each colour go, into the water or into the oil? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three colours. Two liquids. Where will each colour go? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think turmeric will go into the oil? (good: Turmeric stains my plate and water does not wash it.; Oil is yellow like turmeric.; Cooks put turmeric in hot oil.)

Pour 1 tablespoon of oil into each oil jar. 4. Add the same colour to one water jar and one oil jar. Use a quarter of a teaspoon of powder, or 2 drops of food colour.  
  *Parent says:* Every jar gets one tablespoon of liquid. Each colour goes in one water jar and one oil jar. Only the liquid changes, so it is a fair test.  
  *Child mode:* Label six jars. Put 1 spoon of water in three jars. Put 1 spoon of oil in the other three. Add one colour to each pair.
  - Note: One tablespoon of liquid per jar is small, which keeps the colour strong enough to see. Use a quarter of a teaspoon of powder, no more. Too much powder makes every jar cloudy. Wear old clothes.
  - Check: Why does each colour go into one water jar and one oil jar, with the same amount of liquid? → **So that only the liquid changes**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir each jar for thirty seconds with its own stick, counting aloud. 2. Look at every jar at once. 3. Write what you see in the first minute.  
  *Parent says:* Stir each jar for thirty seconds. Count out loud. Now look. What do you see in each pair of jars?  
  *Child mode:* Stir each jar for thirty seconds. Count out loud. Look at the colours. Write what you see.
  - Note: Food colour spreads through water straight away. In oil it stays as red beads and sinks. Turmeric and chilli powder look cloudy in the water and sit in the oil as specks at first.
- **s4 (12 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Child mode:* Wait ten minutes. Hold each jar against white paper. Is the colour clear, or are there bits?
  - Ask: What is the difference between a colour that has dissolved and a colour that is just floating bits? (good: Dissolved colour is clear all through.; Bits make the water cloudy and they settle.; A torch beam shows up in cloudy water.)
  - Note: Warm oil helps because particles move faster. The oil jars with turmeric and chilli should turn golden or orange-red and look clear, with a little powder lying at the bottom. If your oil colour is faint, wait ten more minutes. In the water jars, turmeric and chilli should look cloudy, with bits that slowly settle, and only a pale colour.

  *Parent does:* Fill in the table for each colour. Write what you see in the water jar, what you see in the oil jar, and which took more colour.

  *Child mode:* Fill in the table. For each colour, which liquid took more?
  - Note: Expect: turmeric and chilli, mostly oil. Food colour, only water. If one of your colours does something different, write exactly what you saw.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A strong answer: What I think: some colours go into water and some go into oil. What I saw: food colour spread through the water, and turmeric and chilli turned the oil golden and red while the water stayed pale and cloudy. Why: each colour dissolves in the liquid it is most like. Do not explain it yourself before they have answered.
  - Check: Turmeric colour is yellow and clear in the oil jar, and pale with floating bits in the water jar. What does this show? → **Turmeric colour dissolves in oil better than in water**
### e2 · Fizz and Warmth: does a warm soda hold less gas? (40 min)

  *Child mode:* Two sodas. One cold. One warm. Which balloon grows bigger? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that balloon will be bigger? (good: Warm things go flat faster.; Heat makes the fizz come out.; Cold keeps the gas in.)

  *Child mode:* Wrap the string round each balloon. Measure it on the ruler. Write the numbers.
  - Note: Expect the warm bottle's balloon to be much bigger, and the drink inside to look flat. The cold bottle's balloon is smaller, and the drink still has bubbles. If the two balloons are the same size, swirl the warm bottle ten more times and measure again. If both are tiny, the soda was flat before you began, so try a fresh bottle.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A strong answer: What I think: a warm soda holds less gas than a cold soda. What I saw: the warm bottle's balloon went much bigger, and its drink went flat. Why: the gas was dissolved in both drinks, and in the warm drink it escaped more. Do not explain it yourself before they have answered.
  - Check: The balloon on the warm soda grew bigger than the balloon on the cold soda. What does this show? → **Warm soda lets out more gas than cold soda**
### e3 · Kulfi in a Bag: does salt make ice colder? (40 min)
- **question:** Plain ice and ice with salt: which one can freeze a bag of sweet milk in ten minutes?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the milk freeze in the bag with plain ice? In the bag with ice and salt? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both bags get the same amount of milk, the same amount of ice, and the same shaking for the same time. Only the salt changes, so any difference comes from salt.
- **safety:** Salted ice gets far below zero and can hurt bare skin, so hold the bags in a tea towel or gloves, not for long. Do not let salt water into your eyes or mouth. Rinse the small bag under the tap before opening it. Do not use this test with a child who is allergic to milk.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw what the milk looks like in each bag. Then write what you think, what you saw, and why.
- **results table:** Bag | Ice only, or ice with salt | The milk after ten minutes | The outside of the big bag
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the milk, the ice and the salt. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* We will try to freeze this sweet milk in two bags. One bag sits in plain ice. The other sits in ice with salt. Which one will freeze? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Plain ice or ice with salt. Which freezes the milk? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that bag will freeze the milk? (good: Ice is cold, so plain ice should do it.; Salt makes the ice colder.; Kulfi sellers use salt, so it must help.)

  *Parent says:* Stir the sugar into the milk until it disappears. Pour half into each bag. Press the air out and seal it tightly, so no salt water can get in.  
  *Child mode:* Stir sugar into milk. Pour half into each bag. Seal each bag tightly.
  - Note: A tight seal matters, as salt water leaking in would spoil the milk. A second bag over each small bag is a good extra layer. The sugar dissolves easily in cold milk.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 3 cups of ice in each big bag. 2. Put a small milk bag into each big bag, among the ice. 3. Add 6 level tablespoons of salt to one big bag only. 4. Seal both big bags. 5. Label the salty one.  
  *Parent says:* One big bag gets ice only. The other gets ice and salt. Everything else is the same. Label the salty bag so we know which is which.  
  *Child mode:* Put ice in both big bags. Add salt to one bag only. Seal both. Label the salty one.
  - Note: Both bags get the same amount of ice, so the salt is the only difference. Rock salt works best, but table salt is fine.
  - Check: Why does only one big bag get salt? → **So that salt is the only thing that changes**
- **s4 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wrap each big bag in a tea towel. 2. Shake and knead each bag for ten minutes, by the timer. 3. After five minutes, feel the outside of each bag through the towel. 4. If you have a thermometer, read the ice in each bag.

  *Child mode:* Wrap each bag in a towel. Shake it for ten minutes. Feel the outside. What do you notice?
  - Note: Take turns, as the bags get too cold to hold for long. The salty bag usually feels much colder, and may have a frosty or damp coat on the outside. With a thermometer, the plain ice reads about zero, and the salty ice reads many degrees below zero, perhaps minus ten.

  *Parent does:* 1. Take out each small bag. 2. Rinse it under the tap so no salt water is on the outside. 3. Open it. 4. Look at the milk and write what it looks like in each bag.  
  *Parent says:* Rinse each small bag so the salt water comes off. Open it and look. Is the milk liquid, thick, slushy or firm? Which bag froze?  
  *Child mode:* Take out the small bags. Rinse them. Open them. Look at the milk. Which one froze?
  - Note: Expect the milk from the salty bag to be thick and slushy, like soft kulfi. The milk from the plain ice bag is usually still liquid, just cold. If the salty bag also did not freeze, shake for five more minutes. If you want to taste the kulfi, use a clean spoon, and only if the bag was rinsed well and sealed the whole time.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A strong answer: What I think: salt makes ice colder. What I saw: the milk froze in the ice and salt bag and stayed liquid in the plain ice bag, and the salty bag felt colder. Why: the dissolved salt lowers the freezing point of the water, so the melting ice gets colder than zero. Do not explain it yourself before they have answered.

### Water, Oil and Fizz (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these dissolves much better in oil than in water?  
   Options: The yellow colour of turmeric / Salt / Sugar / Food colour for cakes  
   **Answer:** The yellow colour of turmeric. *Why:* Turmeric colour is oily, so it dissolves in oil. Salt and sugar dissolve in water. Food colour for cakes is made to dissolve in water.
2. (sort) Sort these. Does each one dissolve mostly in water or mostly in oil?  
   **Answer:** salt → Water; camphor → Oil; sugar → Water; the heat of chilli → Oil; honey → Water; ghee → Oil. *Why:* Charged and sticky things such as salt, sugar and honey dissolve in water. Oily things such as camphor, chilli heat and ghee dissolve in oil.
3. (fill) The liquid that does the dissolving in a solution is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['solvent']. *Why:* The solvent is the liquid that dissolves the other thing. The thing that dissolves is the solute.
4. (truefalse) Oil and water can never be made to mix, however you try.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A drop of soap lets oil spread through water as tiny drops, which is how milk and mayonnaise stay mixed. The oil is not dissolved, but it does stay mixed.
5. (mcq) What does it mean to dilute a salty solution?  
   Options: Add more water, so the same salt is spread through more liquid / Add more salt / Take the water away  
   **Answer:** Add more water, so the same salt is spread through more liquid. *Why:* To dilute is to add more solvent. The amount of salt stays the same, but it is spread out, so each sip tastes less salty. Adding salt would make it stronger.
6. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Solute → the thing that dissolves; Solvent → the liquid that does the dissolving; Concentration → how much is dissolved in a certain amount of liquid; Dilute → add more liquid so the solution is weaker. *Why:* These four words describe every solution, whether it is salt in water or turmeric in oil.
7. (mcq) A glass of liquid is cloudy, a torch beam shows up inside it, and nothing settles at the bottom after an hour. What is it most likely to be?  
   Options: Milk in water, a colloid / Salt water, a solution / Muddy water, a suspension  
   **Answer:** Milk in water, a colloid. *Why:* A solution is clear. A suspension settles. A cloudy liquid that shows the beam and does not settle is a colloid, like milk.
8. (write) A grown-up bites a hot chilli. Why does a spoon of curd soothe the burn better than a sip of water?  
   **Answer:** The hot part of a chilli dissolves in fat and hardly in water. Curd has fat, so the hot chemical dissolves into it and is carried away. Water just spreads it around.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the hot chemical is fat-soluble, and that curd has fat while water does not take it up.
9. (truefalse) A warm soda holds more gas than a cold soda.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Warm liquids hold less gas. That is why a warm soda foams and goes flat quickly, and a cold soda keeps its fizz.
10. (mcq) A sealed bottle of soda shows no bubbles at all, yet it is full of gas. Where is the gas?  
   Options: Dissolved in the liquid, squeezed in by pressure / In the cap only / There is no gas until you open it  
   **Answer:** Dissolved in the liquid, squeezed in by pressure. *Why:* In the sealed bottle the pressure squeezes the gas into the water, where it spreads out as tiny pieces. When you open the bottle the pressure drops and the gas comes out as bubbles.
11. (order) Put the steps of the Kulfi in a Bag test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Seal sweet milk in two small bags', 'Put ice in both bags and salt in only one', 'Shake both bags for ten minutes', 'Compare the milk in the two small bags']. *Why:* First make the same starting point for both bags. Then change only the salt. Then shake for the same time. Last, compare the results.
12. (odd) Which one is not a solution?  
   Options: Salt water / Sugar syrup / Milk / Lemon juice mixed into water  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Milk is a colloid. It has tiny fat drops floating in it, and a torch beam shows up in it. The other three are solutions.
13. (draw) Draw two glasses of soda just after they are opened. Show a cold soda and a warm soda. Draw the bubbles in each.  
   **Answer:** The cold soda with a gentle thread of small bubbles. The warm soda with many large bubbles and foam rising over the top.. *Why:* A good drawing shows that the warm soda lets out gas much faster than the cold soda.
14. (label) Label the drawing of a soap molecule at the edge of a grease drop in water: the head, the tail, the water, the grease.

15. (write) A plastic tiffin box has a yellow turmeric stain that plain water does not remove. What would you try, and why?  
   **Answer:** Rub a little oil on the stain first, then wash with soap. Turmeric colour dissolves in oil, so the oil takes it up, and soap washes the oil away.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea that turmeric colour dissolves in oil, and says that soap is needed to rinse the oil away.
16. (perspective) Gayathri, a girl in Class 4, has a yellow turmeric stain on a white plastic box. Three people suggest ways to clean it.

### Salt, Ice and Gas (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)

   **Answer:** A warm soda that is stirred. *Why:* Warmth makes the water hold less gas, and stirring gives the gas many places to escape. A cold soda left still loses gas slowly, and a closed bottle keeps the pressure in.
### Thinking Like a Chemist (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) Plan a test to find out whether warmth changes how much gas a soda gives off. Name what you will keep the same, what you will change, and how you will measure the gas.  
   **Answer:** Use two bottles of the same soda, one cold and one warm. Open both the same way, put a balloon of the same kind on each, swirl them the same, and measure the balloons after the same time. Only the warmth changes. The size of the balloon shows how much gas came out.. *Why:* A strong plan keeps the drink, bottle, balloon, opening and swirling the same, changes only the warmth and measures the gas with the balloon.
2. (mcq) William Henry found that the mass of gas that dissolves in a liquid is in proportion to the pressure. If the pressure on a sealed soda is doubled, about how much more gas will dissolve?  
   Options: About twice as much / About the same / About half as much  
   **Answer:** About twice as much. *Why:* In proportion means that doubling the pressure doubles the gas that dissolves. That is why a sealed bottle holds so much more gas than an open glass.
3. (count) A salt pan dries up 200 litres of sea water that held 35 grams of salt in every litre. How many grams of salt are left behind?  
   **Answer:** 7000. *Why:* Each litre leaves 35 grams. 200 times 35 is 7000 grams, which is 7 kilograms.
4. (perspective) On a mountain road in the Himalayas, ice forms on the road in winter. Three people say what to do.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that salt lowers the freezing point so ice melts, but it only works down to about minus 10 or minus 20 degrees, and in colder weather it cannot help. It weighs this against the harm to streams. Sand gives grip without chemicals. A fair answer picks one and gives a real reason for the other.. *Why:* Each idea has a cost. Salt melts ice but stops working in deep cold and can harm nature. Sand gives grip but does not remove the ice.
5. (mcq) Why do kulfi sellers pack ice and salt around the kulfi pot?  
   Options: Salty ice is colder than plain ice, so it can freeze the kulfi / Salt makes the kulfi sweet / Salt makes the ice last longer  
   **Answer:** Salty ice is colder than plain ice, so it can freeze the kulfi. *Why:* Plain ice cannot freeze sweet milk, because melting ice stays at zero degrees. Salty ice can fall to about minus 20 degrees, which freezes the kulfi.
6. (write) Explain why oil does not dissolve in water. Use the idea of the water molecule's slightly charged ends.  
   **Answer:** Water molecules have slightly charged ends, which let them hold on to each other and to charged or sticky things. Oil molecules have no such ends, so water holds on to other water instead and pushes the oil out. The oil joins into drops and floats.. *Why:* A strong answer says water holds on to its own kind and to charged or sticky things, and has nothing to grip on oil.
7. (match) Match each thing to what it does.  
   **Answer:** A soap molecule → has a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail; A TDS meter → senses charged particles dissolved in water; A cell skin → is a double layer of molecules with tails hiding from water; Carbon dioxide in soda → is dissolved under pressure and escapes when the bottle opens. *Why:* Each one shows how a dissolved thing or a molecule behaves in water.
8. (truefalse) Sea water freezes at a slightly lower temperature than fresh water.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Dissolved salt lowers the freezing point, so sea water freezes at about minus 2 degrees Celsius instead of zero.
9. (odd) Which one is not an example of a dissolved gas leaving a liquid?  
   Options: Bubbles rising from a poured soda / Fish gasping in a warm pond / Big steam bubbles rising in a rolling boil / Tiny bubbles forming on the side of a warming pan of water  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The big bubbles at a rolling boil are water vapour, water that has turned into a gas. The other three are about gases that were dissolved in the liquid and come out as it warms or the pressure falls.
10. (write) The word insoluble really means 'dissolves too little for us to notice'. Explain what this means for a piece of limestone in rain.  
   **Answer:** Each raindrop dissolves only a tiny bit of the limestone. In one day we cannot see any change. But over hundreds of thousands of years the water can dissolve enough to carve a cave. So insoluble depends on how little dissolves and how long we wait.. *Why:* A strong answer joins a very small amount to a very long time.
11. (draw) Draw a zoomed-in picture of a soap molecule at the edge of a grease drop in water.  
   **Answer:** A grease drop in the middle, with tadpole-shaped soap molecules around the drop, tails pointing into the grease and heads pointing out into the water.. *Why:* The tail grips the grease and the head grips the water, so the soap wraps the grease in a ring.
12. (sort) Sort these mixtures into the three kinds.  
   **Answer:** sugar syrup that is clear → Solution; muddy water → Suspension; milk → Colloid; morning fog → Colloid; rasam strained clear → Solution; atta stirred in water → Suspension. *Why:* A solution is clear and never settles. A suspension settles. A colloid has tiny bits that never settle and scatter light, like milk and fog.
13. (mcq) A bottle of soda is opened, closed again, and left in a cold fridge for a day. It is now almost flat even though it is cold. Why?  
   Options: When it was opened the pressure fell and much of the gas escaped, and the closed bottle could not squeeze it back / Cold water cannot hold gas / The fridge removes gas  
   **Answer:** When it was opened the pressure fell and much of the gas escaped, and the closed bottle could not squeeze it back. *Why:* Opening the bottle let the pressure drop, so most of the dissolved gas left as bubbles. Closing it again does not push the gas back into the water. Cold water holds gas well, but there was little gas left.
14. (label) Label the Kulfi in a Bag set-up: the small bag of milk, the big bag, the ice, the salt and the towel.  
   **Answer:** The small bag of milk in the middle. The big bag around it. The ice packed around the small bag. The salt sprinkled in the ice. The towel wrapped around the outside.. *Why:* The milk sits in the coldest part, surrounded by salty ice, and the towel protects the hands.
15. (order) Put the steps of the Rescue the Salt challenge in the best order.  
   **Answer:** ['Take out the clips with a magnet', 'Strain out the rice', 'Dissolve the salt in water and pour through a cloth', 'Let the salty water evaporate']. *Why:* Do the dry steps first, because they use magnetism and size. Water comes later because it dissolves the salt and wets the rest. Evaporation comes last to get the salt back.
16. (write) A child gets these results: the balloon on a cold soda measured 14 centimetres around, and the balloon on a warm soda measured 28 centimetres around. Write a claim, the evidence and the reasoning.  
   **Answer:** Claim: a warm soda lets out more gas than a cold soda. Evidence: the warm soda's balloon went to 28 centimetres and the cold soda's to 14, twice as far around. Reasoning: gas is dissolved in the drink, and warm liquid holds less of it, so more escapes into the balloon.. *Why:* A strong answer states the claim, uses the two numbers as evidence and explains why warmth makes gas leave.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these dissolves in oil but hardly in water?  
   Options: The orange colour of carrots / Sugar / Salt / Lemon juice  
   **Answer:** The orange colour of carrots. *Why:* The orange colour of carrots is carotene, which dissolves in fat. Sugar, salt and lemon juice all dissolve in water.
2. (mcq) A turmeric stain on a plastic box does not come off with plain water. What is likely to lift it?  
   Options: Oil, followed by soap / More cold water / Salt water  
   **Answer:** Oil, followed by soap. *Why:* Turmeric colour dissolves in oil, so oil takes it up and soap rinses the oil away. Cold water and salt water cannot take the colour.
3. (mcq) After eating a hot chilli, which helps most?  
   Options: A spoon of curd, because the hot chemical dissolves in fat / A big glass of water, because water washes it away / A pinch of salt  
   **Answer:** A spoon of curd, because the hot chemical dissolves in fat. *Why:* The hot chemical in chilli dissolves in fat and hardly in water. Curd has fat, so it takes the chemical up. Water only spreads it around.
4. (mcq) Why does a warm soda go flat faster than a cold soda?  
   Options: Warm water holds less dissolved gas / Warm water is heavier / The bubbles in warm soda are bigger  
   **Answer:** Warm water holds less dissolved gas. *Why:* The warmer the water, the less gas it can hold, so the gas leaves sooner. The size of the bubbles is not the reason.
5. (mcq) A sealed bottle of soda has no bubbles. What keeps the gas dissolved?  
   Options: The high pressure inside the bottle / The cold glass / The label  
   **Answer:** The high pressure inside the bottle. *Why:* High pressure squeezes the gas into the water. When the pressure drops at opening, the gas comes out as bubbles.
6. (mcq) Kavya shines a torch through a glass of water from the side and sees a bright beam inside. After an hour nothing has settled. What kind of mixture is it?  
   Options: A colloid / A solution / A suspension  
   **Answer:** A colloid. *Why:* A beam shows when floating bits scatter light, so it is not a solution. Nothing settled, so it is not a suspension. It is a colloid.
7. (mcq) Salt is sprinkled on ice. What happens to the temperature of the mixture?  
   Options: It falls below zero degrees / It stays at zero degrees / It rises above zero degrees  
   **Answer:** It falls below zero degrees. *Why:* Dissolved salt lowers the freezing point, so the ice keeps melting below zero degrees, and melting takes heat from the mixture. It gets colder.
8. (mcq) Why does salt help to freeze kulfi?  
   Options: Salty ice is colder than plain ice / Salt is cold / Salt turns milk into ice  
   **Answer:** Salty ice is colder than plain ice. *Why:* Plain melting ice stays at zero, which is not cold enough. Salty ice falls much lower, enough to freeze sweet milk.
9. (mcq) Why are caves found in limestone hills?  
   Options: Rainwater slowly dissolves the limestone over a very long time / Animals dig them / The rock melts in the Sun  
   **Answer:** Rainwater slowly dissolves the limestone over a very long time. *Why:* Rainwater carries a little carbon dioxide and slowly dissolves limestone. Over hundreds of thousands of years the rock is carved into caves.
10. (mcq) What does the word insoluble really mean, if almost nothing is completely insoluble?  
   Options: It dissolves so little that we do not notice / It never dissolves at all / It dissolves in oil only  
   **Answer:** It dissolves so little that we do not notice. *Why:* Everything dissolves a very little. Insoluble means that so little dissolves that we cannot see it in a short time.
11. (mcq) People once thought air was a single thing. What did Joseph Black find when he heated chalky stones?  
   Options: They gave off a gas that was not ordinary air / They turned into water / They became lighter because they dried  
   **Answer:** They gave off a gas that was not ordinary air. *Why:* The stones lost weight because a gas escaped. This gas was different from ordinary air. It is carbon dioxide, which Priestley later dissolved in water.
12. (mcq) Fish in a warm, still pond swim to the surface. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen / Fish like warm air / The water is too deep  
   **Answer:** Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen. *Why:* Oxygen dissolves in water, and warm water holds less of it. At the surface, the water gets more oxygen from the air.
13. (mcq) Which pair is right?  
   Options: Salt dissolves in water, and camphor dissolves in oil / Sugar dissolves in oil, and ghee dissolves in water / Salt dissolves in oil, and wax dissolves in water  
   **Answer:** Salt dissolves in water, and camphor dissolves in oil. *Why:* Charged and sticky things such as salt dissolve in water. Oily things such as camphor dissolve in oil. The other pairs are the wrong way round.

## Applications
- Fizzy drinks and goli soda hold carbon dioxide dissolved in cold water under pressure.
- Soap, shampoo and washing powder lift oil and grease because one end of their molecules grips water and the other grips oil.
- Cooks fry turmeric, chilli and other spices in oil at the start of a tadka, because their colours and flavours dissolve in oil.
- Curd, buttermilk and raita soothe a hot chilli, because the hot chemical dissolves in their fat.
- Kulfi and ice-cream makers pack ice with salt to make a mixture colder than ice alone.
- In cold hill regions, salt is spread on icy roads to lower the freezing point of water.
- Natural dyers extract colour from onion skins, turmeric and indigo with hot water, and fix it on cloth.
- Doctors and pharmacists choose whether a medicine or vitamin must be taken with food, because some dissolve in fat and some in water.
- Divers rise slowly from deep water, because gas dissolved in their blood under pressure can form bubbles if the pressure falls too fast.
- Perfume makers in Kannauj make attars by catching the smell of flowers in sandalwood oil.
- Fish keepers and pond managers watch the warmth of the water, because warm water holds less dissolved oxygen.
- Water purifiers use TDS meters to estimate how much charged material is dissolved in the water.

## Open questions
- **Why do bubbles begin at some places in a soda and not at others?** Scientists know that bubbles start at tiny scratches and specks. They are still working out exactly how the very first tiny bubble forms inside a clear liquid.
- **How much more carbon dioxide can the sea keep taking in as it grows warmer?** Warm water holds less gas. The sea has taken in a large share of the carbon dioxide people release, but how long it can keep doing so is still being measured.
- **How did the first cell skins form on the early Earth?** Fat-like molecules form skins in water by themselves, which makes this seem possible. Nobody knows exactly how the first living cells came to have one.
- **Why do chilli plants make a chemical that burns mammals, while birds eat them without a burn?** Scientists have shown that birds eat chillies and spread their seeds, while mammals chew the seeds and destroy them. They think this is why the burn evolved, but they are still testing the idea.
- **Can we design a drug that dissolves well in both water and fat?** Many medicines must dissolve in the watery blood and also cross the fatty skin of a cell. Finding the balance for each new drug is one of the hard problems of medicine.
- **Will sea water in the future dissolve rock and shell faster, as it takes in more carbon dioxide?** Scientists see that the sea is slowly becoming a little more acidic, but how this will change shells, corals and reefs in each place is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- A bottle holds 500 millilitres of oil. One tablespoon is 15 millilitres. How many whole tablespoons of oil are in the bottle? Each Water or Oil test uses 3 tablespoons of oil. How many tests can you do? **33 tablespoons, and 11 tests.**. 500 divided by 15 is 33 with 5 left over, so there are 33 whole tablespoons. 33 divided by 3 is 11, so you can do 11 tests. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In the Fizz and Warmth test, the cold soda's balloon was 14 centimetres around and the warm soda's balloon was 28 centimetres around. How many centimetres bigger around was the warm balloon? How many times as big around? **14 centimetres bigger, and 2 times as big.**. 28 take away 14 is 14. 14 doubled is 28, so the warm balloon was 2 times as big around. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L4`)
- Plain melting ice is at 0 degrees Celsius. In the Kulfi in a Bag test, the salty ice reads minus 12 degrees. The sweet milk freezes at about minus 2 degrees. How many degrees colder than plain ice is the salty ice? Is the salty ice colder than the freezing point of the milk, and by how much? **12 degrees colder than plain ice, and 10 degrees colder than the milk's freezing point.**. From 0 down to minus 12 is 12 degrees. From minus 2 down to minus 12 is 10 degrees. So the salty ice is below the milk's freezing point, and the milk can freeze. Plain ice at 0 is above it, so the milk stays liquid. (queues `math.number-sense.negative-numbers.L4`)
- Sea water holds about 35 grams of salt in every litre. A tumbler holds 250 millilitres, which is one quarter of a litre. About how many grams of salt are in a full tumbler of sea water? **About 9 grams.**. A quarter of 35 is 35 divided by 4, which is 8 and three quarters. That is about 9 grams of salt. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A water purifier's meter shows 300 milligrams of dissolved solids in every litre of tap water, and 30 milligrams in every litre of purified water. How many times lower is the purified water? How many grams are dissolved in a 20-litre can of tap water? **10 times lower, and 6 grams.**. 300 divided by 30 is 10, so the purified water has a tenth as much. In 20 litres there are 20 times 300 milligrams, which is 6000 milligrams. 1000 milligrams make 1 gram, so that is 6 grams. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L4`)
- In the Water, Oil, Both or Neither card game there are 24 thing cards. 8 go on WATER, 8 on OIL, 2 on BOTH and 6 on NEITHER. If you turn over one card at random, which mat is it most likely to go on, and which is least likely? What fraction of the pile goes on WATER? **WATER and OIL are equally most likely, BOTH is least likely, and one third of the pile goes on WATER.**. WATER and OIL have 8 cards each, NEITHER has 6, and BOTH has only 2. 8 out of 24 is 1 out of 3, so one third of the pile goes on WATER. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- A dye used 2 tablespoons of tea leaves for 2 cups of water. For a bigger batch of 6 cups of water, how many tablespoons of tea leaves keep the dye equally strong? **6 tablespoons.**. 6 cups is 3 times 2 cups, so you need 3 times as many tablespoons. 3 times 2 is 6 tablespoons. Keeping the same amount for each cup keeps the dye equally strong. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A litre of water at 20 degrees Celsius holds about 9 milligrams of dissolved oxygen. At 30 degrees it holds about 7 and a half milligrams. How many milligrams less does it hold at 30 degrees? Is that more or less than one fifth of the amount at 20 degrees? **1 and a half milligrams less. It is less than one fifth.**. 9 take away 7 and a half is 1 and a half. One fifth of 9 is 1 and four fifths, which is about 1.8. Since 1.5 is less than 1.8, the loss is less than one fifth. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
