# Our Sun: why its light warms things, and why we never look at it
`sci.sky.the-sun.L3`

**Summary.** Sunlight feels gentle on your skin, yet a magnifying glass can gather it into a dot that makes paper smoke. The Sun is a star, a huge ball of very hot gas, and the nearest star to us. About 109 Earths would fit across its face. It shines because deep inside it, hydrogen is squeezed into helium, which gives out energy. It is not a fire. Its light crosses space in about 8 minutes, and turns into heat wherever something absorbs it, such as black paper, a road or your skin. The lens inside your eye gathers light just as a magnifying glass does, so looking straight at the Sun can harm your eyes for ever. We watch the Sun safely through its reflections, shadows and pictures on a wall.

**Intro.** *Stand in a patch of sunlight from a window for one minute. Then step into the shade next to it. Which feels warmer? That sunlight has just crossed 150 million kilometres of nearly empty space, where there is almost nothing to warm. So where does the warmth on your skin come from?* The warmth is made right on your skin. Sunlight is a kind of energy, and when your skin absorbs it, the light turns into heat. In this unit you gather sunlight with a magnifying glass and find out how much heat is hiding in it. You also find out what the Sun is, how it makes its light without any fire, and why the lens in your own eye means you must never look straight at it.

## Terms
- **Star**: A huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. The Sun is a star. *e.g.* The stars you see at night are suns too. They look tiny because they are very far away.
- **The Sun**: The star at the centre of our solar system. The Earth and the other planets go round it. *e.g.* The Sun is about 150 million kilometres from the Earth. Its light takes about 8 minutes to reach us.
- **Energy**: What makes things happen: move, warm up, light up or grow. Sunlight carries energy. *e.g.* Sunlight dries the clothes on the line and warms the balcony floor. Both need energy.
- **Absorb**: To take in. When a thing absorbs light, the light goes into it and turns into heat. *e.g.* A black umbrella gets hot in the sun, because it absorbs most of the light that falls on it.
- **Reflect**: To send light back. A shiny or white surface reflects most of the light that falls on it. *e.g.* A white wall stays cooler than a dark one in the sun, because it reflects most of the light.
- **Heat**: Energy that makes things warmer. Heat moves from warmer things to cooler things. *e.g.* The heat from a sunny tile floor goes into your feet, so the tiles feel warm.
- **Lens**: A clear, curved piece of glass or plastic that bends light. A lens that is thicker in the middle can gather light to a point. *e.g.* A magnifying glass is a lens. So are the glasses some people wear, and there is one inside each of your eyes.
- **Focus**: To gather light into one small spot. The spot where the light meets is also called the focus. *e.g.* Lift a magnifying glass slowly above paper in the sun. The bright circle shrinks to a tiny dot. That dot is the focus.
- **Core**: The very centre of the Sun, where its energy is made. It is about 15 million degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Almost all the Sun's energy is made in its core, the inner quarter of the way out from the centre.
- **Hydrogen and helium**: The two lightest kinds of atom. The Sun is made mostly of hydrogen, with most of the rest helium. *e.g.* Helium is the gas that makes balloons float. Most of the Sun's helium was made from hydrogen.
- **Nuclear fusion**: Joining the centres of tiny atoms together. In the Sun's core, hydrogen joins to make helium, and this gives out energy. *e.g.* Fusion makes all the Sun's light. It does not need air, so the Sun is not burning like a fire.
- **Retina**: A thin layer at the back of the eye that catches light and sends signals to the brain. *e.g.* The lens in your eye focuses light onto your retina. That makes a picture you can see.
- **Ultraviolet light**: Light from the Sun that our eyes cannot see. It can burn skin and fade colours. *e.g.* A curtain that hangs in a sunny window fades, mostly because of ultraviolet light.
- **Infrared light**: Light from the Sun that our eyes cannot see, but our skin feels as warmth. *e.g.* About half of the Sun's energy reaching the ground comes as infrared light.
- **Photosynthesis**: How plants make their food from sunlight, water and air. *e.g.* A tulsi plant on the windowsill photosynthesises with the sunlight that reaches her leaves.
- **Bask**: To lie in the sunshine to get warm. *e.g.* A garden lizard basks on a sunny wall in the morning, to warm her body before she hunts.
- **Solar eclipse**: When the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth and hides part or all of the Sun. *e.g.* During an eclipse, many people are tempted to look at the Sun. It is just as harmful then as on any other day.
- **Eclipse glasses**: Special glasses with a dark filter that lets through only a tiny part of the Sun's light. Safe ones are marked ISO 12312-2. *e.g.* Ordinary sunglasses let through far too much light to look at the Sun. Only eclipse glasses made for this are safe.
- **Solar panel**: A flat panel that turns sunlight straight into electricity. *e.g.* Many buildings in India now have solar panels on the roof, and some have solar water heaters too.

## Explanations
### The Sun is a star · *defined*
**Definition:** A star is a huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. The Sun is a star, and the nearest one to the Earth.

The Sun looks much bigger and brighter than the stars at night only because it is so much closer. It is about 150 million kilometres away. The next nearest star is about 270,000 times further. The Sun is about 109 times as wide as the Earth, and more than a million Earths would fit inside it. It holds almost all the material in the solar system: more than 99 parts in every 100. Most of the Sun is hydrogen, the lightest kind of atom, and most of the rest is helium. Its glowing surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius. That is hot enough to melt any rock or metal.
- e.g. If the Earth were a small marble, 1 centimetre wide, the Sun would be a ball more than a metre wide.
- e.g. The Sun is not the biggest star. Many stars are much bigger, and many are much smaller.
### How the Sun makes its light · *defined*
**Definition:** In the Sun's core, the centres of hydrogen atoms are squeezed together so hard that they join to make helium. This is called nuclear fusion, and it gives out the energy that becomes sunlight.

Many people think the Sun is on fire. It is not. A fire needs oxygen from the air, and there is no air in space. The Sun's core is about 15 million degrees Celsius, and the weight of the whole Sun presses down on it. There, hydrogen is joined into helium. A helium atom made this way weighs a tiny bit less than the four hydrogen atoms that made it. The missing bit of material turns into energy. Every second, the Sun turns about 4 million tonnes of its material into energy. The Sun is so big that it has shone like this for about 4.6 billion years, and will go on for about 5 billion more.
- e.g. A fire the size of the Sun, burning coal, would have burnt out in a few thousand years. The Sun is about a million times older than that.
- e.g. Fusion needs no air. That is why the Sun shines in empty space.
### How long sunlight takes to reach us · *idea*
Once light leaves the Sun's surface, it reaches the Earth in about 8 minutes and 20 seconds. Scientists measure that very exactly. Inside the Sun, the energy's trip is much slower. The Sun's insides are so packed that the energy is taken in and given out again countless times, in random directions, on its way from the core to the surface. Scientists work out how long this takes from models of the inside of the Sun, because nobody can see in there. Their answers range from about 10,000 years to about 170,000 years. So the sunlight on your skin today began its journey in the core many thousands of years ago, and spent only its last 8 minutes crossing space.
- e.g. The sunshine on your balcony at lunchtime left the Sun's surface at about a quarter to one, 8 minutes earlier.
- e.g. If the Sun stopped shining, we would not know for about 8 minutes.
- **Scientists who use one kind of Sun model:** The energy takes about 170,000 years to work its way out. NASA often gives this number.
- **Scientists who use other models:** It could be as short as about 10,000 years. The answer depends on how packed the Sun's insides are, which can only be worked out, not seen.
### Sunlight turns into heat where it is absorbed · *defined*
**Definition:** Sunlight is energy. When a thing absorbs sunlight, that energy makes its tiny particles jiggle faster, and we feel that as heat.

Space between the Sun and the Earth is nearly empty, so there is almost nothing there to absorb the light and warm up. The warmth appears only when the light lands on something. A black thing absorbs most of the light that falls on it, so it gets hot quickly. A white thing reflects most of the light, so it stays cooler. Even the air is warmed mostly from below. Sunlight passes through clear air, warms the ground, and the warm ground then warms the air above it. That is why a black road in Chennai in May can be far too hot to stand on barefoot, while a white wall nearby is only warm.
- e.g. A black car parked in the sun gets hotter inside than a white car parked beside it.
- e.g. Roofs painted white keep the rooms under them cooler, because the white paint reflects most of the sunlight.
### A lens gathers sunlight into a dot · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnifying glass is a lens that is thicker in the middle. It bends the sunlight passing through it, so that all the light meets in one small spot, the focus.

Hold a magnifying glass just above paper in the sun, and you see a bright circle about as big as the lens. Lift it slowly, and the circle shrinks to a tiny, dazzling dot. The lens has made no new light. It has only taken all the light that falls on the glass and packed it into a much smaller place. A magnifying glass about 5 centimetres wide can pack the light from its whole face into a dot smaller than a grain of rice. So the dot gets hundreds of times more light than the same patch of paper in plain sunshine. Black paper absorbs that light and gets hot enough to smoke within seconds. The dot is really a tiny picture of the Sun, which is why it is round.
- e.g. A glass bottle of water left on a sunny windowsill can act as a lens. Fires have been started this way.
- e.g. A magnifying glass must never be left lying in sunlight, near curtains or papers.
### Why we never look straight at the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** The eye has its own lens. If you look at the Sun, that lens focuses the Sun's light into a tiny bright spot on the retina, at the back of the eye, and this harms the retina's cells.

The eye's lens works much like a magnifying glass. It focuses light from the things you look at into a small picture on your retina. That is how you see. When you look at the Sun, the picture of the Sun on your retina is a tiny, fierce dot of light. The retina has no pain nerves, so you feel nothing while it is being harmed. The harm can show only hours later, as a blurred or dark spot in the middle of what you see. It can last for ever. Sunglasses do not make it safe. They let through far too much light. Binoculars and telescopes are even worse, because they gather more light. During a solar eclipse the Sun is just as harmful, and people are more tempted to stare. Safe ways to watch the Sun are its picture on a wall, made by a pinhole or a mirror, or eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2.
- e.g. After solar eclipses, eye doctors in many countries, India among them, have treated people whose eyes were harmed by looking at the Sun.
- e.g. Never look at the Sun through a magnifying glass, binoculars, a camera viewfinder or a telescope.
### The light we cannot see: infrared and ultraviolet · *defined*
**Definition:** Sunlight carries more than the light we see. Infrared light is beyond red, and we feel it as warmth. Ultraviolet light is beyond violet, and it can burn skin and fade colours.

In 1800, William Herschel, an astronomer in England, split sunlight into a rainbow with a prism. He put thermometers in each colour, and one just past the red end, where he could see no light at all. That last thermometer got the warmest. He had found infrared light. A year later, Johann Ritter in Germany found ultraviolet light beyond the violet end, because it darkened a chemical that light darkens. About half of the Sun's energy that reaches the ground is infrared. A small part is ultraviolet. Ultraviolet light is what tans and burns skin, yellows old newspaper and fades a curtain in a sunny window. Our skin also uses a little of it to make vitamin D, which keeps bones strong.
- e.g. A shirt left on the line for weeks fades on the side that faces the Sun.
- e.g. You can feel the warmth of sunlight on your closed eyelids, even though infrared light is invisible.
### Living things use sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** Almost all life on Earth runs on sunlight. Plants catch it to make food, and animals use its warmth.

A plant cannot walk to find food. She makes her own. Her leaves catch sunlight and use its energy to make sugar from water and air. This is photosynthesis. A plant on a dark shelf grows long and pale as she reaches for light, and a plant by a window turns her leaves towards the light. Animals that eat plants, and animals that eat those animals, are all living on that caught sunlight. A garden lizard has a different problem. Her body does not make much heat of its own, so on a cool morning she is slow and cannot chase insects. So she basks on a sunny wall, flat and wide, to absorb as much sunlight as she can. Once she is warm, she can move fast and hunt.
- e.g. The rice in your plate grew with sunlight that fell on a paddy field.
- e.g. In the cool early morning, you can sometimes see a garden lizard or a skink lying still on a sunny wall.
### What colour is the Sun? · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun's light is a mix of all colours, and it is white. It looks yellow or orange from the ground because the air scatters some of its blue light away.

Children often draw the Sun yellow. Seen from space, the Sun is white. Our air scatters blue light more than the other colours. That scattered blue light is what makes the sky blue. With some blue taken out, the Sun looks a little yellow from the ground. At sunrise and sunset, the light passes through much more air, so even more blue and green are scattered away, and the Sun looks orange or red. You can check the Sun's real colour safely: its picture on a white wall, made by a mirror at midday, looks white.
- e.g. Photos of the Sun taken by astronauts in space show it white.
- e.g. At sunset over the sea at Kanyakumari, the Sun looks deep orange.

## Mostly agreed
- The Sun is a star, the nearest one to the Earth, and it is made mostly of hydrogen and helium. *How we know:* The colours in sunlight show which atoms are in the Sun. Other stars show the same kinds of colours.
- The Sun shines by nuclear fusion, joining hydrogen into helium in its core. *How we know:* Fusion gives out tiny particles called neutrinos. Huge detectors deep underground, in Japan, Canada and elsewhere, catch neutrinos from the Sun's core, in about the numbers fusion predicts.
- Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach the Earth. *How we know:* We know the speed of light and the distance to the Sun very exactly. The distance is measured by bouncing radar signals off the planets.
- Looking straight at the Sun can harm the retina for ever, and ordinary sunglasses do not protect it. *How we know:* Eye doctors around the world, including in India, find damage to the centre of the retina in people who watched an eclipse without safe filters.
- Sunlight turns into heat where it is absorbed, and dark things absorb more of it than light-coloured things. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with black and white paper in the sun. Thermometers and heat cameras show the same.

## How we found out
**Hermann von Helmholtz and Lord Kelvin, who were wrong, then Arthur Eddington, Francis Aston, Cecilia Payne and Hans Bethe, 1854 to 1939.** For a long time, people pictured the Sun as a giant fire. In the 1800s, scientists checked this idea with numbers. A ball of coal as big as the Sun, burning as brightly, would burn out in a few thousand years. But rocks and fossils showed that the Earth was far older. In 1854 Hermann von Helmholtz, a German scientist, suggested that the Sun shines because it is slowly shrinking, and the squeezing heats it. In 1862 Lord Kelvin, a famous British scientist, used this idea. He wrote that the Sun had probably not shone for 100 million years. Geologists, who study rocks, said the Earth's rocks needed much longer than that. In 1920 Arthur Eddington, an English astronomer, pointed to a new measurement by Francis Aston: a helium atom weighs a little less than four hydrogen atoms. If the Sun joins hydrogen into helium, the missing material could become energy, enough for billions of years. In 1925 Cecilia Payne showed that the Sun is mostly hydrogen, so the fuel is there. In 1939 Hans Bethe worked out how the joining happens. The Sun is not a fire, and it has shone for about 4.6 billion years.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the Sun was a giant fire, and later that it shone by slowly shrinking. Both would have used up the Sun in far less time than the Earth's rocks show it has existed. Fusion, joining hydrogen into helium, gives enough energy for billions of years.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Sun is a giant fire.** (A very common idea, and what many scientists thought until the 1800s; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun is hot and bright, like a fire, and it gives out heat and light.  
  Against: A fire needs oxygen, and space has none. A fire as big as the Sun would have burnt out in a few thousand years, but the Earth's rocks show the Sun has shone for billions of years. The Sun shines by fusion.
- **Sunglasses make it safe to look at the Sun.** (Many people, children and adults; *rejected*)  
  For: Sunglasses make the Sun look much less bright, so it feels comfortable.  
  Against: Even dark sunglasses let through thousands of times too much of the Sun's light, and some harmful light we cannot see. The retina has no pain nerves, so it gives no warning. Only eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2 are safe for looking at the Sun.
- **The Sun is yellow.** (Most children's drawings, and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: From the ground, the Sun often looks pale yellow, and orange at sunrise and sunset.  
  Against: From space, astronauts see a white Sun. The air scatters some blue light away, which makes the Sun look yellow from below.
- **The Sun is only harmful to look at during an eclipse.** (A common idea, because warnings are given mostly before eclipses; *rejected*)  
  For: Most cases of eye harm from the Sun happen just after eclipses.  
  Against: The Sun is just as harmful on every day. During an eclipse, the sky gets darker, the eyes open wider, and people stare for longer. That is why more people are harmed then.
- **The Sun will go on shining for ever.** (Many people assume this; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun has shone every day of human history, and it looks the same each day.  
  Against: The Sun is slowly using up the hydrogen in its core. In about 5 billion years it will swell into a huge red star, and later shrink into a small, fading white one.

## Roots
We have learned what the Sun is, how its light turns into heat, and why a lens can make it fierce. Now we ask what the Sun's light tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Under the lens, black paper smoked in seconds, though the sunlight on it had only felt warm. The lens made no light. It gathered the light already falling on it into one dot. The lens in your eye gathers sunlight the same way, onto a spot that has no pain nerves.  
*Habit it breaks:* We judge whether something is harmful by how it feels. Sunlight feels gentle, so it seems safe. But how strong light is depends on how tightly it is gathered, and the spot in the eye where it is gathered has no pain nerves.  
*Evidence:* In the Sun Dot experiment, the big circle of light never made the paper smoke in 60 seconds. The tiny dot made from the same light did it in a few seconds. Nothing was added. The light was only packed into a smaller place.

- *who am i:* Your skin, your eyes and your food all take in sunlight. Part of you is always catching the Sun.
- *what is life:* Almost every living thing runs on sunlight caught by plants, and a lizard on a wall warms up in the sunshine to start her day.
- *what is this world:* The Sun is one star among billions. The Earth catches only a tiny share of its light, and that share warms the whole world.

All the Sun's light starts in one small place, its core. From there it spreads out in every direction, across space. When it lands, it becomes many different things: warmth on your back, food in a leaf, a dry vadam, a faded curtain. A lens shows that the same light can be gentle or fierce, depending only on how much of it meets in one spot. One source becomes all these things, without ever changing what it is.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 8.6.2:** Just as a long road runs between two villages, the rays of the Sun run between the Sun and us. They start from the Sun and enter into the channels of the body. (Science agrees that sunlight really does enter us. Our skin absorbs it as warmth, our eyes catch it on the retina, and our food is made from it.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 6 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 500 BCE:** The Sun is new each day. (Every moment, the Sun's core turns about 4 million tonnes of material into energy. The light reaching you today is not yesterday's light. It is new light, made from the Sun itself.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988):** If you look deeply into a sheet of paper, you can see the sunshine in it. Without sunshine, the forest cannot grow, and without the forest there is no paper. (The paper in the Sun Dot experiment was made from trees that grew with sunlight. In the experiment, sunlight met paper made from sunlight.)
- Science (settled): The Sun shines by nuclear fusion, joining hydrogen into helium in its core.
- Science (settled): The Earth catches only about one part in two billion of the Sun's light.
- Science (open): Why the Sun's corona is millions of degrees hotter than its surface.
- Other view, Many physicists: Sunlight is energy. It turns into heat in a leaf or a sheet of paper because of how atoms absorb light, not because the Sun gives anything to anyone. Calling it a gift or a road into the body is a lovely picture, but it adds nothing to the physics.

Now it is turning into warmth, right here on your skin. Take six slow breaths. Notice exactly where you feel the warmth. Then open your eyes, without turning to look at the Sun.

## Story: The Smoke on the Vadam Cloth
*Made up. Nila, Arun, their Paati and their building in Chennai are invented. The science is real: a magnifying glass left in sunlight can scorch cloth, the eye's lens focuses sunlight onto the retina, and a covered mirror makes a safe picture of the Sun on a wall.*

It was April in Chennai, and the sun on the terrace was so strong that the floor tiles were hot by nine in the morning. Nila was nine. Her little brother Arun was six. Every summer, their Paati made rice vadams, and every summer, the children helped to dry them.

Paati spread an old white veshti across the terrace floor and weighed down its corners with bricks. Nila pressed the soft rice dough through the brass mould, and little curly vadams dropped onto the cloth in rows. Arun was meant to help. Instead he lay on his back, squinting at the sky.

'Paati, does the Sun have spots?' he asked. 'My book says it has dark spots.'

'So I have heard,' said Paati. She took out her reading glass, the round magnifying glass she used for the small print in the newspaper. She read out the price of tomatoes, frowned, and put the glass down on the edge of the cloth. Then she went downstairs to bring more dough. Arun ran down after her.

Nila carried on pressing vadams. After a few minutes, she sniffed. Something smelt like a candle that had just been blown out. She looked around. At the edge of the cloth, where Paati's glass lay tilted on a brick, a thin grey thread of smoke was rising.

Nila jumped up and lifted the glass away. Under it, on the white cloth, was a brown spot as small as a mustard seed, with a black rim. It was still smoking. She poured water from her tumbler over it, and the smoke stopped.

When Paati came back, Nila showed her the spot. 'Your glass did this,' she said.

'My glass?' said Paati. 'It only makes the letters big.'

Nila thought about it. 'The glass is thick in the middle,' she said slowly. 'It bends the sunlight. Maybe it gathers all the light that falls on it into one little spot.'

'Show me,' said Paati. She tore a strip off the newspaper and put it on a steel plate. She held the glass above it herself, because she did not want Nila holding a lens in that sun. When she held it low, a big, soft circle of light lay on the paper. Nothing happened. When she lifted it slowly, the circle shrank. It became a tiny dot, so bright that Nila had to look away. Under the dot, a black letter began to smoke. It took only a few seconds. The white paper between the letters took much longer.

'The sun on my hand feels warm, not hot,' said Nila. 'But when the glass squeezes it into a dot, it burns.'

Just then Arun came up the stairs. He was wearing Amma's big sunglasses, and he had Appa's binoculars round his neck. 'I am going to look at the spots on the Sun,' he said. 'The sunglasses will make it safe.'

'Stop!' said Nila, and she stood in front of him.

She made him sit down by the brown spot on the cloth. 'Look at this,' she said. 'Paati's glass gathered the sunlight into a dot, and the dot burned the cloth. Your eye has a lens inside it too. It gathers light the same way, onto the back of your eye. If you look at the Sun, it makes a dot like this, right inside you. Binoculars are two big lenses. They would gather even more.'

'But I would feel it,' said Arun. 'I would shut my eyes.'

'That's the trouble,' said Paati quietly. 'The back of the eye has no pain nerves, so it gives no warning. When I was a girl, my cousin Ravi watched an eclipse with his bare eyes. It did not hurt at all. The next morning there was a grey patch in the middle of everything he looked at. It is still there, sixty years later.'

Arun took off the sunglasses. 'Then I can never see the Sun's spots,' he said, and his lip wobbled.

Nila thought again. 'We can see the Sun without looking at it,' she said. 'Paati, can I borrow your little mirror?'

Paati's small round mirror lived in her handbag. Nila covered it with a piece of paper and cut a square hole in the paper, about as wide as her fingernail. In the corner of the terrace was the door to the dark staircase. Its far wall was painted white. Nila stood in the sun and tilted the mirror until a patch of reflected light landed deep inside, on that white wall.

Arun ran in to look. On the wall was a round, white circle of light, about the size of a biscuit.

'It's round,' said Arun. 'But the hole is square.'

'Because it isn't a picture of the hole,' said Nila. 'It's a picture of the Sun.'

Arun stared at it for a long time. He could stare as long as he liked, because he was looking at a wall. He did not see any spots. 'They're too small for our little mirror,' said Nila. 'But this is the real Sun, and you can look at it as long as you want.'

By evening, the vadams were dry and crisp. Paati folded the veshti with the little brown spot in it. 'All day the sun dried my vadams, and nobody gathered it into any dot,' she said. 'And it nearly burned my veshti when one glass did.'

That night, Paati's reading glass went into her drawer, out of the sun. Arun gave Amma back her sunglasses, and asked Nila to make the wall Sun again the next day.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the magnifying glass made no new light. It gathered the sunlight falling on its whole face into one tiny dot, so that dot got hundreds of times more light than plain sunshine, and the cloth absorbed it as heat. The black letters smoked first because black absorbs more light than white. The eye's lens does the same job, focusing light onto the retina, which has no pain nerves. That is why looking at the Sun gives no warning, and why sunglasses and binoculars make it no safer. The covered mirror sent a beam of sunlight onto a dark wall. Far enough away, any small hole gives a round spot, because the spot is a picture of the round Sun.

- Ask: Why was the spot of light on the wall round, when the hole in the paper was square?

- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them face down.; On your turn, take the top card, read it out, and put it on SAFE or NOT SAFE.; Say why. A reason earns an extra point. A good reason uses the words lens, retina or picture.; Check the answer in the brackets. A right sort scores 1 point.; Playing alone? Sort all 20, then check them all and count your points.; The player with the most points wins.
- **Printable cards: 20 cards to cut out:** Looking at the Sun's picture on a wall, made by a covered mirror (SAFE); A pinhole viewer made from card and foil (SAFE); The little suns under a colander on white paper (SAFE); Eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2, with no scratches (SAFE); Watching the Sun's shadow pictures under a tree (SAFE); Watching an eclipse live on television or on a phone stream (SAFE); The Sun's picture inside a shoebox pinhole viewer (SAFE); Welder's glass, shade number 14 (SAFE); Ordinary sunglasses (NOT SAFE); Two or three pairs of sunglasses worn together (NOT SAFE); Looking through binoculars at the Sun (NOT SAFE); Looking through a telescope with no special Sun filter (NOT SAFE); Looking at the Sun for just one second (NOT SAFE); Looking through an old X-ray film (NOT SAFE); Looking through a piece of glass blackened with candle smoke (NOT SAFE); Looking through a magnifying glass at the Sun (NOT SAFE); Eclipse glasses with a scratch or a hole (NOT SAFE); Looking at the Sun when it is half covered in an eclipse (NOT SAFE); Looking at the Sun through a camera's eyepiece (NOT SAFE); Looking at the Sun through a CD (NOT SAFE)
- **Printable board: A sorting mat with two columns:** SAFE: a picture of an eye with a smile; NOT SAFE: a picture of an eye with a red cross
- **Printable score sheet: A score sheet:** Player name; Cards sorted right; Reasons given
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay out the sorting mat and shuffle the cards.  
  *Parent says:* Each card shows a way people try to watch the Sun. Some are safe, and some can harm your eyes for ever. Let's sort them.  
  *Child mode:* Each card is a way to watch the Sun. Is it safe or not safe? Sort them!
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the card 'Ordinary sunglasses' as a practice round.  
  *Parent says:* Practice card. Ordinary sunglasses. Safe or not safe?  
  *Child mode:* Practice card. Ordinary sunglasses. Safe or not safe?
  - Check: Is it safe to look at the Sun through ordinary sunglasses? → **No, they still let through far too much light**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns to sort the rest of the cards, giving a reason each time.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn. Read the card, sort it, and tell us why.  
  *Child mode:* Take a card. Read it. Sort it. Say why!
  - Note: Listen for reasons that mention the eye's lens focusing the Sun's light onto the retina. The safe ways all have one thing in common: you look at a picture of the Sun, or through a filter made for it, never at the Sun itself.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the points and talk about the pattern.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the SAFE column. What do most of these have in common?  
  *Child mode:* Look at all the safe cards. What do they have in common?
  - Check: What do the colander, the pinhole viewer and the mirror picture have in common? → **You look at a picture of the Sun, not at the Sun**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Sun Dot: when does sunlight make paper smoke? (30 min)
- **question:** Sunlight only warms paper. Does it make paper smoke when a magnifying glass gathers it into a tiny dot? And does black paper or white paper smoke first?
- **predict:** Guess first. When the magnifying glass makes a big circle of sunlight on black paper, what will happen? When it makes a tiny dot, what will happen? Will black paper or white paper smoke first, or neither? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same magnifying glass, the same steel plate and the same sunshine for every try. In part 1, only the size of the spot changes: a big circle, then a tiny dot. In part 2, only the colour of the paper changes. Try each paper 3 times, on a fresh spot each time, because one try can be a fluke. Stop counting at 60 seconds.
- **safety:** A grown-up holds the magnifying glass the whole time. Never look at the Sun, and never look at the Sun through the lens. Never point the dot at skin, at anyone, or at clothes, curtains or plants. Work on a steel plate, away from anything that can burn. Stop as soon as you see smoke and move the lens away. Pour water on the paper at the end. Put the lens away indoors in a drawer, because a lens left in the sun can start a fire.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the lens, the paper and the light: once for the big circle and once for the tiny dot.
- **results table:** Paper | Big circle or tiny dot | Seconds until smoke, try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | What I saw
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose a spot in direct sunshine, on a balcony, a terrace or at an open window. 2. Put the steel plate on the floor there, away from curtains and plants. 3. Put the black paper on the plate, and the tumbler of water beside it. 4. Put on sunglasses, both of you.  
  *Parent says:* Today we gather sunlight with a magnifying glass. I will hold the glass, and you will watch and count. There is one rule above all. We never look at the Sun, not even through the glass.  
  *Child mode:* Find a sunny spot. Put the steel plate down. Lay the black paper on it. Put the water next to it. A grown-up holds the glass. Never look at the Sun.
  - Note: The sun must fall straight on the plate, not through a closed window that is dusty or tinted. An open window or a balcony is best. If the sky is cloudy, try another day. Hazy sun works, but the paper takes longer to smoke. Keep children's fingers out from under the lens.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, and to give a reason, before anything is tried.  
  *Parent says:* Before we try anything, tell me your guess. When I hold the glass low, it makes a big circle of light. When I lift it, the circle shrinks to a tiny dot. What will each one do to the paper? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. What will the big circle do to the paper? What will the tiny dot do? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Will the tiny dot be hotter than the big circle? Why? (good: Yes, because all the light is squeezed into one small place.; I don't know, because it is the same sunlight.)
  - Note: Write the guess down. Many children think the lens makes the light stronger, or that the paper will catch fire at once. Both are fine guesses. Don't explain yet.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the magnifying glass flat, about 2 cm above the black paper, so that a big circle of light lies on it. 2. Count to 60 out loud together. 3. Move the lens away. 4. Let your child touch the paper with one finger.  
  *Parent says:* This is the big circle. The glass is letting all its light through, but spread out. Let's count to 60 and watch. Now touch the paper. What do you feel?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the big circle of light on the paper. Count to 60. Does it smoke? Now touch the paper. Is it warm?
  - Note: Nothing should smoke. The paper will feel warm, about as warm as any black thing in the sun. Write 'no smoke in 60 seconds' in the table.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Slowly lift the glass until the circle shrinks to the smallest, brightest dot you can make. 2. Hold it still. 3. Count seconds until you see a thin thread of smoke. 4. Move the lens away at once and write the number. 5. Do it twice more, each time on a fresh part of the paper.  
  *Parent says:* Now I lift the glass slowly. Watch the circle shrink. Keep counting. Tell me the moment you see smoke. Now we try twice more, on fresh paper, because one try can be a fluke.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the circle shrink to a tiny dot. Count the seconds. Shout when you see smoke! Then try two more times.
  - Note: With a 5 cm lens in strong sun, black paper usually smokes in about 2 to 10 seconds, and a small black hole appears. Glance at the dot, don't stare at it, even with sunglasses on. If nothing happens, the dot is probably not as small as it can be. Lift or lower the lens slightly. If it still does not smoke, the sun is too weak today.
  - Check: The big circle only warmed the black paper. The tiny dot made it smoke. What did the glass do? → **It gathered the same light into a much smaller place**
- **s5 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap the black paper for the white paper. 2. Hold the glass at the same height, making the same tiny dot. 3. Count seconds until smoke, and stop at 60. 4. Try 3 times, on fresh spots.  
  *Parent says:* Now the white paper. Same glass, same dot, same sunshine. Only the paper has changed. Will it smoke sooner, later or not at all?  
  *Child mode:* Now the white paper. Same glass. Same tiny dot. Count again. Stop at 60. Did it smoke?
  - Note: White paper usually takes much longer, and often does not smoke at all within 60 seconds. It may turn brown first. The dot on white paper is very dazzling, so look only in short glances. If your white paper has printed words, a black letter will smoke first. That is a good extra result.
- **s6 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour water over both papers. 2. Take the magnifying glass indoors and put it in a drawer. 3. Look at the holes together.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make the paper safe, and put the glass away in a drawer. A lens left lying in the sun can start a fire on its own.  
  *Child mode:* Pour water on the papers. Put the glass away in a drawer. Look at the little holes.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw, and why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: sunlight smokes paper only when it is gathered into a small dot, and black paper gets hot faster than white. What I saw: the big circle never smoked in 60 seconds. The tiny dot made black paper smoke in about 4 seconds. White paper took over 30 seconds, or did not smoke. Why: the lens packed the light into a small place, and black paper absorbs more light than white.
  - Think: Your eye has a lens inside it too. What would it do with sunlight if you looked at the Sun?
### e2 · Ice on Black, Ice on White: which paper takes in more sunlight? (25 min)
- **question:** An ice cube sits on black paper, and another on white paper, in the same sunshine. Which melts first?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which ice cube will melt first, the one on black paper or the one on white paper? Or will they melt at the same time? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two ice cubes from the same tray, two steel plates that are the same, and two sheets of paper the same size. Put both plates side by side in the same sunshine at the same moment. Only the colour of the paper changes.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun. Steel plates in strong sun can get hot, so touch them only at the edge.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both plates at 10 minutes, showing how big each ice cube is and how big each puddle is.
- **results table:** Paper | What the ice looks like after 5 minutes | After 10 minutes | After 15 minutes | Minutes until fully melted
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay one sheet of paper flat on each steel plate. 2. Put both plates side by side in direct sunshine. 3. Take two ice cubes from the freezer. Put one in the middle of each sheet at the same moment.  
  *Parent says:* Two ice cubes, from the same tray. Two plates, side by side, in the same sun. The only difference is the paper. Black under one, white under the other.  
  *Child mode:* Put the black paper and the white paper in the sun, side by side. Put one ice cube on each, at the same time.
  - Note: The paper will get wet, which is fine. On a hot day in direct sun, each cube may melt in about 15 to 30 minutes.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Which ice cube will melt first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which ice will melt first? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why might the colour of the paper matter, if the sunlight is the same? (good: Black things get hotter in the sun.; The black paper might take in more of the light.)
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every 5 minutes, look at both cubes together. 2. Write down how big each cube is and how big its puddle is. 3. Gently touch each sheet near the edge with the back of a finger, and say which feels warmer.  
  *Parent says:* Five minutes. Let's look. Which cube is smaller? Which puddle is bigger? Touch the edge of each paper. Which one is warmer?  
  *Child mode:* Every 5 minutes, look at both ice cubes. Which is smaller? Touch the edge of each paper. Which is warmer?
  - Note: The cube on black paper should shrink faster and sit in a bigger puddle. The dry edge of the black paper will feel warmer. If clouds cover the Sun, both slow down. Keep going until the Sun comes back.
  - Wait: Check every 5 minutes, about 3 times.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write down the minute each cube finishes melting.  
  *Parent says:* Which one melted first? How many minutes did each take?  
  *Child mode:* Which ice melted first? Write the minutes for each.
  - Check: The ice on black paper melted first. Why? → **Black paper absorbs more sunlight, and the light turns into heat**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for three things: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you think now, what you saw, and why it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: black paper takes in more sunlight than white paper. My ice on black melted in 18 minutes and the ice on white in 27 minutes, and the black paper felt warmer. The black paper absorbed the light and it turned into heat, which melted the ice.
  - Think: Why do people in hot places often paint their roofs white?

## Activities
### a1 · Sun Patch, Shade Patch: what does your skin notice? (6 min)
- **safety:** Keep your back or side to the Sun. Never look at it.
- **record:** Draw yourself half in the sunlight and half in the shade. Write one word for how each half felt.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a patch of sunlight on the floor. 2. Stand in it with your back to the window for one minute. 3. Step into the shade next to it for one minute.  
  *Parent says:* Stand in the sunlight with your back to the window. Wait a minute. Now step into the shade. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Stand in the sunlight, with your back to the window. Count to 60. Now step into the shade. What do you feel?
  - Ask: Where did you feel warmer? Which part of you felt it most? (good: In the sunlight, on my back.; On my hair, because it is dark.)
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put your palm flat on the floor, first in the sunny patch, then in the shade.  
  *Parent says:* Now feel the floor in both places. Which part is warmer? The sunlight came through a window, from 150 million kilometres away.  
  *Child mode:* Put your hand on the sunny floor. Then on the shady floor. Which is warmer?
  - Check: The sunny floor is warmer than the shady floor. Where was the warmth made? → **In the floor, when it absorbed the sunlight**
- **s3 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave your child with a question.  
  *Parent says:* The sunlight felt gentle on your back. Could the same sunlight ever be strong enough to burn paper? We will find out.  
  *Child mode:* The sunlight felt gentle. Could it ever burn paper? Keep that question in your head!
  - Think: What could make gentle sunlight strong enough to burn?
### a2 · The Mirror Sun: a safe picture of the Sun on your wall (20 min)
- **safety:** Never look into the mirror at the Sun. Never shine the reflected sunlight into anyone's eyes, or out of the window at people or vehicles. A grown-up helps with the scissors.
- **record:** Draw the square hole and the round spot. Write the spot's width and its distance from the mirror.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape paper over the whole mirror. 2. Cut a small square hole in the middle, about 1 cm wide.  
  *Parent says:* We will make a picture of the Sun that you can look at as long as you like. First we cover the mirror and cut a small square window in the paper.  
  *Child mode:* Tape paper over the mirror. Cut a small square hole in the middle. A grown-up helps with the scissors.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand in the sunshine at the window. 2. Tilt the mirror until a spot of reflected light lands on a shaded wall across the room. 3. Hold the mirror as still as you can, or rest it on the windowsill against a book.  
  *Parent says:* Tilt the mirror slowly. Find the spot of light on the far wall. What shape is it?  
  *Child mode:* Stand in the sun with the mirror. Tilt it slowly. Find the spot of light on the far wall. What shape is it?
  - Note: The spot on a wall about 4 to 6 metres away is round. Close to the mirror, held up on a book, the spot is square, the shape of the hole. Move a sheet of paper slowly from the mirror towards the wall, catching the spot, and watch it change from square to round.
  - Check: The hole in the paper is square, but the spot on the far wall is round. Why? → **The spot is a picture of the round Sun**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure the width of the round spot on the wall with a ruler. 2. Measure the distance from the mirror to the wall with the tape. 3. Write both numbers down in centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure the spot, and how far it is from the mirror. Scientists measure pictures like this to work out how big the Sun looks from Earth.  
  *Child mode:* Measure how wide the round spot is. Then measure from the mirror to the wall. Write both numbers.
  - Note: On a wall 500 cm away, the spot is about 5 to 6 cm wide. Take away the width of the hole, 1 cm, and divide the distance by what is left. You should get roughly 100. That means the Sun is about 100 times further away than it is wide. The real number is about 108.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to look at the spot for as long as they like, and say what they notice.  
  *Parent says:* This spot is the real Sun's light, and it is safe to look at, because it is on a wall. What do you notice? Is it white or yellow?  
  *Child mode:* You can look at this Sun as long as you like. Is it white or yellow? Does it wobble?
  - Note: The spot usually looks white, which is the Sun's real colour. It wobbles if the mirror moves. Large sunspots are too small to see with a 1 cm hole.
  - Think: Why is this picture safe to look at, when the real Sun is not?
### a3 · What Has the Sun Changed? A home hunt (12 min)
- **safety:** Stay inside the home or on the balcony. Never look at the Sun.
- **record:** Make a list with four headings: warmed, dried, faded, grown. Write each thing you find under one heading.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the hunt.  
  *Parent says:* The Sun leaves marks all over a home. Let's hunt for things it has warmed, dried, faded, or helped to grow.  
  *Child mode:* Hunt for things the Sun has changed. Warmed. Dried. Faded. Grown. How many can you find?
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk round the home together. Look at curtains, posters near windows, old newspapers, clothes on the line, a balcony floor, and any plant by a window.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the curtain. Is the side facing the window paler? Feel the balcony floor. Which plants are by the window?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the curtains. Is one side paler? Feel the balcony floor. Find a plant near the window.
  - Ask: Why is the curtain paler on the side that faces the window? (good: The sunlight faded it.; Ultraviolet light in sunlight breaks down the colour.)
  - Ask: Why does the plant by the window need the sunlight? (good: She uses it to make her food.; For photosynthesis.)
  - Note: Common finds: a curtain or cushion faded on one side, a yellowed newspaper, dry clothes, warm floor tiles, a plant leaning towards the window. If you can't find faded things, the next project shows the Sun fading paper over a few days.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the finds under each heading.  
  *Parent says:* Every one of these needed energy. All of that energy came in through the window as sunlight.  
  *Child mode:* Count your finds. Every one needed energy. It all came from the Sun.
  - Check: Kavitha, a girl in Class 3, finds that her poster by the window has gone pale. What did that? → **Sunlight, mostly the ultraviolet light in it**
### a4 · The Sunbeam's Long Trip: act it out (10 min)
- **safety:** Clear a space so nobody trips on the cushions.
- **record:** Draw the trip: a wiggly line inside the Sun and a straight line across space.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pile the cushions close together at one end of the room. This is the inside of the Sun. 2. Put the plant, the dark cloth and the white paper at the other end. This is the Earth.  
  *Parent says:* These cushions are the packed insides of the Sun. Over there is the Earth. You are a bit of energy, just made in the Sun's core.  
  *Child mode:* The cushions are the inside of the Sun. Over there is the Earth. You are a bit of energy from the core!
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child starts in the middle of the cushions. Each time they touch a cushion, they must turn and go a different way. They zigzag until they get out of the pile.  
  *Parent says:* Inside the Sun, you keep getting taken in and sent out again, in a new direction. Bump, turn, bump, turn. It takes many thousands of years to get out.  
  *Child mode:* Start in the middle. Touch a cushion, then turn a new way. Keep zigzagging until you get out!
  - Note: This shows why energy takes a very long time to leave the Sun. Scientists estimate between about 10,000 and 170,000 years.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Once out, your child walks straight across the room to the Earth while everyone counts slowly to 8.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are sunlight in empty space. Nothing gets in your way. Walk straight to the Earth while we count 8 slow minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Now walk straight to the Earth. We count to 8, for 8 minutes!
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child chooses where to land: on the plant, the dark cloth or the white paper. They act out what happens next.  
  *Parent says:* Where will you land? On the plant, you become her food. On the dark cloth, you turn into heat. On the white paper, you bounce off again.  
  *Child mode:* Where will you land? The plant? The dark cloth? The white paper? Show what happens!
  - Check: The sunbeam lands on the white paper. What happens to most of it? → **It is reflected and bounces away**

## Projects
### p1 · The Sun Print: a picture made only by sunlight (30 min, 6 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up handles the glass and checks it has no sharp edges. Put the print where it cannot fall.
- **record:** Draw the print on day 1 and on day 6. Write which parts changed and which did not.
- **schedule:** day 1: Lay the objects on the paper, cover with glass, and put it in the sun. Draw what it looks like. (10 min); day 3: Look without moving anything. Is the paper around the objects changing colour? (5 min); day 6: Lift off the glass and the objects. Look at the shapes left behind. Explain what made them. (15 min)
- **s1 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the paper on a flat piece of cardboard. 2. Lay the objects on it, spaced apart. 3. Cover with the glass or clear plastic. 4. Put it in a spot that gets direct sun for several hours.  
  *Parent says:* We will let the Sun draw a picture for us. The objects will block the sunlight. Let's see what the uncovered paper looks like in a few days.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the key, coin and leaf on the paper. Cover them with the glass. Put it all in the sun.
  - Ask: What do you think will happen to the paper that is in the sun? (good: It will fade.; It will change colour.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, look at the print without moving anything.  
  *Parent says:* Don't lift anything yet. Can you see the paper changing colour around the objects?  
  *Child mode:* Look, but don't touch! Is the paper changing colour?
  - Note: Kite paper and tissue fade fastest. Newspaper turns slightly yellow. If nothing has changed by day 3, leave it longer, or move it to a sunnier spot.
  - Wait: Leave it in the sun until day 3.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 6, lift off the glass and the objects. Look at the shapes.  
  *Parent says:* Now lift everything off. What did the Sun draw? Why are the shapes a different colour from the rest?  
  *Child mode:* Lift off the glass. Lift off the key and coin. What did the Sun draw?
  - Check: The paper under the key kept its colour. The paper around it faded. Why? → **The key blocked the sunlight, so no ultraviolet light reached that paper**
  - Wait: Leave it in the sun until day 6.

## Challenges
### c1 · Melt the Chocolate with Only Sunlight (60 min)

### Sun Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What kind of thing is the Sun?  
   Options: A planet / A star / A ball of fire / A moon  
   **Answer:** A star. *Why:* The Sun is a star, a huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. It is the nearest star to the Earth. Picking 'a ball of fire' is very common, but the Sun does not burn. It shines by fusion. A strong answer says it is the nearest star.
2. (truefalse) Priya, a girl in Class 3, says, 'The Sun is a giant fire, burning in the sky.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A fire needs oxygen from the air, and there is no air in space. The Sun shines because hydrogen joins into helium in its core. A strong answer gives this reason.
3. (fill) A thing that takes in light, so that the light turns into heat, is said to ____ the light.  
   **Answer:** ['absorb']. *Why:* Absorb means take in. Black things absorb most of the light that falls on them, so they get hot in the sun.
4. (mcq) Remember the Sun Dot experiment, where a grown-up held a magnifying glass over black paper in the sun. When did the paper smoke?  
   Options: When the glass made a big circle of light / When the glass made a tiny dot of light / Never / Only in the shade  
   **Answer:** When the glass made a tiny dot of light. *Why:* The tiny dot had all the light from the whole glass packed into a small place, so it got hot enough to smoke. The big circle spread the light out, so it only warmed the paper. A strong answer says the lens gathered the light.
5. (write) In the Sun Dot experiment, the glass made no new light, but the tiny dot made paper smoke. Explain how.  
   **Answer:** The magnifying glass gathered all the sunlight that fell on it into one tiny dot. The same amount of light was packed into a much smaller place, so that spot of paper got very hot.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea of gathering or focusing, and says the amount of light stayed the same but the place got smaller.

8. (mcq) In the Sun Dot experiment, why was the paper put on a steel plate, with a tumbler of water nearby?  
   Options: To make it smoke faster / To keep it safe, so nothing else could catch fire / To make the dot smaller / To keep the paper flat  
   **Answer:** To keep it safe, so nothing else could catch fire. *Why:* Steel does not burn, and the water can put out any smouldering paper. The steel plate and the water are there for safety. A grown-up holds the glass for the same reason.
9. (mcq) Why can looking at the Sun harm your eyes without any pain?  
   Options: The retina has no pain nerves / The Sun's light is cold / The eye has no lens / It only hurts at night  
   **Answer:** The retina has no pain nerves. *Why:* The eye's lens focuses the Sun's light onto the retina, which has no pain nerves. So the harm happens without warning, and may show only later. That is why we never look at the Sun.
10. (mcq) Which of these is a safe way to watch the Sun?  
   Options: Through dark sunglasses / Its picture on a wall, made by a covered mirror / Through binoculars / A quick look for one second  
   **Answer:** Its picture on a wall, made by a covered mirror. *Why:* A picture of the Sun on a wall is safe, because you look at the wall, not the Sun. Sunglasses let through too much light, binoculars gather more of it into the eye, and even one second can harm the retina.
11. (mcq) Why does a garden lizard lie on a sunny wall in the cool early morning?  
   Options: To hide from birds / To warm her body, so she can move fast and hunt / To drink the sunlight / To sleep  
   **Answer:** To warm her body, so she can move fast and hunt. *Why:* A lizard makes little heat inside her own body. On a cool morning she is slow. Basking lets her absorb sunlight and warm up, so she can chase insects.
12. (mcq) In 1920, Arthur Eddington suggested how the Sun shines. What new measurement helped him?  
   Options: That a helium atom weighs a little less than four hydrogen atoms / That the Sun is shrinking / That the Sun is full of coal / That sunlight takes 8 minutes to arrive  
   **Answer:** That a helium atom weighs a little less than four hydrogen atoms. *Why:* Francis Aston measured that a helium atom weighs a little less than four hydrogen atoms. Eddington saw that if the Sun joins hydrogen into helium, the missing material could become the Sun's energy.

## Applications
- Solar panels on roofs and in fields turn sunlight into electricity.
- Solar water heaters on rooftops heat water with black pipes that absorb sunlight.
- White roof paint keeps homes cooler by reflecting sunlight.
- Farmers and families dry grain, chillies, papads and vadams in the sun.
- Salt is made by letting the Sun evaporate seawater in shallow pans.
- Sun cookers cook food with sunlight and no fuel.
- Solar eclipse viewers and pinhole projectors let people study the Sun safely.
- Space observatories such as India's Aditya-L1 watch the Sun to warn of solar storms that can harm satellites.
- Sunscreen and hats protect skin from ultraviolet light.

## Open questions
- **Why is the Sun's outer layer, the corona, far hotter than its surface?** The surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius, but the thin corona above it is over a million degrees. Scientists have several ideas, involving the Sun's magnetism, and spacecraft such as Aditya-L1 and NASA's Parker Solar Probe are testing them.
- **Exactly how long does the Sun's energy take to get from the core to the surface?** Nobody can see inside the Sun. Models give answers from about 10,000 to about 170,000 years.
- **Can we forecast the Sun's storms days ahead, like rain?** Scientists can now give some warning of big solar storms, but they cannot yet say reliably which dark spot will erupt, or when.
- **Where are the Sun's brothers and sisters?** The Sun was probably born in a crowd of stars from the same cloud of gas, about 4.6 billion years ago. Those stars have since drifted apart, and astronomers are still trying to find them.
- **Could we ever make fusion work on Earth, to make electricity like the Sun does?** Scientists in many countries, including India, are building fusion machines. Some have made fusion happen for short times, but none yet gives out more useful energy than it takes to run.

## Connected maths
- In the Sun Dot experiment, the tiny dot made black paper smoke in 4 seconds. On white paper, the same dot took 40 seconds. How many times as long did the white paper take? **10 times as long.**. The white paper took 40 seconds and the black paper took 4 seconds. 40 divided by 4 is 10. So the white paper took 10 times as long. Check: 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 40. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the Mirror Sun, Lakshmi's round spot of light was 5 cm wide. The wall was 500 cm from the mirror. 500 divided by 5 is 100. What does that tell her about the real Sun? **The Sun is about 100 times further away than it is wide.**. The picture of the Sun and the real Sun are in the same proportion. The picture is 100 times further from the mirror than it is wide. So the Sun is about 100 times further from us than it is wide. Scientists measure about 108, so her answer is close. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach the Earth. How many seconds is that? **500 seconds.**. 1 minute is 60 seconds. 8 minutes is 8 × 60 = 480 seconds. Add the 20 extra seconds: 480 + 20 = 500 seconds. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- About 109 Earths fit in a line across the Sun's face. If each Earth were a 1 cm bead, how long would the line of beads be? Is that more or less than a metre? **109 cm, which is a little more than a metre.**. Each bead is 1 cm. 109 beads in a line make 109 cm. A metre is 100 cm. 109 is 9 more than 100, so the line is 9 cm longer than a metre. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- In the ice test, Revathi's ice on black paper melted in 18 minutes. Her ice on white paper melted in 27 minutes. Then she tried grey paper. Predict how long the ice on grey paper might take, and explain your guess. **Somewhere between 18 and 27 minutes, perhaps about 22 minutes.**. Grey is between black and white. It should absorb more sunlight than white but less than black. So its ice should melt faster than on white but slower than on black: between 18 and 27 minutes. Halfway is 22 and a half minutes, but the real time depends on how dark the grey is. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
