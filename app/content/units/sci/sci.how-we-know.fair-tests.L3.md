# Fair tests: change one thing and keep the rest the same
`sci.how-we-know.fair-tests.L3`

**Summary.** A slice of apple left on a plate turns brown. Does lemon juice stop it? You can only find out if every slice gets the same apple, the same air and the same wait, and only the lemon is different. That is a fair test: change one thing, keep everything else the same, and watch what happens. You will also learn to write results in a table, and why one try is never enough.

**Intro.** *Lakshmi drops her rubber ball from above her head. Her little brother drops his plastic ball from his knee. Her ball bounces much higher. "My ball is bouncier!" she says. Is she right?* We can't tell yet. Two things were different: the ball and the height it fell from. Either one could have made the higher bounce. To find out about the balls, they must fall from the same height onto the same floor. Then the ball is the only thing left that is different.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a fair test is: change only one thing, and keep everything else the same.
1. I can spot what makes a test unfair, and say how to make it fair.
2. I can plan a fair test: what I will change, what I will keep the same, and what I will watch or measure.
3. I can write results in a table with clear headings, and read results from a table.
4. I can say why we repeat a test, and what to do with a result that doesn't fit.
5. I can use my results as evidence: say what I found, what I saw, and why I think it happened.
6. I can tell how James Lind's fair test on sailors in 1747 showed which cure for scurvy worked.

## Terms
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then any difference must come from that one thing. *e.g.* Two balls dropped from the same height onto the same floor. Only the ball is different.
- **Variable**: Anything in a test that could change, such as the height, the floor, the ball or the time. *e.g.* In a bounce test, the height of the drop is a variable. So is the kind of floor.
- **The thing we change**: The one variable you choose to change on purpose, to see what it does. *e.g.* In the apple test, the thing we change is what we put on each slice.
- **The things we keep the same**: All the other variables. You keep them the same so they cannot make a difference. *e.g.* Same apple, same size slices, same plate, same place, same waiting time.
- **Control**: The part of a test where you add nothing or change nothing. You compare everything else with it. *e.g.* The apple slice with nothing on it is the control. It shows what happens without lemon.
- **Prediction**: Your guess about what will happen, with a reason, made before the test. *e.g.* "I think the lemon slice will stay white, because Amma puts lemon on cut fruit."
- **Observation**: Something you notice with your senses while you test, such as a colour, a sound or a smell. *e.g.* "The plain slice has gone light brown at the edges."
- **Measure**: To find out how much of something there is, using a tool and a unit, such as centimetres or seconds. *e.g.* The ball bounced 45 centimetres high, measured against a tape stuck on the wall.
- **Results**: What actually happened in the test, written down as words or numbers. *e.g.* Plain slice: dark brown after 1 hour. Lemon slice: still white.
- **Table**: A way of writing results in rows and columns, so you can find and compare them quickly. *e.g.* One row for each apple slice. One column for each time you checked.
- **Column and row**: A column goes down the page. A row goes across. Each column has a heading that says what is in it. *e.g.* The column headed 'After 30 minutes' shows every slice's colour at 30 minutes.
- **Repeat**: To do the same test again, in the same way, to see if you get the same result. *e.g.* Throw the paper ball 10 times, not once, before you decide which throw is better.
- **Fluke**: A result that happened by chance once and may not happen again. *e.g.* One wild throw that goes straight in the bucket is a fluke if the next nine miss.
- **Evidence**: What you saw or measured that shows whether an idea is right. *e.g.* The white lemon slice next to the brown plain slice is evidence that lemon slows browning.
- **Conclusion**: What you decide from your evidence at the end of a test. *e.g.* "Lemon juice keeps a cut apple white for much longer than plain water."

## Explanations
### What a fair test is · *defined*
**Definition:** A fair test changes only one thing and keeps everything else the same. If the results are different, that one thing must have made the difference.

Many things can change the result of a test. A ball bounces higher if it falls from higher up, or lands on a harder floor, or is a bouncier ball. If you change the ball and the height together, you cannot tell which one made the bounce higher. So you choose one thing to change. You keep every other thing the same. Now only one thing is different, and it is the only thing that can explain a different result.
- e.g. To test which ball is bouncier, drop both from the same height onto the same floor.
- e.g. To test which cloth dries fastest, use pieces of the same size, wet them with the same amount of water, and hang them in the same place.
- e.g. To test whether lemon keeps apple white, cut all the slices from one apple and check them all at the same time.
### Why changing two things at once spoils a test · *defined*
**Definition:** When two things change at once, you cannot tell which one caused the result. A test can answer one question at a time.

Meenakshi puts sugar in two tumblers of water. One tumbler has warm water and she stirs it. The other has cold water and she does not stir it. The sugar in the warm, stirred tumbler disappears first. Was it the warm water or the stirring? She cannot know, because she changed both. To find out, she does two fair tests. First, warm and cold water, both stirred. Then stirred and not stirred, both cold.
- e.g. A plant on the balcony grows better than one inside. But it also got more water. Was it the light or the water?
- e.g. Divya's new chappals feel faster in a race. But she also ran on a smoother road that day.
### The control: what happens when you change nothing · *defined*
**Definition:** A control is the part of a test where you add nothing new. It shows what would happen anyway, so you can see what your change really did.

Suppose you put lemon juice on an apple slice and it stays white. Did the lemon do that? Maybe this kind of apple stays white anyway. You can only know by watching a slice from the same apple with nothing on it. If the plain slice goes brown and the lemon slice stays white, the lemon made the difference. Without the control, a result means very little.
- e.g. In the apple test, the slice with nothing on it is the control.
- e.g. Doctors testing a new medicine give it to one group of people and not to a second group, so they can compare.
- e.g. In a seed test, one pot of seeds gets watered the usual way, and the others get something new.
### Writing results in a table · *defined*
**Definition:** A results table has rows and columns. Each column has a heading that says what it holds, with the unit, such as 'Height (cm)'. Each row is one thing you tested.

When you test, you see many things quickly, and memory forgets. A table keeps every result in its place. Draw it before you start, so you are ready. Put the thing you change in the first column. Put what you measure or see in the next columns. Write the units in the headings, not after every number. Later, you can read across a row to see one thing, or down a column to compare all of them. Florence Nightingale, a nurse in the Crimean War (1854 to 1856), kept careful tables of the soldiers who died in army hospitals. They showed that many more soldiers died of diseases than of their wounds. Her records helped make hospitals cleaner.
- e.g. Apple test: one row for each slice, one column for each time you look.
- e.g. Bounce test: one row for each ball, three columns for three drops.
- e.g. A school's attendance register is a table: one row for each child, one column for each day.
### Why we repeat a test · *defined*
**Definition:** Doing a test only once can mislead you, because one try can be a fluke. Repeating it in the same way shows whether the result is real.

Throw a paper ball at a bucket once and it may go in by luck. Throw it ten times and you learn how good the throw really is. Small things change from try to try: how your hand moves, a breath of wind, where the ball lands. Repeating a test lets these small changes even out. If one result is very different from the others, look for a reason. Maybe the ball hit the edge of the table. Write it down, try again, and say what happened.
- e.g. Drop each ball three times and look at all three heights.
- e.g. Count how many of 10 throws go in, not just the first one.
- e.g. Scientists in different countries repeat each other's tests to check the results.
### Why a cut apple turns brown, and what lemon does · *defined*
**Definition:** When an apple is cut, the broken cells let some of their substances meet the oxygen in the air. A helper substance in the apple, called an enzyme, joins them together into new brown substances.

An apple's skin keeps germs and insects out. A cut opens a way in. Scientists think the brown layer helps the plant here: the brown substances can harm germs and insects at a wound. The browning needs oxygen from the air and a working enzyme. Lemon juice is sour, which means it is an acid. The acid slows the enzyme down. Lemon juice also has vitamin C, which stops the brown substances forming for a while. Salt water slows the enzyme a little too. A slice kept under water gets much less air, so it browns more slowly while it stays under.
- e.g. A cut banana, potato or brinjal also turns brown in the air.
- e.g. Cooks in many Indian kitchens put cut brinjal or potato into a bowl of water until it is time to cook.
- e.g. Lemon juice is squeezed over cut fruit in a fruit chaat to keep it looking fresh.
### Can every question be answered with a fair test? · *debated*
A fair test needs you to change one thing yourself. Sometimes that is impossible, or unkind. Nobody can change the Sun to see what happens. Nobody should make people ill to test a cure. Scientists have other careful ways to find out. They compare many observations, or they wait for nature to make the change for them. People still argue about how sure these other ways can make us.
- e.g. Astronomers cannot change a star, so they compare thousands of stars of different sizes.
- e.g. To learn whether a medicine works, doctors give it to people who are already ill, and compare them with a similar group who get the usual care.
- **Many scientists who do experiments:** A fair test is the surest way to know what causes what. If you can change one thing yourself, do it, because only then do you know nothing else changed.
- **Many astronomers, geologists and doctors who study whole groups of people:** Many of the biggest questions, about stars, the Earth's past or our health, cannot be tested by changing one thing. Careful comparisons of many cases can still give strong evidence.

## Mostly agreed
- To find out what one thing does, change only that thing and keep everything else the same. *How we know:* When tests are done this way, other people who repeat them in the same way get the same results.
- A test needs a control to compare with, such as a slice with nothing on it. *How we know:* Without a control, people often believed things worked when the result would have happened anyway.
- One try can be a fluke, so tests are repeated. *How we know:* Ten throws of a paper ball give a steadier answer than one. Scientists repeat each other's tests for the same reason.
- Results should be written down at the time, in a clear table. *How we know:* Florence Nightingale's tables of hospital deaths showed what nobody had seen from memory alone.
- Lemon juice slows the browning of a cut apple. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with slices from one apple, and the lemon slice stays paler for much longer.

## How we found out
**James Lind, a Scottish ship's doctor in the British navy, 20 May 1747 (the test on HMS Salisbury), 1753 (his book A Treatise of the Scurvy).** For hundreds of years, sailors on long voyages fell ill with scurvy. Their gums bled, their teeth came loose, and many died. Doctors had many ideas about the cure. A popular one in the British navy was 'elixir of vitriol', a weak acid. On 20 May 1747, James Lind, a doctor on the ship HMS Salisbury, did something new. He took twelve sailors with scurvy who were as alike as he could find. They slept in the same part of the ship and ate the same food. He split them into six pairs and gave each pair a different treatment: cider, elixir of vitriol, vinegar, sea water, a paste of garlic, mustard and other spices, or two oranges and a lemon each day. After six days, one sailor from the oranges and lemons pair was fit for work again, and the other was much better. The pair with cider improved a little. The acid, the vinegar and the sea water did not help. Lind published his results in 1753. Even so, it took the navy until 1795 to give sailors lemon juice. We now know that scurvy comes from too little vitamin C.  
*What people believed before:* Many doctors thought acids such as elixir of vitriol or vinegar cured scurvy. Lind's fair test, with the same food and quarters for every pair, showed that these did not help, while oranges and lemons did.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If one thing happens after another, the first thing caused it.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Often it is true. You switch on the fan, and then the air moves.  
  Against: Many things happen after each other by chance. The rain came after the cricket match started, but the match did not cause the rain. Only a fair test can show which thing caused which.
- **One good try is enough to know the answer.** (Many people in a hurry; *rejected*)  
  For: Sometimes the difference is so big that one try shows it clearly.  
  Against: Small differences can come from luck. One throw in the bucket does not make you a good thrower. Repeating shows whether the result stays the same.
- **A test that agrees with my guess must be a good test.** (All of us, without noticing; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels good when the test matches what we thought.  
  Against: A test is good because it is fair, not because it agrees with us. People often stop checking when they get the answer they wanted. Good scientists try hardest to prove their own guess wrong.
- **Every question in science can be answered by a fair test.** (Some people who love experiments; *debated*)  
  For: Fair tests are the clearest way we have to find out what causes what.  
  Against: Some things cannot be changed on purpose, like a star or the Earth's past. Some tests would hurt people or animals. Scientists then compare many careful observations instead.

## Roots
We have run fair tests, kept a control and written our results in tables. Now we ask what fair tests tell us about how we know anything.

**Deep truth.** Your lemon slice stayed white, but on its own that proved nothing. It meant something only beside the plain slice, which browned in the same air. A cause can be seen only by comparing. To know what one thing does, you must also watch what happens without it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think we can see a cause just by watching it work: lemon goes on, the slice stays white. But the white slice alone could not tell us whether it would have stayed white anyway. Only the comparison with the plain slice showed what the lemon did.  
*Evidence:* In the Brown Apple Test, the plain slice and the lemon slice came from one apple, sat on one plate and waited the same time. Only the lemon was different, and only by putting the two side by side could you see what the lemon did.

- *who am i:* You know you have grown only by comparing: a pencil mark on the wall from last year.
- *what is life:* A cut apple browns because the plant's cells answer the cut. Living things are always answering what happens to them.
- *what is this world:* Air, warmth and the cut were acting on every slice at once. In the world, many causes always act together.

On the plate, air, warmth, the cut and the apple's own substances all act together on each slice. Nature does them all at once, as one happening. In a fair test, we hold all but one of them the same, so that one can be noticed. The many causes we name are parts we pick out of one whole happening. Science studies the parts, and the parts still belong to one whole.

- **Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65:** The Buddha told the Kalama people not to accept a teaching only because it is old, often repeated, written in a book, or said by a respected teacher. When you know for yourselves that something is true and good, then accept it. (The navy's doctors trusted acids to cure scurvy because respected people said so. James Lind tested the cures side by side and saw for himself which one worked.)
- **Nyaya Sutra 1.1.3:** The Nyaya school names four ways of knowing: perception (seeing for yourself), inference (working out from what you saw), comparison, and the word of a trustworthy person. (A fair test uses the first three together. You perceive the white and brown slices, you compare them, and you infer that the lemon made the difference.)
- Science (settled): Doctors test new medicines in controlled trials: one group gets the medicine, a similar group does not, and chance decides who goes where.
- Science (settled): Some things, like stars or the Earth's past, cannot be changed on purpose. Scientists compare many careful observations instead.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: The Charvakas said that only what we perceive directly is sure knowledge. Inference can always be wrong, because we can never check every case. You saw a white slice and a brown slice. Saying 'the lemon caused it' is an inference, and a new apple might behave differently.
- Practice, One thing different (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Listen to all the sounds around us for a few breaths. Now I will change just one thing. I will open the window, or switch off the fan. Keep listening. What is different now? Only one thing changed, so you can hear exactly what it did. Open your eyes slowly.
- Ask: If you can only see a cause by comparing, can you ever see the cause of something that has happened only once?
- Ask: Can anything in the world be kept exactly the same, even for a minute?
- Ask: When you learn something about yourself, what are you comparing it with?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your plain apple slice went brown with nothing on it. So what was making it brown? (hint: Think about what every slice shared: air, warmth, the cut.)
- *deep:* Can you ever keep everything else exactly the same? (hint: Even two slices of one apple are not quite the same. How close is close enough?)
- *deep:* How many times must you repeat a test before you are sure? (hint: Think about the paper ball throws. When did the tally stop surprising you?)
- *big:* You can't test the Sun, or the dinosaurs. How do we know anything about them? (hint: Think about comparing many things that nature has already changed for us.)
- *big:* Is it fair to test a medicine on people? What would make it fair to them? (hint: Think about asking them first, and about what the people who don't get the medicine receive.)
- *big:* When two friends disagree, could a fair test settle it? What kinds of arguments could it never settle? (hint: Think about 'which ball bounces higher' and 'which song is nicer'.)

## Story: Kavya's Boat Race
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: where a boat starts in a stream of water can matter more than the boat.*

It had rained all night in Kochi. In the morning the rain stopped, and water was still running off the terrace of the flats. It ran in a wide, shallow stream along a groove in the floor and poured out of a spout at the far end.

Kavya was nine. Her brother Arun was seven. Each of them had made a paper boat from an old newspaper.

"Race!" said Arun. He put his boat down in the middle of the stream. Kavya put hers down near the edge. They let go together.

Arun's boat shot away. It reached the spout long before Kavya's, which turned slowly in circles near the wall.

"My boat is the fastest boat in the building," said Arun. "Because I folded it tighter."

Kavya picked up her boat and looked at it. It was not soggy. It was not torn. Then she looked at the stream. In the middle, the water ran quickly and smoothly. Near the edge, it moved slowly and swirled against the wall.

"Your boat might be faster," she said. "Or you might just have the faster water. We changed two things. The boat, and where it started."

Arun frowned. "So how do we know?"

"We race again," said Kavya. "But this time we swap. You start at the edge, and I start in the middle."

They swapped. This time Kavya's boat raced down the middle and reached the spout first. Arun's boat bumped along the wall.

"That's not fair," said Arun. "You had the fast water."

"Yes," said Kavya. "That's the point. Whoever has the fast water wins. So the race was not testing the boats at all."

Their neighbour, Mrs Thomas, was hanging out her washing. She had been a science teacher before she retired. "If you want to test the boats," she said, "you have to make everything else the same. Same water, same start, same push."

"But there's only one middle," said Arun. "We can't both start there."

"Then take turns," said Kavya. "One boat at a time, from the same spot. And we time each one."

Kavya ran inside and came back with her mother's phone, a piece of chalk and her notebook. She drew a chalk line across the middle of the groove. That would be the start. The spout was the finish.

"What else could be different?" asked Mrs Thomas.

Kavya thought. "How we let go. If Arun pushes his boat, it will start faster." So they agreed: no pushing. Hold the boat on the line, and open your fingers.

"And the water," said Arun. "It's getting slower. The rain stopped." He was right. The stream was thinner than before.

"Then we take turns," said Kavya. "Your boat, my boat, your boat, my boat. That way neither of us always gets the slower water."

In her notebook, Kavya drew a table. It had three columns: Boat, Try, and Seconds to the spout.

Arun went first. Kavya counted on the phone timer. Eleven seconds. Then Kavya's boat. Twelve seconds. Then Arun's again. Twelve. Kavya's. Eleven. Arun's. Thirteen. Kavya's. Thirteen.

They looked at the table. Arun's boat took 11, 12 and 13 seconds. Kavya's boat took 12, 11 and 13 seconds.

"They're the same," said Arun slowly.

"Almost exactly the same," said Kavya. "Both boats took 36 seconds altogether. Your boat isn't faster. It was the water."

Arun was quiet for a moment. Then he grinned. "So in the first race, the water won."

"The water won," agreed Mrs Thomas, laughing. "That happens to scientists too. They think something they made is working, and then they find it was something else all along."

Arun looked at the stream, which was nearly a trickle now. "Next time it rains," he said, "I'm going to make a boat with a sail. And we'll test it fairly. One thing different. Just the sail."

Kavya wrote at the bottom of the table: Conclusion. The middle of the stream is faster than the edge. The two boats are about the same.

Then she added one more line: Next test, the sail.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the first race changed two things at once, the boat and the starting place. The fair test changed only the boat, used the same start and the same way of letting go, took turns so neither boat always got the slower water, and repeated each run three times. The table then showed the boats were about the same.

- Ask: Why did Arun's boat win the first race?
- Ask: What two things were different in the first race?
- Ask: What did Kavya and Arun keep the same in the fair test?
- Ask: Why did they take turns instead of doing all of Arun's runs first?
- Ask: Why did they race each boat three times, not once?

  *Parent says:* Now you are the expert. What must every good results table have?  
  *Child mode:* What must every good table have?
  - Ask: What must a good results table have? (good: Headings with units; A row for every thing tested; No empty boxes; The same number of tries for each)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Brown Apple Test: what keeps a cut apple white? (35 min)
- **question:** Which of these keeps a cut apple slice white longest: nothing, plain water, salt water or lemon juice?
- **predict:** Look at the four bowls: nothing, water, salt water, lemon juice. Which slice do you think will stay white longest, and which will go brown first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The thing we change: what we put on each slice. The things we keep the same: the same apple, slices of the same thickness, all cut at the same time, each dipped for one minute, all on one plate in one place, all checked at the same times. The slice with nothing on it is the control. What we watch: the colour of each slice, scored 0 to 3.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the apple. Lemon juice stings in the eyes and in small cuts, so wash your hands after. You can eat the slices afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Use this colour score: 0 means white, 1 means a little cream, 2 means light brown, 3 means dark brown. At the end, colour four small squares to match the four slices.
- **results table:** Slice | What we put on it | Colour at 10 minutes (0 to 3) | Colour at 30 minutes (0 to 3) | Colour at 1 hour (0 to 3)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set out the four bowls and label them: nothing, water, salt water, lemon. 2. Help your child draw the results table with a ruler.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to test what keeps a cut apple white. First we draw our table, so we are ready to write things down.  
  *Child mode:* We will test what keeps apple white. First draw the table. Use a ruler.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a prediction and a reason. Then ask what must stay the same.  
  *Parent says:* Which slice do you think will stay white longest? Why? And what must we keep the same, so the test is fair?  
  *Child mode:* Which slice will stay white longest? Why? What must stay the same?
  - Ask: What must we keep the same? (good: Use the same apple.; Make the slices the same size.; Cut them all at the same time.; Put them all on one plate.)
  - Note: Write the prediction at the top of the table. Do not say what will happen. If your child misses something, ask: 'What if one slice came from a different apple?'
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut four slices of the same thickness from one apple, quickly. 2. Put one slice in each bowl at the same moment. 3. After one minute, lift them out and lay them on the plate beside their labels, cut side up.  
  *Parent says:* I'm cutting all four slices from one apple, the same thickness. Each one goes in its bowl for one minute. The control goes in the empty bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the grown-up cut four slices. Each slice goes in a bowl. Wait one minute.
  - Note: Cut quickly, so no slice waits in the air much longer than the others. Thin slices, about half a centimetre, show the colour best. Turn the slices so all the cut faces point up.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start the timer. At 10 minutes, look at each slice and give it a colour score from 0 to 3. Write the scores in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Ten minutes. Look closely at each slice. Is it white, a little cream, light brown or dark brown?  
  *Child mode:* Ten minutes! Look at each slice. Give it a score from 0 to 3.
  - Note: The control slice usually shows some browning within 10 to 20 minutes, starting at the edges. Some apples brown slowly. If nothing has changed yet, play Fair or Unfair? and look again at 30 minutes.
  - Wait: Wait 10 minutes after the slices go on the plate.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At 30 minutes, score each slice again and fill in the next column. If you can, check once more at 1 hour.  
  *Parent says:* Thirty minutes. Score each slice again. Has anything changed since last time?  
  *Child mode:* Thirty minutes. Score each slice again. What has changed?
  - Check: Why did we keep one slice with nothing on it? → **To see what happens to a slice with nothing added, so we can compare**
  - Wait: Wait until 30 minutes after the slices went on the plate.
- **s6 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write a claim, the evidence and a reason. Then read the card on why apples turn brown together.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find out? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? What did you see? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Which slice stayed whitest, and how do you know? (good: The lemon slice. It scored 0 or 1 when the plain slice scored 2 or 3.; The lemon one, because next to the plain one it was much whiter.)
  - Ask: Was your prediction right? Does it matter if it wasn't? (good: It doesn't matter. The test tells us what really happens.; A wrong guess still helps, because now we know more.)
  - Note: Usually the lemon slice stays white or nearly white for an hour or more. The plain slice and the water slice brown the most. The salt water slice often browns less than plain. Any honest result is a good result. If two slices look alike, that is a finding too.
### e2 · Ten Throws: underarm or overarm? (20 min)
- **question:** From two big steps away, do more paper balls go into a bucket when you throw underarm or overarm?
- **predict:** Which throw do you think will get more balls into the bucket, underarm or overarm? Why do you think so? Would one throw of each be enough to know?
- **fair test:** The thing we change: underarm or overarm. The things we keep the same: the same paper ball, the same bucket, the same thrower, the same chalk or tape line, the same number of throws. What we count: how many of 10 throws go in.
- **safety:** Throw away from windows, lamps and people. A paper ball is soft and safe indoors.
- **record:** Make a tally in the table for every ball that goes in. Then draw two bars, one for underarm and one for overarm.
- **results table:** Throw | Tally of balls in | Number in, out of 10
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the bucket against a wall. 2. Take two big steps back and mark a line. 3. Draw the table.  
  *Parent says:* Everyone throws from this line, every time. Why do you think the line matters?  
  *Child mode:* Throw from this line every time. Why does the line matter?
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a prediction and a reason. Then let your child throw once underarm and once overarm.  
  *Parent says:* Which throw will get more in? Why? Now one throw each. Does that settle it?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which throw is better. Why? Now one throw each. Are you sure yet?
  - Note: After one throw each, many children think they know. Ask: 'Could that have been luck?'
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Throw 10 underarm, then 10 overarm. After each throw, make a tally mark if it went in.  
  *Parent says:* Ten each. Same line, same ball. I'll fetch the ball. You keep the tally.  
  *Child mode:* Ten throws underarm. Ten throws overarm. Tally each one that goes in.
  - Check: Why do we throw 10 times each instead of once? → **Because one throw can go in or miss by luck**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the tallies, write the numbers and compare.  
  *Parent says:* Which throw got more in? Was the first throw a good guide?  
  *Child mode:* Count the tallies. Which throw won? Did the first throw tell the truth?
  - Ask: What would you change to make the test even better? (good: Throw 20 times each.; Take turns, underarm then overarm, so tiredness is the same for both.; Get someone else to throw too.)
  - Note: Results differ from child to child. Many children land more underarm from close up, but not all. A child who notices that tiredness or practice could change later throws has spotted a real problem: taking turns fixes it.
### e3 · The Brinjal Bowl: why do cooks keep cut brinjal in water? (25 min)
- **question:** Does keeping cut brinjal under water stop it turning brown?
- **predict:** Which brinjal piece do you think will stay pale longest: the one on a plate, the one under water, or the one under salt water? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The thing we change: where each piece waits: on a plate, under plain water, or under salt water. The things we keep the same: one brinjal, pieces the same size, all cut at the same time, all checked at the same time. A brinjal piece floats, so each piece in water is held under with a small katori, or the top would be in the air. The piece on the plate is the control.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the brinjal. Do not eat raw brinjal pieces. Cook them afterwards.
- **record:** Write a word for each colour: pale, a little brown, brown, dark brown. Draw the three pieces at 20 minutes.
- **results table:** Piece | Where it waited | Colour at 5 minutes | Colour at 20 minutes
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the table. 2. Ask for a prediction and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Amma puts cut brinjal in water. Do you think the water really helps? Why? Which piece will stay pale longest?  
  *Child mode:* Cooks put cut brinjal in water. Does it help? Why do you think so?
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut three slices of the same size from the middle of the brinjal. 2. Put one on the plate. 3. Put one in each bowl and press it under with a katori.  
  *Parent says:* Look, the brinjal floats. If it floats, its top is in the air. Is that fair? So I hold each one under.  
  *Child mode:* The brinjal floats! The top is in the air. So hold it under with a katori.
  - Note: Brinjal is full of tiny air spaces, so it floats. A floating piece would brown on top, and the test would be unfair to the water. Holding it under is the fix.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 5 minutes, look at the plate piece. Lift the others out quickly, look, and put them back under.  
  *Parent says:* Five minutes. What colour is each piece? Write one word for each.  
  *Child mode:* Five minutes. Look at each piece. Write one word for each colour.
  - Wait: Wait 5 minutes.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At 20 minutes, take all three out and put them side by side. Compare and talk.  
  *Parent says:* Put them side by side. What do you see? Were the cooks right?  
  *Child mode:* Put all three side by side. Were the cooks right?
  - Ask: What did the water do, and how do you know? (good: The pieces under water stayed paler than the one on the plate.; The plate piece went brown first, so the water slowed the browning.)
  - Note: Cut brinjal browns quickly in air, often within minutes. Pieces kept under water stay much paler. The salt water piece may look a little paler than the plain water one, or about the same. Both are honest results.
  - Wait: Wait until 20 minutes.

### c1 · The Fair Car Race (25 min)

### c2 · The Fair Taste Test (20 min)
- **problem:** Appa says he can taste the difference between tap water and water that was boiled and then cooled. Design a fair test to find out if he can.
- **your turn:** Now design a fair taste test for something else: can someone tell sugar from jaggery in their tea? Can they tell two kinds of rice apart?
- **constraints:** He must not see which water is which.; Both waters must be at the same temperature.; One correct guess is not enough to prove it.
- **general:** Pour both waters into identical tumblers, at the same temperature. Mark the bottoms secretly. Swap the order each time. Ask him to say which is which, ten times.
- **creative:** Toss a coin to decide the order each time, so even you can't make a pattern.; Let someone else pour, so your face can't give a clue.; Do the test on yourself too.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem. Ask how Appa could cheat without meaning to.  
  *Parent says:* How could Appa guess right without really tasting a difference?  
  *Child mode:* How could Appa guess right just by luck or by peeking?

- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* Ten tries. Change the order with a coin toss each time. Write right or wrong.  
  *Child mode:* Ten tries. Toss a coin for the order. Write right or wrong.

- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the right answers and talk about them.  
  *Parent says:* Just guessing, he would get about 5 right out of 10. What did he get? What does that tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Guessing gets about 5 of 10 right. What did he get?

## Worksheets
### Fair Test Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Revathi wants to know which cloth dries faster, cotton or towel. Which test is fair?  
   Options: A small cotton hanky in the sun and a big towel in the bathroom / Two pieces the same size, equally wet, hung side by side / A wet hanky and a dry towel  
   **Answer:** Two pieces the same size, equally wet, hung side by side. *Why:* The pieces must be the same size and equally wet, and dry in the same place. Only the cloth is different.
2. (truefalse) Meenakshi says, 'If I change two things at once, I can still tell which one made the difference.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. If two things change together, either one could have caused the result. Change one thing at a time.
3. (fill) The part of a test where you change nothing, so you can compare with it, is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['control']. *Why:* The control shows what happens anyway. The plain apple slice was the control.
4. (write) Lakshmi drops her ball from above her head and her brother drops his from his knee. Hers bounces higher. Why can't she say her ball is bouncier?  
   **Answer:** Because the balls fell from different heights. A ball dropped from higher bounces higher, so the height could be the reason, not the ball.. *Why:* A strong answer names the two things that were different, the ball and the height, and says either could explain it.
5. (sort) Kavya is testing whether lemon keeps apple white. Sort each thing: does she change it, or keep it the same?  
   **Answer:** what she puts on each slice → Change; the apple → Keep the same; the thickness of the slices → Keep the same; the plate → Keep the same; the time she checks → Keep the same; the place the plate sits → Keep the same. *Why:* She changes only what goes on the slice. Everything else stays the same so it cannot make a difference.
6. (order) Put the steps of a fair test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Ask a question', 'Guess what will happen, and why', 'Plan what to change and what to keep the same', 'Do the test and write the results in a table', 'Say what you found']. *Why:* You need the question and the guess first, then the plan, then the test, then the conclusion.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Prediction → a guess with a reason, made before the test; Result → what really happened; Fluke → a result that happened once by chance; Evidence → what you saw that shows if an idea is right; Conclusion → what you decide from your evidence. *Why:* These words come up in every fair test. A strong answer could give an example of each.
8. (mcq) A results table has a column headed 'Height'. What is missing from the heading?  
   Options: The unit, such as (cm) / A colour / The date  
   **Answer:** The unit, such as (cm). *Why:* The unit is missing. 'Height (cm)' tells everyone the numbers are centimetres.
9. (draw) Draw a results table for the Brown Apple Test. Give it headings for what you put on each slice and the colour at 10 and 30 minutes. Fill in your own results.  
   **Answer:** A table with 4 rows (nothing, water, salt water, lemon) and columns with clear headings, filled in with the child's own scores.. *Why:* A strong table has a heading on every column, one row for each slice, and no empty boxes.
10. (write) In your apple test, which slice stayed whitest? Use your table as evidence.  
   **Answer:** For example: the lemon slice. It scored 0 at 30 minutes while the plain slice scored 2.. *Why:* A strong answer names the slice and quotes the scores or colours from the table, comparing it with the control.
11. (odd) Three of these tests are fair. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Arun throws one plane and his sister throws the other / Two balls dropped from the same height / Four apple slices from one apple / Two cloths of the same size hung side by side  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* Arun and his sister throw differently, so the throwers are different as well as the planes. The others change only one thing.
12. (truefalse) Throwing a paper ball once underarm and once overarm is enough to know which throw is better.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. One throw can be a fluke. Ten throws each give a fairer picture.
13. (perspective) Two cousins in Hyderabad argue about how to test which tumbler keeps water coldest: steel, glass or plastic.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees that tests should be repeated and also sees that fairness comes first: three fair tries beat ten unfair ones. Both girls have a point.. *Why:* Pick-a-side questions have no single right side. Marks go for using evidence and for fairly stating the other view.
14. (fill) Doing a test again in the same way, to check the result, is called ____ the test.  
   **Answer:** ['repeating']. *Why:* We repeat tests because one try can be a fluke.
15. (write) Name two things you would keep the same in a test of which shoe grips best on a slope.  
   **Answer:** The same slope, lifted the same way, and the same floor or board. Also the same person lifting it.. *Why:* A strong answer names things that could change the result if they were different, such as the board or the slope.
### Planning and Recording (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (write) Farhan wants to know if salt makes ice melt faster. Plan a fair test. Write what he changes, what he keeps the same, and what he watches.  
   **Answer:** Change: salt or no salt on the ice cube. Keep the same: cubes from the same tray, same size, same plates, same place, same time. Watch: which cube melts first, or how much water there is after 15 minutes.. *Why:* A strong plan has all three parts and a control cube with no salt.
2. (mcq) Nila tests paper planes. She throws plane A three times: 3 m, 4 m, 3 m. She throws plane B three times: 6 m, 1 m, 2 m. What can she say?

   **Answer:** She can't be sure yet: B's 6 m may be a fluke, and both went 10 m in total. *Why:* A's throws are close together. B had one long throw, 6 m, which may be a fluke. Both planes went 10 m in total, so she can't yet say which is better. She should throw more.
3. (sort) Is each of these an observation (something you notice) or a measurement (a number with a unit)?  
   **Answer:** the slice went light brown → Observation; the ball bounced 45 cm → Measurement; the sugar took 40 seconds to disappear → Measurement; the brinjal floated → Observation; 7 out of 10 throws went in → Measurement; the plain slice smelled sweet → Observation. *Why:* A measurement uses a tool and a unit.    **Answer:** A floating piece has its top in the air, so it would brown on top like the plate piece. Then the test wouldn't really show what water does. Fix it by holding each piece under with a katori.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the air does to the floating part and gives the fix.
5. (mcq) Look at this table from the Family Balance Table. Who balanced longest on average?  
   Options: Kavya / Paati / Appa  
   **Answer:** Paati. *Why:* Paati's times add to 60 seconds, Kavya's to 45 and Appa's to 30. Paati balanced longest.
6. (truefalse) A result you didn't expect is a mistake, so you should rub it out.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Keep it and write what happened. Check the test, then repeat it. Surprising results are how new things are found.
7. (draw) Draw a results table for Ten Throws, with rows for underarm and overarm and columns for the tally and the number in.  
   **Answer:** A table with two rows and three columns: Throw, Tally, Number in out of 10.. *Why:* A strong table has clear headings and room for a tally.
8. (order) Divya does the sugar puzzle. Put her tests in an order that untangles warmth and stirring.  
   **Answer:** ['Warm and stirred against cold and not stirred: she cannot tell which helped', 'Cold and stirred against cold and not stirred: this shows what stirring does', 'Warm and stirred against cold and stirred: this shows what warmth does', 'She says what each one did']. *Why:* First the unfair test shows the problem. Then two fair tests change one thing each. Then she concludes.
9. (match) Match each unfair test to the fix.  
   **Answer:** Two balls dropped from different heights → Drop both from the same mark; A big towel tested against a small hanky → Cut the same size of each; One apple slice cut 20 minutes before the others → Cut all the slices at the same time; One paper plane thrown by Arun and one by his sister → One person throws both. *Why:* Each fix makes one more thing the same, so only the thing being tested is different.
10. (perspective) Lind's twelve sailors were all very ill. He gave only two of them oranges and lemons.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees both sides: without the other pairs, Lind could not know what worked, and ships often had little fruit. But the other sailors stayed ill longer. Today, doctors ask people first and make sure everyone gets proper care.. *Why:* Real scientists still argue about how to test cures kindly. Marks go for reasons on both sides.
11. (write) Why should you draw your results table before you start a test, not after?  
   **Answer:** So you are ready to write each result straight away, and you don't forget any. It also makes you plan what you will measure.. *Why:* A strong answer mentions memory and planning.
12. (fill) The one thing you choose to change in a test is called a ____. The other things that could change are called variables too.  
   **Answer:** ['variable']. *Why:* Anything that could change in a test is a variable. In a fair test you change one of them on purpose.
13. (odd) Three of these are things to keep the same in Ten Throws. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Underarm or overarm / The throwing line / The paper ball / The bucket  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* The way of throwing is the thing being changed. The line, the ball and the bucket are kept the same.
14. (mcq) Asha's apple test: the lemon slice stayed white, but she forgot to keep a plain slice. What can she say?  
   Options: Lemon keeps apple white / She can't be sure what the lemon did, because she had nothing to compare with / Apples never go brown  
   **Answer:** She can't be sure what the lemon did, because she had nothing to compare with. *Why:* Without a control, she can't tell whether this apple would have stayed white anyway.
15. (write) Arun says his toy car is faster because it beat his sister's car down the stairs. Write one question you would ask him to check if his test was fair.  
   **Answer:** For example: Did both cars start from the same step? Did you both let go without pushing?. *Why:* A strong question names one thing that might have been different apart from the car.
### Thinking Like a Scientist (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Many people think a cricket team wins more when someone wears their 'lucky' shirt. How could you test this idea fairly? What makes it hard?  
   **Answer:** Record many matches, with and without the shirt, and compare how many were won. It is hard because so many other things change from match to match: the other team, the weather, who plays.. *Why:* A strong answer plans a comparison over many matches and names at least one thing that is hard to keep the same.
2. (perspective) Two scientists disagree about how to find out if a big neem tree cools a street.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that the first scientist can't plant and remove a tree easily, and that the second must make sure the streets are alike in other ways, such as width and buildings.. *Why:* This is a real problem in science: some things can't be changed on purpose, so scientists compare carefully chosen cases.
3. (mcq) In a fair test of salt on ice, the salted cube makes more water in 15 minutes. But the salt itself has also dissolved into the water. What does this mean?  
   Options: The test is useless / The salt adds a little to the water, so a small difference might not mean much / Salt cannot dissolve  
   **Answer:** The salt adds a little to the water, so a small difference might not mean much. *Why:* The salt adds a little to the liquid, so the comparison is not perfect. Noticing hidden differences like this is what good scientists do. A larger difference than the salt could explain still shows the salt melted more ice.
4. (write) Your plain apple slice went brown with nothing on it. What was making it brown? How does this help you understand what the lemon did?  
   **Answer:** Air, warmth and the cut were browning it, because the apple's broken cells meet oxygen. Every slice had those. So the plain slice shows what happens without lemon, and the difference with the lemon slice is what the lemon did.. *Why:* A strong answer names the causes every slice shared and uses the control to explain the lemon's effect.
5. (sort) Can each question be answered by a fair test you change yourself, or only by comparing what nature has already done?  
   **Answer:** Does lemon keep apple white? → Fair test; Do bigger stars burn out sooner? → Comparing what nature did; Does stirring dissolve sugar faster? → Fair test; Were dinosaurs warm-blooded? → Comparing what nature did; Which throw gets more balls in a bucket? → Fair test; Do people who eat more vegetables live longer? → Comparing what nature did. *Why:* If you can change the thing yourself, you can do a fair test. If not, you compare many cases.
6. (mcq) A shop says its new soap 'kills 99% of germs'. Which question best checks if this is a fair claim?  
   Options: What colour is the soap? / Compared with what? Did plain soap kill as many? / How much does it cost?  
   **Answer:** Compared with what? Did plain soap kill as many?. *Why:* To know the soap helps, you must compare it with something, like plain soap. A claim with no comparison doesn't tell you much.
7. (write) Kavya and Arun took turns with their boats, instead of doing all of Arun's runs first. Why did that make the test fairer?  
   **Answer:** The stream was getting slower as the rain stopped. If Arun went first every time, his boat would always get the faster water. Taking turns shared the slowing water between both boats.. *Why:* A strong answer names the thing that was changing over time, the water speed, and explains how taking turns shared it.
8. (draw) Draw a bar chart of these Ten Throws results: underarm 7, overarm 4. Label both bars and the side of the chart.  
   **Answer:** Two bars, underarm up to 7 and overarm up to 4, with a side scale from 0 to 10 labelled 'Balls in'.. *Why:* A strong chart has labelled bars, a numbered scale starting at 0, and a title.
9. (truefalse) If a test is fair, it will give exactly the same numbers every time you repeat it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Small things change every time, so the numbers differ a little. A fair test gives results that are close, and the pattern stays the same.
10. (match) Match each scientist to what their records or tests showed.  
   **Answer:** James Lind, 1747 → oranges and lemons cured sailors with scurvy; Florence Nightingale, 1850s → more soldiers died of diseases in hospitals than of their wounds. *Why:* Both showed the power of careful records and comparisons.
11. (order) Put the parts of a good conclusion in order.  
   **Answer:** ['What I found (my claim)', 'What I saw or measured (my evidence)', 'Why I think it happened (my reasoning)', 'What I would test next']. *Why:* Claim, evidence, reasoning, and then a new question.
12. (write) Lind's test used only twelve sailors, two for each treatment. Why might a doctor today want many more people in a test?  
   **Answer:** With only two people, one might get better for some other reason, by chance. With many people in each group, chance evens out, and the result is much more trustworthy.. *Why:* A strong answer links the number of people to flukes, like the number of throws.
13. (odd) Which of these is the odd one out?  
   Options: A prediction / A result / An observation / A measurement  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* A guess is made before a test. Results, observations and measurements come from doing the test.
14. (fill) Florence Nightingale kept her records in rows and columns. This way of writing results is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['table']. *Why:* A table lets you compare results by reading across rows and down columns.
15. (write) Think of a question you really want to answer at home. Write it as a fair test: what you change, what you keep the same, and what you measure.  
   **Answer:** Any home question with all three parts, for example: Does a fan dry clothes faster? Change: fan on or off. Same: same cloth, same wetness, same room. Measure: minutes to dry.. *Why:* A strong answer has all three parts and a question that can really be tested at home.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a fair test?  
   Options: A test where everyone wins / A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same / A test you do only once / A test that agrees with your guess  
   **Answer:** A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Then any difference in the result must come from that one thing.
2. (mcq) Farhan wants to know which of two balls is bouncier. What must he do?  
   Options: Drop them from different heights / Drop them from the same height onto the same floor / Throw one and drop the other / Drop one on the rug and one on the tiles  
   **Answer:** Drop them from the same height onto the same floor. *Why:* Only the ball should be different. Same height, same floor, and just let go.
3. (mcq) Divya puts one plant on the sunny balcony and waters that plant every day. She puts another inside and forgets to water it. The balcony plant grows better. What is wrong with her test?  
   Options: Nothing, it is fair / She changed two things: the light and the water / She used plants / She should have used three plants  
   **Answer:** She changed two things: the light and the water. *Why:* Light and water were both different, so she cannot tell which one helped the balcony plant.
4. (mcq) Lakshmi tests whether lemon keeps an apple slice white. Why does she also keep one slice with nothing on it?  
   Options: To eat it / To compare: it shows what happens with no lemon / Because lemon is expensive / To make the plate look full  
   **Answer:** To compare: it shows what happens with no lemon. *Why:* That slice is the control. If it goes brown while the lemon slice stays white, the lemon made the difference.
5. (mcq) In the Brown Apple Test, which of these should be the same for every slice?  
   Options: What you put on it / The apple it was cut from / Its colour at the end / Its label  
   **Answer:** The apple it was cut from. *Why:* All slices come from one apple. Different apples can brown at different speeds, so using two apples would be unfair.
6. (mcq) Meera throws a paper ball once underarm and it goes in. She throws once overarm and misses. What should she do next?  
   Options: Decide underarm is better / Throw many more times each, the same way / Stop playing / Use a different bucket  
   **Answer:** Throw many more times each, the same way. *Why:* One throw can be a fluke. Ten throws each give a much better answer.
7. (mcq) In a results table, where do you write the unit, such as 'seconds'?  
   Options: After every number / In the column heading / At the very bottom / You don't need units  
   **Answer:** In the column heading. *Why:* The unit goes once in the heading, for example 'Time (seconds)'. It tells everyone what all the numbers in that column mean.
8. (mcq) Kavya's table says: underarm 7 out of 10, overarm 4 out of 10. What can she say?  
   Options: Overarm got more in / Underarm got more in, in her test / They were the same / She can't say anything  
   **Answer:** Underarm got more in, in her test. *Why:* 7 is more than 4. So in her ten throws each, more went in underarm. She could repeat it to be surer.
9. (mcq) A cut apple slice turns brown because:  
   Options: It is dirty / Substances in the broken cells meet oxygen in the air / The knife was steel / Light shines on it  
   **Answer:** Substances in the broken cells meet oxygen in the air. *Why:* Cutting breaks the apple's cells. Their substances meet the oxygen in the air, and an enzyme makes brown substances.
10. (mcq) Why did James Lind give all twelve sick sailors the same food?  
   Options: So only the treatment was different / Because they liked it / To save money / So they would sleep better  
   **Answer:** So only the treatment was different. *Why:* With the same food, the same place and the same illness, the treatment was the only difference. So it was the treatment that helped the oranges and lemons pair.
11. (mcq) Which pair of sailors in Lind's test got better fastest?  
   Options: The pair given sea water / The pair given vinegar / The pair given oranges and lemons / The pair given dilute acid  
   **Answer:** The pair given oranges and lemons. *Why:* Oranges and lemons have vitamin C, which the body needs. One of that pair was fit for work after six days.
12. (mcq) Shalini's test shows something she didn't expect. What is the best thing to do?  
   Options: Change the result to match her guess / Keep the result, check the test was fair, and repeat it / Throw the table away / Say the test is broken  
   **Answer:** Keep the result, check the test was fair, and repeat it. *Why:* An unexpected result is still a result. If the test was fair and gives the same result again, she has learned something new.

## Applications
- Doctors test new medicines in fair tests called trials, comparing people who get the medicine with people who don't.
- Farmers test new seeds by growing them in fields next to the old kind, with the same soil and water.
- Cooks change one ingredient at a time to make a recipe better.
- Engineers test one change at a time on a bridge, a car or a phone.
- Sports coaches test new training by comparing players who do it with players who don't.
- Weather stations record numbers in tables every day, so scientists can compare years.
- A doctor's chart of a patient's temperature is a results table.
- Food scientists test which packing keeps food fresh longest.

## Spotted around us
- The cut brinjal or potato kept in a bowl of water in the kitchen before cooking.
- Lemon squeezed over a fruit chaat at a stall, so the cut fruit stays fresh-looking.
- The table of class marks or attendance on a school notice board.
- A cricket scorecard: a table with a row for each batter and columns for runs and balls.
- Train timetables at the station: rows for trains, columns for times.
- A calendar: a table of days, with columns for each day of the week.

## Open questions
- **Why do some people get better after taking a pill with no medicine in it?** This is called the placebo effect. It is real and has been measured many times, but nobody fully knows how believing in a treatment changes the body.
- **How can we test ideas about things that happened only once, like how life began?** We cannot repeat the start of life. Scientists test small parts of the story in the lab, but nobody knows the whole answer.
- **Why do some careful studies give different results when they are repeated?** Scientists are still working out how much comes from chance, small differences between labs, and studies that were too small.
- **How many tries are enough?** There is no single number. It depends on how big the difference is and how much results vary. Scientists use statistics to decide, and argue about the best way.

## Connected maths
- In Ten Throws, Kavya got 7 of 10 balls in underarm and 4 of 10 overarm. How many more went in underarm? **3 more went in underarm.**. 7 take away 4 is 3. So underarm got 3 more balls in out of the same 10 throws. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Kavya and Arun timed their boats three times each. Arun's boat took 11, 12 and 13 seconds. Kavya's took 12, 11 and 13 seconds. What was the total for each boat? What does that tell you? **Both boats took 36 seconds in total, so they were about as fast as each other.**. 11 + 12 + 13 = 36. 12 + 11 + 13 = 36. The totals are the same, so neither boat was faster. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
- Paati stood on one foot for 20, 22 and 18 seconds. Appa stood for 8, 10 and 12 seconds. About how many times longer did Paati balance? **About 2 times as long.**. Paati's total is 20 + 22 + 18 = 60 seconds. Appa's total is 8 + 10 + 12 = 30 seconds. 60 is 2 times 30. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Lind gave 12 sailors six different treatments, the same number of sailors for each. How many sailors got each treatment? If he had 30 sailors and six treatments, how many would each get? **2 sailors each. With 30 sailors, 5 each.**. 12 shared into 6 equal groups is 2. 30 shared into 6 equal groups is 5. More sailors in each group would make a fluke less likely. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
- Nila's plane A flew 3, 4 and 3 metres. Plane B flew 6, 1 and 2 metres. Which plane is more reliable, and why might B's 6 be a fluke? **Plane A is more reliable. Its throws are close together. B's throws jump about, and its 6 metres happened only once.**. Both planes add up to 10 metres. But A's three numbers are all close, while B's are very different from each other. A number far from the others might be a fluke, so Nila should throw more times. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
