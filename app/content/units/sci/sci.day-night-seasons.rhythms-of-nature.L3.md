# Rhythms of nature: how plants and birds keep time
`sci.day-night-seasons.rhythms-of-nature.L3`

**Summary.** A bean plant lifts their leaves in the morning and lowers them at night, even inside a dark box. They keep time with a clock inside their body. Many living things have one. It runs on a rhythm of about 24 hours, and sunlight sets it right each morning. Flowers use their clocks to open when their visitors come: the lotus at dawn, jasmine in the evening, pavazhamalli at night. Over the year, living things also read the length of the day. Shorter days tell barn swallows to fly to India for the winter, and longer nights tell sevanthi plants to flower. So nature keeps a daily rhythm and a yearly rhythm, and living things get ready for each change before it comes.

**Intro.** *Look at a bean plant at noon, and their leaves are spread out flat. Look again at bedtime, and the same leaves hang down. Put the plant in a dark box, where no light can reach. Will the leaves still lift in the morning? How could a plant know it is morning without seeing the sky?* People have noticed these daily movements for thousands of years. Gardeners called them the 'sleep' of leaves. In this unit you grow a bean plant and test them yourself. You find out that living things carry a clock inside them. You also find out how birds know when to fly thousands of kilometres, and how flowers know when to open.

## Terms
- **Rhythm**: Something that happens again and again, at regular times. *e.g.* The tide comes in and goes out about twice a day at Marina Beach.
- **Daily rhythm**: A change that repeats about once every 24 hours. *e.g.* You feel sleepy at night and wake in the morning.
- **Yearly rhythm**: A change that repeats about once a year, with the seasons. *e.g.* The konrai tree, also called the Indian laburnum, covers itself in yellow flowers around April each year.
- **Body clock**: A timer inside a living thing that keeps a rhythm of about 24 hours, even without a clock or a window. *e.g.* A bean plant in a dark box still lifts their leaves in the morning.
- **Circadian rhythm**: The scientific name for a daily rhythm kept by a body clock. 'Circadian' comes from Latin words meaning 'about a day'. *e.g.* Your body is coolest in the early morning and warmest in the late afternoon, every day.
- **Nocturnal**: Active at night and resting in the day. *e.g.* Fruit bats hang in big trees by day and fly out at dusk to eat.
- **Diurnal**: Active in the day and resting at night. *e.g.* Crows and sparrows feed in the day and roost at night.
- **Sleep movement**: The way some leaves fold or droop at night and spread out again in the morning. Scientists call it nyctinasty. *e.g.* Bean, touch-me-not and tamarind leaves all fold or droop at night.
- **Pulvinus**: A small, thick joint at the base of a leaf. Water moves in and out of its cells, and this bends the joint and moves the leaf. *e.g.* Look closely where a bean leaf meets their stalk. The swollen bit is the pulvinus.
- **Pollinator**: An animal that carries pollen from flower to flower, so that seeds can form. *e.g.* Bees, butterflies, moths, birds and bats are pollinators.
- **Migrate**: To travel far to another place at the same time each year, and come back later. *e.g.* Barn swallows fly to India each winter from places further north and fly back in spring.
- **Day length**: How many hours of daylight there are between sunrise and sunset. It changes through the year, more in the north than near the equator. *e.g.* In Chennai the longest day is about 12 hours 55 minutes. In Srinagar it is about 14 hours 25 minutes.
- **Photoperiodism**: The way a living thing uses day length to tell the season. *e.g.* Sevanthi, the chrysanthemum, makes flower buds when the nights grow long.
- **Hibernate**: To spend the cold season in a deep, long rest, using very little energy. *e.g.* Some bats and bears hibernate in the winter. Swallows do not: they migrate.
- **Chronobiology**: The science of time in living things: body clocks, daily rhythms and yearly rhythms. *e.g.* A chronobiologist might study why night-shift nurses feel tired.

## Explanations
### Daily and yearly rhythms · *defined*
**Definition:** A rhythm is a change that repeats at regular times. A daily rhythm repeats about every 24 hours. A yearly rhythm repeats about once a year.

The Earth spins once a day, so light and dark come round every 24 hours. The Earth also goes round the Sun once a year, so the seasons come round too. Almost every living thing changes with these two rhythms. Leaves droop at night. Flowers open at their own hour. Birds sing at dawn. In the year, trees flower, frogs call in the monsoon and birds come and go. People have daily rhythms too: sleeping, waking, feeling hungry.
- e.g. Every evening, house crows in Chennai fly to big trees to roost together.
- e.g. Neem trees flower in March and April, near Ugadi. Neem flowers go into Ugadi pachadi.
- e.g. Termites with wings fly out of the ground after the first big rains.
### The body clock inside living things · *defined*
**Definition:** A body clock is a timer inside a living thing. It keeps a rhythm of about 24 hours, even in steady darkness. Morning light sets it right each day.

A bean plant shut in a dark box still lifts their leaves in the morning and lowers them at night. Nothing outside tells them the time, so the timing must come from inside. Scientists call this a circadian rhythm. A body clock is not exact. In steady darkness it runs a little fast or a little slow, so it slowly drifts. Every morning, light resets it, the way you set a watch. The clock is made by genes and the substances they make. These build up and break down in a loop that takes about a day. In 2017, Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young won the Nobel Prize for finding this loop in fruit flies. Plants, fungi, many bacteria and people have clocks too.
- e.g. Your body is coolest in the early morning, before you wake, and warmest in the late afternoon.
- e.g. A rooster often crows before the sky gets light.
- e.g. In 1929, the Dutch botanist Anthonia Kleinhoonte showed that jack bean leaves kept moving up and down for days in steady darkness.
### Why it helps to be ready before sunrise · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant makes food from light, air and water. This is called photosynthesis. A body clock lets a plant prepare for light before the light arrives.

A plant's problem is that light comes for only part of the day, and every hour of it counts. If a plant waited for sunrise to start getting ready, they would waste part of the morning. With a clock, they lift their leaves and make the parts they need for photosynthesis before dawn. Then they can use the first light straight away. Scientists have tested this. Plants whose clock matched the length of the day grew bigger and made more food than plants whose clock did not match. Animals gain the same way. A bird that is awake and singing at first light is ready to feed as soon as insects move.
- e.g. A bean plant starts lifting their leaves before sunrise, while it is still dark.
- e.g. In 2005, Antony Dodd and his team showed that plants with a matching clock grew better.
- e.g. Many flowers make their scent at the hour their visitors come, and less at other times.
### Why do leaves 'sleep' at night? · *debated*
Bean, tamarind, touch-me-not and rain tree leaves all fold or droop at night. They move by a small joint, the pulvinus, at the base of each leaf. Water moves into the cells on one side of the joint and out of the other side, and the joint bends. Scientists agree on how the leaves move. They do not agree on why. Nobody has shown which answer is right for every plant.
- e.g. Rain trees on the roads of Bengaluru and Chennai fold their leaflets at night and on very cloudy days.
- e.g. Tamarind leaflets fold together after sunset.
- e.g. Touch-me-not folds at night and also when touched.
- **Charles Darwin, 1880:** Folded leaves lose less warmth to the cold night sky. He pinned some leaves open, and on cold nights those leaves were more often damaged by frost.
- **Erwin Bünning, a German botanist, 1960s:** Folded leaves keep moonlight off their surface. Bright moonlight could confuse the plant's clock, which measures how long the night is.
- **Some other scientists:** Drooping leaves stay drier at night, so fewer fungi and germs grow on them. Or the leaves save water. These ideas are still being tested.
### Flowers that open at their own hour · *defined*
**Definition:** Many flowers open and close at about the same time each day. Their body clock and the light together set the time.

A flower's problem is that pollen must reach another flower of the same kind, and most flowers cannot move. So they need visitors, and visitors come at certain hours. Bees work in the day. Many moths fly at night. A flower open at the wrong hour wastes scent and nectar, and can be eaten by a hungry insect that does not carry pollen. So flowers open when their visitors are about. The lotus opens in the early morning and closes again later in the day, for about three days. Jasmine buds open in the evening. Pavazhamalli, the night jasmine, opens at night, and their flowers drop to the ground around dawn. White, sweet-smelling flowers that open at night are often visited by moths. In 1751, the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus suggested a 'flower clock': a garden of flowers that open at different hours, so you could tell the time by looking.
- e.g. Malli buds bought in the afternoon open in the evening.
- e.g. Four o'clock flowers open in the late afternoon. In Tamil they are called andhi mandharai, the evening flower.
- e.g. Brahma kamal, called nishagandhi in Kerala, opens one night and fades by morning.
### Why birds migrate · *defined*
**Definition:** To migrate is to travel far, at the same time each year, and come back later. Birds migrate to find food and good places to raise their chicks.

A bird's problem in the far north is winter. Lakes freeze, snow covers the ground and insects disappear. A small bird must eat many times a day to stay warm, so staying would mean starving. So many birds fly south to warmer places, such as India, where there is food all winter. In spring they fly back north. There the summer days are very long, with lots of insects and space for nests. So they can feed their chicks for many hours each day. Barn swallows reach India from further north in the cold months, and line up on electric wires. Rosy starlings come from Central Asia and leave in spring. Amur falcons stop in Nagaland on their way from China and Siberia to southern Africa. Butterflies migrate too. In South India, huge numbers of blue tiger and crow butterflies travel between the plains and the Western Ghats each year.
- e.g. Barn swallows on the wires in winter, gone again by summer.
- e.g. Lakhs of Amur falcons rest near the Doyang reservoir in Nagaland in October and November.
- e.g. Arctic terns fly from the far north to Antarctica and back each year.
### How birds know when to go · *defined*
**Definition:** Birds tell the season mostly by day length. They also have a yearly clock inside them.

Day length changes the same way every year, so it is a safer signal than the weather. One warm week in winter does not change it. In birds, longer or shorter days change their hormones. Before a journey, they eat much more and store fat for fuel, and they become restless at night. Scientists have a German word for this restlessness, Zugunruhe. The German scientist Eberhard Gwinner kept warblers in a room where the light and warmth stayed the same all year. They still became restless and put on fat at about the times of their journeys. Their clock ran about 10 months instead of 12, so day length is needed to keep it on time. To find the way, birds use the Sun, the stars, the Earth's magnetic field, smells and landmarks.
- e.g. Before migrating, some small birds nearly double their weight with fat.
- e.g. Many small birds migrate at night and rest and eat by day.
- e.g. Young cuckoos fly to Africa alone, without ever having been there.
### How plants tell the season: day length · *defined*
**Definition:** Photoperiodism is the way a plant or animal uses day length to tell the season.

Many plants flower only when the days reach a certain length. This lets all the plants of one kind flower together, so pollen can pass between them. In 1920, two American scientists, Wightman Garner and Harry Allard, found that one kind of tobacco plant flowered only when the days were short. They grew them in a shed and shortened the days by covering the plants, and they flowered. The plant is really measuring the length of the night. A flash of light in the middle of a long night can stop flowering. Farmers use this. Many rice varieties flower by day length. In Japan, some growers light chrysanthemum fields at night, so the plants flower later, when buyers want them.
- e.g. Sevanthi, the chrysanthemum, flowers when the nights grow long.
- e.g. Many traditional rice varieties in India flower at about the same time of year, even when sown at different times.
- e.g. Day length changes much more in Srinagar than in Chennai.
### Rhythms set by the Moon and the tides · *defined*
**Definition:** Some living things keep a rhythm with the Moon or the tides, as well as with the Sun.

The Moon goes from full moon to full moon in about 29 and a half days. The Moon's pull also makes the tides. Living things on the shore and in the sea have a problem: the water comes and goes, and sometimes they must all meet at once. On the Great Barrier Reef in Australia, many corals release their eggs on the same few nights after a full moon, once a year. So eggs from many corals meet in the water at the same time. In California, grunion fish come out of the sea onto beaches, just after the highest tides, to lay their eggs in wet sand. Crabs and other shore animals often move with the tides, about twice a day.
- e.g. Coral spawning on the Great Barrier Reef happens a few nights after a full moon in November or December.
- e.g. Grunion lay eggs on Californian beaches after the highest spring tides.
- e.g. Many crabs on Indian beaches come out at low tide.
### When rhythms go wrong · *defined*
**Definition:** A body clock works best when it matches the real day and the real year. Bright lights at night and a warming world can put rhythms out of step.

Most migrating birds fly at night. Bright lights on tall buildings can pull them off course. They circle the lights until they are tired, or fly into the glass. Some cities turn off building lights in the migration months to help them. In people, light from screens late at night can push the body clock later, so it is harder to fall asleep. As the world warms, some plants are flowering earlier in the year. In 2021, the cherry trees of Kyoto, in Japan, flowered at their earliest date in more than 1,200 years of records. If the flowers come early but their pollinators do not, both can go hungry.
- e.g. Chicago and New York ask tall buildings to dim their lights during the bird migration months.
- e.g. Night-shift workers and people with jet lag feel tired at the wrong times.
- e.g. Streetlights can make some birds sing in the middle of the night.

## Mostly agreed
- Most living things have a body clock that keeps a rhythm of about 24 hours, even without light. *How we know:* Plants, fungi, flies, mice and people kept in steady darkness keep a daily rhythm for days or weeks. Scientists have found the genes that make the clock.
- Morning light resets the body clock each day. *How we know:* Shift the times of light and dark, and the plant or animal shifts their rhythm within a few days. This is why jet lag wears off.
- Many birds migrate each year to find food and places to raise chicks. *How we know:* Rings on birds' legs since 1899, and now tiny trackers, show the same birds flying between the same places year after year.
- Day length tells many plants and animals the season. *How we know:* Change only the day length, keeping the warmth the same, and many plants flower and many birds get ready to migrate.
- Leaves move by a joint at their base, called the pulvinus, where water moves in and out of cells. *How we know:* Scientists have measured the water and salts moving in the joint cells as the leaf rises and falls.

## How we found out
**The people of Klütz and the scientists at the University of Rostock, who still keep the stork, and Hans Christian Mortensen, a Danish teacher who began ringing birds, 1822 (the stork with the spear) and 1899 (the first numbered bird rings).** Every autumn in Europe, storks and swallows disappeared, and every spring they came back. For about 2,000 years, people argued about where they went. The Greek thinker Aristotle wrote that some birds hid and slept through the winter. In 1555, the Swedish writer Olaus Magnus wrote that swallows sank into lakes and slept in the mud until spring. Many people believed this for the next 250 years. Then, in 1822, a white stork was shot near the village of Klütz, in northern Germany. Through the stork's neck was a long wooden spear, about 75 centimetres long. The spear was not from Germany. It was the kind made in central Africa. The stork had been speared in Africa in the winter, survived, and flown thousands of kilometres back with it. So the birds had not slept in mud. They had flown to Africa. Later, about 25 more storks with African arrows were found in Germany. In 1899, the Danish teacher Hans Christian Mortensen began putting numbered rings on birds' legs. Rings found in faraway places showed the journeys clearly.  
*What people believed before:* People thought swallows and other birds spent the winter asleep, hidden or in the mud at the bottom of lakes. The stork with an African spear in their neck showed that birds fly to Africa for the winter and come back.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Swallows spend the winter asleep in the mud at the bottom of ponds.** (Many people in Europe for about 2,000 years, including the Greek thinker Aristotle and the Swedish writer Olaus Magnus in 1555; *rejected*)  
  For: Swallows gather over ponds and reed beds in autumn, then suddenly vanish. Nobody had seen where they went.  
  Against: No swallow was ever found alive in winter mud when people really looked. Rings and trackers show swallows flying to Africa and South Asia. The stork found in 1822 with an African spear in their neck had clearly been to Africa.
- **Some birds fly to the Moon for the winter.** (An English teacher, Charles Morton, in a pamphlet written around 1700; *rejected*)  
  For: Birds disappear high into the sky in autumn and return in spring. He worked out that a trip to the Moon could take about two months.  
  Against: Space has no air to fly in. Trackers show the birds flying across the Earth, not up and away from it.
- **Leaves fold at night only because it gets dark.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Leaves fold when it is dark, and darkness does make many leaves fold faster.  
  Against: In a dark box, bean leaves still lift in the morning and lower in the evening, for several days. Their timing comes from a body clock inside them.
- **Flowers open in the morning because the Sun warms them.** (A common idea, and partly true; *debated*)  
  For: Some flowers, such as tulips and crocuses, open when they get warm and close when they cool down.  
  Against: Many other flowers open at their hour even when the weather is cool or cloudy, because their clock times it. Jasmine opens in the evening, as it cools.
- **Birds migrate south because they feel cold.** (A very common idea; *debated*)  
  For: Birds do leave as winter comes, and winter is cold.  
  Against: Many birds leave while the weather is still warm, as the days shorten. Their real problem is food: frozen lakes and no insects. Birds with plenty of food in winter often stay.
- **Plants feel the time of day and decide when to move their leaves.** (Plant scientists who study plant behaviour; *debated*)  
  For: Plants sense light, darkness and the length of the night. They measure time and act before the change comes, which is what deciding looks like in any living thing.  
  Against: Some scientists call these automatic responses run by genes, with no thinking involved.

## Roots
We have watched bean leaves rise and fall, jasmine open in the evening and swallows come for the winter. Now we ask what nature's clocks tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the dark box, your bean plant still lifted their leaves in the morning. No light reached them, so the timing came from inside the plant. Earlier days on the windowsill had set that inside clock. So a plant expects the morning, and gets ready before it comes.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of plants as only reacting: light comes, then leaves open. But a reaction comes after its cause. Your plant moved before any light came, so they were expecting the day, not answering it.  
*Evidence:* The box cup's leaves were flat in the morning and hanging down at night, like the window cup, with no light at all. Taken out of the box, they kept going on the window's rhythm.

- *who am i:* You have a body clock too. It cools you before dawn and warms you through the day, without asking you.
- *what is life:* Living things keep time. Leaves, flowers, falcons and corals all get ready for what is coming.
- *what is this world:* The Earth spins once a day and goes round the Sun once a year. Every living thing on it has learned those two beats.

The Earth makes one rhythm: one spin a day, one trip round the Sun a year. From that one rhythm come countless clocks. The bean lifts their leaves, the jasmine opens at evening, the swallow leaves the north, the coral waits for the moon, and you wake up. Each keeps their own time, yet all of them follow the same turning Earth.

- **Tirukkural 481 (chapter 49, on knowing the right time):** In the daytime, a crow can defeat an owl. A king who wants to win must also choose the right time. (The crow is diurnal and the owl is nocturnal. Each is strong at their own hour. The Kural saw a daily rhythm in animals and drew a lesson about timing from it.)
- **Ecclesiastes 3:1, the Bible:** To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven. (Sevanthi flowers in the long nights, swallows come in the cold months and termites fly after the first rain. Each living thing acts at their own time.)
- Science (settled): Plants, animals, fungi and many bacteria have body clocks of about 24 hours, made by genes in a loop. Light resets them each day.
- Science (debated): Plants feel the length of the night and use it to decide when to flower. Some scientists call this an automatic response.
- Science (open): How the many clocks in one body stay in step is not fully known.
- Other view, Many plant scientists: A plant's clock is a loop of genes and chemicals, as steady as a ticking wall clock. Saying the plant 'expects' the morning adds a feeling that the experiment cannot show. They would say the plant is timed, not expecting.
- Practice, Guess the time with your body (3 min): Let's hide the clocks and sit still with our eyes closed. Take five slow breaths. Now notice your body. Do you feel sleepy or awake? Hungry or full? Warm or cool? Keep your eyes closed and guess what time it is. Now open your eyes and check the clock. How close was your body?
- Ask: Your bean plant got ready for a morning they could not see. Is getting ready the same as knowing?
- Ask: If every living thing keeps their own clock, whose clock tells the real time?
- Ask: Your body cools before dawn without you deciding it. Who is keeping that time: you, or your body?

## Story: Paati's Balcony Calendar
*Arjun, Paati and their flat are made up. The nature is true: pavazhamalli flowers open at night and fall around dawn, mostly from about August to December. Barn swallows spend the cold months in India. Sevanthi flowers as the nights grow long. Neem flowers around Ugadi. Winged termites fly out after the first heavy rains.*

Arjun was eight when he and his mother moved into Paati's flat in Mysuru. It was on the fourth floor, with a narrow balcony full of pots. There was a big calendar on the kitchen wall, with a picture of Chamundi Hill. Paati never looked at it.

'How do you know what month it is?' Arjun asked her.

'The balcony tells me,' said Paati. She was watering her sevanthi plants, which had no flowers at all.

Arjun laughed. 'A balcony can't tell you the month.'

'Then catch me out,' said Paati. 'Write down everything the balcony shows you, for one whole year. If I'm wrong even once, I'll make you payasam every Sunday.'

So Arjun got a notebook. On the first page he wrote 'Paati's Mistakes', and underlined it twice.

In August, Paati said, 'The pavazhamalli tree by the temple wall will start dropping flowers soon.' Arjun went down for the milk packets at half past six every morning. For a week, the ground under the tree was bare. Then one morning it was white. Hundreds of small white flowers with orange stems lay on the ground, and they smelled sweet. He looked up. There were new buds on the tree, but no open flowers.

'They open at night,' said Paati, 'and fall at dawn. Every night, the same.' Arjun wrote it down, under 'Things Paati Got Right'. He had not planned to have that page.

He tested it. He went down earlier, at six. The flowers were still falling, one by one, like slow snow. He went down at nine in the evening with a torch. The tree was covered in open flowers, and the whole lane smelled of them. In the morning they were on the ground again.

In October, Paati said, 'The swallows will come to the wires.' A week later, Arjun saw them: small dark birds with long forked tails and red throats, sitting in a neat row on the electric wire. In the evening there were thirty. He counted twice.

'Where were they before?' he asked.

'Far to the north,' said Paati. 'Where it's getting cold now, and the insects are going. They come here, where there is food all winter.'

'But how do they know it's October? They don't have a calendar either.'

Paati smiled. 'Watch the evenings,' she said. 'You tell me.'

So Arjun wrote down the time the streetlights came on. In June they had come on at about seven. Now they came on before six. The days were getting shorter. He did not know yet how a swallow could tell. But he could see that something in the sky was changing, and it changed the same way every year.

In December, the nights were long and cool. The sevanthi plants, which had been green leaves for months, grew tight little buds. By the end of the month the balcony was yellow.

'They wait for the long nights,' said Paati. 'Then they know.'

In March, Paati said, 'The neem is flowering. Ugadi is coming.' The neem tree outside the gate was covered in tiny white flowers, and bees buzzed round it all day. Paati picked some and made Ugadi pachadi, sweet and sour and bitter all at once. In April, the swallows were gone from the wires. Arjun's 'Paati's Mistakes' page was still empty.

Then, in May, Arjun had his chance. 'Paati, when do the eesal come out?' he asked. Eesal were winged termites. Every year they flew out of the ground in clouds and danced round the streetlights.

'After the first big rain,' said Paati.

Last year, the first big rain had come in early May. This year, May was dry and hot. The ground cracked. No eesal came. On the last day of May, Arjun opened his notebook to 'Paati's Mistakes' and picked up his pencil.

'Wait,' said Paati. 'Did I say May?'

Arjun stopped. She had not. She had said, 'After the first big rain.'

The rain came on the ninth of June. It poured for an hour, and the road turned to a river. That evening, as the streetlight came on, the air filled with thousands of fluttering wings. Arjun and Paati stood on the balcony and watched them, whirling in the light.

That night, Arjun read his whole notebook from the start. The pavazhamalli opened every night at the same time. The swallows came as the evenings grew short. The sevanthi flowered when the nights grew long. Those things kept to the light, and the light kept to the year. But the eesal had waited for the rain, and the rain was different every year.

'The calendar on the wall counts days,' said Paati. 'The balcony counts light, and rain.'

Arjun turned to the empty page and crossed out 'Paati's Mistakes'. Above it he wrote 'Arjun's Nature Calendar'. Then he went to the kitchen. 'Paati,' he said, 'can we make payasam anyway?'

**For the parent:** For the parent: Paati's signs come in two kinds. Some follow the light: flowers that open at the same hour each night, swallows that arrive as the days shorten, sevanthi that flowers when the nights grow long. Day length changes the same way every year, so these signs are very steady. Other signs follow the weather, like winged termites after the first heavy rain, so they come on a different date each year. Arjun thought Paati was reading the calendar. She was really reading the same signals the living things read.

- Ask: How did Arjun find out when the pavazhamalli flowers open and fall?
- Ask: What did Arjun notice about the streetlights, and how might that help a swallow know the season?
- Ask: Why did the eesal come out in June this year, when last year they came out in May?
- Ask: Which of Paati's signs were the most steady from year to year, and why?
- Ask: Arjun started his notebook to catch Paati out. What did he end up with instead? Have you ever set out to prove someone wrong and learned something else?

### g1 · Nature's Clock: who is awake now? (15 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards. Each player takes 5 cards.; On your turn, put one card on the part of the clock where you think it belongs, and say why.; The other players check the answer in the brackets. Right, and you keep the card as a point. ### e1 · Sleeping Bean Leaves: do they need to see the morning? (45 min, 12 days)

(2 min); day 10: When the first two leaves are open, check both cups at 7 in the morning, 1 in the afternoon, 6 in the evening and 9 at night. 6. (mcq) What do many birds use to tell when it is time to migrate?  
   Options: The full moon / The first rain / The noise of traffic / The days getting shorter or longer  
   **Answer:** The days getting shorter or longer. *Why:* Day length changes the same way every year, so it is a reliable signal. Birds also have a yearly clock inside them.
7. (mcq) Before 1822, many people in Europe thought swallows spent the winter where?  
   Options: Asleep in the mud at the bottom of lakes / In India / In Africa / In nests on houses  
   **Answer:** Asleep in the mud at the bottom of lakes. *Why:* Writers such as Olaus Magnus said swallows slept in mud. A stork found with an African spear in 1822 helped show that birds fly far away instead.
8. (mcq) What do scientists still not agree on?  
   Options: Whether plants have body clocks / Why some leaves droop at night / Whether birds migrate / Whether the Earth spins  
   **Answer:** Why some leaves droop at night. *Why:* Scientists agree that leaves move and that plants have clocks. Why the drooping helps is still argued: keeping warm, keeping moonlight off, or staying dry.
9. (mcq) Sevanthi plants on a balcony grow flower buds in November. What signal tells them it is time?  
   Options: Louder birds / Hotter days / The nights getting longer / More rain  
   **Answer:** The nights getting longer. *Why:* Sevanthi, the chrysanthemum, flowers when the nights grow long. This use of day length is called photoperiodism.
10. (mcq) A body clock in steady darkness keeps going but slowly drifts. Why does it drift?  
   Options: It forgets the day / It counts in weeks / It stops every night / It runs a little faster or slower than 24 hours  
   **Answer:** It runs a little faster or slower than 24 hours. *Why:* A body clock runs close to 24 hours, but not exactly. Without light to reset it, the small difference adds up each day.
11. (mcq) Why does a bean plant gain from lifting their leaves before sunrise?  
   Options: It helps the roots drink / It makes the plant taller / It keeps insects away / They are ready to make food with the first light  
   **Answer:** They are ready to make food with the first light. *Why:* Plants make food from light. A plant that is ready before the light comes wastes none of the morning.
12. (mcq) Amur falcons rest in Nagaland in October. Where do they fly next?  
   Options: Up the Himalayas to sleep / To Antarctica / Across the Arabian Sea to southern Africa / Back to China straight away  
   **Answer:** Across the Arabian Sea to southern Africa. *Why:* After feeding in Nagaland, Amur falcons cross the Arabian Sea to spend the winter in southern Africa, then fly back in spring.

## Applications
- Doctors give some medicines at the time of day they work best, because the body changes through the day.
- Night-shift workers, pilots and astronauts use bright light and dark rooms to shift their body clocks.
- Farmers choose rice and other crop varieties that flower at the right time of year for their region.
- Flower growers use extra light at night to make chrysanthemums flower later.
- Cities dim building lights during the bird migration months.
- Bird scientists use rings and tiny trackers to follow migrations and protect resting places such as Nagaland's Doyang reservoir.
- Weather radar is used to forecast big nights of bird migration.
- Fishers in many places time their work by the tides and the Moon.
- Sleep doctors help people whose body clocks run too early or too late.

## Open questions
- **Why do some leaves droop or fold at night?** Keeping warm, keeping moonlight off and staying dry all have some evidence. Nobody has shown which matters most, or whether it differs between plants.
- **How do all the clocks in one body stay in step?** Almost every cell has a clock. Scientists know the main clock genes, but how the clocks of leaves, roots or organs talk to each other is still being studied.
- **How does a young bird know where to go on their first migration?** Some young birds fly alone to places they have never seen. Scientists think the direction is inborn, but how it is stored is not known.
- **Exactly how do birds sense the Earth's magnetic field to find their way?** The leading idea involves a substance in their eyes. It has strong evidence but is not fully proven in wild birds.
- **How do corals sense the moonlight so exactly?** Corals have no eyes, but have light-sensing substances. How they count the nights after a full moon is still being worked out.
- **How will a warming world change nature's calendar?** Some plants and animals are shifting their timing and some are not. Scientists are watching which ones fall out of step, and what happens then.

## Connected maths
- In the warm and cool test, Kavya's temperature was 36.3 °C just after waking and 36.8 °C at 5 pm. How much warmer was she at 5 pm? **0.5 °C warmer, which is half a degree.**. 36.8 take away 36.3 is 0.5. Think of it as 368 tenths take away 363 tenths, which is 5 tenths. Half a degree is small, but it came back on every day she measured. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- In Chennai, the longest day is about 12 hours 55 minutes and the shortest is about 11 hours 20 minutes. In Srinagar, they are about 14 hours 25 minutes and 9 hours 55 minutes. In which city does day length change more across the year, and by how much? **Srinagar. Day length changes by about 4 hours 30 minutes there, and by about 1 hour 35 minutes in Chennai.**. Chennai: 12 hours 55 minutes take away 11 hours 20 minutes is 1 hour 35 minutes. Srinagar: 14 hours 25 minutes take away 9 hours 55 minutes. From 9 hours 55 minutes to 10 hours is 5 minutes, then 4 hours 25 minutes more to 14 hours 25 minutes. Total 4 hours 30 minutes. That is nearly three times as much change as in Chennai. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In the malli buds test, 7 of the 10 buds on the plate were open at 8 pm, and 6 of the 10 buds in the dabba were open. Is that a big difference, or nearly the same? What would you do to be more sure? **Nearly the same: only 1 bud different out of 10. To be more sure, repeat with more buds, or on another evening.**. 7 and 6 differ by 1. With only 10 buds, one bud that was a little slow could make that difference by chance. If the dabba had 1 open and the plate 7, that would be a big difference. Repeating the test shows whether a small difference is real. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- Amur falcons cross about 4,000 kilometres of sea without stopping. If they fly about 1,000 kilometres a day, how many days and nights are they over the sea? **About 4 days.**. 4,000 divided by 1,000 is 4. Check: 1,000 + 1,000 + 1,000 + 1,000 = 4,000. The falcons cannot land on water, so they must store enough fat before they leave to last all 4 days. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the bean test, Riya gave each leaf position a number: flat is 2, half down is 1, hanging down is 0. Her window cup scored 2 at 7 am, 2 at 1 pm, 1 at 6 pm and 0 at 9 pm. Draw these as a line graph. What do you predict for 7 am the next day? **A line that stays high in the morning and afternoon, then falls in the evening to 0 at night. The next morning should be 2 again.**. A line graph shows change over time. The line is flat at 2 in the day, drops to 1 at 6 pm and to 0 at 9 pm. Because the leaves move on a daily rhythm, the pattern repeats, so at 7 am the next day the score should be back to 2. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
