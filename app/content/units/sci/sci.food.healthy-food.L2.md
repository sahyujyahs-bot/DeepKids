# Healthy food: foods that help us grow, give us energy and protect us
`sci.food.healthy-food.L2`

**Summary.** Stir a little spit into thick rice-flour paste, and in ten minutes the paste turns runny. Stir in water instead, and it stays thick. Spit starts cutting starch into sugar, and that is how rice, ragi and chapati give us energy. Your child sorts foods by the three jobs they do: help us grow, give us energy, and protect us. They also find out why sweets and fried snacks are treats, good in small amounts.

**Intro.** *A plain chapati does not taste sweet. But chew a piece for a long time, and it does start to taste a little sweet. Why would that happen?* Many children notice that a chewed chapati starts to taste a little sweet. Something in your mouth is changing the food. In this unit we test what spit does to rice-flour paste. We find out why food gives us energy. We also sort foods by their jobs: some help us grow, some give us energy, and some protect us from getting ill. And we ask why sweets are treats, not every-meal foods.

## Learning outcomes

1. By the end I can name two foods for each job, from meals we eat at home.
2. By the end I can show that my spit makes thick starch paste runny, and say that it is cutting starch into sugar.
3. By the end I can say why sweets, fried snacks and sweet drinks are treats, good in small amounts.
4. By the end I can say that one food often does more than one job.
5. By the end I can make a guess, test it fairly, and say what I saw.

## Terms
- **Energy**: Energy is what your body uses to move, think, keep warm and grow. *e.g.* You use energy when you run up the stairs of your building.
- **Grow foods**: Grow foods help your body build and mend itself: muscles, skin, hair and blood. Scientists call the main building part protein. *e.g.* Dal, chana, milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fish and groundnuts are grow foods.
- **Energy foods**: Energy foods give your body most of the energy it uses. Grains, potatoes, sugar, ghee and oil are energy foods. *e.g.* Rice, ragi, chapati, idli, poha and potatoes are energy foods.
- **Protect foods**: Protect foods help your body stay well and fight illness. They give tiny amounts of helpers called vitamins and minerals. *e.g.* Greens, carrots, tomatoes, guavas, amla, bananas and papaya are protect foods.
- **Starch**: Starch is the main food stored in grains and potatoes. It is made of many sugar pieces joined in long chains. *e.g.* Rice, ragi, wheat and potatoes are full of starch.
- **Saliva**: Saliva is the proper word for spit. It wets your food and starts breaking it down. *e.g.* Your mouth fills with saliva when you smell hot dosa.
- **Treat**: A treat is a food we have now and then, in a small amount. Treats have lots of sugar, fat or salt and few helpers. *e.g.* A laddu at Diwali, a piece of mysorepak, or a packet of chips on a trip.

## Explanations
### Food has three main jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Food helps your body grow and mend, gives it energy, and protects it from illness.

Your body grows taller every year, mends a cut finger, and runs, thinks and keeps warm. Food does all of this. Grow foods like dal, milk, eggs and fish give protein, the main building part of muscle, skin and blood. Energy foods like rice, ragi, chapati and potatoes give most of your energy. Protect foods like greens, vegetables and fruit give vitamins and minerals, which help you stay well.
- e.g. An idli with sambar and a banana gives energy from the idli, grow food from the dal in the sambar, and protect food from the vegetables and banana.
- e.g. Poha with peanuts, peas and a squeeze of lemon is a breakfast with all three jobs.
- e.g. Fish curry and rice, eaten in Bengal and Kerala, gives grow food and energy food. A side of greens adds protect food.
### How energy foods give us energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy foods are full of starch, and starch is long chains of sugar. Your body cuts the chains into sugar and uses the sugar for energy.

Rice and chapati do not taste sweet, but their starch is made of sugar pieces joined in long chains. Saliva, or spit, has a helper called amylase that cuts the chains into short pieces of sugar. That is why chapati tastes a little sweet if you chew it for a long time. It is also why spit made the thick paste runny in our test. Your gut finishes the job, and the sugar goes into your blood for every part of you to use.
- e.g. A piece of chapati chewed thirty times starts to taste a little sweet.
- e.g. Thick rice-flour paste with spit stirred in goes runny in about ten minutes.
- e.g. Ragi mudde, a ball of cooked ragi flour eaten in Karnataka, is mostly starch, so it is an energy food.
### Treats in small amounts · *defined*
**Definition:** Treats give lots of energy from sugar or fat, but little to help you grow or protect you. So they are best now and then, in small amounts.

Sweets, sweet drinks, chips and fried snacks taste very good and give a lot of energy quickly. But they carry few grow or protect helpers. If treats fill your tummy, there is less room for foods that build and protect you. Sugar also stays on your teeth, where tiny living beings called bacteria feed on the sugar and make acid. So a treat is for sometimes, like payasam at Onam or a sweet at Durga Puja.
- e.g. Many sweet fizzy drinks have about 4 to 5 teaspoons of sugar in one 200 ml glass.
- e.g. A small plate of murukku at Diwali is a treat. A plate every day after school is not.
- e.g. A guava gives sweetness and protect helpers together. A packet of sweets gives only the sweetness.
### One food can do more than one job · *idea*
Most foods are a mix. Milk gives protein to grow, fat and sugar for energy, and calcium for bones and teeth. Dal gives protein and starch. Groundnuts give protein and lots of fat. So when we call dal a grow food, we mean growing is the job it does best. Nutrition scientists sort food in many ways, and they do not all use the same groups.
- e.g. Milk helps you grow, gives energy, and keeps bones strong.
- e.g. Rajma and chana give protein for growing, and starch for energy.
- e.g. Ragi gives energy, and also a lot of calcium for bones.
- **Teachers and doctors who use three food groups:** Grow, energy and protect are easy to remember, and they help a family plan a meal with a bit of everything.
- **Nutrition scientists:** Real foods are mixtures. They count the protein, starch, fat, vitamins and minerals in each food, and use more than three groups.

## Mostly agreed
- The body needs protein to grow and mend, energy to move and keep warm, and vitamins and minerals to stay well. *How we know:* Children who do not get enough protein grow slowly. People who go without certain vitamins fall ill in ways doctors know well, and get better when those foods are added back.
- Saliva starts breaking down starch in the mouth. *How we know:* Saliva has an enzyme called amylase, which cuts starch into sugars. You can see it thin a starch paste, and taste it in a chapati chewed for a long time.
- Lots of sugar, often, harms teeth. *How we know:* Bacteria in the mouth feed on sugar and make acid. Dentists find more holes in the teeth of people who have sweet food and drink often.

## How we found out
**Lucy Wills, an English doctor and scientist, working in Bombay with women patients, at the invitation of Dr Margaret Balfour, She began in Bombay in 1928 and reported the yeast cure in 1931. Folic acid was named in 1941..** In 1928, an English doctor and scientist called Lucy Wills came to Bombay, now Mumbai. Another doctor, Margaret Balfour, had asked her to help. Many young women who worked in the cotton mills were very ill when they were expecting babies. Their blood was weak and pale, a kind of illness called anaemia. Some doctors thought it was the same as another kind of anaemia. Then she looked at what the women ate. The poorest women ate very few vegetables, fruit, milk or eggs. Wills fed rats and monkeys meals like the women's, and they became anaemic too. When she added a cheap paste made from yeast, the animals got better. So did the women. The cure was in the food. Years later, scientists found the helper she had found. They called it folic acid, from the Latin word for leaf, because green leaves like spinach are full of it.  
*What people believed before:* That the anaemia of these women was the same illness as another known anaemia. Wills showed it came from what was missing in their food, and that a food could cure it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Rice is bad for you, and you should stop eating it.** (Some diet advice online; *rejected*)  
  For: White rice gives energy quickly, and eating very large amounts with little else is not healthy.  
  Against: Rice has fed families in South and East India for thousands of years. It gives energy, and some protein. Eaten with dal, vegetables and curd, rice is part of a healthy meal. How much you eat, and what you eat with it, matters more than the rice itself.
- **Fruit juice is as good as eating the fruit.** (A common belief; *debated*)  
  For: Juice comes from fruit, and fresh juice does have vitamins in it.  
  Against: Squeezing leaves the pulp and skin behind, and much of the fibre goes with them. A glass of juice holds the sugar of several fruits, and goes down fast. Doctors advise eating whole fruit more often than drinking juice.
- **Sweet foods are the only foods that give us sugar.** (What many children and grown-ups think; *rejected*)  
  For: Sweets taste of sugar, and plain rice and chapati do not.  
  Against: Starch in rice, ragi, wheat and potatoes is made of sugar pieces in chains. Your body cuts the chains, so these foods give sugar too. The spit test shows it starting in the mouth.

## Roots
We have watched spit turn thick rice paste runny, and learned that starchy foods become sugar inside us. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your spit turned the thick rice paste runny, but water did not. Spit cuts starch into sugar. Rice and ragi do not taste sweet, yet they become sugar inside you. So you cannot tell which foods give you sugar just by tasting them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort food by taste, into sweet and not sweet, and think only sweet foods give us sugar. But taste tells us only what reaches the tongue now, not what the body makes of the food.  
*Evidence:* In the spit test, the rice paste with spit dripped off the spoon almost at once after ten minutes, while the paste with water stayed thick. Curd with spit stayed thick too, because curd is not thickened by starch. A chapati chewed thirty times starts to taste a little sweet.

- *who am i:* Your body is built from the food you eat. The dal you ate last week is part of your muscles now.
- *what is life:* Living beings take food and change it into themselves. Your spit, your gut and every cell in you are doing this all day.
- *what is this world:* The rice on your plate grew from sunlight, rain and soil. Inside you, it becomes the energy you run and think with.

Sunlight falls on a rice plant, and the plant makes sugar and joins the sugar into starch in the grain. You eat the rice, and your spit and gut cut the starch back into sugar. Your cells use that sugar to move, think and grow. The energy that left the Sun is the same energy you run with. Your cells sense how much sugar they have and decide whether to use it or store it, so thinking is not only something brains do. Many scientists call this an automatic chemical response.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1 to 2.2:** The Upanishad describes the body as the 'self made of food'. It says that all beings come from food, live on food, and go back into food. (In the spit test you saw food starting to change inside you. What you eat really does become your body.)
- **Thirukkural 942, by Thiruvalluvar:** The body needs no medicine if you eat only after the last meal has been digested. (Digesting starts in the mouth, as the spit test showed. Eating in a measured way, and not filling up on treats, lets the body do its work.)
- Science (settled): Saliva has an enzyme called amylase that cuts starch into sugar. Digestion starts in the mouth.
- Science (debated): People have different numbers of copies of the gene for spit amylase. Groups with starch-rich traditional diets often have more copies. Whether this changes their health is still argued about.
- Other view, Many nutrition scientists: They say 'it all becomes sugar' is too simple. Sugar from a whole fruit, starch in whole grains, and sugar in a sweet drink reach the blood at different speeds, with different helpers alongside. So the form a food comes in still matters.
- Practice, Chew until it changes (3 min): Take one small piece of plain chapati or a spoon of plain rice. Close your eyes. Chew it slowly, thirty times, and do not swallow yet. Notice the taste at the start. Notice the taste at the end. Your mouth is changing this food. Soon it will be part of you.
- Ask: When you chew a chapati, when does it stop being chapati and start being you?
- Ask: If rice tastes plain but becomes sugar inside you, what else might be different from how it seems?
- Ask: Your cells decide whether to use sugar or store it. Is that a kind of thinking?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your spit started to cut the rice paste before you even swallowed it. When does food stop being food and start being you? (hint: Think about the mouth, the tummy, the blood, and the new skin on a healed cut.)
- *deep:* A chapati does not taste sweet, but your body turns it into sugar. What else might be different from how it tastes or looks? (hint: Think of milk, which looks like water but holds grow, energy and protect helpers.)
- *big:* Why do you think people everywhere make special sweets for festivals? (hint: Think of Pongal, Onam, Eid, Christmas and Diwali. What do the sweets mean to the family?)
- *big:* A cow can grow big and strong on grass. Why can't you? (hint: Think about the tiny living beings in a cow's stomach that help her break grass down.)

## Story: The Payasam That Went Thin
*Lakshmi, Kannan and their family are made up. Payasam is a real sweet dish served at an Onam sadya. It really does go thin if a licked spoon is put back in, when it is thickened with rice or other starch.*

Lakshmi was seven. Every year at Onam, her family went to Ammamma's house in Kochi. Ammamma was her grandmother. This year her cousin Kannan came too. Kannan was six, and he had never been to a sadya before.

A sadya is a big Onam feast, served on a banana leaf. In the morning, the whole house smelled of coconut and ghee. Ammamma stood at the stove, stirring a big uruli of rice payasam. The payasam was thick and creamy, the colour of pale gold.

'Can I taste it?' asked Kannan.

Ammamma laughed. She poured a little payasam into a small steel bowl and gave it to him with a spoon. 'This bowl is yours,' she said. 'But never put your spoon back in my big uruli. Do you know why?'

Kannan shook his head.

'Lakshmi knows,' said Ammamma.

Lakshmi did not know. She thought hard. Maybe it was about germs? Ammamma only smiled and went back to her stirring.

Kannan sat on the step with his bowl. He took a spoonful and licked the spoon clean. Then he dipped the spoon back into his bowl and took another. He licked it again and dipped it again. He was eating very slowly, because he wanted the payasam to last.

After a while, Lakshmi came to sit next to him. She looked into his bowl. 'Kannan, your payasam has gone all thin,' she said. 'Look. It is runny, like milk.'

Kannan tipped his bowl. The payasam slid across the bottom like water. Lakshmi ran to the kitchen and looked into the big uruli. Ammamma's payasam was still thick. When she tipped a ladle, it fell off slowly, in a heavy ribbon.

'Ammamma,' said Lakshmi. 'Kannan's payasam went thin, and yours did not. They came from the same pot.'

'What did Kannan do to his?' asked Ammamma.

Lakshmi thought. 'He licked the spoon,' she said slowly. 'Then he put it back in. Again and again.' Her eyes went wide. 'Is it the spit? Does spit make payasam thin?'

'Find out,' said Ammamma. 'Then you will know it for yourself, not because I told you.'

So Lakshmi did a test. She poured two spoons of thick payasam into one bowl, and two spoons into another. Into the first bowl she stirred a spoon of plain water. Into the second bowl she stirred a little of her own spit. Kannan laughed so much that he fell over. Then they waited, and watched.

The bowl with water stayed thick. The bowl with spit grew thinner and thinner. After ten minutes, it ran like milk.

'The spit did it,' said Lakshmi. 'But how?'

Ammamma sat down with them. 'The rice in the payasam is full of starch,' she said. 'Starch is what makes it thick. Your spit has a helper in it that cuts starch into small pieces of sugar. Once the starch is cut, the payasam cannot stay thick.'

'Sugar?' said Kannan. 'My spit makes sugar?'

'It starts to,' said Ammamma. 'Chew a bit of plain rice for a long time, and you will taste it. That is how your body gets energy from rice and chapati and ragi. It cuts the starch into sugar, and uses the sugar to run and play.'

At lunch, the banana leaves were laid in a long row. Ammamma and the aunties served rice first, then parippu, then sambar, avial, thoran, pachadi, pickle and crisp banana chips. Lakshmi showed Kannan each one.

'Rice is an energy food,' she said. 'Parippu is dal, so it is a grow food. The avial and thoran are vegetables. They are protect foods. They help you stay well.'

'And the payasam?' asked Kannan.

'Payasam is a treat,' said Lakshmi. 'It is very sweet. We have it at Onam, and a little is enough.'

Kannan ate one ladle of payasam at the end. He did not lick his spoon and put it back in the pot. He grinned at Lakshmi.

That evening, Lakshmi lay on the cool floor and thought. Ammamma's payasam had looked like one thing, thick and sweet. But inside it was starch and sugar and milk. Inside her mouth, the starch had started to become sugar. And inside her body, the sugar would become running and jumping and growing. She wondered where the food stopped and she began.

**For the parent:** Lakshmi sees that Kannan's payasam went thin while Ammamma's stayed thick, and works out that the spit on his spoon did it. Her test shows that spit cuts the starch that makes payasam thick.

- Ask: Why did Kannan's payasam go thin, but not Ammamma's?
- Ask: How did Lakshmi test her idea? Why did she use one bowl with water?
- Ask: Which foods on the sadya leaf were grow foods, energy foods and protect foods?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Spit Test: what does saliva do to thick rice paste? (34 min)
- **question:** We stir water into one bowl of thick rice paste, spit into a second bowl, and spit into a bowl of curd. Which ones will go runny?
- **predict:** Look at the three bowls. Which ones do you think will go runny in ten minutes? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The two paste bowls get the same amount of paste from the same pot, the same amount of water or spit, the same number of stirs, and the same waiting time. Only one thing changes between them: water or spit. The curd bowl is a different food, so it is not a fair comparison with the paste. It shows whether spit thins everything or only starch.
- **safety:** A grown-up cooks the paste on the stove and lets it cool fully before the child touches it. Throw the paste and curd away at the end and wash the bowls with soap. Do not eat them.
- **record:** Draw the three bowls. Draw the paste in each one as thick or runny after ten minutes. Write the drip count under each bowl.
- **results table:** Bowl | My guess: runny or thick? | Spoon drip count at the start | Spoon drip count after 10 minutes | Runny or thick?
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir 1 level tablespoon of rice flour into half a small cup of cold water. 2. Heat it on low, stirring, until it turns thick and see-through at the edges. 3. Pour it into a bowl and let it cool fully.  
  *Parent says:* I am cooking rice flour in water. Watch it go thick. Rice flour is full of starch. Hot water makes starch swell and turn thick.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cooks the flour in water. Watch it turn thick.
  - Note: Aim for paste like thick custard: it should fall slowly off a spoon. If it is too stiff to pour, stir in a spoon of water. Cornflour or ragi flour work too. Best of all, cook it 20 minutes before you start, so it is cool. Or stand the bowl in a plate of cold water. Hot paste would stop the test working, and could burn.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 2 tablespoons of paste into bowl 1 and 2 tablespoons into bowl 2. 2. Put 2 tablespoons of curd into bowl 3. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Bowl 1 gets water. Bowl 2 gets your spit. Bowl 3 is curd, and gets your spit too. Which ones will go runny? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Bowl 1 gets water. Bowl 2 and bowl 3 get spit. Which will go runny? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Water makes things thin, so bowl 1.; Spit is wet, so maybe bowl 2 and 3.; Nothing will change. Spit is just water.)
  - Note: Write each guess in the table. Many children guess the water bowl will go runniest. That is a good guess, because water does thin things a little.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Before adding anything, lift a spoonful from each bowl and count how long until it drips off. 2. Write the drip counts down.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure how thick each bowl is now. Lift a spoonful, tip the spoon, and count one, two, three until it falls off.  
  *Child mode:* Lift a spoonful. Tip it. Count until it falls off.
  - Note: Count at a steady speed, about one count each second. Thick paste hangs on for a long count. Runny paste falls off at once. This gives us a number to compare later, so we are not only guessing by eye.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child looks at a lemon or tamarind to make spit come. 2. They let spit run into a clean teaspoon until it is about half full, twice. 3. Stir one lot into bowl 2 and one into bowl 3. 4. Stir half a teaspoon of water into bowl 1. 5. Stir each bowl ten times.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the lemon. Think about how sour it is. Can you feel your mouth filling with spit? Let it run into the spoon.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the lemon. Feel the spit come. Let it run into the spoon.
  - Note: Thinking about sour food really does make saliva flow. Half a teaspoon of spit in each bowl is plenty. Use a different spoon for each bowl. Set a timer for 10 minutes.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave the bowls for 10 minutes without stirring. 2. While you wait, take turns to name foods that are full of starch.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait ten minutes. No stirring. While we wait, let's name foods full of starch. I say rice. Your turn.  
  *Child mode:* Wait ten minutes. Do not stir. Name foods full of starch.
  - Note: Starchy foods include rice, ragi, wheat, jowar, bajra, potato, sweet potato, tapioca, idli, dosa, poha and bread.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir each bowl twice. 2. Do the spoon drip count again for each bowl. 3. Tip each bowl gently and watch how the paste moves.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count again. Which bowl has changed? What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Count again for each bowl. Which one changed?
  - Ask: What happened to each bowl? (good: The spit paste went runny.; The water paste stayed thick.; The curd stayed thick even with spit.)
  - Note: The paste with spit is usually much thinner after 10 minutes, and runs across the bowl when tipped. The water paste stays almost the same. The curd stays thick, because curd is thick from milk protein, not starch. If the spit bowl has hardly changed, wait 10 more minutes and count again. People's spit works at different speeds.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what they think, what they saw, and why. Then tell them about amylase. Throw the paste and curd away and wash the bowls.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think spit does? What did you see that tells you? Spit has a helper called amylase. It cuts starch into sugar.  
  *Child mode:* What did spit do? Spit cuts starch into sugar. That gives you energy.
  - Note: A strong answer has a claim, evidence and a reason. For example: 'Spit changes starch. The spit paste dripped off in 1 count, but the water paste took 7. The curd did not change, so spit works on starch.'
  - Check: Which bowl went runny? → **Rice paste with spit**

### Three jobs, and the spit test (core, 10 questions)
1. (sort) Colour each food by its main job: red for grow, yellow for go, green for protect.  
   **Answer:** dal → Grow; rice → Go; carrot → Protect; egg → Grow; ragi mudde → Go; guava → Protect. *Why:* Dal and egg are rich in protein, for growing. Rice and ragi are mostly starch, for energy. Carrot and guava give vitamins that protect.
2. (match) Draw a line from each job to what it does for you.  
   **Answer:** Grow foods → Build and mend muscle, skin and blood; Go foods → Give energy to run and think; Protect foods → Help you stay well. *Why:* Each kind of food is best at one job, though many foods help with more than one.
3. (truefalse) A chapati gives you sugar, even though it does not taste sweet.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A chapati is mostly starch, and starch is sugar pieces in chains. Your body cuts the chains into sugar.
4. (odd) Three of these are go foods, full of starch. Which one is not?  
   Options: Idli / Potato / Paneer / Chapati  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Paneer is mostly protein and fat from milk, so it is a grow food. Idli, potato and chapati are full of starch.
5. (write) Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it: why did we put water in one bowl in the spit test?  
   **Answer:** To check that it was the spit, not just the wetness, that made the paste runny.. *Why:* A strong answer says the water bowl is there to compare. If the water bowl had gone runny too, it would show that any wet thing thins the paste.
6. (perspective) Priya, a girl of 7 in Chennai, and her brother Vikram, who is 5, are looking at a packet of chips.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, for example: 'Priya, because frying adds lots of oil, and chips have lots of salt.' Then it is fair to Vikram: chips really are made from potato, and they do give energy.. *Why:* Both are partly right. Chips start as potato, a go food. But frying and salt change them, so they give lots of energy and salt and few protect helpers.
7. (label) Here is a plate of idli, sambar and a banana. Write G, E or P next to each food.  
   **Answer:** E next to the idli, G next to the sambar, P next to the banana. The banana could also get an E.. *Why:* Idli is mostly rice starch. Sambar is made with dal, a grow food, and vegetables. A banana gives protect helpers and sugar for energy.
8. (mcq) Aruna, a girl of 7, ate a big plate of sweets and now does not want her dinner. What is the problem?  
   Options: Sweets filled her up, so there is no room for grow and protect foods / Sweets have too much protein / There is no problem  
   **Answer:** Sweets filled her up, so there is no room for grow and protect foods. *Why:* Sweets give lots of energy but few grow or protect helpers. When they fill the tummy, the body misses the foods that build and protect it.
9. (mcq) Tara, a girl in Class 2, says: 'Only sweet foods give us sugar.' What would you tell her?  
   Options: Rice and chapati give sugar too, because the body cuts their starch into sugar / Tara is right / Sugar comes only from sugarcane  
   **Answer:** Rice and chapati give sugar too, because the body cuts their starch into sugar. *Why:* Starch is sugar pieces joined in chains. Spit and the gut cut the chains, so starchy foods give sugar even when they do not taste sweet.
10. (truefalse) A guava can taste sweet and still be a protect food.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A guava has some sugar, and lots of vitamin C. Taste does not tell you a food's job.
### Thinking about food jobs (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (write) Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it: why does a chapati start to taste sweet if you chew it for a long time?  
   **Answer:** Spit cuts the starch in the chapati into sugar, and we can taste the sugar.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the starch was there all along, and spit turns some of it into sugar while you chew.
2. (perspective) Kavya, a girl of 8 in Mysuru, and her grandmother are talking about milk.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason, for example: 'Grandmother, because milk has protein, fat and sugar, and calcium too.' Then it is fair to Kavya: growing is the job milk is best known for.. *Why:* Most foods do more than one job. Sorting by the main job is a handy short cut, but it hides the rest.
3. (draw) Draw a thali with one grow food, one go food, one protect food and one small treat. Label each one.  
   **Answer:** Any thali with a food for each job and one small treat, labelled.. *Why:* A strong drawing has a real food for each job and keeps the treat small.
4. (fill) Rice and chapati are full of ______. Spit cuts it into ______.

9. (write) Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it: Ramya, a girl in Class 2, ate only rice for lunch. Which jobs are missing, and what could she add?

2. (mcq) Which of these is a grow food?  
   Options: Rice / Dal / Jalebi

   **Answer:** Spinach. *Why:* Spinach and other greens give vitamins and minerals that help you stay well.

   Options: Spit cuts starch into sugar / Spit is colder than water / Spit is thicker than water

## Applications
- Doctors and nurses check what young children eat, and give extra grow foods to children who are growing slowly.
- Schools across India serve a midday meal of rice or roti with dal and vegetables, so children get all three jobs at lunch.
- Cooks thicken payasam, gravies and kheer with starch, and food factories use amylase to turn starch into sweet syrups.
- India adds iodine to salt, and many states add iron to rice in schemes for school meals, to protect people from illness.

## Open questions
- **Why does some people's spit have more amylase than others'?** People have different numbers of copies of the gene for spit amylase. Some scientists found that groups with starch-rich traditional diets often have more copies. How much this matters for health is still argued about.
- **How does the body know how much of each food it needs?** Hunger, fullness and cravings come from signals between the gut, the blood and the brain. Many of these signals have been found, but how they all work together is still being studied.
- **Why do sweet things taste so good?** Babies like sweet tastes from birth. One idea is that sweetness once meant a safe food full of energy. Scientists still argue about how much our love of sweet is born in us and how much we learn.

## Connected maths
- In the spit test, the rice paste with water took 8 counts to drip off the spoon at the start, and 7 counts after ten minutes. The paste with spit took 8 counts at the start and 1 count after ten minutes. Which paste changed more? **The paste with spit. It went from 8 counts to 1 count. The water paste went only from 8 to 7.**. 8 take away 1 is 7, so the spit paste dropped 7 counts. 8 take away 7 is 1, so the water paste dropped only 1 count. 7 is much more than 1. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Tumbler A of nimbu pani has 1 spoon of sugar. Tumbler B has 4 spoons. A glass of sweet fizzy drink has about 5 spoons. How many more spoons are in the fizzy drink than in tumbler A? **4 more spoons.**. Count on from 1 to 5: 2, 3, 4, 5. That is 4 more spoons. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In a week of plates, Meera, a girl in Class 2, drew 12 yellow foods, 6 red foods and 3 green foods. She wants as many green as red next week. How many more green foods does she need? **3 more green foods.**. She had 3 green and 6 red. 6 take away 3 is 3. So 3 more green foods would make them equal. (queues `math.data.pictographs.L2`)
- In the Grow, Go or Protect game, Farhana, a girl in Class 2, sorted 20 cards. 7 were grow, 5 were go, and 6 were protect. The rest were treats. How many treat cards were there? **2 treat cards.**. 7 and 5 make 12. 12 and 6 make 18. 20 take away 18 is 2. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)

Saliva quickly lowers the thickness of starch-thickened foods: studies of starch-thickened drinks for people with swallowing difficulty found saliva thins them within minutes (for example B. Hanson and others, Dysphagia, 2012).
- Copy number of the salivary amylase gene AMY1 varies between people, and is higher on average in groups with starch-rich traditional diets: G. H. Perry and others, Nature Genetics, 2007. Its health effects are debated.
- Thinking of or seeing sour food increases saliva flow (a well-known reflex).
- Sugar in many sweet fizzy drinks: about 10 to 11 g per 100 ml. A level teaspoon of sugar is about 4 g. WHO guideline on sugars intake for adults and children, 2015: free sugars below 10% of energy, ideally below 5%.
- Lucy Wills (1888 to 1964), haematologist, worked in India from 1928, mostly at the Haffkine Institute, Bombay, on the anaemia of pregnancy among poor women textile workers, at the invitation of Dr Margaret Balfour. She tested diets in rats at Coonoor and in monkeys, and found that the yeast extract Marmite cured the anaemia (British Medical Journal, 1931). The 'Wills factor' was later shown to be folate. Folic acid was named in 1941 by H. K. Mitchell, E. E. Snell and R. J. Williams, from Latin folium, leaf, after they extracted it from spinach.
- Folate, iron, vitamin A and vitamin C sources: Indian Food Composition Tables, National Institute of Nutrition, Hyderabad, 2017. Vitamin C is partly lost with long cooking.
- Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1 to 2.2 (Brahmananda Valli): the person made of food, annamaya, and the praise of food. Thirukkural 942 (chapter 95, Medicine): the body needs no medicine if one eats only after what was eaten before has been digested.
