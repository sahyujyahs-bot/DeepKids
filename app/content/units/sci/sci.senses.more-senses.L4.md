# Balance as a team: how eyes, ears and feet keep you upright, and the senses other animals have
`sci.senses.more-senses.L4`

**Summary.** Stand on one foot with your eyes shut on a folded blanket, and you wobble far more than on the bare floor with your eyes open. You are the same child with the same ears, so what changed? Balance is not one sense in one place. Your eyes, your inner ears and the stretch sensors in your legs each give a rough answer, and your brain mixes them. Your child tests this by taking one sense away at a time, reads a page while shaking head and then page, and finds that a fingertip lighter than a tomato can steady a whole body. Then the unit follows the same sensing ideas into warmth, pain and the heartbeat, and into flies that steer with tiny clubs, snakes that see heat, and fish that carry ear stones.

**Intro.** *You sit in a train at the station. The train on the next track starts to move slowly. For a moment you feel your own train sliding backwards, though nothing under you has moved. Who fooled you: your eyes or your body?* Your eyes said you were moving. Your inner ears and the seat said you were still. For a second your brain believed your eyes, and then it checked the other signals and put things right. This shows that balance is a team of senses that can disagree. In this unit you take one member of the team away and see what happens. You test how your eyes stay steady when your head shakes. You find out how a fingertip can steady you, how the skin senses warmth and cold, why pain changes with attention, and how a fly keeps steady with two tiny clubs.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the three sources of information my brain uses for balance, and say what each one tells me.
1. I can explain, using my own test, why taking one sense away makes me wobble more but does not make me fall.
2. I can explain how my inner ears keep my eyes steady when my head moves, using my reading test as evidence.
3. I can explain why a moving train beside me can make me feel I am moving, and how this links to feeling sick in a car.
4. I can explain how my skin senses warmth and cold with separate gates, and how my body keeps its temperature steady with a sensing loop.
5. I can explain why there are two kinds of pain, and why pain can change when I pay attention to something else.
6. I can describe a sense that another animal has, say what problem the sense solves, and say how the sense is like one I have.
7. I can plan a fair test that changes one thing at a time, give a guess with a reason, and use what I found to explain the result.

## Terms
- **Balance team**: The three sources of information your brain mixes to know whether you are upright. They are your eyes, your inner ears, and the sensors in the skin, muscles and joints of your legs and feet. *e.g.* On a dark staircase your eyes help little, so your ears and feet do more of the work.
- **Vestibular system**: The balance organs deep inside each inner ear. They have three liquid-filled canals that feel turning, and two pouches of ear crystals that feel tilting and speeding up. *e.g.* When a lift starts to go up, your vestibular system feels it, even with the doors shut.
- **Semicircular canal**: One of three liquid-filled loops in each inner ear. When your head turns, the liquid lags behind and bends tiny hairs, and the hairs send signals to your brain. *e.g.* Shaking your head to say no moves the liquid in one canal.
- **Ear crystals**: Tiny crystals of calcium carbonate, the same stuff as chalk, resting on jelly over hair cells in the inner ear. Gravity pulls on them, so they tell you which way is down. Scientists call them otoliths. *e.g.* When you tilt your head, the crystals slide a little and bend the hairs under them.
- **Head-turn reflex**: A very quick movement of your eyes in the opposite direction to your head turn, which keeps what you are looking at steady. It starts in the inner ear. Scientists call it the vestibulo-ocular reflex. *e.g.* You can read a sign while your head bobs on a bumpy auto ride because of this reflex.
- **Nystagmus**: Fast flicking of the eyes from side to side that happens by itself. It happens after spinning, because the brain thinks the head is still turning. *e.g.* A grown-up can see your eyes flick for a few seconds after you stop spinning.
- **Body sensors**: The stretch and pressure sensors in your skin, muscles, tendons and joints. They tell your brain how your body parts are placed and what the ground feels like. Scientists call this the somatosensory system. *e.g.* The soles of your feet tell you if the floor is hard, soft or tilted.
- **Sway**: The small, constant movements of a standing body, forwards, backwards and sideways, as the body keeps itself upright. Everyone sways, even when they feel perfectly still. *e.g.* If you hold a cup of water while standing, the surface trembles a little because you sway.
- **Reweighting**: Listening more to the senses that are working well and less to the ones that are not. The brain does this without you choosing. *e.g.* In a dark room your brain listens more to your feet and ears.
- **Sensory conflict**: A moment when two of your senses send messages that do not match, so the brain does not know which to believe. *e.g.* Reading in a bus, your eyes say you are still and your ears say you are moving.
- **Vection**: The feeling that you are moving when only the world you see is moving. *e.g.* When the train beside you pulls out, you may feel your own train slide the other way.
- **Light touch**: Resting one fingertip on something still, pressing so lightly that it could not hold you up. Even so, it makes you steadier. *e.g.* Resting one fingertip on a wall helps you stand in the dark.
- **Thermoreceptor**: A nerve ending that reacts to warmth or cold. Warm ones and cold ones are different, and sit in different places. *e.g.* Cold thermoreceptors in your lips fire when you sip cold water.
- **Ion channel**: A tiny gate in the wall of a nerve cell. When a gate opens, charged particles rush in and the nerve sends a signal. Some gates open when it is warm, some when it is cool, some for chilli or mint. *e.g.* The gate that opens for chilli is the same gate that opens when something is too hot.
- **Core temperature**: The heat of the deep parts of your body. It is kept close to 37 degrees Celsius, whether the day is hot or cold. *e.g.* A thermometer under your tongue reads close to your core temperature.
- **Feedback loop**: A loop in which a sensor checks something, and then the body acts to bring it back to the level it should be. The result is checked again, and again. *e.g.* You get too warm, you sweat, you cool down, and then you stop sweating.
- **Fast pain and slow pain**: Two kinds of pain signal. The fast one is sharp and tells you where you were hurt. The slow one is dull and throbbing and lasts longer. *e.g.* When you stub your toe, you feel a sharp jab first, and then a throb a moment later.
- **Interoception**: The senses that look inward. They tell you about your heartbeat, breathing, hunger, thirst and how full you are. *e.g.* You can feel your heart pounding after you run.
- **Haltere**: A tiny club-shaped stalk behind each wing of a fly or a mosquito. It swings up and down as the wings beat, and it senses when the body turns. *e.g.* A housefly that has lost her halteres cannot fly in a straight line.
- **Heat pit**: A small hollow on the face of some snakes, covered by a thin skin that senses warmth from a distance. It lets the snake sense the heat of a warm animal in the dark. *e.g.* A python has a row of heat pits along her lips.
- **Ear stone**: A small hard stone in a fish's inner ear. It rests on sensing hairs and helps the fish know which way is up and when she is moving. It grows a thin new layer every day. *e.g.* Scientists count the layers in a fish's ear stone to learn how old she is.

## Explanations
### Balance is a team of three · *defined*
**Definition:** Your brain knows whether you are upright by mixing three sources: your eyes, your inner ears, and the sensors in your skin, muscles and joints.

Each source tells the brain something different. Your eyes show where the horizon, the walls and the floor are. Your inner ears feel your head turn, tilt and speed up. The sensors in your feet and legs feel the ground under you and how your joints are bent. None of the three is perfect. Eyes do not work in the dark. Inner ears cannot tell a steady lift from standing still. Feet are poor guides on sand or a soft mattress. So the brain compares all three, many times each second, and makes small corrections to your muscles. A doctor called Moritz Romberg, in the 1840s, noticed that some patients stood steadily with their eyes open, but swayed or fell when they shut their eyes. Doctors still use his test today.
- e.g. Walking down a dark staircase in a power cut uses your ears and feet more, because your eyes help very little.
- e.g. Standing in a moving bus with your eyes shut is harder than with your eyes open, because one member of the team is missing.
- e.g. Walking on a sandy beach feels more tiring than on a road, because the soft sand makes the foot sensors less sure.
### What happens when one member of the team is taken away · *defined*
**Definition:** When one source of balance information is missing or unreliable, the brain relies more on the other two. This is called reweighting. You wobble more, but you usually do not fall.

In the 1970s and 1980s, the researcher Lewis Nashner and others built floors that tilted with the person's own sway, and rooms whose walls moved with the person's sway. These tricks made one source of information useless, because it no longer matched the real tilt of the body. Healthy people wobbled more at first. Then, after a few tries, they began to rely on the other sources, and they steadied again. A person who shuts their eyes on a soft surface loses two sources at once, and has only the ears left. That is the hardest case, and most people wobble a great deal. People who have lost the use of their inner ears can still stand quite well on a hard floor with their eyes open. They often fall in the dark, or on soft ground. This shows that no single member of the team holds you up alone. In a space station there is no down, so the ear crystals float on their jelly and the ears give odd messages. Many astronauts feel sick at first, until the brain adjusts.
- e.g. With your eyes shut on a firm floor, you wobble a little more, but you can stand.
- e.g. On a folded blanket with your eyes open, your eyes make up for the feet.
- e.g. On a folded blanket with your eyes shut, only your ears are left, and most people wobble a lot.
### The head-turn reflex: how your eyes stay on target · *defined*
**Definition:** When your head turns, the canals in your inner ear send a signal that moves your eyes the opposite way by almost the same amount, so the picture on your eye stays steady. It is called the vestibulo-ocular reflex.

Imagine filming a video while walking. The picture jerks with every step. Your own eyes would do the same if nothing steadied them. The canals feel the head turn, and within about a hundredth of a second they send a signal to the eye muscles. The eyes swing the opposite way, so the thing you are looking at stays still on your eye. This is faster than anything you could do on purpose. When you follow a moving page with your eyes on purpose, the tracking is much slower, and it cannot keep up with a quick shake. That is why you can read while you shake your head, and cannot read while the page shakes. The same reflex explains why your eyes flick after spinning. The liquid in the canals keeps swirling after you stop, and it keeps telling the eyes to move.
- e.g. You can read a signboard while your head bobs on a bumpy bus ride.
- e.g. A dancer who spins can whip her head round and still keep her eyes on one spot.
- e.g. After spinning, you cannot read a page, because your eyes are flicking by themselves.
### Moving rooms and moving trains: when the senses disagree · *defined*
**Definition:** If your eyes say you are moving but your ears and feet say you are still, the brain may believe your eyes. This is called vection. It is one kind of sensory conflict.

In 1974, the researchers David Lee and Eric Aronson built a room with a floor that stayed still and walls and a ceiling that could slide forwards and backwards. Toddlers who stood in this room swayed or fell when the walls moved, though the floor did not. Their eyes told them the room was moving, and they corrected for it. The train on the next track does the same to you. Sensory conflict also seems to be involved in feeling sick in a car or on a boat. If you read in a car, your eyes say you are still, while your ears feel every turn and bump. The brain does not know whom to believe. Looking out of the window, at the horizon, makes the messages agree again. Scientists still argue about the exact cause of travel sickness, so this is the best-known idea, not the last word.
- e.g. The train beside yours pulls out, and you feel as if you are sliding backwards.
- e.g. A cinema ride that tilts the picture makes you lean, though your seat is still.
- e.g. Looking at the road ahead through the front window can ease sickness in a car.
### A fingertip that steadies a whole body · *defined*
**Definition:** Resting one fingertip on a still object, with a force of less than about 1 newton, makes people sway less, even with their eyes shut. The touch gives the brain a fourth source of information.

In 1994, two researchers, John Jeka and James Lackner, asked people to stand in a difficult pose, with their feet in a line, one in front of the other. They found that a fingertip resting on a rail steadied people a great deal, even when the force of the touch was less than 1 newton. One newton is about the weight of a small tomato. That is far too small to hold up a standing person. So the touch does not work as a prop. It works as a sense. A still wall under the fingertip tells the brain exactly how the body is moving compared with the room. Studies since then have reported the same in older people, and a walking stick held lightly can help in the same way. A stick that you lean on is a support. A stick that you only touch is a sensor.
- e.g. Resting one fingertip on a wall helps you stand on one foot with your eyes shut.
- e.g. A person who walks with a stick touches the ground lightly, as a cat uses her whiskers.
- e.g. Holding the rail on a moving bus helps even when you hardly press on it.
### Ear crystals that wander · *defined*
**Definition:** Sometimes a few ear crystals break loose from their pouch and drift into one of the canals. They make the brain think the head is turning, and cause a short spell of spinning.

The ear crystals are meant to stay on their jelly. In some people, mostly adults, a few crystals come loose and roll into a canal. When the person tips their head in a certain way, the crystals slide and push the liquid, just as if the head were turning fast. The person feels the room spin for about a minute. Doctors call this benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, which means a harmless spell of spinning that comes with a head position. A doctor can cure it by moving the patient's head through a set of positions that rolls the crystals out of the canal and back into the pouch. One well-known set of moves was described by the American doctor John Epley in 1992. Children rarely get it. If a child has spells of spinning for no reason, a doctor should check.
- e.g. Rolling over in bed makes the room spin for a few seconds, then it stops.
- e.g. Looking up quickly to take a coconut from a shelf makes an older person dizzy for a moment.
- e.g. A doctor guides the head through four positions to move the crystals back.
### Warm and cold gates in the skin · *defined*
**Definition:** Warmth and cold are sensed by tiny gates in nerve endings called ion channels. Each kind of gate opens at its own temperature, so different nerve endings report different ranges.

In 1997, the scientist David Julius and his team found the gate that opens in chilli and in heat. It opens when the temperature rises above about 43 degrees Celsius, and also when it meets the chemical in chilli, which is called capsaicin. So a green chilli really does feel hot, because it opens the same gate as hot water would. In 2002, a team led by David McKemy found a gate that opens in the cool and also opens for menthol (later work put its cool point at below about 26 degrees), the chemical in mint. That is why mint feels cool even though it is not cold. Other gates in the same family open at warm, hot, or very cold temperatures. Julius and Ardem Patapoutian shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for finding the gates for temperature and touch. Your sense of warmth is the combined report of many such gates.
- e.g. Chilli feels hot because it opens the heat gate.
- e.g. A peppermint sweet makes your breath feel cool because mint opens the cool gate.
- e.g. Mustard oil makes the nose sting because it opens another gate, one that warns of harm.
### More skin, more warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain adds up warmth from all the places it is felt. The same warmth feels stronger when it touches a larger part of your skin. This is called spatial summation.

Dip a fingertip into bath-warm water, and it feels pleasantly mild. Dip your whole hand into the same water, and it feels warmer. The water has not changed, but many more warm gates have opened, and the brain adds their reports. This is why you test a baby's bath with your elbow or wrist. A larger area of skin gives a clearer message than a fingertip. It is also why a hot water bottle spread over your tummy feels very hot, and a hot spoon touching one finger feels less strong than it would if the whole hand held it. It is a clue that your sense of warmth is not a thermometer. It is a sum of many reports.
- e.g. A bath feels hotter to a whole foot than to one toe.
- e.g. A big sip of hot tea feels hotter than a tiny drop of the same tea.
- e.g. A warm hand over your cheek feels warmer than one finger on your cheek.
### Keeping warm and cool: sensing is half of a loop · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body keeps its core temperature close to 37 degrees Celsius with a feedback loop. Sensors in the skin and deep in the body measure the temperature, and the brain makes the body warm itself or cool itself.

A part of the brain called the hypothalamus works like a thermostat. It gets reports from temperature sensors in the skin and from the blood passing through it. If your core gets too warm, the brain makes you sweat. Sweat takes heat away as it dries. It also widens the blood vessels near the skin, so more heat can leave. If your core gets too cool, the brain narrows those blood vessels, makes your muscles shiver to make heat, and makes the tiny muscles at the base of each hair pull, which gives you goosebumps. Then the sensors check the result again. When you have a fever, the loop is not broken. The brain has raised the level it aims for, so you feel cold and shiver until you reach the higher number. Senses in this loop are not just for knowing. They are for steering.
- e.g. After running, you sweat and your face goes red, as the loop sends heat out.
- e.g. On a cold morning, you shiver and your hairs stand up.
- e.g. A clay matka keeps water cool because water dries off its walls and takes heat away, much like sweat does.
### Two kinds of pain, and why rubbing helps · *defined*
**Definition:** Pain signals travel along two kinds of nerve fibre. Fast ones carry a sharp signal that shows where you were hurt. Slow ones carry a dull, throbbing signal that lasts longer.

When you stub your toe, you feel two things. First comes a short sharp jab. A moment later comes a dull, spreading throb. The sharp jab travels along thin nerve fibres wrapped in a coat that makes the signal fast. The throb travels along even thinner fibres with no coat, which carry the signal slowly, about as fast as a slow walk. The difference is larger when the hurt is far from the brain, as a toe is. In 1965, Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall suggested that touch signals can turn down pain signals at the spinal cord, like a gate. Scientists have changed many details of this idea since then, but the main point holds: rubbing a bumped elbow really does reduce the pain, because the touch signals compete with the pain signals.
- e.g. A bumped knee gives a sharp pain first, and a throb after.
- e.g. Rubbing the spot where you bumped your head makes it hurt less.
- e.g. A paper cut gives a sharp sting, and then a burning that lingers.
### Pain changes with attention and with the situation · *idea*
**Definition:** How much pain you feel depends on more than how much your body is damaged. Attention, fear, hope and the situation all change it.

In 1946, a doctor named Henry Beecher, who had treated wounded soldiers in the Second World War, reported something odd. Many soldiers with serious wounds asked for little or no pain medicine. Civilians with similar wounds from an operation usually asked for much more. Beecher thought that the soldiers felt relief at being alive and away from the battle. Pain is a message about danger, and the brain weighs it against everything else it knows. This idea has been supported by many later tests. People feel less pain when they are distracted, when they trust the person helping them, or when they are not afraid of what the pain means. People feel more when they are worried or when they give the pain all their attention. This does not mean the pain is imaginary. It means the brain builds pain from many clues, not only from the nerve signal.
- e.g. A child who falls while playing a game often does not notice the scrape until the game is over.
- e.g. A shot hurts more when you stare at the needle and are scared of it.
- e.g. Counting backwards in your head can make a cold cube in your hand feel less aching.
### Is pain a sense, or something the brain makes? · *debated*
Nerve endings that react to harm are called nociceptors, and their signals are real and can be measured. But scientists argue about whether pain is the signal or something more. A person can have the signal without pain, as in the soldiers, and pain without the signal, as in a phantom limb. In 2020, the International Association for the Study of Pain gave a new definition: pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked to real or possible damage. The debate matters, because it changes how doctors treat long-lasting pain.
- e.g. A person who has lost a leg can still feel pain in the foot that is no longer there.
- e.g. A soldier with a serious wound may feel almost no pain at first.
- e.g. Long-lasting back pain can continue after the back has healed.
- **Pain as a sense:** Nociceptors are receptors, like those for light or warmth. Pain is what their signals feel like, and it protects the body.
- **Pain as an experience the brain builds:** The signal from the nociceptors is not yet pain. The brain builds pain from the signal, memory, attention and feelings, so the same signal can feel very different.
### Senses that look inward: your own heartbeat · *defined*
**Definition:** Interoception is the sense of what is going on inside you, such as your heartbeat, breathing, hunger, thirst and fullness. People differ a lot in how well they can tell their own heartbeat.

Sensors in the heart, blood vessels, lungs and stomach send signals to the brain all the time. Most of the time you do not notice them. You do notice them when they are strong, such as a pounding heart after running or a growling stomach. In 1981, the researcher Rainer Schandry asked people to count their heartbeats during timed periods, without feeling their pulse, and then compared the count with a real recording. Some people were close, and many were far off. A common mistake is to count too few. Many people do better after exercise, when each beat is stronger. This is not a test of how clever or healthy anyone is. It shows that people differ in how clearly they hear this inner sense, and that it can be practised.
- e.g. Your heart pounds in your ears after climbing the stairs.
- e.g. You feel full before you finish your plate when you eat slowly.
- e.g. You feel thirsty long before your mouth is really dry.
### Flies steer with tiny clubs · *defined*
**Definition:** Flies and mosquitoes have two tiny club-shaped stalks behind their wings, called halteres. They swing as the wings beat, and they sense when the body turns.

A fly has only one pair of wings. Where a second pair would be, there are two tiny stalks with a knob at the end. When the fly flies, the halteres beat up and down as fast as the wings, more than a hundred times a second. If the fly's body turns, the swinging clubs are pushed sideways by the turn. Sensors at the base of each club feel the push, and tell the brain how the body is turning. In 1948, the researcher John Pringle published the first detailed study of how halteres work as tiny turning sensors. Flies whose halteres have been removed can still beat their wings, but they cannot fly steadily. They tumble and lose their direction. Our own semicircular canals do a similar job in a very different way. Both feel the body turn, one with swinging clubs, the other with swirling liquid.
- e.g. A housefly can dodge a swat in the air, because the halteres tell her brain about each turn.
- e.g. A mosquito turns sharply to follow a smell, using her halteres to keep steady.
- e.g. A crane fly, the long-legged fly that bumps against lamps, has halteres large enough to see.
### Heat pits, and the same gate tuned differently · *defined*
**Definition:** Some snakes have heat pits on their faces that sense the warmth given off by a living body. Vampire bats also have a heat sense near their nose. In both, the gate used is one that other animals use for pain or heat.

A pit viper, such as the Malabar pit viper of the Western Ghats, has a pit between each eye and nostril. Pythons have a row of pits along their lips. A thin skin covers each pit, and it warms up when a warm animal is near. The pit works like a very simple pinhole camera, which gives a blurry picture of warm things. In 1937, the researchers Noble and Schmidt showed that rattlesnakes with covered pits did not strike warm objects well. In 2010, a team including Elena Gracheva and David Julius found that the gate in the snake's pit nerves is the same gate that opens for mustard oil in a mouse, but tuned to warmth. In 2011 the same group found that vampire bats have a shorter form of the chilli gate that opens at a lower temperature than ours, so they can find a warm spot on a sleeping animal. Evolution does not always build a new sense. It often takes a gate that already exists and tunes it.
- e.g. A python finds a warm rat in a dark store room by the heat of her body.
- e.g. A vampire bat finds the warm skin of a sleeping animal using her nose.
- e.g. A mosquito flies towards warm skin after she has noticed your breath.
### Ear stones that keep a diary · *defined*
**Definition:** Fish have hard ear stones in their inner ears that help them balance and sense movement. A stone grows a thin layer every day and a thicker layer in good seasons, so it records the fish's life.

Fish have no outer ears, but they have inner ears, and in each one there are hard stones made of calcium carbonate. They rest on sensing hairs. When the fish tilts or speeds up, the stones lag behind, and the hairs bend. This is the same plan as the ear crystals in your own ears, but with bigger and harder stones. The stones also grow, adding thin layers day after day. Scientists cut a thin slice of the stone and count the layers under a microscope. This tells them the age of the fish, and in some cases which waters she lived in. Fisheries scientists use this to learn how long fish like mathi live, and whether too many young fish are being caught.
- e.g. A sardine caught off Kerala has an ear stone that tells her age in days and months.
- e.g. A rohu in a pond tilts her body and her ear stones press on hairs, just as your crystals do.
- e.g. A very old fish has an ear stone with many fine layers.

## Mostly agreed
- Balance comes from the eyes, the inner ears and the sensors in the body together, and the brain gives more weight to the ones that work best. *How we know:* In the 1840s, Moritz Romberg saw that some patients stood well with their eyes open and swayed with them shut. Later, moving floors and moving rooms showed that when one source is made useless, healthy people lean harder on the other two.
- The canals of the inner ear sense turning, the ear crystals sense tilting and speeding up, and the head-turn reflex uses them to keep the eyes steady. *How we know:* In the 1820s, Pierre Flourens cut the canals of pigeons and saw them tumble. About 1906, Robert Bárány showed that warm or cool water in the ear makes the eyes flick, because it makes the liquid in a canal move. Doctors still use both ideas to test patients.
- A fingertip resting on a still object can steady a standing person, even when the touch is far too light to hold them up. *How we know:* In 1994, Jeka and Lackner measured the force of the fingertip and the sway of the body. The force was less than about 1 newton, and the sway dropped a great deal.
- Warmth and cold are sensed by different gates in nerve endings, and each kind of gate opens at its own temperature. The chilli gate also opens for heat, and the mint gate also opens for cool. *How we know:* In 1997 and 2002, scientists put these gates into ordinary cells in a dish. The cells then responded to heat and chilli, or to cool and menthol. The work earned the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
- The body keeps its core temperature close to 37 degrees Celsius by sensing it and then acting, through sweating, shivering and changes in blood flow. *How we know:* Thermometers show the core temperature stays within about a degree whether the day is hot or cold. In animal experiments, warming a small part of the brain's thermostat makes the animal pant, and cooling that part makes her shiver.
- Pain signals travel at two speeds, so a hurt gives a sharp first pain and a dull second pain. *How we know:* Scientists have measured the speed of the two kinds of nerve fibre. When a nerve is partly blocked, people lose one of the two pains before the other.
- Flies steer with halteres, and some snakes sense warmth with heat pits. *How we know:* In 1948, John Pringle published the first detailed study of how halteres sense turning, and flies without halteres cannot fly steadily. In 1937, Noble and Schmidt covered the pits of rattlesnakes, and the snakes struck warm objects badly.

## How we found out
**Pierre Flourens, a French scientist (1794 to 1867), and Robert Bárány, an Austro-Hungarian doctor (1876 to 1936), The 1820s and about 1906.** Many people once assumed that the three loops in the inner ear were part of hearing, because they sit right beside the part that hears. In the 1820s, the French scientist Pierre Flourens cut the loops in pigeons. The pigeons could still hear, but they tumbled and swung their heads wildly. So the loops had another job. Still, nobody knew how doctors could test them. About 1906 in Vienna, a young doctor named Robert Bárány was washing a patient's ears with water. When the water was cooler than the body, the patient's eyes flicked one way and the patient felt dizzy. When it was warmer, the eyes flicked the other way. Bárány worked out that the water was warming or cooling the liquid in the loop and making it flow, just as a spinning head would. For the first time, doctors could test each ear alone. In 1914 he won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.  
*What people believed before:* The idea that the loops of the inner ear were part of hearing. Flourens showed that pigeons without the loops could hear but could not stay upright.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The inner ear is the sense of balance, and the eyes and feet only help a little.** (Many school books, which show only the ear when they draw balance.; *rejected*)  
  For: The canals and ear crystals are the only organs built just for balance, and a damaged inner ear causes dizziness.  
  Against: The eyes and the body sensors do as much of the work. People with damaged inner ears can stand fairly well in the light on a firm floor. Healthy people wobble badly when both eyes and feet are made unreliable.
- **To stay balanced, a body should hold as still as it can.** (Many people, and many children told to 'stand still'.; *rejected*)  
  For: A body that is moving a lot is easy to see falling, so holding still looks safer.  
  Against: A standing body is always swaying a little and correcting itself. People who freeze up stiffly often balance worse. A steady body moves a little all the time.
- **A walking stick is only a support, and needing one means you are weak.** (Many people who feel unsteady but do not want a stick, and some families.; *rejected*)  
  For: A stick can take weight off a sore knee or hip.  
  Against: A stick that is touched lightly works as a sensor, as a fingertip on a wall does. It adds a fourth source of balance information. Using one is a way of using the body's senses well.
- **Hot and cold are the two ends of one feeling, sensed by the same nerve endings.** (A common idea, because warm and cold seem like opposites.; *rejected*)  
  For: Warming a cold hand and cooling a hot hand feel like the same change running backwards.  
  Against: The skin has separate gates for cold and for warmth, in different places. Chilli opens a heat gate and mint opens a cool gate, and neither changes the temperature.
- **A fever means the body has lost control of its temperature.** (Many worried people, when a child feels very hot.; *rejected*)  
  For: The child is hot and uncomfortable, and the temperature is not normal.  
  Against: In a fever, the brain's thermostat is set higher on purpose, so the body shivers to reach the higher number. The control loop works. A very high fever or a long one still needs a doctor.
- **How much pain you feel always matches how much damage was done.** (Many people, because pain feels like a direct reading of the damage.; *debated*)  
  For: Bigger injuries usually hurt more than small ones.  
  Against: Wounded soldiers have reported little pain, while small cuts hurt a great deal. A phantom limb hurts although the limb is gone. The brain weighs pain against fear, attention and what the hurt means.

## Roots
We have stood on one foot with and without our eyes, shaken heads and pages, and felt warmth, cold and pain. Now we ask what these senses tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Nothing in you holds you upright by itself. Your eyes, your inner ears and the sensors in your legs each give a rough answer. Your brain mixes the answers, and when one goes missing it listens more to the others.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'my sense of balance' as if it were one organ in the ears, like the eye for seeing. But the ears alone cannot keep you steady. People with damaged inner ears can often stand in the light on a firm floor, and fall in the dark.  
*Evidence:* In the Balance Team test, eyes shut on a blanket took away sight and made the feet unsure. Only the ears were left clear, and the standing time was usually the shortest. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* You feel 'I am upright'. The three balance helpers are not felt one by one, because your brain builds that feeling from all of them at once.
- *what is life:* A fly steers with two tiny clubs, a python hunts by heat, and a fish carries a diary in her ear stones. Every living being builds the senses that suit the life they live.
- *what is this world:* Down is real, but nobody feels it directly. A fly, a fish and a child each find down in a different way, by clubs, by stones or by crystals, and the same world pushes on all of them.

Gravity pulls on everything, and turning pushes on everything. A crystal in your ear, a stone in a fish's ear and a swinging club in a fly are all small things that gravity or turning pushes, and a nerve feels the push. Your eyes add the light, and your feet add the ground. Each is a different meeting of a body with the same world, and the brain joins the meetings into one feeling. An upright body is a place where the pushes and pulls of the world are noticed and answered, again and again.

- **Tao Te Ching 76:** A person is born gentle and soft, and dies hard and stiff. Plants are tender and full of sap when alive, and dry and brittle when dead. So the stiff and unbending are companions of death, and the gentle and yielding are companions of life. A tree that will not bend is easily broken. (A standing body is never still. It sways a little and corrects, again and again. A body that stiffens up corrects less well, and balance depends on that give and take.)
- **Anguttara Nikaya 6.55 (the Sona Sutta):** The Buddha reminded Sona, a lute player who was trying too hard, that a lute makes music only when its strings are neither too tight nor too loose. Effort needs the same even tuning. (Balance is a kind of tuning. The brain keeps adjusting how much it trusts each helper, neither too much nor too little, and the muscles adjust again and again.)
- Science (settled): The canals of the inner ear sense turning, the ear crystals sense tilting and speeding up, and the brain combines them with sight and the body's own sensors.
- Science (idea): The brain may weigh each source of information by how reliable it is, trusting a steady one more than a noisy one. Tests with sight and touch fit this idea, and it is one of the main ideas in brain science today.
- Science (open): How these mixed signals become the plain feeling of being upright is not known.
- Other view, Control engineers: Staying upright is a feedback controller: sensors, a rule and muscles, and nothing more. Words like team, vote or agreement are only pictures. The science needs only the signals and the rule.
- Practice, Stand like a tree in the wind (3 min): Stand with your feet a little apart and your eyes open. Let your arms hang. Feel the small sways, forwards and back. Do not try to stop them. Now shut your eyes for ten slow breaths. Feel your feet doing the work. Open your eyes and notice what changes.
- Ask: Which of your balance helpers do you feel the most? Do you ever feel them one at a time?
- Ask: A fly, a fish and you all find down in different ways. Is down in the world, or is it made by each body?
- Ask: If standing is made from three rough answers, what else that you feel as one thing might be made from many?

## Story: The Stick Nobody Leaned On
*The story is made up, and the people in it are not real. The science is true. In 1994, John Jeka and James Lackner reported that a fingertip resting on a still rail, with less than about 1 newton of force, steadies a standing person. Using a walking stick lightly, as a sensor, has been studied in older people. The kitchen scale in the story is a classroom idea, not part of the 1994 study.*

Meenakshi was nine, and every Pongal holiday she took the night bus to Madurai to stay with her Paati. Paati's house was tall and narrow and old, with a courtyard in the middle open to the sky. The rooms stood around it like children sitting round a mat.

Meenakshi's mother said that Paati used to walk down to the river Vaigai every morning, with a bag of flowers for the temple. This year she did not go. She said her knees were tired. But Meenakshi saw how Paati stood at the front door each morning, looking at the street, and then turned back in.

Behind the front door a new walking stick leaned against the wall. Meenakshi's uncle had brought it from Chennai. It was shiny and brown, with a rubber foot. Nobody ever touched it.

"That thing is for old people," said Paati, when Meenakshi asked about it. "I am not old. I am only slow."

On the third evening, the power went out. The fan stopped. The whole street went dark at once, and somewhere a dog barked at nothing.

"Meenu, my spectacles are on the bed," Paati said. "I will get them." She stood up and walked to the kitchen door, where the passage to the bedroom began. Then she stopped.

The passage ran along the courtyard. It was only six steps long, and Meenakshi had run along it a hundred times. But Paati stood at the end with one hand on the doorframe, and did not move. In the light of Meenakshi's phone torch, her face looked small.

"I can't," Paati said. "The floor has gone away."

"It's right there, Paati. I'm lighting it."

"My eyes can see it," said Paati. "My feet do not believe it. When there is light, my feet listen to my eyes. In the dark, they have nobody to listen to. And they have grown quiet, these old feet. They used to tell me everything."

Meenakshi thought hard. Something was tugging at her memory. Last month, in school, Sridevi Miss had made them stand in a line, one foot in front of the other, heel to toe, with their eyes shut. Everyone had swayed like banana plants in the wind. Then Sridevi Miss had said, "Now touch the wall with one finger. Do not push. Only touch."

And the swaying had stopped. It felt so strange. Her finger had hardly rested on the wall, and yet she stood like a pillar. Sridevi Miss had put a kitchen scale on a shelf and asked a girl to touch it the same way. The numbers hardly moved. "That is lighter than a tomato," she had said. "It cannot hold you up. So what is it doing?"

Nobody in the class had known. Meenakshi did not know either. But she knew that it worked.

"Paati," she said, "put one finger on the wall. Do not lean. Touch it softly, as if you are checking that a baby is asleep."

"What nonsense is this?" said Paati. But she lifted her hand from the doorframe, and laid one fingertip on the cool, rough wall.

She took a step. Then another. Her finger slid along the wall like a boat on a still river. Her shoulders came down. Her steps grew longer. By the end of the passage, she was nearly walking the way she used to.

She found her spectacles. She came back the same way, finger on the wall. At the kitchen door she stopped, and laughed out loud, a laugh Meenakshi had not heard all year.

"It is not holding me up," Paati said. "I can feel that it is not. I could pull my finger away this minute. But my feet are listening again."

"Your finger tells them where you are," said Meenakshi. "The wall stays still, so it is easy to tell." She was surprised to hear herself say it.

The next morning, Paati went to the front door and took the shiny stick from behind it. She looked at it as she might look at a cousin she had not spoken to for years. "I will not lean on you," she told it. "I will only touch the ground with you. You can be my finger."

Then she walked all the way to the river, with Meenakshi beside her. The stick went tik, tik, tik on the road, as light as a whisper. Paati held it so softly that a passing girl thought she was carrying it for fun. Each time the rubber foot touched the road, her feet knew a little more about where they were.

At the Vaigai, an old man on the steps looked at the stick and then at Paati. "Is that for the knees?" he asked. "No," said Paati. "It is for feet that have gone quiet."

**For the parent:** For the parent: a fingertip resting on something still gives the brain a fourth source of balance information, because a still wall tells the body exactly how it is moving. In 1994, John Jeka and James Lackner found that the touch steadies people even when its force is under about 1 newton, far too little to hold anyone up. A walking stick that is touched lightly works the same way. It is a sensor, not a prop.

- Ask: Why can one fingertip on a still wall steady a person who is not leaning on it?
- Ask: In the dark passage, which two of the three balance sources were weak or missing, and which one was left?
- Ask: What is the difference between a stick you lean on and a stick you only touch?
- Ask: How could a family make the night path to the toilet easier for a grandparent who feels unsteady?

You shake the head or the page side to side at the same beat, about two shakes every second, by about the same distance. The page is held the same distance from your eyes every time. You read for 10 seconds each time. Only what moves, your head or the page, changes.
- **safety:** Shake your head in small, quick turns, as if saying no, and not hard. Stop at once if your neck hurts or you feel dizzy. Do not spin or shake anyone's head for them. A child with a neck problem or a balance problem should only hold the page and count.
- **record:** Write the words read right for each line. Add the two rounds for each test. Draw three bars, one for each test.
- **results table:** Test | Line used | Words read right in round 1 (out of 8) | Words read right in round 2 (out of 8) | What the print looked like
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the page and the three tests. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will read in three ways. Head still and page still. Head shaking and page still. Head still and page shaking. Which will be hardest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* You will read three ways. Which will be hardest? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think shaking the page might be harder than shaking your head? (good: It is the same movement, so it should be the same.; The page moves faster than my head.; My eyes can keep up with my head but not with the page.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children guess that both will be equally hard, and that is a fine guess to test. One child alone works with a parent holding the page. With two to four children, one reads, one shakes the page, one counts the beat and one counts the words right. Then rotate. Each reader does all three tests, so each has own table.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Mark six lines of eight words each. Number them. Put six numbered slips in a bowl. Sit your child on a chair. Hold the page level with the eyes, about 40 centimetres away.  
  *Parent says:* Here are six lines with eight words each. We draw a slip to pick the line, so the choice is fair. Sit up straight. The page is at your eye level.  
  *Child mode:* Sit on a chair. The page is held at your eye level.
  - Note: Check that the print is big enough to read easily when still. Letters about 1 centimetre tall work well. Practise the beat: say 'one and two and', two side-to-side shakes in each second.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test 1: head and page still. Draw a slip, read that line aloud for 10 seconds, and count the words read right. Do it twice. Write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Head still, page still. Draw a slip. Read the line aloud as well as you can in 10 seconds. Ready, go.  
  *Child mode:* Keep still. Read your line aloud for 10 seconds.
  - Note: Count a word only if it is read correctly. The first test shows your child's best reading, so you can compare the other two with it. Do not return slips to the bowl until all six are used.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test 2: shake the head. Test 3: shake the page. For test 2, you hold the page still. Your child shakes the head side to side, saying no, to the beat. For test 3, your child holds the head still. You shake the page about 5 centimetres each way, to the same beat. Read for 10 seconds, twice each.  
  *Parent says:* Test two. Hold your head and say no, no, no to my beat while you read. Test three. Keep your head still. I will shake the page to the same beat.  
  *Child mode:* Shake your head to the beat and read. Then keep still while the page shakes.
  - Note: Keep both beats the same. Write what the print looked like each time, such as clear, smeared or jumping. Stop if your child feels dizzy. If both tests are easy, shake faster. If both are impossible, shake more slowly. The head-shake test is easier than it sounds only if the shakes are small and quick. The exact numbers vary a lot, and the page shake is almost always the harder one.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add the two rounds for each test. Draw three bars. Compare the head shake with the page shake. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning, and then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which was harder, the head or the page? Both moved the same amount at the same beat. What do you think your eyes were doing differently?  
  *Child mode:* Add up your words. Which was harder, head or page? Why?
  - Note: A common result is that reading with a shaking head costs little, and reading a shaking page is much harder. If so, the head-turn reflex is working: the inner ears tell the eyes to move opposite to the head within a hundredth of a second. Following a moving page on purpose is slower. If your results are the same for both, the shakes may have been too gentle. Shake faster and try again.
  - Check: Why can you read while your head shakes, but not when the page shakes at the same beat? → **Your inner ears move your eyes the opposite way almost at once when your head moves**
### e3 · Ice in the Palm: does thinking about something else change the ache? (25 min)
- **question:** Does an ice cube in your palm ache less when you count backwards than when you only watch it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After 20 seconds, will the ache be less, the same or more when you count backwards? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every try uses an ice cube of the same size, wrapped in the same kind of thin cloth, held in the same palm for exactly 20 seconds. You rest for three minutes between tries, until the hand is warm and its colour is back. You do the two kinds of try in the order watch, count, count, watch, so that a second try is not always the same kind. Only what your mind does changes.
- **safety:** Ice can hurt the skin if it is held too long. Always wrap the cube in a thin cloth, and drop it after exactly 20 seconds. Your child may drop it sooner, at any moment, and nobody should say hold on. Do not do this test if your child's hands turn white or blue in the cold, or if there is a skin problem on the hand. The palm may look pink for a few minutes. Wait until it is warm before the next try.
- **record:** After each try, write the ache from 0 to 10. A 0 means no ache, and a 10 means I wanted to drop it at once. Write the colour of the palm. Then compare the two watch tries with the two count tries.
- **results table:** Try | What the mind did (watch or count) | Ache at 20 seconds (0 to 10) | Colour of the palm afterwards
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the ice, the cloth and the scale from 0 to 10. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will hold an ice cube for 20 seconds. Sometimes you will only watch it. Sometimes you will count backwards. Will the ache change? Why?  
  *Child mode:* You will hold an ice cube for 20 seconds. Will counting change the ache? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think counting might change how much the ice aches? (good: My mind is busy, so I notice the ache less.; Nothing will change, because the ice is the same.; Counting would make it worse, because I have to concentrate.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Remind your child that the cube can be dropped at any moment, and that nothing bad will happen if they drop it. With two to four children, each child has own cubes and own scale. One child holds, a second times, and a third is the grown-up's helper. Each child gives the rating quietly or on paper, so others do not hear the number first.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Practise the counting without ice. Count backwards from 50 in twos, aloud: 50, 48, 46. Show how to wrap a cube in a thin cloth and hold it in the open palm.  
  *Parent says:* First, count backwards from fifty in twos, with no ice. Fifty, forty-eight, forty-six. Say it aloud. This must be easy before we add the ice.  
  *Child mode:* Count backwards from 50 in twos. Say it out loud.
  - Note: If counting by twos is too hard, use counting backwards from 20 in ones, or saying the days of the week backwards. It should keep the mind busy but not be frightening.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Try 1 (watch): your child holds a wrapped cube in the open palm and watches the cube and the stopwatch for 20 seconds. Then drop the cube in the bowl. Ask for the ache from 0 to 10. Rest three minutes. Try 2 (count): the same, but counting backwards aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Try one. Hold the ice. Only watch it and think about the cold. Ready, go. Drop it. How much did it ache, from zero to ten? Rest now.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the ice and only watch it. Drop it at 20 seconds. Say the ache from 0 to 10.
  - Note: Stop the watch at exactly 20 seconds and tell your child to drop the cube. Write the number your child gives, and look at the palm. A pink palm is normal. Dry the hand with the towel, and let it warm fully before the next try.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Try 3 (count) and Try 4 (watch), with three minutes of rest between. Write the ache and the colour of the palm each time.  
  *Parent says:* Try three, counting backwards from fifty in twos. Try four, only watching again. We swap the order so that the second try is not always the same.  
  *Child mode:* Do a counting try, then a watching try. Rest between them.
  - Note: If your child keeps losing count, note it. Busy minds do not always count well. Do not push for a specific answer. A rating that does not change is a result too.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add the two watch ratings and the two count ratings. Compare them. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Add the two watch tries. Add the two count tries. Which is bigger? What does that tell you about the ache, and about where it comes from?  
  *Child mode:* Add the watch tries. Add the count tries. Which is bigger?
  - Note: If the watch tries hardly ache at all (below 2), use a thinner cloth next time, but never hold longer than 20 seconds. Many children give a lower number when they count, typically by one to three points, but some give the same and a few give higher. The ache is real in every case. The brain turns the ache up or down depending on how much attention it has for it. Pain is not an exact measure of the cold or the damage.
  - Check: If counting backwards lowered the ache from the ice, what would that show? → **Attention changes how strong a pain feels**

## Activities
### a1 · Under the Spinning Umbrella: does the world turn you? (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up holds the umbrella and keeps the metal tips away from your child's face and eyes. Turn it slowly. Use a steady chair, away from stairs and sharp corners. Stop at once if your child feels dizzy or sick.
- **record:** Write what each person felt: nothing, a little turning, or a lot. Write whether the feeling was of the umbrella turning, or of the child turning.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the umbrella. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* I will turn this umbrella slowly over your head while you sit still. Will you feel anything? Will you feel the umbrella turn, or yourself?  
  *Child mode:* An umbrella will turn above you. Will you feel anything? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think you might feel something, when you are not moving? (good: My eyes will see the pattern turning.; My brain might think I am the one turning.; I will not feel anything, because I am sitting still.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Sit your child on the chair, holding the seat. Hold the open umbrella low, so it fills the view above the head. Turn it slowly, one turn every four seconds, for 30 seconds. Ask what they feel and write a score from 0 (nothing) to 10 (I am sure I am turning). Then do the same with eyes shut, as a comparison.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the chair. Look up at the pattern and keep looking. I will turn the umbrella slowly. Tell me what you feel.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the chair. Look up at the pattern. Say what you feel.
  - Note: Stay beside the chair. Turn slowly. With two to four children, one sits, one turns, one holds the stopwatch and one writes the scores, and then you rotate. The effect is often weak with a small umbrella, and many children feel nothing, so write the scores honestly. Many children feel that they themselves start to turn, or that the chair tilts, after ten to twenty seconds. Some feel nothing, and that is a result too. Stop if your child feels sick or dizzy.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap places. Then ask your child to describe the feeling, and to say what part of the body noticed.  
  *Parent says:* Now it is my turn. After this, tell me: was it your eyes, your ears or your feet that told you that you were turning?  
  *Child mode:* Swap. Now you turn the umbrella. Then say what you felt.
  - Check: Under a turning umbrella, you may feel that you are turning, though you sit still. Which sense is giving the wrong message? → **Your eyes**
### a2 · A Fingertip Lighter than a Tomato (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up stands right beside your child for every try, ready to catch. Use a carpet or a mat, away from stairs, tables with sharp corners and glass. Stop at once if your child feels dizzy.
- **record:** Write the seconds for each try: no touch, light touch under 100 grams on the scale, and a hard push. Write the weight of your child and of the tomato.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Weigh the tomato on the scale. Let your child feel how light 100 grams is. Practise touching the scale with one fingertip so that it reads under 100 grams. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This tomato is about 100 grams. Your fingertip will touch the scale lighter than that. Do you think a touch that light can help you stand? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A tomato weighs about 100 grams. Touch the scale with less. Can that help you stand?
  - Ask: Do you think a touch lighter than a tomato will help you stand? Why do you think so? (good: No, it is too light to hold me up.; Yes, because my finger can feel the table.; It will help a little, because I will know where I am.)
  - Note: Switch the scale on and wait for zero before each touch. Practise until your child can keep the reading steady under 100 grams. Do not correct the guess. A kitchen scale costs about Rs 300 to 500. Without one, use a wall: a fingertip touch is light enough if a sheet of paper held between the fingertip and the wall can be pulled out easily. With two to four children, one stands, one watches the scale or the paper, one times, and one writes. Then rotate.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child stands heel to toe on the mat, arms folded, eyes shut. Time two tries with no touch, up to 20 seconds. Then time two tries with one fingertip resting on the scale, under 100 grams.  
  *Parent says:* Stand with one foot right in front of the other, and fold your arms. Shut your eyes. Try one, no touch. Try two. Now rest one finger on the scale, very lightly.  
  *Child mode:* Stand heel to toe. Shut your eyes. Try with no touch. Then touch the scale lightly.
  - Note: Stand close, ready to catch. A try ends when a foot moves or the child opens their eyes. Watch the scale. If the reading goes above 100 grams, it is not a light touch, so repeat the try.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Do two more tries, leaning on the table with a firm push. Write the seconds. Add up the three kinds of try.  
  *Parent says:* Now push hard on the table with your hand, and try again. Is it steadier than the light touch, or not?  
  *Child mode:* Now push hard on the table. Try again with your eyes shut.
  - Note: Children are often steadier with both kinds of touch than with none, and about as steady with the light touch as with the hard push. Your result may differ, and that is fine.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which was steadier, no touch or light touch? Was the hard push much better? What does the light touch do, if it does not hold you up?  
  *Child mode:* Which was steadiest? Why did a light touch help?
  - Check: A touch of less than 100 grams cannot hold up a child who weighs 25 kilograms. Why does it still help? → **It tells the brain how the body is moving compared with a still object**
### a3 · Warm Hand, Whole Hand: is the same water warmer on more skin? (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up mixes the water, away from the child, and tests it with the inside of their wrist or elbow, as for a baby's bath. Do not carry a hot pan or kettle near the children. The water must be pleasantly warm, never hot, and never hotter than 40 degrees Celsius. Replace the water if it gets cool.
- **record:** For each dip, write the warmth from 0 to 10, where 0 is no warmth and 10 is as warm as is comfortable. Write the water temperature if you measured it.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up mixes the warm water and checks it with the wrist, or with a thermometer. Pour it into the two bowls. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Both bowls have the same warm water. If you dip one finger, and then your whole hand, will the warmth feel the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The water is the same. Will a finger and a whole hand feel the same? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think a finger and a whole hand might feel different in the same water? (good: More skin means more of me feels the warmth.; The water is the same, so it will feel the same.; A finger is smaller, so it feels less.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Keep the water the same temperature in both bowls. The effect is usually clear but small, so use the score each time, and if all three scores are the same, write that down as a result. Cold hands from a fan or air-conditioner can spoil it, so sit in a normal room for five minutes first. With two to four children, each gives a score on paper, so nobody hears the others' numbers first.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Dip one fingertip, up to the first joint, for 5 seconds. Say the warmth from 0 to 10. Dry the hand and rest for a minute. Then dip the whole hand to the wrist for 5 seconds. Say the warmth. Rest. Then dip both hands, one in each bowl, for 5 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Dip one finger for five seconds. How warm, from zero to ten? Dry it. Now your whole hand. Then both hands.  
  *Child mode:* Dip a fingertip for 5 seconds. Say how warm. Then a hand. Then both hands.
  - Note: Keep each dip to 5 seconds, as the skin soon gets used to the temperature. Rest for a minute between dips. If the order may matter, repeat in the opposite order: both hands, whole hand, fingertip.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the three scores. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which dip felt warmest? The water was the same each time. So what changed?  
  *Child mode:* Which dip felt warmest? The water did not change. What did?
  - Check: The water is the same warmth for a finger and a whole hand. Why can the whole hand feel warmer? → **More warm gates open at once, and the brain adds up their reports**
### a4 · Count Your Own Heartbeat (25 min)
- **safety:** The exercise is only 20 star jumps, done on a clear floor. Skip it if your child is unwell. Stop if your child feels dizzy or short of breath. This is not a test of health. Nobody should feel bad about a count that is far off.
- **record:** A table with four rows: at rest by feeling, at rest by pulse, after exercise by feeling, after exercise by pulse. For each pair, write the difference.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Sit your child quietly for one minute, eyes closed, hands on the knees.  
  *Parent says:* Sit still and be quiet for a minute. Can you feel your heart beating anywhere? Do you think you could count the beats without touching your wrist? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Can you feel your heart? Could you count the beats?
  - Ask: Where in your body do you think you might feel your heart? (good: In my chest.; In my ears or neck.; In my fingers, if they are still.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children feel the heartbeat in the chest, the neck or the fingertips. Some feel nothing at rest, and that is common.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Time 30 seconds. Your child counts the beats they can feel without touching a wrist or neck, and says the number. Then time 30 seconds more. Your child finds the pulse at the wrist, and counts the beats with fingers. Write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Count the beats you can feel inside, without using your fingers. I will time thirty seconds. Now find the pulse on your wrist, and count again.  
  *Child mode:* Count the beats you feel inside for 30 seconds. Then count your pulse with your fingers.
  - Note: If your child feels no heartbeat, write 0 and say so. Do not make up a number, because that spoils the result. A count that is far from the pulse is common. Hold two fingers, not the thumb, on the wrist below the thumb.
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child does 20 star jumps and sits down. Sit down at once, because the heart slows within a minute. Repeat both counts for 30 seconds each. Write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Do twenty star jumps, then sit. Count again by feeling, then by pulse. Is it easier now to feel your heart?  
  *Child mode:* Do 20 star jumps. Sit. Count your beats both ways again.
  - Note: After exercise the heart beats harder and faster, so many people can count it more accurately. The count by feeling is often smaller than the pulse count. The heart slows a little during the minute, so the second count is a bit low. Always do the feeling count first, at rest and after exercise, so the comparison is fair. Pairs work well: one child counts and the other times, then swap, and each child can also take the other's pulse.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Subtract each pair of counts. Ask which was closer, rest or exercise. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* How close were you at rest? How close after the star jumps? Why do you think it was easier after running about?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your counts. When were you closest? Why?
  - Check: Many people count their heartbeat more accurately after exercise. Why? → **Each beat is stronger, so the inside sensors report it more clearly**
### a5 · Matka Magic: how does sweat cool you? (30 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up fills the tumblers with warm water, away from the child, and checks it with the wrist, as for a baby's bath. The water must never be hotter than 40 degrees Celsius. Keep the fan, the plug and the wires away from the water.
- **record:** Write the starting temperature of each tumbler and the temperature after 15 minutes. Work out how much each one cooled. Write the temperature of the room.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child both tumblers and the cloths. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* One cloth will be wet and one dry. Both tumblers hold the same warm water, in front of a fan. Which will cool more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One cloth is wet. One is dry. Which tumbler cools more? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the wet cloth might cool the water more, or less? (good: Water drying takes heat away.; The wet cloth covers the steel and keeps the heat in.; A wet cloth is cold, so it cools the water.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A matka, a clay water pot, keeps water cool in the same way.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Wet one cloth with tap water and wring it so that it does not drip. Wrap it round one tumbler with a rubber band. Wrap the dry cloth round the other. A grown-up fills both with warm water. Measure each and write the temperatures.  
  *Parent says:* Wrap the wet cloth round this tumbler and the dry cloth round the other. Now we fill both with the same warm water. What does the thermometer say?  
  *Child mode:* Wrap a wet cloth round one tumbler. Wrap a dry cloth round the other. Measure the water.
  - Note: Wring the wet cloth well. The start temperatures should be almost the same. Fill the tumblers within a minute of each other. If you have only a fever thermometer, use water between 35 and 42 degrees.
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put the tumblers side by side in front of the fan. Start a timer for 15 minutes. While you wait, talk about when you feel cool in a breeze after swimming or sweating.  
  *Parent says:* While we wait, think. When do you feel cold in a breeze? What is on your skin then?  
  *Child mode:* Put the tumblers in the breeze. Wait 15 minutes. Think about sweat.
  - Note: On a very humid day, such as in Chennai during the monsoon, the difference may be small. A table fan helps a lot. Do not touch the tumblers during the wait. Typical result: the wet cloth cools the water a few degrees more in 15 minutes, but the numbers depend on the weather. In a hot room the water may not cool much at all, so also compare how the two feel. If the fever thermometer shows Lo, write under 32. With two to four children, one measures each tumbler, one writes and one watches the clock. Repeat with the cloths swapped so the tumblers are fair.
  - Wait: Leave the tumblers in front of the fan for 15 minutes.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure each tumbler again. Subtract to find how much each cooled. Compare the two. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which tumbler cooled more? By how many degrees? Water on the cloth dried. What did it take with it?  
  *Child mode:* Measure again. Which tumbler cooled more? By how much?
  - Check: The tumbler with the wet cloth cooled more than the one with the dry cloth. What took the extra heat away? → **Water drying off the cloth took heat with it**
### a6 · Be a Pit Viper: find the warm cup with your eyes shut (25 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up fills the tumblers away from the child and tests the warm one with the inside of the wrist, as for a baby's bath. The warm water must never be hotter than 40 degrees Celsius. The child never touches the tumblers during the test. Keep the room quiet, and replace the warm water when it cools.
- **record:** Write a tick for each round you got right, out of 12. Write how far from the tumbler your cheek was. Write the number of right answers you would expect by luck.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three tumblers. Ask for a guess and a reason. Ask how many of 12 they would get right by pure luck.  
  *Parent says:* One tumbler is warm and two are not. They look the same. With your eyes shut, can you find the warm one without touching? How many of twelve by luck alone?  
  *Child mode:* One cup is warm. Two are not. Can you find it with your eyes shut?
  - Ask: How many of 12 rounds would you get right by luck alone, and why? (good: Four, because there are three cups and one is right.; A third of them, which is four.; About four, because one in three is the warm cup.)
  - Note: By luck, one in three would be right, which is 4 of 12. Any score well above 4 would suggest the child really feels the warmth.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up fills one tumbler with warm water and two with tap water. Dry the outsides. Put them in a row. Cover your child's eyes.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. I will shuffle the cups. Do not touch them. Hold your cheek close to each one, like a snake feeling for a warm mouse.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Do not touch the cups.
  - Note: Handle all three tumblers the same way, so no tumbler is warmed by hands. The viper must breathe out away from the tumblers, since breath is warm. With two to four children, one is the viper, one shuffles, and one records, and then you rotate; each viper plays 12 rounds. Do not let the child hear or see the shuffling. Move the cups smoothly and quietly. Dry the outsides so that no wet marks give a clue.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Play 12 rounds. Each round, shuffle the tumblers. Your child holds a cheek about 5 centimetres from each one in turn, not touching, and points to the warmest. Tick if right. Refill with warm water after six rounds. Then try 10 centimetres away for six more rounds.  
  *Parent says:* Hold your cheek near the first cup. Now the second. Now the third. Which feels warmest? Point.  
  *Child mode:* Hold your cheek near each cup. Point to the warmest. Do not touch.
  - Note: Many children can find the warm cup from 5 centimetres. At 10 centimetres it becomes much harder. Write down where it stops working. In hot weather, when the room and tap water are already warm, scores may stay near 4, so chill the two cold tumblers in the fridge. The warm tumbler cools, so refill it after six rounds.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the right answers. Compare them with the 4 you would get by luck. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* How many did you get right? Luck would give about four. What does your score say? What does a snake have that you do not?  
  *Child mode:* Count your ticks. Is it more than 4? What does that tell you?
  - Check: You got 9 of 12 right. By luck you would expect 4. What does this suggest? → **You really can feel warmth at a distance**

- **safety:** Ask each person if they would like to take part, and never push anyone. Older people, and people who feel unsteady, should do the tests close to a chair or counter they can hold, and should skip any test that feels unsafe. A grown-up stands beside everyone doing a test. 3. (mcq) What makes it possible to read a sign while your head bobs in a bumpy bus?  
   Options: The head-turn reflex, which moves the eyes opposite to the head / The sign moves with your head / Your eyes follow the sign on purpose very quickly  
   **Answer:** The head-turn reflex, which moves the eyes opposite to the head. *Why:* The inner ear senses the head moving and starts a very fast reflex that moves the eyes the other way. Following the sign on purpose is much too slow to do this.
4. (mcq) The train beside yours starts to move, and you feel yours slide backwards. Which sense gave the wrong message?  
   Options: Your eyes / Your inner ears / Your feet  
   **Answer:** Your eyes. *Why:* Your eyes saw a moving picture, which is what you see when you move yourself. Your inner ears and feet correctly said you were still.
5. (mcq) Why can a very light fingertip on a wall steady a person?  
   Options: It gives the brain more information about how the body is moving / It takes weight off the feet / It stops the inner ears from swirling  
   **Answer:** It gives the brain more information about how the body is moving. *Why:* A still wall tells the brain how the body is swaying, even though the force is far too small to hold anyone up. The touch is a message, not a prop.
6. (mcq) Why does a green chilli feel hot, though the chilli is not warm?  
   Options: It opens the same gate in your nerve endings that heat opens / The chilli is secretly boiling / The tongue cannot tell hot from cold  
   **Answer:** It opens the same gate in your nerve endings that heat opens. *Why:* Capsaicin, the chemical in chilli, opens the same gate that warm temperatures open. The nerve then sends a signal that means hot. The chilli's temperature does not change.
7. (mcq) What happens in the body's temperature loop when you get too warm?  
   Options: Sensors tell the brain, and the body sweats and widens the skin's blood vessels / The brain stops measuring / The body shivers to make heat  
   **Answer:** Sensors tell the brain, and the body sweats and widens the skin's blood vessels. *Why:* The loop senses the warmth, then acts to cool the body. Sweat dries and takes heat away, and wider blood vessels let heat leave the skin. Shivering is the response to being too cool.
8. (mcq) When you stub your toe, you feel a sharp jab and then a throb. Why two kinds of pain?  
   Options: The signals travel along two kinds of nerve fibre at different speeds / Your toe gets hurt twice / Your brain forgets and remembers the pain  
   **Answer:** The signals travel along two kinds of nerve fibre at different speeds. *Why:* The fast fibres carry a sharp signal, and the slow fibres carry a dull throbbing one. The throb arrives later because its fibres are thinner and have no fast coat.
9. (mcq) A person is distracted by an exciting game and does not notice a scraped knee until the game ends. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Attention changes how strongly pain is felt / The scrape was not real / The nerves in the knee stopped working  
   **Answer:** Attention changes how strongly pain is felt. *Why:* The brain weighs pain against everything else it is paying attention to. The scrape was real, and the nerves worked, but the pain was turned down while the mind was busy.
10. (mcq) What do the halteres of a fly do?  
   Options: They swing and sense when the body turns / They help the fly smell / They make the fly's buzzing sound  
   **Answer:** They swing and sense when the body turns. *Why:* The halteres swing as the wings beat. When the body turns, the swinging clubs are pushed sideways, and sensors at the base feel that push.
11. (mcq) A pit viper has the same kind of gate that other animals use for pain. What does this suggest about how new senses begin?  
   Options: Evolution often tunes an old gate to a new job / Evolution builds every sense from nothing / Snakes copy the senses of other animals  
   **Answer:** Evolution often tunes an old gate to a new job. *Why:* In snakes and bats, the heat sense uses gates related to ones in other animals, tuned to different temperatures. Evolution often reuses what it has.
12. (mcq) To test whether a soft surface changes balance, a child stands on a blanket on one try and on a floor on the next. What else must stay the same for a fair test?  
   Options: The pose, the foot, the eyes and the rest between tries / The colour of the blanket / Nothing, because only the surface matters  
   **Answer:** The pose, the foot, the eyes and the rest between tries. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. If the eyes were open on one try and shut on another, you could not tell whether the surface or the eyes made the difference.

## Applications
- Doctors test a patient's balance by watching the eyes after the head is turned, or after warm or cool water is put in the ear, a method that goes back to Bárány.
- Doctors treat loose ear crystals by moving the head through a set of positions that roll the crystals back into their pouch.
- Physiotherapists ask patients to stand with eyes shut or on foam, to find out which of the three balance helpers has weakened after an injury.
- Pilots are trained to trust their instruments in cloud, because their inner ears can be fooled when the picture outside is missing.
- Handrails and walking sticks that are touched lightly help older people stand steadier, by giving the brain extra information.
- Planners design night paths, bathrooms and staircases for older people with low lights, rough strips and firm mats.
- Thermostats in refrigerators, geysers, irons and air conditioners use the same loop as the body: sense, compare, act, check.
- Clay matkas, wet khus screens and desert coolers keep things cool by letting water dry off a surface and carry heat away.
- Gyroscope chips in phones, drones and cameras sense turning with tiny vibrating parts, using the same push that a fly's halteres feel.
- Thermal cameras and night-vision cameras sense heat from a distance, as the pits of a snake do.
- Nurses and parents use distraction, such as bubbles, songs and stories, to make injections hurt less for children.

## Open questions
- **How does the brain mix signals from the eyes, ears and feet into one steady feeling of being upright?** Scientists have models of how signals are weighed and combined. Nobody has a full account of how this becomes the plain feeling of standing up.
- **Why do some people get travel sickness, and others never?** Two main ideas exist, one about senses that disagree and one about the body struggling to keep steady. Neither explains everything.
- **Is pain a sense, or something the brain builds from many clues?** Pain signals can be measured, and pain can change without the signal changing. Scientists disagree about where to draw the line between the signal and the pain.
- **How do the brains of pit vipers combine the picture from their eyes with the heat picture from their pits?** Scientists know that both kinds of signal meet in the same part of the snake's brain. Nobody knows what the snake's combined picture is like.
- **Are there senses in the human body that we have not yet found?** The gates for touch and temperature were only found in the last thirty years. Other gates and sensors may be waiting to be discovered.
- **Do other animals feel dizzy when they spin?** Many animals show eye flicking after spinning, which suggests that their balance senses are fooled in the same way. Nobody knows what, if anything, that feels like to them.

## Connected maths
- In the Balance Team test, one child stood 30 seconds with eyes open on the floor, and 6 seconds with eyes shut on the blanket. How many times as long was the first? What does the number tell you? **5 times as long. Losing two helpers cut the time to a fifth.**. 30 divided by 6 is 5. So the first time was 5 times the second. The result shows how much the eyes and the feet helped the ears. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A fingertip touch on a wall has a force of 100 grams. A child weighs 25 kilograms. 1 kilogram is 1,000 grams. How many times heavier is the child than the touch? **250 times.**. 25 kilograms is 25 times 1,000, which is 25,000 grams. 25,000 divided by 100 is 250. The child is 250 times heavier than the touch. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L4`)
- Five people held the pose with eyes shut for 8, 12, 15, 9 and 6 seconds. What is the average, and why is it more useful than one person's time? **The average is 10 seconds. One person's time can be a fluke.**. 8 plus 12 plus 15 plus 9 plus 6 is 50. 50 divided by 5 is 10. An average of five people smooths out luck and shows what is typical. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In the head and page test, a child read 14 of 16 words with her head shaking, and 4 of 16 with the page shaking. What fraction was read right in each case? How many times as many words did she read with the head shaking? **7 out of 8 with the head shaking, and 1 out of 4 with the page shaking. About 3 and a half times as many.**. 14 out of 16 is 7 out of 8, and 4 out of 16 is 1 out of 4. 14 divided by 4 is 3 and a half. So she read about 3 and a half times as many words with her head shaking. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In Be a Pit Viper, there are three tumblers and one is warm. Luck would give 4 right answers in 12 rounds. A child got 9 right. How many more than luck is that, and what fraction of the rounds did she get right? **5 more than luck. She got 3 out of 4 of the rounds right.**. 9 minus 4 is 5. 9 out of 12 is 3 out of 4. A score well above luck suggests that she really felt the warmth. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- In Matka Magic, both tumblers started at 39 degrees Celsius. After 15 minutes, the one with the wet cloth was 33.5 degrees and the one with the dry cloth was 36.5 degrees. How much did each cool? How many times as much did the wet one cool? **The wet one cooled by 5.5 degrees and the dry one by 2.5 degrees. The wet one cooled a little over twice as much.**. 39 minus 33.5 is 5.5. 39 minus 36.5 is 2.5. 5.5 divided by 2.5 is 2.2. So the wet one cooled about 2.2 times as much. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In Count Your Own Heartbeat, a child felt 38 beats in 30 seconds. Her pulse count for the same time was 40. How many beats a minute is each? How close was her feeling to the pulse, as a fraction? **76 beats a minute by feeling and 80 by pulse. She was 38 out of 40, which is 19 out of 20.**. A minute is twice 30 seconds, so 38 times 2 is 76 and 40 times 2 is 80. 38 out of 40 is 19 out of 20, which is 95 out of 100. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Mia held an ice cube and rated the ache 7 and 5 when she only watched, and 4 and 6 when she counted. What is the average for each kind of try? Is that enough to be sure that counting helps? **Watching averaged 6 and counting averaged 5. It is not enough to be sure.**. 7 plus 5 is 12, and 12 divided by 2 is 6. 4 plus 6 is 10, and 10 divided by 2 is 5. The difference of 1 is smaller than the difference between her two watch tries, so more tries would be needed. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- Suppose a fast pain fibre carries a signal at 20 metres a second, and a slow one at 1 metre a second. The path from a toe to the brain is 1.2 metres. How long does each signal take? How much later does the slow signal arrive? **The fast signal takes 0.06 seconds and the slow one 1.2 seconds. The slow one arrives 1.14 seconds later.**. Time is distance divided by speed. 1.2 divided by 20 is 0.06. 1.2 divided by 1 is 1.2. The slow signal arrives 1.2 minus 0.06, which is 1.14 seconds later. These speeds are made-up round numbers to show the idea. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
