# Photosynthesis and breathing: how plants and other living beings share one breath
`sci.plants.photosynthesis.L4`

**Summary.** Every dawn, fish in a still green pond swim up and gulp at the surface. The pond is full of plants that make oxygen, so why do the fish run short of it? Your child finds out with jars of cabbage-coloured water, a money plant and a window. A plant in light uses up the carbon dioxide you breathe out. The same plant in the dark adds carbon dioxide back, because every living cell breathes all day and all night. Then the idea goes out to ponds, to sugarcane and pineapples, to coal and oil, and to the whole Earth, which breathes in and out once a year.

**Intro.** *A pond is full of green plants, and plants make oxygen. Yet at dawn the fish in the pond come up to the surface and gulp, as if they cannot breathe. Where did the oxygen go?* Last time you learned that a leaf in the sun takes in carbon dioxide and gives out oxygen. That is only half of the story. The same leaf also takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide, because every living cell needs energy from its food. Which half wins depends on the light. In this unit you catch the carbon dioxide in a jar, watch a leaf use it up in the light and add it back in the dark, and then work out why a pond runs short of air just before sunrise.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that plants do both photosynthesis and respiration, and that the light decides which one wins overall.
1. I can run a fair test with a colour indicator, and use it to show that a plant takes in carbon dioxide in light and adds carbon dioxide in the dark.
2. I can explain why the oxygen in a pond is lowest just before dawn, and why fish gasp then.
3. I can compare photosynthesis and respiration: what goes in, what comes out, and where each happens inside a cell.
4. I can name three things that can hold photosynthesis back, and read a graph that shows it.
5. I can explain how sugarcane, maize and pineapple handle carbon dioxide and water in different ways from rice.
6. I can follow one carbon atom around the carbon cycle, and say why coal and oil are old sunlight.
7. I can say what scientists agree about, what they still argue about, and who found the pieces.

## Terms
- **Photosynthesis**: How green plants, algae and some bacteria build sugar from carbon dioxide and water, using the energy of light. Oxygen is left over. The word means 'putting together with light'. *e.g.* A tulsi leaf on a sunny windowsill is doing photosynthesis right now.
- **Respiration**: How a living cell gets energy out of sugar. The cell uses oxygen to break the sugar down, and gives out carbon dioxide and water. Plants, animals, fungi and bacteria all do it, day and night. *e.g.* A sprouting moong seed respires, and gets a tiny bit warm as they use up their stored food.
- **Chloroplast**: A tiny green part inside a leaf cell where photosynthesis happens. One leaf cell can have dozens of them. *e.g.* Under a toy microscope, a leaf cell of a water plant looks like a box full of green beads. Each bead is a chloroplast.
- **Mitochondria**: Tiny parts inside almost every cell of plants, animals and fungi where respiration happens. One of them is called a mitochondrion. They are the cell's power stations. *e.g.* A muscle cell in your leg is packed with mitochondria, and so is a cell in a growing root tip.
- **Chlorophyll**: The green colouring in leaves that catches the energy of sunlight. It soaks up mostly red and blue light and bounces green light back. *e.g.* Grass looks green because of the chlorophyll in the blades.
- **Stomata**: Tiny holes, mostly on the underside of a leaf, that open to let gases in and out. Carbon dioxide goes in, and oxygen and water vapour go out. One hole is called a stoma. *e.g.* A single leaf of a banana plant has millions of stomata, each far too small to see.
- **Guard cells**: The two bean-shaped cells around each stoma. When they swell with water, the stoma opens. When they lose water, it closes. *e.g.* On a hot, dry afternoon the guard cells of many leaves go limp and the stomata close to save water.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas with no colour or smell. Air has only a tiny amount, about 4 parts in every 10,000. You breathe it out, and leaves take it in to build sugar. *e.g.* The bubbles in a fizzy drink are carbon dioxide.
- **Dissolved oxygen**: Oxygen gas that is mixed into water so that fish and other water creatures can breathe it. Water holds far less oxygen than air does. *e.g.* Fish take dissolved oxygen out of the water through their gills.
- **Indicator**: A colouring that changes colour when something around it changes. Red cabbage juice is an indicator. It is blue-purple in water that is a little alkaline, and turns pink-purple when the water gets more acidic. *e.g.* Blow breath into blue-purple cabbage water, and it slowly turns pinker, because carbon dioxide makes water slightly acidic.
- **Glucose**: The simple sugar that leaves make first. Its molecule has 6 carbon atoms, 12 hydrogen atoms and 6 oxygen atoms. Cells burn it in respiration to get energy. *e.g.* The sweet taste of a ripe banana is partly glucose, made in the banana plant's leaves.
- **Limiting factor**: The one thing that is in the shortest supply and so sets how fast something can go. For photosynthesis it can be light, carbon dioxide, or warmth. *e.g.* On a dark, rainy afternoon, light is the limiting factor for a leaf, so adding more water makes no difference.
- **Compensation point**: The light level at which a plant's photosynthesis exactly equals their respiration. At that light, no gas goes out or in overall. Below it, the plant is using up more than they make. *e.g.* A plant in a dim corner may sit just below the compensation point, so they slowly lose weight even though they are alive.
- **Enzyme**: A protein that speeds up one particular change inside a living body, without being used up. Every cell has thousands of different enzymes. *e.g.* An enzyme in your spit begins to break the starch in a piece of rice into sugar as you chew.
- **Energy carrier**: A small molecule that picks up energy in one place in a cell and drops it off in another, like a rechargeable battery. The main one is called ATP. *e.g.* In a leaf, light charges up the energy carriers. Then the carriers give their energy to build sugar.
- **Carbon cycle**: The path that carbon atoms take round and round between air, living things, soil, the sea and rocks. Photosynthesis moves carbon out of the air. Respiration, rotting and burning move it back. *e.g.* The carbon in a mango was carbon dioxide in the air a few months earlier. It goes back to the air when you breathe out.
- **Fossil fuel**: Coal, oil and natural gas. They formed over millions of years from the bodies of plants and tiny sea creatures that were buried before they could rot. *e.g.* Petrol and diesel are made from oil. Burning them gives back carbon dioxide that plants and plankton took in long ago.
- **Algae bloom**: A sudden, huge growth of tiny algae in water, which turns the water green. When the algae die, rotting uses up oxygen in the water. *e.g.* A village pond that turns thick green in hot weather has an algae bloom.
- **Aerator**: A machine, or a simple tool, that mixes air into water so that more oxygen dissolves. Fish farms use them, especially at night. *e.g.* A fish farmer switches on a paddle that splashes the pond water, so that air mixes in.
- **C4 plant**: A plant that has a pump to gather carbon dioxide, so that photosynthesis goes fast even when the stomata are only a little open. Sugarcane, maize, sorghum and millets such as bajra and ragi are C4 plants. *e.g.* Sugarcane stays green and grows tall in the hot, bright months when many other plants slow down.
- **CAM plant**: A plant that opens the stomata at night to take in carbon dioxide and keeps them closed in the hot day. Pineapples, cactuses and Kalanchoe are CAM plants. *e.g.* A pineapple plant keeps a store of carbon dioxide made overnight, and uses it with sunlight the next day.

## Explanations
### Two jobs in one leaf: photosynthesis builds, respiration spends · *defined*
**Definition:** Photosynthesis uses light to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water, and gives out oxygen. Respiration breaks sugar down using oxygen to release energy, and gives out carbon dioxide and water. A plant does both.

Many people learn that plants take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen, and animals do the opposite. That is not quite right. A plant makes sugar only in their green cells, and only while light falls on them. But a plant needs energy all the time, to grow, to move water, to mend a torn leaf and to make seeds. So every cell in a plant, green or not, breaks down sugar by respiration, day and night. In bright light, a leaf makes much more sugar than they spend, so oxygen flows out and carbon dioxide flows in. In the dark, only respiration runs. Then the plant takes in a little oxygen and gives out a little carbon dioxide, just as you do. A plant grows when the sugar they make is more than the sugar they use. A plant kept in a dark cupboard for weeks keeps spending and never makes any, so they shrink and die.
- e.g. A money plant on a sunny windowsill gives out oxygen at noon and a little carbon dioxide at midnight.
- e.g. A potato in a dark cupboard respires slowly, using up the starch they stored, and goes soft and wrinkled.
- e.g. A root deep in the soil has no chlorophyll and no light, so they only respire. They live on sugar sent down from the leaves.
### Where each job happens: chloroplasts build, mitochondria spend · *defined*
**Definition:** Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts. Respiration happens inside mitochondria. A green plant cell has both, and an animal cell has only mitochondria.

A cell is too small to see, but it is full of tiny parts. Chloroplasts are green because they hold chlorophyll. Inside them, light is caught and sugar is built. Mitochondria are smaller and have no colour. Inside them, sugar is broken down with oxygen, and the energy is stored in energy carriers that the cell can spend. A leaf cell has both. In the light, the chloroplasts make sugar, and some of that sugar goes into the cell's own mitochondria at once. The rest travels to the roots, the fruits and the growing tips. A human cell, or a mushroom's cell, has mitochondria but no chloroplasts. That is why we must eat. Our cells cannot make sugar from light, but they can spend sugar that others have made. A cell can have a hundred mitochondria, or thousands, depending on how much energy the cell uses.
- e.g. A cell in a pumping heart muscle is packed with mitochondria, because it spends energy all the time.
- e.g. A potato tuber cell has no chloroplasts when it is underground, but it has mitochondria to spend the stored starch.
### The compensation point: the light where making equals spending · *defined*
**Definition:** The compensation point is the light level at which a plant's photosynthesis exactly equals their respiration. At that light, no oxygen and no carbon dioxide leaves or enters overall.

Picture a plant at night. There is no light, so they only spend sugar. Now slowly switch on a lamp. At first the light is very dim. The leaf starts making a little sugar, but still spends more than they make. As the light gets brighter, photosynthesis speeds up while respiration stays about the same. At one brightness, the two are exactly equal. That is the compensation point. The plant is just paying for their own living, with nothing left over to grow. Brighter than that, the plant has sugar to spare and grows. Dimmer, the plant is slowly using up their store. Plants that live in the shade of a forest floor, such as ferns, have a low compensation point, so they can live on very little light. Sun-loving plants such as sunflowers have a high one. This is why a sunflower sulks in a dim room and a fern does not.
- e.g. A fern can live in the dim light of a shady corner, because they break even at a low light level.
- e.g. A sunflower seedling on a gloomy windowsill grows long and weak. The light is below what they need to break even.
### Why a pond runs short of oxygen at dawn · *defined*
**Definition:** In a pond, plants and algae make oxygen only in daylight, but everything in the pond, including the plants, uses oxygen all the time. So the oxygen is highest in the afternoon and lowest just before sunrise.

Fish breathe the oxygen that is dissolved in water. Water holds much less oxygen than air does, and oxygen only slowly mixes in from the air above. A pond's main supply comes from the green plants and algae in it. In the day, they make more oxygen than the pond spends, so the oxygen level climbs and can be highest in the late afternoon. At night, the plants and algae stop making oxygen. But the fish, the plants themselves, the algae, the mud bacteria and the rotting leaves keep using it. So the level falls and falls through the night, and reaches its lowest point just before the sun comes up. If the night was warm and the day before was cloudy, the pond may be nearly empty of oxygen at dawn. Then the fish swim up to the top, where the water meets the air and holds a little more oxygen, and gulp. A fish farmer often runs an aerator in the second half of the night for this reason.
- e.g. A fish pond after three cloudy days has less oxygen at dawn than after three sunny days, because the algae made less oxygen in the dim light.
- e.g. A fish farmer who has to feed the fish less on cloudy days is protecting the pond's oxygen, because uneaten food rots and uses oxygen too.
### Warm water holds less gas than cold water · *defined*
**Definition:** The warmer water is, the less gas it can hold. So warm water holds less dissolved oxygen than cold water.

Gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide dissolve in water, a little. Cold water holds more of them. As water warms, some of the gas comes out and forms bubbles. You may have seen this: a glass of cold water from the fridge, left on a table, grows tiny bubbles on the glass as it warms up. A fizzy drink goes flat faster when it is warm for the same reason. In a pond, this matters. In hot weather, the pond water holds less oxygen to start with. And warm animals and rotting leaves use oxygen faster, because everything in a warm pond speeds up. So the hottest nights are the most dangerous ones for fish. The sea is the same. Cold seas hold more oxygen and are often rich with life, while a heated sea holds less.
- e.g. A bottle of cold fizzy drink stays fizzy longer than a warm one.
- e.g. Tiny bubbles appear on the inside of a glass of cold water when it warms in a room.
### What holds photosynthesis back: limiting factors · *defined*
**Definition:** Photosynthesis can only go as fast as its slowest ingredient allows. The ingredient in shortest supply is the limiting factor. For a leaf it can be light, carbon dioxide or warmth.

Imagine a kitchen with a cook, a stove and a pile of vegetables. If the stove is tiny, a second cook does not help. The stove is the limiting factor. A leaf is like that. On a dim morning, light is limiting. If you give the leaf more light, photosynthesis speeds up. But as the light gets stronger, a point comes when more light no longer helps, because the leaf cannot take in carbon dioxide fast enough. Now carbon dioxide has become the limiting factor. Warmth matters too. Photosynthesis uses enzymes, and enzymes work faster when it is warm, up to a point. Very hot leaves slow down or get damaged. Greenhouse growers use this idea. They add light in winter, put extra carbon dioxide in the air, and keep the greenhouse warm, but not hot. They make sure that no single factor is the one that holds the plants back.
- e.g. A tomato plant in a closed greenhouse often grows faster when the grower adds carbon dioxide gas to the air.
- e.g. In the shade on a cold morning, a leaf is limited by light and by warmth at the same time.
### Inside the chloroplast: a light stage and a sugar-building stage · *defined*
**Definition:** Photosynthesis has two stages. In the light stage, chlorophyll catches light and uses it to split water, to give out oxygen and to charge up energy carriers. In the sugar-building stage, the charged carriers give their energy to join carbon dioxide into sugar.

Photosynthesis is not one step. It is a long chain of small steps, each done by its own enzyme. Scientists sort the steps into two groups. In the first group, which needs light, chlorophyll catches photons. The energy splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. The oxygen floats away. The energy is stored in energy carriers, which are like small charged batteries. In the second group of steps, the carriers pass their energy to enzymes that take carbon dioxide from the air and join it into a chain of carbon atoms, which finishes as sugar. This second group does not use light directly, but it needs the charged carriers that only light can make. So it slows down soon after the light goes out. The steps were traced in the 1940s and 1950s by Melvin Calvin, Andrew Benson and James Bassham in California, who gave a plant radioactive carbon and followed it into sugar.
- e.g. A leaf moved into the dark keeps building sugar for a short time, using up the charged carriers that are left, and then stops.
- e.g. The oxygen comes from the first stage, and the sugar from the second.
### Rubisco: the busy enzyme that sometimes grabs the wrong gas · *defined*
**Definition:** Rubisco is the enzyme that grabs carbon dioxide from the air and fixes it into the first sugar. It is slow, and it sometimes grabs oxygen instead, which wastes energy.

Rubisco is the enzyme that takes the first step in building sugar from carbon dioxide. It is probably the most common protein on Earth, because a leaf needs a huge amount of it. Each rubisco works slowly, handling only a few carbon dioxide molecules every second. A leaf makes up for this by having a great deal of it. Rubisco has a weakness. Its pocket sometimes catches an oxygen molecule instead of carbon dioxide, because the two gases are similar in shape. When this happens, the leaf has to spend energy to clean up the mistake, and some carbon is lost as carbon dioxide. This is called photorespiration. It happens more when leaves are hot, because then the stomata close, carbon dioxide runs low inside the leaf, and oxygen builds up. Many of the plants in the next two cards have found ways around this problem.
- e.g. Rubisco makes up a large part of the protein in green leaves. When you eat spinach, much of the protein you eat is rubisco.
- e.g. On a very hot day, a rice leaf wastes more of their energy on photorespiration than on a cool day.
### Sugarcane and maize: a carbon dioxide pump (C4) · *defined*
**Definition:** C4 plants first grab carbon dioxide in outer leaf cells and pump it into inner cells, where rubisco works in a crowd of carbon dioxide. This lets them photosynthesise fast in heat and strong light, even with their stomata only a little open.

Most plants, including rice and wheat, hand carbon dioxide straight to rubisco. Sugarcane, maize, sorghum, bajra and ragi do something cleverer. Their leaves have a ring of outer cells and a ring of inner cells around each vein. The outer cells have a fast enzyme that grabs carbon dioxide and attaches it to a small molecule. The small molecule carries the carbon into the inner cells, which hold rubisco. There it releases the carbon dioxide, so that the inner cells have a strong crowd of carbon dioxide and little oxygen. So rubisco rarely makes the oxygen mistake. The plant can keep their stomata nearly closed on a hot day, losing less water, and still make sugar quickly. This is why sugarcane grows so tall and sweet in India's heat. The pump costs some energy, so in cool, dim places a rice plant can do as well. The first clues were found in the 1960s by several scientists in different countries. Hal Hatch and Roger Slack in Australia worked out the steps. In India, Janaki Ammal, who was born in Thalassery in Kerala, worked on sugarcane breeding in Coimbatore in the 1930s.
- e.g. Ragi and bajra can grow in dry, hot regions where rice would struggle.
- e.g. Sugarcane stores so much sugar in the stalk that we squeeze the stalk for juice.
### Pineapples and cactuses: opening the stomata at night (CAM) · *defined*
**Definition:** CAM plants open their stomata at night, when it is cool, and take in carbon dioxide. They store it in acid form until morning, then use it with sunlight while their stomata stay shut.

A stoma lets carbon dioxide in, but it also lets water out. In a hot, dry place, a plant that keeps the stomata open all day loses a great deal of water. CAM plants solve this by working in two shifts. At night, the stomata open. It is cool, so little water escapes. The plant takes in carbon dioxide and locks it into an acid that is stored in big spaces inside the cells. If you taste a CAM leaf in the early morning, it can taste more sour than in the evening. In the day, the stomata close tight. The plant slowly releases the carbon dioxide from the acid, and the chloroplasts use it with sunlight to make sugar. CAM plants grow slowly, but they can live on very little water. Pineapples, cactuses, agaves, jade plants and Kalanchoe all work this way. A money plant on a windowsill is not one of them.
- e.g. A pineapple plant keeps their stomata open in the cool of the night.
- e.g. A cactus can sit in a pot in a bright window for weeks without being watered.
### A carbon atom's trip: the carbon cycle · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbon atoms move round and round between the air, living things, soil, the sea and rocks. Photosynthesis takes carbon out of the air. Respiration, rotting and burning put it back.

Carbon dioxide is a very small part of the air, only about 4 parts in every 10,000. The air also holds nitrogen, about 78 parts in 100, and oxygen, about 21 parts in 100. Yet this thin supply of carbon dioxide is where almost every living body gets its carbon. Follow one carbon atom. It starts as carbon dioxide in the air above a paddy field. A rice leaf takes it in and builds it into sugar. The sugar becomes starch in a grain of rice. You eat the rice and your cells break the starch down by respiration, and the atom leaves in your breath as carbon dioxide. Back in the air, it may be caught by a tree or a phytoplankton cell in the sea. If a tree falls and fungi rot the wood, the atom returns to the air. If a tree is buried, the atom may stay locked in coal for millions of years. A carbon atom may make this trip many times. The atoms in your body are very old. Only the energy comes in fresh, as sunlight.
- e.g. The carbon in your breakfast idli was carbon dioxide in the air a few months ago.
- e.g. A carbon atom in your breath can be taken in by a leaf in a few days, or by a tree that grows for a hundred years.
### Coal and oil are old sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** Coal, oil and natural gas formed from the bodies of plants and tiny sea creatures that lived millions of years ago, and were buried before they could rot. Their energy is sunlight that was caught by photosynthesis long ago.

About 300 million years ago, huge swampy forests of giant ferns and tree-like plants covered parts of the Earth. When these plants died, they fell into wet mud that had too little oxygen for the fungi and bacteria that rot wood. Layer upon layer built up and was squashed under rock for millions of years. Slowly the buried plants turned into coal. Oil and natural gas formed mostly from tiny sea living things, such as plankton, that sank to the sea floor and were buried in the same way. When we burn coal, petrol or diesel, the carbon atoms that photosynthesis took out of the air long ago return to the air as carbon dioxide, in a single lifetime. Plants take carbon dioxide out of the air slowly, and we have been putting back carbon dioxide from fossil fuels much faster than the plants and the sea can take it up. Coal is not made from dinosaurs. The coal forests grew many millions of years before the first dinosaurs.
- e.g. A lump of coal sometimes still shows the print of a fern leaf.
- e.g. A bus running on diesel is driven by sunlight that fell on the Earth millions of years ago.
### When there is too much green: algae blooms and low-oxygen water · *defined*
**Definition:** An algae bloom is a sudden, huge growth of algae in water. When the bloom dies and rots, decomposers use up so much oxygen that fish and other water animals can suffocate.

Water that gets extra minerals can make algae grow wildly. The minerals can come from fertiliser washed off fields, from sewage, or from detergent that flows into a pond or lake. The water turns thick green, which looks healthy, and by day it can be full of oxygen. But a thick bloom blocks light from the plants below it, and at night the huge crowd of algae all use oxygen. Then the bloom dies, and bacteria and fungi rot the dead algae, using still more oxygen. The water can run so low that fish and crabs die in large numbers, sometimes in a single night. Some blooms also give out poisons. Scientists call large regions of water with too little oxygen 'dead zones'. Dead zones are found at the mouths of big rivers and in lakes around the world. Careful use of fertiliser and treating sewage before it flows into water helps to prevent them.
- e.g. Fish floating belly-up in a green pond after a hot, cloudy spell are often a sign of low oxygen.
- e.g. A pond with no fish and no plants but clear water is not necessarily healthy, while a green pond is not necessarily sick. The thing to watch is the balance.
### The Earth breathes once a year · *defined*
**Definition:** The amount of carbon dioxide in the whole Earth's air rises and falls a little every year, because the land plants of the northern half of the world take in more carbon dioxide in their summer and give back more in their winter.

In 1958, an American scientist named Charles David Keeling began measuring the carbon dioxide in the air on a mountain in Hawaii, far from any city. He found something he did not expect. Every year, the amount of carbon dioxide went down in the northern spring and summer, and went up in autumn and winter. The wiggle repeats each year. The reason is that most of the Earth's land lies in the north. In the northern spring, forests and fields burst into leaf and their photosynthesis pulls carbon dioxide out of the air. In autumn the leaves fall and rot, and respiration by plants, microbes and animals puts it back. It is as if the whole planet breathes in and out once a year. Keeling's measurements also showed a second thing. Underneath the yearly wiggle, the line has been climbing higher every year since 1958, because we burn fossil fuels.
- e.g. The yearly rise and fall can be seen in a graph of carbon dioxide over many years.
- e.g. In India, many crops follow the monsoon. The land greens up each year after the rains, and the whole country's photosynthesis rises with it.
### Is photorespiration a mistake? · *debated*
Rubisco sometimes grabs oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, and the leaf has to spend energy to repair the mistake. For a long time, many scientists have seen this as a flaw that evolution has not yet fixed. Now more scientists are asking whether it might do some good, too. This is an active question, and it matters, because if we could cut the waste, crops could grow faster.
- e.g. Engineers have tried to give plants a shortcut that avoids the wasteful cleanup. In some trials, the plants have grown more.
- e.g. The cleanup process makes some building blocks that the plant needs, such as parts of proteins.
- **Many plant scientists:** Photorespiration is mostly a waste. It costs the plant energy and carbon. Rubisco evolved when the air had little oxygen, so the mistake did not matter then. Cutting down the waste could raise crop yields.
- **Other plant scientists:** Photorespiration is not only a waste. It clears away a poisonous by-product, it makes useful building blocks, and it may protect the leaf from too much light. Removing it could harm the plant.
### Can we make leaves better at their job? · *idea*
A leaf stores only a small part of the light that falls on it. Scientists are testing ways to raise that. One group, led by Johannes Kromdijk and Stephen Long, changed tobacco plants so that their leaves recover faster when a cloud passes over after bright sun. In field trials, the plants were reported to grow about 15 percent more. Others are trying to build the sugarcane carbon dioxide pump into rice, or to add the wasteful-cleanup shortcut. These ideas are promising, but none is a quick fix. A plant is a very tangled system, and changing one step often changes ten others.
- e.g. A big project has been trying to give rice the sugarcane pump since 2008, and it has proved very difficult.
- e.g. Some scientists are asking whether plants could be grown under coloured lights in towers, to use less land.

## Mostly agreed
- Plants, algae and cyanobacteria do both photosynthesis and respiration. In light, photosynthesis is usually faster overall. In the dark, only respiration runs. *How we know:* A plant in a sealed jar takes up carbon dioxide in light and releases it in the dark. This has been measured with gas sensors for over a century.
- Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts, and respiration happens in mitochondria. *How we know:* Under a microscope, chloroplasts are seen full of chlorophyll. Scientists have broken cells open and separated the two kinds of parts, and found that each does its own job.
- Dissolved oxygen in a pond rises through the day and falls through the night, with its lowest point near dawn. *How we know:* Fish farmers and scientists measure the oxygen in ponds with sensors. The same pattern appears in ponds all over the world.
- The speed of photosynthesis is set by light, carbon dioxide and temperature, and the slowest of these is the limit. *How we know:* Researchers change one factor at a time for a leaf in a chamber. They find the rate rises with each and then levels off, where another factor takes over.
- Sugarcane, maize and other C4 plants, and pineapples, cactuses and other CAM plants, have extra steps that help them in hot or dry places. *How we know:* Scientists have followed radioactive carbon into these plants and found different first products. The leaf structure of these plants also looks different under the microscope.
- Coal, oil and natural gas come from the bodies of ancient plants and plankton. *How we know:* Coal often contains the imprints of ferns and tree trunks. Oil contains chemicals that match those made by living things, and its carbon has the chemical signature of photosynthesis.
- The amount of carbon dioxide in the air rises and falls a little each year as the northern land plants grow and die back, and it has been climbing steadily since 1958. *How we know:* Charles David Keeling's measurements on a mountain in Hawaii, and many measurements since from stations around the world, show the same pattern.
- Too many nutrients in water can cause algae blooms that lead to low oxygen and fish deaths. *How we know:* Fish deaths and dead zones have been tracked to fertiliser and sewage flowing into rivers and lakes, and the zones shrink when the flow is reduced.

## How we found out
**Theodor Wilhelm Engelmann, a German scientist who worked in Utrecht (1843 to 1909), 1882.** By the 1840s, people knew that green leaves give out oxygen in light. But which light works best? John William Draper reported that yellow light, which looks the brightest to our eyes, was the most effective. In 1882, a German scientist named Theodor Engelmann, who worked in Utrecht in the Netherlands, found a clever way to test this. He knew that some bacteria swim to where there is oxygen. He laid a long green thread of algae on a slide with these bacteria. Then he used a prism to throw a rainbow across the thread. The bacteria gathered in thick crowds at the red and the blue ends, and hardly any in the green. So red and blue light made the most oxygen, and yellow-green made little. Draper's idea was wrong. Our eyes like yellow-green, but chlorophyll does not. The bacteria had been used as tiny living meters.  
*What people believed before:* Draper and others reported that yellow light, the brightest to our eyes, was best for photosynthesis. Engelmann showed that red and blue light make the most oxygen, and green and yellow the least.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Plants take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen, and animals do the opposite.** (A rule taught in many schools and books for a long time; *rejected*)  
  For: In daylight, a green plant takes in much more carbon dioxide than they give out, and gives out more oxygen than they take in.  
  Against: A plant's cells breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide all the time. At night, the plant does only that. The plant is a net giver of oxygen only while the light is strong enough. Some animals, such as corals and sea slugs, even have photosynthesis inside or with the help of algae.
- **A pond with more plants always has more oxygen for the fish.** (A common belief among people who keep fish; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants make oxygen in the day, so a pond with more plants is full of oxygen in the afternoon.  
  Against: At night the plants use oxygen too, and a thick growth can use more than the pond can supply. Dense plants and algae are often the reason the oxygen is lowest at dawn. A balance matters more than the count.
- **Photosynthesis happens only in leaves.** (A simple answer from many school books; *rejected*)  
  For: Leaves are flat, wide and full of chloroplasts, so most of the photosynthesis on a typical plant happens in them.  
  Against: Green stems do photosynthesis, and so do green fruits and the green outer layer of many young trunks. A cactus has spines in place of leaves and does all of the photosynthesis in the stem. Algae and cyanobacteria have no leaves at all.
- **Yellow light works best for photosynthesis, because it is the brightest part of the rainbow to our eyes.** (John William Draper, an English-American scientist, reported something like this in the 1840s.; *rejected*)  
  For: To our eyes, yellow and green are the brightest colours, so it seemed natural that the plant would use them best.  
  Against: In 1882, Theodor Engelmann showed that a green alga makes the most oxygen in red and in blue light, and the least in green. A plant's chlorophyll does not see colours the way our eyes do.
- **Coal is made from dead dinosaurs.** (A common idea in children's books and in conversation; *rejected*)  
  For: Coal and dinosaurs both come from the distant past, and both are found buried in rock.  
  Against: Most coal formed from swamp plants about 300 million years ago. The first dinosaurs came many millions of years later. Oil comes mostly from tiny sea plants and animals, not from big land animals.
- **Photorespiration is only a waste, and crops would be better without it.** (Many plant scientists, but not all; *debated*)  
  For: It costs the plant energy and carbon, and plants with ways around it, such as sugarcane, grow very fast in heat.  
  Against: The process also removes a harmful by-product and makes useful building blocks. Plants that cannot do it at all die in normal air.

## Roots
We have followed oxygen and carbon dioxide through jars, ponds, sugarcane and coal. Now we ask what this says about giving, taking and belonging to one air.

**Deep truth.** The same leaves made the cabbage water bluer in the light and pinker in the dark. In light they used up more carbon dioxide than they made. In the dark they only made it. So a plant is not simply a giver of breath, because every living cell takes in and gives out.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort living things into givers and takers: plants give us air, and we and the other animals take it. That sorting leaves out that a plant also breathes in oxygen and breathes out carbon dioxide, all day and all night.  
*Evidence:* In the Breath Colours test, the jar with leaves in the light ended bluer than the jar with no leaves, and the jar with leaves in the dark ended pinker. The jars, the water and the breath were the same. Only the light was different. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and try again with more leaves.

- *who am i:* You breathe out carbon dioxide that a leaf may take in tomorrow. The leaf gives out oxygen that you may breathe in. You and the plants take turns with one breath.
- *what is life:* Every living being, from a cyanobacterium to a fish to a banyan tree, takes in and gives out all the time. Life is a flow of matter and energy, and no living being is only a giver or only a taker.
- *what is this world:* The air is shared by all of us. A carbon atom can be in a leaf, a grain, a child's breath and the sky in turn, and the whole Earth's air rises and falls once a year with the leaves of the north.

A pond at dawn shows that the same plants that fill the water with oxygen by day drain it at night. The difference is only light. Plants and animals are not two teams, with one giving and one taking. Both belong to the same flow of carbon and oxygen, and each one's waste is another's food. The sorting of living things into givers and takers is our habit. The pond, the jar and the carbon cycle show a single circulation, where light only decides which way the balance tips at that moment.

- **Bhagavad Gita 3.14 to 3.16:** Beings live on food, food comes from rain, and everything gives back to keep the great wheel turning. The Gita says that someone who only takes from the wheel and does not help it turn lives in vain. (The carbon cycle is a wheel like this. A leaf takes carbon dioxide from the air, and an animal gives carbon dioxide back. Nothing in the wheel only takes.)
- **Thirukkural 12, Thiruvalluvar:** Thiruvalluvar, the Tamil poet, wrote that rain makes the food for those who eat, and the rain is also itself a part of the food. (Water falls as rain, rises in a leaf, and goes back into the air. The rain is both what feeds the plant and a part of the plant.)
- Science (settled): Plants and algae both photosynthesise and respire, and the light decides whether the plant gives out or takes in oxygen overall.
- Science (debated): Whether photorespiration is mainly a waste or has real uses is still argued about.
- Science (open): Whether rice can be given a carbon dioxide pump like sugarcane's is unknown.
- Other view, Many ecologists: Saying that the Earth breathes is only a picture. The yearly wiggle in carbon dioxide is the total of countless separate plants, microbes and animals, not one body that breathes. Calling it breath can help us imagine it, but there is no single breather.
- Practice, Take turns with a leaf (3 min): Sit near a plant, or look at a picture of a leaf. Breathe in slowly for four counts. Breathe out for six counts, towards the leaf. Do this five times. Imagine your breath out becoming part of the leaf.
- Ask: When you breathe out near a plant, who is giving and who is taking?
- Ask: If a plant both makes and uses oxygen, is a plant a giver or a taker? And what about a fish?
- Ask: The carbon atoms in your body have been in air, in plants and in other animals. Are they yours?

## Story: The Pond at Dawn
*Anjana, her family, Beena Miss and the village are made up. The science in the story is real. In ponds, the oxygen in the water is usually lowest just before sunrise, and fish do come up to the surface and gulp then. Fish farmers do splash or churn the water, and feed less, to help. A real pond is harder to read than this one, and a farmer would also check the water with a meter.*

Anjana lived in a village near Kumarakom in Kerala, where the backwaters spread out like green cloth. Behind her grandfather's house was a fish pond, a square of dark water with coconut palms around it. In the pond swam karimeen, the pearlspot fish that Kerala is proud of.

Every morning before school, Anjana carried a steel tumbler of rice bran to the pond and scattered it on the water. Her grandfather, Appooppan, stood beside her with his arms folded and counted the fish by their ripples. He was always right.

It was the monsoon, and for four days the sky had been grey. The rain came and went. The sun never really showed its face.

On the fifth morning, Anjana woke before dawn to the sound of Appooppan banging a pot. She ran outside in her nightdress. In the grey light, she saw something she had never seen before. All the karimeen were at the top of the pond. Their mouths opened and closed at the surface. Gulp, gulp, gulp.

'They cannot breathe,' said Appooppan. His voice shook. 'The pond has turned against us. Mathew's pond went like this last year. Every fish.'

Her uncle Gopan arrived on his bicycle, still wiping sleep from his eyes. 'It is the new feed,' he said. 'Or the water. We should drain the pond and fill it fresh. Or buy the white powder from the shop in Kottayam.' Appooppan nodded slowly at both ideas, which made Anjana nervous.

Anjana looked at the water. It was thick and green, much greener than last month. 'But Appooppan,' she said, 'a green pond is full of plants. Plants make oxygen. Beena Miss said so.'

Gopan laughed. 'Plants make oxygen, kutty, and the fish are choking. Which is it?' It was a fair question. Anjana did not know the answer. She stood very still, the way she did when a sum would not come.

Then she remembered another thing that Beena Miss had said. 'A plant makes oxygen only while the sun shines on it,' Miss had said. 'But a plant uses oxygen all the time, day and night, just like you. A plant sleeps and still breathes.' The whole class had giggled.

The sun finally came out a little after nine, a pale spot behind the cloud. By ten, the fish had sunk back down. By noon they were swimming in slow circles, as calm as ever. Appooppan went inside to eat, looking puzzled.

Anjana stayed at the pond. She watched the fish. She thought about how the trouble came only at dawn, and was gone by noon. The pond had not changed during the night. The fish had not changed. The only thing that changed was the light.

That evening, she found a blank page at the back of her notebook and drew a long line. On the left she wrote the night. On the right she wrote the day. She drew the green things in the pond making oxygen on the day side. On the night side she drew nothing, and then she drew arrows going the other way. Everything in the pond was using oxygen all night. The fish, the green things, the mud, even the old rice bran at the bottom.

'Appooppan,' she said at supper, 'I think I know. In the night, nobody in the pond is making oxygen, but everybody is using it. It keeps going down and down until the sun comes up. After four dull days, the green things made only a little in the daytime. So tonight there will be even less. It is worst just before sunrise. That is when the fish came up.'

Gopan scratched his head. 'Where did you get that?' 'From a jar at school,' said Anjana. 'And from the fish.' She took a breath. 'We can test it. Tonight we wake at four, and we stir the pond with the old boat paddle until the water foams. Stirring mixes in the air. If I am right, the fish will not come up. If I am wrong, tomorrow we buy your powder.'

It rained all evening. At four, Anjana's mother Sreedevi shook everyone awake, and carried a torch out to the pond. Appooppan, Gopan and Anjana took turns with the paddle. Frogs sang in the dark. Leela Chechi from next door came over in her raincoat to see what the noise was, and stayed to hold the lamp.

At five, Anjana counted the fish at the surface with the lamp. There were only three, and they were gulping slowly. Yesterday there had been all of them. 'No feed today,' she said. 'Food that nobody eats rots, and rotting uses oxygen too.' Nobody argued.

By nine, the sun had come out for real. Appooppan stood at the edge of the pond and counted the ripples. 'All here,' he said at last. He did not say anything about powder. Then he said, very quietly, 'Four grey days and I thought the pond was cursed. It was only short of light.'

Gopan handed Anjana the paddle. 'You have the night shift,' he said, 'if the clouds come back.'

A week later, the sun was strong at noon, and Anjana sat on a stone by the pond. Near the edge, silver beads of bubbles clung to the green threads in the water. Each one was a small breath, rising into the water. Above her, a coconut palm was using the air. Below her, the pond was too. She breathed out slowly. 'Share it,' she said to both.

**For the parent:** For the parent: plants and algae make oxygen only in light, but they use oxygen all the time, like every living thing. In a pond, nothing makes oxygen at night while everything uses it, so the oxygen is lowest just before sunrise. After cloudy days, the green things have made less to begin with, so the dawn is worse. Stirring the water mixes in air from above. Feeding less leaves less food to rot.

- Ask: In a pond full of green plants, why can the oxygen still run low just before sunrise?
- Ask: Why would three cloudy days in a row make a pond more dangerous for fish than three sunny days?
- Ask: How could you test, without a meter, whether the fish gulp because of a shortage of oxygen?
- Ask: A fish farmer wakes at night to run a machine that stirs the water. Why at night, and not at noon?
- Ask: If a plant uses oxygen all the time, how can a plant still be called a maker of oxygen?

### e2 · Green Water: how much light does a pond need to turn green? (40 min, 8 days)
- **question:** Tiny green algae can turn a jar of water green, if they get what they need. Does the green grow faster in the sun, in the shade or in the dark?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which jar will be the greenest after a week? Which will be the clearest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three jars hold the same amount of the same water from the same source, with the same tiny pinch of fertiliser. They are the same kind of jar and are covered in the same way. Only the amount of light changes: sunny window, shady corner and dark cupboard. So any difference in the greenness comes from the light. With 2 to 4 children, each child owns one jar and does the same jobs, or the children share the jobs of filler, scorer and recorder.
- **safety:** Do not drink the water. A grown-up measures the fertiliser, which must be kept away from eyes and mouth. Wash your hands after touching it. Cover every jar with cloth, because uncovered standing water can breed mosquitoes. When the test is over, pour the water on the soil of a plant, away from the house. Wash hands after touching pond or tank water.
- **record:** On days 1, 3, 5 and 8, hold each jar in front of the white card with the cross. Give it a see-through score from 0 to 3. Draw the three jars on day 8.
- **results table:** Day | Jar A (sunny window): see-through score | Jar B (shady corner): see-through score | Jar C (dark cupboard): see-through score
- **schedule:** day 1: Fill and set up the three jars. Score the starting see-through. (20 min); day 3: Score the three jars. (5 min); day 5: Score the three jars. (5 min); day 8: Score the three jars, draw them, and write the claim, the evidence and the reasoning. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three jars and the water. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* Tiny green plants called algae live in pond water. We will give three jars different amounts of light. Which will turn greenest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Three jars, three amounts of light. Which jar turns greenest? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that jar will be the greenest? (good: It has the most light, and algae need light to grow.; The sun helps it.; The dark one will stay clear because nothing can grow.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 300 millilitres of water into each jar. Add a spoonful of green water to each, if you have it. Add the same tiny pinch of fertiliser to each.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the same amount of water in each jar. Add the same pinch of fertiliser to each. Make everything exactly the same.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the same water in each jar. Add the same pinch of fertiliser.
  - Note: Fertiliser feeds the algae. Use a very small amount, such as one drop or a pinch the size of a grain of rice. If you cannot find green water from a pond, a rain bucket or a fish tank, use water that has stood under a pot plant for a few days. The green may take longer to start. The best green water is from a bucket or pot saucer that has sat in sun for a week and looks slightly green.
  - Check: Why do all three jars get the same water and the same pinch of fertiliser? → **So that light is the only thing that changes**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover each jar with cloth and a rubber band. Put jar A at a sunny window, jar B in a shady corner and jar C in a dark cupboard.  
  *Parent says:* Cover each jar with cloth. This keeps mosquitoes out. Put one in the sun, one in the shade and one in the dark.  
  *Child mode:* Cover each jar with cloth. Put one in the sun, one in the shade, one in the dark.
  - Note: Choose places with the same temperature as far as you can. A shady corner could be behind a curtain, or under a table near the window. Mark each jar with a letter.
  - Wait: Look again on days 3, 5 and 8
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, bring each jar to the white card with the black cross. Give each a see-through score from 0 to 3. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Hold each jar in front of the card. Can you see the black cross sharply? Score 3 for sharp, 2 for blurry, 1 for faint and 0 for gone.  
  *Child mode:* Hold each jar in front of the card. Can you see the black cross? Score it.
  - Ask: Which jar is changing first? Is that what you expected? (good: The jar in the sun is going green first.; Nothing has changed yet.; I did not expect the shade one to change.)
  - Note: Use the same card, the same spot and the same lighting to judge every jar every time. Take a photograph too. If nothing has changed by day 3, wait. In warm sunny weather the green is usually clear in about 5 to 8 days; in cool or cloudy weather it can take two weeks, so keep scoring every 2 days if you need to.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 5 and 8, score the jars again. On day 8, draw all three jars side by side. Write your claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Score the jars again. Draw all three side by side. Which is the greenest? What does light seem to do?  
  *Child mode:* Score the jars. Draw them side by side. Which one is greenest?
  - Ask: Why does light make a pond turn green? (good: The tiny plants use light to make food, so they grow and multiply.; Light helps the algae grow and they make the water green.; More light, more green things.)
  - Note: If all three jars stayed clear after 8 days, the water may have had no algae to start, or the weather was cool. That is a real result. Add a spoon of green pond water and begin again, or carry on to day 12. If all three went green, check whether the shade jar got more light than you thought.
  - Check: The jar in the dark stayed clear. What does that tell you about algae? → **They need light to grow and multiply**
  - Think: The pond in the story had thick green water, and its fish still ran short of oxygen. What do you think thick green water does at night?
### e3 · Bubbles on the Glass: does cold or warm water hold more air? (30 min)
- **question:** Water holds some air inside it, which fish breathe. Does the water let the air go when it gets warm?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which glass will grow more tiny bubbles on its walls: the cold one or the warm one? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three glasses are filled from the same cold jug at the same time. They hold the same amount of water. Each stands in a bowl, and the only difference is the bowl water: icy, cool or hand-warm. Any difference in bubbles comes from the temperature.
- **safety:** A grown-up checks that the warm water is hand-warm, about 40 degrees Celsius, and never hot. Use glasses that are not chipped. Do not drink the water from the test. Keep the bowl water below the rim of each glass so none gets in, and stand the bowls on a flat place where they will not tip.
- **record:** Note how many minutes pass before the first bubble shows. After 10 and 30 minutes, count the bubbles in a 2-centimetre patch of the glass wall (or score 0 to 5 if there are too many to count). Take a photograph of each. Write what you think the bubbles are.
- **results table:** Glass | Bowl it stands in | Minutes until the first bubble | Bubbles in a 2-centimetre patch after 10 minutes | Bubbles in a 2-centimetre patch after 30 minutes
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three glasses and the bowls. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will stand glasses of cold water in icy, cool and warm bowls. Which glass will get the most bubbles? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three glasses, three bowls. Which glass gets the most bubbles? Why?
  - Ask: Where do you think the bubbles will come from? (good: From the air in the water.; From the glass.; The warm water boils a little.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Accept any reason.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Prepare three bowls: icy, cool and hand-warm. A grown-up checks the warm water with a thermometer. Pour fridge-cold water into three glasses and stand one in each bowl.  
  *Parent says:* Make an icy bowl, a cool bowl and a hand-warm bowl. Check that the warm bowl is not hot. Pour cold water in the glasses and stand them in the bowls.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up helps with the warm bowl. Stand each glass in a bowl.
  - Note: The warm bowl should feel like a pleasant bath, about 40 degrees Celsius. Pour all three glasses from the same jug at the same time. Do not shake the jug before pouring. With 2 to 4 children, give each child one glass and bowl to watch and score, and compare the scores at the end. In hot weather, tap water is already warm, so the cool bowl will be about room temperature, not cold.
  - Check: Why do we pour all three glasses from the same jug of fridge-cold water? → **So the water in every glass starts the same**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the glasses after 10 minutes with the magnifying glass. Score the bubbles from 0 to 5. Wait and score again at 30 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the walls of each glass. Do you see tiny bubbles? Give each glass a score. Look again later and score again.  
  *Child mode:* Look at each glass with the magnifier. Score the bubbles. Look again later.
  - Ask: Which glass has the most bubbles? What are the bubbles made of? (good: The warm one. They are air that came out of the water.; The bubbles are gas, like oxygen.; Air that was dissolved in the water.)
  - Note: Expected: the glass in the warm bowl grows tiny bubbles first and the most, the cool bowl a few, and the icy bowl the fewest or none. The glass in the cool bowl also warms up to room temperature, so it may get bubbles later. Bubbles are small. Look closely, with a magnifier or phone zoom, against a white sheet. If you see no difference, the water may have been too warm to start; repeat with water straight from the fridge, and make the icy bowl really icy.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Photograph the three glasses together. Write your claim, evidence and reasoning. Talk about what this means for a pond in hot weather.  
  *Parent says:* Which glass had the most air come out? What does that tell us about warm water? What could that mean for a fish pond in hot weather?  
  *Child mode:* Which glass got the most bubbles? What does that tell us about warm water?
  - Ask: If warm water holds less air, what might happen to the fish in a pond on a very hot night? (good: There is less oxygen for them.; They might gasp.; They use more oxygen because they are warm, and there is less to use.)
  - Check: In the test, the warm water grew more bubbles than the cold water. What does this show? → **Warm water holds less dissolved air**
  - Think: On a hot night, a pond has less oxygen to start with, and the fish and plants use oxygen faster. What would you do if you looked after the pond?

### a3 · From Air to Lamp Black: the carbon in a flame (20 min)

### c1 · Greenhouse Budget: spend 100 coins to grow the most tomatoes (35 min)
- **problem:** A greenhouse grower has 100 coins. Light, carbon dioxide and warmth can each be raised from level 1 to level 2 or 3, at a price. A tomato plant grows only as fast as the lowest of the three levels. Find the best way to spend the coins.
- **your turn:** Make your best plan on paper. Say which factor limits the growth and why spending more on another factor would be wasted. Then ask: what is the limiting factor for a plant in your own home, and what free thing could you do about it?
- **constraints:** Spend no more than 100 coins in total.; Light costs 20 coins for level 2 and 50 coins for level 3. Carbon dioxide costs 30 coins for level 2 and 60 coins for level 3. Warmth costs 10 coins for level 2 and 40 coins for level 3.; Every factor starts at level 1 for free.; Growth equals the lowest of the three levels. If two plans have the same growth, the plan with the higher total of all three levels wins.
- **general:** Spend the coins evenly: raise every factor to level 2 and keep the spare coins, for growth 2.; Raise the cheapest factor first, which is warmth, then the next cheapest, and stop when you can no longer afford to raise all three.; Check that you are not spending coins on a factor that is already above the lowest.
- **creative:** Find a free way to raise a level: for example, move the plants to the sunniest bench for free light, or open vents for free fresh air with more carbon dioxide.; Share the greenhouse with a neighbour and split the costs of carbon dioxide so that both reach level 3.; Ask which factor is the limiting one on a cloudy winter day, and change the plan for that season.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem and the price list. Ask your child to say what limits a plant on a dim day. Write down the answer.

  - Ask: Which plan gives the most growth, and how much does it cost? (good: Raising all three to level 2 costs 60 and gives growth 2.; Raising only light to level 3 gives growth 1, because the others are still level 1.; Level 3 for all costs 150, which is too much.)
  - Note: The most growth that 100 coins can buy is 2, because level 3 for all three costs 150. All three at level 2 costs 60. The tie-break then picks a plan with a higher total. Light 2, carbon dioxide 2 and warmth 3 costs 90 and totals 7. Light 3, carbon dioxide 2 and warmth 2 also costs 90 and totals 7. Light 3 and warmth 3 with carbon dioxide at 1 costs 90 but gives growth 1.

### Making and Spending (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What does a green plant do with the air during the night?  
   Options: Take in a little oxygen and give out a little carbon dioxide / Take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen / Nothing, because plants sleep at night / Take in only nitrogen  
   **Answer:** Take in a little oxygen and give out a little carbon dioxide. *Why:* At night there is no light, so photosynthesis stops. But every cell of the plant still respires, so they take in some oxygen and give out some carbon dioxide. Picking that plants do nothing is a common wrong idea.
2. (fill) The job that needs light and builds sugar from carbon dioxide and water is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['photosynthesis']. *Why:* Photosynthesis means putting together with light. It happens in the green parts of plants, in algae and in cyanobacteria.
3. (truefalse) The roots of a plant do photosynthesis.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Roots under the ground get no light and have no chlorophyll. They respire, and live on sugar sent down from the leaves.
4. (mcq) Blue-purple cabbage water turns pink when you blow into it through a straw. What does your breath add to the water?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide, which makes the water slightly sour / Oxygen, which makes the water pink / Heat, which changes the colour / Nitrogen, which bleaches the cabbage  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide, which makes the water slightly sour. *Why:* Your breath has a lot of carbon dioxide. It dissolves in water and makes a weak acid, and cabbage water turns pinker with acid. Heat from the breath is far too small to change the colour.
5. (sort) Each of these belongs to photosynthesis or to respiration. Sort them.  
   **Answer:** Needs light → Photosynthesis; Happens in chloroplasts → Photosynthesis; Happens in mitochondria → Respiration; Goes on in the day and the night → Respiration; Gives out oxygen → Photosynthesis; Breaks sugar down to release energy → Respiration. *Why:* Photosynthesis needs light, happens in chloroplasts and gives out oxygen. Respiration happens in mitochondria, goes on all the time and releases energy from sugar. A strong answer keeps the two jobs apart.
6. (mcq) Why do fish in a green pond often gasp at the surface just before sunrise?  
   Options: The oxygen in the water is at its lowest point then / The water is at its warmest then / The plants have made too much oxygen / The fish are hungry  
   **Answer:** The oxygen in the water is at its lowest point then. *Why:* All night, the fish, the plants and the mud use oxygen, and nothing makes any. So the oxygen is lowest just before the sun comes up. Too much oxygen would not make fish gasp.
7. (order) Put these in order, starting from a sunny afternoon, to show what happens to the oxygen in a pond.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Oxygen rises while there is light. When the light goes, the making stops but the using goes on, so the oxygen falls until the sun returns.
8. (truefalse) Warm water holds more dissolved oxygen than cold water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Warm water holds less dissolved gas than cold water. This is one reason hot nights are dangerous for pond fish.
9. (match) Match each part of a plant to its job with gases and sugar.  
   **Answer:** Chloroplast → Sugar is built using light; Mitochondrion → Sugar is broken down with oxygen; Stoma → Gases move in and out of the leaf; Guard cell → Opens and closes the stoma. *Why:* Chloroplasts build sugar and mitochondria spend it. Stomata are the doors for gases, and the guard cells are the two cells that open and close each door.
10. (mcq) A pot plant is kept in a dark cupboard for a whole month. What happens to the sugar the plant has stored?

   **Answer:** It slowly gets used up, because the plant respires and makes no new sugar. *Why:* In the dark, photosynthesis stops, but respiration goes on. The plant keeps spending sugar and makes none, so the store shrinks and the plant weakens.
11. (fill) Pineapples and cactuses open their stomata at night so that they lose less ____.  
   **Answer:** ['water']. *Why:* At night it is cooler, so less water escapes through the open stomata. These plants take in carbon dioxide at night and use it the next day, with their stomata closed.

### Limits, Pumps and the Carbon Trip (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A leaf is at its compensation point. What is true about its gases overall?  
   Options: No oxygen or carbon dioxide leaves or enters overall / Oxygen leaves in large amounts / Only carbon dioxide enters / The leaf makes no sugar  
   **Answer:** No oxygen or carbon dioxide leaves or enters overall. *Why:* At the compensation point, the sugar made equals the sugar spent. The oxygen from photosynthesis is used by respiration, and the carbon dioxide from respiration is used by photosynthesis, so nothing moves in or out overall. The leaf is still making sugar.
2. (mcq) A leaf is in bright light, but there is very little carbon dioxide around them. Adding more light does not speed up photosynthesis. What is the limiting factor?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide / Light / Chlorophyll colour / The size of the pot

3. (fill) A carbon atom in your breath may next be taken in by a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['leaf', 'plant', 'alga', 'algae']. *Why:* Carbon dioxide in the air is taken in by leaves, and by algae and cyanobacteria in the sea. Then it is built into sugar, and the trip goes on.

   **Answer:** The land plants of the north are growing and taking in carbon dioxide. *Why:* Most of the Earth's land lies in the north. When the leaves open in spring, photosynthesis pulls carbon dioxide out of the air. In autumn, the fallen leaves rot and give it back.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which statement about plants and the air is the most complete?  
   Options: Plants respire all the time, and in good light they also photosynthesise, so overall they give out oxygen / Plants only take in carbon dioxide and only give out oxygen / Plants take in oxygen only at night and never in the day / Plants do not respire because they make their own food  
   **Answer:** Plants respire all the time, and in good light they also photosynthesise, so overall they give out oxygen. *Why:* Every living cell respires. Plants also photosynthesise when there is light, and in good light that is faster, so they give out more oxygen than they use. Making food does not stop a plant needing energy from that food.
2. (mcq) A jar of blue-purple cabbage water with breath in it turns pink. A jar with the same water and breath, with leaves in the light, ends up bluer than that jar. What does this show?  
   Options: The leaves in the light used up carbon dioxide / The leaves gave out carbon dioxide / The light turned the water blue / The breath changed itself  
   **Answer:** The leaves in the light used up carbon dioxide. *Why:* Pink means more carbon dioxide. Bluer means less. The jar with leaves in the light lost carbon dioxide because photosynthesis used it.
3. (mcq) When is the dissolved oxygen in a still pond usually lowest?  
   Options: Just before sunrise / At noon / At about three in the afternoon / Just after sunset  
   **Answer:** Just before sunrise. *Why:* The oxygen falls through the whole night, because nothing makes any and everything uses it. It is lowest just before the sun comes up.
4. (mcq) Why is warm water a problem for pond fish on a hot night?  
   Options: It holds less oxygen, and the living things in it use oxygen faster / It holds more oxygen, which is too much / It makes the plants stop breathing / It makes the water heavy  
   **Answer:** It holds less oxygen, and the living things in it use oxygen faster. *Why:* Warm water holds less dissolved gas, and warm bodies and microbes use oxygen faster. Both effects lower the oxygen at once.
5. (mcq) Where in a cell does respiration mainly happen?  
   Options: In the mitochondria / In the chloroplasts / In the cell wall / In the stomata  
   **Answer:** In the mitochondria. *Why:* Mitochondria break sugar down with oxygen to release energy. Chloroplasts are where photosynthesis happens. Stomata are holes in the leaf, not parts of a cell.
6. (mcq) A graph of photosynthesis rises with light, then goes flat. A grower adds carbon dioxide and the graph rises again. What was limiting the plants before?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide / Light / Soil colour / The age of the plant  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide. *Why:* The flat part means light was no longer the limit. When adding carbon dioxide made the rate rise, carbon dioxide was the limiting factor there.
7. (mcq) Why do pineapples and cactuses open their stomata at night?  
   Options: It is cooler, so they lose less water while taking in carbon dioxide / They need moonlight to make sugar / They cannot open them in the day because they are asleep / They breathe out more oxygen at night  
   **Answer:** It is cooler, so they lose less water while taking in carbon dioxide. *Why:* CAM plants take in carbon dioxide at night, when little water escapes, and store it. They keep the stomata shut in the hot day and use the stored carbon dioxide with sunlight.
8. (mcq) Sugarcane stays green and grows fast in hot, bright weather. What helps it do so?  
   Options: A pump that gathers carbon dioxide, so photosynthesis stays fast even when the stomata are only a little open / More chlorophyll than any other plant / It does not need light / It does not do respiration  
   **Answer:** A pump that gathers carbon dioxide, so photosynthesis stays fast even when the stomata are only a little open. *Why:* Sugarcane is a C4 plant. Its carbon dioxide pump keeps a crowd of carbon dioxide round rubisco, so it works well in heat with its stomata partly shut.
9. (mcq) Coal and oil are called fossil fuels. Which is the best description of where their energy came from?  
   Options: Sunlight caught by plants and tiny sea creatures millions of years ago / Heat from inside the Earth only / The Moon / Electricity stored in rocks  
   **Answer:** Sunlight caught by plants and tiny sea creatures millions of years ago. *Why:* The carbon in coal and oil was taken from the air by photosynthesis long ago. The stored energy is that ancient sunlight.
10. (mcq) Each year, the carbon dioxide in the world's air dips in the northern spring and summer. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Land plants in the north are growing and taking carbon dioxide in / Fewer people breathe in summer / The air gets colder / The oceans stop taking in gas  
   **Answer:** Land plants in the north are growing and taking carbon dioxide in. *Why:* Most of the land lies in the north. When the leaves open, photosynthesis pulls carbon dioxide out of the air. In autumn the leaves fall and rot, and it goes back.
11. (truefalse) A very green pond is always a healthy pond for fish.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A thick bloom can make the oxygen crash at night or when the bloom dies and rots. What matters is the balance of making and using oxygen.
12. (mcq) In 1882, Theodor Engelmann shone a rainbow on a thread of green alga with oxygen-loving bacteria around it. Why did the bacteria gather in the red and blue parts?  
   Options: Those colours made the alga give out the most oxygen, and the bacteria swim towards oxygen / The bacteria like to eat red and blue light / Those colours are the warmest / Green light is poisonous to bacteria  
   **Answer:** Those colours made the alga give out the most oxygen, and the bacteria swim towards oxygen. *Why:* The bacteria were a living instrument. They gather where there is most oxygen. The crowds in the red and blue showed that chlorophyll uses those colours best.

## Applications
- Fish and shrimp farmers watching the oxygen in their ponds, and running aerators at night.
- Greenhouse growers adding light, warmth and carbon dioxide so that no single factor limits the crop.
- Breeding crops such as ragi, bajra and sugarcane that thrive in heat and dry weather.
- Choosing house plants that suit a dim room, because they break even at low light.
- Planting forests and protecting seaweed beds and mangroves, which take carbon out of the air.
- Monitoring the Earth's plants from space, using the green colour of chlorophyll.
- Understanding fuels, because coal, oil and gas are ancient photosynthesis.
- Designing sealed life-support systems for space stations, where plants and people trade breath.
- Treating sewage and managing fertiliser so that ponds and lakes do not turn into dead zones.
- Making artificial leaves that split water with sunlight, to make clean fuel.

## Spotted around us
- Tiny silver bubbles on green threads or water plants in a sunny pond or tank. These are bubbles of oxygen.
- Fish coming up to the surface of a pond or tank early in the morning.
- A paddle wheel or a fountain at a fish farm, churning the water.
- A cactus, an aloe or a pineapple plant on a sunny balcony or in a market.
- Thick green sugarcane fields in the hottest months, with sellers pressing sugarcane juice by the roadside.
- A sealed bottle garden or a closed glass terrarium in a shop or a home.
- Black smoke and soot from a diya, a candle or a vehicle's exhaust. The black is carbon.
- A black seam of coal in a picture of a coal mine, or in a lump at a museum, sometimes showing the print of a fern.

## Open questions
- **Is photorespiration a flaw, a safety valve, or both?** Scientists agree that it costs energy. They disagree about how much good it does, and the answer may differ from plant to plant.
- **Could rice be given the sugarcane pump?** A large project has been trying since 2008. The pump needs many changes in leaf shape and chemistry, and no one has finished it yet.
- **How much of the carbon dioxide we put into the air will land plants and the sea keep taking in?** Plants and the sea have taken up a large share so far. Scientists do not agree on how long that will last as the world warms.
- **How do plants and algae decide when to open and close their stomata over the day?** Scientists know parts of the signals, such as light, water and carbon dioxide. They are still working out how the leaf combines them.
- **Could photosynthesis work on a planet around a red star, where the light is redder and weaker?** Some scientists think plants there might look dark or even black. Nobody has yet seen a plant on another planet to check.
- **How did the first living things that made oxygen survive their own waste?** Oxygen is harmful to many cells that did not evolve with it. Scientists have clues from ancient rocks, but the story is far from complete.

## Connected maths
- At rest, Ravi breathes 20 times in one minute. How many breaths does he take in one hour? And in one whole day of 24 hours? **1,200 breaths in an hour and 28,800 breaths in a day.**. There are 60 minutes in an hour. 20 times 60 is 1,200. A day has 24 hours. 1,200 times 24 is 28,800. Respiration never stops, so this many breaths bring in oxygen all day and night. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- Your breath has about 4 parts in 100 carbon dioxide. Ordinary air has about 4 parts in 10,000. How many times more carbon dioxide is in your breath than in ordinary air? **About 100 times more.**. Write 4 in 100 as 400 in 10,000. Now both are out of 10,000. 400 divided by 4 is 100. So the breath has about 100 times more. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- On the sunny day in the pond graph, the oxygen reached 9.0 at 3 pm and fell to 2.8 at 6 am the next morning. By how much did the oxygen fall? **6.2.**. Take away 2.8 from 9.0. 9.0 minus 2.8 is 6.2. The oxygen fell by 6.2 milligrams per litre over the evening and night. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- On the cloudy day, the oxygen peaked at 4.5 and fell to 1.5. The sunny day fell by 6.2. About how many times bigger was the sunny day swing than the cloudy day swing? Why do you think it was bigger? **About 2 times bigger. The sunny swing was bigger because the plants made much more oxygen in the bright light.**. The cloudy swing is 4.5 minus 1.5, which is 3.0. 6.2 is about 6, and 6 divided by 3 is 2. In bright light, photosynthesis makes more oxygen by day, so there is more to fall by night. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- Each jar in the Breath Colours test holds 250 millilitres of water and six leaves. About how many millilitres of water is there for each leaf? If you used twelve leaves, what would happen to this number, and what might it do to the colour change? **About 42 millilitres for each leaf. With twelve leaves it would be about 21, and the colour might change faster.**. 250 divided by 6 is about 42. Twelve leaves is double the leaves, so each has half the water, about 21. More leaves for the same water means each bit of water gets more attention from the leaves, so the colour change could be quicker and easier to see. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- In the Green Water test, jar A scored 3, 3, 2 and 0 on days 1, 3, 5 and 8. Jar C scored 3 every time. How many points did each jar lose from day 1 to day 8? What does the difference tell you? **Jar A lost 3 points and jar C lost 0. The jar in the light became much less see-through, so light let the algae grow.**. Jar A went from 3 to 0, so it lost 3. Jar C stayed at 3, so it lost 0. A lower score means greener water. The jar that changed was the one with light, so light is what the algae needed. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A field receives 500 units of sunlight on a day. Crops store about 2 out of every 100 parts as food. How many units are stored? How many units are not stored? **10 units are stored and 490 are not stored.**. 2 out of 100 is 2 percent. Half of 500 is 250, a tenth of 500 is 50, and one hundredth of 500 is 5. So 2 hundredths is 10. 500 minus 10 is 490. Most light is not stored as food. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the Greenhouse Budget challenge, a grower buys light level 3 for 50 coins, carbon dioxide level 2 for 30 coins and warmth level 2 for 10 coins. How much does it cost? How many coins are left from 100? What is the growth? **It costs 90 coins. 10 coins are left. The growth is 2.**. 50 plus 30 is 80, and 80 plus 10 is 90. 100 minus 90 is 10. Growth is the lowest of the levels 3, 2 and 2, which is 2. The extra level of light does not help, because carbon dioxide and warmth are still at 2. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)
- A pond has 6 milligrams of oxygen per litre at 6 pm. Through the night, the oxygen falls by about 0.5 milligrams per litre each hour. How many hours until it reaches 3, the level where many pond fish are stressed? At about what time will that be? **6 hours, at about midnight.**. The oxygen must fall by 3, from 6 to 3. 3 divided by 0.5 is 6. Six hours after 6 pm is midnight. If the night is hotter, the fall is faster and the time comes sooner. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is photosynthesis? → How plants, algae and some bacteria build sugar from carbon dioxide and water, using light.
- What is respiration? → How a cell gets energy by breaking sugar down with oxygen, giving out carbon dioxide.
- Do plants respire at night? → Yes. Every living cell respires all the time.
- When is the oxygen in a pond lowest? → Just before sunrise.
- Does warm water hold more or less dissolved oxygen than cold water? → Less.
- What does the compensation point mean? → The light level where a plant makes exactly as much sugar as they spend.
- What is a limiting factor? → The thing in shortest supply, which sets how fast something can go.
- Name a plant that opens its stomata at night. → A pineapple, a cactus or a Kalanchoe.
- Where did the carbon in coal come from? → From carbon dioxide taken in by swamp plants about 300 million years ago.
