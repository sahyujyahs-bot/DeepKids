# Day and night: how the spinning Earth makes them
`sci.day-night-seasons.day-and-night.L3`

**Summary.** The Sun never stops shining. When it is night here, our side of the Earth has simply turned away from it. The Earth is a huge ball that spins once in about 24 hours. Half of it always faces the Sun and has day. The other half has night. The spin carries every place into the light and out again. That is why the Sun seems to rise in the east and set in the west. We cannot feel the spin, but a swinging pendulum, the stars turning round the Pole Star and photos from space all show it. The spin is very slowly getting slower. Scientists are still working out the details of how the Earth first got its spin.

**Intro.** *Right now, as you read this, you are moving faster than an aeroplane. You are not in a car or a train. You are sitting still. So how can that be?* In most of India, the ground under your feet is moving at about 1,500 kilometres an hour. A passenger jet flies at about 900. Your house, the trees, the air and you all move together, so you feel nothing. In the afternoon here, it is night for children in America, and their Sun has not gone anywhere. Their side of the Earth is facing away from it. So why can't we feel the Earth spin, and how does the spin give us day and night?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that day and night happen because the Earth spins, so each place turns towards the Sun and then away from it.
1. I can show day and night with a ball and a torch, and say why half of the Earth always has day.
2. I can explain that the Sun only seems to move across the sky, rising in the east and setting in the west, because the Earth turns from west to east.
3. I can say that one whole spin takes about 24 hours, and read the time of sunrise, noon and sunset on a clock.
4. I can explain why places further east, like Arunachal Pradesh, see the Sun rise before places further west, like Kutch.
5. I can give two pieces of evidence that the Earth really spins, like a Foucault pendulum and stars turning round the Pole Star.
6. I can say what scientists agree on about the Earth's spin, and what they still do not know.
7. I can link the spin to bigger ideas: days getting slowly longer, days on other planets, body clocks and why the night sky is dark.

## Terms
- **Day**: The time when our side of the Earth faces the Sun, so we get sunlight. People also say 'a day' for one whole spin of the Earth, which takes 24 hours. *e.g.* At 11 in the morning in Pune, it is day. The sky is bright and you can see without a lamp.
- **Night**: The time when our side of the Earth faces away from the Sun. No sunlight reaches us, so it is dark. *e.g.* At 11 at night in Pune, it is dark. Street lights are on and the stars are out.
- **Earth**: The planet we live on. It is a huge rocky ball, and it floats in space. *e.g.* Every place you have ever been is on the Earth.
- **Sun**: The star at the centre of our solar system. It is a giant ball of very hot glowing gas. It gives us light and warmth. *e.g.* The Sun is so big that about a million Earths could fit inside it.
- **Star**: A giant ball of hot gas that makes its own light. Our Sun is a star. The other stars are so far away that they look like tiny dots. *e.g.* Dhruva Tara, the Pole Star, is a star much bigger than our Sun. It looks small only because it is so far away.
- **Spin (rotate)**: To turn round and round on the spot, like a top or a fan. Scientists say the Earth rotates. *e.g.* A lattu, a wooden spinning top, spins on its point. The Earth spins too, but much more slowly.
- **Rotation**: One whole spin. The Earth makes one rotation in about 24 hours. This rotation gives us day and night. *e.g.* From one sunrise to the next sunrise is about one rotation of the Earth.
- **Axis**: An imaginary line that a spinning thing turns around. The Earth's axis goes through the North Pole and the South Pole. *e.g.* Push a pencil through an orange and twirl it. The pencil is the orange's axis.
- **North Pole and South Pole**: The two ends of the Earth's axis. They are the top and bottom points of the spinning Earth. *e.g.* The North Pole is in the middle of a frozen sea. The South Pole is on the icy land of Antarctica.
- **Sunrise**: The moment the Sun first appears at the edge of the sky in the morning. Really, it is the moment our place on the Earth turns into the sunlight. *e.g.* The Sun always rises somewhere in the east.
- **Sunset**: The moment the Sun goes below the edge of the sky in the evening. Really, it is the moment our place turns away from the sunlight. *e.g.* The Sun always sets somewhere in the west.
- **Noon (midday)**: The middle of the day, when the Sun is at its highest in the sky. Shadows are shortest at noon. *e.g.* At noon your shadow is at its shortest. On a summer noon in India, it is only a small patch near your feet.
- **Horizon**: The line far away where the land or the sea seems to meet the sky. *e.g.* At the beach, the horizon is the line where the sea meets the sky.
- **East and west**: Two opposite directions. The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west. If you face the sunrise, west is behind you. *e.g.* Arunachal Pradesh is in the east of India. Gujarat is in the west.
- **Day-night line**: The line on the Earth between the half in sunlight and the half in darkness. Places on this line are having sunrise or sunset. Scientists call it the terminator. *e.g.* On a ball lit by a torch, you can see a soft line where the light ends.
- **Apparent movement**: When something seems to move, but really it is you who is moving. The Sun's movement across our sky is apparent. *e.g.* From a moving train, the trees seem to rush backwards. The trees are still. You are moving.
- **Shadow**: A dark shape made when something blocks the light. A shadow always falls on the side away from the light. *e.g.* In the morning, when the Sun is in the east, your shadow points west.
- **Pendulum**: A weight hanging on a string or wire, swinging to and fro. *e.g.* A heavy nut tied to a string and swung gently is a pendulum. So is a swing in the park.
- **Pole Star (Dhruva Tara)**: A star that sits almost exactly above the North Pole. As the Earth spins, all the other stars seem to circle around it, but it hardly moves. *e.g.* From India, you find it in the northern sky, using the Saptarishi stars as pointers. The further south you live, the lower it sits.
- **Twilight**: The soft light in the sky just before sunrise and just after sunset. The Sun is below the horizon, but the air above us still catches its light. *e.g.* After sunset, you can still see your way home for a while. That is twilight.
- **Orbit**: The path one thing takes as it goes round another in space. The Earth orbits the Sun once a year. That is a different movement from the spin. *e.g.* The Earth spins about 365 times while it goes once round the Sun.
- **Body clock**: A timer inside living things that keeps track of day and night, even without a clock. It tells your body when to feel sleepy and when to wake. *e.g.* You often wake up at about the same time each day, even on holidays.

## Explanations
### Day and night: half the Earth is always lit · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun lights up the half of the Earth that faces it. There it is day. The other half is in the Earth's own shadow. There it is night.

The Sun shines all the time. It never switches off at night. But sunlight can only reach the side of the Earth that faces the Sun. The far side is dark, like the back of your head when you face a lamp. So at every moment, half of the Earth has day and half has night. When it is 9 at night in India, it is morning in parts of America. Their Sun is the very same Sun as ours.
- e.g. Hold an orange in front of a lamp in a dark room. The side facing the lamp is bright. The back is dark.
- e.g. When you go to bed in Chennai, children in California are getting up for school.
- e.g. From space, astronauts see the Earth as half bright and half dark, with cities glowing on the dark half.
### The Earth spins, once in about 24 hours · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth spins around an imaginary line called its axis. One whole spin takes about 24 hours. This spin is called rotation.

The Earth's axis goes through the North Pole and the South Pole. The Earth turns from west to east. If you looked down on the North Pole from space, you would see it turn anticlockwise. As it turns, every place is carried into the sunlight, across the middle of the day, and out into the dark again. That is one day and one night. The Earth is very big, so even one slow turn a day moves the ground very fast. In India, the ground moves at nearly 1,500 kilometres an hour. At the equator, the fattest part of the Earth, it moves at about 1,670 kilometres an hour.
- e.g. A dot drawn on a spinning ball goes into the torchlight, across it, and out again. That is like your town's day.
- e.g. A ceiling fan spins around its middle rod. The Earth spins around its axis in the same way.
- e.g. Places near the poles move slowly. Places near the equator move fastest, because they have the longest circle to go round.
### The Sun only seems to move · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun seems to rise in the east, cross the sky and set in the west. This is apparent movement. It is really the Earth turning under us.

Sit in a train as it pulls out of the station. The platform seems to slide backwards. The platform is still. You are the one moving. The sky works in the same way. The Earth turns from west to east. So we are carried towards the east, and the Sun seems to slide the other way, towards the west. In the morning our side of the Earth turns towards the Sun, and we call it sunrise. In the evening our side turns away, and we call it sunset. We still use the words sunrise and sunset. Scientists use them too. But nothing is rising or setting. Our ground is turning.
- e.g. From a moving bus, trees and poles seem to rush past backwards.
- e.g. Spin slowly on a swivel chair. The whole room seems to turn around you.
- e.g. The Moon and the stars also seem to rise in the east and set in the west, for the same reason.
### Aryabhata's boat: an idea from India, 499 CE · *defined*
**Definition:** Aryabhata, an Indian astronomer, wrote in 499 CE that the Earth turns, and that the stars only seem to move.

Aryabhata lived in Kusumapura, near today's Patna. When he was 23 years old, he finished a book called the Aryabhatiya. In it he gave an example. A man in a boat moving forward sees the trees on the bank moving backwards. In the same way, he said, the still stars seem to move west. So the Earth must be turning. Most astronomers of his time disagreed. Later Indian astronomers, including Brahmagupta, argued against him. Aryabhata had no way to prove it. About a thousand years later, Copernicus in Poland also wrote that the Earth spins, in 1543. Today we know Aryabhata was right.
- e.g. From a boat on the Ganga, the ghats seem to glide past, though the boat is the thing that moves.
- e.g. Greek thinkers also had this idea. Heraclides of Pontus said the Earth turns, about 2,300 years ago.
### Why we do not feel the Earth spin · *defined*
**Definition:** We do not feel the spin because it is smooth and steady, and everything around us moves with us.

You only feel movement when it changes: a speed-up, a slow-down, a bump or a sharp turn. In a smooth train, with your eyes shut, you can hardly tell that you are moving. The Earth's spin is smoother than any train. It never brakes and never bumps. The ground, the air, the birds and you all move together. So when you jump, you land in the same place. The ground does not slide away under you, because you were already moving with it. Galileo, an Italian scientist, explained this in 1632. He said a stone dropped from the top of a ship's mast lands at the foot of the mast, even while the ship sails.
- e.g. Toss a ball straight up in a smoothly moving train. It falls back into your hand.
- e.g. In an aeroplane flying smoothly, you can pour water into a cup without spilling.
- e.g. Clouds and birds are not left behind. The air moves with the Earth.
### How we know the Earth really spins · *defined*
**Definition:** Several kinds of evidence show that it is the Earth that spins, not the sky that turns. One famous piece is the Foucault pendulum.

In 1851, a French scientist, Léon Foucault, hung a heavy ball on a 67-metre wire in the Panthéon, a great hall in Paris. A swinging pendulum keeps swinging in the same direction unless something pushes it sideways. But as the hours went by, the swing seemed to turn slowly around the floor. Nothing was pushing it. The floor, and the whole Earth, was turning under it. In Paris, the swing goes all the way round in about 32 hours. In Delhi it takes about 50 hours. India's new Parliament building, opened in 2023, has a Foucault pendulum too. There is more evidence. All the stars wheel around the Pole Star once a day. Big storms like cyclones spin one way north of the equator and the other way south of it. And cameras on satellites have filmed the Earth turning.
- e.g. A long-exposure photo pointed at the Pole Star shows the stars as curved streaks around it.
- e.g. Cyclones over the Bay of Bengal always spin anticlockwise. In Australia, they spin clockwise.
- e.g. Satellites photograph the day-night line sliding across the Earth.
### Sunrise travels across India · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth turns from west to east, so places in the east of India turn into the sunlight first. Sunrise sweeps across the country from east to west.

Dong, a village in Arunachal Pradesh, is one of the first places in India to see the sunrise. In June, about two hours later, the Sun rises in the far west of Kutch, in Gujarat. Between them, sunrise reaches Kolkata, then Nagpur, then Mumbai. But all of India uses one clock time, called Indian Standard Time. It is set by the Sun over a line passing near Mirzapur, in Uttar Pradesh, close to the middle of the country. So in Arunachal, the clock says it is early when the Sun is already up. In Gujarat, the clock says it is late when the Sun has only just risen. Tea gardens in Assam have long used their own clock, called tea garden time, one hour ahead of the rest of India.
- e.g. On a summer morning, the Sun can rise in Dong before 4:30, by the clock.
- e.g. In Bhuj, in Kutch, summer sunrise comes at about 6 o'clock.
- e.g. In Guwahati in June, the Sun is up by about 4:30 by the clock, long before shops and offices open.
### What a day really is: two kinds of day · *defined*
**Definition:** One spin measured against the far stars takes about 23 hours and 56 minutes. One day from noon to noon takes 24 hours. Our clocks use the 24-hour day.

While the Earth spins, it is also travelling on its long path around the Sun. So after one full spin, the Sun is no longer in quite the same place in our sky. The Earth has to turn about 4 minutes more to bring the Sun back to the top of the sky. So a star comes back to the same place 4 minutes earlier each night. Over a year, that adds up. That is why the night sky in December shows different stars from the night sky in June.
- e.g. If you see a bright star above a rooftop at 9 tonight, it will be there at about 8:56 tomorrow.
- e.g. Orion, the hunter, is easy to see on winter evenings in India, but not on summer evenings.
### Why the Earth spins at all · *idea*
Scientists think the Earth got its spin when it was born, about 4.5 billion years ago. The Sun and planets formed from a huge, slowly swirling cloud of gas and dust. As the cloud squashed together, it spun faster, the way a spinning dancer turns faster when she pulls her arms in. The lumps that became the Earth were spinning too. Then many think a planet about the size of Mars crashed into the young Earth. The bits thrown out became the Moon. That crash probably changed the Earth's spin as well. In space, there is almost nothing to rub against, so a spinning thing keeps spinning. That is why the Earth is still turning today. The broad story is widely accepted. The details, like how fast the young Earth spun, are still being worked out.
- e.g. Spin on a swivel chair with your arms out, then pull them in. You speed up.
- e.g. A spinning top slows down because its point rubs the floor. The Earth has no floor to rub.
- **Most planet scientists:** The spin came from the swirling cloud the solar system formed from, and was changed by big crashes, like the one that made the Moon.
- **Scientists who study the early Earth:** How long the very first days were is not known. Some estimates say just a few hours. Rocks and computer models give different answers.
### Days are slowly getting longer · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's spin is very slowly slowing down. So each day is a tiny bit longer than days long ago.

The Moon's gravity pulls the oceans into a bulge, which makes the tides. As the Earth turns, the sea rubs against the sea floor. This friction acts like a gentle brake on the Earth's spin. A day gets about 2 thousandths of a second longer every 100 years. That is far too little to notice in a lifetime. But over millions of years it adds up. In 1963, John Wells, an American scientist, counted daily growth lines on fossil corals 400 million years old. He found that a year then had about 400 days, so each day was only about 22 hours long. In 2020, a team led by Niels de Winter studied a fossil shell from the time of the dinosaurs. They found that a day then was about half an hour shorter than ours.
- e.g. A coral adds a thin layer each day, like a diary written in stone.
- e.g. The Moon is also slowly moving away from us, by about 4 centimetres a year. Mirrors left on the Moon by astronauts let scientists measure this with lasers.
### Days on other worlds · *defined*
**Definition:** Every planet spins at its own speed, so a day lasts a different time on each planet.

Jupiter is the biggest planet, yet it spins fastest. Its day is only about 10 hours long. A day on Mars is a little longer than ours, about 24 hours and 40 minutes. Venus spins very slowly, and backwards. On Venus the Sun rises in the west. One spin of Venus takes 243 of our days. Our Moon turns once each time it goes round the Earth. That is why we always see the same face of the Moon. On the Moon, from one sunrise to the next takes about 29 and a half of our days.
- e.g. On Jupiter you could see two sunrises in one of our days, if you could see through its thick clouds.
- e.g. Scientists working with the rovers on Mars once lived on Mars time, so their clock slid later by about 40 minutes every day.
### Midnight Sun and long nights near the poles · *defined*
**Definition:** Near the North and South Poles, there are weeks when the Sun never sets, and weeks when it never rises.

The Earth's axis is tilted, like a top leaning a little to one side. For part of the year, the North Pole leans towards the Sun. Then places near it stay in sunlight all the way round the spin, for weeks on end. The Sun just circles around the sky, low down, all day and all night. People call this the midnight Sun. Half a year later, the North Pole leans away, and the Sun does not rise for weeks. India has a research station called Himadri, in Svalbard, far up in the Arctic. Indian scientists there work under the midnight Sun in summer. Why the tilt also gives us seasons is the subject of another unit.
- e.g. In Svalbard, the Sun does not set from about the middle of April to late August.
- e.g. At India's Antarctic stations, Maitri and Bharati, scientists have long dark weeks in the southern winter.
### Twilight, and the Sun you see after it has set · *defined*
**Definition:** After sunset, the air above us still catches sunlight, so the sky stays bright for a while. This is twilight. The air also bends sunlight a little. So we still see the Sun for about 2 minutes after it has really gone below the horizon.

Air scatters sunlight, sending it off in all directions, and this lights up the whole sky. That is why the daytime sky is bright, and not black like space. After sunset, high air is still in sunlight, so it glows for a while. Air also bends light a little, like water bends a straw in a glass. Near the horizon, this bending lifts the picture of the Sun. So at sunset, the Sun you see touching the horizon is already just below it. Look east just after sunset and you may see a dark blue band low in the sky, with a pink band above it. The dark band is the shadow of the Earth itself, falling on the air.
- e.g. On the Moon there is no air, so the sky is black even in the day, and there is no twilight.
- e.g. Astronauts on the International Space Station go round the Earth so fast that they see about 16 sunrises a day.
### Why the night sky is dark · *defined*
**Definition:** The night sky is dark because the universe as we know it began about 13.8 billion years ago. Light from the most distant stars has not had time to reach us. And light from very far away is stretched so much that our eyes cannot see it.

This sounds like a silly question, but it puzzled great scientists. There are so many stars that, wherever you look, your eye should end up on a star. So why isn't the whole night sky as bright as the Sun? Johannes Kepler wondered about this in 1610. Heinrich Olbers, a German doctor and astronomer, wrote about it in 1823. Then in 1848, Edgar Allan Poe, a writer of stories and poems, made a clever guess. Maybe light from the most distant stars has not reached us yet. Today scientists agree he was largely right. The universe began about 13.8 billion years ago, so light has only had that long to travel. Also, space is stretching, which stretches far-away light until it is too dim and too red to see.
- e.g. Stand in a big forest. Wherever you look, you see a tree trunk. A night sky full of stars should be like that, but it is not.
### Spinning compared with what? · *debated*
A Foucault pendulum keeps its swing still compared with the far-away stars and galaxies, while the Earth turns underneath. Water in a spinning bucket climbs up the sides. In both cases, the spin seems to be measured against the whole distant universe. But why should the far stars decide what counts as spinning? Physicists have argued about this for more than 300 years.
- e.g. Stir water fast in a steel bowl, then stop stirring. While the water spins, it climbs up the sides and dips in the middle.
- e.g. In a spinning room with no windows, a pendulum would still show the spin.
- **Isaac Newton, an English scientist, in 1687:** Space itself is a fixed stage. Spinning means turning compared with that stage, even if nothing else existed.
- **Ernst Mach, an Austrian scientist, in 1883:** Spinning only means something compared with all the matter in the universe. If the whole universe were empty, spinning might mean nothing. His idea inspired Albert Einstein.
- **Physicists today:** Einstein's theory of gravity says the spinning Earth very slightly drags space around with it. A satellite called Gravity Probe B measured this tiny effect in 2011. But whether Mach was right is still argued.
### Living things keep time with the spin · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants, animals and people have body clocks inside them. Body clocks follow a rhythm of about 24 hours, matching the Earth's spin.

In 1729, a French astronomer, Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan, put a touch-me-not plant in a dark cupboard. Their leaves still opened in the day and folded at night, without any sunlight. So the plant had their own clock. In 1962, a young French cave explorer, Michel Siffre, lived alone in a cave for about two months, with no clock and no daylight. He kept a rhythm of waking and sleeping. But it drifted, and when he came out he thought it was about 25 days earlier than it really was. In 2017, three American scientists won the Nobel Prize for finding the genes that work the clock, in fruit flies. Almost every cell in our body has a clock like that. Morning light reaching our eyes resets the main clock, in the brain, every day.
- e.g. Owls, bats and many moths are active at night. Scientists call them nocturnal.
- e.g. Long flights across the world give people jet lag. Their body clock is still on home time.
### Where daylight comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** Daylight is light from the Sun. The Sun makes it deep inside, where hydrogen is squeezed into helium and gives out huge amounts of energy.

In 1925, a young astronomer, Cecilia Payne, showed that the Sun and the stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium. Many senior astronomers did not believe her at first. Later, they agreed she was right. Deep in the Sun's centre, hydrogen is squeezed so hard that it joins together into helium. This gives out the energy that makes sunlight. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to cross space and reach your face. But scientists estimate that its energy spent many thousands of years working its way out from the Sun's centre first. So the energy in the morning light on your face began its journey out of the Sun's centre long before the first towns were built.
- e.g. When you see the Sun, you see it as it was about 8 minutes ago.
- e.g. Plants catch this sunlight and make food from it, and so do we, when we eat the plants.

## Mostly agreed
- Day and night happen because the Earth spins. At any moment, half of the Earth faces the Sun and has day. *How we know:* Photos from satellites show the Earth half lit and half dark, with the day-night line moving across it. A ball and a torch show the same thing at home.
- The Earth spins once in about 24 hours, from west to east. *How we know:* Every place on Earth gets one sunrise and one sunset in about 24 hours, except near the poles. The Sun, Moon and stars all seem to move from east to west.
- The Sun's movement across our sky is only apparent. The Sun is not going round the Earth each day. *How we know:* Foucault's pendulum, first shown in Paris in 1851, turns its swing as the floor turns. Stars trace circles around the Pole Star. Spacecraft have filmed the Earth turning.
- Places further east see the Sun rise earlier. *How we know:* Sunrise times printed in newspapers show it. In June, the Sun rises in Dong, in Arunachal Pradesh, about two hours before it rises in Bhuj, in Kutch.
- The Earth's spin is very slowly slowing down, so days are getting longer. *How we know:* Records of ancient eclipses, daily growth lines in fossil corals and shells, and laser measurements of the Moon all agree. A day gets about 2 thousandths of a second longer every 100 years.

## How we found out
**Aryabhata (India, 499 CE), Nicolaus Copernicus (Poland, 1543) and Léon Foucault (France, 1851), 499 CE to 1851.** For thousands of years, most people believed that the Earth stood still and the whole sky turned round it once a day. It looked that way, and it felt that way. In 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata disagreed. In his book, the Aryabhatiya, he wrote that the Earth turns. He compared us to a man in a moving boat, who sees the trees on the bank slide backwards. Later Indian astronomers, including Brahmagupta, argued against him. In 1543, Copernicus in Poland also wrote that the Earth spins. But for centuries nobody could show the spin directly. In 1851, the French scientist Léon Foucault hung a heavy ball on a 67-metre wire in the Panthéon, a great hall in Paris, and set it swinging. Over the hours, the line of the swing slowly turned round the floor. Nothing was pushing it sideways. The floor, and the whole Earth with it, was turning underneath. Crowds came to watch. Now anyone could see the Earth's spin in a single room.  
*What people believed before:* The Earth stands still, and the Sun and the stars travel round it every day. Foucault's pendulum showed that it is the floor, and the Earth, that turns.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Earth stands still at the centre, and the Sun goes round it every day.** (Most people in most places for thousands of years, and Ptolemy, a Greek astronomer in Egypt, about 1,900 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: It is exactly what we see. The ground feels still. The Sun really does seem to travel across the sky.  
  Against: Seeming to move is not the same as moving. A Foucault pendulum's swing turns as the Earth turns under it. Stars wheel around the Pole Star. Photos from space show the Earth spinning. The Sun stays put, and we turn.
- **If the Earth were spinning, birds and clouds would be left behind, and we would be thrown off.** (Ptolemy, in his book the Almagest, and many people since; *rejected*)  
  For: The ground would be moving very fast. Fast spinning things, like a merry-go-round, can throw you off.  
  Against: The air, the birds and the clouds all move along with the Earth, just as the air inside a train moves with the train. The Earth spins once a day, which is very gentle compared with a merry-go-round. Gravity holds us on far more strongly than the spin could throw us off.
- **Copernicus, in Poland, was the first to say that the Earth spins.** (Many science books; *rejected*)  
  For: Copernicus wrote it in his famous book of 1543, and it changed science in Europe.  
  Against: Aryabhata wrote it in India in 499 CE, more than a thousand years earlier, with his example of a man in a moving boat. Heraclides of Pontus said it in Greece even earlier. Their ideas were argued against and often forgotten.
- **At night, the Sun goes behind the mountains, or sinks into the sea, or goes to sleep.** (Many old stories around the world, and many young children; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun does seem to sink behind the hills or into the sea at sunset.  
  Against: When the Sun sets for us, it is rising for someone else. People phoning or video-calling across the world see this every day. The Sun never goes anywhere. Our side of the Earth turns away from it.
- **At noon, the Sun is straight overhead everywhere, so there is no shadow.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: At noon the Sun is at its highest, and shadows are shortest.  
  Against: In most of the world, the Sun is never exactly overhead, so there is always some shadow at noon. In places between the equator and the Tropic of Cancer, like Bengaluru and Chennai, there are just two days each year with no noon shadow. People in India call them Zero Shadow Days.
- **Water going down a sink swirls one way in India and the other way in Australia, because the Earth spins.** (A popular claim, often shown to tourists near the equator; *rejected*)  
  For: The Earth's spin really does make huge storms, like cyclones, turn one way north of the equator and the other way south of it.  
  Against: A sink is far too small for the spin to matter. The shape of the sink and the way the water was poured decide how it swirls. In careful tests with very still water in a big round tank, scientists have seen the spin's effect, but never in an ordinary sink.

## Roots
We have been learning that day and night come from the spinning Earth. Now we ask what that spin tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the torch-and-ball test, the far side of the ball was dark because the ball itself blocked the light. The Earth does the same. The Sun never stops shining, and night is the half of the Earth turned away from it. So at night, the whole Earth is between you and the Sun.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'night falls' and 'the Sun goes down', as if darkness came from somewhere or the Sun went away. The Sun never stops shining. At night, our own planet is in the way.  
*Evidence:* In the torch and ball experiment, the back of the ball was dark because the ball itself blocked the torch. Just after sunset, the Earth's shadow shows as a dark blue band low in the eastern sky. And when Bhuj has sunset, Dong already has night, under the same Sun.

- *who am i:* You are not standing still while the sky moves. You ride on a turning Earth, at about 1,500 kilometres an hour, together with everything around you.
- *what is life:* Every living thing, from a touch-me-not plant to you, has a body clock that keeps time with the Earth's spin.
- *what is this world:* There is one Sun and one turning Earth. At every moment it is morning somewhere and night somewhere else.

Day and night look like two opposite things. But they are made by one Sun shining on one ball. Morning in Dong and evening in Bhuj happen at the same moment, on the same Earth, in the same light. The turning only changes which places are in the light. The whole world is like this: one whole, which looks like separate pieces depending on where you stand in it.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 3.11.1 to 3.11.3:** The Upanishad speaks of a Sun that no longer rises or sets, but stands alone in the centre. For one who understands this, it says, the Sun does not rise and does not set. For them it is always day. (The Upanishad is speaking about inner understanding, not about astronomy. But it fits what science found in one way. The Sun itself never rises or sets. Rising and setting belong to our place on the turning Earth.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 2:** Long and short shape each other. High and low lean on each other. Front and back follow one another. Each needs the other to exist. (Day and night are like front and back. A ball lit from one side always has a lit half and a dark half. You cannot have one without the other.)
- **Aryabhatiya, Golapada verse 9 (Aryabhata, 499 CE):** A man in a moving boat sees the still trees on the bank moving backwards. In the same way, the still stars seem to move west. (Aryabhata's boat is about science, and also about seeing. What seems to move may be ourselves. Before deciding what is moving, it helps to ask where we are standing.)
- Science (settled): The Earth spins once in about 24 hours, and the Sun's daily movement across the sky is only apparent. This is shown by Foucault pendulums, star trails and photos from space.
- Science (idea): The Sun and the planets formed from one slowly swirling cloud of gas and dust, about 4.6 billion years ago. Most of the planets' spin comes from that cloud's turning, though giant crashes changed some of it.
- Other view, Most physicists: Day and night are fully explained by physics: leftover spin from the cloud the Earth formed from, light travelling in straight lines, and a ball blocking it. They see no need for any deeper oneness behind it. That is an honest view, and it is how science works.
- Practice, Feel the ground turn (3 min): Let's go to a window or terrace at sunset, or stand facing a lamp. Stand still, facing the light, but don't look straight at the Sun. Look at the glow on the buildings or the sky beside it. Don't think of the Sun going down. Imagine the ground under your feet slowly tipping you backwards, away from it. Breathe in slowly. Breathe out slowly. The whole Earth is turning with you. Stay quiet for a moment. Who else is turning with us right now?
- Ask: At night, the ground under you is between you and the Sun. Is night something that comes to us, or somewhere we go?
- Ask: When it is night for you, is it night for the Earth?
- Ask: The Sun moves too, round the middle of our galaxy. Is anything in the universe really still?

## Story: The Rooster Who Raised the Sun
*Dong is a real village in Arunachal Pradesh, one of the first places in India to see the sunrise. In June the Sun rises there about two hours before it rises in Bhuj, in Kutch. Raja, Mili, her family and their journey are made up. The idea of a rooster who thinks his crowing raises the Sun is old, for example in Edmond Rostand's French play Chantecler (1910). This story is new.*

I am Raja, and I am the rooster of Dong. Dong is a small village high in the hills of Arunachal Pradesh, where two rivers meet. Every child in India should know about Dong, because Dong is where the Sun comes up. I know this for certain. I am the one who brings it up.

Every morning, while the whole of India is still asleep, I wake in the dark. I climb onto the woodpile, fill my chest with cold mountain air, and crow. Ko-ko-ro-koooo! The darkness grows thin and grey. I crow again. A line of gold appears behind the eastern hills. One more crow, my loudest, and up comes the Sun. Every single morning. I have never once failed.

My girl is called Mili. She is nine, and she feeds me rice from her own bowl. So when Mili's mother got a teaching job far away in Bhuj, in Kutch, at the other end of India, Mili refused to go without me. 'Raja comes too,' she said, 'or I stay with Grandmother.' So I came, in a bamboo basket with a cloth over the top.

It was a long journey. A jeep took us down the hills. Then we took trains for four days and four nights. On the first morning, on the train, I woke at my usual time. I crowed. People in the compartment groaned and pulled their sheets over their heads. And the Sun did not come up.

I crowed again. Nothing. The sky stayed black. I crowed until a man in the top berth threw a pillow at my basket. It was a long, long time before the window turned grey, and then gold. Mili looked at her mother's watch and frowned.

Every morning on that train, the dark after my crow lasted a little longer. It was as if the Sun were growing deaf. Or, I thought, with a cold feeling in my feathers, as if my crow were growing weak. Perhaps my power belonged to the hills of Dong. Perhaps, far from home, I was just an ordinary bird.

On our first morning in Bhuj, I woke at my usual time and climbed onto the wall of our new courtyard. The air was warm and smelled of dust and salt. I crowed my best crow. The sky stayed as black as a cooking pot.

I crowed for half an hour. A dog barked at me. A neighbour shouted something in Kutchi that I did not understand, and a chappal flew over the wall. I crowed for an hour. My voice cracked. Still no Sun. At last, I gave up. I sat on the wall with my head down. My power was gone. India would have no more mornings, and it was my fault.

And then, while I sat there silent, the sky turned grey. Then pink. Then gold. And the Sun came up over the flat land of Kutch, big and round, as if nothing were wrong at all. I had not made a single sound.

I did not eat my rice that day. Mili noticed. In the evening, she carried me up onto the flat roof to watch the sunset. The Sun hung low and orange over the salty plain. Then Mili's phone buzzed. It was Grandmother, calling from Dong.

'Is it dark there yet?' Mili asked.

'Dark?' said Grandmother. 'Child, the Sun set here an hour and a half ago. The stars are out. I am about to go to bed.' She turned her phone to show us. Her screen was black, with a few small stars. Mili looked at Grandmother's dark screen. Then she looked at our Sun, still sitting there, orange, over Kutch.

Mili ran downstairs. She came back with a mausambi from the kitchen, a pen and her father's torch. On one side of the mausambi she drew a dot and wrote 'Dong'. A little way round, she drew another dot and wrote 'Bhuj'. She pushed a pencil through the fruit, from top to bottom, and shone the torch at it. 'Look, Raja,' she said. 'This is the Earth. The torch is the Sun.'

She turned the mausambi slowly on its pencil. The dot that said Dong came round into the torchlight first. Bhuj was still in the dark. She kept turning. Only later did Bhuj come round into the light too. 'The Sun doesn't come up,' Mili whispered. 'We go round to meet it. Dong turns into the light first. Bhuj turns into it two hours later. Your crow was right on time, Raja. You were just still on Dong time.'

I looked at the mausambi turning in the torchlight. Then I looked at the real Sun. It was sitting quite still. It had always been sitting still. It was the roof under my feet that was moving. The whole of Kutch, with its salt and dust and dogs, was tipping slowly backwards, away from the light. I could almost feel it, a slow, enormous lean, like a boat on a calm river.

The Sun had never been listening to me. All those years in Dong, my hill had simply been the first to turn into the light. I was not the one who raised the Sun. I was the first one who saw it.

That night, I thought about the line of light. It swept across India every morning, like a lamp carried slowly through a house. First it touched Dong, where Grandmother's rooster would be crowing. Then the tea gardens of Assam. Then Kolkata, then Delhi, then Mumbai, and at last, Bhuj. Then it would go on, across the sea, to Arabia and Africa, and on and on around the whole turning world. Somewhere along that line, a rooster was always crowing. Somewhere, it was always morning.

The next day, I woke at my Dong time. I waited in the dark, quietly, for about two hours. When the first grey came, I climbed onto the wall and crowed, softly at first, then loud. Not to call the Sun. Just to tell Bhuj that it had turned round into the morning once again.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the rooster thinks the Sun comes to him. The turn comes when the Sun rises without his crow, and Grandmother's sky in Dong is dark while Bhuj still has sunset. The mausambi on a pencil shows why. The Earth turns from west to east, so Dong turns into the sunlight about two hours before Bhuj. Nothing rises. The ground turns. The rooster's crow was not wrong, only still on Dong time, the way our body clocks keep home time after a long journey. The story also gently asks what happens when we think we are the centre that everything moves around.

- Ask: Why did Raja think he made the Sun rise?
- Ask: On the train, why did the dark after Raja's crow last a little longer every morning?
- Ask: When it was sunset in Bhuj, why was it already night for Grandmother in Dong?
- Ask: What did Mili's mausambi, pencil and torch show? Can you do it too?
- Ask: Raja learned he was 'the first one who saw it', not 'the one who raised it'. Have you ever thought something happened because of you, and then found out it did not?

### e3 · Foucault's Pendulum on a Stool (20 min)

### Day and Night Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) For thousands of years, most people believed the Sun travels round the Earth each day. What really makes night come?  
   Options: The Sun goes behind the hills / Our side of the Earth spins round, away from the Sun / The Sun switches off / Clouds cover the Sun  
   **Answer:** Our side of the Earth spins round, away from the Sun. *Why:* The Earth spins once in about 24 hours. Each place turns into the sunlight, which is day, then away from it, which is night. The Sun shines all the time. The old idea matched what people saw, which is why it lasted so long.
2. (fill) The imaginary line that the Earth spins around is called its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['axis']. *Why:* The axis goes through the North Pole and the South Pole. A pencil through an orange is a good model.
3. (write) Arun, who is 7, says, 'The Earth takes a whole year to spin round once.' His big sister Meenakshi says, 'No, one spin takes a day.' Who is right? What does take a year?  
   **Answer:** Meenakshi is right. One spin takes about 24 hours, one day and one night. A year is how long the Earth takes to travel all the way round the Sun. Arun has mixed up the spin with the journey round the Sun.. *Why:* A strong answer names both movements: the spin makes a day, and the trip round the Sun makes a year.
4. (truefalse) At any moment, about half of the Earth has day and half has night.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Sun can only light the half of the Earth that faces it.
5. (mcq) In which direction does the Sun seem to rise?  
   Options: North / South / East / West  
   **Answer:** East. *Why:* The Earth turns from west to east, so the Sun seems to come up in the east. Picking 'west' swaps sunrise and sunset.
6. (mcq) Today the Earth spins once in about 24 hours, so Chennai gets about 12 hours of night. If the Earth spun twice as fast, about how long would the night be?  
   Options: About 6 hours / About 12 hours / About 24 hours / There would be no night  
   **Answer:** About 6 hours. *Why:* Twice as fast means one spin takes 12 hours instead of 24. Chennai would spend about half of that turned away from the Sun, so about 6 hours of night. A strong answer links the length of a night to how fast the Earth spins.
7. (truefalse) The Sun really moves across the sky every day.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Sun only seems to move. Our Earth turns, and carries us past the Sun, the way trees seem to rush past a moving train.
8. (mcq) You shine a torch at a ball in a dark room. Which part of the ball is like night?  
   Options: The side facing the torch / The side facing away from the torch / The whole ball / No part  
   **Answer:** The side facing away from the torch. *Why:* The side facing away from the torch gets no light, like the night side of the Earth. Picking 'the whole ball' forgets that the torch always lights one half.
9. (sort) Sort these. Does each one mostly happen in the day or at night?  
   **Answer:** an owl hunting mice → Night; a sunbird sipping nectar → Day; bats flying round a street lamp → Night; clothes drying in sunshine → Day; fireflies flashing → Night; your long shadow in the evening sunlight → Day. *Why:* Owls, bats and fireflies are nocturnal, which means active at night. Sunbirds feed in the day. Clothes dry in sunshine, and shadows from the Sun only happen in the day.
10. (write) In a smooth train with your eyes shut, you can hardly tell you are moving. Use this to explain why we cannot feel the Earth spin.  
   **Answer:** We only feel movement when it changes, like a bump, a speed-up or a brake. A smooth train hardly changes, so it is hard to feel. The Earth's spin is smoother than any train. It never bumps or brakes, and the air and everything around us move with us. So we feel nothing.. *Why:* A strong answer says what we really feel: changes in movement, not movement itself. Feeling nothing is not evidence that nothing moves.
11. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Sunrise → our place turns into the sunlight; Sunset → our place turns away from the sunlight; Noon → the Sun is at its highest; Axis → the line the Earth spins around. *Why:* These are the key words of the unit. Sunrise and sunset are really about our place on the turning Earth.
12. (order) Put these in order, starting from early morning: noon, sunrise, night, sunset.  
   **Answer:** ['sunrise', 'noon', 'sunset', 'night']. *Why:* First our place turns into the light at sunrise. It faces the Sun most at noon. It turns away at sunset. Then it is night until the next sunrise.
13. (mcq) In the morning, the Sun is in the east. Which way does your shadow point?  
   Options: East, towards the Sun / West, away from the Sun / Straight down / There is no shadow in the morning  
   **Answer:** West, away from the Sun. *Why:* A shadow always falls on the side away from the light. Picking 'towards the Sun' is a common mix-up. Try it outside tomorrow morning and see.
14. (write) Your little cousin says, 'The Sun goes to sleep at night.' What would you tell them? Use the word spin.  
   **Answer:** The Sun does not sleep. It shines all the time. Our Earth spins, so at night our side has turned away from the Sun. Children on the other side of the Earth have day then.. *Why:* A good answer says the Sun keeps shining, and that the Earth's spin turns us away from it.
15. (draw) Draw the Sun on one side and the Earth on the other. Shade the night half of the Earth. Draw a small person on the day half.  
   **Answer:** The half of the Earth facing the Sun is left bright, with the person on it. The far half is shaded dark.. *Why:* The Sun lights the half of the Earth that faces it. The other half is night.
16. (label) Label the globe: North Pole, South Pole, axis, day side, night side.  
   **Answer:** North Pole at the top end of the axis, South Pole at the bottom end. The axis is the line through both. The side facing the Sun is the day side. The side away is the night side.. *Why:* These five labels show the whole idea of the unit in one picture.

## Applications
- Clocks and calendars are built on the Earth's spin. One spin is one day.
- Sundials, like the giant Samrat Yantra at Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, tell the time with the Sun's shadow.
- India's clock time, Indian Standard Time, is set by the Sun over a line near Mirzapur.
- Countries set their own time zones so that noon comes roughly in the middle of their day.
- Pilots, ships' navigators and travellers once used the Sun and the stars to find their way.
- Solar panels make most power around noon, and none at night. Power companies plan for this every day.
- Weather forecasters use the Earth's spin to predict which way cyclones will turn.
- Rockets are often launched towards the east, to get a free push from the Earth's spin.
- Doctors and scientists study body clocks to help night-shift workers, travellers with jet lag and people who cannot sleep.
- Astronomers plan when to observe a star by working out when the Earth will turn it into view.
- Satellites that photograph the Earth are timed to pass over places at the same sunlit hour each day.
- Farmers, fishers and festival calendars across India follow sunrise, sunset and the Moon.

## Open questions
- **How fast was the Earth spinning when it was very young?** Scientists agree days were much shorter. But rocks that old are rare, and different studies give different answers.
- **Why does Venus spin backwards, so slowly that its day is longer than its year?** Ideas include a giant crash long ago, or the Sun's pull on its thick air. Nobody knows for sure.
- **Spinning compared with what? Why does a spinning thing behave as if it is measured against the far-away stars?** Newton, Mach and Einstein all thought about this. Physicists still argue.
- **Exactly how do body clocks in different parts of the body stay in step with each other?** Scientists know the main genes. How all the clocks in the body talk to each other is still being studied.
- **Why do we need to sleep at night at all?** Sleep helps memory, growth and repair. But exactly why almost every animal sleeps is still an open question.
- **Why do some days come out a tiny bit shorter, even though the spin is slowing overall?** In recent years, some days were shorter than 24 hours by about a thousandth of a second. Scientists think the Earth's liquid core, the oceans and the air shift the spin a little. The details are still being worked out.
- **Why are moths and other insects drawn round street lamps at night?** One idea from 2024 is that insects try to keep their backs towards the brightest light, which used to be the sky. Scientists are still testing it.

The shadow was 40 cm at 9. It was 8 cm at 1. 40 take away 8 is 32. So the shadow was 32 cm shorter at 1 o'clock. You can check: 8 + 32 = 40. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In Pune, the Sun rose at 6 o'clock in the morning and set at 7 o'clock in the evening. How many hours of daylight were there? **13 hours.**. From 6 in the morning to 12 noon is 6 hours. From 12 noon to 7 in the evening is 7 hours. 6 + 7 = 13. So there were 13 hours of daylight. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- The Earth turns all the way round, 360 degrees, in 24 hours. How many degrees does it turn in 1 hour? **15 degrees.**. We share 360 degrees equally among 24 hours. 24 × 15 = 360. So 360 divided by 24 is 15. The Earth turns 15 degrees every hour. That is why the Sun seems to move about one and a half fist-widths across the sky every hour, with your arm held out straight. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Read the clock. It shows the time the Sun rose in Dong, Arunachal Pradesh, on a June morning. The Sun rose in Bhuj, Kutch, 2 hours later. What time did it rise in Bhuj? **6 o'clock, or 6:05 to be exact.**. The clock shows 4:05. The short hand is just past 4, and the long hand is on 1, which means 5 minutes. Add 2 hours: 4:05 becomes 6:05. So the Sun rose in Bhuj at about 6:05. (queues `math.time.telling-time.L3`)
- Fossil corals show that 400 million years ago a year had about 400 days. Today a year has about 365 days. How many more days did a year have then? **About 35 more days.**. 400 take away 365 is 35. So a year had about 35 more days. The year was about as long as now, but each day was shorter, because the Earth spun faster. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)

## Warm-up

- The Sun seems to rise in the ____. → east
- The Sun seems to set in the ____. → west
- Does the Sun really move across our sky each day? → No. The Earth turns, so it only seems to move.
- How much of the Earth has day at any moment? → About half.
- Which sees sunrise first, Arunachal Pradesh or Gujarat? → Arunachal Pradesh, because it is further east.
- When is your shadow shortest? → Around noon, when the Sun is highest.
- Which Indian astronomer said the Earth turns, in 499 CE? → Aryabhata.
- What did Foucault's pendulum show, in Paris in 1851? → That the Earth spins underneath it.
- Are Earth's days getting slowly longer or shorter? → Longer, very slowly, because of the tides.
