# What are things made of?
`sci.materials.what-is-it-made-of.L1`

**Summary.** A paper cup holds hot tea, yet paper goes soft in water. Everything around us is made of a material: wood, metal, plastic, glass, fabric or paper, and a few more. Your child hunts for each material at home, and learns to tell them apart by looking, touching, tapping and lifting. Then they test a paper cup against a newspaper cone, and cut the cup open. They find a thin plastic skin inside. Many things are made of more than one material.

**Intro.** *Tea from a paper cup does not leak. But paper goes soft and soggy in water. So how can a paper cup hold tea?* Look around the room. The door, the fan, the bucket, the window, your shirt and your book are all made of something. What a thing is made of is called its material. In this unit you learn six materials: wood, metal, plastic, glass, fabric and paper. You find them all over your home. Then you solve the puzzle of the paper cup.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name six materials, wood, metal, plastic, glass, fabric and paper, and find things made of each one at home.
1. By the end I can tell what a thing is made of by looking at it, touching it, tapping it and lifting it.
2. By the end I can say that one material can make many things, and one thing can be made of more than one material.
3. By the end I can say where wood, paper, cotton cloth, metal, glass and plastic come from.
4. By the end I can make a guess, test it, and say what I saw in the Paper Cup Puzzle.

## Terms
- **Material**: A material is what a thing is made of. *e.g.* A spoon can be made of the material steel, or wood, or plastic.
- **Wood**: Wood comes from trees. It is hard, and it has thin lines in it called the grain. *e.g.* A wooden chopping board, a door and a pencil are made of wood.
- **Metal**: Metal is hard and often shiny. Steel, iron, aluminium, copper and gold are metals. A metal thing rings when you tap it. *e.g.* A steel tumbler, a key and a coin are made of metal.
- **Plastic**: Plastic is a material made in factories, mostly from oil. It is light, and it can be made in any colour and shape. *e.g.* A bucket, a mug and a lunch box are often made of plastic.
- **Glass**: Glass is hard and smooth, and you can usually see through it. It is made by melting sand in a very hot fire. Glass can break into sharp pieces. *e.g.* A window, a jar and a mirror are made of glass.
- **Fabric**: Fabric is another word for cloth. It is soft and bends easily. It is made of many thin threads woven together. *e.g.* A saree, a shirt and a towel are made of fabric.
- **Paper**: Paper is a thin sheet made of tiny plant threads pressed together. It tears easily and goes soft when it gets wet. *e.g.* A book, a newspaper and a paper bag are made of paper.

## Explanations
### Everything is made of a material · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is what a thing is made of. Wood, metal, plastic, glass, fabric and paper are six materials.

Every thing you can touch is made of a material. A chair might be made of wood. A tumbler might be made of steel, which is a metal. There are more materials than these six. Clay, stone, rubber and leather are materials too. Even you are made of materials, like bone, skin and hair.
- e.g. In the kitchen: a steel kadai, a glass jar, a plastic dabba, a wooden ladle, a cotton towel and a paper bag.
- e.g. A clay diya for Karthigai Deepam is made of clay, another material.
- e.g. Your rubber chappal is made of rubber.
### How to tell what a thing is made of · *defined*
**Definition:** You can often tell a thing's material by looking at it, touching it, tapping it and lifting it.

Wood has thin lines called grain, and it sounds dull when you knock on it. Metal is hard and often shiny. It feels cold to touch, and it rings when you tap it. Plastic is light and smooth, and it can be any colour. Glass is hard and smooth, and you can usually see through it. Fabric is soft and bends, and you can see its threads up close. Paper is thin, tears easily and goes soggy in water.
- e.g. Tap a steel plate with a spoon: ting! Tap a wooden board: tok.
- e.g. Lift a steel tiffin and a plastic tiffin of the same size. The steel one is heavier.
- e.g. Hold a cotton towel up to the light. You can see tiny holes between the threads.
### One material makes many things, and one thing can have many materials · *defined*
**Definition:** One material, like steel, can be made into many different things. And one thing, like a pencil, can be made of more than one material.

Steel makes tumblers, spoons, plates, keys and train carriages. Paper makes books, tickets and bags. A thing gets its name from its shape and its job, not from its material. Many things are made of two or more materials. Each material does one part of the job. A paper cup is paper outside, so it stays stiff. Inside it has a thin skin of plastic, so the water stays in.
- e.g. A pencil is wood outside, with a grey stick inside called graphite. Many pencils also have paint and a rubber on the end.
- e.g. An umbrella has a fabric top, metal spokes and a plastic or wooden handle.
- e.g. A spoon can be steel, wood, plastic or even silver, and it is still a spoon.
### Where materials come from · *defined*
**Definition:** Every material comes from something in nature: a plant, an animal, rock, sand or oil from under the ground.

Wood comes from trees. Paper is made from wood, or from other plant threads like old cotton rags, bamboo or sugarcane waste. Cotton cloth comes from the soft fluff that grows around the seeds of the cotton plant. Silk comes from the cocoons of silkworms, and wool from the coat of a sheep. Metal comes from rocks dug out of the ground. Glass is made from sand, melted in a very hot fire. Most plastic is made from oil or gas found deep under the ground.
- e.g. Karnataka makes more silk than any other state in India.
- e.g. A paper mill in Tamil Nadu makes paper from bagasse, the dry stalks left after sugarcane juice is squeezed out.
- e.g. Firozabad, a city in Uttar Pradesh, makes glass bangles from melted sand and other materials.

## Mostly agreed
- Everything we can touch is made of one or more materials. *How we know:* Every thing people have ever tested, from a spoon to a mountain, is made of materials that can be named and studied.
- Many things are made of more than one material. *How we know:* Cut open a paper cup and you find paper and plastic. Look at a pencil and you find wood, graphite and paint.
- All materials come from nature in the end: from plants, animals, rocks, sand, or oil under the ground. *How we know:* People can follow every material back to where it began. Wood comes from trees, steel from iron in rocks, and plastic mostly from oil.

## How we found out
**Cai Lun, an official at the Chinese emperor's court, and archaeologists in China in the 1900s, The year 105, and the 1900s.** For a long time, people said that paper was invented in China in the year 105, by a man named Cai Lun. He worked at the emperor's court. Old books say he made paper from tree bark, old cloth rags and worn-out fishing nets. He soaked and beat them into a mush, spread it thin on a screen, and let it dry. In the 1900s, archaeologists in China dug up pieces of paper that were older than Cai Lun. One piece, found at a place called Fangmatan in 1986, has a map drawn on it. Scientists still argue about its exact age, but most think it is more than 200 years older than Cai Lun. So Cai Lun did not invent paper. He made it better, and told the emperor about it. Paper reached India much later. Before that, people in South India wrote on dried palm leaves, and people in Kashmir wrote on birch bark. All paper is made in the same way: from thin plant threads pressed together into a sheet. Tear a piece of paper slowly, and you can see those threads at the edge.  
*What people believed before:* Paper was invented by Cai Lun in China in the year 105.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A thing is made of just one material, the one in its name.** (Many of us, when we say 'a paper cup' or 'a steel almirah'; *rejected*)  
  For: Names like 'paper cup' tell us the main material, and they are useful.  
  Against: A paper cup has a thin plastic skin inside. A steel almirah has a glass mirror, a plastic handle and a metal lock. Most things are made of several materials.
- **Paper can only be made from trees.** (Many people today, because most paper is made from wood; *rejected*)  
  For: Most paper in shops is made from wood from trees.  
  Against: Paper can be made from any plant threads. Old paper was made from rags, bark and old fishing nets. Paper in India is also made from bamboo and from sugarcane waste.
- **Plastic lasts forever.** (Many people, because plastic does not rot like a banana peel; *debated*)  
  For: A plastic bag can stay in the ground or the sea for a very long time, maybe hundreds of years.  
  Against: Sunlight slowly breaks plastic into tiny pieces. In 2016, scientists in Japan found bacteria that can slowly eat one kind of plastic, the kind used for water bottles.

## Roots
We have named the materials around us, and found that a paper cup is paper and plastic together. Now we ask what that tells us about things, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** The newspaper cone leaked, but the paper cup held its water. Inside the cup, you found a thin skin of plastic. The paper keeps the cup stiff, and the plastic keeps the water in. Many things are made of two or more materials, each doing its own part of the job.  
*Habit it breaks:* We name a thing after one material: a paper cup, a steel almirah, a wooden door. The name tells us only the main part. To know what a thing is made of, we have to look at every part.  
*Evidence:* In the Paper Cup Puzzle, the newspaper cone dripped within about a minute, while the paper cup stayed dry outside. When the cup was cut open, a thin clear skin stretched at the torn edge and could be peeled from the paper.

- *who am i:* You are made of many materials too: bone, skin, hair, teeth and blood. Each one does its own job in your body.
- *what is life:* Some materials are made by living beings. Trees make wood, cotton plants make cotton, and silkworms spin silk.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of things, and every one is made of materials. A few materials, used again and again, make most of what you see.

Steel makes a tumbler, a spoon and a train. A paper cup is paper and plastic together. All these materials come from the Earth: from trees, plants, rocks, sand and oil. Everything is conscious, including the tree that gave the wood and the rock that gave the steel. Many scientists disagree, as the other view says.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** The teacher Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu: when you know one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay. A pot, a cup and a lamp are only different names and shapes. The clay is what they really are. (In the Material Hunt, the metal card held a tumbler, a spoon and a plate. Their names were different, but they were all made of one material.)
- **J.C. Bose, Response in the Living and Non-Living (1902):** The Indian scientist Jagadish Chandra Bose gave metal wires small electric shocks, again and again. He reported that their response grew weaker, then came back after a rest. He saw the same thing in plants and in muscles, and he called it fatigue, which means tiredness. (We usually sort the world into living beings and materials. Bose reported that a metal responds to what happens to it, much like a muscle does.)
- Science (settled): Every material is made of tiny particles called atoms, far too small to see. Wood, steel and plastic are made of different atoms, joined in different ways.
- Science (open): Scientists keep inventing new materials, and nobody knows how many different materials could be made.
- Other view, Many scientists: Bose's metal wires changed because of changes on the metal's surface. For these scientists, that is a chemical change, and only living beings, or only beings with brains, are aware.
- Practice, Two spoons, eyes closed (3 min): Close your eyes. I will put a spoon in each of your hands. One is steel and one is wood. Hold them still. Which one feels cooler? Now tap each one gently on the table, and listen. Which one rings? Open your eyes. Were you right? One spoon came from a rock and one from a tree.
- Ask: Which part of your body feels hard like wood, and which part feels soft like cloth?
- Ask: If a thing is made of two materials, which one is it really?
- Ask: Bose said a metal can get tired like a muscle. What test could you try on a steel spoon?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The paper cup is paper outside and plastic inside. Should we still call it a paper cup? (hint: What does the name tell you? What does it leave out?)
- *deep:* If you could cut a steel spoon into smaller and smaller pieces, would every piece still be steel? (hint: Scientists say yes, down to pieces far too small to see. The tiniest pieces are called atoms.)
- *big:* Is there anything that is not made of a material? What about light, a shadow or a song? (hint: Can you touch it? Can you put it in a box?)
- *big:* Your wooden chair was once part of a living tree. Is there still a little tree in the chair? (hint: Look at the grain. Those lines show how the tree grew, year by year.)

## Story: The Game on the Train
*Madurai is a city in Tamil Nadu. Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Kavya, Arjun and Paati are made up.*

Kavya was six. One morning she sat by a train window with her cousin Arjun and their grandmother, Paati. They were going from Chennai to Madurai, to visit their aunt.

Arjun was five. He could not sit still. After ten minutes he said, 'Paati, I'm bored. When will we get there?'

'Not till the evening,' said Paati. 'So let's play my game. It is called What Is It Made Of?'

'How do you play?' asked Kavya.

'I point at a thing,' said Paati. 'You tell me its material. A material is what a thing is made of.'

Paati tapped the window. Tak, tak.

'Glass!' said Kavya. 'I can see the fields through it.'

'Yes,' said Paati. 'Glass is made from sand, melted in a fire hotter than any kitchen stove.'

Arjun grabbed his water bottle. It was cold and shiny. He tapped it with his fingernail. Ting!

'Metal!' he shouted.

'Steel,' said Paati. 'Steel is a metal. Metal rings when you tap it.'

Kavya stroked the end of Paati's saree. It was soft, and when she looked close, she saw tiny threads going over and under each other.

'Fabric,' said Kavya. 'Cloth.'

'Cotton cloth,' said Paati. 'Cotton grows on a plant, as soft white fluff around the seeds.'

Then Kavya opened her lunch box. It was blue and very light. She tapped the lid. Tock.

'Plastic,' she said. Paati nodded.

Paati's walking stick leaned against the seat. Arjun ran his finger down it. It had thin brown lines from top to bottom.

'Wood,' said Paati. 'Those lines are called the grain. They show how the tree grew.'

'And your newspaper is paper,' said Kavya. 'That's six!'

Then Paati held up Kavya's pencil. 'And this?'

'Wood,' said Arjun at once.

Kavya took the pencil and looked at the sharp end. 'Not all wood,' she said. 'The point is grey. Wood is brown.'

'You are both right,' said Paati. 'The outside is wood. The grey stick in the middle is called graphite. It is a soft grey material that comes out of the ground.'

'So a pencil is two materials,' said Kavya.

Arjun counted on his fingers. 'Wood, the grey stick, the yellow paint, and the pink rubber at the top. Four!'

'Many things are made of more than one material,' said Paati. 'A good detective looks at every part.'

Just then a woman came down the coach with a big steel can. 'Coffee, coffee! Chai, chai!' she called.

Paati bought a coffee. It came in a small white paper cup. Steam curled up from the top.

Arjun stared at the cup. 'Paati, paper goes soggy in water. I spilled water on my drawing once, and it tore. Why is your cup not leaking?'

'That is a very good question,' said Paati. 'What do you think?'

'Magic paper,' said Arjun.

'Maybe the coffee is too quick to soak in,' said Kavya.

Paati smiled and sipped her coffee. 'Here is some homework for two detectives. When we get home, we will test a paper cup and find out.'

The train ran on past green paddy fields and tall palm trees. Kavya looked around the coach. The fan was metal. The switches were plastic. The ticket was paper. Paati's saree was cotton. Everything was made of something.

And some things, like her pencil, were made of more than one thing.

'Paati,' said Kavya, 'this whole train is part of your game.'

'The whole world is,' said Paati.

**For the parent:** Everything is made of a material, and many things are made of more than one. To name what a thing is made of, look at every part.

- Ask: What were the materials in Kavya's pencil?
- Ask: Why do you think Paati's paper cup did not leak?
- Ask: Look around you. Find one thing that is made of two materials.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Paper Cup Puzzle: is it only paper? (25 min)
- **question:** Paper goes soggy in water. So why does a paper cup hold water? Is a paper cup made only of paper?
- **predict:** Guess first. When we pour water into the newspaper cone, what will happen? What will happen in the paper cup? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both cups get the same amount of water, three spoonfuls each. Both are held over the same steel plate, and we watch both for the same time. Only the cup is different.
- **safety:** Only a grown-up uses the scissors.
- **record:** Draw the two cups side by side. Draw what the water did in each one. Then draw the inside of the cut-open paper cup, and colour the plastic skin a different colour from the paper.
- **results table:** Cup | My guess | What happened to the water | What it is made of
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the paper cup and half a sheet of newspaper on the table. 2. Let your child hold and feel both.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a paper cup and some newspaper. What is the cup made of? What is the newspaper made of?  
  *Child mode:* Feel the paper cup. Feel the newspaper. What are they made of?
  - Ask: What is the cup made of? (good: Paper.; It says paper cup, so paper.)
  - Note: Most children say paper. That is the right first guess. Don't say yet that there is anything else.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Roll the newspaper into a cone, about as big as the cup. 2. Tape or staple the side. 3. Fold the tip up so it is closed.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make a newspaper cup too. Now we have two paper cups.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the grown-up make a cup out of newspaper.
  - Note: The cone does not need to be neat. It only has to hold water for a few seconds.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what will happen in each cup. Write or draw their guesses.  
  *Parent says:* We will pour water into both. What will happen to the newspaper cup? What will happen to the paper cup? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. What will the water do in each cup? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Paper gets wet, so both will leak.; The shop cup will hold it, because people drink tea from it.; The newspaper will go soft.)
  - Note: Wait for a reason as well as a guess. Any guess is fine.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds both cups over the steel plate. 2. Spoon three spoonfuls of water into each. 3. Count slowly to 60 together. 4. Look at both cups and the plate.  
  *Parent says:* Hold them still over the plate. One spoon, two, three. Now let's count to 60 and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Hold both cups over the plate. Count to 60. Watch the water.
  - Ask: What happened? (good: The newspaper went dark and soft.; Water dripped out of the newspaper.; The paper cup is still full and dry outside.)
  - Note: The newspaper darkens almost at once, and usually drips within a minute. The paper cup stays dry outside. If the cone has not dripped yet, wait one more minute and squeeze it gently.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to explain what they saw.  
  *Parent says:* Both cups are paper. So why did only one leak? What could be different about the paper cup?  
  *Child mode:* Both cups are paper. Why did only one leak?
  - Ask: What could be different? (good: The paper cup is thicker.; It has something on it.; Maybe there is something inside.)
- **s6 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the water out of the paper cup. 2. Cut it down one side with scissors and open it flat. 3. Your child feels the inside and the outside. 4. Tear a strip slowly from the cut edge, and look at the torn edge.  
  *Parent says:* Feel the outside, then feel the inside. Are they the same? Now watch the edge as I tear it slowly.  
  *Child mode:* Feel outside. Feel inside. Watch the edge when it tears.
  - Ask: What did you find? (good: The inside is smoother and shinier.; There is a thin clear skin.; The skin stretches when the paper tears.)
  - Note: The inside of most paper cups has a very thin skin of plastic. When you tear slowly, the paper tears first, and the clear skin stretches before it breaks. If you can't see it, soak the strip in water for five minutes. Then rub the paper side with a thumbnail. The soft paper rubs off, and the clear skin is left.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say together what you found. 2. Your child draws the results. 3. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* So what is a paper cup made of? How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* What is a paper cup made of? How do you know?
  - Ask: What is a paper cup made of? (good: Paper and plastic.; Paper outside and a plastic skin inside.; The plastic keeps the water in, and the paper makes it stiff.)
  - Check: Why did the paper cup hold water, when the newspaper cone leaked? → **The paper cup has a thin plastic skin inside**

## Activities
### a1 · The Material Hunt: which material is used most in our kitchen? (15 min)
- **safety:** Glass things stay on the shelf. Only point to them, and a grown-up makes the tally mark.
- **record:** Under each material card, make one tally mark for each thing you found. Circle the material with the most marks.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the six material cards on the kitchen floor. 2. Point to each one and name it.  
  *Parent says:* These are six materials. In our kitchen, which material do you think we will find the most things made of? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the six cards. Which material is used most in our kitchen? Guess, and say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Metal, because we have lots of steel plates.; Plastic, because of all the dabbas.)
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child finds safe things and puts each one on its card. 2. For glass, they point, and you make a tally mark. 3. For big things like the fridge, they make a tally mark.  
  *Parent says:* Find a thing. What is it made of? How do you know? Put it on that card.  
  *Child mode:* Find a thing. Say what it is made of. Put it on the card.
  - Ask: How do you know this is plastic? (good: It is light.; It went tock when I tapped it.; It is smooth and light.)
  - Note: Let your child tap, lift and look. If they are not sure, ask 'Is it shiny? Does it ring? Can you see through it?' A thing with two materials, like a knife with a plastic handle, goes between two cards.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the things or marks on each card together. 2. Compare with the guess. 3. Put everything back.

  - Note: In many Indian kitchens, metal wins because of steel plates and vessels. In others, plastic wins. Either result is a real finding.
### a2 · Rubbing Rainbow: what does each material's surface show? (15 min)

- **s2 (8 min)**

### a3 · Look Closer: the threads in paper and cloth (10 min)

  *Parent says:* How are paper and cloth the same? How are they different?  
  *Child mode:* How are paper and cloth the same?
  - Ask: How are they alike? (good: Both are made of threads.; Cloth threads are woven, and paper threads are squashed together.)
### a4 · Foil or Not? The Scrunch Ball test (10 min)
- **record:** Draw the two balls after one minute. Write or draw what each one is made of.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the foil and the inside of the chips packet.  
  *Parent says:* Both of these are shiny. Kitchen foil is a metal called aluminium. Is the chips packet metal too? What do you think, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Both are shiny. Is the chips packet metal too? Why do you think so?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child scrunches each one into a tight ball. 2. Put both balls on the table. 3. Watch for one minute.  
  *Parent says:* Squeeze each one into a tight ball. Put them down. Now watch them for a minute.  
  *Child mode:* Scrunch both into balls. Put them down. Watch.
  - Ask: What happened? (good: The foil stayed a ball.; The chips packet opened up again.)
  - Note: Kitchen foil stays scrunched, like most metal sheets. Most chips packets slowly open up, because they are mostly plastic. If your packet stays scrunched, it may have a real foil layer inside. That is a good find too.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child what the chips packet is made of. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* A chips packet is mostly plastic. On the inside it has a very, very thin coat of metal, to keep air and light away from the chips.  
  *Child mode:* A chips packet is mostly plastic, with a thin metal coat inside.
  - Check: The chips packet opened up again after scrunching. What does that tell you? → **It is mostly plastic, not metal like the foil**

## Challenges
### c1 · Make a Hand Fan That Works (20 min)
- **problem:** The power has gone and the fan has stopped. You need a hand fan. Which material makes the best one: paper, cloth or plastic?
- **your turn:** Make one fan from each material, all about the same size. Wave each one ten times at the paper strip from the same distance. Which one lifts the strip best? Draw your best fan and label its materials.
- **constraints:** Use only things from home.; The fan must be light enough to wave for one minute.; It must move enough air to lift a small strip of paper from a hand-length away.
- **general:** Fold a sheet of paper back and forth like a zigzag, then pinch one end. This is a folding fan.; Tape a flat piece of cardboard to a stick, like a small bat.; In South India, many families once used fans made of dried palm leaves.
- **creative:** Stiffen the cloth by taping ice-cream sticks across it.; Fold the plastic folder into a zigzag. It springs back, so it moves air with each wave.; Make one fan from two materials: a paper fan with a stick handle.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem to your child. Show the paper, cloth and plastic.  
  *Parent says:* The power is off and it is hot. Which material would make the best fan? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* It is hot. Which material makes the best fan? Why?
  - Ask: Why? (good: Paper, because it is light and you can fold it.; Cloth is too floppy.; Plastic is strong.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Help your child make one fan from each material, all about the same size. 2. Show the zigzag fold for the paper.  
  *Parent says:* Fold the paper forward, then back, then forward. Pinch one end. That is your fan.  
  *Child mode:* Make a fan from each one. Fold the paper back and forth.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a paper strip on the table, with half of it hanging over the edge and a book on the other half. 2. Your child waves each fan ten times from one hand-length away.  
  *Parent says:* Wave each fan ten times from the same place. Which one lifts the strip best?  
  *Child mode:* Wave each fan ten times at the strip. Which works best?
  - Ask: Which worked best? (good: The paper fan.; The cloth one was too floppy.; The plastic one worked well too.)
  - Note: A stiff fan pushes the most air. Floppy cloth or a thin plastic bag bends away. Whichever fan wins is a real result, so write it down.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* If the cloth fan pushed little air, why do you think that was?  
  *Child mode:* Why did the floppy fan push so little air?
  - Check: A floppy fan, like one made of cloth, pushes very little air. Why? → **Cloth is soft and bends too easily**

## Worksheets
### What Is It Made Of? (core) (core, 11 questions)
1. (label) Here is a kitchen shelf. Say or write the material of each thing.  
   **Answer:** ['metal', 'glass', 'plastic', 'wood', 'fabric', 'paper']. *Why:* Each clue tells you the material. Shiny and grey is metal. See-through is glass. Light and bright is plastic. Brown lines are wood grain. Soft is fabric. Thin and easy to tear is paper.
2. (match) Draw a line from each thing to the material it is usually made of.  
   **Answer:** A chair → Wood; A window → Glass; A bucket → Plastic; A saree → Fabric; A book → Paper; A key → Metal. *Why:* Most chairs are wood, windows are glass, buckets are plastic, sarees are fabric, books are paper and keys are metal. Some of these can be made of other materials too.
3. (sort) Colour the metal things yellow and the wooden things brown.  
   **Answer:** A steel plate → Metal; A belan for rolling chapatis → Wood; A coin → Metal; A wooden spinning top → Wood; A safety pin → Metal; A door made of planks → Wood. *Why:* Metal things are hard and shiny, and ring when tapped. Wooden things have grain lines and sound dull when knocked.
4. (draw) Draw something in your home that is made of glass. Draw a ring around the glass part.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of a window, a mirror, a jar or a bottle, with a ring around the glass.. *Why:* A strong drawing rings only the glass part. A window has glass in a wooden or metal frame, so only the middle is glass.
5. (mcq) Meena, a girl in Class 1, taps a tumbler with a spoon. It rings: ting! It is shiny and cold. What is the tumbler made of?  
   Options: Metal / Plastic / Paper  
   **Answer:** Metal. *Why:* Metal is shiny, feels cold to touch and rings when you tap it. A plastic tumbler would go tock, and a paper one would make almost no sound.
6. (odd) Three of these are made of fabric. Which one is not?  
   Options: A towel / A shirt / A spoon / A dupatta  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A towel, a shirt and a dupatta are fabric, soft cloth made of threads. A spoon is hard. It is usually metal, wood or plastic.
7. (write) Remember the Paper Cup Puzzle, where water went into a newspaper cone and a paper cup. Why did the newspaper cone leak, but the paper cup held the water? Say it, and a grown-up writes it down.  
   **Answer:** The newspaper is only paper, and paper soaks up water. The paper cup has a thin plastic skin inside, and water cannot soak through it.. *Why:* A strong answer says what was different: the plastic skin inside the paper cup.
8. (perspective) Lakshmi and her brother Kiran are looking at a pencil.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. For Lakshmi: the grey middle is graphite, not wood, and the rubber is rubber. For Kiran: most of a pencil is wood, so he named the main material.. *Why:* Both are partly right. Most of a pencil is wood, but a pencil is made of several materials. Looking at every part tells you the whole answer.
9. (count) An umbrella has a fabric top, metal spokes and a plastic handle. How many different materials is it made of?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Fabric, metal and plastic make 3 materials. Each one does a job: the fabric keeps the rain off, the metal holds it open and the plastic handle is light to hold.
10. (draw) Draw one thing at home that is made of two materials. Colour each material in a different colour.  
   **Answer:** For example, a knife with a steel blade and a plastic handle, coloured in two colours.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows two clear parts, each made of a different material, and the child can name both.
11. (truefalse) Glass is made from sand.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Glass is made by melting sand with a few other materials in a very hot fire, then letting it cool.
### Material Detective (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Anjali, a girl in Class 1, finds a light, red, smooth box. She taps it: tock. She cannot see through it. What is it most likely made of?  
   Options: Plastic / Glass / Metal  
   **Answer:** Plastic. *Why:* Plastic is light and can be any colour, and it goes tock when tapped. Glass is usually see-through, and metal is heavier and rings.
2. (write) Remember the Scrunch Ball test, where you scrunched kitchen foil and a chips packet. Your friend says a chips packet is metal, because it is shiny inside. What would you tell your friend? Use what you saw. Say it, and a grown-up writes it down.  
   **Answer:** The foil stayed scrunched, but the chips packet opened up again. So the packet is mostly plastic, with only a very thin metal coat inside.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what the child saw: the two balls acted differently, so they are different materials.
3. (sort) Where does it come from? Sort these into 'from a plant' and 'from under the ground'.  
   **Answer:** A cotton shirt → From a plant; A notebook → From a plant; A wooden chair → From a plant; A steel spoon → From the ground; A glass bottle → From the ground; A plastic bucket → From the ground. *Why:* Cotton grows on a plant, and paper and wood come from trees. Steel comes from iron in rocks, glass from sand, and most plastic from oil. All three are dug or pumped out of the ground.
4. (draw) Draw the newspaper cone from the Paper Cup Puzzle, one minute after the water went in.  
   **Answer:** A dark, soft-looking cone with drops of water falling onto a plate.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the paper darker where it is wet, and water dripping out. Paper soaks up water and lets it through.
5. (truefalse) One material, like steel, can be made into many different things.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Steel makes tumblers, spoons, keys, plates and even train carriages. The shape and the job change, but the material is the same.
6. (odd) Three of these are usually made of plastic. Which one is not?  
   Options: A bucket / A comb / A steel tumbler / A toothbrush handle  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A bucket, a comb and a toothbrush handle are light and plastic. A steel tumbler is metal, and it rings when you tap it.
7. (write) Why are windows made of glass and not wood? Say one good reason, and a grown-up writes it down.  
   **Answer:** Glass is see-through, so light comes in and we can see out. Wood would block the light.. *Why:* A strong answer names what the glass does for the job. Glass also keeps out rain and wind while letting light in.
8. (mcq) Why is a raincoat not made of paper?  
   Options: Paper goes soft and tears when it gets wet / Paper is too heavy to wear / Paper is too shiny  
   **Answer:** Paper goes soft and tears when it gets wet. *Why:* Paper soaks up water, then goes soft and tears, like the newspaper cone in the Paper Cup Puzzle. A raincoat must keep water out, so it is made of plastic or a coated fabric.
9. (label) Label the parts of a school water bottle with their materials: metal, plastic, rubber.  
   **Answer:** ['metal', 'plastic', 'rubber']. *Why:* Each part has a job. The metal body is strong, the plastic lid is light, and the bendy rubber ring keeps the water in.
10. (match) Match each clue to the material.  
   **Answer:** You can see through it → Glass; It has threads → Fabric; It has brown grain lines → Wood; It is shiny and rings when tapped → Metal; It tears easily and gets soggy in water → Paper. *Why:* Each material has clues you can see, feel or hear. Detectives use these clues to name a material.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a material?  
   Options: What a thing is made of / The colour of a thing / The name of a shop  
   **Answer:** What a thing is made of. *Why:* A material is what a thing is made of, like wood, metal, plastic, glass, fabric or paper.
2. (mcq) Which of these is made of fabric?  
   Options: A cotton shirt / A steel plate / A glass jar  
   **Answer:** A cotton shirt. *Why:* A cotton shirt is fabric, made of threads woven together. A steel plate is metal and a glass jar is glass.
3. (mcq) You can see right through a window. What is it usually made of?  
   Options: Glass / Wood / Paper  
   **Answer:** Glass. *Why:* Glass is hard and see-through, so light comes in. Wood and paper block the light.
4. (mcq) Which material has thin brown lines called grain?  
   Options: Wood / Glass / Plastic  
   **Answer:** Wood. *Why:* The grain in wood shows how the tree grew. Glass and plastic have no grain.
5. (mcq) Which of these is made of more than one material?  
   Options: An umbrella / A sheet of paper / A steel spoon  
   **Answer:** An umbrella. *Why:* An umbrella has a fabric top, metal spokes and a handle. A sheet of paper is only paper, and a steel spoon is only metal.
6. (mcq) Steel makes tumblers, spoons and keys. What does this show?  
   Options: One material can make many different things / Every material makes only one thing / Steel is a kind of plastic  
   **Answer:** One material can make many different things. *Why:* One material can be shaped into many things. The name of a thing tells you its job, and the material tells you what it is made of.
7. (mcq) Where does wood come from?  
   Options: Trees / Sand / Oil under the ground  
   **Answer:** Trees. *Why:* Wood comes from trees. Glass is made from sand, and most plastic from oil.
8. (mcq) Most plastic is made from what?  
   Options: Oil from deep under the ground / Cotton plants / Sand  
   **Answer:** Oil from deep under the ground. *Why:* Most plastic is made in factories from oil or gas found deep under the ground. Glass is the material made from sand.
9. (mcq) In the Paper Cup Puzzle, what did the cut-open paper cup have on the inside?  
   Options: A thin plastic skin / A layer of glass / Nothing, only paper  
   **Answer:** A thin plastic skin. *Why:* A thin plastic skin lines the inside of most paper cups. Water cannot soak through plastic, so the cup holds water.
10. (mcq) In the Paper Cup Puzzle, what happened to the newspaper cone?  
   Options: It went dark and soft and leaked / It stayed dry / It turned into plastic  
   **Answer:** It went dark and soft and leaked. *Why:* Paper soaks up water. The newspaper went dark, then soft, and the water dripped through.

## Applications
- Builders choose the right material for each part of a house: concrete for walls, glass for windows and wood or metal for doors.
- Kabadiwalas sort old things by material, so paper, metal and plastic can each be made into something new.
- Doctors use steel for tools, because steel can be washed in very hot water and stays clean.
- Tailors pick cotton for hot weather, because it lets air through and soaks up sweat.
- Toy makers use soft fabric for toys for babies, because soft fabric has no sharp edges and does not hurt when it falls.

## Open questions
- **How many different materials are there in the world?** Scientists keep making new ones, and nobody has counted them all. Nobody knows how many more could be made.
- **Can we make a plastic that rots away like a leaf, but is as strong as today's plastic?** Scientists are trying. Some new plastics break down more easily, but none yet does every job as well as the old kind.
- **How exactly do some bacteria eat plastic?** Scientists found bacteria that slowly eat one kind of plastic. They are still finding out how they do it, and whether it could help clean up plastic waste.
