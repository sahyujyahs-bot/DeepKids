# Stretching and springs: why things spring back, and when they stay stretched
`sci.forces.stretching-springs.L3`

**Summary.** A stretched rubber band springs back when you let go, but a pen spring pulled too far stays long. The child hangs coins on a band, 5 at a time, and finds that each 5 coins add about the same stretch, up to a limit. A solid keeps its shape because the pushes and pulls between its atoms balance. Pulled too far, rows of atoms slip and settle beside new neighbours, so the shape changes for good. The same science explains tendons, spider webs and the bent rock that causes earthquakes.

**Intro.** *Hold a rubber band between two fingers and stretch it a little. Feel it pull on your fingers. Stretch it more. Does the pull get bigger? Nothing else is touching the band. So where does that pull come from?* Give your child a rubber band and let them stretch it slowly, then let it go back. Ask two questions. What do you notice? What do you wonder? Good noticing sounds like 'the more I stretch it, the harder it pulls' or 'it gets thinner'. Write their wonders down and don't answer them yet. The coin test comes next, and it lets them find the answers for themselves.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what elastic means: a thing changes shape when pushed or pulled, and goes back when you let go. I can name elastic things at home.
1. I can guess, with a reason, what equal handfuls of coins will do to a rubber band. Then I can test it fairly and find the pattern.
2. I can explain the elastic limit: pull too far and a thing stays stretched or snaps. I can find where a pen spring stops coming back.
3. I can explain that a stretched or squashed spring stores energy, and give uses like catapults, wind-up toys, bows and bouncing balls.
4. I can find springs in machines, in nature and in my own body, and say what job each one does.
5. I can explain why a steel spring springs back: each atom rests where the pushes and pulls from its neighbours balance. Past the limit, rows of atoms slip and settle beside new neighbours, so the shape changes for good.
6. I can give big examples of stretching: rock storing strain before an earthquake, the whole Earth vibrating after a giant earthquake, and space itself stretching as gravitational waves pass. I can say what scientists still do not know.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces can make things move, stop, turn or change shape. *e.g.* You pull a rubber band. You squash a sponge. Both are forces.
- **Stretch**: To pull something so it gets longer. *e.g.* You stretch a rubber band round a bundle of pencils.
- **Squash**: To push on something so it gets shorter or flatter. Scientists also say compress. *e.g.* Pressing the top of a clicky pen squashes the spring inside.
- **Elastic**: A thing is elastic if it changes shape when pushed or pulled, and goes back to its old shape when you let go. *e.g.* A rubber band, a spring, a sponge and a bouncy ball are all elastic.
- **Spring**: A coil of springy metal wire. Pull it and it gets longer. Squash it and it gets shorter. Let go and it goes back. *e.g.* There is a small spring inside a clicky ballpoint pen.
- **Spring back**: To go back to the old shape after being stretched, squashed or bent. *e.g.* Press a sponge. Let go. It springs back.
- **Stretch-and-stay (plastic)**: A thing that changes shape and stays changed when you let go. Scientists call this plastic, which is a different meaning from plastic bags. *e.g.* Playdough, chewing gum and a strip from a thin carry bag stay stretched.
- **Snap (brittle)**: A thing that hardly bends at all, then suddenly breaks. Scientists call this brittle. *e.g.* A dry twig, a papad and a glass bangle snap.
- **Elastic limit**: The furthest you can stretch a thing and still have it spring back. Go past it and the thing stays stretched or breaks. *e.g.* Pull a pen spring very hard. It stays long and wobbly. It went past its elastic limit.
- **Hooke's law**: The rule that, for small stretches, equal extra pulls give equal extra stretches. Robert Hooke wrote it down in 1678. *e.g.* In the coin test, each 5 coins made the band longer by about the same step.
- **Strain**: How much a thing has been stretched, squashed or bent, compared with its own size. Rock that is slowly bent builds up strain, and stores energy, until it slips. *e.g.* Rock under the Himalaya is slowly bent by moving land. Its strain grows a little every year.
- **Pull-back force**: The force a stretched or squashed thing makes as it tries to go back to its old shape. Scientists call it the restoring force. *e.g.* A stretched rubber band pulls back on your fingers.
- **Stiff**: Hard to stretch or bend. A stiff spring needs a big pull to stretch a little. *e.g.* A cycle seat spring is much stiffer than a pen spring.
- **Stored energy**: Energy waiting inside something, ready to be used. A stretched or squashed spring stores energy. *e.g.* When you let go of a stretched catapult, the stored energy flings the paper ball.
- **Bounce**: When a thing hits the ground, squashes a little, then springs back and jumps up. *e.g.* A rubber ball bounces high. A ball of clay just goes splat.
- **Line graph**: A graph where dots are joined by a line. It shows how one thing changes as another thing changes. *e.g.* A line graph can show how long a rubber band gets as you add more coins.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* Your rubber band is made of countless atoms, all holding on to each other.
- **Bond**: The pull that holds two atoms together. A bond acts a bit like a tiny spring between them. *e.g.* Stretching a steel spring pulls each pair of atoms a tiny bit apart. The bond between them pulls them back together.
- **Polymer**: A very long chain made of many small groups of atoms joined in a row. Rubber is made of polymers. *e.g.* Rubber is made of long, tangled chains, like a bowl of cooked noodles.
- **Tendon**: A strong, stretchy cord that joins a muscle to a bone. Tendons work like springs when you run and jump. *e.g.* The thick cord at the back of your ankle is your Achilles tendon.
- **Earthquake**: A sudden shaking of the ground. It happens when rocks that were slowly bent underground suddenly slip and spring back. *e.g.* The ground shakes, and the lights swing.
- **Gravitational wave**: A ripple in space itself. As it passes, it stretches space one way and squeezes it the other way, by an incredibly tiny amount. *e.g.* In 2015, scientists first caught a gravitational wave from two black holes that crashed together.
- **Vibrate**: To shake back and forth very fast. A plucked rubber band vibrates because it keeps springing back past its middle. *e.g.* Pluck a stretched rubber band. It blurs and hums.

## Explanations
### What elastic means · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing is elastic if it changes shape when a force pushes or pulls it, and goes back to its old shape when the force stops.

Pull a rubber band and it gets longer. Let go and it snaps back. Squeeze a sponge and it gets smaller. Let go and it puffs back up. Both are elastic. Elastic things can also be bent or twisted, and then spring back. Steel, wood, stone and even glass are a little elastic too. They need a much bigger force before you can see them change.
- e.g. A hair band stretches round your hair, then goes back to its small loop.
- e.g. A plastic ruler bends when you press its end, then springs straight.
- e.g. A cricket ball squashes a tiny bit when the bat hits it, then springs back round.
### More pull, more stretch · *defined*
**Definition:** For small stretches, equal extra pulls give equal extra stretches. Double the pull and you get about double the stretch. This rule is called Hooke's law.

In the coin test, each 5 coins made the band longer by about the same step. Why? Each 5 coins pull down by the same amount. The band stretches until its pull back matches the pull of the coins. Suppose the band's pull back grows by the same amount for each centimetre of stretch. Then equal handfuls of coins must give equal steps. Robert Hooke wrote this rule down in 1678. He put it in Latin: 'ut tensio, sic vis'. It means 'as the stretch, so the force'. Steel springs follow it closely, for small stretches. Rubber bands follow it only roughly. Their steps often grow a little as the band gets long. This rule is why a spring balance has evenly spaced marks. The unit on measuring forces looks at spring balances more closely.
- e.g. One bag of shopping stretches the spring of a luggage scale a little. Two bags stretch it about twice as far.
- e.g. A thick rubber band needs a bigger pull than a thin one to stretch the same amount. Scientists say it is stiffer.
### The elastic limit: spring back, stay stretched, or snap · *defined*
**Definition:** Every elastic thing has a limit. Stretch it less than its limit and it springs back. Stretch it past its limit and it stays stretched, or it snaps.

Pull a pen spring gently and it springs back neatly. Pull it hard and it stays long and loose. It has gone past its elastic limit. Scientists use three words for what a material does when you stretch or bend it. Elastic things spring back, like rubber. Plastic things keep their new shape, like playdough and chewing gum. Brittle things, like a dry twig or a papad, hardly bend before they break. One thing can do all three. A steel spoon springs back from a small bend. Push hard and it stays bent. Bend it back and forth many times and it can snap.
- e.g. Bend a steel hairpin a little and it springs back. Bend it hard and it stays bent.
- e.g. A green twig bends and springs back. A dry twig snaps.
- e.g. A strip cut from a thin carry bag stretches long and stays long.
### Deep down: a shape is a balance of pulls · *defined*
**Definition:** A solid keeps its shape because each atom rests where the pulls and pushes from its neighbours balance. Stretch a little and those pulls bring the atoms back. Stretch too far and rows of atoms slip, and each atom settles beside new neighbours.

Zoom far into a steel spring. You find atoms in neat rows. Each atom is held to its neighbours by electric pulls called bonds. Push two atoms too close and they push apart. Pull them a little apart and they pull together. So each atom rests at the one place where the push and the pull balance. Stretching the spring moves every atom a tiny bit from that place. For small moves, twice the move gives twice the pull back. That is why a steel spring gives equal steps for equal weights. Now pull much too far. Whole rows of atoms slip past each other, like cards sliding in a pack. Each atom now holds on to new neighbours, and rests in a new balance. Let go, and nothing pulls it back to the old place. That is why the pen spring stayed long. Its atoms are the same atoms. Only which atoms hold which has changed.
- e.g. Scientists often build models of solids with little balls joined by springs.
- e.g. Bend a steel spoon a little and it springs back. Bend it hard and it stays bent, because rows of its atoms have slipped.
- e.g. Heat makes atoms jiggle more on their bonds. That is why a metal rod gets very slightly longer when it is hot.
- e.g. Some wire, called memory metal, goes back to its old shape when you warm it. Its atoms can sit in rows in two different patterns. Warming the wire moves them back into the first pattern, and the old shape comes back with it. Scientists call this shape memory.
### A long band is many springs in a row · *defined*
**Definition:** A long spring stretches more than a short one, because it is like many short springs joined in a row. A thick band stretches less, because it is like many thin bands side by side, sharing the pull.

In the Two in a Row activity, two bands in a chain stretched about twice as far as one band. Each band in the chain carries the whole weight of the coins. So each stretches as much as one band alone. Side by side, each band carries only half the weight, so the pair stretches about half as far. Atoms work the same way. A long wire has more bonds in a row. Each bond stretches a tiny bit, and all the tiny bits add up. Robert Hooke used this idea. A short wire hardly seemed to stretch at all. So he hung weights from a wire 20 to 40 feet long, about 6 to 12 metres. Then the stretch became big enough to measure.
- e.g. A long rubber band stretches further than a short one of the same thickness, with the same coins.
- e.g. A bungee cord is many thin rubber strands side by side, so it can hold a person.
- e.g. A thick rope stretches less than a thin string, because it has more fibres sharing the pull.
### Rubber is different: tangled chains · *defined*
**Definition:** Rubber is made of very long, tangled chains of atoms called polymers. Stretching straightens the chains. They spring back because jiggling atoms shake them back into a tangle.

Think of a bowl of cooked noodles, all tangled. Pull them and they line up straight. In rubber, the chains are joined to each other here and there, so they cannot slide apart. The atoms in the chains jiggle all the time, because of heat. There are far more ways for a chain to be tangled than straight. So when you let go, the jiggling shakes the chains back into a tangle. This is why rubber does odd things. It warms up a little when you stretch it fast. A stretched rubber band even pulls harder when you heat it. Stretch rubber very far, again and again, and some of the links between chains slip or break. That is why an old, over-used band is longer and looser than a new one.
- e.g. A rubber band left round a newspaper for months goes long and floppy.
- e.g. Cold rubber goes stiff, because its chains jiggle less.
### Springs: stretch springs and squash springs · *defined*
**Definition:** A spring is a coil of springy metal wire. Some springs are made to be stretched, and some are made to be squashed. Both push or pull back when you let go.

A stretch spring has its coils packed tightly together. You pull its ends apart. A squash spring has gaps between its coils. You press its ends together. The spring in a clicky pen is a squash spring. The spring inside a hanging spring balance, the kind used to weigh luggage, is a stretch spring. A spring is a long, stiff steel wire wound into a small coil. When the coil stretches, each part of the wire twists only a little. All the little twists add up to a big stretch.
- e.g. A clicky pen has a squash spring. It pushes the tip back in.
- e.g. A trampoline has stretch springs all round its edge.
- e.g. A clothes peg has a twisty spring that snaps its jaws shut.
### Stretching stores energy · *defined*
**Definition:** When you stretch, squash, bend or twist an elastic thing, the energy you put in is stored inside it. Let go, and the stored energy can make something move.

Energy is what it takes to make something move, warm up or change. When you pull back a catapult, your arm puts energy into the stretched rubber. When you let go, the rubber springs back and gives that energy to the stone or paper ball. The more you stretch, the more energy is stored, until the elastic limit. People have used stored elastic energy for thousands of years. Bows fling arrows. Wind-up watches and toys run on a tightly coiled spring. Some old mechanical clocks run for a whole week on one winding.
- e.g. A wind-up toy car runs when its twisted spring unwinds.
- e.g. A bow made of bamboo bends back, stores energy, and flings the arrow.
- e.g. A twisted rubber band can turn the propeller of a toy plane.
### Bouncing is a squash and a spring back · *defined*
**Definition:** When a ball hits the floor, it squashes for a very short moment. Then it springs back and pushes itself up. That is a bounce.

The squash is too quick to see. Slow-motion films show a tennis ball going flat on one side when it lands. Wet a ball and drop it on a dry floor. The wet mark shows how big the squashed part was. No ball bounces all the way back up. Each bounce turns some of the energy into heat and sound. A good rubber ball loses less energy than most balls, so it bounces higher. A ball of clay is not elastic. It squashes and stays squashed, so it does not bounce at all. Tennis has a rule about bounce. A tennis ball dropped from 254 cm must bounce back up between 135 cm and 147 cm.
- e.g. A bouncy rubber ball bounces higher than a sponge ball.
- e.g. A trampoline's springs and stretchy mat squash down under you, then push you up.
### Springs in living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Living bodies are full of elastic parts. Tendons, skin, lungs and blood vessels stretch and spring back. Some animals store energy in springs to jump.

Many living things store energy in springy parts and get it back. When you run, the tendon at the back of your ankle stretches as your foot lands. It springs back as you push off. Tests show that a tendon gives back about nine-tenths of the energy put into it, so your muscles do less work. The arch of your foot works as a spring too. Kangaroos hop a long way on long, springy tendons in their legs. A flea jumps with a pad of a rubbery material called resilin. Torkel Weis-Fogh, a Danish scientist, discovered resilin in insects in 1960. The flea's muscles squash the pad slowly and lock it in place. Then the pad is let go all at once, far faster than any muscle can move. Other elastic parts soak up a hit or keep a shape. A spider's sticky threads stretch to more than twice their length when an insect flies in, then pull back. Your skin springs back because of a stretchy material called elastin. Your biggest blood vessel, the aorta, stretches with each heartbeat and springs back. Inside your muscles, a giant protein called titin works like a tiny spring.
- e.g. Pinch the skin on the back of your hand and let go. It springs back.
- e.g. A spider's web sags when an insect hits it, instead of breaking, then springs back.
- e.g. The mantis shrimp, a sea animal, punches with a spring in their arm. Sheila Patek and her team filmed the punch in 2004.
### Bend, don't break: why springy things survive · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing that can bend and spring back can take a sudden heavy push without breaking. A stiff, brittle thing may snap instead.

When a heavy load suddenly lands on something, its energy has to go somewhere. A springy thing bends, stores the energy for a moment, then gives it back. A stiff, brittle thing cannot bend, so the force builds up until it cracks. That is why bamboo bends in a storm instead of snapping. A green mango branch bends under a swing and springs back. Bent past its limit, it stays bent, and that is the warning that it may break. Tall buildings are designed to sway a little in strong winds, for the same reason.
- e.g. A coconut palm bends far over in a cyclone and often stands up again.
- e.g. A car's springs squash over a bump and spring back, so the car and the people in it are not jolted so hard.
### The Earth is springy: earthquakes and a ringing planet · *defined*
**Definition:** Rock is elastic too. Where giant pieces of the Earth's crust press together, the rock bends slowly and stores strain. When it suddenly slips, it springs back, and the ground shakes. That is an earthquake.

The Earth's outer layer is broken into giant pieces called plates. They move slowly, about as fast as your fingernails grow. The plate India sits on is pushing north into Asia. Where two plates press together, the rock gets stuck. It bends slowly, like a ruler pressed at one end, and stores strain. Across the Himalaya, GPS stations show the rock being squeezed by about 2 centimetres a year. This can go on for hundreds of years. Then the rock slips and springs back in seconds, and the ground shakes. That happened in Nepal on 25 April 2015. Harry Fielding Reid, an American scientist, explained this in 1910, after studying the great San Francisco earthquake of 1906. Because rock is elastic, the shaking travels through it as waves. After a giant earthquake in Chile in 1960, instruments found the whole Earth vibrating slowly for days, like a bell after it is struck.
- e.g. The Himalayas are still being pushed up, which is why north India and Nepal have earthquakes.
- e.g. Bend a plastic ruler slowly until it slips off your finger. It springs back with a twang, like rock in an earthquake.
### Space itself stretches: gravitational waves · *defined*
**Definition:** Space can stretch and squeeze. When huge things like black holes crash together, they send out ripples in space called gravitational waves.

Albert Einstein predicted these ripples in 1916. As a wave passes, space stretches one way and squeezes the other way, then back again. On 14 September 2015, two giant instruments in the United States, called LIGO, caught one for the first time. It came from two black holes that crashed together more than a billion years ago. The wave stretched LIGO's 4-kilometre-long arms by less than a hundredth of the width of a proton. A proton is a speck inside an atom. More than a thousand scientists worked on it, including teams from India. India is now setting up its own detector, called LIGO-India, in Maharashtra. Space stretches in a slower way too. The space between galaxies is growing, so galaxies are slowly getting further apart.
- e.g. Draw dots on a wide rubber band and stretch it. Every dot moves away from every other dot, a little like galaxies in our growing universe.
- e.g. A gravitational wave passing through you would stretch you a tiny bit taller and thinner. Then it would make you shorter and wider.
### What is space, that it can stretch? · *debated*
We know space can stretch, squeeze and ripple. We have measured it. But what is space made of, that it can do this? Is it a kind of stuff, like the rubber of a rubber band? Or is stretching just a way of describing distances between things changing? Nobody knows for sure. This is one of the deepest open questions in physics.
- e.g. A rubber sheet with a heavy ball on it is often used to picture bent space. It is a helpful picture, but space is not really a rubber sheet.
- **Einstein's theory of gravity, called general relativity:** Space and time together bend, stretch and ripple. The theory describes this very well. It does not say what space is made of.
- **Some physicists working on string theory:** Everything might be made of incredibly tiny vibrating strings, a little like plucked rubber bands. Space itself might come from them. Nobody has found any evidence yet that this is true.
- **Other physicists, working on ideas like loop quantum gravity:** Space might be made of tiny pieces, like the threads of a woven net, far too small to ever see. This idea also has no evidence yet.

## Mostly agreed
- Elastic things change shape when pushed or pulled, and go back when the force stops. *How we know:* Anyone can test this with rubber bands, springs and sponges. Engineers test steel, wood and concrete the same way, with big machines that stretch and squash them.
- For many springs, double the pull gives about double the stretch, up to a limit. *How we know:* Robert Hooke tested springs and long wires and published the rule in 1678. In the coin test, each 5 coins add about the same stretch. Spring balances work because of it.
- Every elastic thing has a limit. Past it, the thing stays stretched or breaks. *How we know:* Stretch a pen spring hard and it stays long. Engineers measure the limit of every material used in bridges and buildings.
- A solid keeps its shape because of electric pulls and pushes between its atoms. Past the elastic limit, rows of atoms slip into new places. *How we know:* X-rays show that the atoms in metals sit in neat rows. Electron microscopes show the slipped rows inside metal that was bent too far.
- A stretched or squashed elastic thing stores energy, which it gives back when it springs back. *How we know:* Catapults, bows, wind-up clocks and bouncing balls all use it. Scientists measure the energy that goes in and comes out.
- Earthquakes happen when bent rock underground suddenly slips and springs back. *How we know:* Scientists measured how the ground had bent before and after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. Today GPS stations measure rock slowly bending across the Himalaya.

## How we found out
**Robert Hooke (1635 to 1703), an English scientist. Later, Sophie Germain (1776 to 1831), a French mathematician., He found the rule around 1660, hid it in a letter puzzle in 1676, and published it in 1678..** Robert Hooke was an English scientist. Around 1660, he worked out a rule for how springs stretch. A short wire stretches too little to measure. Hooke hung a pan for weights from a very long wire, 20 to 40 feet long, about 6 to 12 metres. Every part of the wire stretched a tiny amount, and all the tiny amounts added up to a stretch he could measure. He added equal weights, one after another. Each one made the wire longer by about the same step. He tested coiled springs, a watch spring and a strip of dry wood too. In 1676 he hid his rule in a puzzle of jumbled letters, so nobody could claim it first. In 1678 he gave the answer in Latin: 'ut tensio, sic vis'. It means 'as the stretch, so the force'. He said it works for every springy thing, from metal and stone to hair, silk, bone and glass. Later tests showed he had gone too far. Pull anything past its limit, and the steps stop being equal. Rubber follows the rule only roughly. In 1816, Sophie Germain, a French mathematician who taught herself, won a top prize for explaining how springy plates vibrate.  
*What people believed before:* Hooke said every springy thing follows his rule, however it is stretched. Later tests showed that the rule works only for small stretches, below the elastic limit, and only roughly for rubber.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Stretchy things are weak, and strong things are stiff.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: A rubber band breaks easily, while a steel rod does not stretch at all that we can see.  
  Against: Spider silk is very stretchy and, weight for weight, stronger than steel wire. Living roots and bamboo survive storms because they bend. Being able to stretch can make a thing tougher, because it soaks up a sudden push instead of cracking.
- **Hooke's rule works for every springy thing.** (Robert Hooke himself, in 1678, and many people since; *rejected*)  
  For: Hooke found equal steps in metal springs, long wires and dry wood, and said the rule held for every springy body. Steel springs follow the rule very closely for small stretches.  
  Against: Rubber bands follow it only roughly. Their steps often grow as the band gets long. And every spring breaks the rule once it is pulled past its elastic limit. Then the steps stop being equal, and the spring stays long. Hooke's rule is very useful, but it is a rule for small stretches.
- **Robert Hooke alone discovered the rule for springs, and invented the spring for watches.** (Many physics books; *debated*)  
  For: Hooke published the spring rule in 1678. He also worked on springs to control watches in the 1660s.  
  Against: Christiaan Huygens, a Dutch scientist, built a watch with a spiral spring in 1675. Hooke said he had the idea first, and the two men argued bitterly. Historians still disagree about who was first with the watch spring. The spring rule is fairly given to Hooke. In France, Edme Mariotte found the same rule on his own a few years later.
- **Isaac Newton had the only portrait of Robert Hooke destroyed.** (A story often told about Hooke and Newton, who were rivals; *minority*)  
  For: No portrait of Hooke survives from his lifetime. Newton disliked Hooke. After Hooke died, Newton led the Royal Society, the science club where a portrait might have hung.  
  Against: Nobody has found any evidence that Newton destroyed a portrait. It may simply have been lost. In the 2000s an artist, Rita Greer, painted new portraits of Hooke from written descriptions of him.
- **Rubber was invented in Europe or America, by people like Charles Goodyear.** (Many older books; *rejected*)  
  For: Charles Goodyear found in 1839 how to make rubber tough with heat and sulphur. That made car tyres and rubber bands possible.  
  Against: About 3,500 years ago, people in Mexico and Central America were already making bouncy rubber balls. In 1999, Dorothy Hosler and her team at MIT showed how. They mixed the milky sap of the rubber tree with juice from a morning glory vine. Different mixtures made rubber that bounced more, or rubber that was tougher.
- **Cold would not matter to the rubber seals on the space shuttle Challenger.** (Managers at NASA and at the company that made the booster rockets, in January 1986; *rejected*)  
  For: The seals had worked on earlier flights. The managers were under pressure to launch on time.  
  Against: An engineer called Roger Boisjoly warned the night before that cold rubber goes stiff and springs back too slowly. The launch went ahead on a freezing morning. A seal failed, and the shuttle broke apart. The seven astronauts on board died. Later, the scientist Richard Feynman squeezed a piece of the seal rubber in a small clamp. He dipped it in iced water during the inquiry. When he let go, it stayed squashed for a few seconds instead of springing back.
- **A branch that is bending is about to break.** (A common worry; *rejected*)  
  For: Branches do break when they are bent too far.  
  Against: Bending by itself is safe. A green branch bends in every breeze and springs back. The warning sign is a branch that bends and does not come all the way back. That means it has passed its elastic limit. Engineers watch for this same sign in cranes and bridges.

## Roots
We have found that equal pulls give equal stretches, until a spring is pulled too far and stays long. Now we ask what that tells us about the shapes of things, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** After a hard pull, your pen spring rested at a new, longer length, and sprang back to that. Nothing outside was stretching it. Its atoms push and pull on each other all the time, and its length is where those forces balance. So a spring lying still is full of forces that cancel out.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a thing lying still has no forces in it, and that its length is simply fixed. The pen spring settled at a new length by itself, so something inside it must set its length.  
*Evidence:* In the coin test experiment, the pen spring came back from small pulls. After a big pull it rested at a new, longer length, and from then on it sprang back to that length.

- *who am i:* Your body keeps its shape the same way a spring does. Your tendons and skin stretch with every step, then spring back.
- *what is life:* Living things are full of springs: tendons, spider silk, the rubbery pad in a flea's leg. They store energy when they stretch, and give most of it back for the next step or jump.
- *what is this world:* Every solid is a little elastic, even stone and steel. They are just very stiff. The rock under the Himalaya is being bent slowly right now.

A spring lying still has strong pushes and pulls inside it. They cancel out, so the total is zero, and the spring keeps its shape. These pushes and pulls all come from one force, the electric force between atoms. That one force holds the shape of a pen spring, a tendon, a spider's thread and a mountain. Stretch any of them a little, and the same force pulls it back.

- **Heraclitus, fragment DK B51:** Heraclitus said people do not see how something pulling in two directions can hold together. His examples were the bow and the lyre, a stringed instrument. (A strung bow keeps its shape only because the string pulls one way and the wood pulls back. Inside a solid, each atom stays in place because the pushes and pulls from its neighbours balance.)
- **Tao Te Ching 76:** Living plants are soft and bendy. Dead ones are dry and brittle. So the stiff and hard are easily broken, and the soft and bendy last. (In Bend or Snap?, the green twig bends and springs back, and the dry twig snaps. Living tendons, skin and silk are springy too.)
- **Anguttara Nikaya 6.55, the story of Sona:** The Buddha asks Sona, who once played the veena. If the strings are too tight, does it play in tune? If they are too loose? Only strings stretched just right make music. (In the Rubber Band Orchestra, a band's note changes with how tightly it is stretched. Too loose, and it only thuds. Too tight, and it can pass its limit or snap.)
- Science (settled): A solid keeps its shape because of electric pulls and pushes between its atoms. For small stretches, the pull back grows in step with the stretch, which is why Hooke's rule works. Past the elastic limit, rows of atoms slip into new places.
- Science (open): Nobody can yet predict exactly where and when a slip will start, in a bent metal spring or in the rock under the Himalaya.
- Other view, Aristotle in ancient Greece, and some philosophers today: Aristotle taught that every thing has a form, its own shape and kind, which it keeps by its nature. For him, a spring's shape belongs to what the spring is, not to a balance of pushes and pulls. Some philosophers today still say a thing's form is more than the forces between its parts.
- Practice, Feel two pulls make zero (3 min): Hold a rubber band between your two pointing fingers. Stretch it a little and keep your hands still. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly. The band is not moving. But feel it pull on both fingers, all the time. Now let your fingers come together very slowly. Feel the pull fade as the band goes back. Last, rest your hand on your arm. Your arm is still too. What is pulling inside it?
- Ask: After the big pull, the pen spring rests at a new length. Is it the same spring as before, or a new one?
- Ask: If your own shape is held by pulls between atoms, what is holding you together right now?
- Ask: How could anyone tell that a still rock, or a still bridge, is close to its limit?

- *deep:* A spring lying on the table looks as if nothing is happening inside it. Is anything going on in there? (hint: Think of each atom resting where the pushes and pulls from its neighbours balance. What happens to them when you stretch it?)
- *deep:* Why should equal handfuls of coins give equal steps of stretch? What would have to be true inside the band? (hint: What if the band pulls back a little harder for each centimetre it is stretched?)

## Story: The Tired Branch
*Rubber tapping in Kerala, smoked rubber sheets and the Onam swing, the oonjal, are real. Ammu, her family, Biju and the mango tree are made up. The warning sign in the story is real physics: a branch or spring that no longer springs all the way back has gone past its elastic limit, and may soon break.*

In a village near Kottayam, in Kerala, the rubber trees stand in long straight rows, like children lined up for school. Every morning before the sun was up, Ammu's mother walked between them with a lamp on her forehead and a curved knife in her hand. On each tree she shaved a thin spiral strip of bark. Out of the cut crept a white, slow milk, which ran down the spiral into a coconut-shell cup. Latex, her mother called it. Once it dried, it would be rubber.

Ammu was ten. Her job came after school. She carried the full cups home, poured the latex into flat trays and helped her mother squeeze it into sheets through the iron rollers in the shed. Then the sheets were hung in the smoke above the fire to dry, brown and shining, like enormous dosas.

Before any sheet was sold, her mother tested it. She took one corner between her finger and thumb and pulled. A good sheet stretched, and then, when she let go, slid back flat as if nothing had happened. Once Ammu found an old sheet that had been forgotten in the sun for weeks. When her mother pulled it, it stretched and stayed stretched, a tired little bulge. When she pulled again, it tore.

'See,' said her mother. 'When it stops coming back, it is close to breaking.'

Ammu said yes, Amma, the way you say yes to things you are not really listening to. It was August, and Onam was coming, and Onam meant the swing.

Every year the children of the lane hung an oonjal, a festival swing, from the big mango tree at the end of the path. It was a plank seat on two long ropes, decorated with marigolds and mango leaves. This year Biju, who was twelve and the leader of everything, had a plan. 'The low branch is for babies,' he said. 'We'll use the high one. The swing will go up past the roof of the shed.'

The high branch was thinner. It stuck out over the path like an arm reaching for something. Biju climbed up and knotted the ropes round it, and everyone cheered.

On the first day, the swing was glorious. When Biju swung, the branch bent down under him like a fishing rod, and lifted him up again, so that every swing had an extra bounce at the bottom. The children shrieked. They queued all afternoon. Ammu had three turns. At the top of each one she could see over the shed and across the whole green sea of rubber trees, and her stomach floated up into her throat.

On the second day, the bigger children started swinging two at a time, standing up, pumping as hard as they could. 'Higher!' they yelled. 'Make it fly!'

Ammu was waiting her turn when she noticed something. When the two big girls jumped off, the swing hung still. And the branch did not go all the way back up.

She looked again. In the morning, the swing's plank had hung level with her chin. Now it hung level with her shoulder. The branch had bent and stayed bent, just a little, the way the old rubber sheet had stretched and stayed stretched.

She went cold all over, even in the August heat.

'Biju,' she said. 'The branch is tired.'

Biju laughed. Everyone laughed. 'It's a strong branch,' he said. 'It bounced me all day yesterday.'

'It bounced back yesterday,' said Ammu. 'It isn't bouncing back now. Look at the seat. It's lower.'

'You're scared because you're small,' said one of the big girls, and the others made chicken noises. Ammu's face went hot. She wanted to be quiet. Being quiet would have been much easier. But she thought of the old sheet, the tired bulge, and the sound it made when it tore.

Her little sister Kunju was next in the queue, holding up her arms to be lifted on.

'No,' said Ammu, and she pulled Kunju back by her frock, so hard that Kunju began to cry.

'Fine,' said Biju. 'Then I'll show you.' He climbed onto the plank, stood up, and pumped once, twice, three times, swinging higher than the shed roof.

On the fourth swing there was a sound like a coconut splitting.

The branch did not snap all at once. It cracked, and sagged, and Biju, at the bottom of the swing, felt the ropes go soft in his hands. He jumped. He landed on his knees in the mud of the path. A second later the whole branch, ropes, marigolds and all, came down with a heavy, leafy crash exactly where Kunju would have been sitting.

Nobody said anything for a long time. A crow, offended, flew off into the rubber trees.

Biju stood up slowly. His knees were muddy and his face was white. He looked at the broken branch, with its pale, splintered end. Then he looked at Ammu.

'How did you know?' he said.

Ammu told him about the sheet. She showed him how the swing had hung lower and lower. 'When something stops coming back,' she said, 'it's near the end.' She didn't say 'I told you.' She didn't need to.

That evening, Ammu's mother walked down the lane and looked at the branch on the ground. She looked at the high stump, and at the low, thick branch that the swing had always hung from before. She did not scold anyone. She only touched Ammu's head on her way home, very lightly.

The next morning, the children hung the swing again, on the low branch. It was not as exciting. It did not go higher than the shed. But after every turn, Ammu watched the seat as the rider jumped off, and every time, the branch lifted it back up to exactly the height of her chin.

On Onam day, Biju pushed Kunju on the swing himself, gently, for nearly an hour.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a branch, like a rubber band or a spring, is elastic. Pushed a little, it bends and springs all the way back. Pushed past its elastic limit, it bends and stays bent. That small bend that does not come back is the warning. The thing has started to change inside, and it is close to breaking. Ammu spots this because she has seen the same thing in an old rubber sheet. Engineers watch for this sign in bridges, cranes and aeroplane wings.

- Ask: How did Ammu's mother test a rubber sheet? What did it mean when the sheet stayed stretched?
- Ask: What did Ammu notice about the swing seat after the big children jumped off?
- Ask: Why did the branch break on the second day, but not on the first?
- Ask: Why was it hard for Ammu to speak up? What helped her?
- Ask: Have you ever seen something that 'stopped coming back'? A rubber band, a spring or a toy? What happened to it next?

### Springs, Atoms and Stretching Space (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Zoom deep into a steel spring and you find atoms in neat rows. Each atom is held to its neighbours by pulls called bonds. You stretch the spring a little. What do the bonds do?

   **Answer:** They stretch a tiny bit and pull the atoms back to their places. *Why:* Each atom rests where the pulls from its neighbours balance. Pull the atoms a little apart, and the bonds pull them back, like tiny springs. Picking 'only the outside stretches' misses that every row of atoms stretches a little.
2. (write) A girl bends a steel hairpin hard, and it stays bent. Her brother says, 'Some of the metal must have been squeezed out.' Is he right? Say what really happened to the atoms.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says no, nothing was squeezed out. Every atom is still there. The bend went past the elastic limit, so rows of atoms slipped over each other and settled beside new neighbours. That is why the new shape stays.. *Why:* Below the limit, bonds stretch and pull back. Past it, atoms move to new places. Nothing is lost, only moved.
3. (truefalse) Hang coins from a rubber band. Then blow warm air from a hair dryer on the band. The band gets a little shorter.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True, which surprises most people. Warmth makes the tangled chains inside rubber jiggle more. More jiggling shakes them back into a tangle harder, so the band pulls up. Most other things get a little longer when warm.
4. (mcq) Rubber is made of long tangled chains, like a bowl of cooked noodles. What does stretching do to the chains?  
   Options: It pulls them straighter, and jiggling tangles them again when you let go / It cuts them into short pieces / It packs them into neat rows, like steel / It makes them melt  
   **Answer:** It pulls them straighter, and jiggling tangles them again when you let go. *Why:* Stretching lines the chains up. The atoms in them jiggle all the time, and there are many more tangled ways than straight ways. So when you let go, they shake back into a tangle. Picking 'neat rows' is how steel is built, not rubber.
5. (write) Robert Hooke, an English scientist, wanted to measure how a wire stretches. A short wire stretched too little to see. So he hung weights on a wire 6 to 12 metres long. Why did a long wire help?  
   **Answer:** A long wire has many more atoms in a row. Each little piece of wire stretches a tiny bit. All the tiny bits add up to a stretch big enough to measure.. *Why:* This is the same reason two bands in a chain stretch twice as far as one.
6. (mcq) Many people think heavy buildings cause earthquakes. Where do earthquakes really start?

   **Answer:** Many kilometres underground, where rock bends slowly for years and then suddenly slips. *Why:* The Earth's outer layer is broken into giant pieces that push on each other. The rock bends and stores energy, then slips in seconds. Harry Fielding Reid explained this in 1910. Buildings are far too small and too near the top to do it.

   **Answer:** Pressing the ruler down slowly is like the years before: the rock bends and stores energy. Under the Himalaya, rock is being squeezed by about 2 cm a year, storing energy for the next slip.

   Options: About three times as far / About a third as far / The same  
   **Answer:** About three times as far. *Why:* Each band in the chain carries all the coins, so each stretches as much as one band alone. Three stretches add up. Picking 'a third' mixes it up with bands side by side, which share the pull.
13. (mcq) Why does a spring that was pulled past its elastic limit stay long?  
   Options: Rows of its atoms slipped into new places and stay there / Some of its atoms fell out / It got colder and shrank back the wrong way  
   **Answer:** Rows of its atoms slipped into new places and stay there. *Why:* Past the limit, rows of atoms slip and settle beside new neighbours. Every atom is still there, just in a new place, so the new shape stays.
14. (mcq) For hundreds of years before an earthquake, what is the rock deep underground doing?  
   Options: Slowly bending and storing energy / Slowly melting / Staying perfectly still  
   **Answer:** Slowly bending and storing energy. *Why:* Giant pieces of the Earth push on each other, so the rock bends slowly, like a pressed ruler. Then it slips all at once, and the ground shakes.
15. (mcq) What does a gravitational wave do as it passes?  
   Options: It stretches and squeezes space itself, by a tiny amount / It makes waves on the sea / It is a beam of light  
   **Answer:** It stretches and squeezes space itself, by a tiny amount. *Why:* Gravitational waves are ripples in space. LIGO measured one in 2015. Picking 'the sea' mixes them up with ocean waves.
16. (mcq) After a giant earthquake in Chile in 1960, what did instruments find the whole Earth doing for days?  
   Options: Vibrating slowly, like a bell after it is struck / Spinning backwards / Getting bigger  
   **Answer:** Vibrating slowly, like a bell after it is struck. *Why:* Rock is elastic, so the whole planet rang like a bell. The shaking travelled through it as waves.

## Applications
- Hanging spring balances, luggage scales and forcemeters measure weight by how far a spring stretches.
- Clicky pens, staplers, clothes pegs, door latches and mousetraps use small springs.
- Mattresses, sofas, cycle seats and charpais are springy so they are comfortable.
- Cars, buses, autos and trains have big springs and shock absorbers to soften bumps.
- Wind-up watches and clocks run on a coiled spring. HMT made wind-up watches in Bengaluru for decades.
- Catapults, bows and slingshots store energy in stretched wood and rubber.
- Rubber bands, hair bands and the elastic in clothes hold things snugly.
- Balls for cricket, football and tennis are tested for how well they bounce.
- Trampolines and diving boards bend and spring back to throw people up.
- Bungee cords are bundles of rubber strands that stretch to stop a fall gently.
- Musical instruments like the veena, sitar, violin and tabla use stretched strings and skins.
- Earthquake-safe buildings are designed to sway and spring back instead of cracking.
- Dentists use springy wires, often of memory metal, to straighten teeth. Doctors use springy tubes called stents to hold blood vessels open.
- Scientists copy spider silk and resilin to try to make new, tougher materials.
- LIGO and the future LIGO-India use the stretching of space to listen to black holes crashing far away.
- GPS stations across the Himalaya measure the slow bending of rock, to understand where strain is building before earthquakes.

## Open questions
- **What is space made of, that it can stretch and ripple?** We can measure space stretching, but nobody knows what it is made of. String theory and loop quantum gravity are ideas, with no evidence yet.
- **Exactly how do spiders make silk that is so strong and so stretchy, and can we make it in factories?** Scientists know a lot about the proteins in silk. But copying how the spider spins it, at a large scale, is still very hard.
- **Can we predict when bent rock will slip and cause an earthquake?** Scientists can measure rock slowly bending. But they still cannot say when it will slip.
- **Why do springs and rubber get 'tired' and crack after being stretched many times?** This is called fatigue. Engineers test it carefully, but exactly when a crack will start is still hard to predict.
- **How exactly does titin, the giant spring protein in our muscles, work?** Scientists know titin acts like a spring, but how all its parts work together is still being studied.
- **Can we always see the warning signs before something breaks?** A thing that stays bent is a warning. But tiny cracks can grow inside without showing. Engineers use sound waves and X-rays to look, and still cannot catch everything.
- **Could there be a perfectly elastic material that gives back all of its energy?** Every real material turns a little energy into heat each time it springs back. Resilin comes close. Nobody knows how close anything can get.

## Connected maths
- In the coin stretch test, the thin band was 10 cm long with no coins. Each 5 coins made it 1 cm longer. How long will it be with 30 coins? Use the line graph to help. **16 cm.**. 30 coins is 6 lots of 5 coins, because 30 divided by 5 is 6. Each lot adds 1 cm, so the band grows 6 cm. 10 + 6 = 16 cm. On the graph, the line keeps going up 1 cm for every 5 coins. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In the coin test, the band's steps for each 5 coins were: 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 and a half, and 1 and a half centimetres. Which steps are different from the rest? What might that tell you about the band? **The last two steps are different. They are 1 and a half centimetres instead of 1. When the band was already long, the same 5 coins stretched it more.**. The first six steps were all 1 cm, so equal coins gave equal stretches. The last two were half a centimetre bigger. So the band follows the equal-steps rule only up to a point. Rubber follows Hooke's rule only roughly once it is stretched a long way. A steel spring would keep equal steps for longer. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Look at the ruler beside the rubber band in the picture. The big numbers are centimetres. Each small mark is half a centimetre. The bottom of the band is at the small mark just after 12. How long is the band? **12 and a half centimetres.**. The bottom of the band is past 12 but before 13. It is on the small mark halfway between them. Each small mark is half a centimetre. So the band is 12 and a half centimetres long. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- In the Bungee Doll challenge, 2 bands let the doll fall 30 cm, 3 bands 40 cm and 4 bands 50 cm. The table is 75 cm tall. What is the most bands you can use so the doll's lowest point stays above the floor? **6 bands. The doll falls 70 cm, which is 5 cm above the floor. 7 bands would be 80 cm, which hits the floor.**. Each extra band adds 10 cm. 5 bands make 60 cm. 6 bands make 70 cm. 7 bands make 80 cm. The table is 75 cm tall. 70 cm is less than 75 cm, so 6 bands is safe. 80 cm is more than 75 cm, so 7 bands would hit the floor. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- In the Bounce Test, these balls were dropped from 100 cm. Rubber ball 80 cm, tennis ball 55 cm, sponge ball 20 cm, plastic ball 40 cm. Draw a bar chart. Then answer: how much higher did the rubber ball bounce than the sponge ball? **60 cm higher. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each ball, as tall as its bounce. The rubber ball bounced 80 cm. The sponge ball bounced 20 cm. 80 take away 20 is 60. So the rubber ball bounced 60 cm higher. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Your rubber band car went 60 cm with 5 winds. Suppose every 5 winds add about the same distance. About how far would it go with 15 winds? Then compare with the real result of 180 cm. **About 180 cm. That matches the real result.**. 15 winds is 3 lots of 5 winds. Each lot gives about 60 cm. 3 × 60 = 180 cm. The real car went 180 cm, so the guess was right. With many more winds, the band would reach its limit, and the pattern would stop working. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- One rubber band stretched 4 cm with 20 coins. How far would a chain of 3 of the same bands stretch with the same 20 coins? And how far would 2 bands side by side stretch? **The chain of 3 stretches about 12 cm. The 2 bands side by side stretch about 2 cm.**. In a chain, every band carries all 20 coins, so each one stretches 4 cm. 3 lots of 4 cm is 12 cm. Side by side, each band carries half the coins, 10 each. Half the coins gives half the stretch, so 4 divided by 2 is 2 cm. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
