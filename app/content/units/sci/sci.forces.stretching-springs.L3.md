# Stretching and springs: things that bounce back
`sci.forces.stretching-springs.L3`

**Summary.** This unit is about stretching and springing back. Some things, like a rubber band or a spring, change shape when you pull or squash them. Then they go back to their old shape when you let go. Scientists call this being elastic. The harder you pull a spring, the further it stretches. But stretch anything too far and it stays stretched, or it snaps. A stretched or squashed thing also stores energy, ready to fling a stone, wind a watch or bounce a ball. Deep down, things spring back because their atoms are held together by tiny electric pulls that act like springs. The same idea reaches out to your own tendons and to earthquakes. It even reaches space itself, which stretches and squeezes as gravitational waves pass.

**Intro.** *Take a rubber band. Stretch it a little and let go. Now take a strip of plastic from an old carry bag. Stretch that a little and let go. One of them came back. One of them did not. Why?* In Kerala, rubber begins as a white milk that drips from the bark of rubber trees. Farmers dry it into sheets. Before selling a sheet, they pull one corner. A good sheet stretches and slides back flat. A sheet that stretches and stays stretched is old and close to tearing. Why does a thing that stops coming back matter so much? This unit is about stretching and coming back. Scientists call it being elastic.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces can make things move, stop, turn or change shape. *e.g.* You pull a rubber band. You squash a sponge. Both are forces.
- **Stretch**: To pull something so it gets longer. *e.g.* You stretch a rubber band round a bundle of pencils.
- **Squash**: To push on something so it gets shorter or flatter. Scientists also say compress. *e.g.* Pressing the top of a clicky pen squashes the spring inside.
- **Elastic**: A thing is elastic if it changes shape when pushed or pulled, and goes back to its old shape when you let go. *e.g.* A rubber band, a spring, a sponge and a bouncy ball are all elastic.
- **Spring**: A coil of springy metal wire. Pull it and it gets longer. Squash it and it gets shorter. Let go and it goes back. *e.g.* There is a small spring inside a clicky ballpoint pen.
- **Spring back**: To go back to the old shape after being stretched, squashed or bent. *e.g.* Press a sponge. Let go. It springs back.
- **Stretch-and-stay (plastic)**: A thing that changes shape and stays changed when you let go. Scientists call this plastic, which is a different meaning from plastic bags. *e.g.* Playdough, chewing gum and a strip from a thin carry bag stay stretched.
- **Snap (brittle)**: A thing that hardly bends at all, then suddenly breaks. Scientists call this brittle. *e.g.* A dry twig, a papad and a glass bangle snap.
- **Elastic limit**: The furthest you can stretch a thing and still have it spring back. Go past it and the thing stays stretched or breaks. *e.g.* Pull a pen spring very hard. It stays long and wobbly. It went past its elastic limit.
- **Pull-back force**: The force a stretched or squashed thing makes as it tries to go back to its old shape. Scientists call it the restoring force. *e.g.* A stretched rubber band pulls back on your fingers.
- **Stiff**: Hard to stretch or bend. A stiff spring needs a big pull to stretch a little. *e.g.* A cycle seat spring is much stiffer than a pen spring.
- **Stored energy**: Energy waiting inside something, ready to be used. A stretched or squashed spring stores energy. *e.g.* When you let go of a stretched catapult, the stored energy flings the paper ball.
- **Bounce**: When a thing hits the ground, squashes a little, then springs back and jumps up. *e.g.* A rubber ball bounces high. A ball of clay just goes splat.
- **Line graph**: A graph where dots are joined by a line. It shows how one thing changes as another thing changes. *e.g.* A line graph can show how long a rubber band gets as you add more coins.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* Your rubber band is made of countless atoms, all holding on to each other.
- **Bond**: The pull that holds two atoms together. A bond acts a bit like a tiny spring between them. *e.g.* In a steel spring, atoms are joined by bonds. Stretching the spring stretches the bonds a tiny bit.
- **Polymer**: A very long chain made of many small bits joined in a row. Rubber is made of polymers. *e.g.* Rubber is made of long, tangled chains, like a bowl of cooked noodles.
- **Tendon**: A strong, stretchy cord that joins a muscle to a bone. Tendons work like springs when you run and jump. *e.g.* The thick cord at the back of your ankle is your Achilles tendon.
- **Earthquake**: A sudden shaking of the ground. It happens when rocks that were slowly bent underground suddenly slip and spring back. *e.g.* The ground shakes, and the lights swing.
- **Gravitational wave**: A ripple in space itself. As it passes, it stretches space one way and squeezes it the other way, by an incredibly tiny amount. *e.g.* In 2015, scientists first caught a gravitational wave from two black holes that crashed together.
- **Vibrate**: To shake back and forth very fast. A plucked rubber band vibrates because it keeps springing back past its middle. *e.g.* Pluck a stretched rubber band. It blurs and hums.

## Explanations
### What elastic means · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing is elastic if it changes shape when a force pushes or pulls it, and goes back to its old shape when the force stops.

Pull a rubber band and it gets longer. Let go and it snaps back. Squeeze a sponge and it gets smaller. Let go and it puffs back up. Both are elastic. Being elastic is not only about stretching. Things can also be squashed, bent or twisted and then spring back. Here is a surprise. Steel, wood, stone and even glass are a little elastic too. They just need a much bigger force before you can see them change.
- e.g. A hair band stretches round your hair, then goes back to its small loop.
- e.g. A plastic ruler bends when you press its end, then springs straight.
- e.g. A cricket ball squashes a tiny bit when the bat hits it, then springs back round.
### Springs: stretch springs and squash springs · *defined*
**Definition:** A spring is a coil of springy metal wire. Some springs are made to be stretched, and some are made to be squashed. Both push or pull back when you let go.

A stretch spring has its coils packed tightly together. You pull its ends apart. A squash spring has gaps between its coils. You press its ends together. The spring in a clicky pen is a squash spring. The spring inside a spring balance at the vegetable shop is a stretch spring. Making the wire into a coil lets a short piece of stiff steel stretch a long way. Each bit of the wire twists only a little, but all the little twists add up.
- e.g. A clicky pen has a squash spring. It pushes the tip back in.
- e.g. A trampoline has stretch springs all round its edge.
- e.g. A clothes peg has a twisty spring that snaps its jaws shut.
### More pull, more stretch · *defined*
**Definition:** The harder you pull a spring, the longer it stretches. Double the pull and you get about double the stretch. This works until you get near the spring's limit.

Hang a cup from a rubber band and add coins, 5 at a time. Each time, measure the band. For many springs, each 5 coins add the same extra length. Joined up on a line graph, the dots make a straight line. Robert Hooke, an English scientist, wrote this rule down in 1678. He put it in Latin: 'as the stretch, so the force'. It is now called Hooke's law. Steel springs follow it closely. Rubber bands follow it only roughly. This rule is why a forcemeter, a tool that measures pulls, has evenly spaced marks. The unit on measuring forces looks at forcemeters more closely.
- e.g. One bag of shopping stretches the spring of a luggage scale a little. Two bags stretch it about twice as far.
- e.g. A thick rubber band needs a bigger pull than a thin one to stretch the same amount. Scientists say it is stiffer.
### The elastic limit: spring back, stay stretched, or snap · *defined*
**Definition:** Every elastic thing has a limit. Stretch it less than its limit and it springs back. Stretch it past its limit and it stays stretched, or it snaps.

Pull a pen spring gently and it springs back neat. Pull it hard and it stays long and loose. It has gone past its elastic limit. Scientists sort materials into three kinds. Elastic things spring back, like rubber. Stay-changed things, like playdough and chewing gum, keep their new shape. Scientists call this plastic. Snapping things, like a dry twig or a papad, hardly bend before they break. Scientists call this brittle. Many things do all three. A steel spoon springs back from a small bend. Push hard and it stays bent. Bend it back and forth many times and it can snap.
- e.g. A rubber band left round a newspaper for months goes loose and floppy.
- e.g. A green twig bends and springs back. A dry twig snaps.
- e.g. A strip cut from a thin carry bag stretches long and stays long.
### Stretching stores energy · *defined*
**Definition:** When you stretch, squash, bend or twist an elastic thing, the work you do is stored inside it. Let go, and the stored energy can make something move.

Energy is what it takes to make something move, warm up or change. When you pull back a catapult, your arm puts energy into the stretched rubber. When you let go, the rubber springs back and gives that energy to the stone or paper ball. The more you stretch, the more energy is stored, until the elastic limit. People have used stored elastic energy for thousands of years. Bows fling arrows. Wind-up watches and toys run on a tightly coiled spring. Some old mechanical clocks run for a whole week on one winding.
- e.g. A wind-up toy car runs when its twisted spring unwinds.
- e.g. A bow made of bamboo bends back, stores energy, and flings the arrow.
- e.g. A twisted rubber band can turn the propeller of a toy plane.
### Bouncing is a squash and a spring back · *defined*
**Definition:** When a ball hits the floor, it squashes for a very short moment. Then it springs back and pushes itself up. That is a bounce.

The squash is too quick to see. Slow-motion films show a tennis ball going flat on one side when it lands. Wet a ball and drop it on a dry floor, and the wet mark shows how big the squashed part was. No ball bounces all the way back up. Some of the energy turns into heat and sound each time. A rubber ball keeps more of its energy, so it bounces higher. A ball of clay is not elastic. It squashes and stays squashed, so it does not bounce at all. Tennis has a rule about bounce. A tennis ball dropped from 254 cm must bounce back up between 135 cm and 147 cm.
- e.g. A bouncy rubber ball bounces higher than a sponge ball.
- e.g. A trampoline's springs and stretchy mat squash down under you, then push you up.
### Deep down: atoms held by tiny springs · *defined*
**Definition:** Solid things are elastic because their atoms are held together by electric pulls called bonds. A bond acts like a tiny spring between two atoms.

Zoom far into a steel spring. You find atoms in neat rows. Each atom is pulled towards its neighbours by electric forces. Push atoms closer and they push apart. Pull them further apart and they pull together. So every bond is like a tiny spring. When you stretch a steel spring, you stretch countless bonds a tiny bit each. Let go, and the bonds pull the atoms back to their places. Past the elastic limit, rows of atoms slide over each other into new places. They do not slide back. That is why a stretched-too-far spring stays long. Atoms on their bonds are never perfectly still. They jiggle all the time, even at the coldest temperature possible.
- e.g. Scientists often build models of solids with little balls joined by springs.
- e.g. Heat makes atoms jiggle more on their bonds. That is why a metal rod gets very slightly longer when it is hot.
### Rubber is different: tangled chains · *defined*
**Definition:** Rubber is made of very long, tangled chains of atoms called polymers. Stretching straightens the chains. They spring back because jiggling atoms shake them back into a tangle.

Think of a bowl of cooked noodles, all tangled. Pull them and they line up straight. In rubber, the chains are joined to each other here and there, so they cannot slide apart. When you let go, the chains are shaken by the jiggling of heat. Tangled is far more likely than straight, so they wriggle back into a tangle. This is why rubber does odd things. It warms up when you stretch it fast. It cools when it springs back. A stretched rubber band even pulls harder when you heat it. John Gough, a blind scientist in England, found the warming in 1805. He held rubber against his lips while he stretched it. Much later, in 1859, James Joule measured it carefully.
- e.g. Stretch a thick rubber band quickly and hold it to your forehead. It feels warm.
- e.g. Cold rubber goes stiff, because its chains jiggle less.
### Springs in living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Living bodies are full of elastic parts. Tendons, skin, lungs and blood vessels stretch and spring back. Some animals store energy in springs to jump.

When you run, the tendon at the back of your ankle stretches as you land. It springs back as you push off, and gives back much of the energy. The arch of your foot works as a spring too. Kangaroos hop a long way using huge springy tendons in their legs. A flea jumps using a pad of a rubbery stuff called resilin. Torkel Weis-Fogh, a Danish scientist, discovered resilin in insect wings in 1960. The flea squashes the pad, locks it, then lets go all at once. Your skin springs back when pinched, because of a stretchy stuff called elastin. Your biggest blood vessel stretches with each heartbeat and springs back, which keeps blood flowing smoothly. Inside your muscles is titin, the biggest protein your body makes. It works like a tiny spring.
- e.g. Pinch the skin on the back of your hand and let go. It springs back.
- e.g. A spider's web stretches when an insect hits it, instead of breaking.
- e.g. The mantis shrimp, a sea animal, punches with a spring in its arm. Sheila Patek and her team filmed it in 2004.
### Bend, don't break: why springy things survive · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing that can bend and spring back can take a sudden heavy push without breaking. A stiff, brittle thing may snap instead.

When a heavy load suddenly lands on something, its energy has to go somewhere. A springy thing bends, stores the energy for a moment, then gives it back. A stiff, brittle thing cannot bend, so the force builds up until it cracks. That is why bamboo bends in a storm instead of snapping. A green mango branch bends under a swing and springs back. Bent past its limit, it stays bent, and that is the warning that it may break. Tall buildings are designed to sway a little in strong winds, for the same reason.
- e.g. A coconut palm bends far over in a cyclone and often stands up again.
- e.g. A car's springs bend over a bump, so the car does not crack.
### The Earth is springy: earthquakes and a ringing planet · *defined*
**Definition:** Rock is elastic too. Deep underground, huge blocks of rock are slowly bent by moving land. When they suddenly slip, they spring back, and the ground shakes. That is an earthquake.

The Earth's outer layer is broken into giant pieces that move slowly, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Where two pieces rub, the rock gets stuck and slowly bends, like a ruler pressed at one end. For years nothing happens. Then the rock slips and springs back in seconds. Harry Fielding Reid, an American scientist, explained this in 1910. He studied the great San Francisco earthquake of 1906. The shaking travels through the Earth as waves, and those waves can travel only because rock is elastic. After a giant earthquake in Chile in 1960, instruments found the whole Earth vibrating slowly, like a struck bell, for days.
- e.g. The Himalayas are still being pushed up, which is why north India has earthquakes.
- e.g. Bend a plastic ruler slowly until it slips off your finger. It springs back with a twang, like rock in an earthquake.
### Space itself stretches: gravitational waves · *defined*
**Definition:** Space can stretch and squeeze. When huge things like black holes crash together, they send out ripples in space called gravitational waves.

Albert Einstein predicted these ripples in 1916. As a wave passes, space stretches one way and squeezes the other way, then back again. On 14 September 2015, two giant instruments in the United States, called LIGO, caught one for the first time. It came from two black holes that crashed together more than a billion years ago. The wave stretched LIGO's 4-kilometre-long arms by less than a thousandth of the width of a proton, a speck inside an atom. More than a thousand scientists worked on it, including teams from India. India is now building its own detector, called LIGO-India, in Maharashtra. The whole universe is stretching too. Galaxies are slowly getting further apart, because the space between them is growing.
- e.g. Draw dots on a wide rubber band and stretch it. Every dot moves away from every other dot, a little like galaxies in our growing universe.
- e.g. A gravitational wave passing through you right now would stretch you a tiny bit taller and thinner, then shorter and wider.
### What is space, that it can stretch? · *debated*
We know space can stretch, squeeze and ripple. We have measured it. But what is space made of, that it can do this? Is it a kind of stuff, like the rubber of a rubber band? Or is stretching just a way of describing distances between things changing? Nobody knows for sure. This is one of the deepest open questions in physics.
- e.g. A rubber sheet with a heavy ball on it is often used to picture bent space. It is a helpful picture, but space is not really a rubber sheet.
- **Einstein's theory of gravity, called general relativity:** Space and time together bend, stretch and ripple. The theory describes this very well. It does not say what space is made of.
- **Some physicists working on string theory:** Everything might be made of incredibly tiny vibrating strings, a little like plucked rubber bands. Space itself might come from them. Nobody has found any evidence yet that this is true.
- **Other physicists, working on ideas like loop quantum gravity:** Space might be made of tiny pieces, like the threads of a woven net, far too small to ever see. This idea also has no evidence yet.

## Mostly agreed
- Elastic things change shape when pushed or pulled, and go back when the force stops. *How we know:* Anyone can test this with rubber bands, springs and sponges. Engineers test steel, wood and concrete the same way, with big machines that stretch and squash them.
- For many springs, double the pull gives about double the stretch, up to a limit. *How we know:* Robert Hooke tested springs and wires and published the rule in 1678. Forcemeters and spring balances work because of it. Children repeat this test with coins and rubber bands.
- Every elastic thing has a limit. Past it, the thing stays stretched or breaks. *How we know:* Stretch a pen spring hard and it stays long. Engineers measure the limit of every material used in bridges and buildings.
- A stretched or squashed elastic thing stores energy, which it gives back when it springs back. *How we know:* Catapults, bows, wind-up clocks and bouncing balls all use it. Scientists measure the energy that goes in and comes out.
- Earthquakes happen when bent rock underground suddenly slips and springs back. *How we know:* Scientists measured how the ground had bent before and after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. Today they measure the slow bending with satellites.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Stretchy things are weak, and strong things are stiff.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: A rubber band breaks easily, while a steel rod does not stretch at all that we can see.  
  Against: Spider silk is very stretchy and, weight for weight, stronger than steel wire. Living roots and bamboo survive storms because they bend. Being able to stretch can make a thing tougher, because it soaks up a sudden push instead of cracking.
- **Robert Hooke alone discovered the rule for springs, and invented the spring for watches.** (Many physics books; *debated*)  
  For: Hooke published the spring rule in 1678. He also worked on springs to control watches in the 1660s.  
  Against: Christiaan Huygens, a Dutch scientist, built a watch with a spiral spring in 1675. The two men argued bitterly about who was first. Hooke had hidden his rule in a code of jumbled letters in 1676, so that nobody could steal it before he was ready. Historians still disagree about the watch spring. The spring rule is fairly given to Hooke.
- **Isaac Newton had the only portrait of Robert Hooke destroyed.** (A story often told about Hooke and Newton, who were rivals; *minority*)  
  For: No portrait of Hooke survives from his lifetime. Newton disliked Hooke, and he led the Royal Society, the science club where a portrait might have hung.  
  Against: Nobody has found any evidence that Newton destroyed a portrait. It may simply have been lost. In the 2000s an artist, Rita Greer, painted new portraits of Hooke from written descriptions of him.
- **Rubber was invented in Europe or America, by people like Charles Goodyear.** (Many older books; *rejected*)  
  For: Charles Goodyear found in 1839 how to make rubber tough with heat and sulphur. That made car tyres and rubber bands possible.  
  Against: About 3,500 years ago, people in Mexico and Central America were already making bouncy rubber balls. In 1999, Dorothy Hosler and her team at MIT showed how. They mixed the milky sap of the rubber tree with juice from a morning glory vine. Different mixtures made rubber that bounced more, or rubber that was tougher.
- **Cold would not matter to the rubber seals on the space shuttle Challenger.** (Managers at NASA and at the company that made the rockets, in January 1986; *rejected*)  
  For: The seals had worked on earlier flights. The managers were under pressure to launch on time.  
  Against: An engineer called Roger Boisjoly warned the night before that cold rubber goes stiff and springs back too slowly. The launch went ahead on a freezing morning. A seal failed, and the shuttle broke apart. The seven astronauts on board died. Later, the scientist Richard Feynman dipped a piece of the seal rubber in iced water during the inquiry. It stayed squashed instead of springing back.
- **A branch that is bending is about to break.** (A common worry; *rejected*)  
  For: Branches do break when they are bent too far.  
  Against: Bending by itself is safe. A green branch bends in every breeze and springs back. The warning sign is a branch that bends and does not come all the way back. That means it has passed its elastic limit. Engineers watch for this same sign in cranes and bridges.

## Story: The Tired Branch
*Rubber tapping in Kerala, smoked rubber sheets and the Onam swing, the oonjal, are real. Ammu, her family, Biju and the mango tree are made up. The warning sign in the story is real physics: a branch or spring that no longer springs all the way back has gone past its elastic limit, and may soon break.*

In a village near Kottayam, in Kerala, the rubber trees stand in long straight rows, like children lined up for school. Every morning before the sun was up, Ammu's mother walked between them with a lamp on her forehead and a curved knife in her hand. On each tree she shaved a thin spiral strip of bark. Out of the cut crept a white, slow milk, which ran down the spiral into a coconut-shell cup. Latex, her mother called it. Rubber before it knows it is rubber.

Ammu was ten. Her job came after school. She carried the full cups home, poured the latex into flat trays and helped her mother squeeze it into sheets through the iron rollers in the shed. Then the sheets were hung in the smoke above the fire to dry, brown and shining, like enormous dosas.

Before any sheet was sold, her mother tested it. She took one corner between her finger and thumb and pulled. A good sheet stretched, and then, when she let go, slid back flat as if nothing had happened. Once Ammu found an old sheet that had been forgotten in the sun for weeks. When her mother pulled it, it stretched and stayed stretched, a tired little bulge. When she pulled again, it tore.

'See,' said her mother. 'When it stops coming back, it is close to breaking.'

Ammu said yes, Amma, the way you say yes to things you are not really listening to. It was August, and Onam was coming, and Onam meant the swing.

Every year the children of the lane hung an oonjal, a festival swing, from the big mango tree at the end of the path. It was a plank seat on two long ropes, decorated with marigolds and mango leaves. This year Biju, who was twelve and the leader of everything, had a plan. 'The low branch is for babies,' he said. 'We'll use the high one. The swing will go up past the roof of the shed.'

The high branch was thinner. It stuck out over the path like an arm reaching for something. Biju climbed up and knotted the ropes round it, and everyone cheered.

On the first day, the swing was glorious. When Biju swung, the branch bent down under him like a fishing rod, and lifted him up again, so that every swing had an extra bounce at the bottom. The children shrieked. They queued all afternoon. Ammu had three turns. At the top of each one she could see over the shed and across the whole green sea of rubber trees, and her stomach floated up into her throat.

On the second day, the bigger boys started swinging two at a time, standing up, pumping as hard as they could. 'Higher!' they yelled. 'Make it fly!'

Ammu was waiting her turn when she noticed something. When the two boys jumped off, the swing hung still. And the branch did not go all the way back up.

She looked again. In the morning, the swing's plank had hung level with her chin. Now it hung level with her shoulder. The branch had bent and stayed bent, just a little, the way the old rubber sheet had stretched and stayed stretched.

She went cold all over, even in the August heat.

'Biju,' she said. 'The branch is tired.'

Biju laughed. Everyone laughed. 'Branches don't get tired,' he said. 'It bounced me all day yesterday.'

'It bounced back yesterday,' said Ammu. 'It isn't bouncing back now. Look at the seat. It's lower.'

'You're scared because you're small,' said one of the boys, and the others made chicken noises. Ammu's face went hot. She wanted to be quiet. Being quiet would have been much easier. But she thought of the old sheet, the tired bulge, and the sound it made when it tore.

Her little sister Kunju was next in the queue, holding up her arms to be lifted on.

'No,' said Ammu, and she pulled Kunju back by her frock, so hard that Kunju began to cry.

'Fine,' said Biju. 'Then I'll show you.' He climbed onto the plank, stood up, and pumped once, twice, three times, swinging higher than the shed roof.

On the fourth swing there was a sound like a coconut splitting.

The branch did not snap all at once. It cracked, and sagged, and Biju, at the bottom of the swing, felt the ropes go soft in his hands. He jumped. He landed on his knees in the mud of the path. A second later the whole branch, ropes, marigolds and all, came down with a heavy, leafy crash exactly where Kunju would have been sitting.

Nobody said anything for a long time. A crow, offended, flew off into the rubber trees.

Biju stood up slowly. His knees were muddy and his face was white. He looked at the broken branch, with its pale, splintered end. Then he looked at Ammu.

'How did you know?' he said.

Ammu told him about the sheet. She showed him how the swing had hung lower and lower. 'When something stops coming back,' she said, 'it's near the end.' She didn't say 'I told you.' She didn't need to.

That evening, Ammu's mother walked down the lane and looked at the branch on the ground. She looked at the high stump, and at the low, thick branch that the swing had always hung from before. She did not scold anyone. She only touched Ammu's head on her way home, very lightly.

The next morning, the children hung the swing again, on the low branch. It was not as exciting. It did not go higher than the shed. But after every turn, Ammu watched the seat as the rider jumped off, and every time, the branch lifted it back up to exactly the height of her chin.

On Onam day, Biju pushed Kunju on the swing himself, gently, for nearly an hour.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a branch, like a rubber band or a spring, is elastic. Pushed a little, it bends and springs all the way back. Pushed past its elastic limit, it bends and stays bent. That small bend that does not come back is the warning. The thing has started to change inside, and it is close to breaking. Ammu spots this because she has seen the same thing in an old rubber sheet. Engineers watch for this sign in bridges, cranes and aeroplane wings. The story also asks how hard it is to speak up when others laugh, and to listen when someone warns us.

- Ask: Why did the branch break on the second day, but not on the first?
- Ask: Why was it hard for Ammu to speak up? What helped her?

### e1 · Thick Band, Thin Band: the coin stretch test (30 min)
- **question:** How much longer does a rubber band get for every 5 coins? Does a thick band stretch as much as a thin one?

- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only the number of coins. Use the same cup, the same kind of coins and the same ruler. Measure the band the same way every time, from the pencil to the top of the cup. Test the thick band in exactly the same way afterwards.
- **safety:** Stand to the side, not over the cup, in case the band slips or snaps. Use coins, not glass marbles, in the cup.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a line graph for the thin band. Put coins along the bottom and length up the side. Join the dots.
- **results table:** Coins in the cup | Thin band length (cm) | Thick band length (cm)

12. (mcq) Robert Hooke hid his spring rule in a code of jumbled letters in 1676. Why might he have done this?  
   Options: So nobody could claim it before he was ready to explain it / He forgot how to spell / It was a joke / He did not believe it  
   **Answer:** So nobody could claim it before he was ready to explain it. *Why:* Scientists sometimes did this to prove later that they had the idea first. Picking 'a joke' is a fair guess for something so odd. But it was a serious way to protect his claim.
13. (write) Spider silk is very stretchy, and weight for weight stronger than steel wire. Does that change the idea that stretchy things are weak? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Yes. Spider silk shows that a thing can be both stretchy and strong. Stretching lets it soak up a fly hitting the web without snapping.. *Why:* Being able to stretch can make a material tougher, because it soaks up sudden pushes.
14. (write) In the bungee doll challenge, 2 bands let the doll fall 30 cm, 3 bands 40 cm and 4 bands 50 cm. Describe the pattern. Then predict 8 bands.  
   **Answer:** Each extra band adds 10 cm. 4 bands is 50 cm, so 8 bands is 4 more bands, which is 40 cm more. 50 + 40 = 90 cm.. *Why:* The pattern goes up by the same amount each time, like the spring rule. Real rubber bands may curve a little near their limit.
15. (write) Is it better to be stiff or to bend? Give one example where stiff is better and one where bending is better.  
   **Answer:** A balanced answer. For example: a ruler for drawing lines should be stiff. A bridge in a storm, or a tree, is better if it can bend and spring back.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. What counts is a good example for each side.
16. (draw) Draw a model of atoms in a spring, as balls joined by tiny springs. Draw it again when the spring is stretched.  
   **Answer:** Two drawings. In the first, balls are evenly spaced with short springs between them. In the second, the balls are a little further apart and the springs are longer.. *Why:* Stretching a spring stretches the bonds between its atoms a tiny bit each.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does elastic mean?  
   Options: It springs back to its shape when you let go / It is soft / It is made of plastic / It breaks easily  
   **Answer:** It springs back to its shape when you let go. *Why:* Elastic things change shape and then spring back. Picking 'soft' is common, but a steel spring is hard and elastic.
2. (mcq) Which of these is elastic?  
   Options: A sponge / Playdough / A dry twig  
   **Answer:** A sponge. *Why:* A sponge springs back. Playdough stays changed. A dry twig snaps. Picking playdough often means changing shape was mixed up with springing back.
3. (mcq) In the coin stretch test, what should you change to make it a fair test?  
   Options: Only the number of coins / The coins and the cup / Everything  
   **Answer:** Only the number of coins. *Why:* Change only one thing at a time. Then you know what caused the change. Picking 'coins and cup' means testing two things at once.
4. (mcq) Look at the line graph. With 0 coins the band was 10 cm, and each 5 coins added 1 cm. How long was it with 25 coins?  
   Options: 15 cm / 25 cm / 11 cm  
   **Answer:** 15 cm. *Why:* 25 coins is 5 lots of 5 coins. That adds 5 cm. 10 + 5 = 15 cm. Picking 25 cm means the coins were read as centimetres.
5. (mcq) A spring is pulled far past its elastic limit. When you let go, what happens?  
   Options: It stays stretched, or it snaps / It springs back perfectly / It gets shorter than before  
   **Answer:** It stays stretched, or it snaps. *Why:* Past the limit, a spring cannot get back. Picking 'perfectly' means elastic was thought to have no limit.
6. (mcq) Which of these snaps rather than bending or stretching?  
   Options: A papad / A rubber band / Chewing gum  
   **Answer:** A papad. *Why:* A papad is brittle. It hardly bends before it snaps. Picking chewing gum mixes up stays-changed with snapping.
7. (mcq) Why does a stretched catapult fling a paper ball?  
   Options: The stretched band stores energy and gives it back / The ball is light / The air pushes it  
   **Answer:** The stretched band stores energy and gives it back. *Why:* Stretching stores energy. Letting go turns it into movement. Picking 'the ball is light' is true but is not what flings it.
8. (mcq) A ball bounces. What happens when it hits the floor?  
   Options: It squashes for a moment, then springs back / It stays perfectly round / The floor throws it up  
   **Answer:** It squashes for a moment, then springs back. *Why:* A bounce is a squash and a spring back. Picking 'stays round' is natural, because the squash is too fast to see.
9. (mcq) Which spring is in a clicky pen?  
   Options: A squash spring that pushes the tip back in / A stretch spring / There is no spring  
   **Answer:** A squash spring that pushes the tip back in. *Why:* Pressing the pen squashes its spring. It pushes the tip back in when you click again.
10. (mcq) Which part of your body works like a spring when you run?  
   Options: Your Achilles tendon / Your eyebrows / Your fingernails  
   **Answer:** Your Achilles tendon. *Why:* The tendon at the back of your ankle stretches as you land and springs back as you push off.
11. (mcq) Deep down, why do solid things spring back?  
   Options: Atoms are held by electric pulls that act like tiny springs / They have air inside / Gravity pulls them back  
   **Answer:** Atoms are held by electric pulls that act like tiny springs. *Why:* Bonds between atoms act like tiny springs. Picking gravity misses that springs work in every direction.
12. (mcq) Why does a rubber band warm up when you stretch it fast?  
   Options: Its tangled chains are pulled straight, and that gives out heat / Your hand warms it / It rubs on the air  
   **Answer:** Its tangled chains are pulled straight, and that gives out heat. *Why:* This is rubber's special secret, found by John Gough in 1805. Picking 'your hand' is a fair guess, but it warms even if you only hold the ends.
13. (mcq) What happens in an earthquake?  
   Options: Bent rock underground suddenly slips and springs back / The Earth gets bigger / Heavy buildings sink  
   **Answer:** Bent rock underground suddenly slips and springs back. *Why:* Rock bends slowly for years, then slips. Picking 'buildings sink' mixes up the cause with the damage.
14. (mcq) What does a gravitational wave do?  
   Options: It stretches and squeezes space itself, by a tiny amount / It makes the sea wavy / It is a wave of light  
   **Answer:** It stretches and squeezes space itself, by a tiny amount. *Why:* Gravitational waves are ripples in space. Picking 'the sea' mixes them up with tides or ocean waves.
15. (mcq) Do scientists know what space is made of?  
   Options: No, it is still an open question / Yes, it is made of rubber / Yes, it is made of air  
   **Answer:** No, it is still an open question. *Why:* We can measure space stretching, but not what it is made of. Picking 'rubber' takes the rubber-sheet picture too literally.

## Applications
- Spring balances at vegetable shops, luggage scales and forcemeters measure weight by how far a spring stretches.
- Clicky pens, staplers, clothes pegs, door latches and mousetraps use small springs.
- Mattresses, sofas, cycle seats and charpais are springy so they are comfortable.
- Cars, buses, autos and trains have big springs and shock absorbers to soften bumps.
- Wind-up watches and clocks run on a coiled spring. HMT made wind-up watches in Bengaluru for decades.
- Catapults, bows and slingshots store energy in stretched wood and rubber.
- Rubber bands, hair bands and the elastic in clothes hold things snugly.
- Balls for cricket, football and tennis are tested for how well they bounce.
- Trampolines and diving boards bend and spring back to throw people up.
- Bungee cords are bundles of rubber strands that stretch to stop a fall gently.
- Musical instruments like the veena, sitar, violin and tabla use stretched strings and skins.
- Earthquake-safe buildings are designed to sway and spring back instead of cracking.
- Doctors use springy wires to straighten teeth and springy stents to hold blood vessels open.
- Scientists copy spider silk and resilin to try to make new, tougher materials.
- LIGO and the future LIGO-India use the stretching of space to listen to black holes crashing far away.

## Open questions
- **What is space made of, that it can stretch and ripple?** We can measure space stretching, but nobody knows what it is made of. String theory and loop quantum gravity are ideas, with no evidence yet.
- **Exactly how do spiders make silk that is so strong and so stretchy, and can we make it in factories?** Scientists know a lot about the proteins in silk. But copying how the spider spins it, at a large scale, is still very hard.
- **Can we predict when bent rock will slip and cause an earthquake?** Scientists can measure rock slowly bending. But they still cannot say when it will slip.
- **Why do springs and rubber get 'tired' and crack after being stretched many times?** This is called fatigue. Engineers test it carefully, but exactly when a crack will start is still hard to predict.
- **How exactly does titin, the giant spring protein in our muscles, work?** Scientists know titin acts like a spring, but how all its parts work together is still being studied.
- **Can we always see the warning signs before something breaks?** A thing that stays bent is a warning. But tiny cracks can grow inside without showing. Engineers use sound waves and X-rays to look, and still cannot catch everything.
- **Could there be a perfectly elastic material that gives back all of its energy?** Every real material turns a little energy into heat each time it springs back. Resilin comes close. Nobody knows how close anything can get.

## Connected maths
- In the coin stretch test, the thin band was 10 cm long with no coins. Each 5 coins made it 1 cm longer. How long will it be with 30 coins? Use the line graph to help. **16 cm.**. 30 coins is 6 lots of 5 coins, because 30 divided by 5 is 6. Each lot adds 1 cm, so the band grows 6 cm. 10 + 6 = 16 cm. On the graph, the line keeps going up 1 cm for every 5 coins. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Look at the ruler beside the rubber band in the picture. The big numbers are centimetres. Each small mark is half a centimetre. The bottom of the band is at the small mark just after 12. How long is the band? **12 and a half centimetres.**. The bottom of the band is past 12 but before 13. It is on the small mark halfway between them. Each small mark is half a centimetre. So the band is 12 and a half centimetres long. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- In the Bungee Doll challenge, 2 bands let the doll fall 30 cm, 3 bands 40 cm and 4 bands 50 cm. The table is 75 cm tall. What is the most bands you can use so the doll's lowest point stays above the floor? **6 bands. The doll falls 70 cm, which is 5 cm above the floor. 7 bands would be 80 cm, which hits the floor.**. Each extra band adds 10 cm. 5 bands make 60 cm. 6 bands make 70 cm. 7 bands make 80 cm. The table is 75 cm tall. 70 cm is less than 75 cm, so 6 bands is safe. 80 cm is more than 75 cm, so 7 bands would hit the floor. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- In the Bounce Test, these balls were dropped from 100 cm. Rubber ball 80 cm, tennis ball 55 cm, sponge ball 20 cm, plastic ball 40 cm. Draw a bar chart. Then answer: how much higher did the rubber ball bounce than the sponge ball? **60 cm higher. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each ball, as tall as its bounce. The rubber ball bounced 80 cm. The sponge ball bounced 20 cm. 80 take away 20 is 60. So the rubber ball bounced 60 cm higher. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Your rubber band car went 60 cm with 5 winds. Suppose every 5 winds add about the same distance. About how far would it go with 15 winds? Then compare with the real result of 180 cm. **About 180 cm. That matches the real result.**. 15 winds is 3 lots of 5 winds. Each lot gives about 60 cm. 3 × 60 = 180 cm. The real car went 180 cm, so the guess was right. With many more winds, the band would reach its limit, and the pattern would stop working. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
