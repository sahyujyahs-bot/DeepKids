# Reading the Moon: where day meets night, why half Moons show mountains, and how to predict any phase
`sci.sky.moon-phases.L4`

**Summary.** The full Moon is the brightest night of the month, yet it is the worst night to look for mountains and craters. A half Moon, which looks dimmer, shows them best. The reason is where the light comes from. The line where the Moon's day meets its night is where shadows are longest, and shadows are what show shape. Your child lights a dough Moon from three directions and counts craters, finds out how much later the Moon rises each night, and uses a bindi on a window to measure how wide the Moon is. They also learn a rule to predict any phase from the Moon's age, see why tides follow the Moon, and find out how India's Chandrayaan-3 was timed to land at lunar dawn.

**Intro.** *You have a pair of binoculars and one night to look at the Moon's mountains and craters. Do you pick the full Moon, which is the brightest, or a half Moon, which looks dimmer? Most people pick the full Moon. Galileo found out in 1609 that the half Moon is the better choice.* The edge between the Moon's lit part and its dark part is called the terminator. Along it, the Sun is low over the Moon's land, like early morning, and every bump throws a long shadow. At full Moon the Sun shines straight down on the Moon from behind us, and there are almost no shadows at all. In this unit you test this with a dough Moon and a torch. You also learn to predict the Moon's phase, find out when it rises, and measure how wide it is with a bindi.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain what the terminator is and why shadows along it show the Moon's mountains and craters.
1. I can explain why a full Moon is a poor night for seeing the Moon's surface, and a half Moon is a good night.
2. I can use the Moon's age to say its phase, and to say roughly when it rises and sets.
3. I can explain why the Moon's day is about 14 Earth days of sunlight and 14 of darkness, and what that means for a lander such as Chandrayaan-3.
4. I can measure how many Moon-widths away the Moon is, using a small disc on a window, and use the answer to work out how wide the Moon is.
5. I can explain why the Moon makes tides, why there are two high tides each day, and why the biggest tides come at new Moon and full Moon.
6. I can explain why a crescent looks different in different places, and tell how Galileo found mountains on the Moon.

## Terms
- **Terminator**: The line between the lit part of the Moon and its dark part. On the Moon it is the place where it is sunrise or sunset. *e.g.* On a half Moon the terminator is a straight line down the middle of the Moon.
- **Lunar day**: One day and night on the Moon, from one sunrise to the next. It lasts about 29 and a half Earth days. *e.g.* Near the Moon's equator a place has about two weeks of sunlight and then about two weeks of darkness.
- **Lunar dawn**: Sunrise on the Moon, when the terminator passes a place and the Sun first comes up over its horizon. *e.g.* Chandrayaan-3 landed at lunar dawn, with the Sun low in the sky.
- **Moon's age**: The number of days since the last new Moon. *e.g.* A Moon that is 7 days old is a half Moon, lit on the right when seen from India.
- **Synodic month**: The time from one new Moon to the next. It is about 29 and a half days. *e.g.* The Indian calendar's lunar month is a synodic month, about 29 or 30 days.
- **Slanting light**: Light that comes in at a low angle, nearly sideways, so that every bump throws a long shadow. *e.g.* The Sun late in the afternoon gives slanting light, and shadows grow long.
- **Highlands**: The bright, rough, high ground of the Moon. It is covered in craters and is older than the dark plains. *e.g.* The bright parts of the Moon that we see are mostly highlands.
- **Maria**: The dark, flat plains on the Moon. Maria is the Latin word for seas. They are lava that flooded out long ago, and not water. One plain is a mare. *e.g.* The dark patches that people see as a hare or a face are maria.
- **Crater**: A round bowl-shaped hole in the Moon's ground, made when a rock from space hit it. *e.g.* A crater has a raised rim and may have a peak in its middle.
- **Rule of thumb**: A simple rule that gives a good guess, but is not exact. *e.g.* The rule that the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day is a rule of thumb.
- **Tide**: The slow rise and fall of the sea each day, caused mainly by the Moon's pull. *e.g.* On a beach in Chennai, the water edge moves in and out twice a day.
- **High tide and low tide**: High tide is when the sea is at its highest on the shore. Low tide is when it is at its lowest. *e.g.* A fishing boat sits on wet sand at low tide and floats at high tide.
- **Tidal bulge**: A heap of water raised on the sea by the Moon's pull. The Earth has two bulges at a time, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. *e.g.* As the Earth turns under the bulges, a coast has two high tides each day.
- **Spring tide**: A tide with an extra-high high tide and an extra-low low tide. It comes at new Moon and full Moon. *e.g.* Spring tides have nothing to do with the spring season. The name means 'spring up'.
- **Neap tide**: A tide with a small difference between high and low. It comes at the half Moons. *e.g.* At neap tide a beach hardly seems to move.
- **Libration**: A slow nodding and rocking of the Moon that lets us see a little round its edges, so over time we see more than half of its surface. *e.g.* Because of libration, we can see about 59 out of every 100 parts of the Moon's surface.
- **Reflectivity**: How much of the light falling on a thing it sends back. The Moon sends back only about 12 parts in every 100. *e.g.* The Moon is about as dark as a tarred road, yet it looks bright against the night.
- **Lander**: A spacecraft built to touch down gently on another world. *e.g.* Chandrayaan-3's lander was called Vikram.
- **Width in the sky**: How wide something looks, measured as a part of the whole sky. The Moon is about half a degree wide, about the width of a small bindi held at arm's length. *e.g.* The Moon is about 110 of its own widths away from us.

## Explanations
### The terminator: where the Moon's day meets its night · *defined*
**Definition:** The terminator is the line between the Moon's lit part and its dark part. For a place on the Moon, it is the moment of sunrise or sunset.

The Sun lights half of the Moon at all times. The line between the lit half and the dark half goes round the Moon like a slow wave. As the Moon moves through its phases, the terminator seems to sweep across its face. For a growing Moon, the terminator is moving over land that is just having its sunrise. For a shrinking Moon, it is crossing land that is having its sunset. On the real Moon, at the terminator, the Sun is low in the sky, just above the horizon. The land there is like the Earth in the first light of morning. The Sun shines almost along the ground, so a long shadow stretches behind every rock. Beyond the terminator is the dark side of the Moon's night. A place on the near side takes about two weeks to go from sunrise to sunset.
- e.g. At first quarter, the half Moon, the terminator is a straight line down the middle. The land on it is having its sunrise.
- e.g. A crescent Moon is mostly terminator, with the Sun low over most of the lit part.
### Long shadows show shape: why half Moons show mountains and full Moons hide them · *defined*
**Definition:** We see the shape of rough ground by its shadows. Slanting light makes long shadows and shows every bump. Light from straight behind us makes almost no shadows, and everything looks flat.

A face lit by a torch held under the chin looks full of bumps and hollows. A face lit by a torch held right beside the camera looks flat. The Moon is the same. At full Moon, the Sun is behind the Earth, and the sunlight falls on the Moon from the same direction as we look. We see almost no shadows, so craters and mountains look flat and the Moon looks like a bright, plain disc. At first quarter and last quarter, the Sun lights the Moon from the side. Along the terminator, every crater rim and mountain makes a long shadow, and the tops of peaks catch the light. That is the best time to see the Moon's craters through binoculars. A full Moon is still lovely, and it shows the bright rays from young craters, but it hides shape. You can find the height of a mountain from the length of its shadow.
- e.g. A farmer's field looks flat at noon but every furrow shows in the low light of early morning. People who search for old buried walls also look for them in the low light of dawn or dusk.
- e.g. A photographer takes pictures of rock carvings in the early morning or evening, when shadows pick out the carving.
### What the Moon's face is made of: highlands, seas and craters · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon's face has bright highlands, dark flat plains called maria, and craters everywhere. The craters were made by rocks from space.

The bright parts of the Moon are rough, high ground called highlands. They are thick with craters and are the oldest parts of the surface. The dark patches are huge plains called maria, the Latin word for seas. They are not water. They are lava that flooded out long ago and cooled into dark, flat rock. Craters have a raised rim and sometimes a peak in the middle. They were made when rocks from space hit the surface at very high speed. The Earth has made craters too, but wind, rain, rivers and moving ground wore most of them away. The Moon has no air and no water, so its craters stay for billions of years. Because the sunlit near side has more dark plains than the far side, the near side looks different. Why the two sides differ is still debated.
- e.g. The 'hare' that people in India and many other places see in the Moon is made by the dark plains.
- e.g. Some craters have bright streaks, called rays, spreading out from them. They are splashes of fresh, bright rock from a young crater.
### The Moon's age and the 29 and a half day month · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon's age is the number of days since the last new Moon. It tells you the phase.

A new Moon has an age of 0. A thin crescent is 2 or 3 days old. A half Moon, the first quarter, is about 7 days old. A full Moon is about 14 or 15 days old. The last quarter is about 22 days old. After about 29 and a half days, the Moon is new again. So if you know the date of the last new Moon, you can work out the age on any day and so the phase. The Indian calendar does the same with its tithis. Shukla Prathama, the first tithi of the bright half, is near the start, and Ashtami in the bright half falls about when the half Moon shows. Purnima is the full Moon, and Amavasya the new Moon. A month of 29 and a half days means calendars give months of 29 or 30 days. The actual time between new Moons varies by several hours, so the age is a guide and not exact.
- e.g. If the new Moon was on the 2nd of the month, the Moon will be a half Moon around the 9th and full around the 16th or 17th.
- e.g. Hindu festival dates are set by the tithi, which tracks the Moon's age.
### When does each phase rise and set? A rule you can use · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day. So a rough rule is that it sets, or rises, about as many times 50 minutes after the Sun as its age in days.

The Moon moves round the Earth in the same direction as the Earth spins, and it covers about 13 degrees a day. The Earth must turn a bit extra each day to catch up, about 50 minutes. This gives a rule of thumb. Moonrise is about the same number of times 50 minutes after sunrise as its age in days. For a Moon aged 7 days, that is 7 times 50 minutes, which is about 6 hours. So a half Moon rises about noon. At sunset the half Moon is high in the sky and it sets around midnight. A full Moon, aged about 15 days, rises about 12 hours after sunrise, which is near sunset. After that, the Moon rises after dark and is up later each night. The rule is only a guide. The actual gap between moonrises changes from night to night, and it can be as small as about half an hour or as big as over an hour. It also depends on where you live and the season.
- e.g. A 3-day-old crescent sets about 2 and a half hours after the Sun. You must look soon after sunset.
- e.g. A 22-day-old half Moon rises at about midnight and is in the sky in the morning.
### The Moon's day: two weeks of sunlight and two weeks of night · *defined*
**Definition:** One day and night on the Moon lasts about 29 and a half Earth days. A place near the Moon's equator has about 14 days of sunlight, then about 14 days of night.

The Moon turns once on its axis in about 27 days, and the Sun seems to cross its sky very slowly. A place on the Moon has about two weeks of sunshine and then two weeks of darkness. With no air to hold heat in, the temperature swings are huge. Near the equator, the ground can reach about 120 degrees Celsius at midday and fall to about minus 170 in the night. So a spacecraft that has to work only on solar power may need to finish its work in about 14 days. India's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole region on 23 August 2023, when the Sun was just coming up over the site. This gave its lander and rover about one lunar day of sunlight. ISRO reported that they went to sleep as the lunar night began in early September, and that they did not wake up again.
- e.g. On the near side, the full Moon is at lunar noon, and the new Moon is at lunar midnight.
- e.g. A place near the Moon's pole has the Sun low in the sky all day, with long shadows, so some crater floors there never see the Sun.
### The same Moon looks different from different places · *defined*
**Definition:** Everyone on the night side of the Earth sees the same phase at the same time. But how it is tilted and which side is lit depends on where you stand.

The shape of the Moon's lit part depends on where the Sun is, and that is the same for everyone. The lit side faces the Sun. From India, which is in the northern half of the world, a growing Moon is lit on the right. For people in Australia or South Africa, in the southern half, the Moon looks upside down, so a growing Moon is lit on the left. The tilt also changes with how far north or south you are. Near the equator, the lit side of a thin crescent often points upwards, so the crescent looks like a bowl or a boat. Farther from the equator, it leans over. Calendars that start a month by sighting the Moon, such as the Islamic calendar, look for the first thin crescent low in the western sky after sunset.
- e.g. Thin crescents in Kochi or Kanyakumari often look like a bowl, and in northern Europe they tend to lean.
- e.g. A photograph of the half Moon taken in South Africa looks upside down compared with one taken in India.
### How big is the Moon and how far away? A bindi will tell you · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon is about 110 of its own widths away from us. Its width is about 3,500 kilometres, and it is about 384,000 kilometres away.

If you hold a small disc at the right distance from your eye, it just covers the Moon. You find that the distance to the disc is about 110 times the disc's width. A bindi 7 millimetres wide has to be about 77 centimetres from your eye. This tells us the Moon is about 110 of its own widths away. If we know the distance to the Moon, we can work out its width. The distance was found by astronomers by watching how the Moon shifts against the stars from different places, and today by laser light bounced off mirrors that astronauts left on the Moon. The distance is about 384,000 kilometres. Dividing by 110 gives about 3,500 kilometres, a little more than the length of India from Kashmir to Kanyakumari, which is about 3,200 kilometres. The Earth is about 12,700 kilometres wide. About 30 Earths would fit in a row between us and the Moon.
- e.g. Light from the Moon takes about one and a quarter seconds to reach us, much less than the 8 minutes from the Sun.
- e.g. The Moon's width is about a quarter of the Earth's.
### Tides: the Moon pulls on the oceans · *defined*
**Definition:** Tides are the rise and fall of the sea, caused mainly by the Moon's pull on the Earth's water. There are two high tides every day, about 12 and a half hours apart.

The Moon pulls on everything on the Earth, and the pull is stronger on the side nearest the Moon and weaker on the far side. This stretches the oceans into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. The Earth turns once a day beneath the two bulges, so a coast sees two high tides and two low tides in a day. The Moon moves on in its orbit while the Earth turns, so the gap between two high tides is about 12 hours and 25 minutes. This means each high tide arrives about 50 minutes later than on the day before, just as moonrise does. Tide times differ from place to place, because of the shape of the coast and the sea floor. The Gulf of Khambhat in Gujarat has some of the biggest tides in India. The Sun also pulls on the oceans, with a bit less than half the Moon's effect.
- e.g. Fishing boats on many coasts of India are launched at high tide and pulled up the beach at low tide.
- e.g. A tide table in a newspaper or on a phone gives the times for your port.
### Spring tides and neap tides follow the phases · *defined*
**Definition:** At new Moon and full Moon the Sun and the Moon pull in a line, and the tides are the biggest, called spring tides. At the half Moons they pull at right angles and the tides are smallest, called neap tides.

At new Moon, the Moon and the Sun are on the same side of the Earth. At full Moon, they are on opposite sides. In both cases the pulls line up and make a bigger bulge. These are spring tides. At the half Moons, the Sun's pull cuts across the Moon's pull and weakens it, so the high tides are not so high and the low tides not so low. These are neap tides. So the tide's size has a rhythm of about two weeks that follows the phases. Fishing families have long watched Amavasya and Purnima for strong tides. You can see this in a tide table: choose a port, write down the height of the day's high tide for a month, and mark the Moon's phase beside it. The highest highs come a day or two after new Moon and full Moon.
- e.g. At new Moon and full Moon, the seashore is covered and uncovered over a wider strip.
- e.g. A tidal bore, a wave that rushes up a river as the tide comes in, is strongest at spring tides.
### We see more than half of the Moon: libration · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon always shows nearly the same face, but it nods and rocks a little, so over time we see about 59 parts out of every 100 of its surface.

Because the Moon's path is an oval and slightly tilted, the Moon seems to rock from side to side and nod up and down by a few degrees over a month. This is called libration. It lets us peek a little way round the edges. Over a month or a year, astronomers see about 59 percent of the Moon's surface, though never the middle of the far side. Libration also changes the view of craters near the edge. Near the edge a crater looks like a thin oval, and a small libration can turn a crater from hidden to visible. The far side was first photographed in 1959 by the Soviet spacecraft Luna 3.
- e.g. Each month the same few craters near the edge come and go.
- e.g. The rocking is slow and small, a few degrees, but it is real and we can see it in photographs taken on different nights.
### The Moon is dark but looks bright · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon sends back only about 12 parts in every 100 of the sunlight that falls on it. It looks bright only because the night sky is dark.

The Moon's rocks and dust are grey, as dark as a tarred road. If you could bring a handful of Moon dust into daylight, it would look dark. Yet a full Moon is bright enough to read by, because the sky around it is black. The full Moon is about 400,000 times fainter than the Sun. Still, it is bright enough to make shadows on a clear night. The Moon also looks brighter at full than a simple count of its lit area would suggest, because at full Moon there are no shadows to darken it. A half Moon gives much less than half the light of a full Moon, about a tenth. Our eyes see the Moon as white because they adjust to the dark and because the colour in its grey rock is faint.
- e.g. Pictures of the Moon next to a white sheet of paper show it is grey.
- e.g. A candle looks pale in daylight and bright in the dark, for the same reason.
### Is there water on the Moon? · *debated*
The Moon has no seas and no air, but it may have some water. Instruments on India's Chandrayaan-1, launched in 2008, helped to find signs of water in the form of molecules stuck to the surface dust. Some deep craters near the Moon's poles never see sunlight, because the Sun is always low and the crater walls cast shadows. They are among the coldest places known, and ice could last there for billions of years. In 2009 a NASA probe was crashed into one of these craters, and the cloud it threw up contained water. How much there is, and in what form, are still being studied. Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south pole in 2023 to study the ground there.
- e.g. The shadows that show the Moon's mountains also hide the ice.
- e.g. Water on the Moon would be useful for visiting astronauts and for making rocket fuel.
- **Ice in dark craters:** Cold, permanently shadowed craters near the poles may hold ice mixed with the soil, in amounts big enough to be useful. A crash probe, radar and orbiters have each found signs of it.
- **A thin layer of water on dust:** The solar wind may make a very thin layer of water molecules on the dust in sunlit places. The amount is tiny, only traces, and it may come and go with the lunar day.

## Mostly agreed
- The Moon's craters were made by rocks from space hitting the surface, not by volcanoes. *How we know:* The craters have raised rims, rays and rocks melted by impact. Apollo samples show impact melt. Test impacts make the same shapes.
- The terminator is where the Sun is rising or setting on the Moon, and shadows are longest there. *How we know:* Photographs taken from the Earth and from spacecraft show long shadows along the terminator at every phase, and shorter shadows nearer the full Moon.
- The Moon's phase can be worked out from its age, which is the number of days since the last new Moon. *How we know:* Calendars have used a month of about 29 and a half days for thousands of years, and eclipses and new Moons have been predicted correctly from them.
- The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day on average. *How we know:* Moonrise tables for any city show the same slow shift from night to night, though the exact gap varies.
- The Moon's pull on the oceans causes tides, with two high tides a day, and spring tides at new and full Moon. *How we know:* Tide tables from ports around the world match the Moon's position and phase, and they can be predicted years ahead.
- The Moon is about 384,000 kilometres away and about 3,500 kilometres wide. *How we know:* Laser light bounced off mirrors left on the Moon by astronauts gives the distance to within a few centimetres. The width follows from the Moon's width in the sky.
- A lunar day lasts about 29 and a half Earth days, with about two weeks of sunlight and two weeks of night at most places. *How we know:* Landers have recorded the ground heating up in daylight and freezing in the night, and the Sun's movement across the Moon's sky has been watched from orbit.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, Winter of 1609 to 1610.** For about two thousand years, many scholars taught that the Moon and the heavens were perfect, and that the Moon was a smooth, polished ball. The dark patches were explained as stains inside it. In the winter of 1609, Galileo Galilei in Italy pointed a new telescope at the Moon, and he looked at it when it was only partly lit. He saw that the line between light and dark was ragged and not smooth. He saw bright specks in the dark part, which grew and joined the light as the days passed, like mountain tops catching the first sun. He saw round hollows with dark shadows inside their rims. He used the lengths of the shadows to guess how tall the mountains were. He published what he saw in a small book in March 1610. Some scholars said the hollows must be filled with an invisible clear material, to keep the Moon smooth. But nobody could test that, while Galileo's drawings could be checked by anyone who had a telescope.  
*What people believed before:* Many scholars thought the Moon was a perfectly smooth ball. Galileo showed that the line between its day and night is ragged and that it has mountains and hollows, by looking at the Moon when only part of it was lit.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The full Moon is the best night to see the Moon's surface.** (Many people, because the full Moon is brightest.; *rejected*)  
  For: A full Moon is fully lit, so we see the whole face at once and it is very bright.  
  Against: At full Moon the sunlight comes from behind us, so there are almost no shadows. Craters and mountains look flat. At a half Moon, long shadows along the terminator show them clearly. Galileo noticed this in 1609.
- **The Moon's craters are old volcanoes.** (Many scientists, up to the early 1900s.; *rejected*)  
  For: The Moon has dark plains of old lava, and some craters look like volcano mouths.  
  Against: In 1893 the American geologist Grove Karl Gilbert argued they were made by impacts. Craters are round whichever way the rock arrives, have raised rims and bright rays, and match craters made in tests. Apollo rocks later showed signs of impact. Most scientists accepted the impact idea by the 1960s and 1970s.
- **There is one tide bulge, on the side of the Earth facing the Moon.** (A common idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: The Moon pulls the sea towards it, so the sea piles up on the near side.  
  Against: There are two bulges, one on the near side and one on the far side. That is why there are two high tides a day. The far side bulge is there because the pull is weaker there than on the rest of the Earth.
- **High tide comes at the same time every day.** (Many people, because the Sun rises at nearly the same time each day.; *rejected*)  
  For: Tides repeat each day, so it seems like the same times should repeat.  
  Against: The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day, and the tides follow the Moon. So each high tide comes about 50 minutes later than the day before. A tide table shows the changing times.
- **The Moon is white.** (Most people and most children's drawings.; *rejected*)  
  For: The Moon looks white or pale yellow in the night sky.  
  Against: The Moon is grey and reflects only about 12 parts in every 100 of the light that falls on it, about as dark as a tarred road. It looks white because the sky around it is black.
- **There is a lot of water ice on the Moon that we can use.** (Some space agencies and companies hope so.; *debated*)  
  For: Instruments from India, the United States and others have found signs of water, and some craters near the poles never see the Sun.  
  Against: How much water there is, where it is and how easy it would be to use are still unknown. This is an active area of study, and the Moon's poles have been visited by only a few spacecraft.

## Roots
We have looked at the line between the Moon's day and night, at tides and at the Moon's age. Now we ask what the shadows on the Moon tell us about seeing and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** With the torch behind your head, the dough's craters nearly vanished. With the torch at the side, they stood out. Light from the side throws shadows, and shadows show shape. So the Moon shows its mountains best along the line between its day and night.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think the fullest, brightest view is the best view, and that shadows only hide things. On the Moon the brightest night hides the most, and the shadow is what shows the shape.  
*Evidence:* In the dough test, the torch beside your head showed few craters, and the torch at the side showed many. At the half Moon, the same thing happens on the real Moon, as the shadows along the terminator show craters and peaks.

- *who am i:* What you can see about something depends on where you stand and where the light comes from. Your place is part of every view you have.
- *what is life:* Many animals use the Moon to time their lives, and many use shadows to find food. A hunter in dim light finds prey by the shape of its shadow.
- *what is this world:* The Moon is a ball of grey rock with mountains and craters, and always half lit. How rough it seems changes with the angle of the Sun.

The Sun, the Earth and the Moon make a system that is always changing. From the same Moon, a full Moon night gives a flat disc, and a half Moon night gives a landscape. The Moon has not changed. Only the angle of the light has. A bright place and a dark place are not opposites here, because the shadow and the light work together to make shape. A thing is known by its light and by its shadow together.

- **Tao Te Ching 2:** Being and not-being give birth to each other. Long and short shape each other. High and low lean on each other. (Light and shadow shape each other on the Moon. Without the shadow, we would not see the mountain, and without the light, we would not see the shadow.)
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.3.28:** Lead me from darkness to light. (On the Moon, the way from seeing nothing to seeing the mountains runs along the line where dark meets light, and not through the brightest part.)
- Science (settled): Craters on the Moon are made by impacts. Slanting light along the terminator shows them best.
- Science (open): How much water ice lies in the Moon's permanently shadowed craters.
- Other view, Many people who love the full Moon: The full Moon is the most beautiful Moon. Its brightness lights the whole night, and it brings families out to the terrace. Seeing detail is one way to enjoy the Moon, and not the only one. A flat, bright Moon is not a worse Moon, only a different one.
- Practice, Light from the side (3 min): Hold your hand up. Look at it with a lamp behind your head, then with the lamp at your side. Notice the lines and the bumps on your skin in each. Which light shows more? Breathe in slowly three times as you look. Then think of the Moon's rim, where the shadows are long.
- Ask: When you have too much light on something, do you see more of it or less?
- Ask: On the Moon, the shadow shows the mountain. What else do we only understand because of its shadow?
- Ask: The full Moon is flat and the half Moon is rough. Which one is the real Moon?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The brightest Moon shows the least about its own shape. When is looking at more light a way of seeing less? (hint: Think about a face lit from the front and from the side, or a field at noon and at dawn.)
- *deep:* Shadows can look like the opposite of knowledge, a dark place where we see nothing. Here they show shape. What else do we learn from the dark parts of a picture? (hint: Think of a drawing in pencil, a photograph with a dark background, or a silence in music.)
- *deep:* The Moon's lit side always faces the Sun. When you see a crescent in the sky, where is the Sun? (hint: Think about the line from the bright edge to the Sun, even if the Sun has set.)
- *big:* Everyone sees the same phase at the same time, but a child in Australia sees it upside down. Who sees the Moon the right way up? (hint: Think about what 'up' means, and where the people are standing.)
- *big:* Chandrayaan-3 was timed to land at lunar dawn and had about two weeks of sunlight. What does it mean to plan work around something as slow as the Moon's day? (hint: Think of a farmer who plans around the monsoon, or a fisher who plans around the tide.)

## Story: The Line Where the Mountains Stand
*Made up. Abirami, her Paati, her classmate Karthik and their flat in Madurai are invented. Real: a full Moon was in the sky on 1 August 2023, a new Moon on 16 August, and India's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south polar region on 23 August 2023, a little after six in the evening, at lunar dawn, designed to work for about one lunar day of sunlight. The Moon was nearly a half Moon that evening.*

Abirami was nine, and she had a problem. The school science fair was in three weeks, and her project was called Mountains on the Moon. She had a pair of binoculars borrowed from her uncle, a drawing book, and a very short time.

On the first night of August, a full Moon rose over the water tank on the roof of the building opposite. It was huge and golden, and every neighbour came up to their terraces to look. Abirami took her binoculars and her drawing book up to the roof, and she held her breath, and she looked.

The Moon was a bright white plate. It had some grey smudges, which her teacher said were old lava plains. But there were no mountains. There were no craters. There was no shadow anywhere. It was as flat and smooth as a thali.

She sat down on the cold floor of the terrace. 'The book is wrong,' she said. 'The Moon is smooth. My project is finished before it started.'

Her Paati was drying chillies on the next terrace. She came over, wiping her hands on her sari, and looked through the binoculars for a long time without saying anything. 'It is a beautiful Moon,' she said finally. 'But it does look as flat as a thali.'

'See?' said Abirami.

Paati sat down next to her. 'I worked for thirty years in the Survey Department,' she said. 'I walked fields with a chain and a staff, from Melur to Usilampatti. I will tell you what we learned. A field at noon looks flat. You can stand in a field with a hill in it, and at noon you will swear it is a table.'

'So what did you do?'

'We started at dawn,' said Paati. 'In the early light, the sun is low and comes in sideways. Every small ridge, every old wall under the soil, every little mound throws a long shadow. The same field, and at sunrise it looks like a map. The hills were always there. The noon light hid them.'

Abirami looked at the Moon again. 'The sun is behind me now,' she said slowly. 'It is shining straight at the Moon. So there are no shadows.'

'Now you are thinking like a surveyor,' said Paati.

'So when will the Moon have sideways light?'

Paati took the almanac off the nail by the door. 'The new Moon is on the sixteenth,' she said, running her finger down the page. 'The Moon is half lit about seven days after new Moon, in the evening. Then the light comes from the side, and the line between the day and night on the Moon is straight down the middle. Along that line, it is sunrise on the Moon. Look there.'

Abirami counted the days on the calendar. It was a long wait. She drew the full Moon in her book anyway, a plain circle, and under it she wrote, 'The wrong night.'

On the twenty-third, the whole school was talking about only one thing. India was going to land a spacecraft on the Moon that evening. Karthik, who sat next to her, said he could not wait. 'They picked the twenty-third on purpose,' said her teacher. 'Can anybody tell me why that day?' Nobody knew. Abirami had a feeling, but she kept it to herself.

At six o'clock, the whole building crowded round Paati's television. When the lander touched down at four minutes past six, Abirami heard the cheering from every floor of the building. Paati wiped her eyes with the end of her sari.

Then Abirami grabbed her binoculars. 'Paati, come up!'

On the terrace, the sky was a pale orange, turning blue. High in the south hung the Moon, almost, but not quite, a half Moon, with its bright side towards the setting Sun. Abirami put the binoculars to her eyes and gasped.

Along the line between the light and the dark, the Moon was not smooth at all. It was crumpled. Craters, big and small, had black crescent shadows inside their rims. Rows of them marched along the edge like footprints. Beyond the line, in the dark part, three tiny bright dots floated by themselves. 'Paati! They are mountain tops!' she said. 'The sun has reached the tops, but not the valleys yet.'

'Just like the hills at Melur,' said Paati.

Abirami drew it all as fast as she could. Then she lowered the binoculars and looked at the bottom of the Moon, near the line. 'The spacecraft is down there,' she said. 'And the Sun has only just come up over it. That is why they chose today. The Sun is low there, and it has two weeks of daylight before it sets. That is how long the machines will work.'

'And then?' asked Paati.

'Then it will be night for two weeks, and very cold,' said Abirami. 'And they will sleep.'

At the science fair, Abirami's poster had a full Moon on one side, bright and flat, and a half Moon on the other, rough with shadows. Across the top she had written: 'Do not look at the bright Moon. Look at the line.' Karthik read it twice. 'I'm going to try that on Saturday,' he said.

**For the parent:** A bright full Moon hides the shape of the Moon's ground, because the light comes straight from behind us. Sideways light along the line between day and night throws long shadows that show mountains and craters.

- Ask: Why did the full Moon look flat to Abirami?
- Ask: How did a surveyor's trick with early morning light help Abirami find the Moon's mountains?
- Ask: On which night, and along which line, did the craters show up most clearly?
- Ask: If you were planning a landing on the Moon and the lander worked only in sunlight, when in the Moon's day would you land?
- Ask: Karthik wanted to try Abirami's idea on Saturday. If the half Moon was on Wednesday, would that work? Why or why not?

### e3 · The Bindi Moon: how many Moon-widths away is the Moon? (30 min)
- **question:** How far away is the Moon, measured in its own width? Can a small bindi on a window glass cover the whole Moon?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How far from your eye must a small bindi be to cover the Moon exactly? Will it be a few centimetres, or a few metres? How many times its own width? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same bindi, the same window and the same Moon each time. Close the same eye and look from the same height. Measure the distance three times and use the average, because one measurement can be a fluke. Only your position changes, so any difference comes from where you stood.
- **safety:** Use this only for the Moon. Never try it on the Sun, and never look at the Sun. Stand safely at the window, away from a balcony edge. A grown-up should help near high windows.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the bindi, your eye and the Moon in a line, and label the two distances.
- **results table:** Try | Width of the bindi (mm) | Distance from bindi to my eye (mm) | Distance divided by width
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bindi and the window. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* We will stick a bindi on the window. When we stand back, the bindi can cover the whole Moon. How far back must we stand? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. How far back for the bindi to cover the Moon? Why?
  - Ask: Will you stand close to the glass or far from it? (good: Quite far.; Close, because the Moon is small.; I don't know.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Pick a night when the Moon is easy to see. It can be seen in the evening sky from two days after new Moon to the full Moon, and in the morning sky after that.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure the bindi's width in millimetres with a ruler. 2. Stick it on the window glass, at the height of your eye. 3. Write the width in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Measure how wide the bindi is. Write the number. Stick it on the window at the height of your eye.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the bindi. Stick it on the window glass.
  - Note: A 7 millimetre bindi is ideal. A smaller bindi needs you to stand closer, and a bigger one means standing far back. If the bindi is a different shape, use its widest part.
  - Check: Why do we write down the bindi's width before measuring anything else? → **We will need it for the sum later**
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Close one eye. 2. Stand back and move your head until the bindi exactly covers the Moon. 3. Hold still. 4. Ask your helper to hold a string from the bindi to your open eye. 5. Measure the string in millimetres. 6. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Close one eye. Move back and forth until the bindi exactly hides the Moon. Hold still. Your helper measures from the bindi to your eye. Do it three times.  
  *Child mode:* Close one eye. Move until the bindi hides the Moon. Measure.
  - Ask: What did you notice about the distance? (good: It was bigger than I thought.; It was about the same each time.; The Moon is very far away.)
  - Note: Use only one eye, or your two eyes will see the bindi in two places. Your three measurements may differ by a few centimetres. That is normal. For a 7 millimetre bindi, expect about 70 to 80 centimetres. If the Moon moves, redo the try. If you cannot find the Moon, try another night.
  - Check: Why do we close one eye? → **Two eyes see the bindi in two places**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Divide each distance by the bindi's width. 2. Find the average of your three answers. 3. The Moon is about 384,000 kilometres away. Divide 384,000 by your average. 4. Compare the answer with the length of India, about 3,200 kilometres.  
  *Parent says:* Divide the distance by the bindi's width. This tells us how many bindi-widths away the Moon is. Then divide 384,000 kilometres by that. What do you get?  
  *Child mode:* Divide the distance by the width. Find the average. Work out the Moon's width.
  - Ask: How wide is the Moon, and how does it compare with India? (good: About 3,500 kilometres.; A bit wider than India is long.)
  - Note: You should get a ratio near 110, with a range of about 100 to 120. A 7 millimetre bindi at 77 centimetres gives 770 divided by 7, which is 110. Then 384,000 divided by 110 is about 3,490 kilometres. The real width is about 3,474 kilometres. Do not worry if you get a number between 3,200 and 3,800.
  - Check: The bindi is 7 millimetres wide and is 770 millimetres from your eye. What is the ratio? → **110**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw and why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you measure? Why does a small bindi cover a big Moon?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the Moon is about 110 of its own widths away. What I saw: a 7 millimetre bindi covered the Moon at about 77 centimetres, so the ratio was about 110. Why: a small thing close to the eye can cover a big thing far away if they are in the same proportion. The same is true for the Sun, which is also about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times further away.
  - Think: The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon. What does that tell you about how far away it is?

### Lines, Shadows and Phases (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (order) Put these Moons in order by their age, from the youngest to the oldest.  
   **Answer:** ['a thin crescent, 3 days old', 'a half Moon, 7 days old', 'a full Moon, 15 days old', 'a half Moon, 22 days old']. *Why:* The Moon's age counts the days since the new Moon. A thin crescent is a few days old, the first quarter about 7, the full Moon about 15 and the last quarter about 22.
2. (mcq) What is the terminator on the Moon?  
   Options: The line between the lit part and the dark part / The dark side of the Moon / The edge of the Moon in the sky / A very deep crater  
   **Answer:** The line between the lit part and the dark part. *Why:* The terminator is the line where the Moon's day meets its night. For a place on the Moon, it is sunrise or sunset. It is not a dark side or a crater.
3. (mcq) Which night is the poorest for seeing craters through binoculars?  
   Options: Full Moon / First quarter / Last quarter / A five-day-old crescent  
   **Answer:** Full Moon. *Why:* At full Moon, the Sun lights the Moon from behind us, so there are almost no shadows. Craters look flat. The other nights have light from the side.
4. (fill) The line between the lit and dark parts of the Moon is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['terminator']. *Why:* On the real Moon, the terminator is the place where it is sunrise or sunset, and shadows there are long.
5. (truefalse) At full Moon, the Sun is low in the sky over the middle of the Moon's face.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. At full Moon the Sun shines straight down on the middle of the near side, so it is lunar noon there. The Sun is low only near the terminator.
6. (write) A friend lights a ball of dough with a torch beside her head, and says she sees almost no craters. Then she moves the torch to the side and sees many. Explain what changed.

   **Answer:** The sea bulges on the side facing the Moon and on the opposite side, and the Earth turns under both. *Why:* The Moon's pull stretches the sea into two bulges. As the Earth turns once under them, a coast meets two high tides. The Moon does not go round twice in a day.

   Options: At new Moon and full Moon / Only in the spring season / At the half Moons / Only at night

   **Answer:** ['big tides at new Moon', 'small tides at first quarter', 'big tides at full Moon', 'small tides at last quarter']. *Why:* Tides are big at new Moon and full Moon and small at the quarters. The rhythm repeats about every two weeks.

   **Answer:** The torch was not low enough at the side, or the craters were shallow. *Why:* Shadows from the side show shape only if the torch is low and the craters are deep. If the side light is too high, there are few shadows. A good scientist checks the test before the idea.

## Applications
- Choosing the day and hour to land a spacecraft on the Moon so that it has sunlight to work in.
- Picking the best nights to photograph the Moon's craters with a telescope or binoculars.
- Reading tide tables to plan fishing trips, port arrivals and walks on the beach.
- Using the Moon's age to fix the dates of festivals in the Indian lunar calendar.
- Using the length of shadows to measure the height of a mountain, a tree or a tower.
- Searching for old walls and field boundaries from the air in low morning light.
- Measuring far-away things by comparing a small close thing with a big far thing.
- Predicting spring tides to warn coastal towns about extra-high water in storms.
- Designing lunar rovers to survive extreme swings of heat and cold.
- Looking for ice in the shadowed craters of the Moon to support future missions.

## Open questions
- **How much water ice is there in the Moon's shadowed craters, and can we use it?** Several spacecraft have found signs of ice, but only a few have landed near the poles. How much there is and how deep it lies are still being measured.
- **Why are the Moon's near and far sides so different?** The near side has big dark plains, and the far side has hardly any. Scientists have several ideas, such as the young Earth's heat or a giant crash, but none is settled.
- **How long will the Moon keep moving away from the Earth?** The Moon drifts away by a few centimetres each year. How this will change over billions of years depends on the oceans, and scientists are still working it out.
- **What was the Moon's surface like when it was young, and when did the big impacts stop?** Rocks brought back by astronauts give ages for craters, but we have rock from only a few places. Scientists argue about whether there was a late burst of impacts.
- **Do tides on the Earth's early oceans explain how life began?** Some scientists suggest tidal pools wetting and drying shores could have helped to build the first chemicals of life, but there is no firm evidence yet.
- **Could people live and work in the permanently shadowed craters near the Moon's poles?** The cold is extreme, and the ground has never been seen up close. Engineers and scientists are still working out whether people could use it.

## Warm-up
- What is the terminator on the Moon? → The line between the lit part and the dark part.
- Which Moon shows the most craters through binoculars, a full Moon or a half Moon? → A half Moon.
- Why does a full Moon look flat? → The Sun is behind us, so there are almost no shadows.
- About how much later does the Moon rise each day? → About 50 minutes.
- How long is a day and night on the Moon? → About 29 and a half Earth days.
- How many high tides are there in a day? → Two.
- When are the tides biggest? → A day or two after new Moon and full Moon.
- How many Moon-widths away is the Moon? → About 110.
- How much sunlight does the Moon send back? → About 12 parts in every 100.
