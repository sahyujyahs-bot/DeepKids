# Energy at work: how energy makes things happen, and why a still table has energy too
`sci.energy.energy-around-us.L5`

**Summary.** Put a wet coin on an empty bottle from the freezer, and the coin starts to click with nobody touching it. A bottle that was not in the freezer does nothing. Why? In this lesson you will test this yourself, look for energy in a still table, and join machines in an energy chain.

**Intro.** *A fan needs electricity. A scooter needs petrol. You need breakfast. A table needs nothing and does nothing. So does a table have any energy at all?* Energy is what makes things happen. In this lesson you watch a kitchen at work and find where the energy for each thing comes from. You find that a mixie, a stove and your own body all do the same job. They take energy in one form and give it out in another. Then you test the table. You put a cold bottle on it and a coin on the bottle, and you count what happens. By the end you can say what energy a still table has, and why that energy usually does nothing.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what energy does, and find energy moving, lighting, sounding, heating and cooling things at home and outside.
1. I can name the energy that goes into a machine and the energy that comes out, for at least six things at home.
2. I can name six sources of energy, and follow an energy chain back to the Sun.
3. I can name four kinds of energy that a still table has.
4. I can explain, from the Clicking Coin Test, that energy makes something happen only when it moves from one thing to another.
5. I can explain, from the Fan and the Ice, what a fan really does, and why moving air cools me.
6. I can follow five rules for using electricity safely, and say what would stop in my town without electricity.
7. I can tell how Sadi Carnot found that heat does work only by passing from a hot place to a cold one.

## Terms
- **Energy**: What is needed to make anything happen. Energy makes things move, light up, make a sound, get hotter or cooler, and do work. *e.g.* Energy from cooking gas heats the milk. Energy from your breakfast lets you climb the stairs.
- **Form of energy**: One of the ways energy shows itself. The main forms are movement, heat, light, sound, electrical energy and stored energy. *e.g.* A television gives out light, sound and a little heat. All three are forms of energy.
- **Energy change**: Energy going from one form to another. Scientists also call this energy conversion. *e.g.* In a torch, the stored energy of the cell changes to electrical energy, and then to light.
- **Energy changer**: Anything that takes energy in one form and gives it out in another. Every machine is an energy changer, and so is every living body. *e.g.* A mixie takes in electrical energy. It gives out movement, sound and heat.
- **Source of energy**: Where the energy for something comes from. *e.g.* The Sun, food, fuel, electricity, wind and flowing water are sources of energy we use every day.
- **Fuel**: A material that gives out heat and light when it burns. A fuel holds stored energy. *e.g.* Cooking gas, petrol, diesel, coal, firewood and the oil in a diya are fuels.
- **Stored energy**: Energy that is kept in something and can come out later. *e.g.* Food, a charged cell, a stretched rubber band and water in a roof tank all hold stored energy.
- **Chemical energy**: Energy stored in the way the atoms of a material are joined. It comes out when the material burns or is digested. *e.g.* Rice, petrol, wood and the chemicals in a cell hold chemical energy.
- **Energy chain**: A line of arrows that shows where energy came from, step by step. *e.g.* Sun → paddy plant → rice → your muscles → the pedals of a cycle.
- **Heat energy**: The energy of the moving particles inside a thing. The faster the particles move, the hotter the thing is. Scientists also call it thermal energy. *e.g.* A table at room temperature holds heat energy. A spoonful of hot tea holds more heat energy than a spoonful-sized piece of the table, because the tea is hotter.
- **Particle**: A very tiny piece of matter, far too small to see. Atoms and molecules are particles. *e.g.* The air in a bottle is made of particles that fly about and hit the walls.
- **Absolute zero**: The coldest temperature there can be: about 273 degrees below zero on the Celsius scale. At that temperature, particles have the least movement they can ever have. *e.g.* A room at 27 degrees is 300 degrees above absolute zero. So everything in the room holds a lot of heat energy.
- **Electricity**: A form of energy that travels through wires. It is easy to send far and easy to change into other forms. *e.g.* Electricity runs the fan, the fridge, the lights and the water pump.
- **Power station**: A place where electricity is made from another source of energy, such as coal, falling water, wind or sunlight. *e.g.* The Neyveli power station in Tamil Nadu burns a soft brown coal called lignite to make electricity.
- **Turbine**: A wheel with blades. Moving steam, water or wind pushes the blades and turns the wheel. *e.g.* In a dam, falling water turns a turbine.
- **Windmill**: A machine with big blades that the wind turns. It can pump water or grind grain. *e.g.* A farmer's windmill pumps water up from a well into the field.
- **Generator**: A machine that changes movement into electrical energy. A turbine turns the generator. *e.g.* A cycle dynamo is a small generator. The turning wheel makes the lamp light up.
- **Solar cell**: A flat panel that changes sunlight straight into electricity. It has no flame and no fuel. *e.g.* A solar lantern has a solar cell that charges it all day, and a battery that gives light at night.
- **Appliance**: A machine at home that runs on electricity or fuel and does one job. *e.g.* A fan, a mixie, an iron, a fridge and a gas stove are appliances.
- **Energy efficiency**: How much of the energy going into a machine does the job we want. An efficient machine wastes less. *e.g.* A 9 watt LED bulb gives about the same light as an old 60 watt bulb. So the LED bulb needs far less electrical energy to do the same job.
- **Clean energy**: Electricity made without smoke or harmful gases, from sunlight, wind or flowing water. *e.g.* Solar panels on a roof make clean energy.
- **Renewable**: A source of energy that the Sun, wind or water renews every day, so that it does not run out. *e.g.* Sunlight, wind and flowing river water are renewable. Coal is not, because it took millions of years to form.
- **Pollution**: Harmful smoke, gases or waste that make the air, water or land dirty. *e.g.* Smoke from burning coal and wood pollutes the air.

## Explanations
### What energy does · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy is what is needed to make anything happen. It makes things move, light up, make a sound, get hotter or cooler, and do work.

You cannot see energy itself. You see what it does. In a kitchen you can find all of it in five minutes. The mixie blades move. The flame gives light and heat. The cooker whistles. The fridge cools the curd. Doing work is one more thing energy does. In science, work means moving something with a push or a pull, such as lifting a bucket or grinding batter. When nothing at all is happening to a thing, no energy is moving in or out of it. That does not mean the thing has no energy, as the table will show.
- e.g. Moving: a ceiling fan, a scooter, a kite on the wind, a running dog.
- e.g. Lighting up: a tube light, a diya, a firefly, the Sun.
- e.g. Making a sound: a cooker whistle, a mridangam, a temple bell, a koel calling.
- e.g. Heating and cooling: a gas stove, an iron, a fridge, a clay pot of water.
- e.g. Doing work: a lift carrying people up, a pump filling the roof tank, you carrying a school bag.
### Every working thing is an energy changer · *defined*
**Definition:** An energy changer takes energy in one form and gives it out in another. No machine makes energy, and no machine uses energy up.

Ask two questions about any working thing. What energy goes in? What energy comes out? A fan takes in electrical energy and gives out movement, with a little heat and sound. A gas stove takes in the chemical energy of cooking gas and gives out heat and light. A loudspeaker takes in electrical energy and gives out sound. Your body is a changer too. It takes in the chemical energy of food and gives out movement, heat and sound. Often one form is what we wanted, and the rest is given out as warmth that nobody asked for. Add up everything that comes out, and it equals what went in.
- e.g. A mixie takes in electrical energy. It gives out movement, sound and heat.
- e.g. An electric iron takes in electrical energy. It gives out heat.
- e.g. A scooter engine takes in the chemical energy of petrol. It gives out movement, heat and sound.
- e.g. A solar panel takes in light. It gives out electrical energy.
- e.g. A child running takes in the chemical energy of food. She gives out movement and heat.
### Six sources of energy we use every day · *defined*
**Definition:** A source of energy is where the energy for something comes from. At home and in town we use the Sun, food, fuel, electricity, wind and flowing water.

The Sun dries clothes and papads, warms water on a roof and lights the day. Food gives energy to every animal, including us. Fuels are burned for heat or movement. Cooking gas runs the stove, petrol runs a scooter, diesel runs a bus, coal feeds a power station, and oil feeds a diya. Electricity runs fans, lights, pumps and phones. Wind dries clothes, lifts kites, moves sailing boats and turns wind turbines. Flowing and falling water turns the turbines in dams. In the hills of Uttarakhand, a stream turns the stone of a small flour mill called a gharat. Electricity is a special case. Nobody finds electricity in nature ready to use. It is always made from one of the other sources, and wires carry it to us.
- e.g. The Sun dries clothes on a line, heats water in a solar water heater, and dries sea water into salt in the salt pans near Thoothukudi.
- e.g. Food gives energy to a child who is cycling, to a bullock pulling a cart and to a crow flying.
- e.g. Fuel gives energy to an LPG stove, to an auto that runs on CNG and to a diesel lorry.
- e.g. Electricity gives energy to a fan, to a lift and to a metro train.
- e.g. Wind lifts a kite at Uttarayan, and it turns a wind turbine near Kanyakumari.
- e.g. Flowing water turns the turbines at the Idukki dam in Kerala, and it turns the stone of a gharat in the hills.
### Some books list seven kinds of energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Some school books list seven kinds of energy. That list mixes up sources and forms. This unit names six sources instead.

The seven in those books are food, solar, wind, hydro, organic, electrical and magnetic. That list mixes up sources, such as the Sun and wind, with a form, electrical. Magnetism is a force, and biogas is a fuel, so neither is a kind of energy on its own. Scientists have no final answer to what energy is. They describe it by what it does. This unit names six sources: the Sun, food, fuel, electricity, wind and flowing water.
- e.g. Biogas, made from dung and plants, is a fuel. We burn it for heat, as we burn cooking gas.
- e.g. Hydro means water. Hydro energy is the energy of flowing and falling water, so it is in our list as flowing water.
### The Sun at work: drying, cooking and electricity · *defined*
**Definition:** Sunlight carries heat and light. We use both to dry things, to cook and to heat water. Solar cells change sunlight into electricity.

Sunlight is the oldest energy we use. Farmers spread grain on the ground to dry in the Sun before they store it. Clothes and papads dry on a line, much faster than in shade. A solar cooker is a box with a shiny lid. The lid sends sunlight onto a black pot inside. On a sunny day the pot gets hot enough to cook rice or dal in a few hours. A solar water heater on a roof has black pipes under glass. The Sun heats the water in the pipes. A solar cell is different. It is a flat panel that changes light straight into electricity, with no flame and no fuel. Solar cells light bulbs in homes with no wires, and run calculators and small vehicles. They stop at night, so a battery stores some of the electricity for the dark.
- e.g. After harvest, rice is spread out in village yards in Odisha to dry in the Sun.
- e.g. A solar lantern charges all day on a roof and gives light at night in a village far from the wires.
- e.g. Cochin International Airport in Kerala has a large solar plant that supplies the airport's electricity.
- e.g. A solar cooker on a school roof cooks a pot of khichdi by afternoon.
### Wind at work: moving air carries energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Wind is moving air, and moving air carries energy. It can turn a pinwheel, push a sail and turn a windmill.

Moving air means many air particles travelling the same way together. They push on whatever stands in their path. A paper pinwheel spins when you blow on it. Blow harder, and it spins faster. A sail catches the wind and pushes a boat along. A windmill has big blades that turn in the wind. In windy places, farmers used windmills to pump water up from wells, and to grind grain into flour. Today a tall wind turbine turns a generator and makes electricity. A balloon shows it too. Let go of a blown-up balloon with its mouth open. The air rushes out one way, and the balloon shoots the other way. The squeezed air held stored energy. When it moved, the energy did work. You try this in the Balloon Rocket.
- e.g. A paper pinwheel from a village fair spins slowly in a breeze and fast in a strong wind.
- e.g. A windmill turns in the wind and pumps water up from a well into a field channel.
- e.g. A blown-up balloon let go with its mouth open flies across the room.
### Follow the energy back: an energy chain · *defined*
**Definition:** An energy chain is a line of arrows that shows where energy came from, step by step. Most chains on Earth start at the Sun.

Start with something happening and keep asking where its energy came from. A child pedals a cycle. Her muscles got the energy from rice. The paddy plant made the rice using sunlight. So the chain is Sun, paddy plant, rice, muscles, pedals. Try a fan. Its electricity came from a power station. The power station burned coal. Coal is the remains of forests that grew about 300 million years ago, and those trees grew with sunlight. Petrol and cooking gas come from tiny sea beings who lived long ago, and they also lived on sunlight. Wind blows because the Sun warms some places more than others. Rivers flow because the Sun lifted the water as vapour. A few chains do not start at the Sun: the heat inside the Earth, the tides and nuclear power.
- e.g. Sun → paddy plant → rice → muscles → cycle pedals.
- e.g. Sun → ancient forest → coal → power station → wires → fan.
- e.g. Sun → warm and cool air → wind → wind turbine → wires → bulb.
- e.g. Sun → water vapour → rain → river → dam turbine → wires → train.
### Stored energy: energy that waits · *defined*
**Definition:** Stored energy is energy kept in something, ready to come out later.

Energy does not have to be used at once. A cell stores chemical energy until you switch on the torch. Food stores it until your body needs it. A stretched rubber band stores energy in the stretch. A blown-up balloon stores it in the stretched rubber and the squeezed air. Water pumped up to a roof tank stores energy by being high up. Petrol, coal and wood are stores that took millions of years, or many years, to fill. A store lets us carry energy about and use it when we choose. That is why a phone has a battery and a scooter has a tank.
- e.g. A charged phone battery holds stored energy until you use the phone.
- e.g. A wound-up toy or a stretched rubber band holds stored energy in the winding or the stretch.
- e.g. A full gas cylinder holds stored energy in the gas, which is a fuel.
- e.g. A roof tank full of water holds stored energy, because the water is high up.
- e.g. A banana in your school bag holds stored chemical energy that your body can use later.
### A still table has energy too · *defined*
**Definition:** A table that is doing nothing still holds energy in four ways. It holds heat energy, chemical energy, stored energy of height, and energy in its mass.

First, heat energy. The particles of the wood never stop shaking. A room at 27 degrees is 300 degrees above the coldest temperature there can be, so the table holds a lot of heat energy. Very roughly, the heat energy in a 10 kilogram table is about the same as the electrical energy that an iron uses in one hour. Second, chemical energy. Wood burns. Burning that table would give out about 160 million joules. A unit of electricity is what your electricity meter counts, and 160 million joules is about 44 of those units. A tree stored that energy from sunlight. Third, height. A table on the third floor would fall if the floor were not there, so its position stores energy. Fourth, mass. Albert Einstein showed in 1905 that mass itself is a store of energy. The mass of a 10 kilogram table holds as much energy as about 250 billion units of electricity. That is more than all of India uses in a month. Nobody can get that energy out of a table. It is there all the same.
- e.g. Heat energy: the table melts an ice cube placed on it, with no flame.
- e.g. Chemical energy: a wooden matchstick is the same material, and it burns.
- e.g. Stored energy of height: an eraser pushed off the table edge falls and lands with a tap.
- e.g. Mass: all matter is stored energy, and the Sun shines because it turns a tiny part of its mass into light and heat.
### Why the table's energy does nothing · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy makes something happen only when it moves from one thing to another or changes form. Heat energy moves only when one thing is warmer than another.

In the Clicking Coin Test, a room-warm bottle stands on the table and the coin never moves. A bottle from the freezer on the same table makes the coin click many times. The table and the air hold the same heat energy both times. With the warm bottle, everything is at one temperature, so no heat energy moves. With the cold bottle there is a difference. Heat energy moves from the warm air and table into the cold air inside the bottle. The warmed air needs more room, so it lifts the coin and a puff escapes. When the bottle is as warm as the room, the clicking stops. The same rule holds for other energy. Water runs a turbine only if it can fall to a lower place. A cell drives a current only while its two ends differ. So a thing can hold a great deal of energy and do nothing with it.
- e.g. A cold bottle on a warm table: heat energy moves, and the coin clicks.
- e.g. A warm bottle on a warm table: nothing moves, and nothing happens.
- e.g. Water in a lake behind a dam: it turns the turbine only when it falls.
- e.g. Hot tea in a cool room: heat energy moves out until both are the same.
### Still air is not still inside · *defined*
**Definition:** The particles of air fly about all the time, even in a closed room with no breeze. Their movement is the heat energy of the air.

At room temperature, the particles of air move at about 500 metres every second. That is faster than a passenger aeroplane. They move in every direction and hit each other, so the air as a whole goes nowhere. You feel no wind. The air in a small bedroom holds roughly 9 million joules of heat energy. That is about two and a half units of electricity. Warm the air and the particles fly faster and hit the walls harder. In the cold bottle, that is what lifts the coin. Cool a thing and its particles slow down. They would have the least movement they can ever have at absolute zero, about 273 degrees below zero. Even there, physicists have found that a little movement remains. Nothing is ever perfectly still.
- e.g. Still air in a closed room has particles that fly about at about 500 metres every second.
- e.g. Air that is warmed in a bottle has faster particles, and they push the coin up.
- e.g. A steel spoon, a stone and a table are solid, so their particles shake in place.
- e.g. Liquid helium stays liquid even close to absolute zero, because its particles keep moving.
### What a fan really does · *defined*
**Definition:** A fan changes electrical energy into the movement of air. It does not make the air colder.

In the Fan and the Ice, an ice cube in the fan's wind melts faster than one in still air. If a fan made air cold, that ice would last longer. A fan only moves the room's air. Moving air brings the room's heat energy to the ice faster. The same wind cools you, because your skin is warmer than the room. At about 33 degrees, your skin gives heat to the cooler air, and moving air carries it away faster. Sweat also dries faster in a wind, and drying takes heat from your skin. On a very hot day, when the air is hotter than your skin, a fan blows heat into you. A fridge and an air conditioner are different. They use electricity to move heat from inside to outside, which is why the back of a fridge is warm. A clay pot cools water with no electricity at all: water seeps through the clay and dries, and drying takes heat away.
- e.g. A fan in an empty room cools nothing. It warms the room a little.
- e.g. A wet hand in the fan's wind feels much colder than a dry one.
- e.g. The back of a fridge is warm: that is the heat moved out of the food.
- e.g. A matka keeps water cool because water dries off its damp outside.
### Electricity: how a town gets its energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Electricity is made at power stations from other sources of energy, and wires carry it to homes, shops, trains and hospitals.

Most of India's electricity, about 7 units in every 10, still comes from burning coal. The burning coal boils water. The steam turns a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator. Only about one third of the coal's energy becomes electricity. The rest leaves as warmth. Dams, wind turbines and solar panels make most of the rest. Wires on tall towers join the power stations and towns of the whole country in one network, called the grid. Electricity cannot easily be stored, so it is made at the moment it is used. Now think of a town with no electricity for a day. The water pump stops, so the roof tank runs dry. Lifts and metro trains stop. Phone towers and hospital machines run on batteries and diesel generators for a while. Gas stoves, cycles and the Sun go on working.
- e.g. Coal → heat → steam → turbine → generator → wires → your fan.
- e.g. Bengaluru's streets were lit by electricity in 1905. The power came from falling water on the Kaveri, far from the city.
- e.g. A hospital keeps a diesel generator for the hours when the power fails.
- e.g. A solar street light makes and stores its own electricity.
### Five rules for using electricity safely · *defined*
**Definition:** Electricity from a wall socket is strong enough to kill. Five rules keep you safe.

Rule one: keep water away from electricity. Never touch a switch, a plug or an appliance with wet hands. Rule two: never push a finger, a pin or anything else into a socket. Rule three: never touch a wire that is broken, worn or bare, and tell a grown-up at once. Rule four: stay far away from a fallen wire, an electricity pole and a transformer, and never fly a kite near power lines. Rule five: switch off before you pull a plug out, and pull the plug, not the wire. Your body is mostly water with salts in it, and it carries electricity. That is why the rules matter. The electricity from a wall socket pushes very strongly, so it can send a dangerous current through you. The small cells in a torch push only very weakly, so they are safe to handle.
- e.g. Dry your hands before you touch the fan switch.
- e.g. After a storm, a wire lies on the road. Keep away and call a grown-up.
- e.g. The cover of a socket is cracked. Do not use it. Tell a grown-up.
- e.g. A kite is stuck on a power line. Leave it there.
### Using energy well, and keeping the air clean · *defined*
**Definition:** An efficient machine does the same job with less energy. Clean energy is made without smoke.

Every changer gives out some warmth that nobody wanted. An old glass bulb turns most of its electricity into heat, so it is too hot to touch. An LED bulb gives the same light with about one sixth of the electricity. Burning coal, wood and petrol puts smoke and harmful gases into the air. Smoke from a wood fire inside a kitchen harms the lungs of the person cooking, who is most often a woman. Sunlight, wind and flowing water make electricity without smoke. The Sun renews them every day, so they are called renewable. Coal, petrol and cooking gas took millions of years to form, and we burn them far faster than they come back. So a family can help in two ways: use less, and choose cleaner. Switch off fans and lights in empty rooms. Put a lid on the pot. Dry clothes in the Sun.
- e.g. Use a 9 watt LED bulb in place of an old 60 watt bulb. You get the same light for much less electricity.
- e.g. Put a lid on a pot, so that less cooking gas is needed.
- e.g. Soak rice and pulses for half an hour before you cook them. Soaked grains are soft, so they cook in fewer minutes and need less gas.
- e.g. Use only as much water as the food needs. Extra water takes extra gas to heat.
- e.g. Cook dal and rice in a pressure cooker. It holds the steam in, so the food is ready in much less time than in an open pot.
- e.g. Use a solar water heater on the roof in place of an electric geyser.
- e.g. Walk or cycle for short trips, instead of using a vehicle that burns fuel.
### Old ways of using nature's energy well · *defined*
**Definition:** Before electricity, people in India learned to use heat, wind and sunlight well in their homes and on their ships.

In the cold hills of the Himalaya, many families keep cattle on the ground floor and live on the floor above. The warmth of the cattle's bodies rises into the rooms above, so the family stays warmer in winter. Many old houses in Rajasthan and in Chettinad in Tamil Nadu have an open courtyard in the middle. Hot air rises and leaves through the open top. Cooler air flows in through the rooms to take its place, so the house stays cooler. Vaastu is an old Indian way of planning houses with the Sun, the wind and the directions in mind. Some ideas in it, like the courtyard, can be tested. Others are beliefs that science has not tested. Sailors of the Indian Ocean used the monsoon winds to sail across the Arabian Sea. They came home when the wind turned. A sail needs no fuel. It needs only a clever knowledge of the winds.
- e.g. A house in the Spiti valley keeps its cattle below and its family above, so the cattle's warmth rises through the floor.
- e.g. A courtyard house in Chettinad stays cooler in the afternoon than a closed box of a house.
- e.g. A dhow with a triangular sail carries goods along the Gujarat coast with no engine.
### Living bodies are energy changers that never switch off · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living being takes in energy and gives it out again, every moment, even while still or asleep.

A sleeping child looks as still as a table. Inside, the heart beats, the lungs fill, and every cell is at work. A resting grown-up uses the energy of food at about 80 to 100 watts, like a bright old bulb left on. Most of it leaves the body as heat. In parts of Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, families keep their cattle on the ground floor and live upstairs. The warmth of the animals' bodies rises and heats the home. Plants are changers too. A leaf takes in light and stores it as the chemical energy of sugar. Many animals also use energy from outside their bodies. A kite, the bird of prey, soars on rising warm air without flapping. A crocodile lies in the sun to warm up.
- e.g. A sleeping baby is warm to touch: energy is leaving her body as heat.
- e.g. Cattle on the ground floor warm a mountain home.
- e.g. A leaf changes light into the stored energy of sugar.
- e.g. A kite, the bird of prey, circles upward on warm rising air and saves her own energy.
### Will everything one day reach one temperature? · *debated*
**Definition:** If heat moves only from warmer things to cooler things, then after a very long time all differences might vanish. Physicists do not agree that the universe will end this way.

In the Clicking Coin Test, the clicks stopped when the bottle matched the room. Hot tea does the same as it cools. Some physicists expect the whole universe to do this on a vast scale. Stars would burn out and heat would spread. In the end nothing would be warmer than anything else, and nothing could happen. They call this the heat death. It would take far longer than the Sun will shine. Other physicists point out that nobody yet knows what dark energy is, or how the universe will keep growing. So the end is still an open question. Nobody alive will see it either way, but the question comes straight from the coin on the bottle.
- e.g. The coin stops clicking when the bottle matches the room.
- e.g. Hot tea cools until it matches the room.
- e.g. A hot spoon in a cold bowl and the bowl reach one temperature.
- e.g. Stars give out heat and light into cold space, and they slowly use up their fuel.
- **Many physicists, using today's best measurements:** The universe keeps growing and stars run out of fuel. Heat spreads until no place is warmer than another. This is called the heat death.
- **Other physicists:** We do not yet know what dark energy is. The universe may change in ways we cannot yet predict, so its end is still open.

## Mostly agreed
- Energy is never made and never destroyed. It only moves from place to place and changes form. *How we know:* In every test since the 1840s, the energy counted before a change has matched the energy counted after it.
- No machine makes energy. Every machine changes energy that came from somewhere else. *How we know:* For hundreds of years people tried to build machines that run with nothing put in. Every one of them stopped.
- Everything above absolute zero holds heat energy, because its particles are moving. *How we know:* Gases push harder on their containers when warmed, and the amount fits the mathematics of moving particles. Tiny grains in water can be seen under a microscope being knocked about by particles.
- Heat energy moves by itself only from a warmer thing to a cooler thing. *How we know:* Nobody has ever seen a cup of tea grow hotter by cooling the room around it. In the Clicking Coin Test, the coin clicks only when the bottle is colder than the room.
- Almost all the energy we use on Earth came from the Sun. *How we know:* Food, wood, coal, oil, gas, wind and rivers can all be traced back to sunlight. The heat inside the Earth, the tides and nuclear power are the exceptions.
- A living body uses energy all the time, including in sleep. *How we know:* A resting person breathes out carbon dioxide and gives off heat without a break. Both can be measured in a closed room.

## How we found out
**Sadi Carnot, a French engineer, 1824.** In 1824 a young French engineer named Sadi Carnot asked why English steam engines did more work than French ones from the same coal. Many engineers believed that a better engine would one day turn all the heat of its fire into movement. Carnot thought about water mills. A mill works only when water falls from a higher place to a lower one. The work depends on the height of the fall. He wrote that heat is the same. It makes things move only as it passes from a hot place to a colder place. So even a perfect engine must let some heat go into the cooler air. He published this in a small book, Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire. Few people read it. Carnot died of cholera in 1832, aged 36. Later, William Thomson and Rudolf Clausius studied his book and built the science of heat from it.  
*What people believed before:* That a good enough engine could turn all the heat from its fuel into movement. Carnot showed that heat does work only as it falls from hot to cold, so some heat must always pass to a colder place.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A cold thing has no heat energy.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Cold things feel as if all the warmth has gone out of them. We say 'there is no heat in this room'.  
  Against: Ice at 0 degrees is still 273 degrees above absolute zero, and its particles are moving. A room-warm table feels neither hot nor cold only because it matches the room. An ice cube on the table melts, and the energy for that comes from the table and the air.
- **A fan makes the air colder.** (Most children, and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: You feel much cooler in front of a fan, at once.  
  Against: A thermometer in the fan's wind reads the same as one in still air. In the Fan and the Ice, the ice in the wind melts faster. The fan moves air, and moving air carries heat away from warm skin and towards cold ice.
- **Electric scooters and trains run on clean energy.** (Advertisements, and many people; *debated*)  
  For: An electric motor makes no smoke where it runs. City air is cleaner where vehicles are electric.  
  Against: The electricity has to be made somewhere. In India, about 7 units in every 10 come from burning coal, and the smoke comes out at the power station. Electric vehicles are as clean as the electricity that charges them. They get cleaner as more of it comes from the Sun, wind and water.
- **The warm sea is full of heat energy, so ships could run on it for nothing.** (Inventors, for more than a hundred years; *rejected*)  
  For: The sea really does hold a huge amount of heat energy. It seems wasteful not to use it.  
  Against: Heat energy does work only when it can move into something colder. A ship floating in warm water has nothing colder to send the heat to. Where a warm place lies next to a cold place, the heat can be used. At Kavaratti in Lakshadweep, a plant opened in 2005 uses warm surface water and cold deep water to make drinking water from the sea.
- **Only humans use energy from outside their own bodies.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Only humans burn fuel, build dams and make electricity.  
  Against: Kites, the birds of prey, and vultures climb on rising warm air. Albatrosses cross oceans on the wind. Lizards and crocodiles warm their bodies in sunshine. Termites build mounds that the wind and the Sun keep aired. Humans are one of many animals who borrow energy from the world around them.
- **A body at rest uses no energy.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: A sleeping person does not move, and seems to be doing nothing.  
  Against: A sleeping body breathes, pumps blood, keeps warm and repairs itself. A resting grown-up uses about 80 to 100 watts all night. That is why we wake up hungry.

## Roots
We have found that a still table holds energy, and that energy makes something happen only when it moves. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Clicking Coin Test, a room-warm bottle did nothing on the table. A freezer bottle made the coin click until the bottle was as warm as the room. The room-warm bottle held plenty of heat energy, and it gave away as much as it took in. So while the same amount of heat passes in each direction, nothing shows. A click comes only when more heat passes in one direction than in the other.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say that a thing which does nothing has no energy, as if energy belonged only to things that move. 'Still' only describes how a thing looks from the outside. Inside, the particles are always moving.  
*Evidence:* In the Clicking Coin Test, the room-warm bottle held heat energy and its coin stayed still. In the Fan and the Ice, moving air carried the room's heat to the ice, and the ice melted sooner. In both, things happened only where one thing was warmer than another.

- *who am i:* You are warm because your body turns food into heat every second. You hold heat energy like the table does, and you pass it on to every cooler thing you touch.
- *what is life:* A living being stays warmer than the room by using the energy of food. She keeps eating, so she keeps giving her warmth out into the world around her.
- *what is this world:* Everything holds energy. What happens in the world happens where there is a difference, such as warm next to cool or high next to low.

A table, a bottle, the air and your hand all hold heat energy of the same kind. When they are all at one temperature, they trade energy equally in both directions, so nothing shows. When something cold is put beside them, the trade is no longer equal, and then a coin clicks or ice melts. So the quiet and the click come from the same energy. A still room looks calm, but heat is passing between things in every direction all the time.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 16:** “致虛極，守靜篤。 / 萬物並作，吾以觀復。 / 夫物芸芸，各復歸其根。歸根曰靜，是謂復命。” Reach the greatest emptiness and hold to stillness. Everything rises and moves, and everything returns to its root, which is stillness. (A still table is quiet on the outside. Inside, its particles never stop shaking. Stillness is a balance of many small movements, and movement begins when the balance is broken.)
- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2:** “अन्नं ब्रह्मेति व्यजानात् / अन्नाद्ध्येव खल्विमानि भूतानि जायन्ते / अन्नेन जातानि जीवन्ति / अन्नं प्रयन्त्यभिसंविशन्तीति” Beings are born from food. By food they live. When they die, they enter into food. (An energy chain from rice to a rider's muscles to the heat of her body shows this. Plants store sunlight as food, and every body spends that food as movement and heat.)
- Science (settled): Albert Einstein showed in 1905 that mass is a store of energy. A 10 kilogram table holds about 900 million billion joules of energy in its mass, though nobody can take it out.
- Science (open): Why the early universe was arranged so that heat could flow from hot places to cold ones is still not fully explained.
- Other view, Most physicists: Energy is a number that stays the same through every change. The shaking of particles and the passing of heat between things follow the laws of motion. These laws already explain everything we see, so most physicists say that nothing more needs to be added.

- Ask: A room at one temperature looks like a place where nothing happens. Is it quiet, or is it busy?
- Ask: If everything in the universe reached one temperature, would anything ever happen again?
- Ask: Your warmth moves into every cooler thing you touch. Where does it end up?

## Story: The Can That Ticked
*The village, the milk society and all the people in this story are made up. Women's milk societies like this one are real, and there are many in Erode district in Tamil Nadu. The ticking lid is imagined. It follows the same science as the coin on the cold bottle in the Clicking Coin Test. A real milk can has a heavy lid that fits tightly, and it would not tick like this.*

The milk society of Kanjikovil opened at half past five, before the sun was up. It was one long room with a veranda, a weighing scale, and a cold room at the back with a thick grey door.

Thenmozhi was ten. Her mother, Selvi, tested the milk that the women of the village brought in. On holidays Thenmozhi came along to write the numbers in the book.

That April the nights were warm and sticky. Rasathi akka, who ran the society, had started keeping the washed measuring cans inside the cold room overnight. It was the only room with a lock. Each can was steel, as tall as a bucket, with a flat steel lid laid on top while the rim was still wet.

On the first morning of the holidays, Thenmozhi helped to carry the cans out to the veranda. She set the last one down by the scale. It was so cold that her fingers left prints in the mist on its side.

Tik.

She looked round. Nobody was near.

Tik. Tik.

The sound came from the can. The flat lid lifted at one edge, as thin as a fingernail, and dropped. Tik.

'It has started again,' said Valli paati, the watchwoman, from her stool. 'Every morning this month. There is something in those cans. I do not touch them until they go quiet.'

Kathir, who was seven and carried the small pails, backed away to the steps. 'It is a pei,' he whispered. 'A ghost. It lives in the cold room and rides out in the cans.'

Thenmozhi's neck prickled. Then she lifted the lid.

The can was empty. It was clean, cold steel, with three drops of water at the bottom. She put the lid back. For a moment nothing happened. Then: tik.

'A gecko,' said paati. 'A small one, under the rim.'

'There is no gecko,' said Thenmozhi. 'There is nothing in it.'

'Then nothing is knocking,' said Kathir, 'and that is worse.'

She sat down on the floor beside the can and counted. In the first minute the lid ticked twenty-two times. In the next minute, fourteen. Then nine. Then the ticks came so far apart that she lost count. By the time the first women arrived with their milk, the can was silent. She put her palm on it. It was no longer cold.

The next morning she was ready. There were six cans. She carried five out from the cold room. The sixth had been forgotten on the veranda all night, and she put it in the row with the others.

Five cans ticked. The sixth sat as still as a stone.

She swapped the lids. The warm can stayed silent under a cold can's lid. So it was not the lids.

She wiped one cold rim dry with her skirt and put the lid back. That can went quiet, and she felt a tiny breath of air on her wrist where the lid did not quite sit. She wet the rim again. Tik. So the water only closed the gap.

Then she wrapped both arms round one ticking can and held it against her. The lid chattered like teeth. Tik-tik-tik-tik. She let go, and it slowed.

'You are feeding it,' said Kathir from the steps.

'Yes,' said Thenmozhi slowly. 'I think I am.'

She went to find her mother. 'Amma, when air gets warm, does it need more room?'

'It does,' said Selvi, without looking up from her glass tubes. 'That is why a balloon swells in the sun.'

'This can is cold.'

'Then what is warm?'

Thenmozhi looked out at the veranda. The morning was warm. The floor was warm. Her own arms were warm. Everything was warm except the air shut inside those five cans.

She came back to Kathir. 'Listen. The can comes out full of cold air. The morning is warmer than that air. So warmth goes into the can, from the floor and the air and my arms. The air inside swells. It lifts the lid and a puff gets out. The lid drops. Tik. Then it happens again, until the inside is as warm as the outside.'

'And the can that stayed out all night?'

'It is already as warm as the morning. Nothing has anywhere to go.'

Kathir thought about it. 'So the morning is doing it.'

'The morning is doing it. The morning could do it every day. It only needs something cold to do it to.'

He was not sure. So she took an empty glass sample bottle from the shelf in the cold room. She stood it on the veranda floor, wet a one-rupee coin on her tongue, and laid it on the bottle's mouth.

The coin hopped. Tik.

Kathir crept closer. He put one finger on the bottle. He put his whole hand round it. Tik-tik-tik.

'I am feeding it,' he said, and he began to laugh.

Valli paati got up from her stool to see. She held the next cold bottle herself, in both hands, with a coin on top, and he counted aloud until it stopped.

After that, nobody at the society waited for the cans to go quiet. On hot mornings the children raced to carry them out first. Whoever set a can down would stand with one hand on the cold steel and listen to the warm morning going in.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Thenmozhi tests one thing at a time. The warm can never ticks, so the ticking needs cold. Swapping lids changes nothing, so it is not the lids. Hugging the can speeds the ticking, so warmth from outside drives it. She works out that the warm morning holds energy all the time. This energy moves, and makes something happen, only where it meets something colder. The can left out all night is as warm as the morning, so nothing moves and nothing happens.

- Ask: Why did the cans from the cold room tick, when the can left outside all night did not?
- Ask: Why did the ticking get faster when Thenmozhi wrapped her arms round the can?
- Ask: Why did every can go quiet after a few minutes?
- Ask: Thenmozhi swapped the lids and dried one rim. What did each test tell her?
- Ask: The morning was just as warm on the days before the cans were kept in the cold room. Why did nothing tick then?
- Ask: Where else at home does something warm meet something cold? What happens there?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Clicking Coin Test: does a cold bottle make a coin click? (35 min)
- **question:** A bottle at room temperature does nothing on a table. Does a cold bottle make a coin click on the same table? Does a colder bottle make it click more?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the coin click on a room-warm bottle, a fridge-cold bottle and a freezer-cold bottle? Which will click most? Write your guesses, and the reason for each.
- **fair test:** Use the same coin, wet in the same way each time. Use bottles of the same size, and stand each one on the same spot of the same table. Never hold a bottle in your hands during a test. Count the clicks for the same time for every bottle. Change only one thing: how cold the bottle starts.
- **safety:** Cold glass can slip, so hold it with a cloth. Stand the bottle on a flat table and never on a slope. Never pour hot water on a cold bottle, because the glass can crack. Throw away any bottle with a chipped mouth.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Bottle | How it started | Clicks in the first 30 seconds | Clicks in 2 minutes | Time of the last click

  *Parent says:* A coin will sit on the mouth of a bottle on our table. Will it move? Will the answer change for a bottle from the fridge or the freezer? Why?

## Worksheets
### Energy at work: changers, sources and the table (core) (core, 18 questions)
1. (mcq) Which sentence about energy is true?  
   Options: Energy makes things happen / Energy is a liquid in wires / Energy is only in moving things / Energy is used up by a bulb  
   **Answer:** Energy makes things happen. *Why:* Energy is what is needed to make anything happen. It makes things move, light up, make a sound, get hotter or cooler. It is not a liquid and it is not used up. A bulb changes electrical energy into light and heat.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a machine makes new energy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A machine only changes energy from one form to another. A fan takes in electrical energy and gives out movement and a little heat.
3. (match) Match each thing to the energy that goes into it.  
   **Answer:** Ceiling fan → electrical energy; Gas stove → chemical energy of cooking gas; Solar panel → sunlight; Wind turbine → movement of air; Leg muscles → chemical energy of food. *Why:* Each machine takes in one form of energy. The fan takes electrical energy. The stove takes the chemical energy of its gas. A solar panel takes light. The wind turbine takes movement of air. Leg muscles take the chemical energy of food.
4. (sort) Sort these sources of energy by how quickly they are renewed.  
   **Answer:** sunlight → Renewed every day; coal → Took millions of years to form; wind → Renewed every day; petrol → Took millions of years to form; a flowing river → Renewed every day; cooking gas → Took millions of years to form. *Why:* Sunlight, wind and flowing river water are renewed every day by the Sun. Coal, petrol and cooking gas took millions of years to form, so we use them far faster than they come back.
5. (order) Put these in order, starting from the Sun.  
   **Answer:** ['Sunlight on a paddy field', 'A plate of rice', "A rider's leg muscles", 'The pedals of a cycle turn']. *Why:* Most chains start at the Sun. Sunlight lets the paddy grow, the rice feeds the rider, and the rider's muscles turn the pedals.
6. (fill) A still table has heat energy, because its ____ never stop shaking.  
   **Answer:** ['particles']. *Why:* The tiny particles that the wood is made of keep shaking all the time. That shaking is heat energy. 'Atoms' and 'molecules' are also right.

   Options: Heat energy / Electrical energy flowing through it / Chemical energy in the wood / Energy in its mass

## Applications
- Power stations burn coal or use falling water or wind to turn turbines. The turbines turn generators, and wires carry the electricity to homes, hospitals and trains.
- A solar water heater on a roof changes sunlight to heat. It saves cooking gas or electricity that a geyser would use.
- A fridge and an air conditioner use electricity to move heat out of a cool place. That is why the back of a fridge feels warm.
- A matka cools water with no electricity. Water seeps through the clay and dries on the outside, and drying carries heat away.
- Energy ratings on fans, fridges and air conditioners tell you how much electricity each one uses for the same job.
- A power cut stops pumps, lifts and fans. Hospitals keep batteries and diesel generators ready for such hours.
- A cattle shed on the ground floor of a mountain house warms the rooms above with the heat of the animals' bodies.

## Open questions
- **What is the energy of empty space, which scientists call dark energy?** Measurements of far-away exploding stars show the universe growing faster and faster. Nobody knows what is behind it.
- **Why was the early universe arranged so that heat flows from hot to cold?** The rule that heat flows from hot to cold depends on the early universe having been very orderly. Nobody knows why it was.
- **Can we store the Sun's energy cheaply enough to light every home all night?** Batteries are improving fast. Nobody yet knows which kind of storage will be cheap and clean enough for everyone.
- **Will the universe one day reach one temperature, so that nothing can happen?** Some physicists expect this, a very long time from now. Others think the picture may change as we learn more about dark energy.
- **How much of the energy in a meal does each body really use?** People differ in how well they digest food and in how much heat they give out. Measuring it exactly for one person is hard.

## Connected maths
- A freezer bottle clicked 14, 8, 4 and 2 times in four half-minutes. How many clicks in all, and how many in the first minute? **28 clicks in all. 22 clicks in the first minute.**. - A town uses 1,000 units of electricity in an hour, and 7 of every 10 units come from coal. How many units come from coal? **700 units.**. Seven out of every 10 units means 7 tenths of 1,000. One tenth of 1,000 is 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100, and 7 tenths is 100 × 7 = 700 units. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A coal power station takes in 300 units of energy from burning coal and sends out 100 units as electricity. What fraction becomes electricity, and where does the rest go? **One third. The other two thirds leave as heat in the air and water.**. 100 ÷ 300 = 1 ÷ 3. The energy that does not become electricity is 300 − 100 = 200 units, which spreads out as heat. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- Burning a 10 kilogram table would give out about 160 million joules. One unit of electricity is 3.6 million joules. About how many units is that? **About 44 units.**. 160 ÷ 3.6 is about 44.4. So the wood of a table holds as much energy as about 44 units of electricity. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- A resting grown-up gives out about 90 watts of heat. How many watt-hours is that in one night of 8 hours? How many 9 watt LED bulbs is that? **720 watt-hours. That is the same as ten 9 watt LED bulbs.**. 90 watts for 8 hours is 90 × 8 = 720 watt-hours. Each 9 watt bulb gives out 9 watts. 90 ÷ 9 = 10, so a resting grown-up gives out as much heat as 10 such bulbs. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Name three things that energy can do. → Move things, light up, make sound, heat or cool things, and do work.
- Does a machine make energy? → No. It only changes energy from one form to another.
- A fan takes in electrical energy. What comes out? → Movement of air, with a little sound and heat.
- Name a source of energy that comes from the Sun. → Food, wind, coal, flowing water or sunlight itself.
- Which way does heat move by itself? → From a warmer thing to a cooler thing.
- Does a still table have energy? → Yes. It has heat energy, chemical energy in the wood, energy of height and energy in its mass.
- Does a fan make the air colder? → No. It moves the air, and moving air carries heat away faster.
- Why must your hands be dry before you touch a switch? → Wet skin lets electricity pass into the body more easily.
