# Sources of sound: shaking, blowing, popping and rubbing
`sci.sound.sources-of-sound.L4`

**Summary.** Tap a glass bottle that has water in it, and more water gives a lower note. Blow across the same bottle, and more water gives a higher note. Same bottle, same water, opposite order. Your child finds out why, by asking what actually shakes in each case. Then comes the sound of a damp finger squeaking on glass, a clap that has no steady note, and a whispered 'ee' and 'oo' that can still be told apart with no buzz in the throat at all. Every sound starts with air being pushed, but there are four different ways to push it. A note belongs partly to what makes it and partly to the space that shapes it. Nobody yet knows exactly why some pairs of notes sound sweet to nearly everyone.

**Intro.** *Line up three glass bottles with different amounts of water. Tap each one with a spoon and listen. The fullest bottle gives the lowest note. Now blow gently across the top of each bottle, like blowing across a flute. Listen again. Is the fullest bottle still the lowest?* It is not. The order flips over. The same bottle makes a low note when you tap it and a high note when you blow across it. In this unit you find out what shakes in each case, and why that matters. You also meet three other ways to make a sound, by popping, by rubbing and by whispering, and you test them with things from your kitchen and your window.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name four different ways a sound can start: a solid thing shaking, air shaking in a space, a sudden pop and rubbing that sticks and slips.
1. I can explain that pitch is counted in hertz, the number of vibrations each second, and that halving the length of a string or a pipe makes the note an octave higher.
2. I can explain why a plucked string sounds like one note but really holds many, and what overtones and timbre are.
3. I can test with bottles how tapping and blowing give opposite orders of notes, and explain it by what is shaking.
4. I can explain how wind or breath across an edge makes a whistle, a flute note or the hum of a wire.
5. I can explain that a pop is a sudden push of air, and that a squeak comes from a surface that sticks and slips again and again.
6. I can explain that a voice is a buzz from the vocal folds, shaped by the throat and mouth, and show that vowels are made by the shaping.
7. I can say what scientists still argue about, such as why some notes sound sweet together.

## Terms
- **Hertz**: The unit for counting vibrations. One hertz is one vibration each second. It is written Hz. A note of 440 hertz vibrates 440 times each second. *e.g.* A child's speaking voice is often between 250 and 400 hertz. A grown-up man's is often about 100 to 120 hertz.
- **Octave**: The gap between a note and the note that vibrates exactly twice as fast. The two notes sound like the same note, one low and one high. *e.g.* In Indian music, the Sa in the middle and the upper Sa are one octave apart.
- **Fundamental**: The lowest and usually the strongest vibration in a sound. It sets the pitch that you hear. *e.g.* When you pluck a string, the vibration of the whole string is the fundamental.
- **Overtone**: A higher vibration that sounds at the same time as the fundamental. A string vibrates in halves, thirds and quarters at once, and each way makes an overtone. *e.g.* A plucked string rings with its fundamental and with overtones above it, but you hear them all as one note.
- **Timbre**: The quality of a sound, which tells a flute from a violin even when both play the same note. It comes from which overtones are strong and which are weak. Say it TAM-ber. *e.g.* A bansuri and a veena can play the same note, and you can still tell which is which.
- **Resonance**: When something vibrates strongly because the vibration it is given matches its own natural pitch. Air in a bottle has resonance at some pitches and not at others. *e.g.* Sing slowly up a scale near the mouth of an empty bottle. At one note, the bottle suddenly booms.
- **Air column**: The air inside a pipe, bottle or tube, as a standing column of air. A longer air column gives a lower note. A shorter one gives a higher note. *e.g.* In a bottle with water in it, the air column is the part from the water to the top.
- **Edge tone**: A tone made when a stream of air hits a sharp edge. The air swirls to one side, then the other, and each swirl pushes the air around it. *e.g.* A whistle, a bansuri and a bottle you blow across all make edge tones.
- **Vortex**: A spinning swirl in air or water, like the little whirl behind a spoon moved through tea. *e.g.* When wind blows past a wire, it forms vortexes behind the wire, and the wire can hum.
- **Stick-slip**: Two surfaces that rub, grip, then slip, then grip again, many times each second. It makes a squeak or a screech. *e.g.* A damp finger moved across a window sticks and slips, and squeaks.
- **Pressure pulse**: A short, sudden push in the air that spreads out. A pop or a clap is one pressure pulse, not a long steady vibration. *e.g.* A balloon bursting makes a pressure pulse that you hear as a bang.
- **Shock wave**: A very strong pressure pulse that moves faster than sound. It is heard as a crack or a boom. *e.g.* Thunder is a shock wave from the sudden heating of the air by lightning.
- **Vocal tract**: The space above your vocal folds: the throat, the mouth and the nose. It shapes the buzz from your vocal folds into the sounds of speech. *e.g.* When you change your mouth from 'ee' to 'oo', you change the shape of your vocal tract.
- **Vowel**: A speech sound made with the mouth open, like 'ee', 'aa' or 'oo'. Each vowel has its own mouth shape, and the shape decides which overtones are strong. *e.g.* Sing one note and change the mouth from 'ee' to 'oo'. The note stays the same and the vowel changes.
- **Nodal line**: A line on a vibrating plate or skin that stays still while the parts on either side move. Sand sprinkled on the plate collects along these lines. *e.g.* Sand on a steel plate that is rung with a violin bow gathers into lines and shapes.
- **Noise**: A sound that mixes many pitches with no steady note. A hiss, a clap and the crash of waves are noise. *e.g.* Air blown out of a tyre makes a hiss. A tuning fork makes a pure note.

## Explanations
### Four ways to start a sound · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound starts when something pushes on the air. There are four common ways: a solid thing shakes, air shakes inside a space, the air gets a sudden push, or two surfaces rub, stick and slip.

In the earlier unit you learned that every sound comes from something vibrating. That is true, and it is the most important idea. But it hides some differences. A shaking solid thing is the first way. A string, a drum skin, a bell, a steel plate or your vocal folds all push the air as they move. The second way is air shaking in a space. In a bansuri, a bottle or a conch, a stream of air or a tap sets the air inside shaking, and the shape of the space decides the note. The third way is a sudden push. A clap, a balloon bursting, a firecracker and thunder do not vibrate steadily. They squeeze the air once, hard, and the squeeze spreads out as a pop. The fourth way is rubbing. A damp finger on glass, chalk on a board, a squeaky door hinge and a cricket's wing all rub two surfaces together. They grip, slip, grip and slip again, hundreds or thousands of times each second, and that is a vibration too. In every case, something pushes on the air in a pattern, and the air carries the pattern to your ear. The four ways are not four different kinds of sound. They are four different ways to give the air a push. Real instruments often mix them. A bansuri uses air shaking in a space, and the player's breath also makes a hiss.
- e.g. A sitar string is a shaking solid. A bansuri is air shaking in a space. A clap is a sudden push. A squeaky chair is stick-slip.
- e.g. A cricket rubs a rough edge on one wing across a row of ridges on the other, like running a stick along a comb.
- e.g. A violin mixes the first and fourth ways. The bow sticks and slips on the string, and the string shakes.
### Pitch has a number: hertz · *defined*
**Definition:** Pitch is how high or low a note sounds. We count it in hertz, which is the number of vibrations each second. A higher pitch means more vibrations each second.

A string sounds high when it vibrates fast and low when it vibrates slowly. We can count how fast. One vibration each second is one hertz, written 1 Hz. The unit is named after Heinrich Hertz, a German scientist who first made radio waves in a lab in the 1880s. People can hear from about 20 hertz to about 20,000 hertz. The A that orchestras tune to is 440 hertz, which means a string or a column of air shakes 440 times every second. Voices are slower than that. A grown-up man's speaking voice is often about 100 to 120 hertz, a grown-up woman's about 200, and a child's about 250 to 400. The vocal folds open and close that many times each second. A honeybee's wings beat about 230 times each second, which makes her buzz. A mosquito's wings beat roughly 400 times or more each second, and that is why a mosquito whines higher than a bee. These numbers are too fast to count by eye. In the 1630s, a French priest and scientist, Marin Mersenne, found a way. He stretched a very long string until it vibrated slowly enough to count. He found rules for how the count changes with length, and used the rules to work out how fast short strings shake. It was one of the first times anyone counted a sound.
- e.g. A singer's Sa may be about 130 hertz for a man and about 260 hertz for a woman, and the tanpura is tuned to match it.
- e.g. A mosquito whines near your ear because her wings beat several hundred times each second. A slower beat would sound like a buzz or a hum.
- e.g. At 20 hertz, a sound is a rumble that you feel as much as hear. At 15,000 hertz, it is a thin hiss that many grown-ups can no longer hear.
### Shorter means higher, in exact ratios · *defined*
**Definition:** If you halve the length of a string or of an air column, the note goes up by one octave, which is twice as many vibrations each second. Simple ratios of length give the notes that blend best.

Take a stretched string that gives the note Sa. Press it at its middle, so that only half of it can shake. It now vibrates twice as fast and gives the Sa one octave higher. A string two thirds as long vibrates one and a half times as fast and gives Pa. Sa and Pa are called a fifth in the Western system, and in Indian music theory they are samvadi, notes that agree. This is why the tanpura is tuned to Pa and Sa. The same rule works for pipes. The air in a pipe that is closed at one end vibrates at about the speed of sound divided by four times the pipe's length. Sound moves about 343 metres each second in warm air. So a closed pipe 17 centimetres long gives about 500 hertz, and one 34 centimetres long gives about 250 hertz, which is one octave lower. The rule for strings was written down by Mersenne in 1636. The rules say that vibration speed goes up when the string is shorter, tighter or thinner. It goes down when the string is longer, looser or thicker. There was an old story that Pythagoras found the ratios by listening to a blacksmith's hammers of different weights. Galileo pointed out in 1638 that the story cannot be true, because hammers of those weights do not give those notes. The ratios were real. How they were found is a legend.
- e.g. A veena has frets that shorten the string. Stopping it halfway gives the octave above the open string.
- e.g. A bansuri hole near the end gives a high note, because the air column is short. All the holes covered gives the lowest note.
- e.g. If a closed bottle air column is 20 centimetres long and another is 10 centimetres, the 10 centimetre one gives a note an octave higher.
### A note is many notes: overtones and timbre · *defined*
**Definition:** A plucked string or a played pipe vibrates in many ways at once. The slowest is the fundamental, and the faster ones are overtones. The mix of overtones gives a sound its timbre.

Pluck a string and it seems to make one note. But the string does not only swing as one big loop. It also swings in two halves, in three thirds, in four quarters and more, all at the same moment. Each way of swinging makes its own tone. The slowest is the fundamental, and it sets the pitch you hear. The faster ones are overtones, and they are quieter. In 1636 Marin Mersenne was the first to report that he could hear the higher tones ringing above a plucked string. Your ear and brain mix them into one note. How strong each overtone is changes the character of the sound. This is called timbre. A flute has few strong overtones and sounds smooth. A sitar has many and sounds rich and buzzy. The tanpura has a special curved bridge, with a thread slipped under each string. The string brushes against the bridge as it swings, and this adds many strong overtones. The Indian scientist C. V. Raman studied this in the 1920s, and it gives the tanpura its shimmering sound. In 1822 the French mathematician Joseph Fourier showed that any repeating wave can be built from simple waves added together. In 1863 the German scientist Hermann von Helmholtz used this to show that timbre is the mix of overtones. Plates and skins have overtones too. In 1787 the German physicist Ernst Chladni bowed a metal plate with sand on it. The sand jumped away from the parts that shook and gathered along lines that stayed still. Each note gave a different pattern. Napoleon saw this, and the French Academy offered a prize for a mathematical explanation. The self-taught mathematician Sophie Germain entered three times and won in 1816.
- e.g. A bansuri and a violin can play the same Sa, and you can still tell them apart. Their overtones are different.
- e.g. Touch a string lightly at its middle and pluck it. You hear a note one octave higher with a pure, bell-like sound, because the touch stops the fundamental and leaves the second overtone.
- e.g. Sand on a bowed plate gathers into star-like patterns. Each pattern shows one way the plate can vibrate.
### Air in a bottle: why tapping and blowing give opposite orders · *defined*
**Definition:** When you tap a bottle, the glass and the water shake. When you blow across it, the air inside shakes. More water makes the first note lower and the second note higher.

Tap a glass bottle with a spoon. The glass wall shakes, and the water inside shakes with it. More water means more weight to shake, so the note is lower. Now blow across the top. What shakes is the air in the bottle. A bottle with a narrow neck acts like a spring of air with a plug of air in the neck. Less air inside means a stiffer spring, so the note is higher. More water means less air, so a fuller bottle gives a higher note when you blow. In a wider pipe the rule is easier: the air column vibrates at about the speed of sound divided by four times its length, so a shorter air column gives a higher note. The same bottle and the same water give opposite orders because different things are shaking. Helmholtz used glass bottles of different sizes in the 1860s to pick out single overtones from a complex sound. They are still called Helmholtz resonators. They also explain why a conch shell sounds like the sea. The shell is a cavity, and noise from the room makes the air inside resonate, at the pitch that fits the shell. The sound is not the sea at all. A bigger shell gives a deeper sound.
- e.g. A big steel drum booms low and a small tin rings high, when blown across or tapped, because bigger spaces have lower natural notes.
- e.g. Put your ear to a steel tumbler or a katori, and you hear a faint rushing. It is the noise of the room, with the pitch that fits the space inside.
- e.g. A jug filling up from a tap rises in pitch as the air above the water gets shorter. You can tell how full it is by listening.
### Sound from moving air: whistles, flutes and singing wires · *defined*
**Definition:** When a stream of air hits a sharp edge, it swirls from side to side, and the swirls make a tone. This is called an edge tone. A flute or a whistle makes its note by joining an edge tone to an air column.

Blow a thin stream of air across a sharp edge, such as the rim of a bottle or the lip hole of a bansuri. The stream does not flow smoothly. It flips to one side of the edge, then the other, and makes little swirls called vortexes. Each flip pushes the air around it. The flips repeat many times a second, and that is a tone. Blowing faster makes the flips faster, so the pitch rises. In a flute or a bottle, the edge tone is joined to the air column. The air column picks out a few pitches where it resonates, and the edge tone locks onto one of them. That is why you can change a flute note by blowing harder: the edge tone jumps to a higher resonance. Wires hum in the wind in the same way. In 1878 the Czech physicist Vincenc Strouhal studied this and found that the pitch depends on the wind speed and the thickness of the wire. Thin wires in a fast wind give a higher hum. Bamboo clumps, electric wires, tall chimneys and railings can all sing in the wind. The same swirls explain why a cut bamboo or a hollow pole sometimes moans on a windy night. The wind is not making a song. It is flipping from side to side at an edge, and the space inside is picking out one note.
- e.g. A bansuri player blows a thin stream of air against the sharp edge of the blowing hole. Changing how hard they blow can move the note up by a whole octave.
- e.g. Overhead wires can hum on a windy day. The thicker the wire, the lower the hum.
- e.g. A cut bamboo pole with a hole in it can moan on a windy night, because the air inside is excited by the wind blowing over the edge.
### Pops, cracks and booms: a sound without a steady vibration · *defined*
**Definition:** A pop is one sudden push on the air, a pressure pulse. It spreads as a short bang that mixes many pitches, and not as a steady note.

A clap squeezes the air between your palms, and the squeezed air bursts out through the gaps. That one push is the clap. A balloon bursts and the stretched rubber snaps back in a fraction of a second. A firecracker releases a lot of gas at once. In each case the push is short and has no steady pitch. Scientists call such a sound noise, because it holds many pitches mixed together. If you cup your hands, the clap has a hollow pop with a pitch, because the cupped space is a small resonator. Thunder is a bigger pop. Lightning heats the air in its path to tens of thousands of degrees in a split second. The air expands faster than sound can move away, and this makes a shock wave. The shock wave spreads out and slows into the rumble that we hear. Light travels almost instantly, but sound moves about a kilometre in three seconds, so you can count the seconds between the flash and the thunder to find how far away the lightning was. A whip crack is also a shock wave. A loop runs along the whip and speeds up, until the tip moves faster than the speed of sound, and what you hear is a small boom. Scientists worked this out with high-speed photographs and, in 2002, a computer model. In water, the snapping shrimp closes a claw so fast that a bubble forms and then collapses with a loud bang. The bang can stun small fish. In 2001 scientists found that the collapsing bubble also gives off a tiny flash of light.
- e.g. Cupped hands give a lower, hollow clap than flat hands, because the cupped space picks out a pitch.
- e.g. Count the seconds between a lightning flash and the thunder, and divide by three to get the distance in kilometres.
- e.g. Popping a paper bag is a short push of air, and a bigger bag gives a deeper pop.
### Squeaks and screeches: sticking and slipping · *defined*
**Definition:** A squeak happens when two surfaces rub, stick, slip, and stick again, over and over, very fast. The jerks make the surfaces vibrate, and they push the air.

Drag your finger across a window. If the finger is dry, it drags and makes almost no sound. If it is very wet, it glides and makes no sound either. But if the finger is just a little damp, it squeaks. Here is why. The finger grips the glass, and your hand keeps pushing, so the skin stretches a little. When the push is strong enough, the finger slips forward. Then it grips again. It does this hundreds or thousands of times each second. Each jerk is a tiny vibration. A squeak needs the grip to be strong enough to stick and weak enough to slip. This middle zone of friction is why oil stops a squeaky hinge: oil makes the grip too weak to stick. A violin bow works the same way. Rosin, a sticky powder, makes the bow grip the string, pull it sideways, and then let go, again and again. The string vibrates at its own natural pitch, because it is the string's pitch that decides when it slips. A cricket makes sound by rubbing a rough edge on one wing across a row of ridges on the other wing, like running a nail along a comb. Scientists have found that crickets chirp faster when the weather is warmer. Cicadas make their loud calls with a ribbed plate on their body that clicks in and out, like pressing the lid of a tin.
- e.g. A door hinge squeaks until a drop of oil makes it slide smoothly.
- e.g. Chalk on a board can screech, because it sticks and slips along the board.
- e.g. A wet finger on the rim of a glass can make the glass sing, because the finger sticks and slips at the glass's own natural pitch.
### A voice is a buzz shaped by a space · *defined*
**Definition:** Your voice starts as a buzz from the vocal folds, which holds many overtones. The throat, mouth and nose shape the buzz by making some overtones louder than others. The shaping makes the vowels.

Your vocal folds are two soft flaps in your throat. Air from your lungs pushes them apart and they snap shut again, over and over. That makes a buzz of about 100 to 400 times each second, with many strong overtones. The buzz alone sounds like a raw, nasal hum. Then your throat, mouth and nose act like a bottle. They have natural pitches, and they make the overtones near those pitches louder. When you change the shape of your mouth, you change which overtones are boosted. This is how you make different vowels. 'Ee' has a narrow space at the front of the mouth, 'oo' has a round one. Try singing one note and changing the vowel slowly. The pitch stays the same, but the vowel changes. This is called the source and filter idea. It was worked out by the Swedish scientist Gunnar Fant in 1960. A whisper shows it clearly. When you whisper, the vocal folds do not buzz. Instead, air rushes through a narrow gap in your throat and makes a hiss. The hiss has no pitch, but your mouth shapes it, so a listener can still hear 'ee' and 'oo'. Singers in Tuva, in Siberia, use the same idea. They shape their mouth so tightly that one overtone is picked out of the buzz and sounds like a separate whistle above the low drone. Songbirds have a voice box deep in their chest, with two sides. Each side can make its own sound, so a bird can sing two notes at once.
- e.g. Sing one steady note and change from 'ee' to 'aa' to 'oo'. The pitch stays the same and the vowel changes.
- e.g. Put your fingers on your throat. Hum, and you feel a buzz. Whisper, and you feel nothing, yet you can still be understood.
- e.g. Pinch your nose and say 'mama'. The nose is part of the filter, so the sound changes.
### Why do some pairs of notes sound sweet? · *debated*
Some pairs of notes sound smooth and pleasing together, and some sound rough and tense. Musicians in many places have noticed this, and the simplest ratios, like 2 to 1 for the octave and 3 to 2 for Sa and Pa, tend to sound sweet. But scientists do not agree on why. The answer may be in the physics of the sound, in the way the ear works, or in what we have learned from the music around us.
- e.g. Sa and Pa played together on a tanpura sound very settled. Sa and a note just a little above it sound rough and buzzy.
- e.g. Two strings that are slightly out of tune with each other make a slow wobble in loudness, called beats.
- **Pythagoras and his followers in ancient Greece, as reported by later writers:** Notes sound sweet when their string lengths make simple ratios, like 2 to 1 and 3 to 2. The world is built on numbers, and the sweetness is a sign of this.
- **Hermann von Helmholtz, in 1863:** Two notes close together make beats, a fast wobble that sounds rough. Notes sound sweet when their overtones do not beat against each other. The ear is the judge.
- **Josh McDermott and his team, in 2016:** They tested the Tsimane people of Bolivia, who live far from Western music, and found that they did not prefer sweet pairs to rough pairs as Western listeners do. This suggests that learning from the music we hear plays a big part.
- **Indian music theory, for example in the Natyashastra of Bharata:** Certain pairs of notes, such as Sa and Pa, are called samvadi, which means they agree. The theory explains how to build a scale from such pairs and from small steps called shrutis.

## Mostly agreed
- Pitch is the number of vibrations each second, counted in hertz. A higher pitch is a faster vibration. *How we know:* Vibrations can be counted with a stroboscope, with a microphone and a screen, and in slow strings by eye. Whatever the source, the pitch we hear matches the count.
- Halving the length of a string or an air column makes the note one octave higher. A string or pipe vibrates faster when shorter, tighter or thinner. *How we know:* Marin Mersenne wrote down these rules in 1636. Instrument makers in every part of the world use them, and they match the frequencies measured by modern instruments.
- A plucked string, a played pipe and a plate vibrate in many ways at once. The mix of these overtones gives the sound its timbre. *How we know:* Hermann von Helmholtz picked out overtones with bottle resonators in 1863. Modern spectrum analysers show them as separate peaks for every instrument.
- Air moving past an edge can make a tone, and the space it blows into picks the note. A pop is one sudden push of air with no steady pitch. *How we know:* A bottle, a flute and a whistle all work this way. A pop recorded on a screen shows one sharp spike and a jumble of pitches, not a regular wave.
- A squeak comes from stick-slip. Two surfaces grip, slip and grip again many times each second, and the squeak stops if the grip is too strong or too weak. *How we know:* A finger on glass squeaks only when damp, and not when dry or wet. High-speed cameras show surfaces moving in tiny jumps while a squeak sounds.
- A voice is a buzz from the vocal folds that is shaped by the throat, mouth and nose. A whisper has no buzz, yet its vowels can be heard. *How we know:* Gunnar Fant published this source and filter picture in 1960. X-ray and scanner pictures show the mouth taking the shape of each vowel.

## How we found out
**Marin Mersenne, a French priest and scientist (1588 to 1648), and Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist (1564 to 1642), The 1630s.** More than 2,300 years ago, Greek thinkers such as Archytas of Tarentum taught that high notes travel faster than low notes. It seemed right. A high note feels quick and sharp, and a low note feels slow and heavy. In the 1630s two men tested it. In France, a priest and scientist called Marin Mersenne listened to sounds from far away. High notes and low notes reached him at the same moment, so they travel at the same speed. He also stretched a very long string until it was slow enough to count its swings. He found rules for how length changes the count, and used the rules to work out how fast short strings swing. In Italy, Galileo scraped a sharp chisel over a brass plate and found fine lines scratched into it. Close lines made a high squeal and wide lines made a low one. A high note is not a fast traveller. It is a fast vibration.  
*What people believed before:* Greek thinkers, including Archytas, taught that high notes travel faster than low notes. Mersenne and Galileo showed that all notes travel at the same speed, and that pitch is the number of vibrations each second.

## Challenging perspectives
- **High notes travel faster than low notes.** (Greek thinkers such as Archytas of Tarentum, about 400 BCE, and others after him; *rejected*)  
  For: A high note feels quick and sharp. A low note feels slow and heavy. It seems that a quick feeling should travel quickly.  
  Against: In the 1630s Marin Mersenne listened to sounds across a distance and reported that high and low notes arrive at the same moment. Pitch is the number of vibrations each second, and not the speed of travel.
- **Every sound is a steady vibration with one pitch.** (A natural thing to think after learning that sound is vibration; *rejected*)  
  For: Tuning forks, strings and flutes all make a steady note that we can hum along with.  
  Against: A clap, a pop, a hiss and a crash are not steady. They are one sudden push or a jumble of many pitches. Even a flute note holds many overtones at once.
- **A whisper is just a softer version of your normal voice.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: A whisper is quiet and uses the same mouth and words as speech.  
  Against: When you whisper, your vocal folds do not buzz. Air rushes through a gap in the throat and hisses. You can feel the difference by touching your throat. The mouth still shapes the hiss into vowels.
- **A flute's wall material, such as silver or wood, changes the tone of the note.** (Many flute players and makers; *debated*)  
  For: Players say they can feel and hear a difference between a silver flute and a gold one or a wooden one.  
  Against: In 1971 the physicist John Coltman made flutes with walls of different materials and found that listeners could not reliably tell them apart. The shape of the air column matters most. Some players and makers still disagree.
- **Notes sound sweet together because simple ratios are pleasing to everyone, everywhere.** (Followers of Pythagoras and many later writers; *debated*)  
  For: Octaves and fifths sound settled in music from many places, and they match simple ratios.  
  Against: In 2016 a team led by Josh McDermott found that the Tsimane people of Bolivia, who live far from Western music, did not prefer sweet pairs of notes to rough ones. What counts as sweet may be partly learned.

## Roots
We have found four ways to start a sound, and seen that the space around a source shapes the sound as much as the source does. Now we ask what that says about where a sound belongs, and about what is empty.

**Deep truth.** In the whisper test, listeners still told 'ee' from 'oo' with no buzz from the throat. Only a hiss left the throat, and the mouth shaped it. In the bottle test, tapping and blowing gave opposite orders of notes. So a sound belongs to what starts it and to the space that shapes it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a sound comes from its source: the drum, the string or the throat. But the same source sounds different when the space around it changes. So the source alone does not fix the sound.  
*Evidence:* The same bottle and the same water gave opposite orders of notes, depending on whether it was tapped or blown across. In a whisper, the vowels came from the mouth's shape alone.

- *who am i:* Your voice is a buzz in your throat shaped by the space in your head. Your voice belongs to your whole body, and no part of it makes it alone.
- *what is life:* A songbird has a voice box with two sides and sings two notes at once. A cricket rubs his wings. A snapping shrimp pops a bubble. Living beings make sounds in many ways, and use the shapes of their bodies to do it.
- *what is this world:* A jar, a throat and a flute sing with the same air. The note comes from how the air is pushed and how the empty space is shaped.

A string, a skin, a stream of air and a squeeze all push on the air. The air is the same air in every case. What changes is how it is pushed and what empty space it is in. A bottle, a throat, a hall and a flute are all emptiness with a shape, and the shape picks out the notes. So the empty part of a thing is as much a part of its sound as the solid part. Where one stops and the other begins is hard to say.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Clay is shaped into a pot, and it is the empty space inside that makes the pot useful. We profit from what is there, and we use what is not there. (A clay jar moans only because of the air inside it. The jar gives the shape, and the emptiness gives the note.)
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.7:** When a drum is beaten, we cannot grasp the sounds as they spread out. But if we grasp the drum or the person who beats it, we have grasped the sound. The text gives the same teaching for a conch and a vina. (This unit finds that a sound cannot be understood apart from what makes it and the space that shapes it. The Upanishad points to the same unity.)
- **Taittiriya Upanishad 1.2:** This is the opening of an old Indian study of the sounds of speech. It names the parts of a spoken sound: the letter, the pitch, the length, the strength, the evenness and the joining. (Indian teachers studied for centuries how the mouth shapes speech. Today's source and filter picture of the voice finds the same two parts.)
- Science (settled): The voice has a source, the buzz of the vocal folds, and a filter, the shape of the throat, mouth and nose. A whisper uses a hiss as its source.
- Science (debated): Why some pairs of notes sound sweet to nearly everyone is not settled. Physics, the ear and learning may all play a part.
- Other view, Many physicists: A sound is a pattern of pressure in the air, wherever it is. What started it and what shaped it are its history, and the pattern itself is all there is. Asking where a sound belongs does not add anything to what a microphone measures.
- Practice, Hum and shape (3 min): Sit comfortably and breathe in slowly. Hum one steady note with your lips closed, and feel the buzz in your lips and face. Now slowly open your mouth, and let the hum slide from 'mm' to 'ooo' and then to 'eee', without changing the note. Notice how the same buzz becomes different sounds. Close your mouth again and hum for three more breaths.
- Ask: When you speak, where does your voice live: in the throat, in the mouth, or in both?
- Ask: Is the empty part inside a bottle part of the bottle?
- Ask: If the same buzz can become many different vowels, what else do we call different that might be the same thing shaped differently?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A bottle gives opposite orders of notes when it is tapped and when it is blown across. So where does the note belong, to the bottle, to the water or to the way it is played? (hint: Think about what shakes in each case. Is the water the same thing in both?)
- *deep:* A whisper has no buzz from the throat, yet we hear vowels in it. What exactly is a voice? (hint: Think about the two parts: what makes the buzz, and what shapes it.)
- *deep:* A finger squeaks on a window only when it is damp, and not when it is dry or wet. What does this say about how the world sticks together and slips apart? (hint: Think of a hinge, a violin bow and a cricket. All of them depend on the middle of the grip.)
- *big:* A pop contains every pitch at once, and a pure note contains only one. Which is more natural? (hint: Think of the sounds in a forest, a market and a school. How many are pure notes?)
- *big:* A bottle sings because of the empty space inside it. Is the empty part of a thing part of the thing? (hint: Think of a bansuri without its hollow, or a jar with no inside.)

## Story: The Moan in the Courtyard
*Valli, Aachi, Muthu Thatha, Meena, Karthik and the house are made up. Chettinad is a real region of Tamil Nadu with huge old houses and open courtyards. The science is real. Wind across the mouth of a large jar can make a moaning note. The note is set by the length of the air column, and pouring in water shortens the air column and raises the note.*

Valli came to her grandmother's house in Karaikudi for the summer holidays. It was a huge old house in Chettinad, with forty rooms, tall wooden pillars and a courtyard open to the sky in the middle. Her grandmother, Aachi, had lived there all her life.

At the back of the house there was a long verandah. Along its wall stood six big clay jars, taller than Valli, where the family kept rainwater.

On her second evening, when the hot wind began to blow from the south, Valli heard it. A long, low moan, like a very sad person singing one note. Ooooooo. It rose a little, then faded. Then it came back.

'Don't go to the back verandah after dark,' said Muthu Thatha, the old watchman, with a shiver. 'My father said it is a spirit.' Her cousins Karthik and Meena, who were eight and seven, pulled their blankets up to their noses.

Valli did not pull up a blanket. She sat with her chin on her knees and listened. 'A ghost has nothing that shakes,' she thought. 'But every sound has something that shakes. So what is shaking?'

The next evening she took a torch, a pencil and her notebook, and she asked Muthu Thatha to come with her. He grumbled all the way. 'I only come because you are my Aachi's granddaughter,' he said.

On the verandah the moan was louder. Valli walked slowly along the wall with one hand cupped behind her ear. At the third jar it was strongest. She shone her torch into the dark mouth of the jar. She saw only a little rainwater, far below.

'Thatha, please hold the torch.' Valli put her palm flat over the mouth of the jar. The moan stopped at once. She took her palm away, and the moan began again. 'The ghost lives in the jar,' whispered Muthu Thatha. 'No,' said Valli. 'The wind lives in the jar.'

She remembered the feeling of blowing across the top of a bottle. She leaned over, pursed her lips, and blew across the mouth of the jar. Hooo. A deep note came back, a little like the moan. Muthu Thatha's eyebrows rose to the top of his head.

'But why only this jar?' he asked. 'And why only in the south wind?' It was a good question, and Valli did not know the answer yet. She wrote it in her notebook and went to bed.

In the morning she counted the water in the jars. The third jar was nearly empty. The others were full almost to the top. 'In school we blew across bottles with water in them,' she said at breakfast. 'The more water, the higher the note. A long column of air makes a low note, and a short one makes a high note.'

'So the third jar has the longest column of air,' she went on. 'It sings the lowest, and the wind finds it. The full jars have short columns of air. Their notes are too high and thin for this wind.' Aachi, who was peeling a mango, stopped. 'Then if we pour in a bucket of water...' 'The ghost will sing higher,' said Valli.

They tried it that afternoon. Muthu Thatha carried the bucket himself. One bucket went in, and Valli blew across the jar. Hooo, a little higher. A second bucket. Higher still. When the jar was half full, Muthu Thatha blew across it himself, puffing out his cheeks like a conch player, and a clear, bright note came out. He laughed so hard that he had to sit down on the step.

That evening the south wind came, as usual. The third jar did not moan. It sang a lighter note, almost cheerful. Meena and Karthik stood at the end of the verandah, listening, and their blankets lay forgotten on the floor.

Valli had an idea. With Muthu Thatha's help she poured different amounts of water into three of the jars, and left the others. On the next windy evening the verandah did not moan. It played. Three notes rose and fell as the wind came and went, sometimes alone, sometimes together, like an old orchestra tuning up.

'In seventy years in this house, I never knew it was waiting to be tuned,' said Aachi. Muthu Thatha, who had once been afraid of the verandah, now sat on the step every evening with a glass of tea, and called it Valli's raagam. And on the last night of the holidays, Valli slid a lid over the mouth of the last jar and lifted it, once, twice, three times, to hear the house go silent and sing again.

**For the parent:** For the parent: wind blowing across the mouth of the jar sets the air inside shaking, as it does when you blow across a bottle. The note is set by the length of the air column, so the jar with the most air gave the lowest moan. Pouring in water shortens the air column and raises the note. Covering the mouth stops the moan. The sound was not in the wind or in the clay. It came from the empty space shaped by the jar. Valli proved it by stopping the sound, changing it on purpose and making it again.

- Ask: Why did the moan stop when Valli put her palm over the mouth of the jar?
- Ask: Why did the note get higher when water was poured into the jar?
- Ask: Why did the jar with the most air in it give the lowest note?
- Ask: What did Valli do to show that the sound had an ordinary cause?
- Ask: Where in your home might wind or breath across an opening make a sound?

## Experiments
### e1 · Tap or Blow: which way do the notes go? (40 min)
- **question:** Bottles with different amounts of water give notes in one order when you tap them. Do they give the same order when you blow across them?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will tapping and blowing put the bottles in the same order from the lowest note to the highest? Which bottle will give the highest note when tapped, and which when blown? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use five bottles of the same kind. The water amounts are the same for both tests. Tap each bottle in the same place with the same spoon. Blow across each bottle from the same distance. The listener closes their eyes and says which of two bottles gives the higher note. Each pair is tried three times. Only the way of making the sound changes, so any difference comes from tapping or blowing.
- **safety:** Use glass bottles with no chips or cracks, and stand them on a folded towel. Tap gently with a wooden spoon, never a steel one. Wipe up spills at once. When blowing, rest between tries, and stop if anyone feels dizzy. Each child blows into their own bottle, or wipes the bottle's mouth with a clean cloth before the next child blows.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each way of making the sound, write the place of each bottle from lowest note to highest. Draw a line to join the water amounts to the places, once for tapping and once for blowing.
- **results table:** Water in the bottle (millilitres) | Air column length (centimetres) | Tapping: place from lowest (1) to highest (5) | Blowing: place from lowest (1) to highest (5)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bottles and the spoon. Explain that you will tap them and then blow across them. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We have five bottles with different amounts of water. First we tap them, then we blow across them. Will the order of the notes be the same both times? Which bottle will be highest when tapped, and which when blown? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Tap the bottles. Then blow across them. Is the order of notes the same? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think the order will be the same, or different? (good: It will be the same, because it is the same bottle and water.; More water makes it heavier, so lower.; I think blowing will be different, because it is air.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children say the order will be the same. Write the guess at the top of the table, because the result may surprise them. Roles: with one child, you are the helper who listens and writes. With 2 to 4 children, one taps, one listens with eyes shut, one writes the table, and one blows. Swap after each bottle pair, so everyone gets a turn at tapping, blowing and listening.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the bottles on the towel. Fill them with 0, 50, 100, 150 and 200 millilitres of water. Put a small piece of tape on each bottle with the amount. Measure the air column in each with the ruler and write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the water into each bottle. Write the amount on a piece of tape. Now measure from the top of the water to the top of the bottle. That is the air column. Write each one in the table.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bottles. Label each one. Measure the air from the water to the top.
  - Note: The air column gets shorter as the water goes up. If your bottles differ in size, use the same kind for all five. These amounts suit bottles of 250 to 300 millilitres. For a bigger bottle, make equal steps up to about three quarters full. Keep the water below the shoulder of the bottle. If you have fewer than five bottles, use three (for example 0, 100 and 200 millilitres) and say so.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tap each bottle halfway up with the wooden spoon, once, gently. The helper keeps their eyes shut. Play two bottles one after the other and ask which is higher. Repeat each pair three times. Then give each bottle its place.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. I will tap two bottles. Which note is higher, the first or the second? We will do this for every pair of neighbours, three times each. Then we can put the five in order.  
  *Child mode:* Tap two bottles. Which is higher? Do every pair. Then put all five in order.
  - Note: Expected: the empty bottle gives the highest tapped note, and the fullest gives the lowest. If your bottles are plastic, the difference is hard to hear, so use glass. Steps of 50 millilitres are easy to hear on glass, but if two neighbours sound alike, compare the empty bottle and the fullest one first. Real tapped notes are short and dull, so listen closely and let the helper hear each pair more than once. Write the places from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest).
  - Check: Why does the listener keep their eyes closed while the bottles are tapped? → **So that how the bottles look does not change what they say**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now blow across each bottle. Rest your lower lip on the front of the bottle's mouth and blow a thin stream of air across the top. Find the position that gives a clear note. Give each bottle a place from lowest to highest in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Touch your lower lip to the front of the bottle's mouth. Blow a thin stream of air across the top, as if you are blowing across a flute. It takes some tries. Then do the same for each bottle, and put them in order.  
  *Child mode:* Blow across the top. Find the note. Do all five. Put them in order.
  - Note: Blowing takes a little practice. Tilt the bottle a bit, and aim the stream of air so that it hits the far edge of the mouth. Rest between tries. If a bottle will not sing after a minute or two, try the one with the most water first, since its short air column sings more easily. A weak, airy hiss is normal at the start. Expected: the empty bottle gives the lowest blown note, and the fullest bottle gives the highest. This is the opposite of tapping.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the two orders. Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning before you explain anything.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the two orders. What do you notice? What is your claim? What is your evidence? Why do you think the orders are different? What is shaking when you tap, and what is shaking when you blow?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the two orders. What do you notice? What is shaking each time?
  - Ask: What changed between tapping and blowing, if the bottles and water stayed the same? (good: What is shaking changed.; When you tap, the glass shakes. When you blow, the air shakes.; The air column is the part that matters when blowing.)
  - Ask: Why does more water give a higher note when you blow? (good: There is less air, so the air column is shorter.; A short air column makes a higher note.; The water takes up room that was air.)
  - Note: Do not explain the cards yet. Let your child say what they think shakes in each case. If the orders are not reversed in your test, write down what you found and try to blow again, with a thinner stream of air. Real results are not always neat.
  - Check: A bottle has 200 millilitres of water in it, and another has 50 millilitres. Which gives the higher note when blown across? → **The one with 200 millilitres**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the empty bottle. Hum slowly from a low note up to a high note, with your mouth near its opening. Listen for the note where the bottle suddenly booms.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the empty bottle by your mouth and hum slowly, from low to high. Listen for the one note where the bottle seems to answer. That is the bottle's own note.  
  *Child mode:* Hum slowly up from low to high near the bottle. Find the note that booms.
  - Note: The booming note is the natural note of the air in the bottle. A voice is not strong enough to make an empty bottle ring, but you should hear one note get louder. Try with the other bottles. The fuller ones boom at higher notes. If the empty bottle's note is too low for your voice, try the fuller bottles first. The boom is gentle, so hold the bottle's mouth close to your lips and keep the room quiet. If you hear nothing, that is a fair result too, so write it down and move on.
### e2 · The Squeak Test: when does a finger squeak on glass? (25 min)
- **question:** A finger moved across a window can squeak. Does it squeak when it is dry, damp, very wet, soapy or oily?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which finger will squeak the loudest? Which will not squeak at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same clean glass, the same finger, the same gentle press and the same speed every time. Move the finger across about 20 centimetres of glass in about one second. Clean the glass between conditions. Each condition is tried three times, and a listener with eyes shut scores the squeak from 0 to 5. Only the wetness of the finger changes, so any difference comes from that.
- **safety:** Use a window at ground level, never near a ledge or an open balcony edge. Press gently, because glass can crack or cut. Do not use a cracked or chipped pane. Wipe up any water, soap or oil from the glass and the floor at once, because they make the floor slippery. If anyone has sensitive skin or an allergy to soap or oil, skip that condition and wash hands after.
- **record:** Fill in the table with a score from 0 (silent) to 5 (loud squeak) for each try. Then add the three scores for each condition and rank the conditions.
- **results table:** Finger condition | Try 1 (score 0 to 5) | Try 2 (score 0 to 5) | Try 3 (score 0 to 5)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the glass and the five finger conditions: dry, damp, very wet, soapy and oily. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will drag a finger across clean glass five ways. Dry. Damp. Very wet. Soapy. Oily. Which will squeak loudest? Which will not squeak at all? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Five kinds of finger on glass. Which squeaks loudest? Which is silent? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that finger will squeak the most? (good: A wet finger sticks to glass.; A dry finger grips the most.; Soap makes a noise like bubbles.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say a dry finger will squeak most, because it grips most. Write the guess at the top of the table. Roles: with one child, you are the listener and scorer. With 2 to 4 children, one drags the finger, one listens with eyes shut, and one writes the scores. Swap roles after each condition. If each child uses their own finger on the same condition, compare the scores. Fingers differ, which is why one finger is used for a fair test.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Clean the glass with the cloth and let it dry. Practise one smooth sweep of about 20 centimetres in one second, with a dry finger. The helper shuts their eyes and gives a score from 0 to 5. Write it as the first dry try.  
  *Parent says:* Clean the glass first. Now place one finger and drag it across the glass in about one second, with a gentle press. The listener closes their eyes and gives a score for the squeak, from nothing to very loud.  
  *Child mode:* Clean the glass. Drag a dry finger across in one second. Give a score.
  - Note: A dry finger makes a faint scrape or no squeak. Press the same way each time. A harder press changes the sound, so keep it gentle. Before you begin, wash and dry the finger once. Clean fingers and clean glass matter most. If glass or fingers are greasy, nothing will squeak. In very damp weather the glass may mist, so wipe it dry with the cloth before each try.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the damp finger three times, then the very wet finger three times. Dip the finger and shake off the drops for damp. Let it drip for very wet. Wipe the glass between tries. Write the scores.  
  *Parent says:* For a damp finger, dip it and shake off the drops. For a very wet finger, leave the drops on. Do three sweeps each and give scores. Wipe the glass between tries.  
  *Child mode:* Test a damp finger and a very wet finger. Wipe the glass each time. Give scores.
  - Note: Expected: a damp finger squeaks loudly. A very wet finger glides with almost no sound. The squeak only happens when the finger sticks and slips. If even the damp finger is silent, the glass or finger is probably greasy. Wash the finger with soap, clean the glass with a damp cloth and dry it, then try again. A clean steel plate or a mirror also works. A few tries may be needed to find how damp is right. If your results differ, write what you hear.
  - Check: Why must the glass be wiped between each try? → **So that wetness left from the last try does not change the next one**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the soapy finger three times and the oily finger three times. Wipe the glass between tries and wash the finger after. Write the scores. Add up the three scores for each condition.  
  *Parent says:* Dip the finger in the soapy water for the soapy test. Use a touch of oil on the finger for the oily test. Do three sweeps for each and give scores. Wipe the glass well after the oil.  
  *Child mode:* Test a soapy finger and an oily finger. Wipe the glass. Add up the scores.
  - Note: Expected: a soapy finger may squeak, though usually less than damp, and it can also be silent. Soap films vary, so report what you hear. An oily finger is silent. Wipe the oil off the glass with a dry cloth, then a damp one, so that nobody slips.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the totals. Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning before you explain anything.  
  *Parent says:* Which finger squeaked most? Which did not squeak? What is your claim? What is your evidence? Why do you think the damp finger is different from the dry one and the very wet one?  
  *Child mode:* Which finger squeaked most? Which was silent? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why does a damp finger squeak, but a dry or a very wet finger does not? (good: The damp finger grips and slips again and again.; A dry finger grips too much and a wet finger slips too much.; The grip has to be in the middle.)
  - Note: Do not explain the cards yet. A good answer says that squeaking needs both gripping and slipping. If the child can say 'the grip is not too much and not too little', that is the idea. To go further, use the damp finger and try three speeds (slow, medium, fast). Does the pitch of the squeak change?
  - Check: A squeaky door hinge goes quiet after a drop of oil. What is the best reason? → **The oil lets the metal slide without sticking, so the stick and slip stops**

  *Child mode:* Speak the vowel. The listener guesses. Do ten tries. Tick the right ones.
  - Note: Expected: almost all ten are right with a normal voice. Keep the volume the same on every try.
- **s4 (8 min)**

  *Child mode:* Now whisper the vowels. Do ten tries. Tick the right ones.
  - Note: Expected: most are right, often 8 or more out of ten, even though there is no buzz. Guessing alone would give about 3 out of 10. If the listener gets 10 out of 10 in both rows, make it harder with a longer distance, or add 'e' and 'o' slips, so that guessing alone gives about 2 out of 10.

  *Child mode:* Count the right answers. Where do vowels come from? Pinch your nose and say 'mama'.
  - Ask: If a whisper has no buzz, where do the vowels come from? (good: From the shape of the mouth.; From the way the mouth shapes the hiss.; The mouth changes the sound, not the throat.)

### a3 · The Pot That Hums Back: finding the hidden note of a space (20 min)
- **safety:** Hum gently. Do not put your head inside any vessel. Hold the vessel in front of your mouth, about 5 centimetres away.
- **record:** Write which vessel boomed at which kind of note (low, middle or high). Write the depth of each vessel in centimetres. Write whether the bathroom boomed at any note.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the big pot and the small vessel. Ask which will boom at a lower note when a person hums near it, and why.  
  *Parent says:* We will hum near the mouth of a pot, slowly from low to high. At one note the pot will answer back. Which will answer at a lower note, the big pot or the small tumbler? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Hum near a pot. Find the note where it booms. Big or small, which is lower?
  - Ask: Why might a big pot answer at a lower note? (good: It has more air inside.; It is like a long string.; A bigger space makes a deeper sound.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children say bigger means lower. The test can show it.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the big pot about 5 centimetres from your child's mouth. They hum a steady note and slowly slide it from low to high. Ask them to stop where the pot seems loudest. Write if that note was low, middle or high.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the pot near your mouth. Hum a low note and slowly slide up. Listen for the note where the pot suddenly sounds louder. That is the pot's hidden note. Stop there.  
  *Child mode:* Hum low and slide up slowly. Listen for the note where the pot booms.
  - Note: The booming note is the natural note of the air in the pot. Slide slowly. It may be a narrow zone of just one note. If the child's voice is not low enough, try a smaller vessel. Do not shout. The pot's note is about the pitch of a child's or woman's voice, so a child often finds it, while a deep adult voice may slide past it. The boom is soft, so keep the room quiet and listen with the pot close. Typical, not promised: the effect is clearer with a deep pot than a shallow one. Measure the depth with the ruler and write it down.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same with the small vessel. Compare which one boomed at a lower note. Then try the bathroom, with the door shut. Hum a slow slide from low to high and listen for a note that suddenly seems bigger.  
  *Parent says:* Now try the small tumbler. Is its hidden note lower or higher? Then go into the bathroom and shut the door. Hum slowly from low to high. Is there a note where the room seems to boom?  
  *Child mode:* Try the small vessel. Is the note higher? Then try the bathroom.
  - Note: Expected: the small vessel answers at a higher note than the big one. The small bathroom often has a note where a hum sounds big, and this is why singing in the bathroom sounds good. A bathroom's note is very low, so a deep adult voice finds it more easily, and a child may find none. Let a deep-voiced adult try if there is one. In a group, one person hums and the others listen from the doorway to say if it sounded bigger. Compare depth too: the deeper vessel should answer at the lower note. If nothing booms, that is a real result.
  - Check: The small tumbler booms at a higher note than the big pot. What is the best reason? → **The smaller space has a shorter air column, so its natural note is higher**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask where else they have heard a space answer back, such as a stairwell, a temple hall or a well.  
  *Parent says:* What is your claim about which space answers at the lowest note? What did you notice? Why do you think so? Where else have you heard a place answer back when you sing or shout?  
  *Child mode:* Which space gave the lowest note? Why? Where else have you heard this?
  - Ask: Why does singing in a bathroom sound better than singing in a field? (good: The walls bounce the sound back.; The room boosts some notes.; The space answers at some notes and makes them louder.)

### p1 · The Bottle Bansuri Set: tune five bottles to play a scale (50 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** Use glass bottles without chips or cracks, and stand them on a towel. Rest between rounds of blowing, and stop if anyone feels dizzy. Each child blows into their own bottle, or wipes the mouth with a clean cloth before the next child blows. Pick a quiet time, since five bottles can be loud.
- **record:** A table with five bottles, the note names, the amount of water in each and the air column length. A note on whether equal steps in notes need equal steps in water.
- **schedule:** day 1: Tune the five bottles to Sa, Re, Ga, Pa and Dha by adding water, and label them. (30 min); day 2: Practise a short tune, play it for the family, and record the water amounts in a table. (20 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bottles and the harmonium or keyboard. Ask for a guess and a reason about which bottle will need the most water for Sa and which for Dha.  
  *Parent says:* We will tune five bottles to Sa, Re, Ga, Pa and Dha, by changing the water. Which note needs the most water, Sa or Dha? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Tune five bottles to a scale with water. Which needs most water, Sa or Dha?
  - Ask: Why do you think that note needs more water? (good: More water means a shorter air column and a higher note.; A low note needs lots of air, so little water.; I will have to try it.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. The answer is that Dha, the highest, needs the most water, because the air column is shortest. Children may need the Tap or Blow test first to remember this. Roles: with one child, you play the keyboard note and your child tunes the bottle. With 2 to 4 children, each child tunes one or two bottles, then all come together to play the tune, one bottle each.
- **s2 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* First blow across the empty bottle and find its note on the harmonium or keyboard. Call that note Sa. Now play Re, Ga, Pa and Dha above it. For each next bottle, start with the first-guess amount in the parent note and blow across it. If the note is too low, add water. If it is too high, pour a little out. Match it, and label the bottle. Each bottle needs more water than the one before.  
  *Parent says:* Play Sa on the harmonium. Blow across the first bottle and compare. Is yours lower or higher? Add water to go higher, or pour some out to go lower. Match it, and label it. Then do Re, Ga, Pa and Dha.  
  *Child mode:* Play a note. Blow across a bottle. Add water to go higher. Pour out to go lower.
  - Note: A bottle's lowest blown note is often below the harmonium's usual Sa, so we start from the bottle's own note, and the empty bottle is Sa. Typical first guesses for a 250 to 300 millilitre bottle, to be tuned by ear: Re about 50 millilitres, Ga about 90, Pa about 140, Dha about 170. Your bottles will differ, so trust your ears. Add water in small amounts, 10 millilitres at a time. Blow gently, because a hard blow jumps to a higher note. The water goes up and up as you climb the scale. If you have no harmonium, use a keyboard app on a phone, or sing the scale and match by ear. Write the amounts of water as you go.
  - Check: A bottle is blown across and its note is too high. What should you do to lower it? → **Pour a little water out, to make the air column longer**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, check that each bottle still gives the right note, since water may have evaporated or been spilled. Fix any that are off. Practise playing Sa, Re, Ga, Pa, Dha and back.  
  *Parent says:* Check each bottle against the harmonium again. Add or remove a little water if one is off. Then play the scale up and down.  
  *Child mode:* Check each bottle. Fix any that are off. Play the scale.
  - Note: Water in a bottle can evaporate a little overnight, and in hot weather a bit more. Cover the bottles with a plate if you want to keep them. If you need to start again, you only lose ten minutes. The note changes only a little, but it can be heard. Write down any water you had to add.
  - Wait: Do this on day 2.
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a short tune made of those five notes. Practise it, and then play it for the family. Write down the water amounts and the air column lengths in your table. Notice if equal steps in notes needed equal steps in water.  
  *Parent says:* Choose a tune that uses only these five notes. Practise it a few times, and then play it for the family. Afterwards, look at your table. Did each step up the scale need the same extra water?  
  *Child mode:* Pick a tune. Practise it. Play it for your family. Look at your water amounts.
  - Ask: Did each step up the scale need the same extra water? (good: No, the higher steps needed more water.; Some steps needed a lot more than others.; The water amounts were not even.)
  - Note: Expected: the amounts of water do not rise evenly. Notes are spaced by ratios, not by equal steps, and a bottle's note depends on its air volume in a more complicated way than a plain pipe. Do not worry if your numbers look uneven. Typical, not promised: going from Sa to Dha, the air in the bottle shrinks to roughly a third. If you have only a few bottles, play a three-note tune (Sa, Ga, Pa).

### c1 · One Tube, Three Notes (40 min)

## Worksheets
### Four Ways to Start a Sound (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A balloon bursting makes a bang. Which way of starting a sound is this?  
   Options: A solid thing shaking steadily / Air shaking in a space / A sudden pop / Two surfaces sticking and slipping  
   **Answer:** A sudden pop. *Why:* A burst balloon gives the air one sudden push. There is no steady vibration, so it is a pop. Choosing 'solid shaking' pictures the balloon skin vibrating like a drum, but it breaks in one snap.
2. (fill) One vibration each second is called one ____.  
   **Answer:** ['hertz']. *Why:* The hertz is the unit for counting vibrations. A note of 440 hertz vibrates 440 times each second.
3. (truefalse) A string that is half as long vibrates twice as fast and gives a note one octave higher. True or false?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Halving the length doubles the number of vibrations each second, which is one octave. This rule works for strings and for air columns.
4. (mcq) A bansuri gives its lowest note when all its holes are covered. What is the best reason?  
   Options: The air column is at its longest / The air column is at its shortest / The player blows harder / The bamboo is heavier  
   **Answer:** The air column is at its longest. *Why:* Covering the holes makes the whole length of the tube vibrate, which is the longest air column and the lowest note. Uncovering holes shortens the air column and raises the note.
5. (order) Blow across three bottles: one empty, one with 200 millilitres of water and one with 400 millilitres. Put them in order from the lowest note to the highest.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* More water leaves less air, and a shorter air column gives a higher note when you blow. So the empty bottle is lowest and the fullest is highest.
6. (match) Match each sound to the way it is started.  
   **Answer:** A plucked sitar string → A solid thing shakes; A bansuri → Air shakes in a space; Thunder → A sudden pop; Chalk screeching on a board → Two surfaces stick and slip. *Why:* A string is a shaking solid. A bansuri works by air shaking in its tube. Thunder is a shock wave from lightning. Chalk grips and slips along the board.
7. (mcq) You tap a glass bottle and the one with the most water gives the lowest note. What is shaking?  
   Options: The glass and the water / Only the air above the water / Only the spoon / Nothing is shaking  
   **Answer:** The glass and the water. *Why:* When you tap a bottle, the glass wall and the water inside shake. More water is more to shake, so the note is lower. When you blow across, it is the air that shakes, and the order flips.
8. (fill) The mix of overtones in a sound gives it its ____, so a flute and a violin sound different on the same note.  
   **Answer:** ['timbre']. *Why:* Timbre is the quality of a sound. It depends on which overtones are strong and which are weak.
9. (mcq) A damp finger squeaks on glass, but a dry finger hardly does. What is the best reason?  
   Options: A squeak needs the finger to stick and slip again and again, and a dry finger grips too hard to do that / A damp finger is heavier / A dry finger makes no vibration at all / The glass is warmer when damp  
   **Answer:** A squeak needs the finger to stick and slip again and again, and a dry finger grips too hard to do that. *Why:* A squeak is a fast rhythm of gripping and slipping. If the grip is too strong, the finger drags. If it is too weak, the finger glides. Damp is in the middle.
10. (truefalse) When you whisper, your vocal folds buzz very quietly. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In a whisper, the vocal folds do not buzz. Air rushes through a gap in the throat and makes a hiss. You can feel the difference by touching your throat.
11. (mcq) Which parts of the body shape the buzz from the vocal folds into the vowels 'ee' and 'oo'?  
   Options: The throat, mouth and nose / The ears / The lungs only / The stomach  
   **Answer:** The throat, mouth and nose. *Why:* The throat, mouth and nose form a space that boosts some overtones of the buzz more than others. Changing the shape of the space changes the vowel.
12. (sort) Sort these sounds into the four ways a sound can start.  
   **Answer:** a tabla skin → A solid thing shakes; a bottle blown across → Air shakes in a space; a clap → A sudden pop; a squeaky hinge → Stick and slip; a temple bell → A solid thing shakes; a whistle → Air shakes in a space; a balloon bursting → A sudden pop; a cricket's chirp → Stick and slip. *Why:* Skins and bells are shaking solids. Bottles and whistles work by air shaking in a space. A clap and a balloon are one sudden push. A hinge and a cricket rub two surfaces that stick and slip.
13. (mcq) How can you find how far away lightning is?  
   Options: Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder, and divide by three to get kilometres / Count the flashes in a minute / Listen for how loud the thunder is / Measure how bright the flash is  
   **Answer:** Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder, and divide by three to get kilometres. *Why:* Light arrives almost at once, and sound travels about a kilometre in three seconds. So the gap in seconds divided by three is the distance in kilometres.
14. (write) A friend says that every sound is a steady note. Give two sounds that are not steady notes, and say why.  
   **Answer:** A clap and a hiss are not steady notes. A clap is one sudden push of air, and a hiss is a jumble of many pitches with no steady one.. *Why:* A strong answer names two sounds, such as a clap, a pop, a hiss or a crash, and says that they have no single steady pitch.
15. (write) Explain to a younger child why blowing across a bottle with more water gives a higher note.  
   **Answer:** When you blow across a bottle, the air inside shakes. More water leaves less air, so the air column is shorter. A shorter air column shakes faster, and that gives a higher note.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the air is what shakes, that more water means a shorter air column, and that a shorter column is a higher note.
16. (draw) Draw two bottles, one with a lot of water and one with a little. Mark the air column in each, and say which gives the higher note when blown across.  
   **Answer:** A bottle with a lot of water has a short air column marked at the top and gives the higher note. A bottle with a little water has a long air column and gives the lower note.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the air column as the empty part of each bottle, shorter in the fuller one, and gives the higher note to the fuller bottle.
### Notes, Overtones and Whistles (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A closed pipe 17 centimetres long gives about 500 hertz. About what note does a closed pipe 34 centimetres long give?  
   Options: About 250 hertz / About 500 hertz / About 1000 hertz / About 125 hertz  
   **Answer:** About 250 hertz. *Why:* Doubling the length halves the number of vibrations each second. 500 divided by 2 is 250. That is one octave lower.
2. (mcq) A string vibrates 200 times each second. You press it at its middle so only half can shake. What happens?  
   Options: It vibrates about 400 times each second, one octave higher / It vibrates about 100 times each second / It stays at 200 / It stops vibrating  
   **Answer:** It vibrates about 400 times each second, one octave higher. *Why:* Half the length means twice the vibrations each second. 200 times 2 is 400. Picking 100 reverses the rule.
3. (fill) A string two thirds as long as another vibrates one and a half times as fast and gives the note Pa above Sa. So Sa and Pa have a ratio of 3 to ____.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* One and a half is the same as 3 over 2. Sa and Pa have a ratio of 3 to 2, which is what makes them agree.
4. (truefalse) A hollow bamboo pole that moans in the wind is vibrating like a stretched string. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The air in the hollow pole is what shakes. The wind flips from side to side at the edge of the opening, and the air inside picks the note. The bamboo itself hardly moves.
5. (mcq) Why can a flute player move the note up by blowing harder?  
   Options: The edge tone speeds up and jumps to a higher resonance of the air column / Blowing harder makes the flute shorter / The flute gets colder / The player's lips vibrate faster  
   **Answer:** The edge tone speeds up and jumps to a higher resonance of the air column. *Why:* Faster air makes faster swirls at the edge, and the edge tone locks on to a higher resonance of the air column. The flute itself does not change.
6. (mcq) Why does a thin wire hum at a higher note in the wind than a thick wire?  
   Options: The swirls behind a thin wire flip from side to side more often / Thin wires are lighter / Thin wires are colder / The wind blows harder on thin wires  
   **Answer:** The swirls behind a thin wire flip from side to side more often. *Why:* The rate of the flips grows as the wire gets thinner. The thin wire gives faster swirls, so a higher hum. Weight and temperature are not the cause.
7. (sort) Sort each description into 'fundamental' or 'overtone'.  
   **Answer:** a string swinging as one big loop → Fundamental; a string swinging in two halves at once → Overtone; the lowest and usually strongest vibration → Fundamental; a higher vibration that sounds along with the main note → Overtone; the part that sets the pitch you hear → Fundamental; the part that shapes the timbre → Overtone. *Why:* The fundamental is the slowest vibration and sets the pitch. Overtones are faster vibrations above it, and the mix of them gives the sound its timbre.
8. (order) Put these in order from the one whose air gives the highest natural note to the lowest: a big clay jar, a small tin cup, a steel pot.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* A smaller space has a shorter air column and a higher natural note. The tin cup is smallest, then the steel pot, then the big clay jar.
9. (match) Match each action to what is shaking.  
   **Answer:** Tapping a glass bottle → The glass and the water; Blowing across a bottle → The air column; Humming → The vocal folds; Drawing a bow across a violin string → The string, driven by stick and slip. *Why:* Different actions make different things shake. Knowing what shakes explains the note.
10. (mcq) Why does thunder arrive after you see the lightning?  
   Options: Sound travels much more slowly than light / Thunder happens later than lightning / The sky is cloudy / Light is louder  
   **Answer:** Sound travels much more slowly than light. *Why:* Lightning and thunder start together. Light reaches you almost at once, and sound travels about a kilometre in three seconds.
11. (mcq) A mosquito's wings beat about 500 times each second and a bee's about 230. Which whines higher, and why?  
   Options: The mosquito, because her wings vibrate faster / The bee, because she is bigger / They sound the same / The bee, because her wings are slower  
   **Answer:** The mosquito, because her wings vibrate faster. *Why:* A faster vibration gives a higher pitch. 500 is more than twice 230, so the mosquito is more than an octave higher.
12. (truefalse) Whispered 'ee' and 'oo' can be told apart because the vocal folds buzz in different ways. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The vocal folds do not buzz in a whisper. The mouth takes a different shape for each vowel, and the shape boosts different parts of the hiss.
13. (perspective) Why do some pairs of notes sound sweet together? People who have thought about it give different answers.  
   **Answer:** Any view is fine if the reason uses evidence, such as ratios, beats or the results with the Tsimane people.. *Why:* No one has settled this question. A strong answer names a view, gives a reason, and says what test would help, such as playing the same pairs to people who grew up with different music.
14. (label) Label the three parts of the voice on the drawing of a head: 'vocal folds buzz', 'mouth shapes the vowel' and 'air comes from the lungs'.  
   **Answer:** ['throat: vocal folds buzz', 'mouth: mouth shapes the vowel', 'chest: air comes from the lungs']. *Why:* Air from the lungs pushes the vocal folds, which buzz. The buzz passes through the throat and mouth, which shape it into vowels.
15. (draw) Draw a string vibrating as one whole loop, and the same string vibrating in two halves. Mark which one gives the higher note.  
   **Answer:** The first string has one loop between its ends. The second has two loops, with a still point in the middle. The two-loop string is the higher note, one octave above.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows one loop for the fundamental and two loops for the first overtone, and says that two loops vibrate twice as fast.
16. (write) A friend says that a violin and a bansuri playing the same note should sound exactly the same. Write a reply.  
   **Answer:** They have the same pitch, but their timbre is different. A violin has many strong overtones from a stick-slip bow on a string, and a bansuri has a smoother mix from air shaking in a tube.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the pitch is the same but the mix of overtones is different, and mentions that the sound is made in different ways.
17. (write) A squeaky door goes quiet after a drop of oil. Explain what the oil did, using the words stick and slip.  
   **Answer:** The oil made the surfaces slide smoothly, so they no longer stick. Without sticking and slipping again and again, there is no fast vibration, and the squeak stops.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a squeak is a rhythm of sticking and slipping and that oil removes the sticking.
### Numbers, Pipes and Open Questions (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)

   **Answer:** About 343 hertz. *Why:* Four times 0.25 is 1. 343 divided by 1 is 343. Picking 86 forgets to divide by four times the length.

   **Answer:** About 600 hertz. *Why:* Halving the length doubles the vibrations each second. 300 times 2 is 600, one octave higher.
3. (fill) Marin Mersenne found that a string vibrates faster when it is shorter, tighter or ____.  
   **Answer:** ['thinner']. *Why:* A thin string has less mass to move, so it vibrates faster. A thick string vibrates more slowly.
4. (truefalse) Cupped hands make a lower, hollow clap than flat hands because the cupped space picks out a pitch. True or false?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The air trapped in cupped hands acts like a small resonator, so the pop has a pitch. Flat hands give a sharper, higher clap.
5. (mcq) You see a lightning flash, and thunder follows 9 seconds later. About how far away was the lightning?  
   Options: About 3 kilometres / About 1 kilometre / About 9 kilometres / About 27 kilometres  
   **Answer:** About 3 kilometres. *Why:* Sound travels about a kilometre in three seconds. 9 divided by 3 is 3 kilometres.
6. (mcq) A finger takes half a second to cross a window and squeaks at 800 hertz. About how many sticks and slips happen in that sweep?  
   Options: About 400 / About 40 / About 800 / About 1600  
   **Answer:** About 400. *Why:* 800 vibrations each second for half a second is 800 times a half, which is 400. Picking 800 forgets that the sweep only lasted half a second.
7. (fill) A grown-up man's voice buzz is about 110 hertz. In 2 seconds, his vocal folds open and close about ____ times.  
   **Answer:** ['220']. *Why:* 110 vibrations each second times 2 seconds is 220.
8. (match) Match each thing to the idea that explains it.  
   **Answer:** Tanpura strings tuned to Pa and Sa → Notes that agree, with a simple ratio of 3 to 2; A bottle that sings when blown across → An edge tone joined to an air column; A snapping shrimp's loud bang → A bubble collapsing; Wind humming in a wire → Swirls flipping from side to side. *Why:* Each sound has a cause that you can name. Naming it is a first step to testing it.
9. (mcq) In 1971, John Coltman found that listeners could not tell flutes with walls of different materials apart. What does this suggest?  
   Options: The wall material matters much less than the shape of the air column / Flutes make no sound / Metal flutes sound the best / Listeners do not hear well  
   **Answer:** The wall material matters much less than the shape of the air column. *Why:* If listeners cannot tell the walls apart, then the material has little effect on the sound. The air column carries most of the sound. Some players still disagree.
10. (mcq) People of the Tsimane in Bolivia did not prefer sweet pairs of notes to rough ones. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Part of what sounds sweet is learned from the music around us / The Tsimane cannot hear / Ratios do not exist / Sweet notes are the same for all people  
   **Answer:** Part of what sounds sweet is learned from the music around us. *Why:* If sweetness were fully built into the ear, every group would show it. The result suggests that learning plays a part. It does not show that ratios do not exist.
11. (write) Explain why a bottle gives opposite orders of notes when it is tapped and when it is blown across. Use the words 'shaking' and 'air column'.  
   **Answer:** When you tap the bottle, the glass and the water are shaking, and more water is more to shake, so the note is lower. When you blow across it, the air column is shaking, and more water makes the air column shorter, so the note is higher.. *Why:* A strong answer says what is shaking in each case and links it to the direction of the change.
12. (truefalse) A person who pinches their nose while saying 'mama' sounds different because the nose is part of the space that shapes the voice. True or false?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The nose is part of the vocal tract. Blocking it changes the shape of the space, so the sound changes.
13. (perspective) Does the material of a flute change its tone?  
   **Answer:** Any view is fine if the reason uses evidence. A good test would have the same player play several flutes behind a screen, with listeners who do not know which is which.. *Why:* No one has settled this. A strong answer names a view and suggests a test that hides which flute is which, so that expectations do not change what people hear.
14. (draw) Draw the vocal tract as a bottle shape, and show where the buzz starts and where the vowel is made.  
   **Answer:** A bottle shape for the throat and mouth. A zig-zag buzz at the bottom where the vocal folds are, and the mouth shape at the top labelled as where the vowel is made.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the buzz starting at the vocal folds and the space above shaping it.
15. (label) Label what is happening when a violin is played: 'bow sticks and slips', 'string shakes', 'body of the violin shakes' and 'air carries the sound'.  
   **Answer:** ['bow touching the string: bow sticks and slips', 'the string: string shakes', 'the wooden box: body of the violin shakes', 'the space around: air carries the sound']. *Why:* A violin uses more than one way at once. The bow sticks and slips on the string, the string shakes, the body shakes and pushes the air, and the air carries the sound to the ear.
16. (write) Design a fair test to find out whether a sticky finger or a smooth finger squeaks louder on glass. Say what you keep the same.  
   **Answer:** I would use the same glass, the same finger, the same press and the same speed. I would change only how sticky the finger is, and have a listener with eyes shut score each squeak. I would do each condition three times.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing, keeps the rest the same, uses repeated tries and avoids the listener knowing which finger it is.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A sound starts when something pushes on the air. Which of these does not push on the air?  
   Options: A plucked string / A clap / A stream of air across an edge / A stone lying perfectly still  
   **Answer:** A stone lying perfectly still. *Why:* Something has to move to push the air. A stone lying still does nothing to the air. A string, a clap and a moving air stream all push it.
2. (mcq) What does the hertz count?  
   Options: Vibrations each second / How loud a sound is / How far a sound travels / How long a note lasts  
   **Answer:** Vibrations each second. *Why:* One hertz is one vibration each second. Loudness is a different idea.
3. (mcq) Two strings are the same except that one is half as long. Compared with the long one, the short one vibrates...  
   Options: twice as fast / half as fast / at the same speed / four times as fast  
   **Answer:** twice as fast. *Why:* Halving the length doubles the vibrations each second, which is one octave higher.
4. (mcq) What does timbre tell you about a sound?  
   Options: Which instrument or voice is making it, even when the pitch is the same / How loud it is / How far away it is / How long it lasts  
   **Answer:** Which instrument or voice is making it, even when the pitch is the same. *Why:* Timbre is the quality of a sound, which comes from the mix of overtones. Loudness and distance are different ideas.
5. (mcq) You touch a vibrating string lightly at its middle. What do you hear?  
   Options: A note one octave higher, with a pure bell-like sound / A note one octave lower / No sound at all / A louder sound at the same pitch  
   **Answer:** A note one octave higher, with a pure bell-like sound. *Why:* The touch stops the whole-string vibration and leaves the two-half vibration, which is one octave higher.
6. (mcq) You blow across two identical bottles. One has 100 millilitres of water and the other has 300 millilitres. Which gives the higher note, and why?  
   Options: The 300 millilitre bottle, because its air column is shorter / The 100 millilitre bottle, because its air column is shorter / The 300 millilitre bottle, because the water is heavier / They give the same note  
   **Answer:** The 300 millilitre bottle, because its air column is shorter. *Why:* More water leaves less air, so the air column is shorter, and a shorter air column gives a higher note. Picking 'heavier' is the reasoning for tapping, not blowing.
7. (mcq) What makes the note of a flute?  
   Options: Air in the tube, set shaking by a stream of air across an edge / The metal of the flute shaking / The player's voice / The player's lips buzzing  
   **Answer:** Air in the tube, set shaking by a stream of air across an edge. *Why:* A flute is a tube of air. The air stream across the edge sets the air column shaking, and the length of the column picks the note.
8. (mcq) Which of these makes a sound from one sudden push of air, with no steady pitch?  
   Options: A clap / A tuning fork / A flute note / A tanpura string  
   **Answer:** A clap. *Why:* A clap is one sudden squeeze of air. The other three give steady notes.
9. (mcq) A squeak happens when two surfaces...  
   Options: stick and slip again and again / stay perfectly still / slide with no grip at all / get hot and melt  
   **Answer:** stick and slip again and again. *Why:* A squeak is a very fast rhythm of gripping and slipping. If there is no grip, or too much, there is no squeak.
10. (mcq) In a whisper, what turns the hiss into 'ee' or 'oo'?  
   Options: The shape of the mouth / The buzz of the vocal folds / The lungs / The ears  
   **Answer:** The shape of the mouth. *Why:* A whisper has no buzz. The mouth shape boosts different parts of the hiss for each vowel.
11. (mcq) Which statement about vocal folds is true?  
   Options: They buzz when you speak or hum, and do not buzz when you whisper / They buzz when you whisper / They only buzz when you shout / They do not move  
   **Answer:** They buzz when you speak or hum, and do not buzz when you whisper. *Why:* The vocal folds buzz for speech, singing and humming. In a whisper, air only hisses through a gap.
12. (mcq) Which of these is still an open question for scientists?  
   Options: Why some pairs of notes sound sweet to nearly everyone / Whether vibrations make sound / Whether a clap is louder than a whisper / Whether strings vibrate  
   **Answer:** Why some pairs of notes sound sweet to nearly everyone. *Why:* Scientists agree that vibrations make sound. Why certain pairs of notes feel sweet is still debated, with ideas from physics, the ear and learning.

## Applications
- Tuning musical instruments with a tuner that reads the hertz of a note, so that a tanpura, a guitar or a piano is in tune.
- Making flutes, bansuris and organ pipes, where the length of the air column and the places of the holes decide the notes.
- Designing silencers for scooters and cars, which cut down the pops and bangs of the engine.
- Building halls, studios and auditoriums, where scientists check which notes the room boosts, so that speech and music sound clear.
- Speech recognition and teaching of speech, which use the shapes of the vowels to tell sounds apart.
- Designing tall chimneys, bridges and cables so that wind swirls do not make them hum or shake.
- Stopping the squeal of brakes and the squeak of hinges, by changing the grip between surfaces.
- Making whistles, sirens and alarms that can be heard from far away.
- Using rosin on violin bows and chalk on climbing hands, which change how surfaces stick and slip.
- Using the hum and echo of a pipe or a tank to check its size and whether it is full.

## Spotted around us
- The sound of a jug or bucket filling from a tap, which rises in pitch as the air above the water gets shorter.
- A conch being blown at evening time in a temple, where the shell's own space picks the note.
- A pressure cooker's whistle, which is steam rushing through a small opening.
- Wind humming in a bamboo grove or across the top of a hollow gate pole.
- A bus brake squealing as it stops, because the brake pads stick and slip against the wheel.
- The crackle of Diwali fireworks, which are pops loud enough to hurt ears. Stay far away and cover your ears.
- Crickets chirping in the evening, rubbing a rough edge on one wing across ridges on the other.
- The squeak of rubber slippers on a wet bathroom floor.

## Open questions
- **Why do some pairs of notes sound sweet to nearly everyone?** Ideas from ratios, from the ear and from learning all have some evidence. No one idea explains it all.
- **Where does the flash of light come from when a snapping shrimp's bubble collapses?** Scientists found the flash in 2001. The bubble is tiny and lasts a tiny moment, and they are still working out the details.
- **How exactly do the biggest whales make their very deep songs?** Whales sing without opening their mouths, and their bodies are hard to study alive. Scientists are still working out how the sound is made.
- **Can a person who has never heard any music still find some sounds sweet?** It is hard to find people who have never heard music, so the question is hard to test fairly.
- **How does the brain turn a mix of overtones into the single sense of one note?** Scientists know the ear splits sound into pitches, but how the brain joins them back into one note is still being studied.
- **How many different sounds can one human voice make?** People keep inventing new sounds, from throat singing to beatboxing. No one has counted, and perhaps no one can.

## Connected maths
- A note of 440 hertz sounds for 5 seconds. How many vibrations is that? A honeybee's wings beat 230 times each second. How many beats in the same 5 seconds? **2200 vibrations for the note, and 1150 wingbeats for the bee.**. Each second has 440 vibrations. 440 times 5 is 2200. The bee beats 230 times each second. 230 times 5 is 1150. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A singer's Sa is 130 hertz. The upper Sa is one octave higher, so it vibrates twice as fast. What is the upper Sa? What would a note two octaves above the first Sa be? **The upper Sa is 260 hertz. Two octaves above is 520 hertz.**. One octave doubles the number: 130 times 2 is 260. Another octave doubles it again: 260 times 2 is 520. Two octaves is 4 times the first note. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- An empty bottle has an air column of 24 centimetres. Each 100 millilitres of water takes 4 centimetres off the air column. How long is the air column with 100, 200 and 300 millilitres? How many times shorter is the air column with 300 millilitres than the empty one? What do you expect the note to do? **20, 16 and 12 centimetres. The 300 millilitre air column is half as long. The note should rise, perhaps by about an octave for a plain pipe.**. 24 take away 4 is 20, then 16, then 12. 24 divided by 12 is 2, so the column is half as long. A shorter air column gives a higher note, and half the length is about one octave. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A singer's Sa is 240 hertz. Pa vibrates three halves as fast as Sa. How many hertz is Pa? The upper Sa is twice Sa. How many hertz is that? Which is bigger, the step from Sa to Pa or the step from Pa to upper Sa? **Pa is 360 hertz. The upper Sa is 480 hertz. The step from Sa to Pa is 120 hertz, and from Pa to upper Sa is 120 hertz too, but in ratio they are different.**. Half of 240 is 120, and 240 plus 120 is 360. Twice 240 is 480. 360 minus 240 is 120 and 480 minus 360 is 120. The steps are equal in hertz. But 360 to 240 is a ratio of 3 to 2, and 480 to 360 is a ratio of 4 to 3, so the ear hears them as different. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- You see lightning, and count 12 seconds before the thunder. Sound travels about a kilometre in 3 seconds. How far away was the lightning? If it was only 1 kilometre away, how many seconds would you count? **4 kilometres. For 1 kilometre you would count 3 seconds.**. 12 divided by 3 is 4. For 1 kilometre, 1 times 3 is 3 seconds. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)
- A finger takes half a second to cross 30 centimetres of window and squeaks at 800 hertz. How many sticks and slips happen in the sweep? How far does the finger move in each one? **400 sticks and slips. Each moves the finger 0.075 centimetres, which is less than 1 millimetre.**. 800 times a half is 400. The finger travels 30 centimetres in 400 steps. 30 divided by 400 is 0.075 centimetres. There are 10 millimetres in a centimetre, so that is 0.75 millimetres. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In a Whisper Vowels test, a listener got 24 out of 30 whispered vowels right. With three vowels to choose from, guessing alone would give about 10 out of 30. How many more than guessing is that? What share of 30 was right? **14 more than guessing. 24 out of 30 is 8 out of every 10, or 80 out of every 100.**. 24 minus 10 is 14. 24 out of 30 is the same as 8 out of 10, because dividing both by 3 gives 8 and 10. That is 80 out of every 100. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- A closed pipe gives a note of about 343 divided by four times its length in metres. What note does a closed tube 25 centimetres long give? What note does a closed tube 50 centimetres long give? **About 343 hertz for the 25 centimetre tube, and about 172 hertz for the 50 centimetre tube.**. 25 centimetres is 0.25 metres. Four times 0.25 is 1. 343 divided by 1 is 343. 50 centimetres is 0.5 metres. Four times 0.5 is 2. 343 divided by 2 is 171.5, which is about 172. Doubling the length halves the note. (queues `math.maths-and-world.maths-and-science.L4`)
