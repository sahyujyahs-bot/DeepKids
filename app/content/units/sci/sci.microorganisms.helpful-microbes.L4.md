# Idli batter, curd and bread: how tiny living beings make our food
`sci.microorganisms.helpful-microbes.L4`

**Summary.** Idli batter left on the counter overnight rises to nearly double its size and smells a little sour. Nobody added anything to it. Microbes, living beings too small to see, ate the sugars in the batter and made the bubbles. Your child tests what yeast needs to make gas, and what milk needs to turn into curd, with a fair test, a results table and a line graph. They find out how bacteria and yeast make idli, curd, dosa and bread, and how to tell fermented food from spoiled food. They learn how Louis Pasteur showed that living microbes do the work. They also meet the microbes inside us, in the soil and in medicines like penicillin.

**Intro.** *Look at a bowl of idli batter in the morning. Last night it was flat and thick. Now it is full of bubbles and has climbed up the sides. What made the bubbles?* Nobody blew into it, and nobody stirred it all night. Something alive was working in the batter while your family slept. Microbes, tiny living beings too small to see, were eating the sugars in the rice and dal. As they ate, they made a gas. The sticky batter trapped the gas, and the batter rose. In this unit you find out who the microbes are, what they eat, and how warm they like it. You test it yourself with a bottle and a balloon, and with a cup of milk.

## Terms
- **Microbe**: A living being too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria, yeast and moulds are microbes. *e.g.* One drop of curd holds millions of microbes.
- **Bacteria**: Microbes made of one small cell. Bacteria are found almost everywhere, and many are harmless or helpful. *e.g.* Lactic acid bacteria turn milk into curd.
- **Fungus**: A living being that feeds by absorbing food from what they grow on. Mushrooms, moulds and yeast are fungi. *e.g.* Yeast, a single-celled fungus, makes bread dough rise.
- **Yeast**: A fungus with one round cell that eats sugar and makes carbon dioxide gas and alcohol. *e.g.* Yeast in dosa or bread dough makes the bubbles that make it soft.
- **Fermentation**: When microbes eat the sugars in food and change them into gas, acid or alcohol. *e.g.* Idli batter ferments overnight and rises.
- **Carbon dioxide**: An invisible gas with no smell. People and other animals breathe it out, and yeast and bacteria make carbon dioxide when they ferment. *e.g.* The bubbles in idli batter and in a fizzy drink are carbon dioxide.
- **Lactic acid**: A mild acid that bacteria make from the sugar in milk. It gives curd its sour taste. *e.g.* Curd is sour because of lactic acid.
- **Starter**: A small amount of food that already has the right microbes in it, used to begin fermenting a new batch. *e.g.* A spoonful of old curd added to warm milk is the starter for new curd.
- **Gluten**: A stretchy protein in wheat flour. In dough it forms a net that traps gas. *e.g.* A soft bun is full of holes where gas was trapped in gluten.
- **Dormant**: Alive but resting, with almost no activity. Dry yeast in a packet is dormant. *e.g.* Dry yeast wakes up when you add warm water and sugar.
- **Spoilage**: When unwanted microbes grow in food and make it unsafe or unpleasant to eat. *e.g.* A chapati kept damp for three days grows mould and must be thrown away.
- **Pasteurise**: To heat milk or juice to kill harmful microbes, without cooking it. The word comes from Louis Pasteur. *e.g.* Packet milk is pasteurised before it is sold.
- **Antibiotic**: A medicine that kills bacteria or stops them growing. Many antibiotics were first found in moulds and soil microbes. *e.g.* Penicillin comes from a mould called Penicillium.
- **Decomposer**: A living being that breaks down dead plants and animals into simple parts that go back into the soil. *e.g.* Fungi and bacteria in the soil turn fallen leaves into soil.
- **Control**: The part of an experiment where you change nothing, so you can compare what happens. *e.g.* In the yeast test, the bottle with no yeast is the control.
- **Gut bacteria**: The trillions of bacteria that live in your intestines. Most are harmless, and many help break down food. *e.g.* Gut bacteria break down fibre that your own body cannot digest.

## Explanations
### Fermentation: microbes eat sugar and make something new · *defined*
**Definition:** Fermentation is when microbes eat the sugars in food and change them into other things, such as carbon dioxide gas, acid or alcohol.

A microbe is a living being too small to see. Microbes need food to live, just as you do. Many of them eat sugar. When yeast eat sugar, they make carbon dioxide gas and a little alcohol. When some bacteria eat sugar, they make acid. These new substances are what change the food. The gas makes dough and batter rise. The acid makes curd sour and thick. People did not invent fermentation. They noticed it, thousands of years ago, and learned how to look after it. Fermented foods are among the oldest foods people eat. Today we know which microbes are doing the work in many of them.
- e.g. Yeast fermenting sugar in dough makes carbon dioxide and a little alcohol, and the dough rises.
- e.g. Bacteria fermenting milk sugar make lactic acid, and the milk turns into curd.
- e.g. Palm sap, called neera, ferments within hours in the heat. It turns into toddy because yeast change the sugar in the sap into alcohol.
### Yeast: the fungus behind bread, dosa and appam · *defined*
**Definition:** Yeast is a fungus made of a single round cell. Yeast eat sugar and make carbon dioxide gas and alcohol.

A yeast cell is about one hundredth of a millimetre wide. You would need to line up about one hundred of them to cross one millimetre. A single gram of baker's yeast holds billions of cells. Dry yeast in a packet is dormant, which means alive but resting. Warm water and sugar wake the cells up. They sense the sugar, take it in and begin to grow. A yeast cell grows a small bump called a bud. The bud gets bigger and then breaks off as a new cell. In warm sugary dough, yeast can double in number in about an hour and a half. All the while they make carbon dioxide gas, and the gas makes the dough rise.
- e.g. Bread, pav, buns, naan and kulcha are often made with yeast.
- e.g. Appam batter is traditionally risen with toddy, which carries yeast, and many families now use a pinch of dry yeast.
- e.g. In the bottle and balloon test, the balloon fills with the carbon dioxide that the yeast make.
### Curd: bacteria turn milk sugar into acid · *defined*
**Definition:** Curd is milk that has been changed by lactic acid bacteria. The bacteria eat the sugar in milk, called lactose, and make lactic acid.

Milk is mostly water, with sugar, fat and protein dissolved or floating in it. The main protein is called casein. When you add a spoonful of old curd to warm milk, you add millions of lactic acid bacteria. They eat the milk sugar and make lactic acid. The milk slowly becomes more sour. When there is enough acid, the casein sticks together into a soft net. The net holds the water inside it, and the milk sets into curd. That is why curd is thick and tastes sour. A spoonful of old curd is called a starter because it starts the next batch. It works because the bacteria in the starter are alive. Plain boiled milk with no starter stays milk.
- e.g. Warm milk with one spoon of old curd sets overnight in most Indian kitchens.
- e.g. Buttermilk, lassi, raita and kadhi all begin with curd.
- e.g. Paneer is not made by bacteria. It is made by adding lemon juice or vinegar, which are acids, to hot milk.
### Idli and dosa batter: a team of microbes · *defined*
**Definition:** Idli and dosa batter ferments because many kinds of bacteria and yeast, which arrive on the rice, dal, utensils and air, eat the sugars in the batter.

Nobody adds yeast or bacteria to most home-made batter. Microbes are already on the urad dal, the rice and the grinder. Once the batter is mixed and kept in a warm place, they begin to eat. Scientists have studied idli batter in many kitchens. They found that a bacterium called Leuconostoc is often the main gas-maker, with other lactic acid bacteria and some yeasts working alongside. The mix is slightly different in every kitchen. The microbes make carbon dioxide gas and lactic acid. The urad dal makes the batter sticky, so the gas stays trapped in tiny bubbles. Steaming then kills the microbes and sets the idli into a soft, spongy cake.
- e.g. The batter rises in a few hours in a warm Chennai kitchen.
- e.g. The same batter can take a whole day in a cool Shimla winter.
- e.g. Dosa, uttapam and many other batters work in the same way.
### What microbes need: food, water and the right warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** Microbes grow best with food, water and the right warmth. Cold slows them down, and too much heat kills them.

Every living being needs food, water and a place to live, and microbes are the same. Most helpful microbes in the kitchen grow best at about the warmth of a warm room, around 30 to 40 degrees Celsius. In the fridge, at about 4 degrees, they still live but grow very slowly. That is why the fridge keeps food fresh for longer. Above about 50 degrees, most of them begin to die. At the temperature of boiling water, most of them are killed in minutes. That is why we boil milk before making curd, and why we cool it down before adding the starter. If the milk is too hot, the bacteria in the starter would die. The rule is the same for yeast.
- e.g. Curd sets overnight in a warm kitchen, but takes much longer on a cold winter morning.
- e.g. Dry yeast stirred into boiling water dies and does not make bubbles.
- e.g. Leftover cooked rice kept in the fridge stays safe for longer than rice left out in the heat.
### Trapping the gas: why dough and batter rise · *defined*
**Definition:** Dough and batter rise when carbon dioxide gas made by microbes is trapped inside something sticky or stretchy.

A gas made inside a runny liquid would simply bubble up and escape. To make bread or idli rise, the gas must be held in. Wheat dough holds it with gluten. Gluten is a stretchy protein that forms a net when you knead flour and water. The gas pushes into the net and stretches it, like blowing up a balloon. Urad dal batter holds the gas because dal makes the batter thick and sticky. Rice alone would not hold it so well. When you steam the idli or bake the bread, the gas expands and the heat sets the sticky net. It becomes firm and keeps its shape with all the holes inside.
- e.g. Bread cut open shows many holes where gas was trapped.
- e.g. Idli, dhokla and appam have a soft inside full of tiny holes.
- e.g. A balloon over a bottle of yeast and sugar water catches the gas and shows how much was made.
### Fermented food or spoiled food? · *defined*
**Definition:** Fermented food has been changed by microbes that we want. Spoiled food has been changed by microbes that we do not want.

Both kinds of food are changed by microbes. The difference is which microbes, and whether we chose them. In curd and idli batter, the helpful microbes make acid. The acid makes it hard for harmful microbes to grow. That is one reason curd stays safe for days. Spoilage is different. It happens when unwanted microbes grow, often because food was left damp and warm for too long. Fuzzy mould, a slimy surface, an unpleasant smell or odd colours are signs of spoilage. If you are not sure, do not taste it. Throw it away. Never cut off the mould and eat the rest, because mould threads can spread deep into soft food.
- e.g. Sour smell from batter or curd is expected.
- e.g. Fuzzy green, white, black or pink patches on bread, chapati or curd mean it must be thrown away.
- e.g. Leftover rice left in a warm kitchen all day can grow bacteria that cause food poisoning.
### Fermented foods across India · *defined*
**Definition:** Many regions of India have their own fermented foods, made with local grains, vegetables, soybeans, milk and fruit.

People in every part of India have learned to make food with microbes. In the south, idli, dosa and appam use fermented rice and dal batter. In Gujarat, dhokla is made from fermented gram flour batter. In Punjab, kanji is a sour drink made by fermenting black carrots in water with mustard. In Sikkim, kinema is made from fermented soybeans, and in Manipur hawaijar is made the same way. In Nagaland, akhuni is a fermented soybean paste. Gundruk, fermented leafy greens, is eaten in Sikkim and the hills nearby. Many of these foods keep well without a fridge. They also taste different from the plain ingredients they came from.
- e.g. Idli, dosa, appam and uttapam from the south.
- e.g. Dhokla from Gujarat, and kanji from Punjab.
- e.g. Kinema from Sikkim, hawaijar from Manipur and akhuni from Nagaland, all made from soybeans.
### Microbes inside you: gut bacteria · *debated*
Your intestines hold trillions of bacteria, more than the number of stars you could count in a clear night sky. Most are harmless, and many help you. Some break down fibre that your own body cannot digest. Some make vitamin K. Others take up space so that harmful bacteria have less room to grow. Scientists are now studying how much these bacteria affect our health, our mood and even our weight. This is a young science, and results are still being checked. Many claims in advertisements go further than the evidence. What scientists agree on is that a gut full of many kinds of bacteria is usually a healthy gut, and that eating many kinds of plant food helps.
- e.g. Gut bacteria break down some of the fibre in dal, vegetables and fruit.
- e.g. Curd and buttermilk contain living bacteria, and scientists are still studying what these do in the gut.
- e.g. A baby picks up many of her first gut bacteria from her mother, and from the people and the world around her.
- **Many gut scientists:** Gut bacteria clearly help with digestion and may affect health in many ways. We should study them carefully, and be honest about what we do not know yet.
- **Careful critics:** Many products sold as good for gut bacteria have not been tested well. Some studies were small or have not been repeated. A person should be careful before spending money or changing medical treatment.
### Other jobs of helpful microbes · *defined*
**Definition:** Microbes also break down dead plants and animals in the soil, clean water, and make medicines.

Without microbes, the world would be buried under dead leaves and animals. Bacteria and fungi in soil and compost break dead material into simple parts that plants take up again. Sewage plants use microbes to clean dirty water. In 1928, Alexander Fleming noticed that a Penicillium mould on a dish had killed the bacteria around the mould. This led to penicillin, the first antibiotic. Dorothy Hodgkin worked out the shape of the penicillin molecule in 1945. In 1954, India began making penicillin at Hindustan Antibiotics in Pimpri, near Pune. Antibiotics kill bacteria, but they do not work against viruses such as colds and flu.
- e.g. Compost heaps get warm because decomposers are working hard.
- e.g. Penicillin comes from the Penicillium mould.
- e.g. Vinegar is made when bacteria turn alcohol into acid.
### Choosing our microbes: pasteurised milk and starters · *defined*
**Definition:** Pasteurising milk kills most harmful microbes by gentle heating. After that, we choose the microbes we want by adding a starter.

Raw milk from a cow can carry harmful bacteria. Heating milk to boiling kills most of them, and packet milk is pasteurised in a factory, which means it is heated just enough to kill harmful ones. Louis Pasteur showed in the 1860s that gentle heating could stop wine from going sour, by killing the microbes that spoil it. The word pasteurise comes from his name. Later, dairies used the same idea for milk. After heating, the milk has almost no microbes left. We then add a spoonful of curd, which has the lactic acid bacteria we want. These bacteria grow fast, make acid, and leave little room for others. Cooks have used the same idea for centuries, long before anyone knew why it worked. They kept a bit of the last batch to begin the next.
- e.g. A kitchen starter kept going for many years from one batch of curd.
- e.g. Packet curd is made in a dairy with chosen bacteria added to pasteurised milk.
- e.g. Sourdough bread uses a starter that has been fed with flour and water for days, years or even centuries.

## Mostly agreed
- Fermentation is done by living microbes, such as yeast and bacteria. *How we know:* In 1857 and 1860 Louis Pasteur showed that fermentation stopped when the living microbes were killed, and started when they were added.
- The gas in bread dough and idli batter is carbon dioxide made by microbes eating sugar. *How we know:* The gas can be caught in a balloon, and it puts out a lit match. Without the microbes, no gas appears.
- Microbes grow fastest when it is warm, slow down when it is cold and die when it is very hot. *How we know:* Curd, batter and yeast tests have been done many times at different temperatures, and each time they show the same pattern.
- Lactic acid bacteria make milk sour and thick by turning milk sugar into lactic acid. *How we know:* The acid can be measured, and milk with the bacteria killed does not set.
- Most microbes are harmless or helpful. Only a small fraction cause disease. *How we know:* Scientists have counted the microbes in soil, water and our bodies, and found that far more are harmless than harmful.
- Fuzzy mould on soft food means the food should be thrown away. *How we know:* Mould sends thin threads deep into soft food, and some moulds make poisons that cooking does not remove.

## How we found out
**Louis Pasteur, a French chemist (1822 to 1895), 1857 to 1861.** For thousands of years, people made bread, curd and wine without knowing how it worked. In the 1830s, many scientists, including the famous German chemist Justus von Liebig, taught that fermentation was just chemistry. Sugar fell apart as it rotted, they said, and no living thing was needed. Louis Pasteur, a French chemist, did not agree. In 1857 he looked at fermenting milk under a microscope and saw tiny living things, which he said made the acid. In 1860 he showed that yeast cells grew and multiplied as sugar fermented. Then he boiled a sugar broth in a flask with a long, curved neck. Dust and microbes could not climb in, and the broth stayed clear for months. When he tipped the neck, microbes arrived and the broth turned cloudy. Fermentation needed living microbes.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought fermentation was only chemistry, a kind of rotting with nothing alive in it. Pasteur showed that it stopped when the microbes were killed, and began again when they were let back in.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Idli batter rises because of the grinding, or because of baking soda.** (Some people, and some recipe books.; *rejected*)  
  For: A fresh batter can puff up a little from air whipped in during grinding. Some quick idli recipes use baking soda or fruit salt.  
  Against: Batter ground and left in a cold fridge stays flat. Batter left in a warm place rises over hours, and smells sour. That points to living microbes, not air or a powder.
- **All microbes are germs, and we should kill them all.** (Many advertisements for cleaning products.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some microbes do cause illness, and washing hands and cleaning wounds matter.  
  Against: Most microbes are harmless, and many help. Curd, idli, bread, soil and our own gut depend on them. Killing all the microbes would also kill the helpful ones.
- **Fermented food is just food that has gone bad.** (Some people who have never made it.; *rejected*)  
  For: Both fermented food and spoiled food are changed by microbes, and both can smell sour.  
  Against: In fermented food, helpful microbes make acid that keeps harmful ones away. In spoiled food, unwanted microbes take over. People have eaten fermented food safely for thousands of years.
- **Eating curd or probiotic drinks every day is needed to keep your gut healthy.** (Some advertisements.; *debated*)  
  For: Curd has living bacteria and is a good food. Some studies find small benefits for some people.  
  Against: Many probiotic drinks have not been well tested. Your gut gets bacteria from many foods and from the world around you. A varied diet with plenty of plant food matters more than any one product.
- **Yeast and bacteria are only tiny machines.** (Many microbiologists.; *debated*)  
  For: Their responses can be explained by chemistry: sugar arrives, certain genes switch on, and the cell responds.  
  Against: Bacteria and yeast sense food and poisons, swim or grow towards food and away from poison, and signal to each other. The same chemistry is what any living being uses to sense and respond, including us.

## Roots
We have tested what yeast and curd bacteria need, and learned how they change our food. Now we ask what this tells us about who does the work, and who we are.

**Deep truth.** In the curd test, milk with a spoon of old curd set. The same milk without a spoon stayed milk. The spoon carried living bacteria, who changed the milk as they ate the sugar in the milk. A cook only makes the warm, sweet place where they can work.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'I made the curd' and 'the dough rose' as if the cook did all the work. The cook prepared the place. Living beings we cannot see did the changing.  
*Evidence:* In the curd test, the cup with a starter set and the cup with no starter stayed liquid. In the yeast test, the bottle with no yeast made no gas. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your gut holds trillions of bacteria. You are a home for living beings, and they help you digest your food.
- *what is life:* Yeast and bacteria sense sugar, grow, split and make more of themselves. They are tiny, and they are alive in the same way you are.
- *what is this world:* Most of the living beings on Earth are microbes. They have been working on this planet for billions of years, long before people arrived.

A spoon of starter curd carries bacteria from the last batch, and the batch before that, back through many kitchens. When one bacterium splits in two, no old body is left behind. Both new cells carry the same instructions, in a chain that goes back billions of years. Idli, curd and bread connect you to that long line of living beings, and to the people who kept a spoonful of the last batch for the next.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** A father asks his son to put salt in water, and to bring the water back the next morning. The salt cannot be seen. But the father asks the son to taste the water from the top, the middle and the bottom, and it is salty everywhere. The father teaches that something unseen can be in everything. (You cannot see the microbes in batter, but you know they are there because of what they do: the bubbles, the sour smell and the rise. The unseen is not absent.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, Peace Is Every Step (1991), chapter on interbeing:** Thich Nhat Hanh asks us to look deeply at a sheet of paper. If you look carefully, you can see a cloud, the sun and a tree inside the paper. Nothing exists alone. (In a bowl of curd you can find the grass the cow ate, the farmer, the sun and the bacteria. Each one is needed for the curd to exist.)
- **Lynn Margulis, Symbiotic Planet (1998):** Margulis, an American biologist, argued that life has been shaped as much by partnership as by competition. Her work showed that mitochondria, the parts of our cells that release energy, began as free-living bacteria that moved into other cells. (The bacteria in your gut and the mitochondria in your own cells show that bodies are built from living partners.)
- Science (settled): Mitochondria, which release energy in every cell of your body, began as free-living bacteria that moved into another cell more than a billion years ago.
- Science (open): How much gut bacteria shape our health and mood is still being studied. Scientists do not yet agree on how large the effects are.
- Other view, Many microbiologists: Yeast and bacteria respond to sugar through chemistry. Sugar arrives, certain genes switch on, and the cell grows. They would say this is an automatic process, like our skin healing a cut, and not a feeling or a choice.
- Practice, Listen to the batter (3 min): Find a bowl of batter that has been fermenting, or a glass of yeast and sugar water. Sit quietly beside it. Bring your ear close to the surface. Listen for a very quiet fizzing, like tiny bubbles popping. Close your eyes and breathe in slowly three times. Smell the faint sourness. Think about the many tiny beings inside, working together right now.
- Ask: If a spoon of curd carries the bacteria, is the new curd a new thing, or a continuing one?
- Ask: When you eat curd, are you eating a food, or meeting a community?
- Ask: What might a yeast cell be doing at the moment sugar arrives?

## Story: The Batter That Would Not Rise
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Idli batter rises because microbes make gas, and it rises slowly or not at all when it is cold. A spoonful of batter from an earlier day brings in microbes that are already active.*

In December, Thara and her mother travelled from Chennai to Shimla to stay with Reena Aunty. Thara was in Class 4. She had never seen snow, and she had never felt a kitchen so cold. Her breath made clouds in the air.

On the first evening, Thara and Reena Aunty decided to make idli, for the whole family. Reena Aunty had a mixer and a bag of idli rice. Thara had her grandmother's rule in her head. Three parts rice, one part urad dal. They soaked the rice and dal, ground them, added salt and poured the batter into a big steel pot.

'Now we leave it overnight,' said Thara. 'In the morning it will be double.'

In the morning, the batter was exactly where they had left it. It was flat, and smooth, and a little cold to touch. There were no bubbles.

'I must have ground it wrong,' said Reena Aunty, looking worried.

Raghu Uncle came into the kitchen with his tea. 'Add a pinch of baking soda,' he said. 'My mother always did.' He took a spoon.

'Wait,' said Thara. 'I think the batter is fine.'

'Then what is wrong?' asked Uncle.

Thara thought about what she knew. In Chennai, her grandmother's batter always rose by morning. In Chennai, the kitchen was warm even at night. She remembered how yeast made bubbles in warm sugar water, and not in cold. 'The batter is full of living microbes,' she said. 'They eat the sugar and make the gas. But they eat very slowly when it is cold. I think your kitchen is too cold for them.'

'Microbes,' said Uncle. He looked at the pot. 'You mean there are tiny living things in there?'

'Yes. The dal and the rice are covered in them. They were asleep all night, almost. They need a warm place.'

Reena Aunty thought for a moment. 'The oven,' she said. 'If I switch on only the little light inside, it gets warm, like the inside of a cupboard in summer.'

Thara put her hand inside the oven after a few minutes. It felt as warm as a kitchen in Chennai. They put the pot in, closed the door, and left a note on the oven so that nobody would switch on the heat.

That evening, they lifted the lid. The batter had risen right to the top, and the top was covered in tiny bubbles. It smelled a little sour, exactly like her grandmother's kitchen in Chennai Thara put her ear close, and heard a very quiet fizzing, like tiny bubbles popping.

They steamed the idlis and everyone ate them hot. Raghu Uncle ate four. 'I was wrong about the baking soda,' he said. 'The batter was not broken. It was just cold.'

Thara smiled. 'The batter was alive all the time. It just needed to be warm.'

Before bed, Thara told Reena Aunty one more thing her grandmother did. She always kept a spoonful of the old batter, to stir into the next one. 'She calls it the starter,' said Thara. 'It is already awake.' Reena Aunty found a small steel cup and set a spoonful of batter aside, in the fridge.

Reena Aunty went to bed smiling. Thara stayed in the kitchen a little longer, thinking about the microbes in the batter. They had been eating and making their gas all day. None of them knew about Shimla or Chennai. They only knew warm, and food, and each other.

**For the parent:** The batter was not broken. It was alive, and it was cold.

- Ask: What would you do if your batter would not rise in a cold house? Why would that work?
- Ask: How could you check whether a batter has living microbes in it, without tasting it?
- Ask: Why might a spoonful of yesterday's batter help today's batter rise?

### e2 · The Curd Test: does milk need a starter, and does it need warmth? (38 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Does boiled milk set into curd without a starter? And does it set faster in a warm place, on the counter, or in the fridge?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which cup will set first? Which will not set at all? Why do you think so?

## Applications
- Making curd, buttermilk, lassi and raita at home from a spoon of starter.
- Making idli, dosa, appam and dhokla from fermented batter.

- Making vitamins and some other nutrients in factories with the help of microbes.

## Open questions
- **Why does idli batter in different kitchens have different microbes, and does that change the taste?** Scientists have studied only a few kitchens. They do not yet know how much the microbes change from place to place or why.
- **How much do the bacteria in our gut change our health and our mood?** Studies often find links, but it is hard to tell which comes first. Many results have not yet been repeated.
- **How did the first people discover that a spoon of curd makes more curd?** No written records survive from that time. Archaeologists have found traces of fermented food that are thousands of years old, but not how it began.
- **How do yeast and bacteria sense and signal to each other?** Scientists have found chemical signals between bacteria, called quorum sensing. They are still learning how much of this goes on in food.
- **Could microbes in soil and compost help us grow food without chemicals?** Early results are promising, but scientists are still finding out which microbes help and which do harm.
- **Are there microbes no one has ever found?** Scientists think most microbes on Earth have never been grown in a laboratory, so they have not been described.

## Connected maths
- A balloon on a bottle of yeast, sugar and warm water measures 4 cm around at 10 minutes, 9 cm at 20 minutes, 15 cm at 30 minutes and 22 cm at 40 minutes. If it grows by the same amount in the next 10 minutes as it did in the last 10 minutes, how big will it be at 50 minutes? **29 cm.**. From 30 to 40 minutes it grew 22 minus 15, which is 7 cm. Adding 7 cm to 22 cm gives 29 cm. This is a guess from the line on the graph. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- A bacterium in warm milk splits into two every hour. Mehr, a girl in Class 4 in Hyderabad, starts with 5 bacteria. How many are there after 5 hours? **160 bacteria.**. The number doubles each hour. 5 becomes 10, then 20, then 40, then 80, then 160. That is 5 times 32, because doubling five times is multiplying by 32. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A dosa batter recipe uses 4 cups of rice for every 1 cup of dal. Preeti has 2 cups of dal. How many cups of rice does she need to keep the same ratio? **8 cups.**. She has 2 times as much dal as the recipe. So she needs 2 times as much rice. 2 times 4 is 8. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A recipe says to use 1 teaspoon of starter curd for every 250 millilitres of milk. How many teaspoons do you need for 2 litres of milk? One litre is 1000 millilitres. **8 teaspoons.**. 2 litres is 2000 millilitres. 2000 divided by 250 is 8. So you need 8 teaspoons of starter. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L4`)
- Sai sets curd at 9:30 in the evening. It sets after 9 hours and 15 minutes. At what time is it ready? **6:45 in the morning.**. From 9:30 pm, 9 hours later is 6:30 am. Then 15 more minutes makes 6:45 am. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Batter was 6 cm high at 8 in the evening, and 9 cm high at 8 the next morning. By what fraction of its starting height did it rise? Write it in its simplest form. **One half.**. It rose 9 minus 6, which is 3 cm. 3 out of 6 is 3 over 6. Both numbers divide by 3, so 3 over 6 is 1 over 2, one half. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Five friends each did the Curd Test and counted how many homes had set curd by the morning. Use the bar chart. Cups B and D were kept in the same place, but only B had a starter. How many more homes had set curd in cup B than in cup D? **4 more homes.**. The bar for cup B is 4 and the bar for cup D is 0. 4 minus 0 is 4. B and D were kept in the same place, so the difference comes from the starter. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- Louis Pasteur showed in 1857 that living microbes make milk turn sour. How many years before 2026 was that? **169 years.**. 2026 minus 1857 is 169. First 2026 minus 1800 is 226. Then 226 minus 57 is 169. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
