# Choosing materials: no material is best, so how do we choose?
`sci.materials.using-materials.L4`

**Summary.** Paper bends and never breaks. Dried flour paste snaps like a biscuit. Glue four layers of paper together with that paste, and the bar holds more than either one alone. So what is the strength of a bar made of two weak things? Your child makes bars, bends them to the limit and finds out. Then they ask a harder question that every maker faces: if no material is best at everything, how do we choose? They score materials for a job, follow a thing from the ground to the bin, and bury scraps to watch what time does to them.

**Intro.** *A steel tumbler never breaks, but it is heavy and it gets too hot to hold. A paper cup is light and cheap, but it goes soft and you throw it away. A clay cup keeps water cool, but it can crack. A plastic cup is light and tough, but it may stay in the ground for hundreds of years. Which cup is the best cup?* There is no best cup. Each one wins at something and loses at something else. In the Class 3 unit you matched properties to jobs, one job at a time. Real jobs ask for many things together, and the things pull against each other. Here you go a step further. You will score materials for a job, see why mixing two weak materials can make a strong one, and follow a thing from where it comes from to where it goes. Then you will bury scraps in soil and watch which ones come back to the earth.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can list what a job needs, score two or more materials against it, and say why the best choice can change when the job or the place changes.
1. I can explain what a composite is, and why two weak materials joined together can give a strong one.
2. I can explain what an alloy is, and why mixing metals can make them harder.
3. I can follow one object through its whole life, from its source to what happens after, and say what each stage costs.
4. I can tell the difference between a material that rots, one that can be made again and one that lasts for a very long time.
5. I can work out the cost of an object for each year or each use, and say why the cheapest to buy is not always the cheapest to own.
6. I can say what I do not know yet about a choice, and which test or fact I would need to find out.

## Terms
- **Requirement**: One thing a job needs from a material, such as strong, light, cheap or waterproof. A job usually has many requirements at once. *e.g.* A school bag must be light, strong, waterproof and cheap. Those are four requirements.
- **Trade-off**: When getting more of one good thing means getting less of another. Every material choice is a trade-off. *e.g.* A thicker steel plate is stronger but heavier. A thinner plate is lighter but weaker.
- **Decision matrix**: A table that scores each material on each requirement, so you can compare them side by side. The rows are materials and the columns are requirements. *e.g.* A table that scores steel, plastic and glass for a lunch box on cost, weight and safety.
- **Weight (importance)**: A number that says how much a requirement matters for this job. A requirement that matters more gets a bigger weight. *e.g.* For a hot-day water bottle, keeping water cool gets a weight of 3 and looks gets a weight of 1.
- **Score**: A number you give a material for one requirement. A higher score means it does better. *e.g.* Steel scores 5 for strong and 2 for light.
- **Composite**: A material made of two or more different materials joined together, so that the result can do what neither could do alone. *e.g.* Mud mixed with straw makes a brick that does not crack as easily as mud alone.
- **Fibre**: A very thin thread. In a composite, the fibres usually carry the pull. Straw, paper, cotton and glass fibre are examples. *e.g.* The paper layers in a papier-mâché bar are fibres.
- **Matrix**: The material that holds the fibres in place and passes the load from one fibre to the next. It is often a glue, a paste, mud or plastic. *e.g.* Flour paste is the matrix in a paper and paste bar.
- **Alloy**: A metal made by mixing two or more metals, or a metal and another element, such as carbon. An alloy is often harder than the metals it is made from. *e.g.* Brass is copper mixed with zinc. Steel is iron mixed with a little carbon.
- **Life cycle**: The whole life of an object: where its material comes from, how it is made, how it is used and what happens after. *e.g.* The life cycle of a steel katori begins as iron ore in the ground and ends as scrap that can be melted again.
- **Source**: Where a material first comes from, such as a tree, a field, the ground or a factory. *e.g.* The source of cotton is a plant. The source of plastic is oil or gas from the ground.
- **Renewable**: A source that can grow back or be refilled in a person's lifetime, such as cotton, wood or bamboo. *e.g.* Bamboo can grow tall again within a few years after it is cut.
- **Non-renewable**: A source that cannot be replaced within a human lifetime, such as oil, coal or metal ore. *e.g.* Oil took millions of years to form. We use it up far faster than it forms.
- **Biodegradable**: Able to be broken down by living things such as bacteria and fungi, and turned back into soil. *e.g.* A banana peel is biodegradable. A steel spoon is not.
- **Compost**: The dark crumbly soil made when plant and food waste is broken down by tiny living things, and which feeds new plants. *e.g.* Vegetable peels in a pot of soil turn into compost in a few weeks.
- **Recycle**: To break a used material down and make it into new things. Steel, glass, aluminium and paper can be recycled. *e.g.* Old newspapers are soaked and made into new paper.
- **Reuse**: To use a thing again for the same job or a new job, without breaking it down first. *e.g.* A glass jar that held pickle is reused to hold rice.
- **Repair**: To mend a thing instead of throwing it away. Repair uses less new material than making a new thing. *e.g.* A torn bag mended with a patch and thread is repaired.
- **Landfill**: A big pit or heap where rubbish is buried. Things that do not rot stay there for a very long time. *e.g.* Plastic bags that are not recycled often end up in a landfill.
- **Corrosion (rust)**: The slow eating away of a metal by air and water. Rust is the corrosion of iron and steel. *e.g.* A steel pin left in a damp place turns orange.
- **Embodied energy**: All the energy used to make a thing, from digging up the material to bringing it to the shop. *e.g.* Making a new aluminium can from ore takes far more energy than making one from old cans.
- **Cost per use**: The price of a thing divided by the number of times you use it. It is a fairer way to compare a cheap thing that breaks with an expensive thing that lasts. *e.g.* A cloth bag costs 40 rupees and is used 200 times. The cost per use is 20 paise.
- **Microplastic**: A tiny piece of plastic, smaller than a grain of rice. Big plastic things break into microplastics over many years, and the pieces get into water, soil and living bodies. *e.g.* A synthetic cloth sheds tiny threads in the wash, and the threads are microplastics.
- **Claim, evidence and reasoning**: Three sentences. The claim says what you think is true. The evidence says what you saw. The reasoning says why the evidence supports the claim. *e.g.* Claim: layered paper and paste is stronger. Evidence: the bar held three times the water. Reasoning: the paper carries the pull and the paste joins the layers.

## Explanations
### There is no best material, only trade-offs · *defined*
**Definition:** A trade-off means that getting more of one good thing costs you some of another. Every material choice has one.

Think about a drinking cup. Steel does not break and lasts for years, but it is heavy, it conducts heat to your hand, and it costs more. Glass is clear and does not change the taste, but it breaks. Paper is light and cheap, but it goes soft and is used once. Clay keeps water cool, but it cracks if it falls. Plastic is light and tough, but it can last in the ground for a very long time. Each one wins in some ways and loses in others. This is a trade-off: to get more of one good thing, you give up some of another. Because every material has a catch, choosing is a skill. First, you decide what the job needs. Then you decide which needs matter most. Only then can you pick the material that gives up the least of what matters.
- e.g. A steel tumbler is strong but heavy and hot to hold. A paper cup is light but weak.
- e.g. A thick glass bottle is safe from leaks but breaks if dropped. A plastic bottle is the other way round.
- e.g. A bicycle frame of thin steel tube is light and strong, but costs more than a heavy frame.
### A decision matrix: scoring materials for a job · *defined*
**Definition:** A decision matrix is a table that scores each material against each need, using weights for how much each need matters.

When a job has many needs, a table helps you compare. Put the materials down the side and the needs along the top. Give each need a weight from 1 to 3 for how much it matters. Then give each material a score from 1 to 5 for each need. Multiply each score by the weight and add up the row. The highest total is the best fit for this job. Suppose you want a tiffin box for school. Cost has weight 2, light has weight 2, does not leak has weight 3 and lasts has weight 1. A plastic box might score 4 on cost, 5 on light, 3 on leaks and 3 on lasting. A steel box might score 2, 3, 5 and 5. The matrix adds up the answers. But be honest: the scores are your judgement, not facts. Change the weights and the winner may change. That is useful, because it shows what the argument is really about.
- e.g. Plastic: 4 times 2, plus 5 times 2, plus 3 times 3, plus 3 times 1, which is 30.
- e.g. Steel: 2 times 2, plus 3 times 2, plus 5 times 3, plus 5 times 1, which is 30. It is a tie, and the weights decide.
- e.g. If the box will travel in a bag with the curry, the weight for leaks goes up and steel wins.
### The same job in a different place needs a different material · *defined*
**Definition:** The best material for a job depends on the place, the people and the price, as well as the job.

In Chennai in May, a school water bottle must keep water cool and survive a hot bag. In Leh in January, water in a bottle freezes, so the bottle must not crack when the water expands. A house in Cherrapunji, where the monsoon is very heavy, needs roofs that shed water and walls that dry. A house in the Thar desert needs thick walls that keep out the midday heat. The job looks the same on paper, but the place changes what matters. So does the price people can pay, and the materials near them. Mud and straw are free in a village. Steel and glass are cheaper in a city with a factory. A good engineer asks, 'where?' and 'for whom?' before asking 'which material?'. A choice that is smart in one place can be foolish in another.
- e.g. Thick mud walls keep the Thar desert's afternoon heat out of a house.
- e.g. Roofs in rainy Kerala and Meghalaya are sloped to shed water quickly.
- e.g. A thick down jacket is a good choice in Leh in January and a bad choice in Chennai in May.
### Composites: two weak things that make a strong one · *defined*
**Definition:** A composite is a material made of two or more materials joined so that the result can do what neither could do alone.

A composite is a material made of two or more different materials joined together. Usually one is a fibre and the other is a matrix. The fibre is a thin thread that is strong when pulled. The matrix is a paste, mud or glue that holds the fibres and shares the load between them. Mud alone cracks, because it is brittle and a crack runs straight through it. Straw alone can be pulled, but it has no shape. Mixed together, the straw stops cracks from running far, and the mud holds the straw in place. The brick is better than either part. Paper and paste work the same way. Concrete is a composite too: stone and cement are strong when squeezed, and steel bars inside carry the pull. Bone is a composite of hard mineral and soft protein. Wood is a composite of long cellulose fibres held by a stiff glue. Nature uses this trick everywhere.
- e.g. Mud and straw bricks, used in many villages, crack less than plain mud.
- e.g. Reinforced concrete is stone and cement with steel bars inside. Most big buildings use it.
- e.g. A fishing boat made of fibreglass is glass fibres held in hard plastic.
### Alloys: why mixing metals makes them harder · *defined*
**Definition:** An alloy is a metal mixed with other atoms. The odd-sized atoms stop the layers of metal from sliding, so the alloy is usually harder.

A pure metal is made of atoms packed in neat layers. When you push hard, the layers slide over each other, so pure copper and pure iron bend easily. An alloy is a metal mixed with other atoms of a different size. The odd-sized atoms get in the way when the layers try to slide, like marbles jammed in a stack of oranges. So the alloy is harder. Bronze is copper mixed with tin. Brass is copper mixed with zinc. Steel is iron mixed with a tiny amount of carbon, often less than two parts in a hundred. Bronze was one of the first alloys people made. The small bronze statue known as the Dancing Girl from Mohenjo-daro, about 4,500 years old, was made of a copper alloy. In India, bell metal, called kansa, is an alloy of copper and tin used for bells, gongs and dinner plates for centuries.
- e.g. Brass is harder than pure copper, so it is used for taps, locks and instruments.
- e.g. Stainless steel has chromium mixed in, which keeps it from rusting.
- e.g. Bell metal is copper with a good deal of tin, which makes a clear ringing sound.
### The whole life of a thing: source, making, use and after · *defined*
**Definition:** The life cycle of an object is its whole story: where the material comes from, how it is made, how it is used and what happens after.

Every object has a life. It starts at a source: a cotton field, an iron mine, an oil well. It goes to a factory or workshop where it is made, and this takes energy, water and labour. Then it is used, perhaps for days, perhaps for years. Finally it ends up somewhere: it rots, it is melted and made again, it is burned, or it sits in the ground. Looking at all four stages is called looking at the life cycle. A steel katori starts as iron ore, takes a lot of energy to make, lasts for decades and can be melted and made again. A plastic bag starts as oil or gas, takes little energy to make, lasts a few minutes of use and may stay in the ground for a very long time. A cotton bag starts as a plant, needs water to grow, and rots at the end. The cheapest at the shop is not always the cheapest for the world. Each stage has a cost, even if nobody sends us a bill for it.
- e.g. A paper bag takes trees and water to make, and it rots in soil in weeks to months.
- e.g. A cloth bag takes more energy to make than a plastic bag, but it can be used hundreds of times.
- e.g. A glass bottle is heavy to carry and takes energy to make. It can be washed and refilled, or crushed and melted into a new bottle.
### What happens after: rot, rust, remake or stay · *defined*
**Definition:** After use, a material may rot, rust, be remade or stay for a very long time. Which one depends on what it is made of.

After we finish with a thing, its material goes one of four ways. Some things rot. Banana leaf, paper, cotton cloth and vegetable peel are food for bacteria and fungi, who break them down into soil. Some things rust. Iron and steel combine slowly with oxygen and water and crumble. Some things can be remade. Steel, aluminium and glass can be melted and made into new things, again and again, without losing their quality. Paper can be pulped and remade a few times, but its fibres get shorter each time, so the paper gets weaker. Some things stay. Most plastics are made of very long, tough molecule chains that bacteria and fungi did not meet until the last hundred years or so, and so they cannot easily break them. Big plastics slowly crack into tiny pieces called microplastics. They get smaller but they do not disappear. A material that cannot rot has to be remade or kept in use, or it piles up.
- e.g. Banana peel, left in damp soil, rots within weeks.
- e.g. An aluminium can can be melted and made into a new can again and again.
- e.g. A plastic bottle can last for hundreds of years in a landfill, according to estimates, and no one has seen one finish.
### Can plastic be eaten? The race against plastic waste · *debated*
For many years, people thought no living being could break down plastic. In 2016, a team of scientists in Japan led by Shosuke Yoshida reported a bacterium, Ideonella sakaiensis, that they found at a plastic bottle recycling site. She could use the plastic in some bottles, called PET, as food. Her enzymes cut its long chains into smaller pieces. Other groups have found enzymes in other microbes, and have improved them in laboratories. Some wax worm caterpillars and some fungi have also been reported to chew some plastics. But the pace is slow, and it is not clear this can solve the waste problem. Many plastics are not PET. Some are mixed. Some contain additives. Scientists disagree about whether enzymes can clean up a landfill, or whether the better answer is to make less plastic, reuse it more and design new plastics that are made to break down. Both views agree that we do not yet have a simple fix.
- e.g. Some labs are making enzymes that break PET bottles down into their building blocks in a few days, which can then be made into new bottles.
- e.g. Cotton, wool and paper are made by living beings, and living beings can break them down. That is why they rot.
- e.g. Some new plastics made from plants are designed to break down, but many only do so in hot industrial compost.
- **Scientists who study enzymes:** Enzymes that break down PET could let us remake bottles from old bottles, so we do not need new oil. We should keep improving them.
- **Scientists who study waste:** Enzymes work only on some plastics, in the right place, at the right heat. The biggest saving is to make and throw away less plastic.
- **Product designers:** We should design things from the start for their whole life, so that the material rots or can be remade, and no clean-up is needed.
### Old choices, tested by long use · *defined*
**Definition:** Old choices of materials were tested by long use. Many have good reasons, and some beliefs about them still need testing.

Before laboratories and plastics, people chose materials by trying them for generations. Many of their choices have good reasons. Banana leaf plates are light, flat, waterproof enough for a meal and rot in a few weeks, so there is nothing to wash and nothing left. Clay pots keep water cool, because a little water seeps through the clay and dries on the outside. Coir ropes and mats are strong, do not rot quickly in salt water and come from a coconut tree that grows again. Mud and lime plaster let a wall breathe. Brass and copper vessels were used for water and cooking for a very long time. Not every old belief is proved. For example, many people believe that water from a copper vessel is healthier. Studies show copper can kill some germs on its surface, but the claims about health are still being debated. A good scientist honours old knowledge and tests it. Some of it turns out to be wise, and some of it does not.
- e.g. Banana leaf plates and areca leaf plates are used in many parts of India and are made of a leaf.
- e.g. Coir mats and ropes are made from the husk of coconuts.
- e.g. A clay kullad is used once and then returns to the earth as clay pieces.
### The Iron Pillar of Delhi: why has it hardly rusted? · *debated*
In Delhi, near the Qutub Minar, stands an iron pillar about seven metres tall. It was made more than 1,500 years ago, probably in the Gupta period. It has stood in the open air through monsoons for centuries, and it has not rusted away. Iron usually rusts in months when it is left out in the rain. So what is special? One explanation, from R. Balasubramaniam and others at IIT Kanpur, is that the iron has a lot of phosphorus in it, and a thin skin of a phosphorus compound formed on the surface and shielded the iron below. Other scientists have stressed that Delhi's climate is dry for much of the year, so the iron stays dry for months. Others note that the pillar is also protected by the oils and touch of many hands over centuries. Maybe all of these matter. It shows that a good material choice can be a combination of the right material and the right place.
- e.g. Iron with a lot of phosphorus, like the pillar's, is different from the low-phosphorus iron of most modern steel.
- e.g. Delhi has a dry climate for most of the year, which slows rusting.
- e.g. A coastal pillar in a damp place such as Mumbai or Kochi would probably rust faster.
- **Metallurgists at IIT Kanpur:** A thin protective skin containing phosphorus formed on the surface and slowed rusting. The metal itself was made in a special way.
- **Scientists who study climate and corrosion:** Delhi is dry for much of the year. Iron lasts longer where the air is dry, so the place did a lot of the work.
- **Archaeologists and conservators:** The history of the pillar, including how people have touched and treated it, matters too. We do not know the whole story.
### A material's real cost can show up later · *defined*
**Definition:** The full cost of a material may show up years after it is used. A test answers only the question it was built to ask.

For a long time, asbestos was a popular material in many countries, including India, for roof sheets and pipes. It does not burn, it is cheap and it is strong. Later, doctors found that tiny asbestos fibres in the lungs could cause serious illness, many years after breathing them in. Lead was added to paint and to petrol because it helped paint last and engines run smoothly. Later, scientists showed that lead harms children's brains, and most countries banned it. In each case, the first tests asked 'does it do the job?' Nobody asked, for years, 'what does it do to bodies, many years later?' A test answers only the question it asks. This is why engineers and doctors now try to look at the whole life, including the long-term effects. It is also why we should be careful with a new material, especially one that will be everywhere.
- e.g. Asbestos roof sheets were used in India for decades, and many are still on roofs.
- e.g. Lead in petrol was phased out in India in 2000.
- e.g. Some chemicals used in non-stick pans have been found in water and blood, and scientists are studying their effects.
### Cost per use: why the cheapest is not always cheapest · *defined*
**Definition:** Cost per use is the price of a thing divided by the number of times, or years, you use it.

A steel tumbler costs 120 rupees. A plastic tumbler costs 20 rupees. At the shop, the plastic one is six times cheaper. But the steel one may last 20 years, and the plastic one may crack in 2. So the steel one costs 6 rupees a year, and the plastic one costs 10 rupees a year. For a cloth bag at 40 rupees and a plastic bag at 2 rupees, the cloth bag has paid for itself after 20 shopping trips. After that, every trip is free. This way of thinking is called cost per use or cost per year. It is not perfect. It does not count the cost of cleaning and mending, and it does not count the cost to the world. But it is a fairer way to compare than price alone. Many families already do this without noticing, when they buy a good pair of school shoes.
- e.g. A pair of 600 rupee shoes that lasts 2 years costs 300 rupees a year.
- e.g. A pair of 300 rupee shoes that lasts 6 months costs 600 rupees a year.
- e.g. A pressure cooker bought for 1,800 rupees and used for 10 years costs 180 rupees a year.
### Reduce, reuse, repair, recycle: which comes first? · *defined*
**Definition:** Reduce means to make and buy less. Reuse means to use a thing again. Repair means to mend a thing. Recycle means to break it down and make something new.

People often say 'recycle'. But recycling uses energy and often makes weaker material, so it is the last of four steps. First is to reduce: do not make or buy the thing at all if you can do without it. Second is to reuse: use the thing again, perhaps for a new job. Third is to repair: mend it before it is thrown away. Last is to recycle: break it down and make something new from the material. A glass jar reused as a rice container uses no new energy at all. A shirt mended with a patch uses a bit of thread. Melting the same jar to make a new one takes a lot of heat. Metals such as steel and aluminium can be recycled many times with little loss. Paper loses strength each time. Mixed plastics are hard to sort and often cannot be made into good new things. The order tells us where the biggest saving is: not making in the first place.
- e.g. Reducing: carry a steel bottle so you never need a plastic one.
- e.g. Reusing: a glass pickle jar becomes a jar for rice.
- e.g. Repairing: a tailor mends a torn school bag.

## Mostly agreed
- No material is best at everything. Every choice gives up something. *How we know:* Every material tested by engineers has a weak side: steel rusts or is heavy, glass breaks, wood burns or rots, plastic lasts too long. Designers expect to trade one good thing for another.
- The best material for a job depends on the place, the people and the price, as well as on the job. *How we know:* Mud walls suit a dry desert village, steel and glass suit a city, and sloping tiled roofs suit rainy hills. The same job is done with different materials across India.
- Two materials joined together can do what neither can do alone. *How we know:* Mud with straw, concrete with steel bars, bone, wood and fibreglass are all composites. Their strength has been measured and is bigger than the strength of the parts.
- Mixing a metal with other atoms usually makes it harder. *How we know:* Bronze, brass and steel are all harder than the pure metals they are made from. Odd-sized atoms stop the layers of metal from sliding.
- Every object has a whole life: its source, its making, its use and what happens after. *How we know:* Engineers measure the energy and water used at each stage, and they measure what happens to a thing after it is thrown away. The measures differ widely from one material to the next.
- Some materials rot, some rust, some can be remade and some last a very long time, and this matters when we choose. *How we know:* Bacteria and fungi break down plant and animal materials. Metals and glass can be melted again. Most plastics resist breaking down and stay in the ground for a very long time.

## How we found out
**Per-Ingvar Brånemark, a Swedish doctor, and the colleagues who tested titanium after him, About 1952, with the first uses in people in the 1960s.** For a long time, surgeons believed the body would fight any metal put inside it. They expected scar tissue to wall it off, or the body to push it out. Around 1952, in Lund in Sweden, a doctor named Per-Ingvar Brånemark was studying how bone heals. He screwed a tiny titanium chamber into the leg bone of a rabbit, so that he could watch blood flow through a microscope. When the study was over, he tried to take the chambers out. They would not come out. The bone had grown tightly around the titanium and held it fast. Brånemark realised that bone could join itself to titanium without scar tissue. He called it osseointegration. Over the next years, with many tests, he and others made titanium roots for teeth, and later joints, which could last for years inside people. The old idea that metal in the body would always be rejected was wrong for titanium.  
*What people believed before:* That the body always walls off or pushes out metal placed inside it. In his rabbit studies, bone grew so tightly onto titanium that the chambers could not be removed. This showed that the right material can join the body.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Natural materials are always better than made ones.** (Many people, and many adverts that say 'all natural'; *rejected*)  
  For: Natural materials such as cotton, wood and clay come from sources that can grow back and usually rot at the end.  
  Against: Natural does not always mean harmless or low-cost. Asbestos is a natural mineral that harms the lungs. Growing cotton can use a great deal of water. Some made materials, such as a steel pan that lasts forty years, can be kinder in the long run than something natural that is used once.
- **The strongest material is the best material.** (A common idea, and the way many adverts talk; *rejected*)  
  For: Strength is easy to measure, and a stronger material will not break as easily.  
  Against: A super strong material may be too heavy, too costly, too hard to shape or too long-lasting. A school bag strap made of steel cable would be strong and useless. A good choice gives enough strength and then looks at everything else.
- **Plastic is the problem, so we should stop using it everywhere.** (Many campaigners, and many families; *debated*)  
  For: Plastic waste fills landfills and rivers, breaks into microplastics and lasts for a very long time.  
  Against: Plastic is also used to make helmets, syringes, water pipes and food packaging that keeps food from spoiling. The trouble is mostly the huge amount of plastic made to be used once and thrown away. Some scientists say we should use plastic where nothing else will do, and nowhere else.
- **Recycling solves the waste problem.** (Many people, and some companies; *debated*)  
  For: Recycling steel, aluminium and glass saves energy and raw material, and keeps them out of landfills.  
  Against: Recycling takes energy, and often makes weaker material. Paper fibres get shorter each time. Many plastics are mixed and cannot be sorted well. Only a small part of the world's plastic has ever been recycled. Many scientists say the first steps are to make less and to reuse and mend what we have.
- **Old, traditional ways of choosing materials are just superstition.** (Some people who prefer modern materials; *debated*)  
  For: Some old beliefs have not been tested, and a few are plainly wrong.  
  Against: Many traditional choices, such as banana leaf plates, coir ropes, mud and lime walls and clay pots, have good reasons that can be tested. The iron pillar in Delhi has stood for about 1,500 years. A careful scientist tests old ideas, and neither throws them all away nor believes them all.
- **If something rots, it is automatically the greener choice.** (Many shoppers; *debated*)  
  For: Things that rot return to the soil and do not pile up.  
  Against: A paper cup lined with plastic does not rot easily. Some 'compostable' plastics only break down in very hot industrial composters. Growing and carrying the plant needs land, water and fuel. The honest answer comes from looking at the whole life, and often the best choice is something used many times.
- **The cheapest thing to buy is the cheapest thing to own.** (Many shoppers, when money is short; *rejected*)  
  For: When money is tight, a low price today is a real help.  
  Against: A cheap thing that breaks soon may cost more per year than a dearer thing that lasts. Cost per use is a fairer way to compare. It is also unfair that poorer families often cannot afford the dearer thing that would save them money.

## Roots
We have made bars from paper and paste, scored cups and followed things from the ground to the bin. Now we ask what these tell us about the stuff we are made of.

**Deep truth.** A bar of paper bends and never breaks. A bar of dried paste snaps. Glue four layers of paper with that paste and the bar holds many times more than either. The strength is in how the parts are joined.  
*Habit it breaks:* We name what a thing is made of, 'it is paper' or 'it is paste', and think that tells us what it can do. That leaves out how the parts are put together, and one list of ingredients can give two very different things.  
*Evidence:* In the Paper and Paste experiment, the plain paste bar and the loose paper stack each failed early. The paper glued with paste held far more. Your own numbers may be different, and you should write them just as they are.

- *who am i:* Your bones are a composite of a hard mineral and a soft protein. The mineral alone would be brittle like chalk, and the protein alone would be bendy like rubber. Your bones work because the two are joined.
- *what is life:* Where two fig roots touch, they can join and grow into one thicker root. A living bridge is mended by the same roots that make it, and not by anything brought in from outside.
- *what is this world:* Mud with straw, steel in concrete, copper with tin: the world's most useful things are mixtures. What each mixture can do depends on how the parts are put together, as well as on the parts.

Every question in this unit came back to joining. Paper and paste, straw and mud, copper and tin, and a root and a tree are each more than their ingredients. The way parts are joined, the place a thing is used and the years it lasts decide what it is good for. A thing is not only what it is made of. It is also what it is made with, where it is, and what becomes of it. Choosing a material is therefore not looking up a number in a table. It is looking at the whole picture.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4 to 6.1.6, an Indian text more than 2,500 years old:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that by knowing one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay, since the different shapes are only names and the clay is the same. He says the same of gold and of iron. (A pot, a cup and a kullad are different shapes of one clay. Choosing a material means choosing the stuff, but what we make from it, and how, decides its use.)
- **Kabir, a poet-saint of the 15th century, in a well-known doha:** The clay says to the potter, why do you trample me? One day I will trample you. (Everything made from the earth returns to the earth, including the potter. What happens after is part of what a thing is.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 11, a Chinese text of about 2,300 years ago:** Clay is shaped into a pot, but it is the empty space inside that makes the pot useful. (A pot, a bridge and a school bag are useful because of how they are shaped and joined, as well as the material they are made from.)
- Science (settled): Composites such as reinforced concrete, wood, bone and fibreglass get their strength from how fibres and matrix share the load, and this has been measured many times.
- Science (debated): Why the Iron Pillar of Delhi has hardly rusted is explained by several ideas, including the iron's phosphorus, the dry climate and its history of care, and scientists still debate how much each matters.
- Science (open): Whether we can make strong materials that also rot or can be remade easily everywhere, at low cost, is not yet known.
- Other view, Many materials engineers: A composite's strength can be worked out from the strengths of the parts and the way they share the load, using a rule. So nothing new appears when parts are joined. The strength was already there in the numbers for the fibre and the matrix, and the maths just adds it up. They would say the new property is not a mystery, but a result you can calculate.
- Practice, Braid and pull (3 min): Take three loose threads or hair ties. Hold one thread at each end and pull gently. Notice how weak it is. Now braid the three together, and pull again. Feel how much stronger it is. Breathe in slowly and out slowly three times. Think of your family, or your friends, as threads. Each one is easy to bend, and together they hold.
- Ask: Is the strength of a braid in any one thread, or in the braiding?
- Ask: If you list everything that a bar is made of, have you told someone what it can do?
- Ask: Which part of your life is a composite, with parts that hold each other up?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Paper alone bends, dried paste alone snaps, and the two together are strong. Where does the strength of the bar live? (hint: Think about what the paper does when the bar is pulled, and what the paste does.)
- *deep:* If there is no best material, what does it mean to choose well? (hint: Think about how a decision table can be changed by what we care about.)
- *big:* Whose job is it to decide what matters most when we choose a material: the maker, the buyer, the neighbour who will live near the factory, or the children who will live with the waste? (hint: Think about who pays when a material's cost shows up later.)
- *big:* A bamboo bridge, a steel bridge and a living root bridge can all cross a river. Is a bridge that grows stronger every year cheaper or more expensive? (hint: Cost today and cost over fifty years can give different answers.)
- *big:* Plastic lasts almost for ever. Is that a gift or a curse? (hint: Think of a medicine bottle, and then of a bag used for ten minutes.)
- *deep:* Atoms in a banana leaf, a steel spoon and a plastic bag are all made of the same kinds of atoms. Why do they have such different lives after we are done with them? (hint: Think about how the atoms are joined, and who can break the joins apart.)
- *big:* Old people chose banana leaf plates for good reasons without ever using a laboratory. How do we know if a tradition is wise? (hint: Think about what you could test, and what you could not.)
- *big:* A material that did its job well turned out to harm people many years later. How can we choose well when we cannot see the future? (hint: Think about asbestos and lead. What could makers do before they use a new material everywhere?)
- *deep:* Everything we make comes from the ground, and goes back into the ground, the air or the sea. Is there such a thing as 'away' when we throw something away? (hint: Think about where the atoms of a plastic bag are a hundred years from now.)
- *big:* A girl in a village and a woman engineer in a city look at the same river. Why might they choose different bridges, and who should decide? (hint: Think about place, price, who will use it and who will look after it.)

## Story: The Bridge That Is Never Finished
*A made-up story. Living root bridges are real and are found in the Khasi and Jaintia hills of Meghalaya. People guide the roots of the rubber fig tree, Ficus elastica, across rivers, sometimes along hollow trunks of betel-nut palms, and the roots thicken and join as they grow. Some bridges are many generations old, and the largest take many years to become usable. Daiaphi, her grandmother, the engineer and the village are imagined, and the names are only meant to sound like names from the hills. The steel rusting in the heavy rain is real.*

Daiaphi lived in a village in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, one of the wettest parts of India. From June to September, the rain came down like a curtain, and the river below the village swelled until it roared. Every day, to reach her school on the far side, she crossed that river on a bridge made of roots.

The bridge was alive. It was made from the roots of a rubber fig tree that grew on the near bank. Long ago, the people of her village had led the roots across the river, along hollow trunks of betel-nut palm, and tied them in place. The roots had touched, and joined, and grown thick. Now the bridge was as wide as two people walking side by side, and it swayed a little, and it never broke.

Her grandmother, Kong Rimai, walked it every morning with a basket on her back. 'My grandmother helped to tie the first of these roots,' she said. 'I tied a few. You will tie some. This bridge is never finished.'

Daiaphi liked the idea. But one July, a flood came down the river and took away the planks of the old path that led to the bridge. A man arrived from the city of Shillong. His name was Mr. Das, and he was an engineer. He carried a roll of drawings under his arm.

'A steel bridge,' he told the village meeting. 'I can build it before next monsoon. Strong, quick, and no waiting. Steel is much stronger than roots.' Some of the men nodded. A bridge by next year sounded good.

Daiaphi was ten. She was sitting at the back. At school, her teacher had shown her a table with materials down the side and needs along the top. 'Excuse me,' she said, and everybody turned. 'What does the bridge need to do?'

They made the list on the ground with a stick. It must carry people and baskets. It must survive floods. It must last. It must not cost more than the village could pay. And it must not leave a mess when it was done. Mr. Das added one more: 'it must be ready soon'.

'So steel is strong,' said Daiaphi, drawing columns in the mud. 'Steel is quick. What happens to steel here?' She pointed to the iron railing by the school gate. It had been painted two years before. Orange rust had come through the paint in patches, and it flaked when she touched it.

Mr. Das went quiet. 'In heavy rain, steel needs paint every few years,' he admitted. 'And after thirty years or so, it must be replaced.'

'And where does the old steel go?' she asked. He rubbed his chin. 'To a scrap dealer in Shillong, if someone carries it that far. If not, to the river.'

Then Daiaphi took his hand and led him down to the root bridge. 'Hold this root,' she said. It was as thick as his leg. 'Pull.' He pulled. It did not move. 'This root was thinner than my arm when Kong's grandmother tied it. Every year it gets stronger. When a root is hurt in a flood, it grows back. And when the bridge is very old, it does not become rubbish. The tree is still alive.'

'But,' said Mr. Das, 'you wait twenty years for a new root bridge to be ready.' Kong Rimai had followed them. 'We wait for rice,' she said. 'We wait for children to grow. Waiting is not a cost, if you plan for it.'

Mr. Das opened his roll of drawings, and then he put it down. He took a pencil and a page. 'Teach me your table,' he said. So Daiaphi taught him. They put steel and roots along the side. They gave each need a weight. For 'ready soon', the weight was big. For 'lasts', and 'leaves no mess', the weight was big too. They gave scores. The totals were close.

'It depends,' said Mr. Das slowly. 'On what we count. If we count only next year, steel wins. If we count fifty years, the roots win.' 'Then count both,' said Daiaphi.

The village decided to do both. Mr. Das would build one small, light steel footbridge, quickly, for the village to cross while the old path was mended. And the next week, the village led new roots from the fig on the far bank, along fresh trunks of betel-nut palm, across the river. Daiaphi tied the first knot. It would not be ready for many years. By then she would be a grown woman, and her own children would cross.

**For the parent:** Steel wins when only next year counts. The roots win when fifty years count, and they leave no waste. The turn comes when Daiaphi asks what the bridge must do, and scores both materials on every need. The best material depends on what we count. So the village uses both, and plans for the long run.

- Ask: What needs did the village list for the bridge?
- Ask: Why did steel win when only next year counted, and the roots win when fifty years counted?
- Ask: What happens to the old steel, and what happens to an old root bridge?
- Ask: How did making a table help Mr. Das change his mind?
- Ask: Why did the village decide to build both bridges?

### e1 · Paper and Paste: is a layered bar stronger than its parts? (65 min, 3 days)
- **question:** A strip of paper bends easily. A dried bar of flour paste snaps easily. Is a bar of paper layers glued with paste stronger than either one alone, and what about paste with tiny fibres mixed in?

### e2 · Back to the Earth: what does soil do to our scraps? (55 min, 14 days)
- **question:** After two weeks in damp soil, which of six scraps will have rotted, which will have changed a little and which will look the same as the day you buried them?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which scraps will rot, which will change a little and which will stay the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each scrap is the same size and goes in the same kind of soil, in the same kind of cup, in the same place. Each cup gets the same amount of water on the same days. One cup holds soil with nothing in it, as a control. A spare piece of each scrap is kept dry in an envelope to compare at the end. Only the scrap changes.
- **safety:** Wash your hands after touching soil, and do not put your fingers in your mouth. Do not sniff the cups, because mould spores are not good to breathe in. Use gloves when you dig out the scraps. A grown-up should dig if the child has asthma or a mould allergy. Banana peel can attract fruit flies, so keep the cups loosely covered. A grown-up should cut the foil and plastic with scissors. Keep the cups away from babies and pets. Throw the soil and scraps away outside when you finish.
- **record:** Write down what you see through the side of each cup on day 7. On day 14, give each scrap a score from 0 to 4. Tear each dug-up piece and its spare, and write which is easier to tear. Draw a table, and then sort the scraps into rots, changed a little and unchanged.
- **results table:** Scrap | Guess | Looked like on day 7 (through the cup) | Score on day 14, from 0 for unchanged to 4 for gone | How easily it tore on day 14, compared with the spare piece
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Bury six scraps in damp soil, and a seventh cup with soil only. (19 min); day 7: Look through the side of each cup. Do not dig. (8 min); day 14: Dig out each scrap, score it, tear it and compare it with its spare. Sort the scraps and write what you found. (28 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the six scraps. Ask for a guess and a reason for each: rots, changes a little, or stays the same.  
  *Parent says:* Here are six scraps. We will bury them in damp soil. Which will rot? Which will stay the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Six scraps. Which will rot? Which will stay the same? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that scrap will rot? (good: It comes from a plant.; It is soft.; It is food.)
  - Ask: Why do you think that scrap will stay the same? (good: It is plastic.; It is metal.; It is man-made.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Write each guess in the table. Some children think that anything from a plant rots at the same speed. We will see.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut each scrap to about 5 centimetres square. 2. Cut a spare piece of each and put the spares in an envelope. 3. Fill each cup three-quarters full with soil. 4. Dampen the soil with a spoon of water. 5. Press a scrap against the glass of each cup and cover it with soil.  
  *Parent says:* Fill each cup with soil. Dampen the soil. Press one scrap against the glass. Cover it. Label the cup.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the cups. Press a scrap against the glass. Cover it.
  - Note: Seven cups: one for each of six scraps, and one with soil only. Put the cups in a warm, shady place such as a balcony or a windowsill, but not in direct hot sun. Loosely cover the tops with a plate or paper, so that flies stay out and air gets in. With 2 to 4 children, everyone makes a guess for all six scraps, but each child buries and looks after only two cups. One child looks after the soil-only cup. On day 14 each child reports to the others.
  - Check: Why do we keep a cup with soil only and no scrap? → **As a control, to see what the soil does by itself**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every three days, touch the soil. It should feel like a squeezed-out sponge. If it feels dry, sprinkle one or two spoons of water on the cup. If it feels wet or sticky, add no water and leave the cover off for a day. In hot dry weather you may need water more often. In the monsoon, you need less. Write the date on the label.  
  *Parent says:* Every three days, add one spoon of water to each cup. Keep the soil damp, not wet.  
  *Child mode:* Check the soil every three days. Add a little water if it is dry.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 7. Look at each cup from the side. Do not dig. Draw or describe each scrap. Which has changed?  
  *Parent says:* Day seven. Look from the side. Do not dig. What has changed? What has stayed the same?  
  *Child mode:* Look from the side. Do not dig. What has changed?
  - Ask: Which scrap has changed the most so far? (good: The banana peel.; The newspaper.)
  - Ask: Which have not changed at all? (good: The plastic.; The foil.; The polyester.)
  - Note: By day 7, the banana peel is usually brown and soft, and may have white or grey mould. The paper may be darker and soft. The rest usually look the same. If you see mould, that is normal. Do not open the cups and do not sniff them. Which scraps changed first may differ in your home, as the weather changes the speed.
- **s5 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 14. Put on gloves. Lift out each scrap with a spoon and put it on a plate. Score it from 0 to 4. Tear it, and tear its spare, and compare. Write everything down. Then put it all in the outdoor bin.  
  *Parent says:* Day fourteen. Put on gloves. Lift out each scrap. Score it. Tear it. Tear its spare piece too. Which tears more easily?  
  *Child mode:* Wear gloves. Lift out each scrap. Score it. Tear it.
  - Note: Score 0 is unchanged. Score 1 is stained or slightly soft. Score 2 is soft with holes. Score 3 is mostly gone. Score 4 is gone. Cotton often scores only 1 or 2 after two weeks. That is a real result. Cotton can take months to rot fully. Be honest about what you see.
  - Check: The polyester cloth looks the same after two weeks. Does that mean it will never rot? → **No. It means it did not change in two weeks, and tells us it changes very slowly or not at all**
- **s6 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results table. Sort the scraps into rots, changed a little and unchanged. Write a claim, evidence and reasoning, and one thing that your test could not tell you.  
  *Parent says:* Sort the scraps into three groups. Write a claim, evidence and reasoning. What did the test not tell you?  
  *Child mode:* Sort the scraps. Write claim, evidence and reasoning.
  - Ask: What do the scraps that rotted have in common? (good: They came from plants.; They were once alive.)
  - Ask: What do the scraps that stayed the same have in common? (good: They were made in factories.; They are plastic or metal.)
  - Note: A good claim: the banana peel and the newspaper rotted fastest because they come from plants, and bacteria and fungi can break down plant material. The foil and the plastic did not change in two weeks. Remind your child that cotton also comes from a plant, but is slower.

## Worksheets
### Choosing Materials (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which sentence best describes a trade-off?  
   Options: Getting more of one good thing means giving up some of another / Choosing the cheapest thing every time / Having two good materials and using both / Finding the one best material for every job  
   **Answer:** Getting more of one good thing means giving up some of another. *Why:* A trade-off means that gaining something costs you something else. A thicker steel plate is stronger but heavier. Picking 'the one best material' usually means thinking one material can win on every need.
2. (truefalse) A strong material is the best material for every job.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A strong material may be too heavy, too costly or too hard to shape for a given job. A school bag strap of steel cable would be strong and useless.
3. (fill) A table that scores materials against the needs of a job is called a decision ____.  
   **Answer:** ['matrix']. *Why:* A decision matrix puts the materials down the side and the needs along the top, with scores and weights in the boxes.
4. (sort) Is each of these a composite, or not?  
   **Answer:** Mud mixed with straw → A composite; A steel spoon → Not a composite; Concrete with steel bars inside → A composite; A glass tumbler → Not a composite; Bone, made of hard mineral and soft protein → A composite; A wire of pure copper → Not a composite. *Why:* A composite is two or more different materials joined, so each does part of the job. A steel spoon is an alloy and a glass tumbler is mostly one material, so neither is a composite.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Fibre → A thin thread that carries the pull; Matrix → The material that holds the fibres and shares the load; Alloy → A metal mixed with other atoms; Renewable → A source that can grow back in a lifetime; Landfill → A pit where rubbish is buried. *Why:* Each word names one idea: the thread, the glue, the mixed metal, the source that can come back and the place where rubbish goes.
6. (mcq) In a bar of paper layers glued with paste, which part is the matrix?  
   Options: The paste / The paper / The water poured in the cup / The books under the bar  
   **Answer:** The paste. *Why:* The matrix is the material that holds the fibres and shares the load. In the paper and paste bar, the paste is the matrix and the paper is the fibre.
7. (order) Put the stages of a thing's whole life in order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* The material has to come from somewhere first. Then it is made into a thing. Then the thing is used. Only after that does it end up somewhere.
8. (mcq) A banana peel is left in damp soil for two weeks. What will most likely happen to it?  
   Options: It rots, because bacteria and fungi break the peel down / It stays exactly the same / It turns into steel / It rusts  
   **Answer:** It rots, because bacteria and fungi break the peel down. *Why:* A banana peel comes from a plant, and living things such as bacteria and fungi break plant material down. Rust belongs to iron, not to peel.
9. (write) A plastic tumbler costs 20 rupees and cracks after 2 years. A steel tumbler costs 120 rupees and lasts 20 years. Explain which costs less for each year.  
   **Answer:** The plastic tumbler costs 20 divided by 2, which is 10 rupees a year. The steel tumbler costs 120 divided by 20, which is 6 rupees a year. So the steel tumbler costs less for each year, even though it costs more at the shop.. *Why:* Dividing the price by the number of years gives a fair way to compare a cheap thing that breaks with a dear thing that lasts.
10. (mcq) What is brass?  
   Options: An alloy of copper and zinc / A pure metal / A kind of plastic / A kind of stone  
   **Answer:** An alloy of copper and zinc. *Why:* Brass is copper mixed with zinc. The zinc atoms are a different size, so they stop layers of copper atoms from sliding, and the brass is harder than copper.
11. (truefalse) Steel is a pure metal with no other atoms in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Steel is iron mixed with a small amount of carbon. It is an alloy, and the carbon makes it harder than pure iron.
12. (fill) The four steps, in order, are reduce, reuse, ____ and recycle.  
   **Answer:** ['repair']. *Why:* Reduce means to make and buy less. Reuse means to use a thing again. Repair means to mend it. Recycle, the last step, means to break it down and make something new.
13. (sort) Where does each thing most likely end up? Does it rot, or can it be remade?  
   **Answer:** A banana leaf plate → Rots; A steel katori → Can be remade; A glass bottle → Can be remade; A vegetable peel → Rots; An aluminium can → Can be remade; A wooden spoon → Rots. *Why:* Plant materials rot, because living things can break them down. Steel, glass and aluminium can be melted and made again.
14. (mcq) Look at the table. How many times more water did the layered bar C hold than the plain paste bar A?  
   Options: 5 times / 3 times / 120 times / 8 times  
   **Answer:** 5 times. *Why:* Bar C held 150 millilitres and bar A held 30. 150 divided by 30 is 5. Picking 120 means taking away instead of dividing.
15. (draw) Draw the life of a cotton shirt in four boxes in a circle, from source to after. Write one thing in each box.  
   **Answer:** Source: a cotton field. Making: a mill and a tailor. Use: worn and washed many times. After: mended, passed on, or rotting in soil. Arrows go round the circle.. *Why:* A life cycle has four stages. Drawing them as a circle shows that what comes after can lead back to a source.
16. (perspective) A village needs a footbridge over a stream. People have different ideas.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer weighs the needs: how soon, how long, cost, what happens after. Bamboo is quick and cheap but rots in a few years. Steel lasts but needs paint and is hard to dispose of. Roots take longest but last longest and leave no waste. Each person is right about one need. A village could also choose two.. *Why:* The best choice depends on what the village counts. A decision matrix shows how the weights decide.
### Trade-offs and Whole Lives (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A plastic tiffin box and a steel tiffin box both score 30 in a decision matrix. What is the best next step?  
   Options: Look at the weights and ask which need matters most for this job / Pick the plastic one / Pick the steel one / Throw both out  
   **Answer:** Look at the weights and ask which need matters most for this job. *Why:* A tie shows that the scores alone cannot settle the matter. The weights, which say what matters most, can. If the box is carried with curry, 'does not leak' gets a bigger weight.
2. (mcq) A 600 rupee pair of shoes lasts 2 years. A 300 rupee pair lasts 6 months. Which costs less for each year?  
   Options: The 600 rupee pair, at 300 rupees a year / The 300 rupee pair, at 300 rupees a year / The 300 rupee pair, at 600 rupees a year / They cost the same  
   **Answer:** The 600 rupee pair, at 300 rupees a year. *Why:* 600 divided by 2 years is 300 a year. 300 divided by half a year is 600 a year. So the dearer shoes cost less each year.
3. (truefalse) Every time paper is recycled, the fibres get longer and the paper gets stronger.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each time paper is pulped and remade, the fibres get shorter and fewer, so the paper gets weaker.
4. (fill) A material made of fibres held by a matrix is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['composite']. *Why:* A composite is made of two or more materials joined so that the result can do what neither could do alone.
5. (sort) For a steel pot, does making the walls thicker make each of these better or worse?  
   **Answer:** How strong the pot is → Better; How light the pot is → Worse; How cheap the pot is → Worse; How long the pot lasts → Better; How fast the pot heats up → Worse; How well the pot keeps food hot → Better. *Why:* Thicker steel gives more strength and long life, and holds more heat. It also adds weight and cost, and takes longer to heat. That is a trade-off.
6. (write) Give two reasons why the best roof for a house in Cherrapunji might not be the best roof for a house in the Thar desert.  
   **Answer:** In Cherrapunji, the roof has to shed very heavy rain, so it is sloped and does not rot in the damp. In the Thar desert, the roof has to keep out the midday heat, so it is thick and made of a material that heats slowly. The place changes which needs matter most.. *Why:* The same job can need different materials in different places, because the needs have different weights.
7. (match) Match each place to a good reason for its choice.  
   **Answer:** A mud wall in the Thar desert → Thick walls heat slowly and keep the inside cool; Sloped tiles in rainy hills → Rain runs off quickly; A wooden chair in Leh → Wood is not icy to sit on; A clay matka in a village home → Water seeps through and cools the water; A steel bottle in a school bag → It does not break. *Why:* Each choice answers a need that the place makes important: heat, rain, cold, cooling and rough handling.
8. (mcq) Why does stainless steel rust much less than ordinary steel?  
   Options: It has chromium mixed in, which forms a thin protective skin on the surface / It has no iron in it / It is painted / It is made of aluminium  
   **Answer:** It has chromium mixed in, which forms a thin protective skin on the surface. *Why:* Chromium atoms in stainless steel form a very thin invisible layer that protects the metal below. Stainless steel is still an alloy of iron.
9. (order) Put these scraps in order, from rotting fastest to rotting slowest, in warm damp soil.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* A banana peel is soft food for bacteria and fungi. Paper is made from plant fibres, but is drier and harder. Cotton is a tougher plant fibre and takes months. Plastic does not rot at all in a short time.
10. (draw) Draw a mud brick with straw in it, and show a crack starting from one side. Show where the crack stops, and say why.  
   **Answer:** A rectangle with straw lines through it. A crack starts at an edge and stops when it meets a straw fibre. The fibre carries the pull and stops the crack from running through.. *Why:* In a composite, the fibres stop cracks from running straight through the matrix.
11. (truefalse) Aluminium can be melted and made into new things again and again with little loss of quality.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Aluminium is a metal, and its atoms stay the same when it is melted. Recycling it takes far less energy than making new aluminium from ore.
12. (mcq) In the past, tests of asbestos asked, 'does it do the job?' What question was missing?  
   Options: What does it do to people's lungs years later? / What colour is it? / How cheap is it? / How big is it?  
   **Answer:** What does it do to people's lungs years later?. *Why:* A test answers only the question it asks. Asbestos did the job, but doctors found years later that its fibres could harm the lungs. This is why engineers look at the whole life.
13. (perspective) Some campaigners want to ban all plastic. Others disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer weighs the harm against the benefits. The campaigner is right that single-use plastic is a huge problem. The doctor is right that some uses save lives. The designer's idea tries to keep what is useful and remove the harm.. *Why:* Banning all or keeping all are both too simple. Looking at the job, the whole life and the alternatives helps to decide.
14. (mcq) In reinforced concrete, what do the steel bars do?  
   Options: They carry the pulling, which concrete is weak at / They make the concrete wet / They make the concrete lighter / They hold the concrete's colour  
   **Answer:** They carry the pulling, which concrete is weak at. *Why:* Concrete is strong when squeezed but weak when pulled. The steel bars carry the pull. This is a composite at work.
15. (fill) A material that can be broken down by bacteria and fungi is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['biodegradable']. *Why:* Biodegradable materials return to the soil. A banana peel is one, and a plastic bag is not.
16. (write) Explain in your own words why a bar made of weak paper and weak paste can be strong.  
   **Answer:** The paste is brittle and snaps, and the paper bends. Together the paste holds the paper layers so they cannot slide, and the paper stops cracks from running through the paste. Each part does the job the other cannot do.. *Why:* A composite works because each material covers the other's weakness.
17. (mcq) After two weeks, the cotton cloth in the Burial Test looks hardly changed. Which claim is fair?  
   Options: It changed little in two weeks, and may take months to rot / Cotton never rots / Cotton rots faster than banana peel / The test failed  
   **Answer:** It changed little in two weeks, and may take months to rot. *Why:* Two weeks is a short time. A test shows what happened in that time only. Cotton comes from a plant and can rot, but much more slowly than a peel.

1. (mcq) What does it mean to say that a choice is a trade-off?  
   Options: Getting more of one good thing costs you some of another / One material wins at everything / Two materials are the same / The choice is wrong  
   **Answer:** Getting more of one good thing costs you some of another. *Why:* A trade-off means every choice gives up something. Picking 'one material wins' ignores the catch every material has.
2. (mcq) A decision matrix gives two cups the same total. What helps most to decide?  
   Options: Checking which need matters most for the job / Choosing the shinier cup / Choosing the heavier cup / Flipping a coin without thinking  
   **Answer:** Checking which need matters most for the job. *Why:* The weights say what matters most. When totals tie, the argument is really about the weights.
3. (mcq) A bar of paper glued with paste holds far more than a bar of dried paste alone. What best explains it?  
   Options: The paper carries the pull and the paste holds the layers so they cannot slide / The paper is a metal / The paste is stronger than steel / The bar is wetter  
   **Answer:** The paper carries the pull and the paste holds the layers so they cannot slide. *Why:* In a composite, each material covers the other's weakness. The fibre carries the pull and the matrix holds it in place.
4. (mcq) Why is bronze harder than pure copper?  
   Options: Tin atoms of a different size get in the way when layers of copper atoms try to slide / Bronze is lighter / Bronze is a plastic / Bronze is made colder  
   **Answer:** Tin atoms of a different size get in the way when layers of copper atoms try to slide. *Why:* Atoms of a different size jam the layers. This is how alloys are harder than pure metals.
5. (mcq) Which stage is not part of the whole life of a steel katori?  
   Options: A stage where the katori turns into a living plant / Mining iron ore / Making it in a factory / Using it in the kitchen  
   **Answer:** A stage where the katori turns into a living plant. *Why:* A katori's life is source, making, use and after. It never turns into a plant. It can be melted and made again.
6. (mcq) A banana peel, a steel can and a plastic bag are left in damp soil for a long time. Which statement is most accurate?  
   Options: The peel rots, the can rusts or is remade, and the bag stays a very long time / All three rot at the same speed / None of them changes / Only the bag rots  
   **Answer:** The peel rots, the can rusts or is remade, and the bag stays a very long time. *Why:* Living things break down the peel. Iron in the can slowly rusts. Most plastics resist breaking down. These are different endings.
7. (mcq) Shoes at 400 rupees last 2 years. Shoes at 150 rupees last 6 months. Which costs less for each year?  
   Options: The 400 rupee shoes, at 200 rupees a year / The 150 rupee shoes, at 150 rupees a year / They cost the same / The 150 rupee shoes, at 300 rupees a year  
   **Answer:** The 400 rupee shoes, at 200 rupees a year. *Why:* 400 divided by 2 is 200 a year. 150 divided by half a year is 300 a year. The dearer shoes cost less each year.
8. (truefalse) A test that shows a material did its job today proves it is safe for many years.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A test answers only the question it asks. Asbestos and lead did their jobs, and showed harm years later.
9. (mcq) Which comes first when we try to throw away less?  
   Options: Reduce: make and buy less / Recycle: melt it down / Repair: mend it / Reuse: use it again  
   **Answer:** Reduce: make and buy less. *Why:* The order is reduce, reuse, repair, recycle. Not making the thing in the first place saves the most.
10. (mcq) Why does the best desk for Leh differ from the best desk for Chennai?  
   Options: The place changes which needs matter most, such as cold or damp / Chennai children are bigger / Steel cannot be made in Leh / Desks are only made in Chennai  
   **Answer:** The place changes which needs matter most, such as cold or damp. *Why:* The needs change with the place, so the scores and the best choice change.
11. (mcq) Why is recycled paper often weaker than new paper?  
   Options: The fibres get shorter each time paper is pulped and remade / Recycled paper has no fibres / Recycled paper is thicker / Recycled paper is wetter  
   **Answer:** The fibres get shorter each time paper is pulped and remade. *Why:* Short fibres make fewer links, so the paper is weaker.
12. (mcq) What do the steel bars do in reinforced concrete?  
   Options: They carry the pull, which concrete is weak at / They make it heavier / They make it a liquid / They stop it drying  
   **Answer:** They carry the pull, which concrete is weak at. *Why:* Concrete is strong when squeezed and weak when pulled. The bars carry the pull. It is a composite.

## Applications
- Building houses, bridges and roads from composites such as reinforced concrete.
- Making bicycle frames, cricket bats and aeroplane parts from fibre composites that are light and strong.
- Choosing a material for a medical implant, such as titanium for joints and tooth roots.
- Designing packaging that protects food and can be remade or composted.
- Designing cars and trains with parts that can be taken apart and recycled.
- Making alloys such as stainless steel, brass and bronze for tools, taps, bells and statues.
- Planning a school's furniture to suit the local climate and budget.
- Working out the cost of a thing over its whole life when buying school shoes, bags and appliances.
- Setting up recycling for metals, glass and paper in towns.
- Repairing and reusing, as in cobblers, tailors and tinkers who mend things.

## Open questions
- **Can we make a plastic that is strong when we need it and rots when we are finished, in any ordinary place?** Some new plastics rot only in hot industrial composters. Scientists are still looking for ones that break down in soil, rivers and the sea without leaving tiny pieces.
- **Why has the Iron Pillar of Delhi lasted so long without rusting?** Several explanations may each be partly right, and scientists still debate how much each one matters.
- **Can enzymes from bacteria and fungi clean up the plastic already in the ground?** Enzymes work on some plastics in a laboratory, but nobody yet knows if they can work at the scale of a landfill or an ocean.
- **How do we find out the long-term harm of a new material before it is used everywhere?** Some harms, such as asbestos in the lungs, take many years to show. Scientists are still working out how to test for slow harm quickly.
- **Could we grow materials, like a living root bridge, instead of making them in factories?** Scientists are growing packaging from fungus and building materials from bacteria, but nobody knows yet how strong, cheap and widely useful they can become.
- **Is there an honest way to compare the costs of two materials when the costs fall on different people and different years?** Economists and engineers use different methods and disagree about which costs to count and how much the future should matter.

## Connected maths
- A steel tumbler costs 120 rupees and lasts 20 years. A plastic tumbler costs 20 rupees and lasts 2 years. How much does each cost for each year? Which costs less each year, and by how much? **Steel: 6 rupees a year. Plastic: 10 rupees a year. The steel tumbler costs 4 rupees less each year.**. 120 divided by 20 is 6. 20 divided by 2 is 10. 10 take away 6 is 4. So the steel tumbler costs 4 rupees less each year, even though it costs 6 times as much at the shop. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- In a decision matrix for a cup, the needs have weights: light 3, comfortable 1, does not leak 3, cost for each year 2 and what happens after 1. Steel scores 2, 3, 5, 4 and 4. Plastic scores 5, 4, 4, 3 and 1. Work out each total. Which wins? If the weight for what happens after goes up to 3, which wins now? **Steel 36, plastic 38, so plastic wins. With after at weight 3, steel is 44 and plastic is 40, so steel wins.**. Steel: 2 times 3 is 6, 3 times 1 is 3, 5 times 3 is 15, 4 times 2 is 8, 4 times 1 is 4. Add: 6, 9, 24, 32, 36. Plastic: 15, 4, 12, 6, 1, which adds to 38. If the last weight is 3, steel gains 8 more to make 44 and plastic gains 2 more to make 40. The winner flipped because what we care about changed. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In made-up Paper and Paste results, bar A held 30 millilitres and bar C held 150 millilitres. How many times more did bar C hold? Bar B held 8 millilitres. How many times more did bar C hold than bar B? Give the second answer to the nearest whole number. **Bar C held 5 times as much as bar A and about 19 times as much as bar B.**. 150 divided by 30 is 5. 150 divided by 8 is 18.75, which is about 19. So the glued layers held far more than either the paste alone or the loose paper stack. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Making a new aluminium can from ore uses about 100 units of energy. Making one from old cans uses about 5 units, as reported. If you have 100 units of energy, how many cans can you make from old cans? How many from ore? **20 cans from old cans, and 1 can from ore.**. 100 divided by 5 is 20. 100 divided by 100 is 1. So the same energy makes 20 times as many cans when they are made from old cans. The numbers are rough, and they differ in different studies, but the saving is big. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A cloth bag costs 40 rupees. A plastic bag costs 2 rupees and is used once. After how many uses has the cloth bag cost the same as buying plastic bags? After 50 uses, how many rupees has the cloth bag saved? **20 uses. After 50 uses, the cloth bag has saved 60 rupees.**. 40 divided by 2 is 20 plastic bags. After 50 uses, 50 plastic bags would cost 50 times 2, which is 100 rupees. The cloth bag cost 40. 100 take away 40 is 60 rupees saved. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)
- A family uses 3 plastic carry bags a day. About how many bags is that in a year of 365 days? In ten years? Round 365 to 400 to estimate. **About 1,200 bags in a year, and about 12,000 in ten years. The exact answers are 1,095 and 10,950.**. 3 times 400 is 1,200. Ten years is 12,000. The exact number is 3 times 365, which is 1,095, and then 10,950 for ten years. The estimate is a little high but close, and it is quick to work out in your head. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- In the Burial Test, 6 scraps were buried. 2 rotted a lot, 1 changed a little and 3 did not change. What fraction rotted a lot? What fraction changed at all? **One third rotted a lot, and one half changed at all.**. 2 out of 6 is the same as 1 out of 3. The scraps that changed at all are 2 plus 1, which is 3 out of 6, or one half. (queues `math.fractions.parts-of-a-whole.L4`)
- You bury the scraps on Monday 3 March. You look through the cups on day 7 and dig them out on day 14. What are the two dates? What day of the week is each? **Day 7 is Monday 10 March. Day 14 is Monday 17 March.**. 7 days after 3 March is 10 March. 14 days after 3 March is 17 March. Seven days is exactly one week, so the day of the week stays Monday each time. (queues `math.time.calendar.L4`)
- In this made-up example, a fig root is 4 centimetres across and gets 1 centimetre thicker every year. How thick is it after 15 years? In which year does it first pass 12 centimetres? **19 centimetres after 15 years. It passes 12 centimetres in year 9.**. 4 plus 15 is 19. To pass 12 centimetres, it needs more than 8 years of growth, so in year 9 it is 13 centimetres. The numbers are made up. Real roots grow at different speeds in different years. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.L4`)
- A sample of bone is made of about two-thirds hard mineral and one-third soft protein, in this made-up example. A sample weighs 300 grams. How many grams are mineral? How many are protein? **200 grams of mineral and 100 grams of protein.**. One third of 300 is 100. Two thirds is 2 times 100, which is 200. So 200 grams are mineral and 100 grams are protein. The two add up to 300. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a trade-off? → Getting more of one good thing means giving up some of another.
- What is a decision matrix? → A table that scores materials on the needs of a job.
- What is a composite? → Two or more materials joined so they do what neither could do alone.
- What does the matrix do in a composite? → It holds the fibres and shares the load.
- What is an alloy? → A metal mixed with other atoms, often harder than the metal alone.
- What are the four stages of a thing's whole life? → Source, making, use and after.
- What are the four things that can happen to a material after use? → It can rot, rust, be remade or stay a very long time.
- What does cost per use tell you? → The price divided by the number of uses, a fairer way to compare.
- What is the order of the four steps for less waste? → Reduce, reuse, repair, recycle.
