# Animal body plans: comparing bodies to find out who is related
`sci.animals.body-plans.L5`

**Summary.** A dolphin looks like a shark, but its flipper holds the bones of an arm and five fingers. What does that say about who is related? In this lesson you will survey the wrists of everyone at home, find leftover parts in your own body, and sort nine animals by what is inside.

**Intro.** *Touch the tip of your thumb to the tip of your little finger. Now bend your wrist a little towards you. Does a thin cord stand up in the middle of your wrist? Some people have this cord and some people have none. Why would a body part be there in one person and missing in another?* The cord is a tendon. A tendon is a tough string that joins a muscle to a bone. This one belongs to a small muscle in the forearm. People who have no such muscle grip just as strongly as people who have one. So why is the muscle there at all? The answer comes from comparing bodies. Orangutans climb with their arms, and they use this muscle fully. Humans share ancestors with orangutans, and those ancestors climbed. In this lesson you compare bodies the way scientists do. You survey the wrists in your family. You look for other leftover parts in a mirror. You also find out why a dolphin is a closer relative of a cow than of a shark.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name five things scientists check to describe a body plan: cell layers, gut openings, body cavity, nerve cord and skeleton.
1. I can name the nine biggest groups of animals and say one feature of each body plan.
2. I can name the four main features of the chordate body plan and find traces of them in my own body.
3. I can explain the difference between homologous parts and analogous parts, and say which kind shows that animals are relatives.
4. I can say what a vestigial part is, find some in my own body, and explain why bodies have them.
5. I can run a fair survey of a body part that differs between people, and say what my results show.
6. I can explain how embryos, fossils and DNA are used together with anatomy, and what scientists do when they disagree.
7. I can explain, with an example, why a body is an altered older body and not a new design.

## Terms
- **Body plan**: The basic layout of an animal's body: which main parts there are and how they are arranged. *e.g.* A crow, a frog and you share one body plan: a head end, a backbone and four limbs.
- **Phylum**: One of the biggest groups of animals. All the animals in a phylum share one body plan. The plural is phyla. *e.g.* Ants, crabs and spiders are in one phylum, the arthropods.
- **Embryo**: A very young animal who is just starting to form, before hatching or birth. *e.g.* Inside a hen's egg, the embryo is a small pale patch on the yolk in the first days.
- **Germ layer**: One of the first sheets of cells in an embryo. Each sheet goes on to build certain parts of the body. *e.g.* Your skin and nerves grew from the outer germ layer, and your muscles from the middle one.
- **Gut**: The space or tube inside an animal where food is broken down and taken in. *e.g.* Your gut is a tube about eight metres long, from your mouth to your anus.
- **Anus**: The opening at the end of the gut, where waste leaves the body. *e.g.* An earthworm has a mouth at the front end and an anus at the back end.
- **Body cavity**: A space filled with liquid between the gut and the body wall. Scientists call it a coelom (say SEE-lome). *e.g.* Your heart, lungs and intestines hang in body cavities, so they can move without moving your skin.
- **Nerve cord**: The main bundle of nerves that runs along the body and carries signals between the brain and the rest. *e.g.* Your nerve cord is your spinal cord. It runs down your back inside your backbone.
- **Notochord**: A firm, bendy rod that runs along the back of every chordate embryo, under the nerve cord. *e.g.* You had a notochord before you were born. The soft discs between your backbones grew from what was left of it.
- **Chordate**: An animal who has a notochord at some stage of life. Fish, frogs, lizards, birds and mammals are all chordates. *e.g.* A crow and a shark are both chordates. A crab is not.
- **Comparative anatomy**: The study of how the bodies of different animals are alike and different, part by part. *e.g.* Comparing a bat's wing with a human arm, bone by bone, is comparative anatomy.
- **Homologous parts**: Parts of different animals that are the same part, inherited from a shared ancestor, even if they now do different jobs. *e.g.* Your arm and a whale's flipper are homologous. They hold the same bones in the same order.
- **Analogous parts**: Parts of different animals that do the same job but are built differently, and were not inherited from a shared ancestor. *e.g.* A butterfly's wing and a crow's wing are analogous. Both fly, but only the crow's wing has bones.
- **Vestigial part**: A part that has lost most or all of the job it did in the animal's ancestors. *e.g.* The small pink fold in the inner corner of your eye is vestigial. In a crow the same part is a full third eyelid.
- **Tendon**: A tough string that joins a muscle to a bone, so that the muscle can pull the bone. *e.g.* The cords that move on the back of your hand when you wiggle your fingers are tendons.
- **Ancestor**: A parent, grandparent, or anyone further back in the family line that an animal comes from. *e.g.* Dolphins and cows share ancestors who walked on four legs more than 50 million years ago.
- **Fossil**: The remains or the mark of a living being from long ago, kept in rock. *e.g.* Fossil bones of early whales have been found in Kutch in Gujarat.
- **DNA**: The long molecule inside every cell that carries the instructions for building a body. It is passed from parents to young. *e.g.* Scientists compared the DNA of whales and hippos and found them to be close relatives.
- **Convergent evolution**: When animals who are not close relatives come to look alike over many generations, because they live in the same way. *e.g.* Sharks and dolphins both have a smooth, pointed shape for swimming fast.

## Explanations
### A body plan is set early, in the embryo · *defined*
**Definition:** A body plan is the basic layout of an animal's body. Its main features are set while the animal is still an embryo, a very young animal who is just starting to form.

You can see some of a body plan from outside: a ring or two matching sides, segments or none. Scientists also ask five questions about the inside. How many layers of cells does the embryo have? Does the gut have one opening or two? Is there a liquid-filled space round the gut? Where does the main nerve cord run? Where are the hard parts? Why do these features matter more than colour or size? Because they are decided very early in an embryo's life, and everything later is built on them. A change at such an early step would upset every later step. The usual explanation is that this is why body plans have stayed almost the same for hundreds of millions of years, while colour, size and shape have changed many times.
- e.g. A mouse and a blue whale differ hugely in size, but both embryos lay down a backbone, a skull and four limb buds in the same order.
- e.g. An ant and a crab look different, but both are built in segments with a hard outer skeleton and jointed legs.
- e.g. A builder can repaint a house easily. Moving the foundation means pulling the house down.
### Two layers of cells or three · *defined*
**Definition:** Germ layers are the first sheets of cells in an embryo. Jellyfish and their relatives have two germ layers. Most other animals have three.

Early in life, an embryo folds into sheets of cells. Heinz Christian Pander found three such sheets in chick embryos in 1817. Each sheet builds certain parts. In animals with a backbone, the outer layer makes the skin, the nerves and the brain. The inner layer makes the lining of the gut and the lungs. The middle layer makes muscle, bone, blood, the heart and the kidneys. A jellyfish embryo has only the outer and inner layers, with a jelly between them. Why does a third layer matter? It gives an animal thick muscles and solid organs between the skin and the gut. That makes fast swimming, digging and walking possible. This is a simplified picture. A jellyfish can still squeeze and swim, because some cells in her two layers can shorten the way muscle does. Sponges are different again. They have no germ layers at all.
- e.g. Your brain and your skin both grew from the outer layer, which is why both began at the surface of the embryo.
- e.g. The muscle you eat in a piece of fish or chicken grew from the middle layer.
- e.g. A jellyfish is mostly the jelly that lies between her two thin layers.
### One gut opening or two: the sac and the tube · *defined*
**Definition:** A sac gut has one opening, which takes food in and lets waste out. A tube gut has two openings: a mouth where food enters and an anus where waste leaves.

A sea anemone, a jellyfish and a flatworm each have a gut shaped like a bag. Food goes in through the mouth. What is left over comes back out through the same mouth. An earthworm, a snail, an ant, a starfish and you have a gut shaped like a tube. Food travels one way. Why does this matter? In a tube, the animal can eat again while the last meal is still being digested. Also, every bite passes the same places in the same order. So each part of the tube can do one job well: grinding at the front, breaking food down in the middle, taking in water near the end. You test this with a sock and a paper tube in the experiment Sac or Tube. A sac is not a worse gut. Sea anemones and their relatives have lived well with one for more than 500 million years. A sac suits an animal who waits for a big meal and then rests.
- e.g. A sea anemone who has eaten a small fish pushes the bones back out through her mouth.
- e.g. Your own gut has a mouth to grind, a stomach to churn and a long intestine to take food into the blood, in that order.
- e.g. An earthworm eats soil at the front end while leaving worm casts from the back end.
### The body cavity: a tube inside a tube · *defined*
**Definition:** A body cavity is a liquid-filled space between the gut and the body wall. Its scientific name is coelom.

Think of an earthworm as two tubes, one inside the other. The inner tube is the gut. The outer tube is the body wall, with its skin and muscles. Between them is a space filled with liquid. Why is the space useful? First, the gut can squeeze food along without the whole body having to move. Second, organs such as the heart have room to grow and to move. Third, the liquid cushions the organs from knocks. In a worm, the liquid also keeps the body firm, so that the muscles have something to push against. Your body has such spaces too. Your heart beats and your intestines churn all day, and your skin stays still, because they hang in liquid-lined cavities. A flatworm has no body cavity. The space between her gut and her skin is packed with cells. She also has no blood system, so every cell has to lie near the surface to get oxygen. That is why flatworms are thin and flat.
- e.g. After a meal your intestines squeeze and slide about, and you see nothing from outside.
- e.g. When a cook cleans a fish, the gut lifts out easily, because it lies loose in the body cavity.
- e.g. A flatworm is solid all the way through, like a folded dosa with no air inside.
### Which opening forms first: the mouth or the anus · *defined*
**Definition:** In an early embryo, a small dent forms and deepens into the gut. In starfish, fish, humans and their relatives, this first opening becomes the anus, and the mouth forms later.

An embryo starts as a hollow ball of cells. One side pushes inwards, as when you press a thumb into a soft ball. The dent becomes the gut. Its opening is the embryo's first opening. In starfish, sea urchins and all animals with a backbone, that first opening becomes the anus. The mouth breaks through later at the other end. Scientists call this group the deuterostomes, which means 'mouth second'. In snails, earthworms and insects, textbooks long said that the first opening becomes the mouth. That group is called the protostomes, 'mouth first'. This is a simplified picture. In some of the 'mouth first' animals, the first opening closes in the middle and gives both a mouth and an anus. Scientists now sort the two groups mainly by DNA. But the main point holds. A starfish begins life the way you did. So a starfish is a closer relative of yours than a snail or a bee is.
- e.g. A sea urchin embryo and a human embryo both make the anus first and the mouth second.
- e.g. A starfish has no head and five arms, yet her embryo makes these two openings in the same order as a fish embryo.
- e.g. An ant, a snail and an earthworm all belong to the other big group.
### The nine biggest groups of animals · *defined*
**Definition:** A phylum is one of the biggest groups of animals, and every member shares one body plan. Scientists count about 30 phyla. Nine of them hold most of the animals you will ever meet.

Here are the nine, with roughly how many kinds have been named. Sponges, about 10,800: no tissues, no gut, a body full of holes. Cnidarians, about 16,000: jellyfish, corals and sea anemones, with two layers, a ring shape and a sac gut. Flatworms, about 29,500: three layers, two matching sides, a sac gut and no body cavity. Roundworms, about 25,000: a tube gut and a smooth body with no segments. Annelids, about 17,000: earthworms and leeches, with a tube gut, a body cavity and segments. Molluscs, about 85,000: snails, clams and octopuses, with a soft body and often a shell. Arthropods, about 1,257,000: insects, spiders and crabs, with segments, jointed legs and a skeleton on the outside. Echinoderms, about 7,500: starfish and sea urchins, with five-part bodies and the anus formed first. Chordates, more than 70,000: fish, frogs, lizards, birds and mammals, with a rod along the back. More than 1.5 million kinds of animals have been named so far. About four in every five of them are arthropods.
- e.g. In a city flat you can usually find arthropods (ants, flies, spiders), chordates (people, crows, geckos) and sometimes a mollusc (a snail after rain).
- e.g. A bath loofah looks like a sponge, but it is the dried inside of a gourd, a plant.
- e.g. Coral reefs in the Gulf of Mannar and in Lakshadweep are built by cnidarians.
### The chordate body plan: four features you had before you were born · *defined*
**Definition:** Every chordate has four main features at some stage of life: a notochord, a hollow nerve cord along the back, openings or folds in the throat, and a tail that goes on past the anus.

A fish keeps all four features for life. You had all four as an embryo, and each one left a trace. The notochord is a firm, bendy rod along the back. Your backbone formed round yours, and what was left of the rod became part of the soft discs between your backbones. The hollow nerve cord became your spinal cord and brain. In the fourth week after you began, folds called pharyngeal arches formed in your throat. In a fish they hold up the gills. In you they became parts of the jaw, the three tiny bones of the middle ear and a small bone in the throat. You also had a tail. It showed most clearly when you were about 31 to 35 days old. It shrank, and its base is your tailbone. Chordates have a fifth feature too, a groove in the throat that became your thyroid gland. Why does every chordate share these? Because all chordates inherited them from the same ancestors.
- e.g. The lancelet, a small animal who lives in sea sand, keeps the notochord for life and never grows a backbone.
- e.g. A sea squirt has the four features only as a swimming larva, the young form. The adult sits on a rock like a small bag.
- e.g. The bones that carry sound through your ear grew from the same folds that hold up a fish's gills.
### Comparative anatomy: comparing bodies to find relatives · *defined*
**Definition:** Comparative anatomy is the study of how the bodies of different animals are alike and different, part by part. It is one of the main ways to find out which animals are relatives.

In 1555 the French traveller Pierre Belon drew a bird's skeleton beside a human skeleton and matched them bone for bone. In 1680 the English doctor Edward Tyson cut open a porpoise. He found a folded brain like the brains of four-legged land animals, and he placed the porpoise with them. That is the method. You look past the outside, which is shaped by how the animal lives. You compare the inside, part by part. Why does the inside tell you more? Because a way of life can change the outside quite fast. A swimmer becomes smooth and pointed, whoever her ancestors were. But the deep build of a body, such as which bones there are and how they join, is inherited and changes slowly. So when two animals share many detailed inside features that their way of life does not explain, the best explanation is that they inherited them from the same ancestors.
- e.g. A bat and a sparrow both fly, but a bat has fur, milk and four long finger bones in each wing. Those features put her with mice, not birds.
- e.g. A caecilian of the Western Ghats looks like a big earthworm, but has a skull, teeth and a backbone. Caecilians are amphibians, like frogs.
- e.g. A barnacle looks like a small shell on a rock, but the young swim on jointed legs. Barnacles are relatives of crabs.
### The same part, or the same job? Three checks · *defined*
**Definition:** Homologous parts are the same part in different animals, inherited from a shared ancestor. Analogous parts do the same job but were not inherited from a shared ancestor.

In 1843 the English anatomist Richard Owen gave the word homologue its meaning: 'the same organ in different animals under every variety of form and function'. How can you tell that two parts are the same part? Owen used three checks. Position: is the part in the same place, joined to the same neighbours? Make-up: is it built from the same pieces? Growth: does it form in the same way in the embryo? Your arm, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper pass all three. Each has one upper bone, then two bones, then many small bones, then finger bones. Each grows from a bud at the shoulder. A butterfly's wing fails all three. It has no bones, and it grows from the body wall. Here is a careful point. A bat's wing and a crow's wing are homologous as front limbs, because both inherited the limb. They are analogous as wings, because flying arose separately in bats and in birds. Only homologous parts show that animals are relatives.
- e.g. A mole's digging hand and a horse's front leg are homologous. The jobs differ and the bones match.
- e.g. A dolphin's tail fin and a shark's tail fin are analogous. The dolphin's has no bones in the blades and beats up and down.
- e.g. The spines of a cactus and the thorns of a rose both protect, but spines are changed leaves and rose thorns grow from the skin of the stem.
### One pattern, used twice in you: your arm and your leg · *defined*
**Definition:** Serial homology means that one body uses the same pattern more than once, in parts that repeat along the body.

You do not need another animal to compare bodies. Compare your arm with your leg. The arm has one bone above the elbow, two below it, a cluster of small bones at the wrist, five bones in the palm, and finger bones. The leg has one bone above the knee, two below it, a cluster at the ankle, five bones in the sole, and toe bones. Your thumb has two bones and each finger has three. Your big toe has two and each other toe has three. Why would a hand and a foot match so closely, when one holds a pencil and the other is walked on? Because both grow from limb buds that follow the same instructions in the embryo. The body uses one pattern twice and then changes each copy to suit a job. You check this on yourself in the activity Your Leg Is a Second Arm.
- e.g. Your elbow bends forwards and your knee bends backwards, because the arm buds and leg buds turn opposite ways before birth.
- e.g. The bones of your backbone repeat one pattern more than thirty times, changed a little from neck to hips.
- e.g. A prawn's feelers, jaws, walking legs and swimming legs are one repeated limb, changed for each job.
### Leftover parts: vestigial structures · *defined*
**Definition:** A vestigial part is a part that has lost most or all of the job it did in an animal's ancestors. Vestigial does not mean useless.

Look in a mirror at the inner corner of your eye. Beside the pink bump is a small pink fold. In crows, cats and crocodiles, that part is a full third eyelid that sweeps across the eye. In you only the fold is left. You have other leftovers. Three small muscles are joined to each ear. Most monkeys use theirs to turn the ears towards a sound. In people they do very little, though some people can wiggle their ears. Goosebumps are tiny muscles lifting your body hair. In a furry animal this traps warm air and makes the animal look bigger. Your tailbone is the base of a tail. The wrist muscle you look for in the Wrist Tendon Survey is missing in some people. Why are such parts still there? Because bodies are inherited. A part that is no longer needed is not removed at once. Over many generations it shrinks, and in some members of the family it is not built at all. Many leftover parts keep a smaller job. Your tailbone still anchors muscles.
- e.g. Pythons have two small spurs near the tail, the remains of hind legs. They are sometimes used in mating.
- e.g. Fish who live in dark caves have eyes that no longer see.
- e.g. An ostrich cannot fly, but uses her wings in displays and to keep cool.
### Embryos show what adults hide · *defined*
**Definition:** Comparative embryology is the comparing of embryos. Young embryos of related animals look much more alike than the adults do.

A young fish embryo, a young chick embryo and a young human embryo look much more alike than the adults do. Each has a row of folds in the throat and a tail. As they grow, they become more and more different. Why are embryos useful to someone who compares bodies? Because they show parts that the adult has lost or changed past recognising. A starfish embryo shows two matching sides. A barnacle's young shows jointed legs. A human embryo shows a tail. In 1866 Ernst Haeckel went further. He said that an embryo replays the adult forms of the animal's ancestors, one after another, so a human embryo passes through a 'fish stage'. That turned out to be wrong, and his drawings made embryos look more alike than they are. A human embryo never has gills and is never a fish. What is true is simpler. Embryos of relatives resemble each other, because they are built by inherited steps, and the earliest steps are changed the least.
- e.g. The folds in a human embryo's throat never open into gill slits. They become parts of the jaw, ear and throat.
- e.g. A dolphin embryo grows small hind limb buds, which then shrink away.
- e.g. The tail of a human embryo shows most clearly at about five weeks, and then shrinks.
### The same genes build very different bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** Many of the genes that lay out a body are shared by animals with very different body plans. A gene is one instruction in an animal's DNA.

A fly's eye is made of hundreds of tiny lenses. A mouse's eye has one lens, as yours has. For a long time scientists took these two eyes to be fully separate inventions. In 1995 Georg Halder, Patrick Callaerts and Walter Gehring switched on one gene, called eyeless, in the wrong parts of a growing fly. Extra eyes grew on the wings, the legs and the feelers. Mice have a matching gene, called Pax6. When the mouse gene was put into a fly, it also made eyes grow. They were fly eyes, not mouse eyes. So the gene does not hold the design of an eye. It works like a switch that says 'build an eye here', and each animal builds the eye of their own kind. Why does a fly's switch work like a mouse's? The best explanation is that both inherited it from one ancestor, more than 500 million years ago, who probably already used it for sensing light. Scientists call this deep homology: the parts look unrelated, and the instructions under them are shared.
- e.g. People born with a damaged Pax6 gene have eyes with no iris, the coloured ring.
- e.g. The Hox genes mark out head, middle and tail in flies, mice and humans.
- e.g. An octopus eye and a human eye arose separately, yet both use the Pax6 switch.
### When bones and DNA disagree · *defined*
**Definition:** DNA can be compared between animals, letter by letter, in the same way that bones are compared. Close relatives have more of their DNA in common.

Most of the time, bones and DNA tell the same story. Sometimes they disagree, and then scientists look for more evidence. Whales are the best example. Early whale fossils had teeth like those of an extinct group of wolf-like, hoofed meat-eaters. So for years those were taken to be the whale's ancestors. Then DNA put whales next to hippos, inside the group of even-toed hoofed animals such as cows, deer and pigs. Which was right? In 2001 two teams found early whales with legs in Pakistan. Their ankle bones had a double-pulley shape that only the even-toed hoofed animals have. The bones had agreed with the DNA all along. The right bone had not yet been found. DNA has also shown that termites are a branch of the cockroaches, and that falcons are closer to parrots than to kites and eagles. Why not use DNA alone? Because almost no fossil has any DNA left. For animals of the distant past, bones are all there is.
- e.g. Termites live in colonies as ants do, but a 2007 DNA study placed them inside the cockroach family tree.
- e.g. A pangolin has armour like an armadillo, but DNA puts pangolins closest to cats, dogs and bears.
- e.g. A swift looks like a swallow, but shares a very short, thick upper wing bone with hummingbirds.
### Is an insect built like us, but upside down? · *debated*
**Definition:** In an insect, the main nerve cord runs along the belly and the heart lies along the back. In an animal with a backbone it is the other way round.

In 1822 the French scientist Etienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire cut open a lobster and laid her on her back. In that position the order of her parts matched an animal with a backbone: nerve cord on top, gut in the middle, heart below. He said that all animals share one plan. In 1830 he argued this in public in Paris against Georges Cuvier, who said that body plans are separate and are shaped by what each animal needs. Most scientists of the time judged that Cuvier had won. In 1996 the question came back. Edward De Robertis and Yoshiki Sasai showed that the genes which decide 'back' and 'belly' in a fly embryo match those in a frog embryo, but work on opposite sides. You draw both layouts and turn one over in the activity The Upside-Down Test.
- e.g. When you peel a prawn, the dark line along the back is the gut, and the nerve cord is underneath on the belly side.
- e.g. Your own nerve cord is at your back, inside the backbone, and your heart is towards your front.
- e.g. In a fly embryo a signal on the back side stops nerves from forming there. In a frog embryo the matching signal works on the belly side.
- **Scientists who support the flip:** An ancestor of the chordates turned over, so that the old belly became the back. The matching genes on opposite sides are the evidence. The nerve cord of an insect and your spinal cord are then the same inherited part.
- **Scientists who doubt it:** The shared ancestor may have had only a loose net of nerves all over the body. Each group then gathered the net into a cord on a different side. On this view nothing turned over, and the two nerve cords arose separately from shared genes.
### A body is an altered older body, not a new design · *defined*
**Definition:** Each kind of animal comes from earlier animals by small inherited changes. So every body is an older body that has been altered, and it keeps signs of the older one.

An engineer who needs a new machine can start with a blank sheet. Living bodies never do. Each generation can only alter what the parents passed on. In 1977 the French biologist Francois Jacob put it this way: evolution works like a tinkerer, someone who makes do with whatever is already at hand. The clearest sign is a part that takes a strange route. A nerve runs from your brain to your voice box. In a fish, the matching nerve goes straight to the gills, passing a blood vessel near the heart. In animals with necks, the heart sits lower in the chest. The nerve still loops under that blood vessel. So it runs down the neck, round the vessel and back up. In a giraffe this detour is about 4.6 metres long. Nobody planning a giraffe from a blank sheet would lay the nerve that way. It makes sense only as a fish's layout, stretched.
- e.g. Your food and your air cross paths in your throat, which is why people sometimes choke. Lungs began as a pouch off the gut.
- e.g. A whale's flipper holds finger bones, though a whale never uses fingers.
- e.g. A panda strips bamboo with a 'thumb' that is a wrist bone grown long, because her real thumb was already lined up with the other fingers.
### What counts as 'the same part'? Nobody has a complete answer · *idea*
Comparative anatomy rests on one idea: two parts are the same part if they were inherited from one ancestor. Owen's three checks work well for bones. But the genes have brought puzzles. Sometimes two parts that are clearly the same part in two animals turn out to be built by partly different genes. Sometimes one gene, such as Pax6, helps to build parts that arose separately. So is 'sameness' in the bones, in the genes, or in how the embryo builds the part? Scientists and philosophers still argue about this. Most use all three kinds of evidence together and say plainly where they disagree.
- e.g. Insect wings may have grown from old leg parts, from the body wall, or from both. The evidence is still argued over.
- e.g. The earliest four-limbed animals had more than five fingers. Acanthostega, who lived about 365 million years ago, had eight on each hand.
- e.g. The three little bones in your ear match jaw and gill-arch parts in a fish. Are they still 'the same' bones?

## Mostly agreed
- The front limbs of all animals with a backbone and four limbs are built from the same bones in the same order. *How we know:* Pierre Belon matched a bird's skeleton to a human skeleton bone for bone in 1555. Every skeleton studied since, from frogs to whales, fits the same pattern of one bone, two bones, small bones and finger bones.
- Whales, dolphins and porpoises come from four-legged land mammals of the even-toed hoofed group. Hippos are their closest living relatives. *How we know:* DNA places whales next to hippos. Early whales with legs, found in Pakistan in 2001, have the double-pulley ankle bone of that group. Indohyus, a fox-sized fossil animal from Kashmir, has the thick ear bone otherwise known only from whales.
- Some healthy people have no palmaris longus muscle in one or both forearms, and their grip is just as strong. *How we know:* A study of 385 young adults in Jabalpur in 2010 found the tendon missing on one side in about 17 in every 100 people, and on both sides in about 3 in every 100. Surgeons often remove this tendon to repair another, and the hand works as before.
- A human embryo has folds in the throat and a tail, as a fish embryo has. *How we know:* The folds, called pharyngeal arches, appear in the fourth week. The tail shows most clearly at 31 to 35 days and then shrinks. Both can be seen in embryos under a microscope.
- A fly and a mouse use matching genes to start building an eye. *How we know:* In 1995 Georg Halder, Patrick Callaerts and Walter Gehring switched on the gene in other parts of a fly, and extra eyes grew there. The matching mouse gene had the same effect in a fly.
- Termites are a branch of the cockroaches. *How we know:* A DNA study by Inward and colleagues in 2007 placed termites inside the cockroach family tree, next to wood-eating cockroaches.

## How we found out
**Edward Tyson, Carl Linnaeus, Philip Gingerich, Hans Thewissen, Sunil Bajpai and A. Ranga Rao, From 1680 to 2007.** For a very long time people counted whales as fish, because whales live in the sea and have fins. In 1680 the English doctor Edward Tyson cut open a porpoise in London. He found a folded brain like the brains of four-legged land animals, and he placed the porpoise with them. In 1758 Carl Linnaeus moved whales into the mammals. But which mammals were their relatives? Early whale fossils had teeth like those of an extinct group of wolf-like, hoofed meat-eaters. So in 1966 Leigh Van Valen proposed that group as the ancestors. Later, DNA studies disagreed. They put whales next to hippos, among the even-toed hoofed animals such as cows and deer. In 2001 two teams found early whales with legs in Pakistan. Their ankle bones had a double-pulley shape that only even-toed hoofed animals have. In 2007 a team led by Hans Thewissen, with Sunil Bajpai, studied Indohyus, a fox-sized fossil animal from Kashmir. The skull had a thick ear bone otherwise known only from whales. The Indian geologist A. Ranga Rao had collected those rocks years before, and his widow gave them to the scientists.  
*What people believed before:* First, that whales are fish, because of their shape and their home. Tyson's dissection in 1680 showed a mammal's body inside. Second, that whales came from wolf-like meat-eaters, judged from teeth alone. Ankle bones found in 2001 and an ear bone described in 2007 showed that whales belong with the even-toed hoofed animals, as the DNA had said.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A whale is a kind of fish.** (Most people in Europe until the 1700s. Carl Linnaeus listed whales as fish in 1735.; *rejected*)  
  For: Whales live their whole lives in water and have fins and a fish-like shape.  
  Against: Whales breathe air with lungs, feed their young on milk, and have arm and finger bones in the flippers. Linnaeus moved them to the mammals in 1758.
- **An embryo replays the adult forms of its ancestors, so a human embryo passes through a fish stage.** (Ernst Haeckel, a German biologist, in 1866.; *rejected*)  
  For: Human embryos do have throat folds and a tail, as fish embryos do.  
  Against: The folds never become gills. Embryos resemble other embryos, not adult ancestors. Haeckel's drawings made the embryos look more alike than they are.
- **A vestigial part is a useless part.** (A common belief. In 1893 Robert Wiedersheim listed 86 parts of the human body as wholly or partly without a job.; *rejected*)  
  For: People with no palmaris longus muscle lose no grip strength. Cave fish with blind eyes get no sight from them.  
  Against: Many leftover parts keep a smaller job or gain a new one. The tailbone anchors muscles, and an ostrich uses her wings in displays. Jobs were later found for a number of the parts on old lists.
- **An insect is laid out like an animal with a backbone, turned upside down.** (Etienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, a French scientist, in 1822.; *debated*)  
  For: The genes that set 'back' and 'belly' in flies and frogs match, and work on opposite sides.  
  Against: The shared ancestor may have had no single nerve cord at all. Then each group built a cord separately, and nothing was turned over.
- **Now that DNA can be read, comparing bones is no longer needed.** (A view heard since DNA methods became cheap.; *minority*)  
  For: DNA corrected mistakes that anatomy had made about whales, termites, falcons and pangolins.  
  Against: Almost no fossil has any DNA left, so extinct animals can only be placed by their bones. In whales, an ankle bone found later agreed with the DNA.
- **The human body is the best-designed of all animal bodies.** (A common belief, old and widespread.; *rejected*)  
  For: Human hands and brains can do a very wide range of things.  
  Against: Human bodies carry the same odd leftovers as other animals: a nerve that takes a detour, food and air paths that cross, a backbone that began as a level beam and now stands upright. Humans are one animal among many, altered from older animals in the same way.

## Roots
In the Wrist Tendon Survey you looked for a muscle cord that some people have and some people lack. You also found a fold in your eye that is a full eyelid in a crow. Here we ask what these leftover parts tell us about what a body is.

**Deep truth.** In the Wrist Tendon Survey you looked for a forearm muscle that some people have and some people lack. Hands with no such muscle grip just as strongly. Orangutans, who climb with their arms, still use theirs fully. So a body holds parts whose main job belonged to ancestors who lived differently. No body is made new for the life it leads now: each one is an older body, altered.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of each animal as built for the way they live, part by part, the way a tool is made for a job. That mixes up what a part does now with why the part is there. Many parts are there because ancestors had them, and the present job came later or has almost gone.  
*Evidence:* Some wrists in the survey show the tendon and some may not, and studies find no loss of grip without it. The pink fold in your eye is a working third eyelid in a crow. A whale's flipper holds finger bones. In a giraffe, one nerve runs about 4.6 metres down the neck and back, on a route that is direct only in a fish.

- *who am i:* Your body is a record of your ancestors. The fold in your eye, the bump at the end of your backbone and the cord in your wrist come from animals who lived long before any human did.
- *what is life:* Life does not start again from nothing. Every living body was made by altering a body that already worked.
- *what is this world:* The animals around you are not separate inventions. A crow's wing, a dolphin's flipper and your arm are one limb, changed in three ways.

All animals alive today come from one group of small early animals. From that one beginning came many body plans: bodies in two layers and in three, guts with one opening and with two, skeletons inside and outside. Under these differences, the same instructions are still at work. A fly and a mouse use matching genes to start an eye, and a starfish makes her gut openings in the same order as you did. Many sages taught that one life takes many forms. Comparative anatomy shows a precise version of that. The many kinds of animals are one family, and each body still carries the marks of the family it came from.

- **Tiruvacakam 1 (Sivapuranam), lines 26 to 31, by Manikkavacakar (paraphrased):** The Tamil poet says that he has been born as grass, as a shrub, as a worm, as a tree, as many kinds of beast, as a bird, as a snake, as a stone and as a human. He has been through every kind of birth, among beings that move and beings that stay still, and he has grown tired. (The poem is about the soul's many births, not about bones. But it treats the worm, the bird and the human as stages of one traveller, not as strangers. Comparative anatomy finds its own version of this in the body: a worm's tube gut, the throat folds of a fish and a climber's wrist muscle, all in one human.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 18 ('Perfect Happiness'), in Burton Watson's translation (paraphrased):** The Chinese text says that 'the seeds of things have mysterious workings'. It describes a long chain in which one kind of living being turns into another, from water plants to insects and at last to leopards, horses and humans, who in time return to the same workings. (The details are not science. Leopards do not give rise to horses. But the idea is that each kind of living being comes out of an earlier kind, and that humans are inside the chain. Bones, embryos and DNA show the same thing in detail.)
- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), chapter 13:** Darwin asks: 'What can be more curious than that the hand of a man, formed for grasping, that of a mole for digging,' the leg of a horse, the paddle of a porpoise and the wing of a bat should all be built on one pattern, with the same bones in the same places? (Darwin's answer was that all five inherited the limb from one ancestor, and each kind then altered it. This is the main idea of this lesson, in the words of the scientist who first explained it.)
- Science (settled): All animals share ancestors. Matching bones, matching embryos and matching genes are the evidence, and they agree with each other.
- Science (debated): Whether the nerve cord of an insect and your spinal cord are one inherited part, turned over, or two parts that arose separately.
- Science (open): Why animals have only about thirty body plans, and why no new one has appeared for about 500 million years.
- Other view, Georges Cuvier, the leading anatomist in France in the early 1800s: Cuvier compared more animal bodies than anyone of his time. He held that each animal's parts are as they are because the animal's way of life requires them. On his view, parts that are alike show that needs are alike. They do not show shared ancestors, and he held that kinds of animals do not change into other kinds. Later evidence went against him on that. But his main point is still true and still used: the life an animal leads shapes every part of the body.
- Practice, Read your own hand (3 min): Sit still and hold one hand open in front of you. Breathe slowly. Open and close the hand three times. Watch the cords move on the back of it. Now think of a bat's wing. It has the same bones as this hand, grown long. Think of a whale's flipper. The same bones are inside it, grown short and wide. Look at your hand again for five slow breaths. Ask yourself quietly how old this hand is.
- Ask: If every part of your body came to you from ancestors, is there a part that is only yours?
- Ask: Your hand, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper are the same limb. Where does one animal's story end and the next begin?
- Ask: A leftover part has lost its old job. Does that make it less a part of you?

- *deep:* Some people have the wrist tendon and some have none. Which of them has the 'normal' human body? (hint: Think about whether 'normal' means the most common, the oldest, or the one that works. Do the three always agree?)

## Story: The Zigzag Line
*A made-up story. Yazhini, Kathir, Ammamma, her sisters and Saroja akka are imagined, and so is the mango with no stalk. These things are real: in a true Kanchipuram silk sari the body and the border are woven as separate cloths and locked together, the join is strong, and a zigzag line often marks where the body meets the end-piece, called the mundhi. Three shuttles are used, and a helper works one side of the loom.*

The trunk had not been opened since before Yazhini was born. It stood under the stairs of Ammamma's house in Kanchipuram, and when the lid went up it let out a smell of camphor and old silk.

'Somewhere in there,' said Ammamma, 'are three pieces of my mother's wedding sari.' She knotted her keys back into the end of her own sari. 'She cut it in three when we were girls, one piece for each daughter. My sisters sent theirs back to me long ago, and I put them away with everything else. Now Malar is getting married. I want the three together again before the wedding.'

'What colour was it?' asked Kathir. He was seven, and he already had both arms in the trunk.

'Arakku. The red of lac. With a gold border of mangoes.' Ammamma picked up her cloth bag. 'I am going for flowers. Find them, and do not fight.'

The door closed. Kathir found it in two minutes.

'Here!' He held up a wide piece of silk, red as a new brick, with fat gold mangoes curling along the edge. 'Red. Gold. Mangoes. We are finished. Can we have the murukku now?'

Yazhini wanted to say yes. But all through the last holidays she had sat in the weaving shed two doors down, beside Saroja akka's loom. She had watched three shuttles fly, one from Saroja akka's hand and one from the hand of the helper on the far side. And Saroja akka had told her something, without once looking up from the threads.

'Anyone can make a cloth that looks like ours from the front, kanna. Turn it over. With us the border is its own cloth. It grows beside the body, and we lock the two together, thread by thread, as we go. Tear the sari and the border still holds. And where the body meets the mundhi, the two colours meet in a zigzag. That line is how you know.'

Yazhini turned Kathir's red silk over and held it against the window. The red threads ran straight on into the gold, from one edge of the cloth to the other. There was no join. There was no zigzag anywhere. It was one flat cloth, made in one go.

'This was made to look like one,' she said. 'It is not one.'

'But it is red and gold, and it has mangoes!'

'So does half of Gandhi Road. Help me take everything out.'

They emptied the trunk onto the mat. There were pillow covers and a baby's cap. There was a child's long skirt, a pavadai, in a blue so dark it was nearly black. There was a bag stitched from brown cloth worn as soft as a petal. There was a cradle cloth the colour of weak tea. Kathir pushed all of these to one side, because not one of them was red.

Yazhini picked up the dark pavadai, only because its hem felt heavy. She turned the hem up. On the inside, along the edge, ran a line of tiny locked loops. The border was its own cloth. She held it to the light. Under the dark dye she could just see the shapes woven into it. Mango, mango, mango, each with a small curled stalk.

'Someone dyed it,' she said slowly. 'Someone who did not like red.'

Kathir was not listening. He was counting with one finger. 'Four, five, six. Akka. This one has no stalk.'

She looked. The seventh mango was bare at the top, as if the weaver had missed one small step long ago and never noticed. Seven more along, there was another bare one. And seven after that.

There was something else. Just under the waistband, right across the skirt, the dark cloth changed its weave along a line of small sharp points. Up, down, up, down. A zigzag.

'A skirt does not need that,' said Yazhini. 'Only a sari has a mundhi. This was the end of a sari before it was ever a skirt.'

After that Kathir would not let her touch anything first. He unpicked one seam of the brown bag with a safety pin. Locked loops. He counted mangoes with his nose almost on the cloth. 'Seven. No stalk!' He shook out the cradle cloth and counted again, and shouted so loudly that the neighbour's dog barked.

Then, because he was a fair boy, he went back to his own bright red piece and counted its mangoes all the way round. Every one of them had a stalk.

When Ammamma came in with jasmine in her bag, the red silk lay folded on the stairs. On the mat, in a row, lay a nearly black skirt, a brown bag with one seam open and a cradle cloth the colour of tea.

'What is this? I said red.' Then she stopped. She put down the flowers and knelt, which she did not often do now. 'That pavadai. Kamala wore that. She would never wear red, not from the day she could talk.' She turned up the hem and ran her thumb along the locked edge, and for a while she said nothing at all.

'How did you know?' she asked at last. 'They do not even look like each other.'

'We turned them over,' said Kathir.

Ammamma laid the three pieces end to end, the way they must once have lain on the loom. The bag came first, then the cradle cloth, then the skirt with its zigzag at the far end. Yazhini counted along the border, across the first cut and across the second. The mango with no stalk came round every seventh time, and it did not miss once.

**For the parent:** The colour and the use of each piece had changed completely, so looking at the outside gave the wrong answer. The way the cloth was built had not changed: the locked border and the same small fault repeated in all three. The zigzag in the skirt had no job there. It was left over from the time the cloth was a sari, and it showed what the skirt had once been.

- Ask: Why can two things that came from the same beginning end up looking very different?
- Ask: Why can two things with different beginnings end up looking the same?
- Ask: Which tells you more about where a thing came from: what it does now, or how it is built? Why?
- Ask: A fault that is copied again and again can be strong evidence. Why is a shared fault better evidence than a shared colour?
- Ask: Name a part of something in your home that has no job now and is left over from an older use.

### e2 · Sac or Tube: which gut can eat while it digests? (23 min)
- **question:** A sock has one opening and a tube has two. Which one gets twelve balls of 'food' digested sooner, and about how many times as fast?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. Which will finish twelve balls sooner, the sock or the tube? About how many times as fast? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Both guts get the same twelve paper balls. In both, a ball counts as digested only when it has stayed inside for a slow count of ten. The same person counts aloud at the same speed, one count each second, using the seconds on a timer. The same person moves the balls. With the sock, balls go in and come out through the one opening only. With the tube, balls go in at one end and come out at the other. Only the kind of gut changes.
- **record:** Write the seconds for each run in the table. Under the table, write how many times as fast the tube was as your best sock run. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Gut | Plan used | Seconds to digest 12 balls | Balls taken out too early
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the sock and say that it stands for a gut with one opening. Say that the tube you will make stands for a gut with two. Give the rule: a ball is digested only after a slow count of ten inside. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* A sea anemone's gut is a bag like this sock. Yours is a tube. Which will finish twelve balls sooner? How many times as fast? Why?  
  *Child mode:* The sock is a gut with one opening. The tube has two. Guess which is faster.
  - Ask: Which will be faster, and why? (good: The tube, because food can keep moving along.; The sock, because it is wider and holds more.; They will be the same, because each ball waits for ten counts either way.)
  - Note: Write the guess down. Many children expect no difference, because the count of ten is the same for both.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Crumple twelve paper balls, each about the size of a lemon. Number them 1 to 12. Roll the newspaper into a tube a little wider than a ball and tape it. Trim the tube so that it holds exactly five balls in a row.  
  *Parent says:* Make twelve paper balls and number them. Now roll a tube that the balls can slide through. It must hold five balls and no more.  
  *Child mode:* Make twelve paper balls and number them. Roll a tube that holds five balls.
  - Note: The tube must hold five balls, so that each ball spends ten counts inside. If the balls stick, roll the tube a little wider.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start with the sock. Your child may use any plan they like. Start the timer. Count aloud, one count each second. Stop when all twelve balls have been in for ten counts and are out again. Write the seconds and the plan.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the sock. Every ball must stay inside for ten counts. They go in and out through the top only. Use any plan you like. Go.  
  *Child mode:* Digest twelve balls in the sock. Each stays in for ten counts. Use any plan.
  - Note: Do not suggest a plan. Most children start with one ball at a time. If they pull a ball out before its count of ten, it goes back in, and you mark one in the last column.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what slowed the sock down. Let your child think of a faster plan and run the sock again. Time it and write it.  
  *Parent says:* What slowed you down? Can you think of a faster plan for the same sock? Try it.  
  *Child mode:* What slowed the sock down? Think of a faster plan. Try again.
  - Ask: With several balls in the sock, which one comes out first? (good: The last one that went in, because it is on top.; The first one in is at the bottom, so I must take the others out to reach it.; So new food comes out before it is ready.)
  - Note: The fastest plan is to put in several balls, wait for the last one to finish its count, and then empty the sock. While the sock is being emptied, it cannot take in food.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now the tube. Hold it level over the table. Start the timer. Your child pushes one ball in at the mouth end on every second count. From the sixth ball, each push sends the oldest ball out at the far end. Push the last five out with the ruler, one on every second count. Write the seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Now the tube. Push one ball in at this end on every second count. Watch what happens at the other end.  
  *Child mode:* Push one ball into the tube on every second count. Watch the far end.
  - Note: Check that the balls come out in the order 1, 2, 3. Each one has been inside for five pushes, which is ten counts.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the three times. Your child works out how many times as fast the tube was as the best sock run. Then point to three places along the tube and name them grind, break down and take in. Your child writes what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the three times. How many times as fast was the tube? Why? And what did every ball pass on its way through the tube?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the times. Why was the tube faster? Write what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain. The tube could take in a new ball while five others were still inside, and the oldest ball always left first. In the sock, the oldest ball was at the bottom, so new food had to wait or be taken out again. Counted on paper, one ball at a time takes about 170 counts, five at a time about 80, and the tube about 35. A sea anemone lives well with a sac. She eats a big meal now and then, and rests while she digests.
  - Check: Why can a tube gut have one part for grinding and another for taking food in? → **Every bite passes the parts in the same order**
  - Think: A sea anemone sits still and waits for food. An earthworm eats all night long. Which gut suits which life?

### a1 · Three Leftovers in the Mirror (10 min)
- **record:** Draw your eye with the pink fold in the inner corner, or tell your grown-up where it is. Write the three leftovers you looked for and one thing you wonder about each.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child looks in the mirror at the inner corner of one eye, the corner nearest the nose. There is a small pink bump. Just beside it, on the white of the eye, is a thin pink fold. Ask what they notice and what they wonder.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the corner of your eye, next to your nose. Find the pink bump. Now find the thin fold beside it. What do you notice? What might it be for?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the corner of your eye, near your nose. Find the thin pink fold. What might it be for?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the fold, and what do you wonder? (good: It does not move when I blink.; It looks like the edge of something.; Do other animals have one?)
  - Note: Let your child guess first. Then say that crows, cats and crocodiles have a third eyelid in that corner. It sweeps across the eye to clean and protect it. In humans only this fold is left. If a crow or a pigeon sits at your window, watch them blink. A pale film sometimes flicks across the eye.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child holds the ice cube against the back of the neck for ten seconds and watches one forearm. Look for small bumps and for hairs standing up. If none come, ask when they last had goosebumps.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the ice on the back of your neck and watch your arm. What happens to the hairs? What use could that be to an animal with thick fur?  
  *Child mode:* Hold ice on the back of your neck. Watch your arm hairs. What happens?
  - Ask: What use could standing hairs be to a furry animal? (good: Fluffed-up fur keeps more warm air in.; A cat looks bigger when her fur stands up.; We have so little hair that it hardly helps us.)
  - Note: Each body hair has a tiny muscle at its root. When the muscle pulls, the hair stands up and the skin makes a bump. Goosebumps do not always come with ice, so do not force it. A cold bath, a fright or a favourite song can also bring them.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child tries to move their ears without touching them, watching in the mirror. Then ask which of the three leftovers might be missing altogether in some people. Write the guess down for the Wrist Tendon Survey.  
  *Parent says:* Try to move your ears. Now think about the fold, the goosebumps and the ear muscles. Could a part like these be missing altogether in some people?  
  *Child mode:* Try to move your ears. Could a leftover part be missing in some people? Guess.
  - Check: What is the best explanation for the pink fold in the corner of a human eye? → **It is left over from a third eyelid**
  - Think: If a part has lost its old job, what might happen to it over thousands of generations?
### a2 · Nested Boxes: sort nine animals by what is inside (15 min)
- **record:** Keep the sheet with the four nested boxes. Under it, write which two of the nine animals are the closest relatives of each other on your sheet, and how you can tell.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the nine names on slips: sponge, jellyfish, flatworm, roundworm, earthworm, snail, ant, starfish, crow. Ask your child to sort them into groups by how they look. Ask for the reason for each group. Keep a note of the groups.  
  *Parent says:* Here are nine animals. Put the ones that look alike together. Tell me why you made each group.  
  *Child mode:* Sort the nine animals by how they look. Say why you made each group.
  - Note: Most children put the three worms together, and the starfish with the jellyfish. Say nothing yet.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay all nine slips on a sheet. Read out one clue at a time. For each, your child draws a box round the animals who have that feature. Clue 1: tissues and a gut. All but the sponge. Clue 2: three layers of cells in the embryo. All in box 1 but the jellyfish. Clue 3: a gut with two openings. All in box 2 but the flatworm. Clue 4: the embryo's first opening becomes the anus. Only the starfish and the crow.  
  *Parent says:* Now sort by the inside. I will read four clues. For each one, draw a box round the animals who have that feature.

  - Note: Each new box sits inside the one before. Box 3 still holds six animals: roundworm, earthworm, snail, ant, starfish and crow. Box 4 holds two. Four clues are far too few to sort the others. Scientists use hundreds of features, and DNA as well.

  *Parent does:* Compare the boxes with the first sort. Ask where the three worms ended up, and where the starfish is. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your first groups and at the boxes. Where did the three worms go? Who is the starfish with now? What does a small shared box tell you?  
  *Child mode:* Where did the three worms go? Who is the starfish with now?
  - Note: A long thin body with no legs suits burrowing and sliding, so it has arisen many times. The three worms belong to three different phyla.
  - Check: On your sheet, which pair shares the smallest box? → **Starfish and crow**
  - Think: The roundworm, earthworm, snail and ant are still in one box. What extra clue could split them?
### a3 · The Upside-Down Test: is a prawn laid out like a fish, turned over? (10 min)
- **record:** Keep the fish drawing. On the back, write the prawn's colour order from top to bottom. Then write one sentence for the idea that the plan was turned over and one sentence for the other explanation.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child runs a hand down the bumps of their own backbone. The nerve cord is inside those bones. Then they put a hand on the chest, where the heart is. On the first sheet they draw a fish from the side with three stripes: blue for the nerve cord on top, yellow for the gut in the middle, red for the heart below. A child who would rather not draw tells you the colours and you draw.  
  *Parent says:* Feel your backbone. Your main nerve cord runs inside it, along your back. Your heart is at your front. A fish is laid out the same way. Draw it.  
  *Child mode:* Feel your backbone. Your nerve cord is inside. Draw a fish: blue on top, yellow, then red. Or tell your grown-up what to draw.
  - Note: Blue is the nerve cord, yellow the gut and red the heart with its main blood vessel. The drawing is a simple map, not a true picture.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Beside the fish drawing, your child lays the three pencils in a row for a prawn: red on top, yellow in the middle, blue below. Ask what they notice.  
  *Parent says:* In a prawn, an ant or an earthworm, the heart runs along the back and the nerve cord along the belly. Lay the pencils like that: red on top, yellow in the middle, blue below. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the pencils for a prawn: red on top, yellow, then blue below.
  - Ask: What do you notice when you look at the two rows? (good: The same three parts are in both.; The order is the other way up.; The gut is in the middle in both.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child turns the pencil row upside down, so red is at the bottom, and compares it with the fish drawing. Ask what could explain the match. Then ask what does not fit: both mouths open on the belly side. After the flip, are the two bellies on the same side?  
  *Parent says:* Turn the pencil row upside down. What do you see now? What could explain it? A prawn's mouth and a fish's mouth both open on the belly side. After the flip, are the two bellies on the same side?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the pencil row upside down. Do the colours match the fish now? Where is the belly of each?
  - Ask: What could explain why the order matches when one is turned over? (good: Maybe an ancestor of the fish turned over long ago.; Maybe it is chance, since there are only a few ways to stack three parts.; Maybe each built a nerve cord separately, on different sides.)
  - Note: Both explanations in your child's answers are held by scientists today. The genes that mark 'back' and 'belly' in flies and frogs match, and work on opposite sides, which supports a turn. But the mouth is on the belly side in both animals. If the body turned over, the mouth must have moved, and nobody is sure how.
  - Think: Three parts can be stacked in only six orders. How much should a matching order count as evidence?
### a4 · Your Leg Is a Second Arm: one pattern, used twice (10 min)
- **record:** Make two columns, one for the arm and one for the leg. In each, write the bones you found from top to bottom: one, two, a cluster, five in a row, and the number in each finger or toe.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child grips the upper arm and feels one bone. Then they hold the forearm near the wrist and turn the palm up and down. Two bones move, one rolling over the other. Then they bend each finger and the thumb, and count the straight parts between the bends.  
  *Parent says:* Feel your upper arm. How many bones? Now hold your forearm and turn your palm over. How many? Now count the parts of a finger, and of your thumb.  
  *Child mode:* Feel your upper arm, then your forearm. Count the bones. Count the parts of a finger and a thumb.
  - Note: The answers are one bone, then two, then a cluster of eight small bones in the wrist, five long bones in the palm, three in each finger and two in the thumb.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do not touch the leg yet. Ask your child to guess the leg bones from top to bottom. Ask how many bones are in the big toe. Write the guess. Then they feel the thigh, the shin, the ankle and the toes, and count.  
  *Parent says:* Now guess your leg, from the top down. How many bones in the thigh? Below the knee? In your big toe? Why do you think so? Now feel and count.  
  *Child mode:* Guess the bones of your leg, from the top down. Then feel and count.
  - Ask: How many bones did you guess for the big toe, and why? (good: Two, like the thumb, if the foot copies the hand.; Three, like the other toes.; I did not know toes had separate bones.)
  - Note: The thigh has one bone. Below the knee there are two. The shin bone is at the front, and the lower end of the thinner one is the bump on the outside of the ankle. The ankle has a cluster of seven bones, and the sole has five long ones. The big toe has two bones and each other toe has three, though the small toes are hard to count.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the two columns. Ask what is the same and what is different. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your two lists. What is the same? What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your arm list and your leg list. What is the same? What is different?
  - Note: Differences worth finding: the knee has a kneecap and the elbow has none. The forearm can twist and the shin cannot. The knee and the elbow bend in opposite directions.
  - Check: Why do your hand and your foot have the same pattern of bones? → **Both grow from limb buds that follow the same instructions**
  - Think: A horse walks on one toe on each foot. Which of your bones do you think a horse still has, and which has she lost?

  *Child mode:* Bend the wing at its two joints. Which is the elbow? Which is the wrist? Say why.
  - Ask: Which section of the wing matches your upper arm, and how can you tell? (good: The thick one that joined the body.; It has one joint at each end, like my upper arm.; I am not sure. We can look at the bones.)

  *Child mode:* Lay the bones in order. Count the bones in each part of the wing.
  - Note: The first bone matches your upper arm bone. The two in the middle match your two forearm bones. The tip is the hand.   *Child mode:* Hold each wing bone against your arm. Point to its match. Write its name beside it.
  - Check: A hen's wing has one bone, then two, then a small hand. What is the best explanation? → **Birds and humans inherited the limb from shared ancestors**
  - Think: A bat's wing is skin stretched over long fingers. A bird's wing is feathers on a short stiff hand. Which bones would you look at to tell them apart?

### p1 · The Family Leftover Map: three leftovers across three generations (45 min, 4 days)
- **safety:** Ask each relative whether they are happy to take part, and accept a no. Some people do not like questions about their teeth or their body.
- **record:** On the tree, beside each person, write how many of their two wrists show the cord: 0, 1 or 2. Write E beside anyone who can move their ears. Beside each grown-up write the number of wisdom teeth, from 0 to 4, or a question mark. On day 4, write one claim, the evidence and one thing that could have misled you.

(15 min); day 3: Call the rest. Mark the tree. (10 min); day 4: Look for patterns, and write your claim, your evidence and what you are not sure of. (10 min)

  *Parent does:* Your child draws a family tree, or arranges one paper slip for each person. Include grandparents, parents, aunts, uncles, cousins, brothers and sisters. Aim for twelve people or more. Ask for a guess: will a missing cord or moving ears turn up in people who are closely related? Write the guess and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Draw everyone you can reach. Do you think a missing wrist cord runs in families, like the shape of a nose? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make your family tree. Do missing cords run in families? Guess, and say why.
  - Note: Include relatives by marriage too, but mark them, because they do not share ancestors with your child's side.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the people at home, then call three or four relatives. Each one does the wrist test on both sides and tries to move their ears. Ask each grown-up how many wisdom teeth have come through or been taken out. Mark the tree after each person.  
  *Parent says:* Tell each person exactly how to do the wrist test: palm up, thumb to little finger, bend the wrist, look for a thin cord. Then ask about ears. Then ask the grown-ups about their wisdom teeth.  
  *Child mode:* Test each person's wrists and ears. Ask grown-ups about wisdom teeth. Mark the tree.
  - Note: Wisdom teeth are the last four back teeth. They usually come through after the age of seventeen, and in some people one or more never form. Many grown-ups will not be sure of their number. Write a question mark for them.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Call the remaining relatives and mark the tree in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Who is still missing from the tree? Call them, and test them in exactly the same way as the others.  
  *Child mode:* Call the relatives who are still missing. Test them the same way. Mark the tree.
  - Note: A wrist on a small screen is hard to read. Write 'not sure' beside it and leave the mark empty with a dot.

  *Parent says:* Look along the lines of the tree. Does anything turn up in a parent and their child? How sure can you be, with this many people?  
  *Child mode:* Look for features shared by parents and children. Write your claim, your evidence and one doubt.
  - Ask: What would make you more sure of your claim? (good: More people in the tree.; Seeing the same feature in a parent and several children.; Testing another family to compare.)
  - Note: A missing cord is much more common in some parts of the world than in others, which suggests that genes play a part. How it is passed on has not been fully worked out. A tree of twelve people can hint at a pattern, but it cannot prove one. That is a fair thing for your child to write.
  - Think: If fewer and fewer people are born with this muscle, what might a wrist look like in a hundred thousand years?

## Challenges
### c1 · Old Hand, New Job: turn a five-finger hand into a wing (25 min)
- **problem:** A cardboard hand with spread fingers moves very little air when you fan with it. Alter the hand so that one sweep sends a ball of tissue paper as far as possible across a table. You must keep all five fingers.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps with cutting cardboard. Broomsticks have sharp ends, so tape over the tips and keep them away from eyes.
- **your turn:** Now alter a fresh five-finger hand for a different job: digging in soil, or paddling in water. Say which fingers you changed, what you had to give up, and which real animal's limb your design is most like.
- **constraints:** Start from a tracing of your own spread hand, cut from thin cardboard.; Keep all five fingers. You may make a finger longer, shorter or wider, and you may join fingers with 'skin'. You may not add a sixth finger or a new part that does not grow from the hand.; Test each design in the same way: one sweep, three tries, without touching the ball.; Measure how far the ball rolls each time, and use the middle one of the three distances.
- **general:** Tape skin between all five fingers, as in a frog's foot.; Make the fingers much longer with sticks and stretch skin between them, as in a bat's wing.; Tape the fingers together into one wide, stiff paddle, as in a whale's flipper.
- **creative:** Make only one finger very long, and stretch skin from that finger back to the wrist. Flying reptiles called pterosaurs did this with the fourth finger.; Join the fingers into a short, stiff hand and tape long paper strips along it as feathers. Birds did this.; Leave the thumb free and small, so that the wing can still hook onto things. Bats keep a clawed thumb.; Make the skin loose in one design and tight in another, and test which moves more air.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child traces their spread hand twice on cardboard and cuts both out, with help. Mark a start line on a smooth table and put the tissue ball on it. Hold the plain hand upright, a hand's width behind the ball. Sweep once towards the ball without touching it. Measure the roll. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* First we test a plain hand. One sweep, no touching. How far does the ball roll? Do it three times and keep the middle distance.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out two cardboard hands. Fan the ball with a plain one. Measure three tries.
  - Note: The plain hand usually moves the ball only a few centimetres, because air slips between the fingers. Use the same sweep each time, as well as you can.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the rule aloud: keep all five fingers, and only alter what is there. Ask your child how they could change the hand to move more air. Let them sketch or describe two ideas before they build.  
  *Parent says:* You may make fingers longer, shorter or wider, and you may add skin between them. You may not add new parts. What will you change, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Keep all five fingers. Change only what is there. Sketch two ideas, or tell them to your grown-up.
  - Ask: Why did the plain hand move so little air, and what would fix that? (good: The air went through the gaps between the fingers.; Skin between the fingers would close the gaps.; Longer fingers would make a bigger surface.)
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child builds one design on the second hand with sticks, tape and skin. Test it three times in the same way and write the distances. If there is time, alter it once more and test again.  
  *Parent says:* Build your design. Then test it exactly as before: one sweep, three tries. Write each distance down.  
  *Child mode:* Build your design. Test it three times, the same way as before. Write the distances.
  - Note: A webbed hand usually sends the ball several times as far as the plain one. Very long fingers with loose skin can flop, which is a useful thing to find out.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the middle distances. Read out the usual solutions and the creative ones. Ask which real animal's limb the design is most like. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* How much farther did your design send the ball? Which animal's limb is it most like? What did your hand give up to become a wing?  
  *Child mode:* How much farther did the ball go? Which animal's limb is your design like?
  - Check: A bat's wing and a whale's flipper are both altered five-finger hands. What does that show? → **New limbs come from changing an old limb**
  - Think: Your design can fan air, but can it still hold a pencil? What does a bat give up by having wings for hands?

### Same Part or Same Job? (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A dolphin's flipper and your arm hold the same bones in the same order. What are such parts called?  
   Options: Homologous parts / Analogous parts / Vestigial parts  
   **Answer:** Homologous parts. *Why:* Homologous parts are the same part, inherited from a shared ancestor, even when the jobs differ. Analogous parts share a job and not a build. Vestigial parts have lost most of an old job.
2. (mcq) Which pair of wings does the same job with a different build?  
   Options: Butterfly wing and crow wing / Bat wing and human arm / Whale flipper and mole hand  
   **Answer:** Butterfly wing and crow wing. *Why:* A butterfly's wing has no bones and a crow's wing has arm bones, so the two are analogous. The other two pairs hold the same bones, so they are homologous.
3. (truefalse) A vestigial part is always a useless part.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Vestigial means that a part has lost most of the job it did in ancestors. Many such parts keep a smaller job. Your tailbone still anchors muscles.
4. (fill) An animal who has a notochord at some stage of life is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['chordate']. *Why:* The notochord is the firm rod along the back of the embryo. Fish, frogs, lizards, birds and mammals are all chordates.
5. (match) Match each body plan feature to an animal who has it.  
   **Answer:** Sac gut with one opening → sea anemone; Skeleton on the outside and jointed legs → ant; Five-part body, anus formed first → starfish; Rod along the back of the embryo → crow. *Why:* A sea anemone is a cnidarian, an ant is an arthropod, a starfish is an echinoderm and a crow is a chordate. Each group has a body plan of their own.
6. (sort) Sort each part: is it homologous to your arm, or not?  
   **Answer:** a bat's wing → Homologous to your arm; a whale's flipper → Homologous to your arm; a horse's front leg → Homologous to your arm; a butterfly's wing → Not homologous to your arm; an octopus's arm → Not homologous to your arm. *Why:* The bat, the whale and the horse inherited the same limb bones that you did. A butterfly's wing and an octopus's arm have no bones and arose separately.
7. (mcq) In a wrist survey, why are the left and right wrists written down separately?  
   Options: The cord can be missing on one side / Left wrists are always weaker / To make the table longer  
   **Answer:** The cord can be missing on one side. *Why:* Some people have the tendon in one forearm and not in the other. Counting people instead of wrists would hide that.
8. (truefalse) A jellyfish embryo has two layers of cells, and a frog embryo has three.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The frog's middle layer builds muscle, bone and blood. A jellyfish has only the outer and inner layers, with jelly between.
9. (write) Explain why a sea anemone has to finish one meal before starting the next big one.  
   **Answer:** A sea anemone's gut is a sac with one opening. Waste has to leave through the mouth, so the sac must be emptied before new food goes in.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the gut has one opening, and that food and waste use the same way in and out.
10. (draw) Draw your arm bones from shoulder to fingertips. Label one bone, two bones, wrist bones and finger bones.  
   **Answer:** A drawing with one upper arm bone, two forearm bones side by side, a cluster of small wrist bones, five palm bones and the finger bones.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the order: one, two, a cluster, five in a row, then fingers. This is the order found in every limb of this kind.
11. (label) Draw a young embryo from the side. Label the notochord, the nerve cord, the throat folds and the tail.  
   **Answer:** The nerve cord runs along the back. The notochord lies just under it. The throat folds are below the head. The tail goes on past the anus.. *Why:* These are the four main features of the chordate body plan. A human embryo has all four.
12. (fill) The small pink fold in the corner of your eye is left over from a third ____.  
   **Answer:** ['eyelid']. *Why:* Crows, cats and crocodiles have a full third eyelid in that corner. In humans only the fold is left.
13. (order) Put these in order, from the shoulder to the fingertip.  
   **Answer:** ['One upper arm bone', 'Two forearm bones', 'Small wrist bones', 'Five palm bones', 'Finger bones']. *Why:* This order is the same in your arm, a bat's wing, a whale's flipper and a frog's front leg.
14. (count) A thumb has 2 bones and each finger has 3. How many thumb and finger bones are in one hand?  
   **Answer:** 14. *Why:* Four fingers with 3 bones each make 12. The thumb adds 2. 12 and 2 make 14. A foot has the same count: 2 in the big toe and 3 in each other toe.
15. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Ant. Crab. Spider. Snail.  
   **Answer:** Snail. The other three are arthropods, with jointed legs and a skeleton on the outside. A snail is a mollusc.. *Why:* Ants, crabs and spiders share one body plan. A snail has a soft body with no jointed legs.
16. (fill) A diving beetle's flat back legs, fringed with hairs, work like ____ when the beetle swims.  
   **Answer:** ['oars']. *Why:* Oars push the water back, and the beetle's flat legs do the same. A land beetle's back legs are built for walking on land.
### Clues From the Inside (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A caecilian looks like a big earthworm but has a skull, teeth and a backbone. Who are their closer relatives?  
   Options: Frogs / Earthworms / Millipedes  
   **Answer:** Frogs. *Why:* Caecilians are amphibians with no legs. A skull, teeth and a backbone are inherited features that earthworms do not have. The long shape suits life in soil and has arisen many times.
2. (mcq) Early whales with legs had a double-pulley ankle bone. What did that bone show?  
   Options: Whales belong with the even-toed hoofed animals / Whales once climbed trees / Whales are close relatives of sharks  
   **Answer:** Whales belong with the even-toed hoofed animals. *Why:* Only even-toed hoofed animals, such as cows, deer and hippos, have that ankle bone. Swimming does not need it, so it is an inherited mark.
3. (mcq) A fly's eye-starting gene and a mouse's match. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Both inherited the gene from one ancestor / Flies and mice eat the same food / The two eyes are built alike  
   **Answer:** Both inherited the gene from one ancestor. *Why:* The eyes themselves are built very differently. A matching gene is best explained by a shared ancestor who already had it.
4. (truefalse) A human embryo is, for a short time, a fish.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A human embryo has throat folds and a tail, as a fish embryo has. But the folds never become gills. Embryos of relatives resemble each other. They do not turn into each other's adults.
5. (truefalse) Termites are closer relatives of cockroaches than of ants.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. DNA places termites inside the cockroach family tree. Termites and ants look alike because both live in large colonies.
6. (fill) Animals who are not close relatives but come to look alike show ____ evolution.  
   **Answer:** ['convergent']. *Why:* Convergent evolution happens when animals live in the same way. Sharks and dolphins both became smooth and pointed for fast swimming.
7. (match) Match each leftover part in humans to the working part in another animal.  
   **Answer:** Pink fold in the eye → third eyelid of a crow; Tailbone → tail of a monkey; Goosebumps → fluffed-up fur of a cat; Small ear muscles → turning ears of a monkey. *Why:* Each human part is in the same place and is built the same way as the working part in the other animal. In humans it has lost most of that job.
8. (sort) Sort each animal by gut: one opening, or two?  
   **Answer:** sea anemone → One opening; jellyfish → One opening; flatworm → One opening; earthworm → Two openings; snail → Two openings; human → Two openings. *Why:* Cnidarians and flatworms have a sac gut. Annelids, molluscs and chordates have a tube gut with a mouth and an anus.
9. (order) Put these steps in the making of an embryo's gut in order.  
   **Answer:** ['A hollow ball of cells forms', 'One side of the ball pushes inwards', 'The dent deepens into a gut', 'A second opening breaks through at the far end']. *Why:* The dent gives the first opening. In starfish and in animals with a backbone, that first opening becomes the anus, and the second becomes the mouth.
10. (count) A tube gut holds 5 balls. One ball goes in every 2 counts. How long does each ball stay inside?  
   **Answer:** 10 counts. *Why:* A ball leaves when five more pushes have happened. 5 pushes of 2 counts each make 10 counts.
11. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Whale's flipper. Bat's wing. Mole's hand. Shark's fin.  
   **Answer:** Shark's fin. The other three hold arm and finger bones inherited from the same ancestors. A shark's fin has none.. *Why:* The flipper, the wing and the hand are homologous. The shark's fin is only analogous to the flipper.
12. (write) A swift and a swallow look alike. Explain how you would find out whether they are close relatives.  
   **Answer:** Compare the inside, part by part: the wing bones, the feet, the embryos and the DNA. Look for many detailed features that catching insects in the air does not explain.. *Why:* A strong answer looks past shape and way of life, and names at least two kinds of inside evidence.
13. (write) Ten wrists are tested and all ten show the cord. Explain why this does not prove everyone has one.  
   **Answer:** The cord is missing in only a small share of hands, so a group of ten can easily have none by chance. Many more people would have to be tested.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a small group can miss something uncommon, and that a bigger survey is needed.
14. (draw) Draw a sac gut and a tube gut. Use arrows to show how food and waste move in each.  
   **Answer:** The sac has one opening, with arrows going in and coming back out the same way. The tube has arrows going in at the mouth and out at the anus.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows one opening used twice in the sac, and one-way travel in the tube.
15. (perspective) A python has two small spurs where the hind legs of her ancestors were.  
   **Answer:** Both can be right. Vestigial means that a part has lost most of its old job, which was walking. Such a part can still keep or gain a smaller job.. *Why:* A strong answer separates two questions: where the part came from, and what it does now.
### Altered, Not New (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A giraffe's voice-box nerve loops down to the chest and back up. What does this route show?  
   Options: The body is an older body, altered / Giraffes need very long nerves / The nerve grows longer every year  
   **Answer:** The body is an older body, altered. *Why:* In a fish the matching nerve takes a direct path. As necks grew longer over generations, the nerve stayed looped round the same blood vessel. The detour is a sign of the older layout.
2. (mcq) Which statement about a bat's wing and a crow's wing is correct?  
   Options: Homologous as limbs, analogous as wings / Homologous in every way / Analogous in every way  
   **Answer:** Homologous as limbs, analogous as wings. *Why:* Both inherited the front limb with its bones, so the limbs are homologous. But flying arose separately in bats and birds, and the wings are built differently, so as wings they are analogous.
3. (mcq) Why is DNA alone not enough to place most extinct animals in the family tree?  
   Options: Almost no fossil keeps any DNA / Extinct animals had no DNA / Bones are easier to read than DNA  
   **Answer:** Almost no fossil keeps any DNA. *Why:* DNA breaks down over time, so nearly all fossils are bone or shell with no DNA left. Extinct animals did have DNA while they lived.
4. (truefalse) A starfish is a closer relative of a human than of a jellyfish.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Starfish and humans both make the anus first as embryos, and DNA agrees. The ring shape of a starfish and of a jellyfish arose separately.
5. (truefalse) Animals of very different sizes can share one body plan.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A mouse and a blue whale are both chordates and both mammals. Size changes easily, and the body plan stays.
6. (fill) The firm, bendy rod along the back of a chordate embryo is the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['notochord']. *Why:* In animals with a backbone, the backbone forms round the notochord. What is left of the rod becomes part of the discs between the bones.
7. (fill) Each generation can only ____ the body that the parents passed on. Nothing starts from a blank sheet.  
   **Answer:** ['alter', 'change']. *Why:* Bodies are inherited. So every body is an older body with changes, and it keeps signs of the older one.
8. (match) Match each check for 'the same part' to the question it asks.  
   **Answer:** Position → Is it in the same place, beside the same neighbours?; Make-up → Is it built from the same pieces?; Growth → Does it form in the same way in the embryo?. *Why:* Parts that pass all three checks are very likely to be the same inherited part. Your arm and a bat's wing pass all three.
9. (sort) Sort each clue: does it show that two animals are relatives, or only that they live alike?  
   **Answer:** the same bones in the same order → Shows they are relatives; matching DNA → Shows they are relatives; embryos built by the same steps → Shows they are relatives; a smooth, pointed body → Shows only that they live alike; both live in water → Shows only that they live alike; both have armour → Shows only that they live alike. *Why:* Shape, home and armour can arise separately in animals who live in the same way. Bones, DNA and embryo steps are inherited.
10. (order) Put these animals in order, from your most distant relative to your closest.  
   **Answer:** ['Sponge', 'Jellyfish', 'Earthworm', 'Starfish', 'Crow']. *Why:* Each animal shares one more inherited feature with you than the one before: tissues, three layers, anus formed first, and a notochord and backbone.
11. (count) About 4 in every 5 named animals are arthropods. Out of 100 named animals, about how many are not arthropods?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* 4 in every 5 is the same as 80 in every 100. 100 take away 80 leaves 20. All the fish, birds, snails, worms and mammals fit into that 20.
12. (write) Food and air cross paths in your throat, so people can choke. Explain how comparing bodies makes sense of this.  
   **Answer:** Lungs began as a pouch off the gut in fish ancestors. So the airway still branches off the food tube. The body was altered from an older one and was not planned afresh.. *Why:* A strong answer says where lungs came from, and that an inherited layout can only be altered.
13. (write) 'Animals who look alike must be relatives.' Give one example that shows why looking alike is not enough.  
   **Answer:** A dolphin and a shark look alike, but the dolphin has lungs, milk and arm bones, and is a relative of cows. Pangolins and armadillos both have armour, but are not close relatives.. *Why:* A strong answer names two look-alikes and gives an inside feature that separates them.
14. (label) Draw an embryo with three germ layers. Label each layer and write one body part that grows from it.  
   **Answer:** Outer layer: skin and nerves. Middle layer: muscle, bone and blood. Inner layer: the lining of the gut.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows three sheets, one inside the other, each with a correct body part.
15. (perspective) A prawn's nerve cord runs along the belly. A fish's nerve cord runs along the back.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side with a reason, and names evidence such as fossils of early ancestors, the genes that set back and belly, or animals whose nerves are still a loose net.. *Why:* Both views accept the same facts about where the cords lie. They differ on whether the cords are one inherited part or two.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a feature of an animal's body plan?  
   Options: Whether the gut has one opening or two / The colour of the fur / How fast the animal runs  
   **Answer:** Whether the gut has one opening or two. *Why:* A body plan is the basic layout that is set in the embryo. Colour and speed can change quickly over generations, and the layout changes very little.
2. (mcq) Ants, crabs and spiders are in one phylum. What do they all share?  
   Options: Jointed legs and a skeleton on the outside / A backbone / A sac gut with one opening  
   **Answer:** Jointed legs and a skeleton on the outside. *Why:* They are arthropods. A backbone belongs to the chordate body plan, and a sac gut to jellyfish and flatworms.
3. (mcq) Which feature does every chordate have at some stage of life?  
   Options: A notochord, a firm rod along the back / A hard skeleton on the outside / Six jointed legs  
   **Answer:** A notochord, a firm rod along the back. *Why:* The notochord gives the chordates their name. You had one as an embryo. The other two answers describe arthropods.
4. (mcq) A whale's flipper and a shark's fin look alike. Are they homologous parts?  
   Options: No. Same job, different parts / Yes. Both are fins / Yes. Both animals live in the sea  
   **Answer:** No. Same job, different parts. *Why:* The flipper holds arm and finger bones, and the fin holds none. The two are analogous. Doing the same job in the same place does not make parts the same part.
5. (mcq) Which is the best evidence that two animals are close relatives?  
   Options: Many matching parts inside the body / The same body shape / Living in the same place  
   **Answer:** Many matching parts inside the body. *Why:* Shape and home can be shared by animals who simply live alike. Many detailed inside features that the way of life does not need are best explained by shared ancestors.
6. (mcq) Why do some healthy people have no palmaris longus muscle in the forearm?  
   Options: The muscle has lost its main job / They did not exercise enough / They were hurt as babies  
   **Answer:** The muscle has lost its main job. *Why:* The muscle did more in ancestors who climbed. In humans it does very little. A part like that is not always built, and some people are born with none. Exercise and injury have nothing to do with whether a muscle is built.
7. (truefalse) If a survey of ten wrists finds the cord in every one, then no human lacks the cord.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Ten wrists is a small group, and a small group can easily miss something uncommon. Bigger studies find people with no cord.
8. (mcq) Bones pointed to one ancestor for whales and DNA pointed to another. What did scientists do?  
   Options: Looked for more evidence, and found ankle bones / Chose the bones, because bones are older / Gave up on the question  
   **Answer:** Looked for more evidence, and found ankle bones. *Why:* When two kinds of evidence disagree, the answer is to find more. Ankle bones of early whales, found later, agreed with the DNA.
9. (mcq) What do very young embryos of a fish, a chick and a human show?  
   Options: They look much alike / Each is a tiny copy of the adult / The human one has working gills  
   **Answer:** They look much alike. *Why:* All three have throat folds and a tail. Embryos of relatives are built by inherited steps, and the earliest steps differ least. A human embryo never has gills.
10. (mcq) Which sentence about animal bodies is correct?  
   Options: Each body is an older body, altered / Each body is planned fresh for its job / Bodies stay the same over generations  
   **Answer:** Each body is an older body, altered. *Why:* Every animal inherits a body and passes it on with small changes. That is why bodies keep leftover parts and odd routes, such as the long nerve in a giraffe's neck.
11. (mcq) Why can each part of a tube gut do one job well?  
   Options: Food passes the parts in one order / The tube is always full / Food moves both ways along it  
   **Answer:** Food passes the parts in one order. *Why:* Food moves one way in a tube, so grinding can come first and taking in can come later. In a sac, new food and old waste share one space.
12. (mcq) A diving beetle in a pond has flat back legs fringed with hairs. What are they mainly for?  
   Options: Digging up roots from the mud / Rowing through the water / Holding a bubble of air  
   **Answer:** Rowing through the water. *Why:* The flat legs push the water back like oars, which is how the beetle swims. The leg joints are the same as a land beetle's, but the shape is changed for life in water. The legs are not for digging, and the air bubble is carried under the wing cover, not held by the legs.

## Applications
- Surgeons repair a damaged tendon in the hand or the wrist with the palmaris longus tendon from the same person's forearm, because the hand works without it.
- Medicines are tested on mice and other animals because their organs are homologous to human organs. The same reasoning warns that a mouse is not a small human.
- Scientists who find a single fossil bone can often tell what kind of animal it came from, by comparing it with the bones of living animals.
- Wildlife officers identify bones, teeth and scales seized from smugglers by comparing them with known skeletons, to find out which protected animal was killed.
- Doctors who study how babies form use fish, chick and mouse embryos, because the early steps are shared.
- Engineers who design flapping robots study bat wings and bird wings to see two different ways of building a wing from one kind of limb.
- Vets use the same names for a dog's bones as doctors use for a human's, because the bones are the same bones.

## Spotted around us
- A crow or a pigeon blinking at your window. Watch for a pale film that flicks across the eye from the front corner. That is the third eyelid, the part your pink eye fold is left over from.
- A prawn on the kitchen counter before cooking. The dark line along the back is the gut. The nerve cord runs along the belly side, the opposite of yours.
- A bat leaving a banyan or a temple tower at dusk. Each wing is a hand: skin stretched between four very long fingers, with a small thumb free at the front.
- A street dog or a cat turning one ear towards a sound while the head stays still. You have the same three muscles round each ear.
- Barnacles on a fishing boat, a jetty post or a rock at the beach. They look like tiny shells, but they are relatives of crabs.
- A fish being cleaned at the market. The gut lifts out in one piece, because it lies loose in the body cavity. The backbone is the row of bones that formed round the notochord.
- A termite mound beside a village road, or winged termites round a light after the first rain. They live like ants, but they are a branch of the cockroaches.

## Open questions
- **Did the tube gut, with a mouth and an anus, arise once or several times?** The genes that build an anus differ a good deal between groups of animals. Some scientists read this as a sign that the second opening arose more than once. Others think it arose once and the genes changed later.
- **Is an insect's nerve cord the same inherited part as your spinal cord?** The genes that mark back and belly match in flies and frogs, on opposite sides. But the shared ancestor is unknown, and may have had only a loose net of nerves.
- **Why do animals with four limbs have five fingers and not eight?** Acanthostega, an early four-limbed animal who lived about 365 million years ago, had eight fingers on each hand. Nobody knows why five became the usual number in all the groups that came later.
- **Do the small muscles round human ears still do anything?** A study in 2020 found that these muscles become slightly active on the side a person is listening to. Whether that weak pull helps hearing at all has not been shown.
- **What exactly makes two parts 'the same part'?** Bones, embryos and genes usually agree about which parts match. Sometimes they do not: one gene can help to build parts that arose separately. Scientists and philosophers have no single agreed definition yet.

## Connected maths
- Kavya's family tested 16 wrists. 13 showed the cord, 2 showed none and 1 was not sure. What fraction of the wrists they were sure about had no cord? **2 out of 15.**. 16 wrists take away the 1 not sure leaves 15. Of those 15, 2 had no cord. So the fraction is 2 out of 15, which is a little more than 1 in 8. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- A study in Jabalpur tested 385 young adults. In 65 of them the cord was missing in one wrist, and in 13 it was missing in both. About how many people in a class of 30 would you expect to have a cord missing? **About 6.**. 65 and 13 make 78 people with a cord missing. 78 out of 385 is close to 80 out of 400, which is 1 in 5. One fifth of 30 is 6. A real class could have a few more or a few fewer. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In one study in South India, the cord was missing in about 1 hand in every 15. Farhan's family tested 12 wrists and found the cord in all of them. Does this show that the study was wrong? Explain with numbers. **No. With 12 wrists you would expect fewer than 1 without a cord, so finding none is not surprising.**. 1 in 15 means that 15 wrists would have about 1 missing, on average. 12 is fewer than 15, so the expected number is less than 1. A result of 0 fits well. Farhan would need a few hundred wrists to check the study. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- In a made-up run of Sac or Tube, the sock took 84 seconds for 12 balls with its best plan, and the tube took 36 seconds. About how many times as fast was the tube? How many balls a minute did the tube digest? **A little more than 2 times as fast. The tube digested 20 balls a minute.**. 84 divided by 36 is 2 and a third. For the rate, 12 balls in 36 seconds is 1 ball every 3 seconds. A minute has 60 seconds, and 60 divided by 3 is 20. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- One hand has 8 wrist bones, 5 palm bones and 14 finger and thumb bones. One foot has 7 ankle bones, 5 sole bones and 14 toe bones. A grown-up has 206 bones. Are more than half of them in the hands and feet? **Yes. The two hands and two feet hold 106 bones, and half of 206 is 103.**. One hand has 8 and 5 and 14, which is 27. One foot has 7 and 5 and 14, which is 26. Two hands make 54 and two feet make 52. 54 and 52 make 106. Half of 206 is 103, and 106 is more than that. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L5`)
- About 1,500,000 kinds of animals have been named. About 1,257,000 of them are arthropods. Roughly what fraction is that? If you picked one named kind without looking, would you more likely pick an arthropod or a chordate? **About four fifths. You would much more likely pick an arthropod.**. 1,257,000 is close to 1,200,000. 1,200,000 out of 1,500,000 is the same as 12 out of 15, which is 4 out of 5. Chordates are only about 70,000 kinds, which is less than 1 in 20. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What do we call parts of different animals that are the same part, inherited from a shared ancestor? → Homologous parts.
- A butterfly's wing and a crow's wing do the same job. Are they homologous or analogous? → Analogous. Only the crow's wing has bones.
- What is a vestigial part? → A part that has lost most or all of the job it did in ancestors.
- Name one leftover part in your own body. → The pink fold in the eye, the tailbone, the ear muscles or goosebumps.
- Which gut has one opening: a sea anemone's or an earthworm's? → The sea anemone's. An earthworm has a tube gut with two openings.
- Who are the closest living relatives of whales? → Hippos.
- What is the firm rod along the back of every chordate embryo called? → The notochord.
- Which phylum has the most named kinds of animals? → The arthropods: insects, spiders, crabs and their relatives.
