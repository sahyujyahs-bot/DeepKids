# Who cares for the young? Sexual selection, family systems and fathers who carry the eggs
`sci.animals.courtship-and-mates.L5`

**Summary.** On a lotus pond in India, the bird sitting on the eggs is the father. The mother is the bigger bird, and she may have four mates. A seahorse father carries the eggs in a pouch and gives birth. An elephant family is led by the oldest female. Why do animals have such different families, and who ends up looking after the young? Your child guesses who cares in eight animal families. Then they run the Warm Egg Test with three steel tumblers of warm water. It shows why most birds need two parents. They also learn what makes an animal female or male, and how Charles Darwin's idea of sexual selection explains horns, harems and choosy partners.

**Intro.** *A seahorse mother places her eggs in a pouch on the father's belly. He carries them for weeks, and then he gives birth. On Indian ponds, a jacana father sits on the eggs alone. Why does the father do the caring in some animals, and the mother in others?* Animals raise their young in many ways. Two pigeons share one nest and take turns. One male elephant seal lives with dozens of females and looks after no pups at all. A turtle mother buries her eggs and never sees them hatch. In this unit you first guess who looks after the young in eight animal families. Then you test, with warm water in steel tumblers, what an egg needs from the ones who sit on it. The result explains a lot about bird families. After that you meet the idea that explains the rest. It is called sexual selection.

## Terms
- **Sex cell (gamete)**: A special cell that carries half of the instructions for building a new animal. Two sex cells must join to start a new life. *e.g.* An egg cell and a sperm are both sex cells.
- **Egg cell**: The larger kind of sex cell. Egg cells carry food for the first days of the new life. *e.g.* A human egg cell is about a tenth of a millimetre wide, like a tiny dot of pencil.
- **Sperm**: The smaller kind of sex cell. Sperm carry almost no food, and most kinds can swim. *e.g.* A sperm's head is about twenty times narrower than an egg cell.
- **Female and male**: A female is an animal who makes the larger sex cells, the egg cells. A male is an animal who makes the smaller ones, the sperm. *e.g.* A seahorse father is male because he makes sperm, even though he carries the young.
- **Fertilisation**: The joining of one egg cell and one sperm. The joined cell can grow into a new animal. *e.g.* A frog's eggs are fertilised in the pond water, outside the mother's body.
- **Sexual selection**: The way some animals have more young than others because they do better at winning partners. Over many generations it shapes bodies and behaviour. *e.g.* A blackbuck's long twisted horns came from sexual selection.
- **Competition for partners**: One part of sexual selection. Animals of one sex fight or outdo each other to get partners. *e.g.* Two male elephant seals rear up and slam their chests together on a beach.
- **Mate choice**: The other part of sexual selection. An animal watches or listens, compares, and accepts or refuses a partner. *e.g.* A peahen walks past several peacocks before she accepts one.
- **Parental care**: Anything a parent does after eggs are laid or young are born that helps the young to live. *e.g.* A pigeon sitting on eggs and feeding the chicks is giving parental care.
- **Parental investment**: Everything a parent spends on one young: the food in the egg, the time, the energy and the risk. What is spent on one young cannot be spent on another. *e.g.* An emperor penguin father goes without food for about four months to hatch one egg.
- **Incubation**: Keeping eggs warm until they hatch, usually by sitting on them. *e.g.* Pigeon parents take turns to incubate two eggs for about 18 days.
- **Monogamy (a pair)**: A family system in which one female and one male stay together for at least one breeding season. *e.g.* Two sarus cranes hold a patch of wetland together and both sit on the eggs.
- **Polygyny**: A family system in which one male has several female partners at the same time. *e.g.* One silverback gorilla lives with several females and their young.
- **Harem**: A group of several females who share one male partner, or of several males who share one female partner. *e.g.* A male elephant seal guards a harem of dozens of females on a beach.
- **Polyandry**: A family system in which one female has several male partners at the same time. *e.g.* A female bronze-winged jacana may have up to four mates, and each sits on a clutch of her eggs.
- **Promiscuity**: A family system in which both females and males have several partners and no lasting pair forms. *e.g.* Chimpanzees live in big groups where females and males each have several partners.
- **Cooperative breeding**: A family system in which helpers who are not the parents also feed, carry or guard the young. *e.g.* In an elephant family, aunts and older sisters watch over a calf.
- **Matriarch**: The female who leads a group. She is often the oldest. *e.g.* An elephant matriarch leads her family to water she remembers from years ago.
- **Brood pouch**: A pocket of skin in which a parent carries eggs or young. In seahorses the pouch is on the father. *e.g.* A seahorse father's brood pouch gives the growing young oxygen and some food.
- **Role reversal**: A family system in which the females compete for partners and the males do the caring. It is the reverse of the more common pattern. *e.g.* Female jacanas fight each other over patches of pond, and the males raise the chicks.
- **Hermaphrodite**: An animal who makes both egg cells and sperm, either at the same time or at different times in life. *e.g.* An earthworm makes both egg cells and sperm.

## Explanations
### What female and male mean: two sizes of sex cell · *defined*
**Definition:** A female is an animal who makes the larger sex cells, called egg cells. A male is an animal who makes the smaller sex cells, called sperm.

A new animal usually starts when two sex cells join. Each sex cell carries half of the instructions, which are written in a long molecule called DNA. In almost all animals the two sex cells are very different in size. Egg cells are large, because they also carry food and the working parts of a cell. Sperm are tiny, and carry little more than their half of the instructions. A human egg cell is about a tenth of a millimetre wide, so you could just see one as a dot. The head of a human sperm is about twenty times narrower. This difference in size is the only thing that makes an animal female or male, in every kind of animal. Colour is not what makes an animal female or male. Neither is size, strength or caring. A seahorse father carries the young, and he is male because he makes sperm. But why would the difference in sex cells matter for families? Because large cells cost more to make. A female's body can make only a limited number of egg cells. A male's body can make a very large number of sperm. The other explanations follow from that one fact.
- e.g. A hen's yolk is one giant egg cell, packed with food. A rooster's sperm are far too small to see.
- e.g. A female jacana is bigger and fiercer than her mates. She is still the female, because she makes the egg cells.
- e.g. The tiny power stations inside your cells, called mitochondria, almost always come from the egg cell, so from your mother.
### Why are there two sizes at all? · *idea*
Nobody watched the two sizes of sex cell first appear, because it happened long before there were any animals, in living beings made of one cell or a few cells. Scientists think the first sex cells were all one size. Some pond algae alive today still make sex cells of one size, so they have no females and no males. In 1972 Geoff Parker, Robin Baker and Vic Smith worked out with maths how two sizes could arise. A parent has a fixed amount of material to make sex cells from. A parent can make a few large cells or many small ones. Two large cells that join make a well-fed start, which is more likely to live. Many small cells find partners more often, simply because there are more of them. A middle size does badly both ways, because such cells are too few to find partners and too small to feed the young. So over many generations the middle disappears and two kinds are left. This is the leading idea, and it fits what we see in algae. It has not been proved.
- e.g. Some green pond algae have sex cells of one size. Their close relatives have large and small ones.
- e.g. Think of sharing one lump of dough. You can make three big balls or thirty tiny ones, but not thirty big ones.
- **Geoff Parker, Robin Baker and Vic Smith, 1972:** Two sizes win because many small cells find more partners and large cells feed the young. Middle-sized cells lose on both counts.
- **Other scientists:** Other causes may matter too, such as how easily sex cells meet in open water. The question is still being worked on.
### Sexual selection: competing and choosing · *defined*
**Definition:** Sexual selection is the way some animals leave more young than others because they are better at winning partners. It has two parts: competing with rivals of your own sex, and being chosen by the other sex.

Charles Darwin described sexual selection in his book The Descent of Man in 1871. He was puzzled by features that do not help an animal to find food or to stay safe. A blackbuck's horns are long and twisted, and only the males usually have them. A peacock's train makes him easy for a tiger to see. Darwin's answer was that these features help their owner to have young. This happens in two ways. The first is competition. Animals of one sex fight or threaten each other, and the winners get more partners. Horns, tusks and big bodies come from this. The second is choice. Animals of the other sex compare and pick, and the ones who are picked have more young. Trains, songs and dances come from this. A feature that wins more partners is passed on to more young, so it spreads, even if it makes life harder in other ways. Both parts can act on one animal. A male blackbuck fights for a small patch of ground, and then females choose among the patches.
- e.g. Chital stags grow antlers and push against each other with them. The does have no antlers.
- e.g. Male Asian elephants push and chase each other for the chance to mate, and the larger male usually wins.
- e.g. Female jacanas compete with each other for mates, so competition is not only something males do.
### Parental investment: whoever gives more to each young chooses more carefully · *defined*
**Definition:** Parental investment is everything a parent spends on one young, including the food in the egg, the time, the energy and the risk. The sex that spends more on each young is usually the choosier one. The other sex usually competes.

In 1972 Robert Trivers asked why males are usually the ones who compete and females the ones who choose. His answer was about cost. Think of a cow and a bull. The cow carries a calf for nine months and then feeds the calf milk for months. She can have about one calf a year, whoever her partner is. A poor choice of partner costs her a whole year. The bull spends very little on each calf, so a poor choice costs him almost nothing. That is why she is careful. There is a second reason. While the cow is busy with a calf, she is not looking for a partner. So at any moment many bulls are ready to breed and few cows are. The bulls must compete for the few cows who are ready. Trivers's idea makes a prediction that can be tested. If the father of some species spends more than the mother, the roles should swap. They do. Jacana fathers do all the sitting and chick care, and jacana females are the ones who fight. A male Mormon cricket gives the female a large packet of food when they mate. Darryl Gwynne found in 1981 that these males refuse most females, and the females compete for the males.
- e.g. A male giant water bug carries the eggs glued to his back until they hatch. While he carries them he cannot mate again, and females seek out males who are free.
- e.g. A female elephant seal fasts for about 23 days while she feeds her pup. The father gives the pup nothing.
- e.g. A pigeon mother and father spend about the same on each chick, and they look alike and court each other.
### Every young has one mother and one father · *defined*
**Definition:** In animals with two sexes, each young has exactly one mother and one father. So all the females together have the same number of young as all the males together.

This sounds too obvious to matter, but it corrects a common mistake. People often say that males can have far more young than females. One male can. All males cannot. Suppose a beach has 10 male and 10 female seals, and 10 pups are born. The females have 10 pups between them, about one each. The males also have 10 pups between them, because every pup has a father. If one big male fathers 8 of the pups, the other nine males share only 2. Most of them father none. So the average is the same for both sexes, and what differs is how evenly the young are shared out. Among the females, nearly every one has a pup. Among the males, a few have many and most have none. That uneven sharing is why the males fight so hard. A male who loses has no young at all. Scientists call this rule the Fisher condition, after Ronald Fisher, who used it in 1930.
- e.g. In elephant seals a few large males father most of the pups on a beach, and many males never father any.
- e.g. In pigeons, who live as pairs, nearly every female and nearly every male raises chicks, so there is little to fight over.
### Five family systems · *defined*
**Definition:** A family system, which scientists call a mating system, is the usual pattern of who pairs with whom in a species, and for how long.

Scientists use five main names. In monogamy, one female and one male stay together for a season or longer. Sarus cranes, pigeons and hoolock gibbons live this way. In polygyny, one male has several female partners. A group of females with one male is called a harem. Gorillas and elephant seals live this way. In polyandry, one female has several male partners. Jacanas live this way. In promiscuity, females and males each have several partners and no pair lasts. Chimpanzees live this way. In cooperative breeding, helpers who are not the parents also raise the young. Elephants do this, and so do people. These names describe what animals are seen doing. They are not grades from good to bad, and no system is more advanced than another. One species can also use more than one system, depending on where the animals live and how many partners are about.
- e.g. Pair: two great hornbills raise one or two chicks in a tree hollow in the Western Ghats.
- e.g. One male, several females: a silverback gorilla with his group.
- e.g. One female, several males: a bronze-winged jacana on a lotus pond.
- e.g. Helpers: older sisters and aunts in an elephant family.
### A pair you can see is not always the whole story · *defined*
**Definition:** A social pair is two animals who live together and raise young together. It does not always mean that every chick in the nest has that male as the father.

Until the 1980s, scientists could only watch who lived with whom. Most birds live in pairs, so most birds were called faithful. Then a way was found to read DNA and work out who a chick's real parents are. The surprise was large. In 2002 Simon Griffith, Ian Owens and Katherine Thuman put together more than 150 such studies. In about nine out of ten kinds of birds tested, some chicks had a father who was not the male at the nest. In birds that live as pairs, about 11 chicks in every 100 had another father. So the pair is real. The two birds do build the nest and feed the chicks together. But who lives together and who the parents are turn out to be two separate questions. This is also a lesson about evidence. What we see from outside is a start, and a new tool can show what watching alone missed.
- e.g. A pair of pigeons usually stays together for years, but matings outside the pair do happen.
- e.g. A female bird may live with one male and still have a chick fathered by a neighbour.
### Why most birds are pairs and most mammals are not · *defined*
**Definition:** In about four out of five kinds of birds, both parents care for the young. In mammals, fathers help with the young in only about one kind in ten or fewer.

The difference comes from what the young need and who is able to give it. A bird's egg must be kept warm almost all the time, close to the warmth of the parent's body. A mother or a father can do this equally well. But a bird who sits cannot look for food. If one parent sits alone, the eggs cool each time that parent leaves to eat. You measure this cooling in the Warm Egg Test. With two parents, one sits while the other feeds. After hatching, many chicks need food brought many times an hour, and two parents can bring about twice as much. So a bird father who stays usually raises far more chicks than one who leaves. A baby mammal grows inside the mother, which keeps the baby warm with no sitting. After birth the baby needs milk, and only the mother makes milk. There is much less a father can add, so in most mammals he gains more by seeking other partners. About 9 in 100 kinds of mammals live as pairs. Where mammal fathers do help, they carry, guard or feed the young, as in wolves and some monkeys.
- e.g. A great hornbill mother seals herself inside a tree hollow, leaving only a slit. The father passes food to her and the chicks through the slit for weeks.
- e.g. Both pigeon parents make a rich food called crop milk in a pouch in the throat, so both can feed the newly hatched chicks.
- e.g. A newborn calf drinks only milk, so only the mother can feed her.
### The seahorse: a father who carries the young · *defined*
**Definition:** In seahorses the female places her eggs in a brood pouch on the male's belly. He fertilises them there, carries them until they hatch, and gives birth to live young.

A pair of seahorses greet each other soon after dawn each day. They swim side by side and brighten in colour. When the female's eggs are ready, the two rise through the water together. She puts a tube into the opening of his pouch and passes the eggs in. In one species that was studied, the pouch stays open for only about six seconds. The eggs are fertilised inside the pouch. Then the pouch closes. For the next 9 to 45 days, depending on the kind of seahorse, the father's body does much of what a mammal mother's body does. The lining of the pouch brings oxygen to the young and carries their waste away. It gives them some fat and calcium. It also keeps the liquid around them at the right saltiness. Then the father squeezes his body, usually at night, and pushes out the young, usually 100 to 1,000 of them. They get no care after that, and fewer than 1 in 200 lives to grow up. The father is often ready for new eggs by the next morning.
- e.g. Seahorses live in seagrass beds and river mouths along the coasts of India.
- e.g. Pipefish are relatives of seahorses. Pipefish fathers also carry the eggs.
- e.g. The seahorse mother's eggs cost her a great deal. Making one batch can use up about a third of her body weight.
### Why seahorse fathers, and not mothers? · *idea*
Nobody knows for certain why the carrying fell to the father in seahorses. Several ideas have been offered, and none has been proved. One idea is about speed. While the father carries one batch, the mother's body is already making the next batch of eggs. The two jobs run at the same time, so the pair can produce young faster than if one parent did both jobs in turn. A second idea is about certainty. The eggs are fertilised inside his own pouch, so the father can be sure the young are his. Care is less wasted when a parent is sure. A third idea starts earlier in history. In many fish the male already guards a patch of sea floor where eggs are laid. Guarding eggs on your patch, then on your skin, then in a fold of skin, are small steps, and pipefish alive today show each of them. Here is a surprise that tests your thinking. Seahorse males still compete more than females do, by wrestling with their tails. Carrying the young did not swap every role.
- e.g. Some pipefish fathers carry eggs stuck to the outside of the belly. Others have skin flaps over them. Seahorses have a closed pouch.
- e.g. Male seahorses snap their heads at rivals and wrestle with their tails.
- **The speed idea:** Father carries while mother makes the next eggs, so the pair has more young in a season.
- **The certainty idea:** Fertilising the eggs inside his own pouch makes the father sure that the young are his.
- **The small steps idea:** Male fish already guarded eggs on their patch. A pouch is the far end of a long row of small changes.
### Fish and frogs: when the eggs are outside, the father often guards · *idea*
Most fish give their eggs no care at all. But in about 3 fish families out of 10, some kinds do care. When one parent cares alone, that parent is the father more often than the mother. This is the reverse of mammals. Why? In most fish the eggs are fertilised in the water, outside the mother's body. A male fish often holds a patch of the bottom and waits for females to lay there. He is already guarding that patch against rivals. Guarding the eggs that lie on it costs him little extra. He can also guard the eggs of several females at once. Meanwhile the female can go and feed, and make more eggs. This is the explanation most scientists favour today, but it is not settled. A clownfish father fans the eggs with his fins and picks out the dead ones for six to eight days.
- e.g. A clownfish father guards and fans the eggs beside the sea anemone where the pair lives.
- e.g. Sardines and many other sea fish release their eggs into open water and give them no care.
- **The patch idea, favoured by most scientists:** A male who already defends a patch to attract females can guard eggs on it at little extra cost.
- **The certainty idea:** When eggs are fertilised outside the body in front of him, a male is surer that they are his.
### The jacana: a test that the theory passed · *defined*
**Definition:** In jacanas the usual roles are reversed. The female holds a territory with one to four males in it. Each male incubates a clutch of her eggs and raises the chicks alone.

The bronze-winged jacana walks on lotus and water lily leaves on ponds across India, on very long toes. The female is much heavier than the male. In one sample from southern India, females weighed about 282 grams and males about 176 grams. She defends a stretch of pond against other females. Inside it, each of her mates has a smaller patch. She lays four eggs for one male, and he sits on them for about 29 days. Then she lays for the next male. When danger comes, a father tucks the chicks between his wings and his body and carries them off. Why did this family system arise? One likely part of the answer is that many jacana nests are lost to floods and egg-eaters. A female who can quickly lay a new clutch, for the same male or another, replaces what was lost. She can do that only if she is free of sitting. Stuart Butchart and his team found something more in 1999. The males in a harem compete too, by calling loudly to bring the female to them. Males who called more got more matings. So even here, both competing and choosing go on.
- e.g. A female pheasant-tailed jacana may lay up to ten clutches in one season, each for a different male to raise.
- e.g. Female jacanas chase and fight other females who come into their stretch of pond.
- e.g. The male, who spends the most on each chick, is the one the females compete for. Trivers's idea predicted this.
### What one animal can guard · *defined*
**Definition:** One animal can keep several partners only where those partners, or the things they need, are gathered closely enough to be guarded.

Why do elephant seals have harems when pigeons do not? In 1977 Stephen Emlen and Lewis Oring gave an answer that you can test on a table with coins. Female seals must come ashore to give birth, and good beaches are few. So the females crowd together. One strong male can stand in the middle and keep other males away from dozens of females. Female pigeons are spread over a whole city, each at a nest. No male could guard several. A male pigeon does better by staying at one nest and helping. So the family system depends partly on where the females are and how closely they gather. Kavita Isvaran of the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru studied blackbuck in this way. She found that where female blackbuck move in large groups, males gather on small patches packed close together. Such a gathering is called a lek. Females visit the lek and choose among the males. Where females are few and scattered, males hold larger patches far apart.
- e.g. In the One Guard Test, you find out how many coins one hand can guard when they are close together, and when they are spread out.
- e.g. A male elephant seal weighs more than three tonnes. Only the biggest males can hold the middle of the beach.
- e.g. Sarus crane pairs hold a large patch of wetland. One male could not guard two such patches.
### Groups that females lead · *defined*
**Definition:** In some animals the group is built around related females, and a female leads. She is called the matriarch.

An elephant family is made of mothers, daughters, sisters and their calves. Young males leave in their teens and live alone or with other males. So the adults who stay together for life are the females, and the oldest usually leads. What does she give the group? Karen McComb and her team tested this in Kenya and reported it in 2001. They played recorded calls of other elephants to elephant families. Families with older matriarchs were better at telling friends from strangers. They bunched together in defence at a stranger's call and stayed calm at a friend's call. Those families also had more calves. So the leader's memory helps everyone in her family. Spotted hyenas are different again. Hyena females are a little larger than the males, and almost every female outranks every male. A clan can have up to 80 members and is usually led by a female. A cub takes the rank just below their mother. Why females lead in hyenas is still argued over.
- e.g. An elephant calf is watched by her mother, her aunts and her older sisters.
- e.g. A hyena cub born to the leading female outranks adult females who rank below the cub's mother.
- e.g. In a troop of bonnet macaques or langurs, the daughters stay for life and the sons leave.
### Care is paid for in energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Every kind of care uses up energy from the parent's own body. Energy spent on one young is not available for the parent or for other young.

Energy is what a body needs to move, to keep warm and to grow. An animal gets energy only from food. Care always takes some. A sitting bird burns food to keep the eggs warm, and gives up time for feeding. A mammal mother turns her own body fat into milk. An elephant seal mother eats nothing for about 23 days while her pup drinks. An emperor penguin father stands through the Antarctic winter with one egg on his feet. He eats nothing for about 120 days, and may fall from about 38 kilograms to about 18. This is why care is called an investment. A parent's body has only so much energy. What goes to this chick cannot go to the next one, or to the parent's own safety. So parents in every species are pulled two ways, between the young they have now and the young they could have later. The different family systems are different ways of settling that pull.
- e.g. A seahorse mother's batch of eggs can use up about a third of her body weight.
- e.g. A male elephant seal guarding a harem may also go without food for more than three months.
- e.g. A turtle mother puts all her investment into the eggs, and none into care afterwards.
### People: many hands raise a child · *idea*
Humans are animals, so the same questions apply to us. A human child needs care for longer than almost any other young animal, about fifteen years or more. The scientist Sarah Blaffer Hrdy studied langur monkeys at Mount Abu in Rajasthan in the 1970s. She saw females other than the mother carrying infants. Later she asked how early human mothers could ever have raised such slow-growing children, often with a new baby arriving before the last child could find food alone. Her answer is that mothers never did it alone. Fathers, grandmothers, aunts, older brothers and sisters, and neighbours all fed and carried children. She calls this cooperative breeding, and many scientists now agree that people are cooperative breeders. It is still an idea about the distant past, and that is hard to test. Human families today come in many forms too. There are two parents, one parent, grandparents who raise grandchildren, joint families and adopted children. Children are raised well in all of these.
- e.g. In the Many Hands activity, you count how many different people looked after you in one day.
- e.g. A paati who walks a child to the bus stop is doing what an elephant aunt does.
- **Sarah Blaffer Hrdy:** Human children were always raised by many carers, not by the mother alone. Without helpers, our long childhood could not have arisen.
- **Scientists who stress the pair:** The bond between mother and father, and the father's help, mattered most. Helpers added to it.
### Female and male are not always fixed for life · *defined*
**Definition:** In some animals one individual makes both kinds of sex cell, or changes from one sex to the other, or becomes female or male depending on the warmth of the nest.

Because female and male are defined by sex cells, an animal's sex can be less fixed than we expect. An earthworm makes both egg cells and sperm, so each earthworm is both female and male. When two earthworms mate, each gives sperm to the other. All clownfish start life as males. In a group living at one sea anemone, the largest fish is the female and the second largest is her mate. If the female is removed, her mate changes into a female, and the next fish in size becomes the breeding male. In sea turtles, the sex of a hatchling is not decided when the egg is laid. It depends on how warm the sand is around the nest. For olive ridley turtles, who nest on the coasts of Odisha and Tamil Nadu, a nest at about 29 to 31 degrees gives about half females and half males. Warmer nests give more females and cooler nests more males. Scientists worry that as beaches warm, too few males will hatch.
- e.g. A garden snail, like an earthworm, is a hermaphrodite.
- e.g. A clownfish who is a father this year may be a mother next year.
- e.g. People who protect turtle nests on Chennai's beaches sometimes measure the warmth of the sand.
### Does the size of the sex cells decide the roles? · *debated*
Scientists agree on what female and male mean. They disagree on how much follows from it. One side says the size of the sex cells pushes females and males in different directions in nearly all animals. Females start out having spent more on each young, so they tend to care and to choose, and males tend to compete. In 2016 Tim Janicke and his team put together 72 studies of 66 kinds of animals. On average, competition for partners was stronger among males. The other side says the push is weak, and that surroundings decide most of it. Hanna Kokko and Michael Jennions pointed out in 2008 that having spent more in the past is no reason, in itself, to spend more in future. They say care goes to whoever gains most by giving it, and that depends on things such as how many partners are free and how sure a parent is of being the parent. Patricia Gowaty showed in 2012 that a famous experiment behind the first view had a flaw. Both sides accept the facts in this unit. They differ on how strong the pull of the sex cells is.
- e.g. For the first view: in most kinds of mammals, mothers do the caring and males compete.
- e.g. For the second view: jacanas, seahorses and hyenas have the same two sizes of sex cell as cows, yet they have very different roles.
- **Tim Janicke and his team, 2016:** Across 66 kinds of animals, competition for partners was on average stronger in males. The size of the sex cells sets a pattern, and surroundings can strengthen or weaken it.
- **Hanna Kokko and Michael Jennions, 2008:** Past spending does not decide future care. Who cares depends on what each parent gains or loses now, and that differs from species to species.
- **Patricia Gowaty and her team, 2012:** The fruit fly test that started the idea of eager males and choosy females was flawed. Roles vary much more than that idea allowed.

## Mostly agreed
- An animal is female if they make the larger sex cells and male if they make the smaller ones. Nothing else defines the two sexes across all animals. *How we know:* Egg cells and sperm have been measured in thousands of species. Animals whose roles are reversed, such as jacanas and seahorses, still have large egg cells in females and small sperm in males.
- Sexual selection is real, and it works through both competing and choosing. *How we know:* Darwin proposed it in 1871. Since then, experiments have changed tails, calls and colours and counted partners. Fights between males have been watched and scored in seals, deer and antelope.
- When the father gives most of the care, the usual roles tend to swap. *How we know:* In jacanas the males incubate and the larger females fight over territories. In Mormon crickets, where the male gives a big packet of food, Darryl Gwynne found in 1981 that males refuse most females.
- Most kinds of birds raise their young as two parents, and most kinds of mammals do not. *How we know:* Andrew Cockburn counted the bird families in 2006. Both parents care in about 81 of every 100 kinds of birds. Dieter Lukas and Tim Clutton-Brock found in 2013 that about 9 of every 100 kinds of mammals live as pairs.
- Two birds who live as a pair are not always the only parents of the chicks in their nest. *How we know:* DNA tests on chicks, reviewed by Simon Griffith and others in 2002, found chicks with an outside father in about nine out of ten kinds of birds tested.
- In elephants, an older female leader helps her whole family. *How we know:* Karen McComb's team played recorded elephant calls to families in Kenya. Families with older matriarchs told strangers from friends better, and had more calves. They published this in 2001.

## How we found out
**Angus Bateman in England, Sarah Blaffer Hrdy at Mount Abu in India, and Patricia Gowaty with Yong-Kyu Kim and Wyatt Anderson in the United States, Bateman published in 1948. Hrdy's book The Langurs of Abu came out in 1977. Gowaty's team published their repeat in 2012..** In 1948 Angus Bateman put fruit flies together in bottles and counted their young. He reported that males had more young when they had more partners, and that females did not. For about forty years this one test was taken as a rule for all animals. Males were said to be eager, and females coy and faithful. The evidence did not hold. In the 1970s Sarah Blaffer Hrdy watched langur monkeys at Mount Abu in Rajasthan. She reported that the females mated with more than one male. From the late 1980s, DNA tests on birds showed that chicks in one nest often had different fathers. Then, in 2012, Patricia Gowaty's team repeated Bateman's test as closely as they could. They found a flaw. Bateman had told the parents apart by marks that the young flies inherited. Some combinations of those marks killed the young before they could be counted. So his counts of who had mated were wrong. The repeat showed no such difference between the sexes. Scientists still argue about how different the sexes are on average, but the old rule is gone.  
*What people believed before:* That males of all animals are eager and gain from many partners, while females are coy and gain nothing from more than one. Field studies, DNA tests on chicks and a careful repeat of the first test all showed that this was not a rule.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Males are eager by nature, and females are coy and faithful by nature.** (Many biologists from 1948 until about the 1980s, following Angus Bateman's fruit fly test.; *rejected*)  
  For: In many mammals and birds, males do court more openly, and females do refuse more often.  
  Against: Females of many species seek several partners, as DNA tests on bird chicks showed. Jacana females fight for mates. When Bateman's test was repeated in 2012, the result did not hold.
- **In nature the male leads the group and the female follows.** (A common belief, also found in many old nature books.; *rejected*)  
  For: In gorillas, a silverback male does lead the group and settle quarrels.  
  Against: Elephant families and hyena clans are led by females. Jacana females hold the territory. Many groups, such as pigeon pairs, have no leader at all.
- **The male in a harem is in charge of it.** (A common way of describing harems.; *debated*)  
  For: A harem male does drive rival males away.  
  Against: Many female elephant seals also mate at sea with males who hold no harem. A harem male may go without food for three months, and many males never win a harem at all.
- **If the father carries the young, the females must be the ones who compete.** (Many books and websites about seahorses.; *debated*)  
  For: It is true of jacanas and of some pipefish, where females are brighter and fight for males.  
  Against: In the seahorses that have been studied, males still compete more than females. They wrestle with their tails for partners.
- **A parent who has already spent more on a young should keep spending more.** (Part of Robert Trivers's reasoning in 1972.; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels sensible, and mothers who have spent more often do keep caring.  
  Against: Richard Dawkins and Tamsin Carlisle showed in 1976 that this is a mistake in reasoning. What matters is what a parent would gain or lose from now on. What has already been spent cannot be got back.
- **What other animals do shows what is right for people.** (People on every side of arguments about families.; *rejected*)  
  For: Humans are animals, and we can learn how bodies and families work by studying other animals.  
  Against: Other animals do almost everything. Some pair for life, some keep harems, some leave their young at once. A fact about what happens cannot tell us what ought to happen.

## Roots
In the Warm Egg Test you found out what an egg loses each time the sitter leaves. You also met fathers who carry the young and mothers who lead. Now we ask what this tells us about the words mother and father, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the Warm Egg Test, the tumbler covered for half the time cooled much more than the one always covered. A bird's egg needs a sitter nearly all the time, and either parent can sit. So in most birds both parents care, and among jacanas the father sits alone. An animal is female or male only by the size of the sex cells they make. Who cares for the young depends on what the young need and who can give it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We use the word mother to mean the one who looks after the young, and father to mean the one who fights or goes away. We treat those jobs as part of being female or male. The science defines female and male only by sex cells, and it finds the jobs shared out in every possible way.  
*Evidence:* In the Warm Egg Test the bare minutes cost the tumbler warmth that the covered minutes could not put back. A pigeon father and mother take turns for that reason. A calf needs milk, which only the mother makes, so cattle fathers give no care. A seahorse father has the pouch, so he carries.

- *who am i:* You are an animal whose kind raises children with many hands. You are here because several people spent their time and energy on you.
- *what is life:* No life goes on to the next generation unless someone gives up part of their own energy for the young. Different animals share out that giving in different ways.
- *what is this world:* The world has no single correct family. A pond, a beach, a tree hollow and a herd each led to a different one.

The first living beings that mixed their instructions made sex cells of one size. From that one kind came two kinds, large and small. From those two kinds came every family in this unit: the pair of pigeons, the seal harem, the jacana mother with four mates, the seahorse father with a pouch and the elephant grandmother. They look very different. Under each of them are the same two cells and the same need, which is that someone must spend energy on the young.

- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.4.3:** As paraphrased: in the beginning the Self was alone, and was as large as a woman and a man holding each other. The Self divided into two, and from the two halves came wife and husband. (The Upanishad says female and male came from one being who was both. Science says the two sizes of sex cell came from one earlier size. The two accounts are different kinds of statement, but both begin with one and arrive at two.)
- **Tao Te Ching 28:** As paraphrased: know the male, but keep to the female, and be like a valley that all the streams of the world run into. (Lao Tzu asks one person to hold both. A jacana father who guards his patch and also warms the eggs does both jobs in one body, and so does a clownfish who is father one year and mother the next.)
- **Sutta Nipata 1.8, the Metta Sutta (verse 149):** As paraphrased: as a mother would guard her only child with her life, so let a person grow a heart without limit towards all beings. (The Buddha takes a mother's care as the model, and then asks everyone to give it. In nature too, care is given by fathers, aunts, sisters and helpers, and is not the mother's alone.)
- Science (settled): Female and male are defined by the size of the sex cells. In different species the young are cared for by the mother, the father, both, a group, or nobody.
- Science (idea): The first sex cells were probably all one size, and the two sizes arose later.
- Science (debated): How strongly the size of the sex cells pushes females towards caring and males towards competing is still argued over.
- Other view, Tim Janicke and other biologists who study sexual selection across many species: They say the roles are not open to every arrangement equally. In the 66 kinds of animals they compared, males on average competed more and females cared more. They trace this to the sex cells themselves. In their view jacanas and seahorses are real, but they are exceptions to a pattern that the sex cells set.

## Story: The Big One Never Sits
*A made-up story. Thamarai, her aunt Vasuki, her cousin Kathir, the village tank and the kite are imagined. The birds are real. Bronze-winged jacanas walk on floating leaves on ponds across India. The female is much heavier than the male and holds a stretch of pond with one to four mates. She lays four eggs for each mate. The male alone sits on the eggs for about 29 days and looks after the chicks. He calls loudly to bring the female to him, and he carries the chicks under his wings when there is danger.*

The first page of Chithi's notebook had a rule on it, written in green ink. 'Write what you saw. Write what you guessed. Never mix the two.' Thamarai read the rule, nodded, and broke it within the hour.

The tank lay behind the village, and after the August rain half of it was covered in lotus leaves. Vasuki Chithi, her mother's younger sister, counted the birds here every monsoon for a wetland survey. This year she had a sprained ankle and a niece on holiday. 'You will be my legs,' she said. She hung the binoculars around Thamarai's neck and gave her the notebook. 'Find the jacana nests. Then tell me who is who.'

The jacanas were easy to find. They were dark birds with bronze-brown wings, and they walked on the lotus leaves as if the pond were a floor. Their toes were as long as Thamarai's fingers. By noon she had found three nests. Each was a small wet heap of weeds on the leaves, and on each heap a bird sat. A fourth bird walked around all three. That bird was much bigger than the others. When a strange jacana landed at the far end, the big one ran at the stranger with both wings raised, and the stranger left.

Thamarai sat on the bund and wrote. 'Three mothers on three nests. One father, very big, guards them all. He is like a king with three queens.'

Chithi read the page. She said nothing. She turned to the first page and tapped the rule.

'But it is obvious,' said Thamarai. 'The big strong one is the father. The ones on the eggs are the mothers.'

'What did you see?'

'Three small ones sitting. One big one walking about and chasing.'

'Then that is what you write. Nest three has only one egg so far. Come at first light tomorrow, and watch how the second one arrives.'

At first light the tank was grey, and mist lay on the water. The small bird at nest three was standing beside the heap of weeds. The bird stretched up and called. It was a loud, rough yell, and it came again and again. Across the leaves, the big bird turned and came walking.

The two of them stood together for a while. Then the big bird stepped on to the heap, settled low, and was very still.

Thamarai held her breath. She did not know why.

The big bird stood up, shook, and walked away across the leaves without looking back. Where one egg had been, there were two. The small bird stepped on to the heap, pushed both eggs under his belly, and sat.

Thamarai lowered the binoculars. She looked at what she had written the day before. Then she walked back along the bund to where Chithi sat with her foot up on a stool.

'The big one laid the egg,' she said. 'I saw it. The big one is the mother.'

'Of nest three,' said Chithi. 'And nests one and two?'

Thamarai opened her mouth and shut it again. 'I don't know,' she said slowly. 'I did not see those eggs laid.'

Chithi smiled for the first time that week. 'That is the best sentence you have said so far. Write it down.'

She watched for nine more mornings. Two more eggs came to nest three, each one after a visit from the big bird. The big bird never sat for longer than it took to lay. The small bird sat through the rain. He left only for a few minutes at a time to feed, and he ran back across the leaves each time as if the eggs were calling him. Chithi told her the rest in the evenings. Among jacanas the female is the larger one. She holds the whole stretch of pond and keeps other females out. She may have four mates, and she lays four eggs for each.

'So she is not a king with three queens,' said Thamarai. 'What is she, then?'

'What have you seen her do?'

'Lay eggs. Chase strangers. Walk about.'

'And him?'

'Sit,' said Thamarai. 'Sit, and sit, and sit.'

Nest one hatched in the third week. Four chicks came out on legs that looked too long for them. They followed the small bird across the leaves, pecking at whatever he pecked at. Thamarai was drawing them when a shadow slid over the tank. A kite had dropped low and was turning above the lotus.

She stood up and shouted before she knew she was going to. The small bird crouched. The chicks ran to him, and then there were no chicks at all. He stood up and walked quickly towards the reeds. From under each of his wings hung two pairs of thin legs, swinging as he went.

Thamarai laughed out loud. Then she sat down again and was quiet for some time. She opened the notebook at her first day. She did not cross anything out. Beside it she wrote in small letters: 'Guessed, and wrong. I thought big meant father and sitting meant mother. Seen: the big one lays the eggs. The small ones sit on them and carry the chicks.'

That evening her cousin Kathir, who was seven, came running along the bund and pointed. 'Look, the mother bird is taking her babies for a walk!'

Thamarai took the binoculars from around her neck and put them around his. 'Come tomorrow at first light,' she said. 'And bring a pencil.'

**For the parent:** Thamarai decided who was the mother and who was the father from their size and from who sat on the eggs. Then she watched an egg being laid. Among jacanas the larger bird who guards the pond is the female. The smaller birds who sit on the eggs and carry the chicks are the males.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Warm Egg Test: what does an egg lose when the sitter leaves? (38 min)
- **question:** How much warmth does an 'egg' lose if the cover is on all the time, half the time, or never? Is the half-covered tumbler nearer to the always-covered one or to the bare one?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. Put the three tumblers in order from warmest to coolest at the end. Then guess whether the half-covered tumbler will end nearer to the always-covered one or nearer to the bare one. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** All three tumblers are the same size and are filled to the same level from one jug, so they start equally warm. They stand on the same folded newspaper, away from a fan, a window and the stove. The two covers are the same kind of lid and the same kind of cloth. Only the time under cover is different.
- **safety:** A grown-up mixes the warm water. It should be warm bath water, about 40 degrees, and never hot enough to hurt a finger held in it. Do not use water straight from a kettle.
- **record:** Write the warmth of each tumbler at the start and after 20 minutes, and take one from the other to find the warmth lost. If you have no thermometer, write the order from warmest to coolest that the blind finger test gave.
- **results table:** Tumbler | Time under cover | Warmth at the start | Warmth after 20 minutes | Warmth lost
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three empty tumblers, the lids and the cloths. Explain what will happen to each tumbler. Ask for both guesses and the reason. Write the guesses above the table. Do not say whether you agree.  
  *Parent says:* Each tumbler of warm water is a clutch of eggs. A is covered all the time. B is covered for half the time. C is never covered. Which will end warmest? Will B end nearer to A or nearer to C? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A is always covered. B is covered half the time. C is bare. Guess the order at the end. Will B end nearer A or C? Say why.
  - Ask: Will B end nearer to A, nearer to C, or exactly in the middle? Why do you think so? (good: In the middle, because it is covered half the time.; Nearer to C, because the heat that leaves does not come back.; Nearer to A, because the cloth stays warm.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is a good start. Most children get the order right. The second guess is the real question, and nobody at the table knows the answer yet.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Mix warm water in a jug, about as warm as a nice bath. Fill the three tumblers to one finger-width below the rim. Stand them on the folded newspaper with their slips. Measure each with the thermometer, or feel each with a finger. Write the start in the table.  
  *Parent says:* I will mix the water. You fill the three tumblers to the same level. Now measure each one. Are they the same at the start?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the three tumblers to the same level. Measure or feel each one. Write it down.
  - Note: A fever thermometer reads only from about 32 to 42 degrees. If you use one, start with water at 40 to 42 degrees. Dip only the tip, and wait for the beep. If the three starts differ by more than half a degree, pour them all back into the jug and fill again.
- **s3 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a lid on A and wrap a cloth around it. Do the same to B. Leave C bare. Start a 20-minute timer. Your child is the timekeeper for B. At 5 minutes they take B's lid and cloth off. At 10 minutes they put them back. At 15 minutes they take them off again. While you wait, your child draws the results table.  
  *Parent says:* You are the parent of tumbler B. At 5 minutes you leave to feed, so take the cover off. At 10 minutes you come back. At 15 minutes you leave again. Do not touch A or C at all.  
  *Child mode:* Cover A and B. Leave C bare. At 5 minutes uncover B. At 10 cover it. At 15 uncover it again. Draw your table while you wait.
  - Ask: If these were real eggs, what kind of family would tumbler A be? And B? And C? (good: A has two parents who take turns, so the eggs are never bare.; B has one parent who must leave to find food.; C has nobody sitting.)
  - Note: Keep the room as it is: no fan switched on or off halfway. When B is uncovered, put its lid and cloth down beside it, not on top of another tumbler.

  *Parent does:* When the timer rings, take all covers off. Measure A, then B, then C, with the thermometer. With no thermometer, your child shuts their eyes. You slide two tumblers in front of them. They dip one finger of each hand and say which is warmer. Do all three pairs. Write the results.

Your tumbler B usually ends somewhere between A and C. Whether it is nearer to A or to C depends on your room, and either is a fair result. A real bird does add warmth from her or his own body when sitting, which a cloth cannot do. But the bird must first eat to make that warmth, and must warm the eggs up again after every trip away. This is why two pigeons take turns on one nest, and why a hornbill father feeds the mother while she stays inside. If all three tumblers ended almost the same, your room is very warm. Run the test again for 30 minutes, or near a fan that blows on all three equally.
  - Think: A calf grows inside the mother for nine months. Who keeps the calf warm, and can the father take a turn?
### e2 · The One Guard Test: how many can one guard hold on to? (25 min)
- **question:** How many of 12 coins can one guard keep for 30 seconds when the coins are close together, spread out, or far apart?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. How many of the 12 coins will the guard still have after 30 seconds in each of the three layouts? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same person guards in every try and the same person takes, because people differ in speed. Every try lasts 30 seconds. The taker follows the same rules each time. Each layout is tried three times, because one try can be a fluke. Only how far apart the coins are is different.
- **safety:** Play kneeling on the floor, not at a table edge. The guard taps the back of the taker's hand gently with open fingers. No grabbing, slapping or pushing. Keep heads apart.
- **record:** Write the coins the guard still has after each 30-second try. For each layout, circle the middle of the three numbers.
- **results table:** Layout | Try 1: coins kept | Try 2: coins kept | Try 3: coins kept | Middle number
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three layouts with the plate, the newspaper and two big steps. Explain the rules. Ask for a guess for each layout, and the reason. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* You will guard 12 coins with one hand for 30 seconds. I will try to take them. How many will you keep when they are close? Spread out? Far apart? Why?  
  *Child mode:* You guard 12 coins with one hand. Guess how many you keep each time. Say why.
  - Ask: In which layout will guarding be hardest, and why? (good: Far apart, because my hand cannot be everywhere.; Far apart, because the taker can go where I am not.)
  - Note: Decide who guards and who takes, and keep to it for the whole test. A child usually enjoys guarding. You can swap for a second full test afterwards.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Agree the rules and try them once slowly. The guard kneels and uses one hand. The taker uses one finger to slide one coin at a time to the home bowl behind them. If the guard taps the back of the taker's hand, the taker drops the coin and touches the bowl before trying again.  
  *Parent says:* One hand for you, one finger for me, one coin at a time. If you tap my hand, I go home with nothing. Let us try it slowly once.  
  *Child mode:* Guard with one hand. A tap sends the taker home. Try it slowly once.
  - Note: The home bowl should be an arm's length behind the taker, so that every trip takes a moment. Keep the taps gentle. A coin that was dropped goes back where it was.
- **s3 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do three tries of 30 seconds with the close layout. After each, count the coins the guard still has and write it. Then do three tries with the spread layout, and three with the far layout. Rest between tries.  
  *Parent says:* Thirty seconds, starting now. Stop. How many coins do you still have? Write it down. Now we set them out again.  
  *Child mode:* Guard for 30 seconds. Count your coins. Write the number. Do every layout three times.
  - Note: Take as many as you fairly can, in the same way each time. If you go easy in one layout, the test shows nothing. In the far layout the guard may move about on their knees.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Circle the middle number for each layout. Your child writes what I think, what I saw, and why. Only after that, give the explanation in the parent note.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your middle numbers. What changed as the coins moved apart? Write what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Circle the middle numbers. What changed as the coins spread out? Write what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What changed as the coins moved further apart? (good: I kept fewer coins.; My hand could not reach the far ones in time.; The taker went wherever I was not.)
  - Ask: Female seals crowd together on one small beach. Female pigeons nest far apart across a city. Which is like which layout? (good: The seals are like the close layout, so one male can guard many.; The pigeons are like the far layout, so one male cannot guard several.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain. Stephen Emlen and Lewis Oring said in 1977 that harems arise where females, or the food and nesting places they need, are gathered closely enough for one animal to guard. Where they are spread out, an animal who tried to guard several partners would lose them all, and does better with one. The coins are a model. Real females are not coins. They move, and they choose, and many female seals also mate with males away from the harem.
  - Check: Why can one male elephant seal keep a harem, when a male pigeon cannot? → **Female seals gather closely, so one male can guard them**
  - Think: The coins could not walk away or choose. What would change if they could?

### c1 · The Parent Is Away: build a nest that keeps an egg warm alone (25 min)
- **problem:** A parent who sits alone must leave the eggs to find food. Build a nest that keeps a small tumbler of warm water as warm as possible for 15 minutes while the parent is away. Test it against a bare tumbler.
- **your turn:** Now design a nest for the opposite problem. Some birds lay their eggs on hot open sand, where the eggs could cook. Draw a nest, or a thing the parent could do, that keeps an egg cool for 15 minutes.
- **constraints:** Use only things already at home, such as newspaper, cloth scraps, cotton wool, a cardboard box, dry leaves or coconut fibre.; Nothing may heat the tumbler. No hot water bottle, no sunlight and no stove.; The parent must be able to get back in. The top must open in five seconds or less.; The nest must fit on a dinner plate, and you have ten minutes to build it.
- **general:** Wrap the tumbler in several layers of cloth or newspaper.; Stand it inside a box packed with crumpled paper.; Cover the top, because a lot of warmth leaves with the damp air that rises from the water.
- **creative:** Line the nest with something fluffy that traps still air, as an eider duck lines her nest with her own down feathers.; Lift the tumbler off the cold floor on a thick pad, as a penguin father keeps the egg on his feet.; Bury the tumbler in a deep heap. The Nicobar megapode, a bird of the Nicobar Islands, buries eggs in a mound and lets the mound keep them warm.; Make the nest small and snug. A big empty space inside lets the air move and carry warmth away.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem and the constraints aloud. Ask your child where the warmth of a tumbler goes, and what could slow it down. They sketch a plan.  
  *Parent says:* Where does the warmth go when a tumbler cools? Up, down or sideways? What could slow it down? Sketch your nest before you build.  
  *Child mode:* Where does the warmth go? What could slow it? Sketch your nest.
  - Ask: Where does the warmth of the tumbler go? (good: Up into the air from the top of the water.; Into the table through the bottom.; Out through the steel sides.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child builds the nest around an empty tumbler in ten minutes. Check that it fits on the plate and that the top opens in five seconds.  
  *Parent says:* You have ten minutes. Build it around the empty tumbler. Show me that the top opens quickly.  
  *Child mode:* Build your nest in ten minutes. Check that it fits the plate. Check that the top opens fast.
  - Note: Let your child make their own choices, even ones you expect to fail. The test will show what works.
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Mix warm water and fill both tumblers to the same level. Put one in the nest and close the top. Leave the other bare beside it. Measure or feel both. Start a 15-minute timer.  
  *Parent says:* I will pour the water. You put one tumbler in the nest and close it. The bare one is for comparing. Start the timer.  
  *Child mode:* Put one tumbler in the nest. Leave one bare. Start the timer.
  - Note: The water should be warm and never hot. While you wait, your child can start the 'your turn' drawing.
  - Wait: Leave both tumblers for 15 minutes
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 15 minutes, open the nest. Compare the two tumblers with a thermometer, or with one finger in each and eyes shut. Your child says what worked, changes one thing and says what they expect it to do.  
  *Parent says:* Open it. Which is warmer, and by how much? What part of your nest did the most? Change one thing and tell me what you expect.  
  *Child mode:* Compare the two tumblers. What worked best? Change one thing and say why.
  - Ask: Which part of your nest did the most to keep the warmth in? (good: The cover on top.; The thick paper all around.; Lifting it off the floor.)
  - Note: If the two tumblers feel the same, the nest is probably open at the top or thin underneath. A good nest keeps the water clearly warmer than the bare tumbler after 15 minutes.

## Worksheets
### Who Cares for the Young? (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What makes an animal female?  
   Options: She makes the larger sex cells / She looks after the young / She is smaller than the male  
   **Answer:** She makes the larger sex cells. *Why:* A female is the one who makes egg cells, the larger sex cells. Caring and size do not decide it. Jacana females are bigger than males and do not sit on the eggs.
2. (mcq) A seahorse father carries the young. Why is he still called male?  
   Options: He makes sperm / He is the bigger one / He does not feed the young  
   **Answer:** He makes sperm. *Why:* Male means the maker of the smaller sex cells, the sperm. Carrying the young does not change which sex cells he makes.
3. (truefalse) In most kinds of birds, both parents care for the young.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both parents care in about four out of five kinds of birds. Eggs need warmth almost all the time, and either parent can give it.
4. (truefalse) In most kinds of mammals, the father helps to raise the young.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Fathers help in about one kind of mammal in ten, or fewer. The young grow inside the mother and then need her milk.
5. (fill) A female who leads her group, such as an old elephant, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['matriarch']. *Why:* A matriarch is a female leader. In elephants she is usually the oldest female in the family.
6. (match) Match each family system to an animal that lives that way.  
   **Answer:** A pair → sarus cranes; One male, several females → gorillas; One female, several males → jacanas; Helpers also raise the young → elephants. *Why:* Sarus cranes live as pairs. A silverback gorilla lives with several females. A female jacana has several mates. Elephant aunts and sisters help with calves.
7. (sort) Sort each animal by who looks after the eggs or young.  
   **Answer:** elephant seal → Mother alone; king cobra → Mother alone; bronze-winged jacana → Father alone; giant water bug → Father alone; rock pigeon → Both parents; great hornbill → Both parents. *Why:* A seal pup needs only milk. A king cobra mother guards her nest. Jacana and water bug fathers carry or sit alone. Pigeons and hornbills need two parents.
8. (mcq) In the Warm Egg Test, which tumbler lost the least warmth?  
   Options: The one always covered / The one covered half the time / The one never covered  
   **Answer:** The one always covered. *Why:* A cover slows the loss of warmth. The tumbler that was never bare lost the least. If you chose the half-covered one, remember that it lost warmth in every bare minute.
9. (mcq) What are the two parts of sexual selection?  
   Options: Competing for partners and choosing partners / Finding food and hiding from hunters / Laying eggs and guarding them  
   **Answer:** Competing for partners and choosing partners. *Why:* Sexual selection is about winning partners, by competing with rivals and by being chosen. Finding food and hiding are about staying alive, which is a different matter.
10. (count) A guard kept 12, 11 and 12 coins in three tries. What is the middle number?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* Put the numbers in order: 11, 12, 12. The middle one is 12. The middle number is not pulled about by one odd try.
11. (write) Why can a pigeon father help with the eggs, when a bull cannot help much with a calf?  
   **Answer:** Eggs need warmth, and a father can sit on them as well as a mother can. A calf grows inside the mother and then needs milk, which only she makes.. *Why:* A strong answer names what the young need in each case, and says who is able to give it.
12. (write) Name two things a seahorse father's pouch does for the young inside.  
   **Answer:** Any two of these. It brings them oxygen. It gives them some food. It carries away their waste. It keeps them safe from being eaten.. *Why:* The pouch does much of what a mammal mother's body does for a baby before birth.
13. (draw) Draw a jacana family on a pond: one female and her mates. Label who sits on the eggs.  
   **Answer:** A larger bird walking on the leaves, and two to four smaller birds each sitting on a nest. The smaller birds are labelled as the males who sit on the eggs.. *Why:* A strong drawing makes the female larger, shows her off the nests, and labels the males as the sitters.
14. (mcq) Why does an elephant family do better with an old leader?  
   Options: She remembers friends, strangers and places / She is the strongest fighter / She has the most calves  
   **Answer:** She remembers friends, strangers and places. *Why:* In tests, families with older leaders told friends from strangers better. Her memory is what helps the family. Strength was not what the scientists found.
15. (perspective) A jacana mother lays her eggs and leaves them with the father.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer notices that the chicks are cared for by the father, and asks whether a word for human duties fits a bird.. *Why:* Both views agree on what the jacana does. They differ on whether a human judgement can be put on it.
### Competing, Choosing and Counting (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Ten female and ten male seals have ten pups. One male fathers eight. How many do the other nine males share?  
   Options: 2 / 8 / 10  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Every pup has one father. If one male fathered 8 of the 10, only 2 are left for the other nine males. Most of them fathered none.
2. (mcq) Why is the sex that spends more on each young usually the choosier one?  
   Options: A poor choice costs them more / They are shyer by nature / They are the weaker sex  
   **Answer:** A poor choice costs them more. *Why:* A cow who chooses badly loses a whole year. A bull loses almost nothing. Choosiness follows the cost. In jacanas the father spends more, and he is the one the females compete for.
3. (truefalse) All the females in a group together have the same number of young as all the males together.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each young has exactly one mother and one father, so the two totals must be equal. What differs is how evenly the young are shared out.
4. (truefalse) Two birds who share a nest are always the only parents of the chicks in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. DNA tests found chicks with an outside father in about nine out of ten kinds of birds tested. Living together and being the parents are separate questions.
5. (fill) A family system with one female and several males is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['polyandry']. *Why:* Polyandry means one female with several male partners. Jacanas live this way.
6. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Polygyny → one male, several females; Polyandry → one female, several males; Monogamy → one female and one male; Promiscuity → several partners each, no lasting pair; Cooperative breeding → helpers also raise the young. *Why:* The names describe who pairs with whom. They are not grades from good to bad.
7. (order) Put in order what happens when seahorses breed.  
   **Answer:** ['The pair greet each other each morning', "The female passes her eggs into the male's pouch", 'The eggs are fertilised inside the pouch', 'The pouch brings oxygen and food to the young', 'The father pushes out the live young']. *Why:* The mother makes the eggs, and the father does the carrying and gives birth.
8. (mcq) In one kind of animal, males are five times as heavy as females. Which family is most likely?  
   Options: One male guards many females / Pairs who share the care / Groups that females lead  
   **Answer:** One male guards many females. *Why:* Males become much heavier where a few males win most partners by fighting, as in elephant seals. In pairs, the sexes are usually close in size.
9. (mcq) A male Mormon cricket gives the female a big packet of food. What did scientists find?  
   Options: Females compete, and males choose / Males fight harder than ever / Nobody chooses at all  
   **Answer:** Females compete, and males choose. *Why:* The male spends a lot on each mating, so he becomes the choosy one. This is the role swap that Trivers's idea predicts.
10. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Jacana. Seahorse. Giant water bug. Elephant seal.  
   **Answer:** Elephant seal. In elephant seals the mother alone cares for the young. In the other three, the father does.. *Why:* Jacana fathers sit on the eggs, seahorse fathers carry them in a pouch, and water bug fathers carry them on their backs.
11. (count) Tumbler A fell from 41 to 39 degrees. Tumbler C fell from 41 to 35. How many more degrees did C lose?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* A lost 2 degrees and C lost 6. 6 take away 2 is 4. The bare tumbler lost three times as much as the covered one.
12. (write) A fish father already guards a patch of sea floor. Explain why guarding eggs there costs him little extra.  
   **Answer:** He is already there, driving rivals away from the patch. The eggs lie on the same patch, so guarding them needs almost no extra time or energy. He can also guard the eggs of several females at once.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the guarding was already being done for another reason.
13. (write) Hyena females lead, and jacana females fight. What does this show about the claim 'in nature, males lead'?  
   **Answer:** The claim is not true of all animals. It was drawn from a few well-known animals, such as gorillas. Other animals do the reverse, and many have no leader.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a few examples cannot make a rule, and gives at least one animal that does not fit.
14. (draw) Draw an egg cell and a sperm side by side. Label which is larger and what each one carries.  
   **Answer:** A large round egg cell labelled 'half the instructions, plus food'. A much smaller sperm with a tail, labelled 'half the instructions, almost no food'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a big difference in size, and shows that both carry half of the instructions.

### Test the Idea (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Some pond algae make sex cells of one size only. What follows from that?  
   Options: They have no females and no males / They are all female / They cannot have young  
   **Answer:** They have no females and no males. *Why:* Female and male are defined by two sizes of sex cell. With one size there are no two sexes, yet the cells still join and make young.
2. (mcq) Why might middle-sized sex cells do badly?  
   Options: Too few to find partners, too small to feed young / They swim too fast to join / Other cells eat them first  
   **Answer:** Too few to find partners, too small to feed young. *Why:* Tiny cells win by being many. Large cells win by feeding the young well. A middle size gets neither gain. This is the leading idea, and it is not proved.
3. (mcq) The sand on a turtle beach gets warmer every year. What is likely to change?  
   Options: More hatchlings will be female / More hatchlings will be male / The eggs will grow larger  
   **Answer:** More hatchlings will be female. *Why:* In sea turtles the warmth of the nest sets the sex of the hatchling. Warmer nests give more females. Too few males could become a problem.
4. (truefalse) A clownfish can be a father one year and a mother in a later year.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. All clownfish start as males. If the female of a group is lost, her mate changes into a female.
5. (truefalse) What a parent has already spent on a young is a good reason to keep spending.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. What is spent cannot be got back. What matters is what the parent would gain or lose from now on. Trivers made this mistake in 1972, and others corrected it.
6. (fill) An animal who makes both egg cells and sperm is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['hermaphrodite']. *Why:* Earthworms and garden snails are hermaphrodites. Each one is both female and male.
7. (sort) Sort each one: can you find it out by watching, or does it need a DNA test?  
   **Answer:** two birds feed the chicks → Seen by watching; the male sits on the eggs → Seen by watching; the big bird chased an intruder → Seen by watching; this male fathered every chick → Needs a DNA test; two chicks have different fathers → Needs a DNA test. *Why:* Watching shows who lives with whom and who does what. Only a DNA test shows who the parents are.
8. (order) Put these in order, from males much heavier to females heavier.  
   **Answer:** ['Elephant seal', 'Gorilla', 'Sarus crane', 'Spotted hyena', 'Bronze-winged jacana']. *Why:* A male elephant seal is about five times as heavy as a female. A female bronze-winged jacana is much heavier than a male. The order follows who competes for partners.
9. (count) A female jacana lays 4 eggs for each of her 3 mates. How many eggs is that?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 4 times 3 is 12. She can lay so many because she does not sit on any of them.
10. (write) A new bird is found. The females are bigger and brighter than the males. Predict who sits on the eggs, and explain.  
   **Answer:** Probably the males. When females are the bigger, brighter sex, they are usually the ones competing for partners. That happens when males do most of the caring, as in jacanas.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the link between who spends more on the young and who competes, and says 'probably', because it is a tendency.

   **Answer:** Other animals live in every kind of family, so you could pick an animal to support any rule. What happens in nature is a fact. It cannot tell people what is fair or right.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a fact about what happens is not a rule about what ought to happen, and notes that other animals do the opposite.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Among all animals, which one is the female?  
   Options: The one who makes egg cells / The one who sits on the eggs / The smaller of the two  
   **Answer:** The one who makes egg cells. *Why:* Female means the maker of the larger sex cells, the egg cells. If you chose 'sits on the eggs', remember the jacana father and the emperor penguin father.
2. (mcq) Peahens pick among peacocks. Blackbuck males fight each other. Both are parts of what?  
   Options: Sexual selection / Parental care / Cooperative breeding  
   **Answer:** Sexual selection. *Why:* Choosing and competing are the two parts of sexual selection. Parental care is what happens after the eggs or young arrive.
3. (truefalse) Every pup has one mother and one father, so female and male seals have the same total number of pups.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The totals must match. But the pups are shared evenly among the mothers and very unevenly among the fathers.
4. (mcq) One female jacana has three mates. What is this family system called?  
   Options: Polyandry / Polygyny / Monogamy  
   **Answer:** Polyandry. *Why:* Polyandry is one female with several males. Polygyny is the reverse, one male with several females.
5. (mcq) One male gorilla lives with several females. What is this family system called?  
   Options: Polygyny / Polyandry / Promiscuity  
   **Answer:** Polygyny. *Why:* Polygyny is one male with several females. In promiscuity, both sexes have several partners and no group stays around one male.
6. (mcq) In jacanas the father does all the caring. Who competes for partners?  
   Options: The females / The males / Nobody competes  
   **Answer:** The females. *Why:* The sex that spends less on each young competes for the sex that spends more. In jacanas the fathers spend more, so the females compete.
7. (mcq) In the Warm Egg Test, what did the lid and cloth do?  
   Options: They slowed the loss of warmth / They made new warmth / They made no difference  
   **Answer:** They slowed the loss of warmth. *Why:* A cover only slows the loss. The warmth that left in the bare minutes did not come back. If you chose 'made new warmth', remember that even tumbler A cooled a little.
8. (mcq) Why do fathers help with the young in so few kinds of mammals?  
   Options: Only mothers carry the baby and make milk / Mammal fathers are lazy / Mammal babies need very little  
   **Answer:** Only mothers carry the baby and make milk. *Why:* What the young need most, a warm body to grow in and then milk, only the mother can give. So in most mammals a father can add little.
9. (mcq) Where do young seahorses grow before they are born?  
   Options: In a pouch on the father / Inside the mother / In a nest of seagrass  
   **Answer:** In a pouch on the father. *Why:* The mother puts her eggs into the father's brood pouch. He carries them for 9 to 45 days and then gives birth.
10. (truefalse) A spotted hyena clan is usually led by a female.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Hyena females outrank the males, and a cub takes the rank just below their mother.
11. (mcq) In tests, what did elephant families with older leaders do better?  
   Options: Tell friends from strangers / Run faster from danger / Find more partners  
   **Answer:** Tell friends from strangers. *Why:* Families with older matriarchs bunched together at a stranger's call and stayed calm at a friend's. The leader's memory helped everyone.
12. (mcq) Some animals keep harems. Does that show how people should live?  
   Options: No. A fact is not a rule / Yes. Nature is always right / Yes, if the animal is a mammal  
   **Answer:** No. A fact is not a rule. *Why:* Other animals live in every kind of family. What happens in nature is a fact about them. It cannot tell people what is fair or right.

## Applications
- People who protect sea turtle nests measure how warm the sand is, because the warmth decides how many hatchlings are female and how many are male.
- Zoos that breed rare animals now often let the animals choose their own partners, because pairs who choose each other tend to have more young.
- People who protect elephants try hardest to keep the old females safe, because a family that loses the matriarch loses what she remembers.
- Scientists use DNA tests to find out which animals are the parents of which young, in wild birds and in breeding programmes for rare animals.
- Poultry farms hatch eggs in incubators that hold a steady warmth and turn the eggs, doing the work of a sitting hen.
- In Arunachal Pradesh, a nest adoption programme pays local people to guard hornbill nest trees, so that sealed-in mothers and chicks stay safe.

## Open questions
- **Why did carrying the young fall to the father in seahorses?** There are several ideas, about speed, about being sure of being the father, and about small steps from guarding. Nobody has found a test that settles it.
- **Why do so many living beings mix their instructions with a partner at all, instead of simply copying themselves?** Copying yourself is quicker, and some lizards, insects and plants do it. Scientists think mixing helps the young to resist germs and to cope with change, but they still argue about which reason matters most.
- **Why do females lead among spotted hyenas?** Hyena females are only slightly larger than males. Ideas include help from relatives, and the way cubs take their mother's rank. Scientists have not agreed.
- **How did two sizes of sex cell first arise?** The maths of 1972 shows that it could have happened through many small cells finding more partners. It happened too long ago to watch, and other causes may have played a part.
- **As beaches warm, will sea turtles change where or when they nest, so that some males still hatch?** Turtles have lived through warm and cool ages before. Nobody knows whether they can change quickly enough this time.

## Connected maths
- In Kavya's Warm Egg Test, all three tumblers started at 41 degrees. After 20 minutes, A was at 39, B was at 37.5 and C was at 35. How many degrees did each lose? B was covered for half the time. Did B lose exactly halfway between A and C? **A lost 2 degrees, B lost 3.5 and C lost 6. Halfway between 2 and 6 is 4. B lost 3.5, so B ended a little nearer to A than to C.**. 41 take away 39 is 2. 41 take away 37.5 is 3.5. 41 take away 35 is 6. To find halfway between 2 and 6, add them and halve: 8 divided by 2 is 4. B lost half a degree less than that. Your own result may fall on the other side of halfway, and that is fine. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L5`)
- A beach has 10 female and 10 male elephant seals. 10 pups are born, one to each female. One huge male is the father of 8 of them. What is the average number of pups for a female? For a male? What is the largest number of the other nine males who can have fathered a pup? **The average is 1 pup for a female and 1 pup for a male. Only 2 pups are left for the other nine males, so at most 2 of them are fathers. At least 7 males have no pups.**. 10 pups shared among 10 females is 1 each. Every pup also has one father, so 10 pups shared among 10 males is also 1 each on average. But 10 take away 8 leaves 2 pups for nine males. The average is the same for both sexes. How evenly the pups are shared is very different. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L5`)
- A pheasant-tailed jacana father sits on a clutch for about 27 days. The breeding season lasts about 120 days. Even if he took a new clutch the day the last one hatched, how many clutches could he hatch? A female can lay up to 10 clutches in a season. What does she need? **He could hatch 4 clutches at the very most. She can lay 10, so she needs several mates. There are not enough free fathers, so the females compete for them.**. 120 divided by 27 is 4, with 12 days left over, which is not enough for a fifth clutch. In real life he also looks after the chicks, so he manages fewer than 4. The female can lay clutches faster than any one male can raise them. Males who are free to sit are the scarce thing, and the scarce sex is the one competed for. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- A female bronze-winged jacana weighs about 280 grams and a male about 175 grams. A male elephant seal weighs about 3,300 kilograms and a female about 600. In each animal, how many times as heavy is the heavier sex? Which difference is bigger? **The female jacana is 1.6 times as heavy as the male. The male seal is 5.5 times as heavy as the female. The seal's difference is much bigger, and it runs the other way.**. 280 divided by 175 is 1.6. 3,300 divided by 600 is 5.5. You cannot compare grams with kilograms directly, but you can compare the two 'times as heavy' numbers. In both animals the heavier sex is the one that fights for partners. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- An emperor penguin father may weigh 38 kilograms when winter starts and 18 kilograms about 120 days later. How many kilograms has he lost? Is that more or less than half of his starting weight? About how many grams is that each day? **He has lost 20 kilograms. Half of 38 is 19, so he has lost a little more than half of his weight. That is about 167 grams each day.**. 38 take away 18 is 20. Half of 38 is 19, and 20 is more than 19. 20 kilograms is 20,000 grams. 20,000 divided by 120 is about 167. That is roughly the weight of a medium-sized tomato or two idlis, every day for four months, with nothing eaten. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- In Meenakshi's One Guard Test, the guard kept these coins out of 12. Close layout: 12, 12, 11. Spread layout: 9, 10, 4. Far layout: 5, 6, 4. Find the middle number for each layout. Which single try looks odd? Should she leave it out? **The middle numbers are 12, 9 and 5. The try of 4 in the spread layout looks odd. She should keep it in the table and write a note about it. Using the middle number already stops one odd try from changing the result.**. In order, the close tries are 11, 12, 12, so the middle is 12. The spread tries are 4, 9, 10, so the middle is 9. The far tries are 4, 5, 6, so the middle is 5. The 4 in the spread layout is far from 9 and 10. Perhaps the guard was tired or looked away. Scientists do not hide a result that does not fit. They record it and say what may have happened. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- On the jacana card in Stay or Go, the young get 3 points if one or both parents stay, and 0 if nobody stays. The mother's bonus for going is 3 and the father's is 0. If the father stays, what does the mother score for staying, and for going? If the mother goes, what does the father score for staying, and for going? **If the father stays, the mother scores 3 for staying and 6 for going, so she does better to go. If the mother goes, the father scores 3 for staying and 0 for going, so he does better to stay.**. Mother stays: 3 young points. Mother goes while father stays: 3 young points plus her bonus of 3 makes 6. Father stays alone: 3 young points. Father goes too: nobody stays, so 0 young points plus his bonus of 0 makes 0. Each parent's best choice depends on what the other does. (queues `math.logic.if-then.L5`)
