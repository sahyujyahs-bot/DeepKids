# Solids, liquids and gases: why water takes the shape of its glass
`sci.states.solids-liquids-gases.L2`

**Summary.** If you pour water from a tumbler into a tall bottle, does it become more water? In this lesson you will pour one tumbler of water into three different shapes and check, sort things into solids, liquids and gases, and look closely at a boiling kettle to see where the steam is.

**Intro.** *Fill a glass tumbler halfway with water. If you pour that water into a tall, thin bottle, will there be more water, less water, or the same?* Many children say the thin bottle will have more, because the water will stand taller. Some say less. You are going to pour it and find out. Water can change its shape in a moment. An ice cube cannot. Why is that? This lesson answers that question, and you will test the answer yourself.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can sort things into solids, liquids and gases, and say why.
1. By the end I can say that a solid keeps its own shape, a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, and a gas spreads out to fill all the space it has.
2. By the end I can show that water poured into a new shape is still the same amount of water.
3. By the end I can say that ice, water and steam are the same water, as a solid, a liquid and a gas.
4. By the end I can explain that real steam is invisible, and the white cloud above a kettle is tiny drops of water.
5. By the end I can guess first, give a reason, and then say what my test showed.

## Terms
- **Solid**: A solid keeps its own shape. It does not flow, and it does not need a container. *e.g.* A steel spoon, a stone, a wooden block and an ice cube are solids.
- **Liquid**: A liquid flows. It has no shape of its own, so it takes the shape of whatever holds it. *e.g.* Water, milk, oil and rasam are liquids. Pour rasam from a pot into a bowl, and it takes the shape of the bowl.
- **Gas**: A gas spreads out to fill all the space it can get to. Most gases cannot be seen. *e.g.* Air is a gas. Blow up a balloon, and the air fills the whole balloon, not just the bottom.
- **State**: Whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas. These are the three states we see most. *e.g.* Water can be in all three states: ice, liquid water and steam.
- **Flow**: To move along and spread, the way water runs when you pour it. *e.g.* Water flows down a drain. Honey flows too, but slowly.
- **Container**: Something that holds things inside it, such as a tumbler, a bottle, a box or a bag. *e.g.* A water bottle is a container for water.
- **Melt**: To change from a solid into a liquid, when it gets warm. *e.g.* An ice cube melts into water on a warm plate. Ghee melts in a hot pan.
- **Freeze**: To change from a liquid into a solid, when it gets very cold. *e.g.* Water freezes into ice in the freezer.
- **Steam**: Water as a gas. It comes from water that is boiling. Real steam is invisible. *e.g.* Just above a kettle spout there is a small clear gap. That is where the real steam is.

## Explanations
### Solids keep their own shape · *defined*
**Definition:** A solid is something that keeps its own shape. It stays the same shape when you move it into a new container.

Put a steel spoon in a tumbler, then in a bowl, then on a plate. It is the same spoon shape every time. An ice cube is a solid too. In the experiment One Tumbler, Three Shapes, the ice cube was too big to go into the thin bottle, because it could not change its shape to fit. You can change a solid's shape by pushing, cutting or breaking it. But on its own, a solid stays the way it is.
- e.g. A stone keeps its shape in your hand or in a bucket.
- e.g. An ice cube would not go into the thin bottle.
- e.g. A wooden block stays a block wherever you put it.
- **Everyday view:** A solid keeps its shape for ever.
- **Closer look:** Some solids flow very slowly. The ice of a glacier creeps downhill, and pitch drips in an old experiment, a drop about once a decade. Window glass does not flow noticeably, which is a common mistake.
### Liquids flow and take the shape of their container · *defined*
**Definition:** A liquid flows. It has no shape of its own, so it takes the shape of whatever holds it. Its amount stays the same.

In the thin bottle, the water stood tall. In the wide bowl, it spread out low and flat. It looked like more, then less. But when we poured it back into the tumbler, it came up to the same line, which we had marked with a bindi. The shape changed. The amount did not. A liquid also always settles at the bottom of its container, with a flat top.
- e.g. Water from the thin bottle filled the tumbler back to the bindi line.
- e.g. Rasam poured from a pot takes the shape of each bowl.
- e.g. Coconut water takes the shape of the inside of the coconut.
- **Everyday view:** A liquid flows and takes the shape of its container.
- **Physicists:** Find in-between things. Honey, ketchup and a mix of cornflour and water flow in odd ways. The cornflour mix is hard when hit and runny when poured slowly.
### Gases spread out to fill every corner · *defined*
**Definition:** A gas spreads out to fill all the space it can get to. It has no shape of its own, and it does not settle at the bottom.

In the Three Bags activity, the water lay at the bottom of its bag. The air filled the whole bag, right up to the top corners. When you squeezed one end, the air pushed into the other end. Most gases cannot be seen, so we notice them by what they do. Cooking gas is kept in a metal cylinder. It is squeezed so hard there that most of it is a liquid. When it comes out of the pipe, it turns back into a gas.
- e.g. Air fills a balloon to its round shape.
- e.g. Air filled the tied bag up to every corner.
- e.g. Bubbles in a glass of soda are a gas.
- **Everyday view:** A gas spreads to fill every space.
- **Physicists and mountaineers:** Know that Earth's gravity holds air near the ground. The air gets thinner as you climb, and climbers carry oxygen on Everest because the gas does not fill space evenly.
### Ice, water and steam are the same water · *defined*
**Definition:** Ice is water as a solid. Liquid water is water as a liquid. Steam is water as a gas. Cooling and warming change water from one state to another.

Water freezes into ice when it gets very cold. Ice melts back into water when it gets warm. Water boils when it gets very hot, and turns into steam. Real steam is invisible. Look just above a kettle spout: the small clear gap is the steam. A little higher, the steam cools and turns back into tiny drops of liquid water. Those drops are the white cloud we see. It is the same water all the way through.
- e.g. Ice cubes melt into water in a glass of nannari sherbet.
- e.g. Water in an idli pot boils, and steam cooks the idlis.
- e.g. Snow on the Himalayas melts and flows down into the Ganga.
- **Aristotle:** Taught that water and air are two different elements, so water that turns into vapour changes into air.
- **Chemists:** Show that ice, water and steam are all the same molecule, H2O, in three arrangements. They also add other states, such as plasma, the glowing gas of the Sun and of lightning.
### Why are they so different? Tiny particles · *defined*
**Definition:** Everything is made of tiny particles, far too small to see. How tightly they hold on to each other decides whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas.

In a solid, the particles hold on tight and stay in their places. That is why a solid keeps its shape. In a liquid, the particles stay close, but they slide past each other. That is why a liquid flows. In a gas, the particles fly apart and move around freely. That is why a gas spreads out. When ice melts, the water particles loosen their hold. When water boils, they fly apart.
- e.g. Ice: water particles holding on tight.
- e.g. Water: the same particles sliding past each other.
- e.g. Steam: the same particles flying apart.
- **Democritus:** Said that things differ because their atoms differ in shape and in how they link.
- **The Vaisheshika school:** Taught that earth atoms make solids, water atoms make liquids, and so on. Modern science keeps the idea of atoms, and says the difference comes from how tightly the same particles hold each other.

## Mostly agreed
- A solid keeps its own shape, a liquid takes the shape of its container, and a gas spreads out to fill its container. *How we know:* In the Three Bags activity, the stone stayed a stone shape, the water lay at the bottom of its bag, and the air filled the whole bag.
- Pouring a liquid into a new shape does not change how much of it there is. *How we know:* In the experiment One Tumbler, Three Shapes, the water from the thin bottle and the wide bowl filled the tumbler back to the same bindi line.
- Ice, liquid water and steam are the same substance in three states. *How we know:* An ice cube on a plate melts into water. Water boiled in a pan disappears as steam, and that steam turns back into drops of water on a cold lid.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, and the followers of Aristotle who argued with him, 1611 (the argument) and 1612 (his book).** In 1611, in Florence, Italy, some learned men had an argument with the scientist Galileo Galilei. They asked a simple question: why does ice float? The men followed the old teachings of Aristotle, a thinker from ancient Greece. They said ice is water that the cold has squeezed and made thicker and heavier. It floats only because it is flat and wide, they said, like a thin chip of wood. Galileo did not agree. He said ice floats whatever its shape. If you push a lump of ice to the bottom of a bowl and let go, it rises back up. So ice must weigh less than the water that would fill the same space. In 1612, he wrote a book about things that float in water. Today we know Galileo was right. When water freezes, it spreads out and takes up about one-tenth more space. That is why a full bottle of water can split in the freezer. You can check Galileo's test with an ice cube and a tumbler.  
*What people believed before:* That ice is water made thicker and heavier by cold, and floats only because of its flat shape. Galileo showed that ice floats in any shape, and rises when pushed down, so it is lighter than water.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Water in a tall, thin glass is more than the same water in a wide bowl, because it stands higher.** (A common guess by young children. Alina Szeminska and Jean Piaget studied it in Switzerland and wrote about it in 1941.; *rejected*)  
  For: The water really does stand higher in the thin glass, and we often judge amounts by height.  
  Against: The thin glass is narrow, so the same water must stand higher in it. Pour the water back into the tumbler it came from, and it reaches the same line as before.
- **The white cloud above a kettle or a cooker is steam.** (What most people say; *rejected*)  
  For: The cloud comes from the boiling water, and everyone calls it steam.  
  Against: Real steam is a gas, and it is invisible. Look closely, and there is a clear gap just above the spout. The white cloud is tiny drops of liquid water, made as the steam cools in the air.
- **Ice is heavier than water, because it is hard.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Ice feels hard and solid, and many hard things, like stones, sink.  
  Against: Ice floats in a tumbler of water. When water freezes, it takes up a little more space, about one-tenth more. So an ice cube weighs a little less than the water that would fill the same space.

## Roots
We have poured water into new shapes, squeezed bags of a stone, water and air, and looked for steam. Now we ask what that tells us about water, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the pouring test, the water looked like more in the thin bottle and less in the wide bowl. Poured back into the tumbler, it reached the same bindi line each time. Water has no shape of its own, so it takes the shape of whatever holds it. How much water there is stays the same.  
*Habit it breaks:* We judge how much there is by how big or tall it looks. The pouring test showed that the height of the water came from the container, while the amount never changed.  
*Evidence:* In the pouring test, the water stood tall in the thin bottle and low in the wide bowl. Each time, poured back into the tumbler, it came to the bindi line.

- *who am i:* More than half of your body is water. It is held inside your skin and inside the tiny cells that make you.
- *what is life:* A plant stands up firm when the tiny cells in its stems and leaves are full of water. Without water, the plant droops.
- *what is this world:* The same water is ice on the Himalayas, liquid in the Kaveri river, and an invisible gas in the air of a monsoon day in Chennai.

Ice, liquid water and steam look completely different. Yet they are the same water, and nothing is added or taken away when one turns into another. Water particles only hold on to each other more tightly or more loosely. Everything is conscious, down to these tiny water particles.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** “यथा सोम्यैकेन मृत्पिण्डेन सर्वं मृन्मयं विज्ञातं स्यात् / वाचारम्भणं विकारो नामधेयं मृत्तिकेत्येव सत्यम्” Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu: when you know one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay. The things made from it have different names and shapes. What they really are is clay. (In the pouring test, the water in the thin bottle and in the wide bowl had different shapes. What it really was, and how much of it there was, stayed the same.)
- **Tao Te Ching 8:** “上善若水。 / 水善利萬物而不爭，處眾人之所惡，故幾於道。” The highest good is like water. Water helps all things and does not fight them. It flows to the low places. (In the three bags, the water always ran to the lowest part of its bag and fitted that shape. The Tao Te Ching, from China, praises water for fitting itself to whatever is around it.)
- Science (settled): On Earth, water is found naturally as a solid, a liquid and a gas: as ice and snow, as rivers and seas, and as invisible water vapour in the air.
- Science (open): Scientists still argue about exactly how the particles of liquid water are arranged, because they join and let go of each other very fast.
- Other view, Many physicists: Many scientists who study matter say a drop of water has no awareness at all. They explain everything water does with particles and forces, and they find no need for a mind to explain it.

## Story: Who Got More Neer Mor?
*Neer mor is thin, salty buttermilk with curry leaves and ginger, drunk in Tamil Nadu in the hot months. Kavya, Arjun and Paati are made up.*

It was May in Chennai, and the afternoon was very hot. Kavya was eight. Her cousin Arjun was six, and he had come to stay for the holidays.

Paati, their grandmother, was making neer mor in the kitchen. She stirred curd and water together in a big steel vessel. She added salt, a little ginger and a few curry leaves. It smelled cool and green.

"Bring me two cups," said Paati.

Kavya brought a tall, thin glass bottle. Arjun brought a wide steel davara, the low bowl that goes under a coffee tumbler.

Paati filled one small steel tumbler with neer mor, right to the brim. She poured it carefully through a small funnel into Kavya's thin bottle. Then she filled the same tumbler to the brim again, and poured it into Arjun's davara.

Arjun looked at the two cups. Then he looked again. His face went red.

"Kavya got more!" he said. "Look, hers goes up so high. Mine is only at the bottom."

It was true that Kavya's neer mor stood tall in the thin bottle. Arjun's lay low and flat in the wide davara.

"Paati poured the same tumbler both times," said Kavya. "I watched."

"But it looks like more," said Arjun.

Kavya thought for a moment. Then she took two small steel tumblers, exactly like the one Paati had used.

"Watch," she said. She poured her neer mor from the thin bottle into the first tumbler. It came right up to the brim. Then she poured Arjun's neer mor from the davara into the second tumbler. It came right up to the brim too.

Arjun bent down until his nose nearly touched the tumblers. The two tops were level.

"They are the same," he said slowly. "But in the bottle it was tall."

"The bottle is thin," said Kavya. "So the neer mor has to stand tall to fit inside. The davara is wide, so it spreads out low. Neer mor has no shape of its own. It takes the shape of whatever holds it. The amount stays the same."

Paati smiled and said nothing. She only handed each of them their own tumbler back.

Arjun drank half of his. Then he had an idea. "I want ice in mine," he said.

Paati took the ice tray out of the freezer and twisted it. Out popped a row of square ice cubes.

Arjun had poured his neer mor back into the thin bottle, because he liked how tall it looked. He tried to push an ice cube into the mouth of the bottle. It would not go in. He turned it this way and that way. It was too big.

"The neer mor went in easily," he said. "Why won't the ice?"

"The neer mor can change its shape to fit," said Kavya. "The ice cube is a solid. It keeps its own shape. It is still a square, and the square is too big."

"Put it in the davara," said Paati. "Wait a little."

Arjun put the ice cube in the empty davara. He sat on the kitchen step and watched it. The corners went round first. A little pool of water grew around it. The cube got smaller and smaller.

After about twenty minutes, there was no cube at all. There was only cold water in the bottom of the davara, lying flat and low.

Arjun picked up the davara and poured the water into his thin bottle. It went in easily, every drop.

"The same ice went in," he said, "once it had turned into water!"

"When ice melts, it becomes a liquid," said Kavya. "Then it can take any shape."

Just then, the idli pot on the stove began to hiss. Paati lifted the lid, and a white cloud rose up.

"Is that the water too?" asked Arjun.

"Yes," said Paati. "The water in the pot boiled and turned into steam. Look just above the pot, where the cloud begins. Do you see a clear gap? That is the real steam. It is a gas, so you cannot see it. Higher up, it cools and turns back into tiny drops of water. The drops make the white cloud."

Arjun looked at the ice tray, the cold water in his bottle and the white cloud over the stove.

"Ice, water and steam," he said. "All the same water."

"All the same water," said Paati, and she gave him the first hot idli.

**For the parent:** Neer mor and water have no shape of their own. They take the shape of whatever holds them, and the amount stays the same. Ice keeps its own shape until it melts. Ice, water and steam are the same water.

- Ask: Why did Arjun think Kavya had more neer mor?
- Ask: How did Kavya show that they both had the same amount?
- Ask: Why would the ice cube not go into the bottle, when the water from the melted cube did?

## Experiments
### e1 · One Tumbler, Three Shapes: does the amount of water change? (25 min)
- **question:** When we pour water into a container of a different shape, is there more water, less water, or the same amount?
- **predict:** Look at the thin bottle and the wide bowl. When the water goes into the thin bottle, will there be more, less or the same? What about the wide bowl? And when we pour it back, will it reach the bindi line? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same water every time, and fill the same tumbler to the same bindi line. We pour slowly over a tray, so no water is lost. We always look at the tumbler at eye level.
- **record:** Write your guesses at the top. Fill in the table. Take a photo of the water in the tumbler, the thin bottle and the wide bowl, or draw the heights you saw.
- **results table:** Container | My guess: more, less or the same? | How did it look in the container? | Poured back: at the bindi, above it, or below it?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the tray on a table. 2. Fill the tumbler about halfway with water. 3. Your child sticks the bindi on the outside, exactly at the top of the water. 4. Show them the thin bottle and the wide bowl.  
  *Parent says:* This bindi marks how much water we have. We will pour it into the thin bottle, and then into the wide bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Stick the bindi at the top of the water. Look at the bottle and the bowl.
  - Ask: Will the thin bottle have more, less or the same water? Why? (good: More, because it will go up high.; The same, because it is the same water.; Less, because the bottle is small.)
  - Note: Don't say if a guess is right. Many children of this age say the thin bottle will have more, because the water will stand taller. Any guess with a reason is good. Write the guesses down.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the funnel in the mouth of the thin bottle. 2. Your child pours all the water from the tumbler into it, slowly. 3. Hold the bottle next to the empty tumbler. 4. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly, so we don't lose any water. Then hold the bottle next to the empty tumbler and look.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the water into the bottle, slowly. Look at how high it stands.
  - Ask: How does the water look in the thin bottle? (good: It goes up much higher.; It looks like more water.)
  - Note: In a thin bottle the water stands much higher than it did in the tumbler. Let your child say what they see. Don't explain yet.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child pours the water from the bottle back into the tumbler. 2. They bend down so their eye is level with the bindi. 3. Write the result in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now pour it back into the tumbler. Bend down so your eye is level with the bindi.  
  *Child mode:* Pour it back into the tumbler. Bend down and look at the bindi.
  - Ask: Is the water above the bindi, below it, or at it? (good: Right at the bindi.; A tiny bit below, because some drops stayed in the bottle.)
  - Note: The water comes back to the bindi, or a hair below it, because a few drops cling to the inside of the bottle. Point that out if the water is slightly low.
  - Check: The water went into the thin bottle and came back to the bindi. What does that show? → **It was the same amount of water all along**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child pours the water from the tumbler into the wide bowl. 2. Ask how it looks. 3. Then they pour it back into the tumbler, over the tray. 4. Check the bindi again and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Now pour the water into the wide bowl. Then pour it back into the tumbler, over the tray.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the water into the bowl and look at it. Then pour it back and look at the bindi.
  - Ask: What happened with the wide bowl? (good: In the bowl it looked like only a little.; Back in the tumbler, it came to the bindi again.)
  - Note: Pouring from a wide bowl can spill. Tip it slowly over the tray, or use the funnel. If a little spills, count it as a drip, not as water that vanished.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child an ice cube and a steel spoon. 2. They put each one in the tumbler, then in the bowl, then try to push it into the mouth of the bottle. 3. Ask the questions below. 4. Listen to their answer before you explain.  
  *Parent says:* The water changed its shape for every container. Now try an ice cube and a steel spoon. Does their shape change?  
  *Child mode:* Put the ice cube in the tumbler, then in the bowl. Try to push it into the bottle. Try the spoon too.
  - Ask: Why did the water fit in the thin bottle but the ice cube did not? (good: Water changes its shape to fit.; The ice cube keeps its own shape, and it is too big.)
  - Note: After your child answers, say it plainly. Water is a liquid. It has no shape of its own, so it takes the shape of whatever holds it, and the amount stays the same. The ice cube and the spoon are solids. They keep their own shape. If your ice cube is small enough to slip into the bottle, look closely: it is still a cube inside.
  - Think: If the ice cube melts, will its water fit in the bottle?
### e2 · The Ice Glove: what shape will frozen water be? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** If we freeze water in a glove, what shape will the ice be? And what happens to that shape as the ice melts?
- **predict:** What shape will the ice be when we peel off the glove? When it melts on the plate, what shape will the water be? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We put the ice from the glove and one ordinary ice cube from a tray side by side on the same plate, in the same room. We look at both every 10 minutes, so we compare them the same way.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the glove off the ice with scissors. Hold frozen ice with a cloth, because very cold ice can stick to wet fingers.
- **record:** Take a photo of the ice hand when you first take it out, after 10 minutes and after 20 minutes. Take one of the water on the plate at the end. Or draw them.
- **results table:** Time | What the ice hand looks like | What the ice cube looks like | What the water on the plate looks like
- **schedule:** day 1: Fill the glove with water, close it, and lay it flat in the freezer overnight. (5 min); day 2: Peel or cut off the glove. Put the ice hand and an ice cube on a plate. Look every 10 minutes and fill in the table. (20 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the glove open under the tap. 2. Your child fills it with water until the fingers are fat. 3. Squeeze out any big air bubble. 4. Close it tightly with the rubber band. 5. Lay it flat on a plate in the freezer.  
  *Parent says:* The water is a liquid, so it takes the shape of the glove. Tonight it goes in the freezer.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the glove with water. Close it tight. Then put it in the freezer.
  - Ask: What shape will the ice be after a night in the freezer? Why? (good: A hand, because the water filled the glove.; A lump, because ice is a cube.)
  - Note: Lay the glove flat with the fingers spread, so they freeze apart. A home freezer freezes it overnight. A small single-door fridge freezer may need a whole day.
  - Wait: Leave it in the freezer overnight.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the glove out of the freezer. 2. Cut the glove open with scissors and peel it off. 3. Put the ice hand on the big plate. 4. Put an ordinary ice cube next to it.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the ice. The glove is gone.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the ice. Count its fingers.
  - Ask: Why is it still a hand when the glove is gone? (good: Ice is a solid, so it keeps its shape.; It froze in the glove shape and stays that way.)
  - Note: The ice keeps the shape of the glove, with every finger. A solid keeps its own shape, even after the container is gone.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start the timer. 2. Every 10 minutes, your child looks at the ice hand, the ice cube and the water on the plate. 3. They say what has changed, or take a photo. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* What is happening to the fingers? Where is the water going?  
  *Child mode:* Look every 10 minutes. Compare the ice hand and the ice cube.
  - Ask: What shape is the water on the plate? (good: Flat, spread over the plate.; It took the shape of the plate.)
  - Note: The fingertips round off first. Water spreads flat across the plate, up to the rim. In a warm room the thin fingers may melt off in 20 to 40 minutes. The thick palm takes much longer.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the questions below. 2. Listen before you explain.  
  *Parent says:* While it was ice, it kept the shape of a hand. Now it is water, spread flat on the plate.  
  *Child mode:* As ice, it was a hand. As water, it spreads flat.
  - Ask: Where did the hand shape go? (good: It went away, because water takes the shape of the plate.; The water has no shape of its own.)
  - Note: The hand shape came from the glove. As ice, the water kept it. As soon as it melts, the water has no shape of its own, so it takes the shape of the plate.
  - Check: Why does the melted water spread flat over the plate? → **Because a liquid takes the shape of whatever holds it**

  *Child mode:* Put a stone in one bag. Pour water into another bag. Blow air into the third bag. Guess what each will do.
  - Note: Use thick bags if you have them. Hold the water bag closed at the top and tie it well. Do it over a tray in case it leaks.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child turns each bag upside down, slowly. 2. Then they squeeze one end of each bag gently. 3. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Turn each bag upside down, slowly. Then squeeze one end of each bag gently.  
  *Child mode:* Turn each bag over. Squeeze one end gently. Watch the stone, the water and the air.
  - Ask: What did the stone, the water and the air do? (good: The stone stayed a stone and fell to the bottom.; The water ran to the new bottom of the bag.; The air stayed everywhere in the bag.)
  - Note: The stone keeps its shape and drops to the lowest point. The water flows to the lowest part and takes the shape of that end of the bag, with a flat top. The air fills the whole bag all the time. Squeeze one end, and the air pushes into the other end.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the three bags side by side. 2. Ask the question below. 3. Then say the three words: solid, liquid, gas.  
  *Parent says:* Look at what each bag did. Which bag has the solid? The liquid? The gas?  
  *Child mode:* Point to the solid, then the liquid, then the gas.
  - Ask: How can you tell which bag holds the liquid? (good: The water ran to the new bottom of the bag.; It flowed and took the shape of the bottom.)
  - Note: A solid keeps its own shape. A liquid takes the shape of the bottom of its container. A gas spreads out to fill all of it.
  - Check: Which bag stayed puffed full, even upside down? → **The air bag**
### a2 · Where Is the Steam? Look closely at a boiling kettle (8 min)
- **problem:** When water boils, it turns into steam, which is water as a gas. Most people say steam is white. In this activity you look closely at a boiling kettle to find out where the steam really is.
- **safety:** Steam burns badly, even though you cannot see it. A grown-up works the stove. The child stands at least one big step away and never puts a hand, a spoon or a face near the spout.
- **record:** Take a photo of the kettle from a safe distance, or draw it. Point to the clear gap and the white cloud, and say which one is the real steam.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Boil water in the kettle. 2. Hang or hold something dark behind it. 3. Your child stands at least one big step away. 4. Ask them to guess what they will see.  
  *Parent says:* When the water boils, it turns into steam. What colour do you think steam is? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Stand one big step back and watch the kettle. Guess what colour steam is.
  - Note: Most children say steam is white. Let them guess. Keep them well back from the spout at all times.
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When the kettle is steaming, point to the spout. 2. Ask your child to look at the tiny space just above it, before the white cloud starts.  
  *Parent says:* Look just above the spout, before the white cloud begins.  
  *Child mode:* Look just above the spout, then at the cloud. Is there a gap between them?
  - Ask: What do you see in the clear gap just above the spout? (good: Nothing I can see.; It is clear, like air.)
  - Note: There is a small clear gap, usually about one or two finger-widths, between the spout and the white cloud. Real steam is in that gap. It is a gas, and it is invisible.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn off the stove. 2. Ask the question below. 3. Listen, then explain.  
  *Parent says:* The gap was clear, but the cloud is white.  
  *Child mode:* The gap is clear. The cloud is white.
  - Ask: What is the white cloud? (good: Water drops.; Steam that cooled down and turned back into water.)
  - Note: Real steam is water as a gas, and you cannot see it. As it rises, it cools in the room air and turns back into tiny drops of liquid water. Those drops make the white cloud. Higher up, the cloud fades as the drops spread into the air.
  - Check: Where is the real steam? → **In the clear gap just above the spout**
### a3 · The Home Hunt: solids, liquids and gases (10 min)
- **record:** The sheet with three columns, with at least 4 solids, 4 liquids and 2 gases written, drawn or photographed.
- **s1 (6 min)**

  *Child mode:* Find 4 solids and 4 liquids. Then find 2 gases. Say each one, and a grown-up writes it in its column.
  - Ask: What gas did you find? (good: The air in my football.; The bubbles in soda.; My breath.)
  - Note: Gases are hard to find, because most are invisible. Hints: the air in a ball or a tyre, the bubbles in soda, the steam from cooking, the air you breathe out. Never open the cooking gas to look.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the sheet together. 2. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Which column was easiest to fill? Which was hardest?  
  *Child mode:* Count the things in each column. Which has the most? Which has the fewest?
  - Ask: Why were gases hard to find? (good: You can't see most of them.; They spread out, so they are everywhere.)
  - Note: Most gases are invisible, so we find them by what they do: air puffs up a ball, and soda bubbles rise.

### Keep, Flow or Spread? (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each word to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Solid → keeps its own shape; Liquid → flows and takes the shape of its container; Gas → spreads out to fill all the space; Steam → water as a gas. *Why:* These are the main words in this lesson. Each state does something different with its shape.
2. (mcq) In the pouring test, the water went into the thin bottle and then back into the tumbler. Where did it come to?  
   Options: Far above the bindi / Right at the bindi, or a hair below / Far below the bindi  
   **Answer:** Right at the bindi, or a hair below. *Why:* It was the same water all along, so it came back to the same line. A few drops may stay in the bottle. If you picked 'far above', you may be thinking that the bottle made more water.
3. (sort) Sort these into solid, liquid and gas.  
   **Answer:** a steel spoon → Solid; milk → Liquid; the air in a football → Gas; an ice cube → Solid; rasam → Liquid; the bubbles in soda → Gas. *Why:* The spoon and ice keep their own shape. Milk and rasam flow and take the shape of their bowl. Air and soda bubbles are gases.
4. (draw) Draw the same water in the tumbler, in the thin bottle and in the wide bowl. Show how high it stood in each one.  
   **Answer:** The water at the bindi in the tumbler, tall in the thin bottle, and low and flat in the wide bowl.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows three different heights, and a label such as 'same water' on all three.
5. (truefalse) Water poured into a tall, thin bottle becomes more water, because it stands higher.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The bottle is thin, so the same water has to stand taller. Poured back, it reaches the same line.

   **Answer:** The ice cube is a solid and keeps its own shape, so it was too big. Water is a liquid. It changes its shape to fit.. *Why:* A strong answer says the ice keeps its shape and the water changes its shape.

9. (perspective) The first child and the second child are taking water on a long, hot train ride from Chennai to Madurai.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one with a reason, such as 'ice stays cold for longer'. 10. (odd) Three of these keep their own shape. Which one does not?  
   Options: An ice cube / A steel tumbler / Buttermilk / A wooden spoon

11. (mcq) When you blow up a balloon, where does the air go?  
   Options: Only to the bottom of the balloon / It fills the whole balloon / It stays near the mouth

## Applications
- Moulds for kulfi, ice cubes and candles work because a liquid takes the shape of the mould, then sets into a solid that keeps that shape.
- Pipes, taps and drains carry water, because a liquid flows.
- Balloons, footballs and cycle tyres are filled with air, because a gas spreads out to fill them.
- Idli pots and pressure cookers cook food with steam, which is water as a gas.
- Cooking gas is squeezed into a cylinder until it turns into a liquid, so a lot fits in. When it comes out of the pipe, it turns back into a gas.

## Open questions
- **How many states of matter are there?** Solid, liquid and gas are the three we see most. Lightning and the Sun are made of plasma, a fourth state. Some very cold states were predicted from the work of Satyendra Nath Bose of Kolkata, and scientists first made one in 1995. Scientists keep finding new ones, so nobody can say how many there are.
- **What does liquid water look like on the inside?** In ice, the water particles are lined up neatly. In liquid water, they keep joining and letting go very fast. Scientists still argue about exactly how they are arranged.

## Connected maths
- In the pouring test, you fill the tumbler to the bindi with 8 spoons of water. You pour it into a thin bottle, then back into the tumbler. Then you pour it into a wide bowl, then back. How many spoons of water are in the tumbler at the end? What if 1 spoon of water stayed stuck to the walls of the bottle, and 1 more to the walls of the bowl? **8 spoons if none were lost. If 1 spoon stayed behind each time, 6 spoons were left.**. Pouring does not change the amount, so it stays 8. If 1 spoon is left on the walls of the bottle and 1 on the walls of the bowl, 8 take away 2 is 6. That is why we pour slowly and count the drips. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- In the Home Hunt, suppose you found 5 solids, 4 liquids and 2 gases. Draw three columns: solid, liquid and gas. Draw one block for each thing you found. Which column is tallest? Which is shortest? Why do you think gases were the hardest to find? **Solids are tallest with 5 blocks. Gases are shortest with 2 blocks. Gases are hard to find because most of them are invisible.**. 5 is more than 4, and 4 is more than 2. The short gas column tells us something about gases: we cannot see most of them, so we miss them. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- One ice tray has 2 rows of 7 little boxes. You fill 2 trays. How many ice cubes do you get? If each cube is 2 spoons of water, how many spoons of water did you pour? **28 ice cubes, and 56 spoons of water.**. 2 rows of 7 make 14 cubes in one tray, and 2 trays make 28. Each cube is 2 spoons, so 28 cubes are 28 + 28 = 56 spoons. When they melt, they make 56 spoons of water again. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- You have a tall, thin glass and a short, wide tumbler. You fill the thin glass with water and pour it all into the wide tumbler. The wide tumbler is not full. Which holds more: the thin glass or the wide tumbler? How do you know? **The wide tumbler holds more.**. All the water from the full thin glass fitted into the wide tumbler, with room left over. So the wide tumbler can hold more, even though it is shorter. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L1`)
