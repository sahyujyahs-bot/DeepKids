# Why a camel has a hump, a woodpecker a hard head and an octopus no bones
`sci.animals.animal-bodies.L4`

**Summary.** A camel can cross the Thar for days with almost nothing to drink, yet the hump on her back holds no water at all. It holds fat. Your child melts ice under cloth to find out why the fat sits in one lump and not all over her body. Then they test why a camel's wide foot does not sink, and meet a woodpecker whose head takes thousands of hard knocks a day, and an octopus with three hearts and no bones. Every odd body part turns out to be an answer to a problem, and every answer has a price.

**Intro.** *A camel can walk for days across the Thar desert without a drink. On her back she carries a big hump. Most people say the hump is full of water. Is that true?* Before you read on, guess what is inside the hump. Is it water, fat, muscle or air? Say your guess and the reason for it. Now think of two more animals with a part that looks odd. A woodpecker has a pointed beak and bangs it on wood all day. An octopus has soft arms and no bones. Each odd part is a clue to a problem that animal has to solve to stay alive. In this unit you find the problems, test two of them with ice and dough, and discover that every answer has a price.

## Terms
- **Adaptation**: A feature of a body that helps an animal live in the place and the way she lives. It builds up over many generations, not in one life. *e.g.* A camel's wide foot pads are an adaptation to walking on loose sand.
- **Trade-off**: A deal where a body part gives a gain and also costs something. A feature that is good in one life is often poor in another. *e.g.* A camel's wide foot floats on sand, but it slips on wet clay.
- **Fat store**: Fat the body keeps as a supply of energy for later. Fat holds about twice as much energy as the same weight of sugar or starch. *e.g.* A camel's hump is a store of fat.
- **Insulation**: Anything that slows heat as it travels in or out. Cloth, fur, feathers and fat are all insulation. *e.g.* A woollen shawl slows the heat leaving your body.
- **Pressure**: How hard a weight pushes on each small patch of the ground it stands on. The same weight on a small patch gives a big pressure, and on a big patch gives a small one. *e.g.* A girl standing on one toe presses the floor harder than when she stands on her whole foot.
- **Pad**: A thick, springy cushion under the foot. A camel's pad is broad, and it spreads out when she puts her weight on it. *e.g.* The leathery pad under a camel's toes.
- **Zygodactyl feet**: Feet with two toes pointing forward and two pointing back. This grip is good for clinging to a trunk. *e.g.* Most woodpeckers have zygodactyl feet.
- **Hyoid**: The small bone system that holds up the tongue. In many woodpeckers it is long and curls around the back of the skull. *e.g.* A woodpecker's tongue is rooted at the back of her head and curls around her skull.
- **Mantle**: The muscular bag of an octopus's body that holds her organs and gills. It fills with water and squeezes it out. *e.g.* An octopus breathes by moving water through her mantle.
- **Jet propulsion**: Moving forward by pushing a stream of fluid backwards. The fluid pushes back on the body with an equal force. *e.g.* An octopus squirts water out of a funnel and shoots away.
- **Haemocyanin**: A copper-based chemical that carries oxygen in the blood of an octopus. It makes the blood blue. *e.g.* Octopus blood is blue because of haemocyanin.
- **Chromatophore**: A tiny stretchy sac of colour in the skin of an octopus. Muscles open it wide or squeeze it small, so the colour of the skin can change in a moment. *e.g.* An octopus turns brown, then white, as her chromatophores open and close.
- **Variation**: The small differences between animals of the same kind. No two are exactly alike. *e.g.* Some camel calves have slightly wider feet than others.
- **Natural selection**: When animals with a helpful difference survive and have more young than others, so that the difference becomes common over many generations. *e.g.* Calves with broader pads walked farther on sand, lived longer and left more young.
- **Convergent evolution**: When two kinds of animals that are not close relatives end up with a similar body part because they faced a similar problem. *e.g.* Octopuses and humans both have eyes with a lens, but their eyes grew separately.
- **Camouflage**: Colour or shape that makes an animal hard to see against her background. *e.g.* An octopus on a reef turns the colour and pattern of the rock beside her.

## Explanations
### Every odd body part answers a problem · *defined*
**Definition:** An adaptation is a feature of a body that helps an animal survive and have young in the place and the way she lives.

An animal has to solve the same few problems every day. She must get food, get water, get oxygen, move around, stay at the right temperature and stay safe. Each place sets these problems in a different way. A dune is hot and soft and short of water. A tree trunk is straight and hard, and food hides inside it. A reef has many cracks and many hungry fish. So the body parts of animals in these places are different. The way to understand an odd part is to ask three questions. What problem does it solve? How does it solve it? What does it cost? We use these three questions all through this unit. Humans are animals too. Our feet, hands and backbone are answers as well, and they also have prices.
- e.g. A camel's hump keeps fat for lean times.
- e.g. A woodpecker's stiff tail props her against a trunk.
- e.g. An octopus's soft body squeezes into a crack to hide.
### The camel's hump holds fat, not water · *defined*
**Definition:** A camel's hump is a lump of fat under the skin. The camel uses the fat as a store of energy when food is hard to find.

The hump of a big dromedary camel can hold up to about 30 kilograms of fat. When the camel finds little to eat, her body slowly uses the fat, and the hump shrinks and flops to one side. When she eats well again, the hump fills out. Fat is a good store because it holds about twice as much energy as the same weight of sugar. Why is it all in one lump? Fat is also a blanket, and a thick blanket all over would trap the heat inside her body. By keeping the fat on her back, the camel leaves the rest of her skin thin, so the heat can leave. The lump on top may also shade her back from the Sun. Burning fat does make a little water, but it also needs more breathing, and breathing loses water. So the hump is a food store, not a water tank.
- e.g. The Ice Test in this unit: the same cloth keeps ice cold when it is spread all over, but not when it is in one lump.
- e.g. A thin, hungry camel has a small hump that flops. A well-fed camel has a firm, tall hump.
- e.g. A Bactrian camel, from the cold deserts of Central Asia, has two humps and a thick winter coat.
### How a camel saves water · *defined*
**Definition:** Water saving is any way a body loses less water or takes in more, so that the animal can go longer between drinks.

A camel is not good at finding water. She is good at keeping it. First, her body temperature goes up by day and down by night. In one study, a camel with no water let her temperature rise from about 34 to about 41 degrees Celsius. A body that is allowed to get hot does not need to sweat as much to stay cool. Second, the inside of her nose is damp and cool. As she breathes out, it catches water from the air, and she breathes it back in. Third, her dung is dry and her wee is thick. Fourth, her red blood cells are oval, not round, and they can swell a great deal without bursting when she drinks. A very thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres or more in a few minutes. She can lose about a quarter of her body weight in water and live. A person is in danger long before that.
- e.g. A dehydrated camel's temperature can swing by 6 or 7 degrees in a day.
- e.g. A camel's long double eyelashes and closing nostrils keep sand out.
- e.g. A person drinks about 2 litres a day, and a very thirsty camel can drink about 50 times as much at one go.
### A wide foot floats on soft ground · *defined*
**Definition:** Pressure is the push of a weight on each small patch of the surface it stands on. Pressure equals the weight divided by the area.

A big camel can weigh 600 kilograms, and a sharp hoof would sink in sand like a nail. A camel's foot is a broad, soft pad that spreads out as she puts her weight down. The same weight is shared over a bigger patch, so each small patch is pushed less, and the sand holds. This is why a person in snowshoes walks on snow, and why a tractor tyre with some air let out grips a muddy field. In the Foot Test, doubling the width of the foot makes the area 4 times as big, so the pressure becomes 4 times smaller. It is a fair trade only on soft ground. On wet clay, the broad pad slides, and a camel can slip and fall. The Kharai camels of Kutch wade and swim out to islands to eat mangrove leaves. Their wide feet help them in soft mud.
- e.g. Camel feet, snowshoes, a duck's webbed feet and a wide tractor tyre all spread weight.
- e.g. A high-heeled shoe presses the floor much harder than a flat sandal on the same person.
- e.g. In the Foot Test, a tumbler on a bottle cap sinks into dough, and the same tumbler on a plate hardly does.
### How a woodpecker climbs, eats and hits wood · *defined*
**Definition:** A woodpecker is a bird whose body is built for climbing trunks and getting food from wood.

A woodpecker has four problems. First, she has to cling to a straight trunk. Most woodpeckers have two toes forward and two back, with sharp claws. Their tail feathers are stiff, and the woodpecker leans on them as a third foot. Second, she has to open the wood. Her beak is like a chisel, and her neck muscles are strong. She can hit up to about 20 times a second, in short bursts. Third, she has to reach the insects deep in the wood. Her tongue is long and sticky, with a barbed tip. In some woodpeckers it reaches as much as 10 centimetres past the beak. The tongue is anchored at the back of her head, and it curls around her skull. Fourth, she has to do this without hurting her brain.
- e.g. The Black-rumped Flameback, a woodpecker you can see in city parks in India, taps on tree trunks.
- e.g. A woodpecker's tongue is rooted far back and curls around the skull, so it can reach very far.
- e.g. Her stiff tail props her up like the third leg of a stool.
### How does a woodpecker's head survive the hits? · *debated*
A woodpecker's head stops very suddenly each time it hits the wood. A hit like this would badly hurt a person's brain. For years, books said that woodpeckers have a built-in shock absorber, like a helmet. Scientists are no longer sure. The ideas below are all being studied, and they may be partly true together.
- e.g. Her skull and beak are strong, and her brain is small and sits snugly in the skull.
- e.g. She hits in a straight line, so her head does not twist, and twisting is what harms brains most.
- e.g. Some researchers think she braces her neck muscles at the moment of the hit, and closes a thin eyelid to protect her eye.
- **The shock-absorber idea (older books, and a study of 2011):** Soft, spongy bone and the long tongue bone around the skull take the knock, and spread it over the head, so the brain is spared.
- **The hard-hammer idea (a study of 2022):** High-speed videos showed that the head works like a stiff hammer. It sends almost all of its energy into the wood, and takes little of it into the skull. The cushion matters less than we thought.
- **The small-brain idea:** A tiny brain has very little weight, so the force on it in each hit is small. A larger animal with the same hits would be hurt.
### How an octopus moves, breathes and squeezes · *defined*
**Definition:** An octopus is a soft-bodied sea animal with eight arms, three hearts and no bones. Her only big hard part is her beak.

An octopus has no skeleton, so she can pour herself through any gap that is wider than her beak. She uses this to hide in cracks and to open shells and jars. She has two ways to move. Most of the time she crawls over the sea floor on her arms, which is slow and cheap. When she is in danger, she fills her mantle with water and squeezes it out through a funnel. The water jets backwards, and she shoots away. The same mantle space holds her gills and she breathes through it. Her blood is blue, because it carries oxygen with copper instead of iron. Two of her three hearts push blood through the gills, and the third pushes it round the body. Her arms are lined with suckers that can taste what they touch.
- e.g. A common octopus can squeeze through a hole not much bigger than her beak.
- e.g. In the Squeeze Test in this unit, a soft dough ball passes through a small hole, and a hard ball of the same size cannot.
- e.g. An octopus drilling a shell, and then eating the soft animal inside.
### Every part has a price · *defined*
**Definition:** A trade-off is a deal in which getting one gain means paying a cost somewhere else.

A camel's wide pad floats on sand, but she slips on wet clay. Her hump is a store of food, but it is heavy to carry, and it flops when food is short. An octopus has no bones, so she can squeeze into a crack, but swimming tires her quickly. While she swims by jet, the heart that pumps blood to her body slows or stops, so she must rest. Her blue blood carries less oxygen than red blood does, which is a price she pays in warm water. A woodpecker's head is built for hitting, but she can live only where there are trees with wood to open. Humans have trade-offs too. Walking upright frees our hands, and many of us get backache. So no body is the best at everything. A body is good for one way of life.
- e.g. The same fat is a good coat in a cold sea and a trap for heat in a desert.
- e.g. An octopus squeezes through a crack but cannot run or swim far.
- e.g. A human's upright back frees the hands, and often aches.
### Different bodies, same problem · *defined*
**Definition:** Convergent evolution is when two kinds of animals that are not close relatives end up with a similar body part, because they met a similar problem.

Octopuses and humans both have eyes with a round lens that makes a sharp picture. Our last shared ancestor lived more than 500 million years ago and had no such eyes. So the two eyes grew separately. They are not the same. In our eye, the nerve fibres run in front of the light-sensing cells, and make a blind spot where they leave the eye. In an octopus's eye, the nerve fibres run behind, so there is no blind spot. Bats and birds both fly with wings, but a bat's wing is a hand with long fingers, and a bird's wing is a row of feathers. The problem was the same, and the answers are different, because each started from a different body.
- e.g. Eyes of an octopus and of a human.
- e.g. Wings of a bird and a bat.
- e.g. Long, sticky tongues for catching insects in woodpeckers and in anteaters, who are not close relatives.
### How a body part comes to exist · *defined*
**Definition:** Natural selection is the way that helpful differences become common. Animals with a helpful difference survive and have more young, and the young inherit it.

An animal does not grow a hump or a wide foot because she needs one. A camel that stretches her feet all her life does not pass wider feet to her calves. What happens is slower. In every generation, young animals differ a little. Some calves have slightly wider pads. On soft sand, those calves walk farther, find more food and live longer. They have more calves, and their calves inherit the wider pads. After thousands of generations, wide pads are normal. The camel family began in North America, tens of millions of years ago, and spread across the world. No one planned the hump. It is the result of many small differences that survived. This is also why bodies are never perfect. They are made from what was already there.
- e.g. Calves with slightly broader pads walk better on sand, so they leave more calves.
- e.g. A giraffe stretching her neck in her own lifetime does not make her calves' necks longer.
- e.g. Octopuses came from shelled ancestors, and lost the shell over a very long time.

## Mostly agreed
- A camel's hump is made of fat, and it does not hold water. *How we know:* In the 1950s, Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and his team weighed camels in the Sahara as they went without water. The weight lost was water from the whole body, and the camels got it all back in one long drink. The hump itself is fat.
- A camel can let her body temperature rise and fall by several degrees, and so use less water for sweating. *How we know:* In the Sahara in 1956, scientists took the temperature of camels every day. Camels with no water had a much wider daily swing than camels that were given water.
- An octopus has three hearts and blue blood. *How we know:* Dissections and blood tests show two gill hearts and one body heart. The blue colour comes from copper in the chemical haemocyanin.
- An octopus has about 500 million nerve cells, and about two thirds of them are in the arms. *How we know:* Counts of nerve cells in the common octopus were made by researchers, and a famous review of 2012 reports them.
- Animals with similar problems can end up with similar parts that grew separately. *How we know:* The genes and the early growth of octopus eyes and human eyes are different, even though the finished eyes look alike.
- Wider feet press softer on the ground, because pressure is weight divided by area. *How we know:* This is measured in physics laboratories and shown in the Foot Test.

## How we found out
**Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and Bodil Schmidt-Nielsen, with colleagues, in the Sahara, 1956.** For centuries, travellers said that a camel's hump was a tank of water, and some said that a camel kept water in her stomach too. In 1956, a Norwegian-American scientist named Knut Schmidt-Nielsen took a team to Beni Abbes in the Algerian Sahara. His wife, Bodil Schmidt-Nielsen, also a scientist, worked with him. They weighed camels every day as they went without water in the summer, took their temperatures, and measured everything that went in and out. The camels lost about a quarter of their weight, and it was water from the whole body. When they were allowed to drink, they took back the lost water in one long drink. The hump was fat. And the camels let their temperature rise by day and fall by night, and so saved the water they would have sweated away.  
*What people believed before:* People thought that the hump or the stomach was a tank of water. The team showed that the hump is fat, and that the camel's real trick is to lose less water. Her body temperature swings, her nose catches water from her breath, and her blood cells can swell after a big drink.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A camel's hump is a tank of water.** (Many people, and many old books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Camels go for days without a drink, and the hump is the biggest thing on a camel that looks like a store.  
  Against: When the hump is cut open, it is fat. A camel that has gone without water shrinks all over, and the hump does not hold the water she lacks.
- **A woodpecker has a built-in shock absorber in her head.** (Many books and websites, and some studies.; *debated*)  
  For: A woodpecker's skull has spongy bone, and her tongue bone curls around it. A study of 2011 argued that these take part of the knock.  
  Against: A study of 2022 used high-speed video of living woodpeckers and found that the head works like a stiff hammer. It said very little of the knock is soaked up by the skull.
- **An octopus has eight tentacles.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: The word tentacle is often used for any long, soft limb.  
  Against: Scientists call an octopus's limbs arms, because they carry suckers along their whole length. A squid has eight arms and two longer tentacles that have suckers only at the tip.
- **An octopus cannot match colours, because her eyes cannot see colour.** (Some scientists, because octopus eyes have only one kind of light-sensing cell.; *debated*)  
  For: With one kind of light-sensing cell, an octopus should not see colours as we do.  
  Against: Octopuses match the colours of rocks and weed very well. Some scientists think the skin itself senses light. Others think her oddly shaped pupil lets her work out colours from how the light blurs. Neither idea has been settled.
- **An animal grows a new body part because she needs it.** (Many people, and a very old idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: It seems natural. Giraffes need to reach, and they have long necks.  
  Against: A giraffe who stretches her neck does not pass a longer neck to her calves. Small differences already exist, and the animals with helpful ones live and have more young. Needing a part does not make the part appear.
- **An octopus's arms think for themselves, like eight small brains.** (Some octopus researchers and writers.; *debated*)  
  For: A cut-off arm still reaches for food and pulls it toward its mouth. Most of an octopus's nerve cells are in her arms.  
  Against: The arms are linked to the central brain, which sends them general orders. Many scientists say each arm acts on local reflexes, and they do not call that thinking.

## Roots
We have looked at three bodies and the problems they solve. Now we ask what these odd bodies tell us about who we are, what life is and what the world is.

**Deep truth.** No body part is simply good. Each one is a deal between what it gives and what it costs. A camel keeps all her fat in one hump, so the rest of her body can stay cool. An octopus has no bones, so she can squeeze through a crack, but swimming tires her.  
*Habit it breaks:* We rank animals as simple or advanced, strong or weak, as if one body could be the best at everything. A body is good for one way of life, and it pays for that somewhere else.  
*Evidence:* In the Ice Test, the same cloth slowed melting when it was spread all over and hardly at all when it was in one lump. A camel's fat, spread all over, would trap heat. In one lump, it leaves the rest of her free to cool. The camel's wide pad floats on sand and slips on clay.

- *who am i:* Your feet, your back and your hands are answers to problems too, and they have prices too. You are an animal with a body made of trade-offs, like a camel or an octopus.
- *what is life:* To stay alive, every animal solves the same few problems each day: food, water, air, movement, warmth and safety. A body is a long, shared answer to them.
- *what is this world:* The same Earth sets different problems in different places. Hot sand, hard wood and cold salt water ask for very different bodies.

A foot and the ground it walks on fit each other, like a key and a lock. Sand has shaped the camel's foot, and the camel's foot has shaped how she moves on sand. Wood, water and rock did the same for the woodpecker's beak and the octopus's arms. This is the one world becoming many bodies, each shaped by the place it lives in. Nothing in this is a plan. It is many small differences, slowly kept or lost.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.2:** The text says that creatures are born from food, live by food and return into food. In this teaching, a body is food made into a living being for a while. (A camel's hump is fat, which is sunlight stored by plants, eaten by a camel and kept for a hard time. Her body is built from food, and her fat is food kept for later.)
- **Zhuangzi (Chuang Tzu), chapter 17, 'Autumn Floods':** The Chinese teacher Zhuangzi said that every creature and tool has a use and a limit. A battering ram can break a city gate but cannot plug a small hole. A fine racehorse is poor at catching rats, and a cat is good at it. An owl sees a hair at night but not a mountain by day. (A wide pad is good on sand and poor on clay. A soft octopus is good in a crack and poor on a long swim. No body is the best at everything.)
- **Udana 6.4 (the story of the blind men and the elephant, in the Pali scriptures):** Blind men each touched a different part of an elephant and each told a different story, so that they quarrelled. Each was right about the part and wrong about the whole. The Jains call this many-sidedness, anekantavada. (An octopus's arm tastes a stone, a woodpecker's tongue finds insects hidden in wood, and a camel's foot feels the sand. Each body meets one world from its own side.)
- Science (settled): Fat, the camel's store, is sunlight kept by plants, made into food and then into fat. A gram of fat holds about twice the energy of a gram of sugar.
- Science (debated): In 2012, a group of scientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, saying that many animals, including octopuses, have the brain structures that make conscious experience possible. Not every scientist agrees, and what an octopus feels is still an open question.
- Other view, Many neuroscientists: An octopus's arm tasting a stone, or reaching for food after it is cut off, is a reflex made of nerve signals. It shows a clever body, they say, but not a feeling in the arm. They would say that we need more evidence before saying that an arm, or even a whole octopus, feels anything.
- Practice, Feel your feet (3 min): Take off your chappals and stand on a firm floor. Spread your toes wide and feel the floor under your whole foot. Now rise slowly onto your toes. Feel the push gather into a small patch. Come back down, and breathe out. Where is the push gentle, and where is it strong? Your foot is an answer to a problem too.
- Ask: Is a body made to fit a place, or does a place make the body?
- Ask: If every body is a deal, what is yours giving up?
- Ask: When an octopus's arm tastes a stone, who is tasting?
- Ask: Is there a body that is simply better than all the others?

## Story: The Jeep and the Camel's Foot
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Letting some air out of tyres on soft sand makes the tyre flatter, so the same weight is spread over a bigger patch and the vehicle sinks less. A camel's wide pad does the same job, and camels do slip on wet clay. Always pump the tyres up again before driving on a hard road.*

Dr. Revathi had come from Chennai to Bikaner for a month's work with camel doctors. Now she had driven three hours into the Thar. A camel calf at a small dhani had stopped drinking. The vaccine in her cool box would not last in the heat, and the jeep was already late.

Then the road ended. Ahead lay a long slope of pale sand, rippled like the skin of milk. 'Hold on,' said Karim, the driver, and he pressed the pedal.

The jeep roared up the slope. Halfway, the back wheels began to spin. Sand flew out in two brown fans, and the jeep sank slowly to its belly. Karim pressed the pedal harder. The wheels dug two deep pits, and the jeep sank even more.

'Stop,' said Revathi. 'You are digging us in deeper.' Karim switched off the engine. The silence of the desert came in, and a small bell rang somewhere in it.

Over the dune came a camel with a girl on her back. The girl was about nine. Her name was Gauri, and the calf was her grandfather's. She had walked out to look for the doctor.

The camel was a big female called Bhuri. She stepped down the slope, and Revathi noticed something. Bhuri was heavy, as heavy as the jeep's engine. But she did not sink. Her feet left shallow, round marks, as if someone had pressed saucers into the sand.

Karim got out and dug at the wheels with a plate from the cool box. Gauri slid down and watched him for a while. Then she looked at the jeep's tyre, and at Bhuri's foot, and at the tyre again.

'Uncle,' she said. 'Why does your wheel sink and Bhuri's foot does not?'

'She is much lighter than the jeep,' said Karim, not looking up.

'She is not,' said Gauri. 'Dadaji weighed her at the fair. She is six hundred kilos.' She crouched and put her hand flat on Bhuri's foot. 'Look at it. It is wide, and it is soft. When she steps, it spreads out like a roti on a tava.'

Revathi bent down. The pad of the camel's foot was as wide as a dinner plate, and when Bhuri lifted it, it was a round shape. When she put it down, it flattened and widened.

'Now look at your tyre,' said Gauri. 'It is hard, and round, and only this wide.' She showed a hand's width. 'All of the jeep sits on four small patches. Bhuri's weight is shared over four big patches. So her patches are pushed less.'

Karim stopped digging. 'You think I should have a bigger tyre?'

'You do not need a bigger one,' said Gauri slowly. 'You need to make this one wider. When the tractor comes for the wheat, my uncle lets the air out of the tyres on sand. He says they get fat and sit on top.'

Revathi laughed in surprise. 'She is right. I read this once. A softer tyre flattens where it touches the ground. The patch gets bigger, so the same weight is pushed over more sand.'

Karim looked at the girl, and at the camel's calm foot, and he knelt at the back wheel. With a small stick, he pressed the valve. The air hissed out for a long time. The tyre sagged and bulged out at the bottom, as if it were sitting down. He did the same at all four wheels.

Then he started the engine and pressed the pedal very gently. The jeep rocked once, twice, and then rolled out of its pits as if the sand had changed its mind. It climbed the slope and stopped at the top, where the ground was hard again. Gauri whooped. Bhuri, who had watched everything with her long eyelashes, calmly chewed.

At the dhani, Revathi gave the calf her injection, and by evening the little one was drinking. Karim pumped his tyres up again with the hand pump, and said he would never drive onto sand with tyres full of air again.

A week later, it rained. The Thar does not rain often, and the sand turned to a wet, shining clay. Gauri was leading Bhuri to the well when the camel's feet began to slide. Her legs slipped outwards, and she stood trembling with her feet far apart, her wide pads skating on the mud. Gauri held her rope and spoke to her softly, until she found her footing, one slow step at a time.

'She is clever on sand,' said Dadaji, who had come out to help. 'But on wet clay, she is like a child in new slippers.'

Gauri stroked Bhuri's neck. 'The same foot,' she said. 'It is good for sand and no good for mud.'

'That is how it is with feet,' said Dadaji, 'and with most things.'

**For the parent:** The camel's wide foot and the softer tyre do the same job: they spread a weight over more ground. The same foot that floats on sand slides on clay, so a body part is good for one kind of place.

- Ask: Why did the jeep sink when Karim pressed the pedal harder?
- Ask: What did Gauri notice about Bhuri's foot, and how did the tyre change?
- Ask: Why was Bhuri good on the sand, and not on the wet clay?
- Ask: What is a place where a wide foot would be a problem?

## Games
### g1 · Part, Problem, Price: a card game (25 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them face down in a pile.; The first player turns over the top card but shows only the front. She reads it aloud.; She says what problem the part solves, and guesses one price it might have.; She turns the card over and reads the back. She scores 1 point if she was close on the problem, and 1 point if she was close on the price.; Any other player may add a better answer. If it is better, that player also scores a point.; Play passes to the left. When the pile is empty, add up the points. The highest score wins.; Playing alone: draw 12 cards and score yourself. Try to beat your own score the next time.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: an animal and a body part on the front, and the problem and the price on the back:** Camel: a big hump of fat on her back. (back: Problem: food can be scarce for weeks, and fat all over her body would trap heat. Price: the hump is heavy to carry, and it flops when food is short.); Camel: wide, soft pads under her feet. (back: Problem: sinking into loose sand. The wide pad shares her weight over more sand. Price: the pad slips on wet clay.); Camel: her body temperature goes up to about 41 degrees by day. (back: Problem: sweating wastes scarce water. A hotter body needs less sweating. Price: it works best in dry air with cool nights, where she can cool down again.); Camel: double eyelashes and nostrils that can close. (back: Problem: blowing sand in her eyes and nose. Price: with the nostrils closed she cannot breathe or smell, so she can only close them for a short time.); Woodpecker: two toes forward, two toes back, and a stiff tail. (back: Problem: clinging to a straight trunk without falling backwards. Price: she is clumsy on the ground, and her tail feathers wear against rough bark.); Woodpecker: a chisel beak and strong neck muscles. (back: Problem: opening hard wood to reach food inside. Price: hammering uses a lot of energy, so she must find plenty of insects every day.); Woodpecker: a long, sticky tongue that curls round her skull. (back: Problem: reaching insects deep in narrow tunnels. Price: she depends on insects in wood, and goes hungry if the trees die.); Woodpecker: a nest hole chiselled into a tree. (back: Problem: a dry, safe place for the eggs. Price: she needs big, old or dead trees, so she cannot live where there are none.); Octopus: a body with no bones. (back: Problem: hiding in cracks and gaps. Price: she has no armour and no stiff levers, so she cannot run or swim far.); Octopus: a jet of water from a funnel. (back: Problem: escaping a hunter very fast. Price: jet swimming tires her quickly, because the heart that feeds her body slows down.); Octopus: three hearts and blue blood. (back: Problem: carrying oxygen in cold sea water. Price: blue blood carries less oxygen than red blood in warm water, so she tires easily.); Octopus: eight arms lined with suckers that taste. (back: Problem: finding food in dark cracks by touch. Price: so many arms need a huge number of nerve cells, and nerves cost a lot of food energy.); Giraffe: a very long neck. (back: Problem: reaching leaves high up in trees. Price: she needs a very strong heart to push blood up to her head, and it is hard for her to drink.); House gecko: tiny hairs on her toes. (back: Problem: walking up walls and across the ceiling. Price: dust and water on the hairs make her sticky grip weaker.); Elephant: big, thin ears. (back: Problem: cooling down in the heat, because blood in thin ears loses heat to the air. Price: thin ears tear easily.); Buffalo: a stomach with four rooms. (back: Problem: digesting tough grass, with help from microbes. Price: it is slow, and she must spend hours chewing her cud.); Snake: no legs and a very bendy backbone. (back: Problem: slipping into burrows, cracks and grass. Price: she cannot carry anything, and she must swallow her food whole.); Bat: wings of skin stretched over long fingers. (back: Problem: catching insects in the air at night. Price: she walks very badly on the ground.); Kingfisher: a long, straight beak and a head-first dive. (back: Problem: spearing fish under the water. Price: she needs clear water to see the fish, and goes hungry in muddy water.); Crow: a strong beak that suits many foods. (back: Problem: finding food in a city where it changes every day. Price: she is not the best at any one food. A sunbird sips nectar better, and an eagle tears meat better.); Sunbird: a thin, curved beak. (back: Problem: sipping nectar from deep flowers. Price: she depends on flowers with the right shape, and has trouble when there are none.); Human: an upright back and two legs. (back: Problem: freeing the hands to carry and to make tools. Price: many people get backache and sore hips.); Human: a big brain. (back: Problem: learning, planning and making things. Price: the brain is about 2 parts in 100 of the body's weight, but it uses about 20 parts in 100 of the body's energy.); Peacock: a long, bright tail. (back: Problem: attracting a peahen. Price: the tail is heavy and easy for hunters to see, so it makes flying and hiding harder.)
- **Printable score sheet: A score sheet with a row for each player:** Player name. Problem points. Price points. Total.; Write one line per round. A card is worth up to 2 points: 1 for the problem, 1 for the price.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the score sheet. Shuffle the cards and put them face down. Each player gets a pencil.  
  *Parent says:* We have 24 cards. Each one shows an animal and one odd body part. Your job is to say what problem it solves and what it might cost the animal.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Shuffle them. Put them face down. Each card has an animal and a part.
  - Note: Print the cards on both sides of a page, if you can, with the fronts and backs lined up. If you cannot, glue the back text onto the back of each card.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play one practice card together. Turn over the top card and read the front aloud. Say the problem and the price before you look at the back.  
  *Parent says:* Practice card. Read the front, but do not look at the back. What problem might this part solve? What might it cost? Say both, and then we will check.  
  *Child mode:* Read the front. Say the problem. Say a price. Then turn the card over.
  - Note: A price can be a plain cost, a place where the part is no use, or a thing the animal has to give up. It does not have to be a disaster.
  - Check: Practice card: a camel's wide, soft pads. What is the price? → **They slip on wet clay**
- **s3 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns. Turn over the top card, say the problem and a price, then read the back. Score points. Keep going until the pile is empty, or until 12 cards for one player.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn. Read the front. Tell us the problem it solves and one price. Then we read the back. One point for each part you got close.  
  *Child mode:* Take a card. Say the problem and a price. Then read the back. Score your points.
  - Ask: Why might this part be a problem in a different place? (good: A camel's wide pad would slip on mud.; A bat's wings are poor for walking.; A big brain needs a lot of food.)
  - Note: Be generous. If the child's answer is close in meaning, give the point. Ask for a reason, not just a word.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the points. Choose the card you liked best, and tell the others why.  
  *Parent says:* Add up the points. Which card surprised you the most? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Add up your points. Which card surprised you most? Why?
  - Check: Which sentence best matches what the cards show? → **Every body part gains something and costs something**

## Experiments
### e1 · Ice Test: does it matter where the fat goes? (50 min, 2 days)
- **question:** A camel has fat in one hump. Does the same amount of insulation keep a block of ice cold when it is spread all over, when it is lumped in one place, and when it is missing?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess which block of ice will melt the least and which will melt the most. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The three ice blocks come from three identical cups and start at the same size. Blocks B and C get two pieces of cloth of exactly the same size and thickness. All three sit side by side in the same room for the same time. Only one thing differs. Block A has no cloth, block B has the cloth all around, and block C has the cloth lumped on top. Block A is the control.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps to take the ice blocks out of the cups. Do not hold an ice block in your bare hands for long, because it can sting your skin. Wipe up any water on the floor.
- **record:** Write the height of the meltwater for each block, and rank the blocks from least to most melted. Then draw a model: a camel with the fat spread all over, and a camel with the fat in a hump, with arrows showing where the heat escapes.
- **results table:** Block | What is on it | Height of meltwater in the glass (cm) | Rank (1 = melted least)
- **schedule:** day 1: Fill three identical cups with water and freeze them overnight. Make your guess and give a reason. (5 min); day 2: Do the Ice Test, measure the water and draw your model. (45 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill three identical cups with 100 millilitres of water each. Label them A, B and C. Put them in the freezer overnight. Ask for a guess and a reason before the cups go in.  
  *Parent says:* Tomorrow we will melt three ice blocks. One stays bare. One is wrapped all around in cloth. One gets the same cloth lumped on top. Which melts the least? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Tomorrow we melt three ice blocks. Which melts the least? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Which block will melt the least, and why? (good: The wrapped one, because the cloth keeps the warm air away.; The one with the cloth on top, because the cloth is thick.; They will all melt the same, because the cloth is the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Write it down with the reason. Check that all three cups are the same size and have the same amount of water. Use plastic cups that can go in a freezer.
  - Wait: Leave the cups in the freezer overnight.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Hold each cup under the tap for 5 seconds. Tip the ice block into the katori with the same letter. Check that the blocks look the same size.  
  *Parent says:* Run the tap on the outside of each cup for 5 seconds. Then tip the block out into its katori. Do the blocks look the same?  
  *Child mode:* Run the tap on the outside of a cup. Tip the ice into its katori. Do this three times.
  - Note: A grown-up helps if a block sticks. If one block is clearly smaller, swap it for a spare, or the test is not fair.
  - Check: Why must the three ice blocks be the same size? → **So that any difference in melting comes from the cloth**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave block A bare. Wrap block B in one cloth, all around its sides, top and bottom. Fold the other cloth into a thick pad and lay it on top of block C only.  
  *Parent says:* Block A stays bare. Block B is wrapped in one cloth, all around. Block C gets the other cloth, folded into a pad on top. The sides of C stay bare.  
  *Child mode:* A stays bare. Wrap B all over in one cloth. Fold the other cloth. Put it on top of C.
  - Note: Both cloths must be the same size and the same kind. Wrap B loosely, as a blanket. Do not press the cloth hard against the ice. C's cloth sits on the top, and the sides are open.
  - Check: Why do B and C get cloths of exactly the same size? → **So the only difference is where the cloth is, all around or on top**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three katoris side by side on a table, away from the fan and from sunlight. Set a timer for 25 minutes. Write down the time.  
  *Parent says:* Put all three side by side, out of the Sun and the fan. Set the timer for 25 minutes. Now we wait.  
  *Child mode:* Put the katoris side by side. Not in the Sun. Set a timer for 25 minutes.
  - Note: If your room is very hot, melting is fast. If a block has melted completely before the time is up, use 15 minutes next time. If it is cold, melting is slow, so use 40 minutes.
- **s5 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* While you wait, draw two camels. Draw one with fat spread all over her body, and one with fat in a hump. Draw arrows for the heat leaving each camel. Do not touch the blocks.  
  *Parent says:* While we wait, draw two camels. One has fat spread all over her body. One has fat in a hump. Draw arrows where you think her body heat can leave.  
  *Child mode:* Draw two camels. One has fat all over. One has a hump. Draw arrows for heat leaving.
  - Ask: On which camel can the heat leave more easily, and why? (good: The one with the hump, because the rest of her skin is thin.; The one with fat all over has a thick blanket that holds the heat in.)
  - Note: This is the child's own model. Any drawing with a reason is good. Do not explain the answer yet. Let the ice give the evidence.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When the timer rings, lift block A out. Pour the water from katori A into the tall glass. Measure its height with the ruler. Do the same for B and C. Squeeze each cloth over its katori first.  
  *Parent says:* Time is up. Lift out each block. For B and C, squeeze the cloth over the katori. Then pour all the water into the tall glass and measure how high it goes.  
  *Child mode:* Lift out the block. Squeeze the cloth. Pour the water into the glass. Measure the height. Do A, B and C.
  - Note: Empty and wipe the glass before each block. Measure to the nearest millimetre. Do not worry if your numbers are small. The pattern is what matters.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill in the table. Rank the blocks. Then ask for the three parts: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which block melted the least? Which melted the most? Which two were closest? Now tell me three things. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Fill in the table. Which block melted the least? Which melted the most? Why?
  - Ask: What do you notice about A and C? (good: They melted almost the same.; The cloth on top did not help much.; C melted a little less than A.)
  - Ask: Which block is like a camel with fat all over, and which is like a camel with a hump? (good: B is fat all over. C is the hump.; The hump leaves the sides bare, so heat gets in or out there.)
  - Note: In most homes block B melts the least. Blocks A and C come out close, and C melts a little less than A. Your numbers will differ. Let the child say what the test shows before you explain. The model is honest only in part: the ice is colder than the room, and a camel is hotter than the night air. But a layer slows heat in both directions.
  - Check: Which result best matches this idea? The same cloth works as a good coat only when it is spread all over. → **B melted far less than A and C**
### e2 · Foot Test: why does a camel not sink in the sand? (42 min)
- **question:** If the same weight stands on a small foot, a medium foot and a wide foot, how deep does each sink into soft ground?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess which foot will sink the deepest and which will hardly sink at all. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The weight is the same every time, a steel tumbler with 300 millilitres of water. The soft ground is the same slab of dough, smoothed flat before each try. Each foot stays down for 30 seconds. Only the width of the foot changes. We try each foot twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** The dough is made of wheat. Do not eat it raw, and wash hands afterwards. If anyone is allergic to wheat, use clay or play-dough instead. Wipe up any spilt water.
- **record:** Write the width of each foot and the depth of each dent in millimetres. Work out the average for each foot. Then draw the three dents side by side, to scale.
- **results table:** Foot | Width (cm) | Dent, try 1 (mm) | Dent, try 2 (mm) | Average (mm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three feet: the bottle cap, the tumbler base and the plate. Measure the width of each with the ruler. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* The same tumbler of water will stand on three feet. A small cap, its own base and a wide plate. Which foot will sink deepest into the dough? Which will sink the least? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Meet the three feet. Which one sinks the deepest? Which hardly sinks? Why?
  - Ask: Which foot do you think will sink the deepest, and why? (good: The small cap, because all the weight is on a tiny patch.; The wide plate, because it is heavy.; They will all sink the same, because the weight is the same.)
  - Note: Write the widths in the table. Do not correct any guess. Many children think that the plate is heavier so it sinks more. Let the dough show them.
  - Check: The tumbler has the same weight on all three feet. Which foot will push on the dough with the greatest pressure? → **The small cap, because the weight is shared over the smallest patch**
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Roll the dough into a slab about 2 centimetres thick in the tray. Smooth the top with the back of a spoon. Fill the tumbler with 300 millilitres of water.  
  *Parent says:* Roll the dough flat, about two fingers thick. Smooth it with the back of a spoon. This is our soft ground, like loose sand. Now fill the tumbler to 300 millilitres. This is our weight.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dough flat. Smooth it. Fill the tumbler with 300 millilitres of water.
  - Note: The dough should be soft enough to take a dent from a gentle press, but not sticky. If it is too hard, add a little water. If you cannot see a dent from the cap, add more water to the tumbler, and use the same amount every time.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the bottle cap flat on the dough. Balance the tumbler on it, steadying it with one fingertip at the side. Wait 30 seconds. Lift off both. Measure the dent with the toothpick and pencil. Smooth the dough and try again.  
  *Parent says:* Cap first. Put the tumbler on top and steady it with one finger, without pressing down. Count to thirty. Lift off. Slide the toothpick into the dent, and pinch it where it meets the pencil. Measure it on the ruler.  
  *Child mode:* Put the cap on the dough. Stand the tumbler on it. Wait 30 seconds. Measure the dent.
  - Note: To measure: lay a pencil across the dent. Slide the toothpick straight down to the bottom. Pinch the toothpick where it meets the pencil and lay it along the ruler. Smooth the dough with the spoon between tries. Do two tries.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Smooth the dough. Stand the tumbler on its own base on a fresh patch for 30 seconds. Lift it off. Measure the dent. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Now the tumbler on its own base, the medium foot. Wait thirty seconds. Lift off and measure the dent. Then smooth the dough and do it again.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the tumbler on its own base. Wait 30 seconds. Measure the dent. Do it twice.
  - Note: Remember to measure the width of the tumbler base and write it in the table. If a dent is too faint to measure, write 0 and the word 'faint'.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Smooth the dough. Lay the plate flat on it. Stand the tumbler in the middle of the plate for 30 seconds. Lift both off. Look for a dent and measure it. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Last, the wide foot. Lay the plate on the dough and stand the tumbler in the middle. Wait thirty seconds. Lift off. Is there a dent? If there is, measure it.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the plate on the dough. Stand the tumbler on it. Wait 30 seconds. Is there a dent?
  - Note: A dent from the plate may be very faint or none at all. Write 0 if you can see none. That is a true result and not a failure.
- **s6 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out the average dent for each foot. Rank the feet from deepest to shallowest. Ask for three things: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Add the two tries for each foot and halve. Which foot sank the deepest? Which the least? Now tell me three things. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Find the average dent for each foot. Which sank deepest? Which sank least? Why?
  - Ask: The weight was the same each time. Why did the dents differ? (good: The same weight was shared over a bigger patch on the plate.; A small foot pushes harder on each bit of dough.)
  - Ask: How is this like a camel in the sand? (good: Her wide pad shares her weight over more sand, so she sinks less.; A thin hoof would sink like the cap.)
  - Note: In most homes the dents get shallower as the foot gets wider, and the plate leaves almost none. Your numbers will differ. The pattern is what matters. Let the child give the reasoning before you add the word pressure.
  - Check: Why does a camel's wide foot sink less than a narrow hoof of the same animal weight? → **The weight is shared over a bigger patch of sand, so each bit is pushed less**

## Activities
### a1 · Squeeze Test: how small a hole can a soft body pass? (25 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the holes with strong scissors. Keep the scissors away from small children. Dough is made of wheat. Do not eat it raw, and wash hands afterwards. If anyone is allergic to wheat, use clay or play-dough instead.
- **record:** Write which holes each body passed, and which it did not. Draw the three bodies and the smallest hole each passed.
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Draw five squares in a row on the cardboard, with sides 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 centimetres. A grown-up cuts them out. Press the marble into the middle of the dough ball and close the dough around it.  
  *Parent says:* We will test three bodies. A hard ball, a plain dough ball, and a dough ball with a marble inside. Which is the smallest hole each one can pass? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw five squares. A grown-up cuts them out. Hide a marble inside a dough ball.
  - Ask: Which body will fit through the smallest hole, and why? (good: The dough, because it is soft and can squash.; The dough with the marble, but not through a hole smaller than the marble.; The hard ball can only fit the big holes.)
  - Note: Make the plain dough ball the same size as the dough ball with the marble inside. Write the guesses down before the test. Do not correct them.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each body, start with the biggest hole. Try to push it through. If it goes, try the next smaller hole. Write the smallest hole it passed. Use the same push each time, with your fingers.  
  *Parent says:* Start with the biggest hole. Push the body through, using your fingers. If it goes, try the next hole. Write down the smallest hole it passes.  
  *Child mode:* Push each body through a hole. Start with the biggest. Go smaller. Write the smallest hole.
  - Note: The dough ball may come out the other side as a long worm. That counts as passing. The hard ball passes only the biggest hole. The marble in the dough should stop it at the 1 centimetre hole. Redo any try where the cardboard tears.
  - Check: What sets the smallest hole that the dough ball with the marble can pass? → **The hard marble inside**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare your results with your guesses. Say what the hard part of an octopus is, and what that tells you about the cracks she can enter.  
  *Parent says:* Which body went through the smallest hole? What set the limit for the dough with the marble? An octopus has only one big hard part, her beak. What does that tell us about the holes she can use?  
  *Child mode:* Which body passed the smallest hole? What stopped the marble ball? What is hard on an octopus?
  - Ask: What is the price of having no bones? (good: No armour to protect her.; Nothing stiff to push or swim far with.; She is easy to bite.)
  - Note: Many octopuses are caught in jars and pots in the sea because they will go into any space that is wider than their beak. Let the child say what the test shows before you add this.
### a2 · Tongue Tools: reach the raisin in the crack (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up checks that the skewer has a blunt end. Do not put tools near the eyes.
- **record:** Write how many raisins each tool took out in one minute. Draw the best tool and say what is like a woodpecker's tongue.

  *Parent does:* Press the five raisins to the deep end of the tube. Lay out the five tools. Ask your child which tool will get the most raisins in one minute, and why.  
  *Parent says:* A woodpecker has to get insects out of a narrow tunnel in the wood. Here are five tools. Which will get the most raisins out in one minute? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Raisins are deep in a tube. Which of five tools gets the most? Why?
  - Ask: Which tool will get the most raisins, and why? (good: The sticky one, because the raisin will stick to it.; The thread, because it is thin.; The pencil, because it is long.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. The fork and spoon may not fit in the tube at all. That is a result.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try each tool for one minute. Count the raisins you pull out. Push the raisins back in before the next tool. Write the number for each tool.  
  *Parent says:* One minute for each tool. Count the raisins that come out. Then push them back in for the next tool.  
  *Child mode:* Try each tool for one minute. Count the raisins. Put them back.
  - Note: The sticky stick usually wins. The pencil and thread may push the raisins further in. Write down exactly what happens.
  - Check: Which tool is most like a woodpecker's tongue? → **The sticky stick**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at your numbers. Say which tool was best and why. Ask your child what the tongue of a woodpecker needs to be like.  
  *Parent says:* Which tool was best? Which did not work at all? What must a woodpecker's tongue be like to reach an insect deep in a tunnel?  
  *Child mode:* Which tool won? What must a woodpecker's tongue be like?
  - Ask: What must a woodpecker's tongue be like? (good: Long and thin.; Sticky.; Able to go far out of her beak.)

## Worksheets
### Core worksheet: problem, part and price (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is the best way to start understanding an odd body part?  
   Options: Ask what problem it solves and what it costs / Ask what colour it is / Ask whether the animal likes it  
   **Answer:** Ask what problem it solves and what it costs. *Why:* An odd part is usually an answer to a problem in the animal's way of life, and every answer has a price. Colour and liking do not tell you what the part is for.
2. (mcq) What is inside a camel's hump?  
   Options: Fat / Water / Air  
   **Answer:** Fat. *Why:* The hump is a lump of fat, which the camel uses for energy when food is scarce. Many people think it is water, but when camels were studied, the water they lost came from their whole body.
3. (truefalse) The same amount of fat keeps heat in better when it is spread all over a body than when it is lumped in one place.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Heat can escape from the bare parts of a body with a lump. In the Ice Test, the cloth all around slowed the melting, and the lump on top did little for the open sides.
4. (fill) The same weight pushes less on the ground when it is spread over a bigger ____.  
   **Answer:** ['area']. *Why:* Pressure is weight divided by area. A bigger area shares the same weight over more ground, so each small patch is pushed less. This is why a camel's wide foot sinks less in sand.
5. (match) Match each animal part to its job.  
   **Answer:** Camel's wide pad → Floats on soft sand; Woodpecker's stiff tail → Props her against a trunk; Octopus's funnel → Squirts out a jet of water; Octopus's beak → The only big hard part she has. *Why:* Each part answers a problem. The pad spreads the camel's weight. The tail works like a third foot. The funnel gives a jet to push the octopus forward. The beak sets the size of the smallest gap she can pass.
6. (sort) Sort each body part by the problem it helps to solve.  
   **Answer:** fat kept in one hump, so the rest of the skin is thin → Staying cool; a camel's wide pad → Moving over soft ground; a duck's webbed foot on mud → Moving over soft ground; a woodpecker's chisel beak → Getting food from wood; a woodpecker's sticky tongue → Getting food from wood; a camel's temperature rising by day → Staying cool. *Why:* Each part has a main job. Fat in a hump leaves bare skin to lose heat. Wide and webbed feet spread weight. A beak and a tongue work together to get food from wood. Letting the body warm up saves sweating.
7. (order) Put these in order to show how wide pads could become common in camels.  
   **Answer:** ['Some calves are born with slightly wider pads', 'On soft sand, those calves walk farther and find more food', 'They live longer and have more calves', 'Their calves inherit the wide pads', 'After many generations, wide pads are normal']. *Why:* The small difference comes first. Survival and having young then make it more common, one generation at a time. No camel grew wider pads because she needed them.
8. (odd) Which one is not true of an octopus, and why? Three hearts, blue blood, bones, a beak.  
   **Answer:** Bones, because an octopus has no bones. The other three are true of an octopus.. *Why:* An octopus has no skeleton. Her only big hard part is her beak. She has three hearts and blue blood, which carries oxygen with copper.
9. (count) A square foot is 2 centimetres on each side. A bigger square foot is 4 centimetres on each side. How many times as big is the area of the bigger foot?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* The small foot has an area of 2 times 2, which is 4 square centimetres. The big foot has 4 times 4, which is 16 square centimetres. 16 divided by 4 is 4. Doubling the width makes the area 4 times as big.
10. (write) A friend says, 'A camel's hump is full of water, because camels live in the desert.' What would you say to your friend? Give a reason.  
   **Answer:** The hump is fat, not water. The camel uses the fat for energy when food is short. Her real water-saving tricks are letting her body warm up so she sweats less, and catching water from her breath.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the hump is fat, and gives at least one reason or piece of evidence. It may also say how a camel really saves water.
11. (draw) Draw one camel with fat spread all over her body, and one camel with her fat in a hump. Draw arrows for heat leaving each one.  
   **Answer:** Two camels. The first has a thick layer all around with few or no arrows. The second has a hump on her back and arrows leaving her bare sides.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that heat leaves through the thin skin on the sides of the camel with a hump, and is held in by the thick layer on the other camel.
12. (mcq) How does a woodpecker keep from falling backwards while she clings to a trunk?  
   Options: Her stiff tail props her like a third foot / She glues herself to the bark / She holds her breath  
   **Answer:** Her stiff tail props her like a third foot. *Why:* A woodpecker leans on her stiff tail, and her toes grip with two forward and two back. Together they hold her tight against the trunk.
13. (truefalse) An octopus can pass through any gap that is wider than her beak.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The beak is her only big hard part. The rest of her body is soft, so it squeezes through. This is why octopuses escape from jars and tanks.
14. (write) Choose any animal, including a human. Name one body part, the problem it solves and one price it has.  
   **Answer:** A giraffe's long neck helps her reach high leaves. The price is that she needs a very strong heart to push blood up to her head. Or: a human's upright back frees the hands, and the price is backache.. *Why:* A strong answer names a real part, a real problem and a real price. The price may be a cost, or a place where the part does not work.
15. (fill) A woodpecker's tongue is long and ____, so insects stick to it.  
   **Answer:** ['sticky']. *Why:* The tip is also barbed. This lets her pull insects out of narrow tunnels in wood.
16. (mcq) Which of these works for the same reason as a camel's wide foot?  
   Options: A snowshoe / A sharp nail / A knife blade  
   **Answer:** A snowshoe. *Why:* A snowshoe is wide, so it spreads a person's weight over more snow, and the snow holds. A nail and a knife do the opposite. They put all the push on a tiny patch.
### Stretch worksheet: gains, costs and different answers (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In an ice test, a block with cloth all around melts least. A block with the same cloth lumped on top melts almost as much as a bare block. What does this show?  
   Options: Insulation helps only where it covers / Cloth makes ice melt faster / Cloth on top works better than cloth all around  
   **Answer:** Insulation helps only where it covers. *Why:* Heat reaches the block through the parts that stay bare. A lump on the top does little for the sides. This is why a camel's fat in a hump does not trap the heat of her whole body.
2. (mcq) Why does a camel's temperature rising by day save her water?  
   Options: A body allowed to warm up does not need to sweat as much / A hot body drinks less / Sweat is cooler when the body is hotter  
   **Answer:** A body allowed to warm up does not need to sweat as much. *Why:* Sweating cools the body but loses water. If a camel lets her body warm up, she needs less sweating, so she saves water.
3. (mcq) A giraffe stretches her neck all her life. Will her calf have a longer neck because of that?  
   Options: No, a calf inherits only the differences she is born with / Yes, stretching passes on to the calf / Yes, but only a little  
   **Answer:** No, a calf inherits only the differences she is born with. *Why:* What an animal does in her life does not change what she passes on. Giraffe necks became long because longer-necked giraffes in each generation reached more food and had more young.
4. (truefalse) In warm water, an octopus's blue blood carries more oxygen than red blood does.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The copper-based blue blood works well in cold water with little oxygen, but it carries less oxygen than red blood in warm water. This is one of the prices of being an octopus.
5. (truefalse) Octopuses and humans both have lens eyes because they share a recent ancestor with such an eye.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Our last common ancestor lived over 500 million years ago, and had no lens eye. The two kinds of eyes grew separately, which is called convergent evolution.
6. (fill) A woodpecker has two toes pointing forward and two toes pointing ____.  
   **Answer:** ['back', 'backward', 'backwards']. *Why:* This grip is called zygodactyl. It lets her cling to a straight trunk, with her stiff tail pressed against it.
7. (match) Match each pair of animals to the problem they both solved, in different ways.  
   **Answer:** A bird and a bat → Flying with wings; An octopus and a human → Seeing with a lens eye; A woodpecker and an anteater → Catching insects with a long sticky tongue. *Why:* Each pair met the same problem. They are not close relatives, so each pair found its own answer, with a different body to start from.
8. (sort) Sort each fact into a gain or a price.  
   **Answer:** A camel floats over soft sand → A gain; A camel slips on wet clay → A price; An octopus squeezes into a crack → A gain; An octopus tires when she swims → A price; A woodpecker opens hard wood → A gain; A woodpecker needs trees with wood → A price. *Why:* Every body part has both. The gain is what the part does well. The price is where it fails or what the animal gives up.
9. (order) Put the steps of a fair test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Ask a question and guess the answer, with a reason', 'Change only one thing', 'Keep everything else the same', 'Measure each result', 'Say what I thought, what I saw and why']. *Why:* A fair test starts with a question and a guess. Then only one thing changes, so any difference must come from it. The measuring comes last, followed by saying what it means.
10. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? A chisel beak, a stiff tail, a long sticky tongue, a wide soft pad.  
   **Answer:** The wide soft pad, because it belongs to a camel. The other three belong to a woodpecker.. *Why:* A woodpecker has a chisel beak, a stiff tail and a long sticky tongue. A wide soft pad is a camel's foot.
11. (count) A foot 3 centimetres wide sinks 6 millimetres into dough. A foot twice as wide sinks 2 millimetres. How many times as deep is the first dent?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 6 divided by 2 is 3. The narrow foot sank 3 times as deep. Doubling the width makes the area 4 times as big, so the push on each patch is 4 times smaller, and the dent is much shallower.
12. (write) Explain why a camel with fat spread all over her body would find desert heat harder than a camel with a hump. Use what you know about insulation.  
   **Answer:** Fat is an insulator, and it traps heat. If it covered the whole body, the camel's heat would stay in and she would need to sweat a lot, using up water. In a hump, the fat leaves most of the skin thin, so her body heat can leave.. *Why:* A strong answer says that fat is insulation, that insulation holds heat in, and that the hump leaves the rest of the body free to lose heat.
13. (perspective) A visitor at an aquarium says that an octopus is a simple animal, because she has no bones and no shell.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says that having no bones does not make an animal simple, and gives evidence such as the nerve cells, the three hearts or the escapes.. *Why:* The biologist and the fisher give evidence. The visitor judges by one missing part. A body without a skeleton is made differently, and not more simply.
14. (draw) Draw an octopus squeezing into a crack in a rock. Label the one big hard part that sets how small a gap she can pass.  
   **Answer:** An octopus with soft arms and body flowing into a crack, and a label pointing to the beak between her arms.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the soft body pouring into the gap, and labels the beak as the hard part that sets the limit.
15. (write) Octopuses and humans both have lens eyes, but their eyes grew separately. Why might two kinds of animals end up with the same kind of eye?  
   **Answer:** They met the same problem: focusing light to see a sharp picture. A lens is a good answer to that problem, so it appeared twice, from different starting bodies.. *Why:* A strong answer names the shared problem, and says that a similar answer can appear twice when the problem is the same.
### Challenge worksheet: reasoning about bodies (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A student repeats the Ice Test but gives block C a cloth twice as thick as block B's. C melts less than B. What is wrong with this test?

   **Answer:** That the skull works mainly as a shock absorber. *Why:* The older idea says the skull and tongue bones take up most of the knock. The video study suggests the head passes most of its energy into the wood instead. Scientists are still testing both.
4. (truefalse) An animal that needs a new body part will grow it during her lifetime.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Needing a part does not make it appear. Small differences already exist in each generation, and the animals with helpful ones survive and have more young.
5. (fill) An octopus breathes with ____ inside her mantle.  
   **Answer:** ['gills']. *Why:* Water flows through the mantle and over the gills, where oxygen passes into her blood. The same mantle also gives the jet when she squeezes water out through the funnel.

   **Answer:** A camel's wide pad → Slips on wet clay; An octopus's soft body → Has no armour; A woodpecker's chisel beak → Uses a lot of energy for hammering; A giraffe's long neck → Needs a very strong heart. *Why:* Every part has a price. A wide pad has no grip on clay. A soft body has nothing hard to protect it. Hammering is hard work. A tall neck means blood must be pushed far up to the head.
7. (sort) Sort each thing by whether it spreads weight over a big patch or puts it on a small patch.  
   **Answer:** a camel's wide pad → Spreads weight over a big patch; a snowshoe → Spreads weight over a big patch; a wide tractor tyre → Spreads weight over a big patch; a sharp nail → Puts weight on a small patch; a high heel → Puts weight on a small patch; the tip of a needle → Puts weight on a small patch. *Why:* Wide things share a weight over many small patches, so each is pushed a little. Pointed things push hard on one tiny patch.
8. (count) A camel weighs 600 kilograms. She can lose a quarter of her weight in water. How many kilograms of water is that, and how much does she weigh then?  
   **Answer:** 150 kilograms lost, 450 kilograms left. *Why:* A quarter of 600 is 600 divided by 4, which is 150. 600 take away 150 is 450. She can lose this much water and live. A person is in danger long before losing a quarter of their weight.
9. (count) In a test, block A melted to 1.6 centimetres of water in the glass and block B melted to 0.8 centimetres. How many times as much did A melt as B?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 1.6 divided by 0.8 is 2. Block A melted twice as much as block B.
10. (odd) Which pair is the odd one out, and why? A bird's wing and a bat's wing, an octopus eye and a human eye, a human hand and a chimpanzee hand.  
   **Answer:** The human hand and the chimpanzee hand, because the two are alike since they come from a recent shared ancestor. The other pairs grew separately to solve the same problem.. *Why:* Humans and chimpanzees share a recent ancestor with a grasping hand, so their hands are alike by inheritance. Birds and bats, and octopuses and humans, are far apart, and their similar parts grew separately.
11. (write) Explain how a camel family could end up with wide pads, without any camel trying to grow them.  
   **Answer:** In every generation, calves differ a little. Some have wider pads. On sand, they walk farther and find more food, so they live longer and have more calves. Those calves inherit wide pads. After many generations, wide pads are normal.. *Why:* A strong answer names the small differences, the survival and having more young, and the inheriting. It does not say that a camel grew pads by trying.
12. (write) Pick an animal from your home or street. Name two of her body parts, the problem each solves and a price for each.  
   **Answer:** A house crow has a strong all-purpose beak that eats many foods, and the price is that she is not the best at any one food. Her feet grip railings, and the price is that she walks with a clumsy hop.. *Why:* A strong answer names real parts, real problems and honest prices. Any animal you can watch will do.
13. (write) A friend's ice test shows the fully wrapped block melting least. She says, 'This proves that fat all over is always better.' Use claim, evidence and reasoning to reply.  
   **Answer:** Claim: not always. Evidence: wrapped ice melted least, but that was a model with a cold block in a warm room. Reasoning: a coat is good when you want to keep heat in or out. A camel in the desert wants heat to get out of her body, so fat all over could be a problem.. *Why:* A strong answer separates what the test showed from what it did not. It also says that a good part in one place may be a poor part in another.
14. (perspective) A cousin says it would be wonderful to have a camel's body, because camels never need water.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says that a camel saves water but still needs it, and that her body works well in one kind of place.. *Why:* The keeper and the vet say what the evidence shows. The camel's tricks are real, but they save water and do not remove the need for it.
15. (draw) Draw an octopus using her jet. Show the water going out backwards and the octopus going forward. Add arrows.  
   **Answer:** An octopus with a funnel pointing backwards, an arrow for the water going back and an arrow for the octopus going forward.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the jet going one way and the octopus moving the other way. The water is pushed back, and it pushes her forward.

   Options: What problem does this beak solve? / How old is the bird? / What is her favourite colour?  
   **Answer:** What problem does this beak solve?. *Why:* An odd part is an answer to a problem in the animal's way of living. Asking about the problem leads you to the food, the place and the price.
2. (mcq) A camel keeps her fat in one hump, not all over her body. What is the best reason?  
   Options: The rest of her skin stays thin, so her body heat can leave / Fat is too heavy to spread out / Fat only grows on the back  
   **Answer:** The rest of her skin stays thin, so her body heat can leave. *Why:* Fat is insulation. A layer all over would trap her heat, and she would have to sweat more and lose water.
3. (mcq) A thin, hungry camel's hump flops to one side. What does this tell you?  
   Options: Her body has been using up the fat in it / The hump was full of water and it leaked / She has grown a new hump  
   **Answer:** Her body has been using up the fat in it. *Why:* The hump is a store of fat. When food is scarce, her body uses it up and the hump shrinks and flops. It is not a tank of water.
4. (mcq) The same weight stands on a narrow foot and on a wide foot on soft dough. Which sinks deeper, and why?  
   Options: The narrow foot, because the same weight is on a smaller patch / The wide foot, because it is bigger / Both sink the same, because the weight is the same  
   **Answer:** The narrow foot, because the same weight is on a smaller patch. *Why:* Pressure is weight divided by area. The narrow foot puts all the weight on a smaller area, so each small patch is pushed harder.
5. (mcq) A camel's wide foot floats on sand. What is a likely price?  
   Options: It slips on wet clay / It makes her too light / It stops her from running  
   **Answer:** It slips on wet clay. *Why:* A wide pad shares her weight on soft ground, but on slippery clay it has little grip. Every good part is good for one kind of place.
6. (mcq) Why do most woodpeckers have two toes pointing backwards?  
   Options: To grip a straight trunk / To swim / To scratch the ground for seeds  
   **Answer:** To grip a straight trunk. *Why:* Two toes forward and two back make a strong clamp on a trunk. Her stiff tail props her at the same time.
7. (mcq) What do scientists agree on about how a woodpecker's head survives the hits?  
   Options: They are still testing whether the skull absorbs the shock or acts like a stiff hammer / It is settled that the head has a built-in cushion / It is settled that her brain is never hurt  
   **Answer:** They are still testing whether the skull absorbs the shock or acts like a stiff hammer. *Why:* Older books said she has a shock absorber, but a video study in 2022 found the head works more like a stiff hammer. The question is still open, and we can say so.
8. (mcq) How does an octopus move away fast from danger?  
   Options: She squirts water out of her funnel and shoots the other way / She flaps her wings / She runs on her bones  
   **Answer:** She squirts water out of her funnel and shoots the other way. *Why:* The water jets backwards, and the push on the water pushes her forward. It works fast but tires her, because the heart that feeds her body slows down.
9. (mcq) Why can an octopus squeeze through a gap about as wide as her beak?  
   Options: She has no bones, so only her beak is hard / She is made of water / She can shrink her beak  
   **Answer:** She has no bones, so only her beak is hard. *Why:* The beak is the one big hard part. The rest of her body is soft and flows through any gap that the beak can pass.
10. (mcq) Which of these is evidence, not a claim, about the Ice Test?  
   Options: Block B had the least water in the glass / Fat all over is always best / Insulation traps heat  
   **Answer:** Block B had the least water in the glass. *Why:* Evidence is what you measured or saw. A claim says what you think is true. Reasoning explains why the evidence supports the claim.
11. (mcq) Which is the best explanation of how camels came to have wide pads?  
   Options: Calves born with wider pads survived better on sand and left more calves / Camels stretched their feet to fit the sand / Camels wanted wider feet  
   **Answer:** Calves born with wider pads survived better on sand and left more calves. *Why:* Wanting or stretching does not change what an animal passes on. Helpful differences spread because those animals survive and have more young.
12. (mcq) Octopus eyes and human eyes both have lenses, yet the animals are not close relatives. What best explains this?  
   Options: Both met the same problem, focusing light, and each found its own answer / An octopus is a kind of human / They share a recent ancestor with lens eyes  
   **Answer:** Both met the same problem, focusing light, and each found its own answer. *Why:* This is convergent evolution. A lens is a good answer to the problem of seeing a sharp image, so it appeared twice, from different bodies.

## Applications
- Wide, low-pressure tyres and let-down tyres help vehicles cross desert sand and soft mud.
- Snowshoes and wide skis spread a person's weight on snow, in the same way as a camel's pad.
- Engineers study octopus arms to build soft robots that can grip odd shapes and enter small gaps.
- Engineers study woodpecker heads for ideas for protective packaging and helmets, though how the head works is still debated.
- In hot cities, roofs are coated white or insulated, because the roof is where the Sun hits a building.
- Doctors and nurses use ideas about pressure when they place people in beds, so that the weight is shared over a bigger area.
- Kharai camels of Kutch swim out to islands to eat mangrove leaves, so the camel body can suit a sea coast as well as a desert.
- Designers think in trade-offs: a coat, a shoe or a tyre that is better in one place is worse in another.
- Farmers use wide-wheeled carts and tractors in soft fields, to avoid sinking.
- Cuttlefish and octopus camouflage give ideas for materials that change colour.

## Open questions
- **How does a woodpecker's head survive thousands of knocks a day?** Studies disagree about how much the skull and the tongue bones soak up the shock. High-speed video is new, and tests on living woodpeckers are hard to do.
- **How does an octopus match the colour of her background when her eyes seem to see no colour?** Some scientists think her skin senses light. Others think the shape of her pupil lets her work out colour. Neither idea has been proved.
- **How much does each octopus arm decide for herself?** Most of her nerve cells are in her arms, but nobody knows how orders pass between the arms and the brain, or what, if anything, an arm feels.
- **Why do most octopuses live only one to a few years?** Many octopuses die soon after breeding, and a mother often dies as her eggs hatch. Scientists have found a gland that triggers this, but they do not know why evolution kept it.
- **How exactly does a camel's kidney make such thick wee, and how does the body avoid damage when very thirsty?** The basic idea is known, but many details of the cells involved are still being studied.
- **Could a robot with a soft body work as well as an octopus?** Engineers have made soft robot arms, but none matches the control that an octopus has over eight arms.

## Connected maths
- A square foot that is 1 centimetre wide has an area of 1 square centimetre. Feet 2, 3 and 4 centimetres wide have areas of 4, 9 and 16 square centimetres. What is the area of a foot 5 centimetres wide, and what pattern did you use? **25 square centimetres. The areas are square numbers: 1 times 1, 2 times 2, 3 times 3, 4 times 4, so 5 times 5.**. Each area is the width times itself. 5 times 5 is 25. A foot 5 times as wide has 25 times the area, so the same weight gives a push that is 25 times smaller on each patch. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In the Foot Test, the narrow foot made dents of 6 millimetres and 8 millimetres in two tries. What is the average dent? **7 millimetres.**. 6 plus 8 is 14. 14 divided by 2 is 7. The average is 7 millimetres. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- Fat holds about twice as much energy as the same weight of sugar. A big camel's hump holds about 30 kilograms of fat. How many kilograms of sugar would hold the same energy? **60 kilograms.**. Fat holds twice the energy of sugar, so you need twice the weight of sugar. 30 times 2 is 60 kilograms. This is why a store of fat is so light for the energy it holds. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A person drinks about 2 litres of water a day. A very thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres in a few minutes. How many days of a person's drinking is that? **50 days.**. 100 divided by 2 is 50. A thirsty camel can drink in a few minutes what a person drinks in about seven weeks. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- An octopus has about 500 million nerve cells, and about two thirds of them are in her arms. About how many nerve cells are in her arms? **About 333 million.**. 500 million divided by 3 is about 167 million, which is one third. Two thirds is 2 times 167 million, which is about 333 million. So most of her nerve cells are in her arms. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A woodpecker hits the wood 15 times a second in a burst that lasts 6 seconds. She does 10 bursts while she looks for food. How many hits is that in all? **900 hits.**. 15 times 6 is 90 hits in one burst. 90 times 10 is 900 hits. A woodpecker's head takes hundreds of hard knocks each time she feeds. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In an ice test, block A (no cloth) melts to 1.6 centimetres of water, block B (cloth all around) to 0.8 centimetres and block C (the same cloth lumped on top) to 1.4 centimetres. Is block C closer to A or to B? Use the differences to decide. **Closer to A. C is 0.2 centimetres from A and 0.6 centimetres from B.**. A minus C is 1.6 minus 1.4, which is 0.2. C minus B is 1.4 minus 0.8, which is 0.6. C is much closer to the bare block, so the lump on top helped little. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
