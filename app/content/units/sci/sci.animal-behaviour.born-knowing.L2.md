# Born knowing and learning: what animals can do from birth, and what they learn
`sci.animal-behaviour.born-knowing.L2`

**Summary.** A baby spider spins her first web with no one to show her how. Some things an animal can do from the day they are born, with no teaching. This is called instinct. Other things an animal learns, by trying again and again, or by watching others. Humans are animals too: a newborn baby grips your finger without being taught, but has to learn to talk. Your child tests their own knee kick, a born-with action, to find out whether they can stop it by trying.

**Intro.** *Many spider mothers leave before their babies hatch. Nobody ever shows the babies how to spin a web. Yet each one spins a web of the right shape. How does she know how?* Some things you could do on the day you were born. You could cry, drink milk and hold a finger tight. Nobody taught you. Other things you learned later, like talking and brushing your teeth. Other animals are the same. A spider is born ready to spin. A kitten learns to hunt by watching her mother. In this unit you meet animals who are born knowing, and animals who learn. Then you test a born-with action in your own body: can you stop it if you try?

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that some things an animal can do from birth, with no teaching, and that this is called instinct.
1. By the end I can say that other things an animal learns, by trying again and again or by watching others.
2. By the end I can give examples of both in humans and other animals: a spider spins a web by instinct, and a child learns to talk.
3. By the end I can say that many things are both: an animal is born ready to do them and gets better with practice.
4. By the end I can make a guess with a reason, test whether I can stop my knee kick by trying, count my results and say what I found.
5. By the end I can say that a reflex is a quick action the body does by itself, like a knee kick or a sneeze.

## Terms
- **Instinct**: An instinct is something an animal can do from birth, without being taught. *e.g.* A baby spider spins her first web by instinct. Nobody shows her how.
- **Learning**: Learning is getting a new skill or knowing something new, by trying, by watching others or by being taught. *e.g.* A child learns to speak Kannada by hearing her family speak Kannada every day.
- **Practice**: Practice means doing something again and again, so that you get better at it. *e.g.* Your first kolam may be wobbly. After a week of drawing one every morning, the lines get straighter.
- **Reflex**: A reflex is a very quick action that the body does by itself, before you decide to do it. *e.g.* When something flies at your face, you blink. When dust tickles your nose, you sneeze. Both are reflexes.
- **Spiderling**: A spiderling is a baby spider, just come out of the egg. *e.g.* Spiderlings come out of a silk egg bag. Each spiderling goes off alone, and spins her own web.
- **Hatchling**: A hatchling is a baby animal who has just come out of an egg. *e.g.* Olive ridley turtle hatchlings dig out of the sand on Odisha's beaches and crawl straight to the sea.
- **Genes**: Genes are tiny instructions inside every living body. Parents pass them on to their young. *e.g.* A spider's genes, passed on from her parents, help her body build a web of the right shape.

## Explanations
### Born knowing: instinct · *defined*
**Definition:** An instinct is something an animal can do from birth, the first time, without being taught.

In many kinds of spider, the mother lays her eggs in a silk bag and leaves. The spiderlings hatch alone. Yet each one spins a web of the right shape for her kind. A newborn calf stands up and walks within a few hours. A turtle hatchling crawls towards the sea the moment she comes out of the sand. Human babies have instincts too. A newborn baby turns her head to a touch on the cheek, and sucks to drink milk. The know-how for an instinct comes from the parents, passed on in the body. Scientists say it is carried in genes, the tiny instructions inside every cell.
- e.g. A spiderling spins her first web with no teacher.
- e.g. A newborn calf stands up and walks on the day he is born.
- e.g. A newborn baby sucks to drink milk, and grips a finger tight.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtle hatchlings crawl to the sea at night.
### Learned: by trying and by watching · *defined*
**Definition:** Learning is getting a new skill or new knowledge after birth, by trying again and again, by watching others or by being taught.

Some things nobody is born knowing. A kitten is born with the urge to pounce. But she learns which animals to hunt, and how, from watching her mother and from many tries. A crow learns which balcony puts out rice every morning, and flies there at the right time. A child is not born knowing Tamil, Bengali or English. She learns the words her family speaks around her. Learning takes time and practice. The first tries are often wrong, and each try teaches something.
- e.g. A kitten learns to hunt by watching her mother and trying.
- e.g. A crow learns which window puts out rice, and comes back every day.
- e.g. A child learns the language her family speaks.
- e.g. A child learns to ride a cycle after many falls.
### Most things are both · *defined*
**Definition:** Many actions start as an instinct and get better with practice. Instinct and learning work together.

A chick pecks at grains on the day she hatches. Nobody teaches her, but she misses many grains at first. After a few days she hits more of them. Weaver birds are born ready to weave a nest of grass. Elsie Collias and her husband Nicholas, scientists in the United States, studied the village weaver, an African cousin of the baya. Young males weave untidy nests at first, and get better with practice. A human baby is born moving her legs in steps when she is held up. But walking takes about a year of falling and trying again. Every baby is born ready to learn a language, but the one she speaks depends on what she hears.
- e.g. A chick pecks from birth, and her aim gets better with practice.
- e.g. A weaver bird is born ready to weave, and young males' nests get neater with practice.
- e.g. A baby is born ready to learn a language, but learns the one she hears.
### Your born-with actions: reflexes · *defined*
**Definition:** A reflex is a very quick action the body does by itself. It happens before you decide to do it.

When someone taps just below your kneecap, the tap stretches the big muscle on the front of your leg. A message runs from that muscle up to your backbone. Inside your backbone is your spinal cord, a thick bundle of nerves. The message comes straight back down to the muscle, and the muscle tightens. Your leg kicks. All this happens in a tiny part of a second, before you decide anything. That is why trying hard to stop it often does not work. You can hold your leg stiff against it, but you cannot simply switch it off. You were born with this kick. Babies, children and grandmothers all have it. Blinking at dust and sneezing are reflexes too.
- e.g. The knee kick when the spot below the kneecap is tapped.
- e.g. A sneeze when dust tickles your nose.
- e.g. Pulling your hand back from a hot tawa before you even feel it hurt.
### Does an instinct need thinking? · *debated*
A spider is born knowing how to spin. But she still makes many choices. She picks a place for her web, such as a corner where flies come. She fixes holes after the wind. In 1973, two garden spiders were taken to a space station, where nothing has weight. Arabella's first web was messy. Her next webs were much more like the webs spiders spin on Earth. So she seemed to change what she did, to suit a strange new place. Scientists agree on what spiders do. They argue about what goes on inside the spider while she spins.
- e.g. A spider chooses the corner for her web.
- e.g. A spider mends a hole in her web.
- e.g. Arabella, a spider in space, spun better webs after her first messy one.
- **Scientists who study spider minds:** An instinct gives the spider her know-how, and her mind uses that know-how. She senses the place, decides where to fix each thread and changes her plan when something goes wrong. Born know-how and thinking work together.
- **Scientists who call instinct automatic:** An instinct is a set of steps built into the body, and it runs the same way every time it is switched on. They prefer to say that the spider responds, not that she thinks.

## Mostly agreed
- Some actions animals can do from birth, with no teaching. Spiders spin webs, newborn calves stand, and human babies suck and grip. *How we know:* Spiderlings that hatch alone, away from any other spider, still spin the web shape of their own kind. Doctors check a newborn baby's grip and sucking on the first day.
- Many things are learned after birth, such as which foods are safe, where food can be found and which language to speak. *How we know:* Children speak the language they hear around them as babies, whatever language their parents' parents spoke. Crows and pigeons come to the places where people feed them every day.
- Many born-with actions get better with practice. *How we know:* Scientists who counted a chick's pecks found that newly hatched chicks miss many grains, and miss fewer after a few days of pecking. Elsie and Nicholas Collias, scientists in the United States, found in the 1960s that young male village weavers weave better with practice.

## How we found out
**Judith Miles, a high school student who suggested the test, with NASA and the astronaut Owen Garriott, who watched the spiders Arabella and Anita, 1973, on the Skylab space station.** In 1973, astronauts lived for weeks on Skylab, a space station going round the Earth. Judith Miles, a high school student from Lexington in the United States, asked a question. Could a spider spin her web in space, where nothing has weight? On Earth, a spider uses her own weight as she drops and swings on her threads. Many people expected the web-spinning would go wrong without it. NASA sent up two garden spiders, Arabella and Anita, and the astronaut Owen Garriott watched them. At first, Arabella spun a messy, uneven web. But her next webs looked much more like the round webs that spiders spin on Earth. Anita spun a fine web too. The spiders were born knowing how to spin. Arabella's later webs show that her spinning could change a little in a strange new place.  
*What people believed before:* That a spider's web-spinning is a fixed set of steps that would fail in a strange place with no weight. Arabella's first web was messy, but her next webs were much better.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A newborn baby knows nothing at all. Everything has to be learned.** (John Locke, an English thinker, in 1690, and many people since; *rejected*)  
  For: Babies cannot talk, walk or feed themselves, and almost everything a grown-up does was learned.  
  Against: Newborn babies already suck, grip, cry and turn to a touch on the cheek. Babies a few months old can hear the difference between sounds from many languages, which grown-ups often cannot. A baby is born ready to do some things and ready to learn others.
- **Something done by instinct is done perfectly the first time.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A spiderling spins a web of the right shape with no teacher, and a calf stands on his first day.  
  Against: A chick's first pecks miss many grains, and her aim gets better over a few days. Young weaver birds build messy nests at first. Arabella the spider spun a messy web in space before she spun better ones.
- **Other animals act by instinct, and only humans learn.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Humans learn a huge amount: languages, reading, cooking, music.  
  Against: Humans have instincts too, like sucking and gripping as babies, and reflexes like the knee kick. And other animals learn all the time. A crow learns which window gives rice, and a kitten learns to hunt from her mother.
- **When an animal acts by instinct, the animal is not thinking.** (Some scientists, for over a hundred years; *debated*)  
  For: An instinct can run in the same steps every time, even in an animal who has never seen the job done.  
  Against: Animals use their instincts with choices: a spider picks her web's place and mends holes, and Arabella changed her webs in space. Many scientists say instinct and thinking work together.

## Roots
We have met animals who are born knowing and animals who learn, and tried to stop our own knee kick. Now we ask what this tells us about who is in charge of a body.

**Deep truth.** When you tried hard not to kick, your leg often kicked anyway. Nobody ever taught you that kick, and trying could not switch it off. It was ready in your body on the day you were born. So knowing how to do something can come before any lesson.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think that knowing how to do something always starts with being taught. But some know-how is ready in the body at birth, like the knee kick and a spider's web, and this know-how works without any lesson.  
*Evidence:* In the stop test, the knee often kicked even when the child tried hard not to kick, and kicked biggest when the mind was busy pulling the fingers. Spiderlings spin the web of their own kind with no teacher.

- *who am i:* You are the one who decides, and you are also a body that knew how to grip, sneeze and kick before you ever decided anything.
- *what is life:* Every living being starts life already knowing some things, and then keeps learning. A spider, a calf and a baby all do both.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of know-how passed from parents to young, in every web on every balcony and every nest in every palm tree.

Every spider alive today got her web know-how in her genes from her parents, who got theirs from their parents. Nobody wrote it down, and no spider taught another. It travelled inside bodies, from one life to the next, for millions of years. So one small spider on a balcony grill carries the know-how of a long line of spiders before her. Your knee kick and your grip came to you the same way, down a long line of people and the animals before them.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son to split a tiny banyan seed. The son sees nothing inside. Uddalaka says the whole great banyan tree grows from that nothing you cannot see. (A spiderling's know-how to spin cannot be seen inside her either. Yet the whole round web comes out of her, the first time she spins.)
- **Tirukkural 391:** Thiruvalluvar says: learn well, without mistakes, what is worth learning. Then live by what you have learned. (Some things we are born with, like the knee kick. But most skills, like a kolam or a language, have to be learned well, through practice.)
- **Plato, Meno:** In this old Greek book, Socrates asks a boy who has never studied geometry a series of questions. The boy works out a hard answer. Socrates says that some knowing was already in him before any lesson. (Scientists today say that born-with know-how is passed on in genes from parents, not remembered from before. But Plato also noticed that we do not start with nothing.)
- Science (settled): Spiderlings spin the web of their own kind with no teacher, and newborn babies have reflexes such as gripping and sucking.
- Science (open): How genes carry know-how such as a web pattern, from parent to young, is not yet understood.
- Other view, John Locke, an English thinker, in 1690: Locke said a baby's mind at birth is like a blank sheet of white paper, and everything we know comes from what we see, hear and do. Many people still think most of what makes a person comes from learning, and in a way they are right: most of our skills are learned.
- Practice, Born-With Breath (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Notice your breath going in and out. Nobody taught you to breathe. You did it from your first minute. Now slow it down on purpose. Breathe in while I count to four. Breathe out while I count to four. Do this three times. Your breath came with you, and you can also guide it.
- Ask: If your leg kicks before you decide, who kicked: you, or your leg?
- Ask: Which matters more to who you are: what you were born with, or what you learned?
- Ask: Is a gift passed down to you from long ago still yours?

## Story: The Grip and the Web
*A made-up story. The facts in it are true: newborn babies grip a finger tight, the knee kick happens by itself at every age, and many spiders that spin round webs build them at dusk and rebuild them in the same place.*

Kayal lived on the third floor of a building in Visakhapatnam. She was seven. Her little brother Sabari was five. Their grandmother, Ammamma, lived with them.

One Sunday, Pinni came to visit. Pinni was their mother's younger sister. That is what a mother's younger sister is called in Telugu. She carried a bundle wrapped in a soft cotton towel. Inside was baby Nila, only two weeks old.

Kayal sat very still on the sofa, and Pinni laid Nila in her lap. Nila's fingers were tiny and pink. Kayal put one finger into Nila's open hand.

At once, the little fingers closed around hers. Nila held on tight. Kayal gently lifted her finger, and Nila still did not let go.

'She is so strong!' said Kayal. 'Who taught her to hold on like that?'

'Nobody,' said Pinni. 'Every baby is born able to grip. Nila also knew how to drink milk on her very first day. Doctors check for that grip when a baby is born.'

Kayal thought about this. Then she waved her hand slowly in front of Nila's face. 'Bye-bye, Nila. Wave bye-bye!'

Nila just looked at her with big dark eyes. Her hands stayed still.

'She cannot wave yet,' said Pinni, laughing. 'Waving is not something babies are born with. She will learn it in a year or so, by watching all of you.'

Sabari was jumping on the other end of the sofa. 'I can whistle!' he said, and he whistled a wobbly tune. 'Nobody taught me. I was born whistling.'

Ammamma laughed so much that her glasses slipped. 'Born whistling? You puffed and puffed on the balcony for two whole weeks. Nothing came out but air and spit.'

'Oh,' said Sabari. 'Then I learned it.'

'You learned it by practice,' said Ammamma. 'Some things we are born knowing. Other things we learn.'

That evening, after Pinni and Nila went home, Kayal went out to the balcony. The sky was turning orange. Between two bars of the balcony grill and the jasmine pot, something was moving.

There was a small brown spider. She was dropping down on a thin silk thread, then climbing back up and across. Kayal fetched Ammamma's torch and called everyone.

They watched without touching. First the spider made lines from the middle outwards, like the spokes of a cycle wheel. Then she walked round and round the spokes, laying down a spiral of thread. Round and round she went, the circles getting closer together.

'She has done it so neatly,' whispered Kayal. 'Did her mother teach her?'

'In many kinds of spider, the mother lays her eggs in a silk bag and goes away,' said Ammamma. 'The babies come out alone. Each little one spins her first web by herself, with nobody to copy.'

'So the spider was born knowing how to spin,' said Kayal. 'Like Nila was born knowing how to grip.'

The next morning, the web was gone. Only a few broken threads were left on the grill. But that evening, at the same time, the spider was back in the same place, spinning a new web.

'She does this every day?' asked Sabari.

'Many spiders like her spin a new web every evening,' said Ammamma.

Kayal watched for a long time. 'Ammamma, can she get better at it? Or is a born-with thing always the same?'

Ammamma did not answer. Instead she said, 'Sabari, sit up on the dining table. Let your legs hang down.'

Sabari climbed up. Ammamma felt for the soft spot just below his kneecap. She gave it a gentle tap with the side of her hand.

Sabari's leg kicked forward by itself. Sabari's mouth fell open. 'I did not do that!' he shouted. 'My leg did it!'

Everyone laughed. Then Kayal tapped Ammamma's knee, very gently. Ammamma's leg kicked too.

'Even you, Ammamma!' said Kayal.

'Even me, at seventy-one,' said Ammamma. 'Nila has this kick too. We were all born with it.'

Kayal looked at her own knee. 'My leg kicks without me deciding,' she said slowly. 'Just like Nila grips without deciding.'

She frowned. 'But it is my leg. If I try really, really hard, can I stop it from kicking?'

'What do you think?' asked Ammamma.

'I don't know,' said Kayal. 'But I can find out.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kayal notices two kinds of know-how side by side: the baby's grip, the spider's web and the knee kick, which nobody taught, and waving and whistling, which have to be learned. The story ends on a real question that the stop test will answer: can you stop a born-with action just by trying?

- Ask: What could baby Nila do that nobody had taught her?
- Ask: How did Ammamma know that Sabari was not born whistling?
- Ask: Who taught the spider to spin her web?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Stop Test: can you stop your knee kick by trying? (20 min)
- **question:** Can you stop a born-with action, like your knee kick, just by trying hard?
- **predict:** Guess first. When you try as hard as you can not to kick, will your leg still kick? And when you pull your hooked fingers hard, will the kick be smaller, the same or bigger? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same grown-up taps the same spot below the same knee, gently, with the side of the hand, 5 times in each way. You always look away, so that you cannot see the tap coming. We use 5 taps each time, because one tap can miss the spot.
- **safety:** Tap gently, with the side of the hand, on the soft spot just below the kneecap. Never tap the kneecap itself, and never hit hard. Stand to the side of the foot, because the leg kicks forward.
- **record:** Fill in the results table for the three ways. Draw your leg three times, with a short arrow for a small kick and a long arrow for a big kick.
- **results table:** Which way | Kicks (out of 5 taps) | How big: none, small or big
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a bed or table where your child can sit with feet hanging free. 2. Draw the results table on the paper. Your child can tell you what to write.  
  *Parent says:* Your knee has a born-with action. Nobody taught it to you. Today we find out if you are the boss of it.  
  *Child mode:* Sit where your feet hang free. Let your legs hang loose.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, and why. Write the guess on the paper.  
  *Parent says:* When I tap your knee and you try hard not to kick, will your leg still kick? And if you pull your fingers hard, what will the kick do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Can you stop the kick if you try? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: I can stop it, because it is my leg and I move it.; I cannot stop it, because the boy's leg in the story kicked by itself.; Pulling will make it smaller, because I will be busy.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine, as long as there is a reason. Do not tell your child what will happen.
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Way 1, relaxed: 1. Your child looks away and lets the leg hang loose. 2. Tap gently just below the kneecap, 5 times, a few seconds apart. 3. Count the kicks and say how big they were.  
  *Parent says:* Look away at the window. Let your leg hang like a wet towel. I will tap five times.  
  *Child mode:* Look away. Let your leg hang loose. Count the kicks with your grown-up.
  - Note: Feel for the soft spot just below the kneecap, in the middle. Use a quick, light tap, not a slow push. If nothing happens, move a finger-width up or down. A small kick moves the foot less than a finger's length. A big kick swings it further. Some people have only a small kick, which is normal. If one knee will not kick, try the other knee.
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Way 2, trying to stop: 1. Your child looks away and tries as hard as they can not to kick. 2. Tap 5 times. 3. Count the kicks and say how big they were.  
  *Parent says:* Now try as hard as you can to keep your leg still. Don't let it kick! I will tap five times.  
  *Child mode:* Look away. Try your hardest not to kick. Count the kicks.
  - Note: Many children still kick on some taps, though the kick may be smaller. Some can nearly stop it by making the leg stiff. If that happens, ask: did you switch the kick off, or did you hold your leg stiff against it?
- **s5 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Way 3, pulling: 1. Your child hooks the fingers of both hands together in front of the chest. 2. Just before each tap, say 'Pull!' and they pull their hands apart hard. 3. Tap 5 times. 4. Count the kicks and say how big they were.  
  *Parent says:* Hook your fingers together like this. When I say 'pull', pull your hands apart hard, and keep looking away.  
  *Child mode:* Hook your fingers. When you hear 'pull', pull hard. Count the kicks.
  - Ask: What did your leg do this time? (good: It kicked more.; It kicked bigger, and I was not even thinking about my leg.)
  - Note: Pulling the hooked fingers usually makes the kick bigger. Doctors use this trick when a kick is hard to see. It works because the busy arms stop you from holding your leg stiff.
- **s6 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child tap your knee 5 times, gently, while you relax and look away.  
  *Parent says:* Now you tap my knee. Gently, on the soft spot. Does my leg kick too?  
  *Child mode:* Now tap your grown-up's knee. Gently! Does their leg kick?
  - Note: Most grown-ups kick too, at any age. The kick is the same kind of action in a baby, a child and an old person.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what your child thinks, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Could you stop the kick by trying? When was it biggest? Why do you think that happened?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Could you stop the kick? When was it biggest?
  - Ask: Why is the kick so hard to stop? (good: It happens before I decide anything.; The message goes from my knee to my backbone and back, without asking me.; I was born with it, so it works by itself.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the numbers: 'Relaxed, I kicked 5 times. Trying to stop, I still kicked 3 times, but small. Pulling, I kicked 5 big kicks.' Now read the card on reflexes.
  - Check: In the stop test, what usually happens when you try hard not to kick? → **The leg often still kicks, maybe smaller**

### a1 · Web Watch: a web that nobody taught (if you find a web) (15 min)
- **safety:** Look, but do not touch the spider or break the web.
- **record:** A drawing of the web, with the spider drawn where you found her.

  *Child mode:* Look in corners and on the balcony. Shine the torch from the side.
  - Note: Most flats have at least one web in a corner. House spiders often spin messy tangle webs. Spiders that spin round webs are more often on balconies and in plants, and many spin at dusk. If you find no web, try again at dusk tomorrow, when round webs are spun.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child draws the web. Look for spokes, a spiral, or a tangle.

### a2 · A Newborn's Grip (only if there is a baby under three months in the family) (10 min)
- **safety:** Wash your hands with soap first. Only do this with the baby's parent there, and when the baby is calm and awake. Never pull or lift the baby by the grip.
- **record:** Draw the baby's hand around your finger. Write how long the baby held on.

  *Child mode:* Put one finger in the baby's palm. Wait. What happens?
  - Ask: Did the baby decide to hold on? (good: No, the hand closed by itself.; Maybe. It happened so fast.)
  - Note: In a baby under about three months, the fingers usually close around the finger at once. This grip fades by about four to six months, and then the baby learns to reach and hold on purpose.
- **s3 (3 min)**

  *Parent says:* Nobody taught the baby to grip. Who else is born knowing how to do something?  
  *Child mode:* Who taught the baby to grip? What else are babies born knowing?

### a3 · Born Ready for Any Language: can you say 'zh'? (15 min)
- **record:** A table: each person's name, the language they heard as a baby, and whether the 'zh' sound was easy, a bit hard or very hard. A grown-up can write the names.

  *Child mode:* Listen. 'Tamizh'. 'Mazhai'. Can you hear the 'zh' sound?
  - Note: The sound is made by curling the tip of the tongue up and back, and letting the sound flow without touching. If nobody at home speaks Tamil or Malayalam, many free videos and dictionary apps say these words aloud.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child tries the words five times.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn. Curl your tongue up and back. Try 'mazhai' five times. Is it easy or hard?  
  *Child mode:* Curl your tongue up and back. Say 'mazhai' five times.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask three people you know, family or friends, to try. For each one, write down the language they heard as a baby, and whether 'zh' was easy.  
  *Parent says:* Let's ask three people. What language did each of them hear as a baby? Was the sound easy for them?  
  *Child mode:* Ask three people to try. Which language did they hear as a baby?
  - Note: Write the table for your child. Anyone who speaks Tamil or Malayalam will find it easy. Others may find it hard, and that is fine.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together.  
  *Parent says:* Who found it easy? Did they hear Tamil or Malayalam as babies? Were they born knowing the sound, or did they learn it?  
  *Child mode:* Who found it easy? Did they learn the sound as a baby?
  - Note: Babies a few months old can hear the difference between sounds from many languages. By about one year, they hear best the sounds of the language around them. Scientists like Janet Werker found this.
  - Check: A baby is born in Bengaluru to a family who speak Malayalam at home. Which sounds will she learn to say easily? → **The sounds of Malayalam, the language she hears every day**

## Challenges
### c1 · Beak Weaving: weave like a baya (25 min)
- **problem:** Can you weave a small mat from paper strips, with one hand as a beak, that stays together when you lift it by the pencil and holds 10 chana lying on it?
- **your turn:** Weave your mat. Lay it flat and put 10 chana on it. Do any fall through? Then weave a second mat. Is it better than your first? What did you learn from the first try?
- **constraints:** Weave with only the thumb and first finger of one hand. Your other hand may only hold the pencil, like a branch.; Use 24 paper strips, each about as wide as your finger and as long as a ruler. Twelve are for the first mat and twelve for the second.; No glue and no tape.; Fold 6 strips over the pencil so they hang down. Weave the other 6 across them, over and under.
- **general:** Lay a strip across the hanging strips, over one and under the next, like a mat.; Push each new strip up close to the one before, so there are no big gaps.; Tuck the ends in tightly, so the weave does not come apart.
- **creative:** Twist each strip into a thin rope first, so it is stronger and easier to tuck.; Tie a knot by looping a strip around itself, the way a weaver bird knots his first strips onto a branch.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the challenge together. Talk about how a baya weaves his nest with only his beak and feet.  
  *Parent says:* A baya weaves a hanging nest with only his beak. Can you weave a mat with only two fingers of one hand?  
  *Child mode:* Weave a mat with only two fingers. Can it hold 10 chana?
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts the strips and folds the first 6 over the pencil. Your child weaves the first mat. Help only with the cutting and folding.  
  *Parent says:* Where will you start? How will you keep the strips from coming apart?  
  *Child mode:* Weave your first mat. Use only two fingers.
  - Note: Let your child try their own way first, even if it falls apart. A first try that fails teaches the most for the second try.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the first mat by the pencil. Does it stay together? 2. Lay it flat and put 10 chana on it. 3. Weave a second mat. 4. Test it too.  
  *Parent says:* How many chana stayed on? Now weave a second one. What will you do differently?  
  *Child mode:* Test it. Then weave a second mat. Is it better?
  - Ask: Why was your second mat better? (good: I learned to tuck the ends in tight.; I knew where to start this time.; My fingers got better at it.)
  - Check: A young weaver bird's first nests are often untidy. Why are his later nests better? → **He gets better with practice**

   Options: Weaving is all instinct / Weaving is all learned / He is born ready to weave, and gets better with practice

9. (mcq) Which of these is a reflex?  
   Options: Blinking when a ball flies at your face / Brushing your teeth / Drawing a kolam  
   **Answer:** Blinking when a ball flies at your face. *Why:* Blinking when something flies at your eye happens by itself. Brushing teeth and drawing a kolam are learned.
10. (mcq) Do humans have instincts?  
   Options: No, only other animals do / Yes. Babies are born able to suck, grip and cry / Only grown-ups do  
   **Answer:** Yes. Babies are born able to suck, grip and cry. *Why:* Humans are animals, and human babies are born with instincts like sucking, gripping and crying.

## Applications
- Doctors and nurses check a newborn baby's grip, sucking and other reflexes in the first days, to see that the baby is healthy.
- Doctors tap the knee with a small rubber hammer to check that the nerves in the leg and backbone are working.
- Volunteers in Chennai and Odisha guard olive ridley turtle nests and ask for beach lights to be dimmed, so that hatchlings follow their instinct to the sea.
- Teachers of music, dance and sport know that skills improve with short practice every day, which is why classes meet often.
- Engineers study how spiders spin silk and build webs, to design light, strong nets and threads.

## Open questions
- **How do genes carry the know-how to spin a web of the right shape?** Scientists know that each kind of spider spins her own kind of web, and that this is passed on from parents. Nobody knows how tiny instructions inside cells become the steps of spinning a web.
- **What does a spider sense and think while she spins?** Scientists can watch every step a spider takes. What it is like to be a spider spinning a web, and how much she plans, are still argued about.
- **How much of what a person is like comes from birth, and how much is learned?** Scientists agree that both matter, and that they work together. Exactly how much each matters for any one thing, such as being good at music, is still argued about.

## Connected maths
- Four people in a family did the stop test while trying not to kick. Their kicks out of 5 were 3, 4, 2 and 5. Who kicked most? How many kicks in all? **The person with 5 kicked most. There were 14 kicks in all.**. 5 is the biggest number. 3 and 4 make 7, and 2 more makes 9, and 5 more makes 14. Out of 20 taps, the legs kicked 14 times, even with everyone trying to stop. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- A spider has 8 legs. Three spiderlings come out of an egg bag. How many legs do they have in all? **24 legs.**. There are 3 groups of 8 legs. 8 and 8 make 16, and 8 more makes 24. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- A girl counts lights from her window. Night 1: 12 lights. Night 2: 12 lights. Night 3: 9 lights. How many fewer lights on night 3 than night 1? **3 fewer.**. Count up from 9 to 12: 10, 11, 12. That is 3. Maybe three shops closed early. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- In the Turtle Run game, 4 hatchlings crawl to the sea when the street light is off. When it is on, only 1 reaches the sea. How many fewer reach the sea with the light on? **3 fewer.**. 4 take away 1 is 3. The light led 3 hatchlings the wrong way. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
