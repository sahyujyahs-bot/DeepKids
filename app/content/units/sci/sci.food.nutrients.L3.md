# Nutrients: what food is made of, and what each part does
`sci.food.nutrients.L3`

**Summary.** Pour lemon juice into hot milk, and it splits into white lumps and a pale, watery liquid. Milk was never one thing. Like almost every food, it is a mix of nutrients: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water. Each nutrient does a different job in your body. Food groups sort foods by the nutrient they give most, but most foods carry several.

**Intro.** *Your school chart puts dal in the 'protein' box. But if you could pull apart a spoon of dry toor dal, more than half of it would be carbohydrate. So what is a 'protein food'? And what is inside the food we eat?* Every food is made of a few kinds of nutrients. A nutrient is a part of food that your body uses to live, grow and move. In this unit you split milk into its parts, dry vegetables to find their water, and look for minerals hiding in clear water. Then you will know what the food group boxes really mean.

## Terms
- **Nutrient**: A part of food that the body uses to live, grow, move and mend itself. There are six kinds: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water. *e.g.* A glass of milk carries five kinds of nutrient at once: water, protein, fat, milk sugar and minerals, plus some vitamins.
- **Food group**: A box we sort foods into, by the nutrient they give most or the job they mostly do. Different books use different boxes. *e.g.* Rice goes in the carbohydrate group, because most of a grain of rice is starch.
- **Carbohydrate**: The nutrient your body uses most for energy. Sugars, starch and fibre are all carbohydrates. *e.g.* Idli, rice, ragi, chapati and banana are rich in carbohydrates.
- **Starch**: A carbohydrate made of many sugar pieces joined in a long chain. Plants store food as starch. *e.g.* Rice, potato and atta are mostly starch. Chew rice for a long time and it starts to taste a little sweet, as your spit breaks the starch into sugar.
- **Fibre**: A carbohydrate from plants that your body does not break down. It keeps food moving through your gut, and many of your gut microbes feed on it. *e.g.* Whole dal, green vegetables, fruit with its skin and whole grains have lots of fibre.
- **Protein**: The nutrient your body uses to build and mend itself. Your muscles, hair, nails and skin are made mostly of protein. *e.g.* Dal, milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fish, peanuts and chana all give protein.
- **Amino acid**: One of the small pieces that a protein is built from. There are 20 kinds, joined in long chains. *e.g.* Your body breaks the protein in dal into amino acids. Then it joins them again into your own proteins.
- **Fat**: A nutrient that stores a lot of energy. It also builds the thin skin around every cell and helps the body take in some vitamins. *e.g.* Ghee, coconut oil, groundnut oil, peanuts and coconut are rich in fat.
- **Vitamin**: A nutrient the body needs in very small amounts to stay healthy. Each vitamin has its own jobs. They are named by letters: A, B, C, D, E and K. *e.g.* Amla and guava have lots of vitamin C, which helps cuts heal.
- **Mineral**: A nutrient that comes from rocks and soil, through plants, animals and water. The body needs small amounts. Calcium, iron and iodine are minerals. *e.g.* The white rings that dry on a steel tumbler after you drink tap water are minerals left behind.
- **Calcium**: A mineral that makes bones and teeth hard. *e.g.* Ragi, milk, curd, til and green leaves give calcium.
- **Iron**: A mineral that your blood uses to carry oxygen around the body. *e.g.* Green leafy vegetables, dal, ragi, eggs, fish and meat give iron.
- **Curdle**: When milk splits into soft white lumps and a watery liquid. An acid, like lemon juice, makes milk curdle. *e.g.* Lemon juice stirred into hot milk curdles it. This is how paneer is made.
- **Curds and whey**: When milk curdles, the white lumps are the curds. The pale, watery liquid left behind is the whey. *e.g.* Strain curdled milk through a cotton cloth. The curds stay in the cloth and become paneer. The whey drips through.
- **Energy**: What your body uses to move, think, grow and keep warm. Your body gets it from carbohydrates, fats and proteins. *e.g.* Running round the terrace uses energy that came from the idli you ate for breakfast.
- **Dehydrated**: When the body has lost more water than it has taken in. You may feel thirsty, tired, or get a headache. *e.g.* After playing in the hot sun without drinking, you can become dehydrated.

## Explanations
### What a nutrient is · *defined*
**Definition:** A nutrient is a part of food that the body uses to live, grow, move and mend itself. Scientists sort nutrients into six kinds: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water.

Your body takes food apart into its nutrients. It burns some of them for energy. It uses others to build new parts, like muscle, bone and blood. Your body needs large amounts of carbohydrates, proteins, fats and water every day. It needs only tiny amounts of vitamins and minerals, but it cannot stay healthy without them.
- e.g. A plate of curd rice gives carbohydrate from the rice, and protein, fat, calcium and water from the curd.
- e.g. A guava gives water, sugar, fibre and a lot of vitamin C.
### Food groups: boxes to sort food · *defined*
**Definition:** A food group is a box of foods that give mostly the same nutrient, or do mostly the same job in the body.

Food groups help families plan meals. Different books use different boxes. Many Indian school books use three: energy-giving foods, body-building foods and protective foods. The Cambridge book uses five: carbohydrates, fruit and vegetables, protein, dairy, and fats and sugars. All of these are ways of sorting. A food is put in the box of the nutrient it gives most, or that it is best known for. But most foods carry several nutrients, so one food can belong in two or three boxes.
- e.g. Milk is in the dairy group. It also gives protein, fat and calcium, so it could go in the protein box too.
- e.g. Peanuts are often called a protein food. But more than a third of a peanut is fat.
### Most foods are a mix of nutrients · *defined*
**Definition:** Almost every food from a plant or an animal contains several nutrients at once. Only a few, like sugar, salt and oil, are nearly one nutrient.

Scientists find out what is in a food by taking it apart in a laboratory. In India, the National Institute of Nutrition in Hyderabad has done this for hundreds of Indian foods. Their numbers surprise most people. Dry toor dal is about one-fifth protein, but more than half carbohydrate. Cooked rice is about two-thirds water. Milk is almost nine-tenths water. A cucumber is nearly all water.
- e.g. Out of every 10 spoons of dry toor dal: about 6 spoons carbohydrate, 2 protein, 1 fibre and 1 water.
- e.g. Out of every 10 spoons of milk: about 9 spoons water. The last spoon is shared by milk sugar, fat, protein and minerals.
### Carbohydrates: the body's main fuel · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbohydrates are sugars, starch and fibre. Your body breaks starch and sugars down into a sugar called glucose, and burns glucose for energy.

Starch is a long chain of sugar pieces joined together. Plants make starch to store food in their grains, roots and seeds. When you eat idli or rice, your body cuts the chain back into sugar. Your blood carries this sugar to every cell. Your brain runs mostly on glucose. Fibre is a carbohydrate too, but your body does not break it down. It keeps food moving through your gut, and many of the microbes living there feed on it.
- e.g. A runner eats rice or bananas before a race, because they give energy quickly.
- e.g. Ragi, rice, wheat, millets, potatoes and sweet potatoes are all rich in starch.
### Proteins: building and mending the body · *defined*
**Definition:** Proteins are long chains of small pieces called amino acids. Your body uses them to build and mend itself.

Your muscles, skin, hair and nails are made mostly of protein. So are the tiny workers inside every cell that break food down. When you eat dal or curd, your body takes their proteins apart into amino acids. Then it joins those amino acids into its own proteins. Children need protein every day, because they are growing. In milk, most of the protein is one called casein. When milk becomes acidic, casein clumps together. That is why lemon juice makes milk curdle.
- e.g. When you scrape your knee, new skin grows over it. That new skin is built from protein.
- e.g. Rice and dal eaten together give a better mix of amino acids than either one alone.
### Fats: stored energy and the skin of every cell · *defined*
**Definition:** Fats are nutrients that store a lot of energy. A spoon of fat gives about twice as much energy as a spoon of sugar or starch.

Your body stores spare energy as fat, under the skin and around the organs. Fat also builds the thin skin around every cell. And some vitamins, A, D, E and K, mix into fat and not into water. So the body takes them in better when a meal has a little fat. Fats that are liquid at room temperature are called oils.
- e.g. A carrot poriyal cooked in a little coconut oil helps your body take in the carrot's vitamin A.
- e.g. Ghee and coconut oil go solid on a cold morning and melt on a hot day.
### Vitamins: tiny amounts, big jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Vitamins are nutrients the body needs only in tiny amounts. Each one has its own jobs, and missing one for a long time makes a person ill.

Vitamin A helps your eyes see in dim light. It is in carrots, mangoes, papaya and green leaves. The B vitamins help your cells get energy out of food. They are in whole grains, dal, milk and eggs. Vitamin C helps cuts heal and keeps gums healthy. It is in amla, guava, lemon and oranges. Vitamin D helps your body use calcium. Your skin makes it when sunlight falls on it.
- e.g. Whey, the liquid left when you make paneer, is pale yellow-green partly because of vitamin B2 from the milk.
- e.g. Ten to twenty minutes of play in the morning sun helps your skin make vitamin D.
### Minerals: from rocks to your bones · *defined*
**Definition:** Minerals are nutrients that come from rocks and soil. Plants take them up from the soil with water. We get them from plants, from animals that ate plants, and from water.

Calcium makes bones and teeth hard. Ragi, milk, curd, til and green leaves give calcium. Iron is used by blood to carry oxygen. Green leaves, dal, ragi, eggs and fish give iron. Iodine helps the body grow and the brain develop. India adds iodine to most salt for this reason. Many minerals dissolve in water. That is why tap water can leave white rings when it dries on a steel tumbler.
- e.g. 100 grams of ragi flour has nearly three times as much calcium as 100 millilitres of milk.
- e.g. Iron from green leaves is taken in better when the meal also has vitamin C, like a squeeze of lemon.
### Water: the nutrient you need most of · *defined*
**Definition:** Water is a nutrient. More than half of your body is water, and you need more of it each day than any other nutrient.

Your blood is mostly water. It carries food and oxygen to your cells. Water in sweat cools you on a hot day. Water carries waste out of your body in urine. You lose water all day, in your breath, sweat and urine, so you must keep putting it back. A person can live for weeks without food, but only a few days without water. Much of your water comes from food. Rasam, curd rice, buttermilk, watermelon and cucumber are mostly water.
- e.g. Breathe onto a cold steel tumbler. The mist that forms is water from your own body.
- e.g. Pale yellow urine usually means you have had enough to drink. Dark yellow means drink more.
### Is fat bad for you? · *debated*
For about 40 years, many doctors told people to eat as little fat as possible, to keep their hearts healthy. Today most scientists agree the body needs some fat, and the kind of fat matters. They still argue about some fats, like ghee and coconut oil. Most agree that trans fats, found in some reused frying oils and some packaged snacks, are harmful.
- e.g. Peanuts, til and fish give fats that most scientists agree are good for the heart.
- e.g. Oil used again and again for frying pakoras can form harmful fats.
- **Many heart doctors and health guidelines:** Eat some fat, mostly from plant oils, nuts, seeds and fish. Keep ghee, butter and coconut oil to small amounts, because they may raise the kind of fat in the blood that clogs arteries.
- **Some other scientists:** The evidence against ghee and coconut oil is weaker than people think. What matters more is the whole diet: too much sugar and too many fried, packaged foods.

## Mostly agreed
- The body needs six kinds of nutrient: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water. *How we know:* When people go without one of them for a long time, they fall ill in a way that is typical for that nutrient. Without vitamin C, gums bleed. Without iodine, the neck can swell. Giving back the missing nutrient makes them well.
- Most foods contain several nutrients at once. *How we know:* Food scientists have taken thousands of foods apart in laboratories and measured what is in them. In India, the National Institute of Nutrition in Hyderabad publishes these numbers for Indian foods.
- Muscles get most of their energy from carbohydrates and fats. Protein is used mostly to build and mend the body. *How we know:* In 1865, Fick and Wislicenus measured how much protein their bodies broke down while climbing a mountain. It was far too little to give the energy they used. Many experiments since then agree.
- Proteins are built from amino acids, and the body builds its own proteins from the amino acids in food. *How we know:* Scientists can follow labelled amino acids from a meal into the proteins of muscle and blood.
- Milk curdles when it becomes acidic, because its main protein, casein, clumps together. *How we know:* Anyone can see it at home with lemon juice. Dairy scientists measure the exact acidity at which casein clumps.

## How we found out
**Adolf Fick, a physiologist, and Johannes Wislicenus, a chemist, both professors in Zurich, August 1865.** In the 1840s, Justus von Liebig, a famous German chemist, taught that working muscles burn up their own protein. So people thought protein foods were the main fuel for hard work. In 1865 two professors in Zurich, Adolf Fick and Johannes Wislicenus, decided to test this on themselves. Protein is the only nutrient in food with a lot of nitrogen in it. When the body breaks protein down, that nitrogen leaves in urine. So by measuring the nitrogen in their urine, they could work out how much protein their bodies had used. In August 1865 they climbed the Faulhorn, a mountain in Switzerland, nearly 2,000 metres up from the lake below. Before and during the climb they ate no protein, only food made of starch, fat and sugar. They collected their urine and measured its nitrogen. The protein they had used could give only a small part of the energy needed to lift their bodies up the mountain. The rest of the energy had to come from starch, fat and sugar. Liebig was wrong. Muscles run mainly on carbohydrates and fats. Protein is used mostly to build and mend the body.  
*What people believed before:* Justus von Liebig taught that working muscles burn their own protein, so protein is the main fuel for work. The Faulhorn climb showed that protein gave only a small part of the energy. Most of it came from carbohydrates and fats.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Dal is a protein food, so it is mostly protein.** (A common idea, from food group charts; *rejected*)  
  For: Dal is one of the best sources of protein for vegetarian families. It has much more protein than rice or wheat.  
  Against: Measured in a laboratory, dry toor dal is about one-fifth protein and more than half carbohydrate. It is in the protein box because it gives more protein than most plant foods, not because it is mostly protein.
- **Muscles burn their own protein to do work, so hard workers need mostly protein.** (Justus von Liebig, a famous German chemist, in the 1840s; *rejected*)  
  For: Muscles are made of protein, so it seemed natural that working muscles use up protein.  
  Against: Fick and Wislicenus climbed a mountain in 1865 and measured the protein their bodies broke down. It could supply only a small part of the energy they used. The rest came from carbohydrates and fats.
- **Water is not food, so it is not a nutrient.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: Water has no energy in it and does not fill you up like a meal.  
  Against: Your body uses water in every cell, in blood, sweat and urine. You need more of it each day than any other nutrient, and you would fall ill within days without it. So scientists count it as a nutrient.
- **Whey is waste and should be thrown away after making paneer.** (Many home cooks; *rejected*)  
  For: The paneer is what you wanted. The whey looks thin and watery.  
  Against: Whey holds milk sugar, minerals, some vitamins and a little protein. Many cooks use it to knead chapati dough or to cook dal or rice.
- **The more vitamins you take, the healthier you are.** (Some adverts for vitamin tablets; *rejected*)  
  For: Vitamins are needed for health, and a child short of a vitamin does get better with more of it.  
  Against: The body needs only small amounts. Most children who eat a mix of foods get enough. Too much of some vitamins, like A and D, can make a person ill. Doctors give vitamin tablets when a test shows a child needs them.

## Roots
We have learned that food is made of six kinds of nutrient, and that most foods carry several at once. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Lemon juice split your hot milk into white curds and pale, watery whey. The curds held most of the protein and fat. The whey kept the water, the milk sugar and some minerals. So one glass of milk carries many nutrients, and its food group names only one of them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort foods into boxes: dal is 'protein', rice is 'carbohydrate', milk is 'dairy'. The box makes each food sound like one thing. But a box is a label for planning meals, and most foods are mixes that the label leaves out.  
*Evidence:* In the milk-splitting test, lemon juice split milk into curds and much more whey. Dried on a plate, the whey left a sticky film of milk sugar and minerals. Measured in a laboratory, dry toor dal is about one-fifth protein and more than half carbohydrate.

- *who am i:* Your muscles, hair and nails are built from protein you ate, taken apart into amino acids and joined again.
- *what is life:* A cow makes milk for her calf, with water, protein, fat, sugar and minerals mixed in one drink.
- *what is this world:* Sugar, oil and paneer look completely different, yet they are built mostly from the same three kinds of atoms.

Sugar, starch, fat and protein look and taste very different. Yet all of them are built mostly from three kinds of atoms: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with nitrogen added in protein. Joined one way, these atoms make sweet sugar. Joined another way, they make oil, or the white curds of paneer. Inside you, they are taken apart and joined again into muscle, blood and the skin of your cells. A few kinds of atoms, joined in many ways, make every food and every body.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2.1:** Bhrigu came to understand that food is Brahman. From food all beings are born. By food, once born, they live. Into food they return. (The protein in the dal you ate yesterday is being built into your muscles today. Your body really is made from food, and goes on being remade from it.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.5.1:** Food, when eaten, is divided into three. Its coarsest part becomes waste. Its middle part becomes flesh. Its finest part becomes mind. (The sage saw that food splits into parts with different jobs. Science sorts the parts differently, into nutrients, but it also finds that each part of a food goes to a different use in the body.)
- **Lucretius, On the Nature of Things, Book 1:** The same first particles make the sky, the sea, the land and living things. They differ only in how they are mixed and arranged, as the same letters make many different words. (Sugar, fat and protein are made from the same few kinds of atoms. How the atoms are joined decides whether you get sweet sugar or paneer.)
- Science (settled): Carbohydrates, fats and proteins are built mostly from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Proteins also contain nitrogen.
- Science (settled): The body takes the proteins in food apart into amino acids, and builds its own proteins from them.
- Science (debated): Exactly how much protein a growing child needs is still studied, and countries set slightly different numbers.
- Other view, Many nutrition scientists and teachers: Food groups are a useful shortcut, not a mistake. A parent planning a meal needs a quick rule, not a chemistry table. Calling dal a protein food is fair, because for vegetarian families it is one of the best sources of protein.

## Story: The Green-Yellow Water
*Kavya, her aunt and her brother are made up. The science is true: lemon juice curdles hot milk, the curds hold most of the protein and fat, and whey is mostly water with milk sugar, minerals and some vitamins. Its yellow-green colour comes partly from vitamin B2 (riboflavin).*

Kavya was nine, and she lived in Madurai, in a flat on the third floor. On Friday her teacher gave the class a job for the weekend. 'Make a food group chart,' she said. 'Draw five boxes. Put ten foods in the right boxes.'

On Saturday morning Kavya sat at the dining table with a big sheet of paper. She drew five boxes: carbohydrates, fruit and vegetables, protein, dairy, and fats and sugars. Her little brother Arjun, who was six, watched her and ate a banana.

'Rice goes in carbohydrates,' Kavya said, and drew a heap of rice. 'Banana goes in fruit.' Arjun held up his banana skin. 'Dal goes in protein. Ghee goes in fats.' She drew a glass of milk in the dairy box and sat back. Ten foods, five boxes. It was easy.

Her aunt, Revathi Athai, came in from the kitchen. Athai worked in a laboratory at a big dairy, where she tested milk all day. She was staying with them for Arjun's birthday, and she was making paneer for the birthday dinner.

Athai looked at the chart for a long time. 'Very neat,' she said. 'But I have a question. Why is the milk only in the dairy box?'

'Because milk is dairy,' said Kavya. 'Dairy means things made from milk.'

'True,' said Athai. 'But what is milk made of? Come and see.'

In the kitchen a big pan of milk was on the stove. Small bubbles were forming at the edges, and steam was rising. Athai turned off the flame. She squeezed two lemons into a small cup. 'Stand back a little, because the milk is very hot,' she said. Then she poured the lemon juice into the pan and stirred gently with a big spoon.

Kavya watched. For a moment nothing happened. Then soft white lumps began to form, all through the milk. They grew bigger and joined together. Around them, the milk was turning into a thin, clear liquid. It was not white any more. It was a pale green-yellow.

'Where did the milk go?' asked Arjun, who had come to watch.

'It is all still here,' said Athai. 'It has only split into two parts.' She laid a thin cotton cloth over a steel strainer, set it on a big bowl and poured the pan out. The white lumps stayed in the cloth. The green-yellow liquid ran through into the bowl.

'The lumps are called curds,' said Athai. 'They hold most of the milk's protein, with most of its fat caught inside, and some water. When I press them, they become paneer. The liquid is called whey. It is mostly water, but it also carries the milk's sugar, some minerals and some vitamins. One of those vitamins, B2, is part of why it looks green-yellow.'

Kavya looked into the bowl. There was much more whey than curd. 'So milk is mostly water?'

'Almost nine-tenths water,' said Athai. 'The other tenth is protein, fat, milk sugar and minerals, all mixed up so finely that the milk looks like one thing. The lemon juice made the protein clump together, so now we can see them apart.'

Kavya picked up the bowl of whey to pour it into the sink. 'Wait!' said Athai. 'That is not waste. Let me show you.' She took a clean steel plate, dropped two spoons of whey onto it and put it on the balcony in the sun. 'Look at it tomorrow,' she said.

That afternoon Athai kneaded the chapati dough with whey instead of water. At dinner, Arjun ate four chapatis and three pieces of paneer, and said it was the best birthday food ever.

On Sunday morning Kavya ran to the balcony. The whey on the plate had dried. The water had gone into the air. Left behind was a thin, sticky, yellowish film. She touched it with a finger. It was a little sticky, like the inside of a sweet wrapper.

'That is the milk sugar and the minerals,' said Athai. 'The water went into the air, and they stayed.'

Kavya went back to her chart. She looked at the glass of milk in the dairy box. Then she drew a dotted line from the milk to the protein box, and another to the fats box. She wrote next to it: 'Milk is mostly water. It also has protein, fat, milk sugar and minerals.'

Then she looked at the dal. 'Athai, is dal only protein?'

Athai smiled. 'What do you think? We could look it up in the book of Indian foods.'

They looked it up together. Dry toor dal was about one-fifth protein, and more than half carbohydrate. Kavya drew another dotted line, from the dal to the carbohydrate box.

By lunchtime her chart had dotted lines going everywhere. Peanuts went to protein and fats. Ragi went to carbohydrates, and had a note: 'lots of calcium'. Curd went to dairy, protein and fats. Arjun looked at it and frowned. 'It is messy now.'

'It is true now,' said Kavya. 'A box only says what a food gives the most. It does not say everything that is in it.' She showed Arjun the dried whey on the plate. 'See? Milk has lots of things hiding in it. You only see them when you split it apart.'

Arjun licked his finger, touched the sticky film and tasted it. 'Sweet,' he said. 'A little bit.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: a food group names the nutrient a food is best known for, but almost every food is a mix. Splitting milk with lemon juice shows this. The curds hold most of the protein and fat, and the whey holds water, milk sugar, minerals and some vitamins.

- Ask: Why did the milk split into two parts when Athai poured in the lemon juice?
- Ask: What did Athai say the curds were mostly made of? And the whey?
- Ask: What was left on the plate when the whey dried in the sun? Where had the water gone?
- Ask: Why did Kavya draw dotted lines on her chart? Do you think the chart was better or worse afterwards?
- Ask: Which food in your kitchen would you like to split apart, to see what is inside?

### e1 · Splitting Milk: which kitchen liquids curdle it? (35 min)
- **question:** Which liquids from our kitchen make hot milk split into curds and whey, and which do not?
- **predict:** Look at the six cups of liquid: lemon juice, vinegar, tamarind water, salt water, sugar water and plain water. Which ones do you think will make milk split into lumps? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same amount of milk in every tumbler, half a cup, all from the same pan. Add the same amount of each liquid, 1 tablespoon. Stir each one the same way, five slow stirs, and wait the same time, 3 minutes. Only the liquid changes. Plain water is the comparison, so we can see what milk does with nothing special added.
- **safety:** Hot milk can scald. A grown-up heats the milk and pours it. The child adds the liquids with a spoon only after the grown-up says the tumblers are cool enough to touch on the outside. Never taste vinegar straight from the bottle.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the lemon tumbler after 3 minutes and label the curds and the whey. Then write: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Liquid added | My guess: will it curdle? (yes or no) | After 3 minutes: lumps? (none, a few, many) | Colour of the liquid around the lumps
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the six tumblers in a row. 2. Put a paper label in front of each: lemon, vinegar, tamarind, salt, sugar, water. 3. Put 1 tablespoon of each liquid in a small cup beside its label.  
  *Parent says:* Milk looks like one smooth white liquid. Today we find out if it is really one thing. Look at these six liquids. Which ones do you think will make milk split into lumps? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put six tumblers in a row. Label them: lemon, vinegar, tamarind, salt, sugar, water. Which ones will make milk go lumpy? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that one? (good: Lemon makes things sour, and sour things spoil milk.; Salt water is just water, so nothing will happen.; I have seen milk go lumpy when it goes bad.)
  - Note: Write down the guesses in the table before you start. Any guess with a reason is good. Many children think salt will curdle milk, because salt 'does things' in cooking. Don't correct the guesses now. The test will answer them.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Heat the milk in a pan until it steams and tiny bubbles form at the edge. Do not let it boil over. 2. Turn off the flame. 3. Pour half a cup into each of the six tumblers. 4. Wait 2 minutes for the tumblers to cool a little.  
  *Parent says:* I am heating the milk, because hot milk splits much faster than cold milk. Stay back from the stove while I pour.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up heats the milk and pours it. Stand back. Hot milk can burn you. Wait until they say it is safe.
  - Note: Hot milk works best, about as hot as tea you would wait to sip. Cold milk also curdles with lemon juice, but slowly and with tiny lumps that are hard to see. Feel the outside of each tumbler before your child touches it.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add 1 tablespoon of lemon juice to the lemon tumbler. 2. Stir slowly five times. 3. Do the same for each liquid in its own tumbler, with a rinsed spoon each time. 4. Start a 3-minute timer after the last one.  
  *Parent says:* Add one liquid to each tumbler. Five slow stirs. Rinse the spoon each time, so no lemon gets into the next cup.  
  *Child mode:* Add one spoon of liquid to its tumbler. Stir five times, slowly. Rinse the spoon. Do the next one.
  - Note: Rinsing the spoon matters. A drop of lemon in the sugar tumbler could start it curdling and spoil the test.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 3 minutes, tilt each tumbler gently towards the light. 2. Look for white lumps, and for a thin, clear liquid around them. 3. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Tilt each tumbler and look closely. Do you see lumps? What colour is the liquid around them?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt each tumbler gently. Do you see white lumps? Is the milk still smooth? Write what you see.
  - Note: You should see thick white lumps and pale green-yellow liquid in the lemon and vinegar tumblers within a minute or two. Tamarind water usually curdles hot milk too, sometimes more slowly, with brownish whey. Salt, sugar and water tumblers stay smooth. If the lemon tumbler shows only tiny lumps, the milk was not hot enough. Add one more tablespoon of lemon juice and stir.
  - Check: Lemon, vinegar and tamarind made the milk split into lumps. Salt, sugar and plain water did not. What do lemon, vinegar and tamarind have in common? → **They are all sour**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the cotton cloth over the strainer and hold it over a clear glass. 2. Pour the lemon tumbler through it. 3. Gather the cloth and gently squeeze. 4. Compare how much is in the cloth and how much is in the glass.  
  *Parent says:* Let's separate the two parts. The cloth will catch the lumps. Which part do you think will be bigger?  
  *Child mode:* Pour the lumpy milk through the cloth. The lumps stay. The liquid runs into the glass. Which part is bigger?
  - Ask: Why is there so much more whey than curd? (good: Milk is mostly water, and the water went into the whey.; The lumps are only a small part of the milk.)
  - Note: The white lumps are called curds. They hold most of the milk's protein and fat, with some water still inside. Pressed together, they are paneer. The pale liquid is whey. It is mostly water, with milk sugar, minerals and some vitamins. There will be much more whey than curd. Keep 2 spoons of whey in a small cup for the Hidden Minerals test (e3).
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Then tell them what the curds and whey are made of.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now about milk? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: milk is not one thing, it is a mix of parts. What I saw: sour liquids made white lumps and a pale watery liquid, and salt, sugar and water did nothing. There was much more liquid than lumps. Why: the sour liquids made the protein in milk clump, so it came out of the water. After their answer, explain that the curds hold most of the milk's protein and fat, and the whey is mostly water, milk sugar and minerals. So milk, which we put in the dairy box, also gives protein, fat, water and minerals.
### e2 · Sun-Dried Slices: how much of a vegetable is water? (25 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Which foods lose the most when they dry in the sun: cucumber, tomato, potato, carrot or chapati? What does that tell us about the water in them?
- **predict:** Put the five slices in order, from the one you think will shrink most to the one you think will shrink least. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut every slice the same thickness, about half a centimetre, and about the same size. Dry them all on the same plate, in the same place, for the same number of days. Only the food changes. If you have a kitchen scale, weigh each slice before and after, because a number is easier to compare than a shape.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the slices with the knife. Do not eat the slices after drying, because they have sat out for days.
- **record:** Draw each slice's outline on day 1 and again on day 3, one inside the other. If you weighed them, write both weights and how much each slice lost.
- **results table:** Food | My guess (1 = shrinks most) | Size on day 1 (outline drawn) | Size on day 3 (outline drawn) | Weight on day 1 (grams, if you have a scale) | Weight on day 3 (grams)
- **schedule:** day 1: Cut five slices the same thickness. Guess which will shrink most. Draw round each one on paper. Weigh them if you have a scale. Put them by a sunny window, covered with a thin cloth. (15 min); day 2: Look at the slices. Turn them over so both sides dry. (3 min); day 3: Draw round each slice again, on top of the first outline. Weigh them again. Put them in order from most shrunk to least. (7 min)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut one slice from each vegetable, about half a centimetre thick. 2. Cut a piece of chapati about the same size. 3. Lay all five on a sheet of paper and draw round each one with a pencil.  
  *Parent says:* All five slices start about the same size. Which one do you think will shrink the most when it dries? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Draw round each slice on the paper. Which one will shrink the most? Put them in order. Say why.
  - Ask: Why did you put that one first? (good: The cucumber feels wet, so it has lots of water.; The chapati is already dry, so it won't shrink much.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. If you have a kitchen scale, weigh each slice and write the number. 2. Move the slices onto the steel plate. 3. Cover with a thin cloth to keep flies off. 4. Put the plate by a sunny window or on a balcony.  
  *Parent says:* The cloth keeps flies off but lets air in. Sun and moving air help water leave the slices.  
  *Child mode:* Weigh each slice if you have a scale. Put them on the plate. Cover them with a thin cloth. Put them in the sun.
  - Note: A sunny window works in two or three days. In the monsoon, or in a dark flat, put the plate under a fan and give it an extra day. If a slice goes mouldy, throw it away and try again with a thinner slice in a sunnier spot.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, look at the slices and turn each one over.  
  *Parent says:* What has changed since yesterday? Which slice has changed most?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your slices. Turn each one over. Which one has changed most?
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 3, lay each slice back on its outline and draw round it again. 2. Weigh them again if you have a scale. 3. Put them in order, from most shrunk to least.  
  *Parent says:* Where did the missing part of each slice go?  
  *Child mode:* Put each slice on its first outline. Draw round it again. Which one shrank most? Where did the rest go?
  - Note: With a scale, a cucumber slice of 20 grams usually ends at about 1 gram. A potato slice keeps about a fifth of its weight, because a potato is about four-fifths water and the rest is mostly starch. The tomato and carrot fall in between. A home scale that reads to 1 gram is good enough to see this.
  - Check: The cucumber slice shrank to a thin, wrinkly disc. The chapati hardly changed size. What does this tell us? → **The cucumber was mostly water, and the water went into the air**
### e3 · Hidden Minerals: what is left when clear water dries? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Clear water looks empty. Does it leave anything behind when it dries?
- **predict:** Look at five spoons of liquid that all look clear or nearly clear: filtered water, tap water, salt water, coconut water or sugar water, and whey. Which ones do you think will leave something on the plate when they dry? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same amount of each liquid, one teaspoon. Put them all on the same clean, dry steel plate, a hand-width apart. Dry them in the same place for the same time. Only the liquid changes. Filtered or RO water is the comparison.
- **safety:** Do not taste the spots. Wash the plate afterwards.
- **record:** Draw the plate from above, with each spot and its label. Next to each spot, write what was left.
- **results table:** Liquid | My guess: will it leave something? (yes or no) | What was left (nothing, a faint ring, a white ring, crystals, a sticky film)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess which liquids will leave something behind. Put one teaspoon of each on the plate, labelled. Put it in the sun or under a fan. (12 min); day 2: Look at each dried spot closely, in good light. Fill in the table. Say what the spots tell you. (13 min)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wipe the steel plate clean and dry. 2. Put a paper label by each spot's place: filtered, tap, salt, coconut, whey. 3. Ask for a guess for each liquid.  
  *Parent says:* These liquids all look clear or nearly clear. Which ones do you think hide something inside? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Label five places on the plate. Look at the five liquids. Which ones will leave something when they dry? Guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think tap water will leave something, or not? (good: Our steel tumblers get white marks after we drink water.; Water is just water, so nothing will be left.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one teaspoon of each liquid on its place, a hand-width apart. 2. Put the plate flat in the sun or under a fan, where nobody will knock it.  
  *Parent says:* We use one teaspoon of each, so the test is fair. Now the water has to leave by itself.  
  *Child mode:* Put one spoon of each liquid on its label. Do not let them touch. Put the plate in the sun.
  - Note: In the sun, the spots dry in a few hours. Under a fan, leave them overnight.
  - Wait: Leave it until the spots are dry, a few hours or overnight.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tilt the plate in good light. 2. Look closely at each spot. 3. Touch the coconut and whey spots with a finger. 4. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* The water has gone into the air. What did each liquid leave behind?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt the plate to the light. What is left of each spot? Touch the coconut and whey spots. Are they sticky?
  - Note: What you see depends on your water. Hard water, common from borewells in Chennai, Bengaluru and Hyderabad, leaves a clear white ring. Soft water may leave only a faint ring, so look closely. Salt water leaves small white crystals. Coconut water and whey leave a sticky, yellowish film: their sugars, with minerals mixed in.
  - Check: The tap water left a white ring, but the filtered water left almost nothing. What is the white ring? → **Minerals that were dissolved in the tap water**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think about clear water now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: clear water can carry minerals that I cannot see. What I saw: the tap water left a white ring and the filtered water left almost nothing. The whey left a sticky film. Why: minerals and sugar were dissolved in the water. When the water went into the air, they stayed on the plate. Your body gets some of its minerals from water in the same way.

## Applications
- Paneer, chhena for rasgulla, and cheese are all made by making milk's protein clump.
- Doctors use blood tests to check iron, and give iron tablets to children who are short of it.
- India adds iodine to most salt, which has greatly reduced goitre, a swelling in the neck.

- School midday meals in India are planned so that children get enough protein and energy.
- Food scientists measure the nutrients in foods so that packet labels can list them.
- Water filters and RO machines take minerals out of hard water.
- Athletes plan their carbohydrates before a long race, because muscles run mainly on them.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how much protein does a growing child need?** Different countries set slightly different numbers, and scientists still study how growth, activity and food mixes change the need.
- **Are ghee and coconut oil good or bad for the heart?** Studies disagree. Some find they raise harmful fats in the blood, others find little effect when the whole diet is healthy.
- **Why does life use so many different vitamins?** We know what many vitamins do, but not why living things came to need so many different small helpers.
- **How much of the protein in a meal does each person really take in?** It depends on the food, the cooking, the person's gut and their microbes. Scientists are still measuring this for many Indian foods.
- **Are there nutrients we have not found yet?** The last vitamin was named in 1948. Scientists still find substances in food that seem to help the body, and argue about whether to call them nutrients.

## Connected maths
- In Ten-Spoon Foods, dry toor dal is about 2 spoons of protein out of 10. What fraction of the dal is protein? Write it two ways. **2 out of 10, which is the same as 1 out of 5.**. 2 spoons out of 10 is two-tenths. If you put the 10 spoons into 5 equal groups of 2, the protein fills 1 group. So it is also one-fifth. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- Kavya split 200 millilitres of milk with 20 millilitres of lemon juice. She collected 180 millilitres of whey. About how much went into the curds? **About 40 millilitres.**. She started with 200 + 20 = 220 millilitres of liquid. 180 millilitres came out as whey. 220 - 180 = 40. So about 40 millilitres went into the curds. That is much less than the whey. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- A cucumber slice weighed 20 grams and dried to 1 gram. A potato slice weighed 20 grams and dried to 4 grams. How much water did each lose? Which food had more that was not water? **The cucumber lost 19 grams. The potato lost 16 grams. The potato had more that was not water: 4 grams instead of 1.**. 20 - 1 = 19 grams of water from the cucumber. 20 - 4 = 16 grams from the potato. What is left is the part that is not water. 4 grams is 4 times as much as 1 gram. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)
- Meena's steel tumbler holds 200 millilitres. On Monday she drank 6 tumblers. On Tuesday, a hot sports day, she drank 9. How much more did she drink on Tuesday, in millilitres? Why might that be? **600 millilitres more. On a hot sports day she sweated more, so she needed more water.**. Monday: 6 x 200 = 1,200 millilitres. Tuesday: 9 x 200 = 1,800 millilitres. 1,800 - 1,200 = 600. That is 3 more tumblers, because sweat carries water out of the body. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- 100 grams of ragi flour has about 340 milligrams of calcium. 100 millilitres of milk has about 120 milligrams. Is the ragi's calcium closer to 2 times or 3 times the milk's? **Closer to 3 times.**. 2 x 120 = 240, and 3 x 120 = 360. 340 is much closer to 360 than to 240. So ragi has nearly 3 times as much calcium as the same amount of milk. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- In Two-Box Foods, Arjun found a second box for 8 of the 20 food cards. Kavya found a second box for 12. Make a tally chart for both and say what the numbers suggest about food groups. **Arjun: 8 tallies. Kavya: 12 tallies. More than a third of the foods fit two boxes for both players, so many foods are a mix of nutrients.**. Tally in groups of five: Arjun has one group of 5 and 3 more. Kavya has two groups of 5 and 2 more. 8 and 12 are both more than a third of 20, which is about 7. So many foods belong in two boxes. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
