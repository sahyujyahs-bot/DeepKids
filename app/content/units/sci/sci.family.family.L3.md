# Family: who we are to each other, and the work that keeps a home going
`sci.family.family.L3`

**Summary.** Every family runs on hours of cooking, washing and caring each day, and most of that work is never paid. In this unit you name your relatives and find that English has one word, uncle, for four different relations. You count and time every job done in your home for one evening, and draw a pictograph of who did what. You learn what parents and guardians must give a child, how girls and women were kept out of school and other rights, and how that is changing. You also meet animal families, where a father, a group or even another kind of bird raises the young.

**Intro.** *Who in your home works the most? Before you answer, think about yesterday. Who cooked, washed the vessels, folded clothes, and put the baby to sleep?* A family is a group of people who look after each other. Some families are big, with grandparents, aunts and cousins under one roof. Some are small, with one parent and one child. In every family, someone cooks, cleans, shops and cares for the youngest and the oldest. This work takes many hours, and in most homes nobody is paid for it. In this unit you find out exactly how many minutes of work your own home needs in one evening, and who does it. You will be surprised.

## Terms
- **Family**: A group of people who care for each other and are joined by birth, marriage, adoption or by choosing to live as a family. *e.g.* Kavya lives with her Amma, her Appa, her Ajji and her little brother. They are her family. Her Mama in Bengaluru is family too, though he lives far away.
- **Relative**: A person who belongs to your family, even if they live far away. *e.g.* Your mother's brother, your father's sister and your cousins are all relatives.
- **Generation**: All the people in a family who were born at about the same time. Grandparents are one generation, parents the next, and children the next. *e.g.* Ajji, Appa and Kavya belong to three different generations.
- **Mother's side and father's side**: Your relatives through your mother are on your mother's side. Your relatives through your father are on your father's side. *e.g.* In Tamil, Mama is your mother's brother and Chithappa is your father's younger brother. In English, both are called uncle.
- **Joint family and nuclear family**: A joint family is a big family where grandparents, parents, uncles, aunts and cousins live together. A nuclear family is parents, or one parent, living with their children. *e.g.* Farhan lives with his grandparents, his parents, his uncle, his aunt and two cousins. That is a joint family.
- **Guardian**: A grown-up who takes the place of a parent and is responsible for a child. *e.g.* Meenakshi's grandmother is her guardian, because her parents work in another country.
- **Unpaid work**: Work that nobody pays money for, such as cooking for your own family, washing your own clothes or looking after a sick grandparent. *e.g.* When a house help washes the vessels, she is paid. When Amma washes the same vessels, nobody pays her. Both are doing work.
- **Care work**: Looking after people who need help: feeding a baby, bathing a toddler, giving medicine, helping with homework, sitting with someone who is ill. *e.g.* Lakshmi spends half an hour each evening helping her grandfather with his eye drops and his walk. That is care work.
- **Discrimination**: Treating someone unfairly because of who they are, for example because she is a girl. *e.g.* Sending a son to school but keeping a daughter at home to do housework is discrimination.
- **Equal rights**: Rights that every person has in the same way, whoever they are. India's Constitution gives women and men equal rights. *e.g.* Every girl and every boy in India from 6 to 14 has the right to go to school for free.

## Explanations
### What a family is · *defined*
**Definition:** A family is a group of people who care for each other and are joined by birth, marriage, adoption or by choosing to live as a family.

Families come in many shapes. A joint family has grandparents, parents, uncles, aunts and cousins under one roof. A nuclear family is parents and their children. Some children live with one parent, some with grandparents, and some were adopted. Some families live apart, with a parent working in another city or country. What makes all of these families is the same: the people in them look after each other.
- e.g. Farhan in Hyderabad lives in a joint family of eight people.
- e.g. Divya in Kochi lives with her mother only. They are a family of two.
- e.g. Arjun was adopted as a baby. His parents are his parents, just as much as anyone's.
### How relatives are related · *defined*
**Definition:** A relation is the way two people in a family are connected, such as 'my mother's brother'. A relation word is the name we call them, such as Mama.

Each person has two parents, four grandparents and eight great-grandparents. Your relatives come through your mother's side and your father's side. Many Indian languages have a separate word for each relation. In Tamil, Mama is your mother's brother, Chithappa is your father's younger brother and Periyappa is your father's elder brother. Hindi has Mama, Chacha and Tau. English calls all of them uncle. The words tell you exactly how a person is related. Children also get about half of their genes from their mother and half from their father. Genes are the instructions inside every cell of a body.
- e.g. Tamil: Paati and Thatha are grandmother and grandfather. Athai is your father's sister.
- e.g. Kannada: Ajji is grandmother.
- e.g. Hindi: Bua is your father's sister and Mausi is your mother's sister.
### Home work is work · *defined*
**Definition:** Unpaid work is work done for your own home or family that nobody pays for. Care work is looking after people who need help.

Cooking, washing, cleaning, shopping and caring take hours every day in every home. When a house help or a cook does this work, she is paid. When a family member does the same work, nobody pays. When India adds up the value of all the work done in the country, it leaves out unpaid home work. In 2019, India's Time Use Survey asked people how they spent each minute of a day. It found that women who did unpaid home work spent about 299 minutes a day on it. Men who did any spent about 97 minutes.
- e.g. Cooking three meals for a family of five can take more than three hours a day.
- e.g. Sitting with a sick grandparent at night is care work.
- e.g. A house help who sweeps and mops is doing paid work. The same sweeping by Amma is unpaid work.
### What parents and guardians must give a child · *defined*
**Definition:** Parents and guardians are responsible for a child's food, home, health, safety, schooling and love.

A child cannot feed, clothe or protect themself for many years. So parents and guardians must give enough good food, a safe home and clothes. They take the child to a doctor and get them vaccinated. A vaccine teaches the body to fight a disease before the child catches it. They must send every child to school, daughters and sons alike. In India, the Right to Education Act of 2009 says every child from 6 to 14 can go to school for free. The law also says children under 14 must not be sent to work for money. Parents also give time: talking, listening and helping a child to decide things. Children help the family too, with jobs that suit their age.
- e.g. Getting polio drops and other vaccines on time.
- e.g. Sending a daughter to school just as a son is sent.
- e.g. Listening when a child says they are scared.
### Girls and women, then and now · *defined*
**Definition:** Discrimination against girls and women means treating them unfairly because they are female, for example by giving them less schooling, food, money or say.

For a long time, many Indian families sent sons to school and kept daughters at home to work. In 1848 in Pune, Savitribai Phule and Jyotirao Phule opened a school for girls. People threw mud and stones at Savitribai on her way to school, and she carried a spare sari to change into. In 1950, India's Constitution gave women and men equal rights. Many things have changed, but not all. In the 2011 Census, about 82 in every 100 men could read and write, but only about 65 in every 100 women. Women still do most of the unpaid work at home.
- e.g. Savitribai Phule, who opened a girls' school in Pune in 1848 and wrote poems.
- e.g. In Kerala, more than 90 in every 100 women could read and write in the 2011 Census.
- e.g. A daughter now has the same right as a son to go to school, by law.
### How animal families raise their young · *defined*
**Definition:** Parental care is everything a parent or helper does to keep the young alive until they can live on their own.

Animals share this work in many ways. A cow feeds her calf with her milk. A pair of house crows build the nest together, and both feed the chicks. A male emperor penguin keeps the egg warm on his feet for about two months while the mother is at sea. A mother mugger crocodile guards her nest and carries her hatchlings to the water in her mouth. Jungle babblers live in groups, and older birds in the group help to feed the chicks. An Asian koel lays her eggs in a crow's nest, and the crows raise the koel chicks as their own. An olive ridley turtle lays her eggs on a beach and swims away. Her hatchlings live on their own from the start.
- e.g. House crows: both parents feed the chicks.
- e.g. Seahorses: the father carries the eggs in a pouch until they hatch.
- e.g. Elephants: a herd of related females, led by an older female, looks after the calves together.
### Why human children need so many helpers · *debated*
A calf walks within hours of being born. A human baby takes about a year to walk and many more years to look after themself. Scientists are still arguing about how human families managed this for most of history.
- e.g. Grandmothers, aunts, older brothers and sisters often feed and carry babies in Indian families.
- e.g. In many homes, a neighbour or a house help also cares for a child.
- **Sarah Blaffer Hrdy, an American scientist who studies animals and people, in her book Mothers and Others (2009):** Human mothers have always had help from others, such as grandmothers, fathers and older children. Without these helpers, our long childhood would not have been possible.
- **Kristen Hawkes and her team, who studied the Hadza people of Tanzania:** Grandmothers matter most. Hadza grandmothers dig food for their grandchildren, so mothers can have the next baby sooner. This is called the grandmother idea.
- **John Bowlby, a British doctor, in his early writing:** A baby needs one main carer, usually the mother, above all others. Later studies found that most babies attach to several people.

## Mostly agreed
- Babies and children need comfort and touch from people who care for them, not only food. *How we know:* Harry Harlow's studies of baby monkeys in the 1950s, John Bowlby's work, and Mary Ainsworth's studies of mothers and babies in Uganda and the United States.
- In India, women do much more unpaid home work and care work than men. *How we know:* India's Time Use Survey of 2019 found that women who did this work spent about 299 minutes a day on it, and men who did any spent about 97 minutes.
- Each child gets about half of their genes from their mother and half from their father. *How we know:* Studies of how features are passed on in families, and the reading of human DNA.

## How we found out
**John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner Watson, Harry Harlow at the University of Wisconsin, John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, 1928 (Watson's book), 1950s (Harlow's experiments, reported in 1958).** In 1928, John B. Watson, a famous American psychologist, wrote a book for parents with Rosalie Rayner Watson. It told them never to hug or kiss their children. Many scientists then thought a baby loves their mother only because she feeds them. In the 1950s, Harry Harlow tested this idea at the University of Wisconsin in the United States. He took baby rhesus monkeys from their mothers and gave each baby two model mothers. One was made of wire and held a bottle of milk. The other was covered in soft cloth and gave no milk. The babies drank from the wire mother, then went straight back to cling to the cloth one. When something frightened them, they ran to the cloth mother. Harlow reported this in 1958, in a talk called The Nature of Love. Comfort mattered to the babies as much as food. John Bowlby in Britain, and Mary Ainsworth, who worked with him, showed that human children also need a trusted carer to feel safe. Harlow's experiments hurt the baby monkeys, and many scientists today say they were cruel.  
*What people believed before:* People thought babies love their mother only because she feeds them, and that hugging spoils children. Harlow's baby monkeys chose a soft cloth mother with no milk over a wire mother with milk, and ran to her when afraid.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Housework is not real work, because nobody is paid for it.** (A common view, and the way countries add up their income; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody pays money for it, so it does not appear in a country's income figures.  
  Against: The same jobs are paid when a house help or a cook does them. Without this work, nobody could eat, stay clean or go out to a paid job. Many countries now measure it with time use surveys.
- **Hugging and comforting a child too much spoils them.** (John B. Watson and Rosalie Rayner Watson, in their book for parents, 1928; *rejected*)  
  For: They thought children who were cuddled would grow up weak and clingy.  
  Against: Harlow's baby monkeys ran to a soft cloth mother when scared. Children's studies since then show that comfort makes children braver, not weaker.
- **A child needs only their mother to grow up well.** (John Bowlby's early writing, in the 1950s; *minority*)  
  For: Babies often cling most to one person, and are upset when that person goes away.  
  Against: Rudolph Schaffer and Peggy Emerson followed babies in Glasgow in 1964 and found most of them grew attached to several people. Grandmothers, fathers and others raise children well.

## Roots
We have named our relatives, counted every minute of home work in one evening, and met animal families. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your count found that cooking, washing and caring take hours in a home every evening. A house help is paid for this work, but a family member doing it is not. When India adds up the value of all its work, it leaves out unpaid home work. So the work that keeps every family alive is missing from the count.  
*Habit it breaks:* We use the word 'work' for a paid job. People say 'Appa goes to work' and 'Amma doesn't work', even when Amma works for hours at home. The word hides the work that everything else depends on.  
*Evidence:* In the Home Work Count, the jobs added up to hours in one evening, and only a house help, if there was one, was paid. India's Time Use Survey of 2019 found women doing about 299 minutes of unpaid home work a day.

- *who am i:* You are someone who has been fed, washed and carried by other people's hands, every day of your life.
- *what is life:* Every young being, a crow chick, a calf or you, stays alive because someone gives them time and care.
- *what is this world:* It is a world where some of the most needed work is done without pay, and often without being counted.

A home looks like one thing from outside. Inside, it is many people and many jobs: one person cooks, one washes, one carries the baby, one earns money. Each job needs the others. A crow pair building a nest, a group of babblers feeding chicks and a family sharing dinner are the same care, done by many bodies. The one life of a family shows up as many hands.

- **Tirukkural 101:** Help given to you when you had done nothing for the helper is so great that even the earth and the sky together cannot repay it. (Most of the minutes in the Home Work Count are help of this kind. They are given every day, and the person who receives them, often a child, has done nothing to earn them.)
- **Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11:** A teacher tells students who are leaving: treat your mother as a god, treat your father as a god, treat your teacher as a god, treat your guest as a god. (The mother is named first. The work count shows one reason why: in many homes, her minutes are the ones the whole day stands on.)
- **Ubuntu, a teaching of the Nguni-speaking peoples of southern Africa:** A person is a person through other people. (Nobody grows up alone. Human children, like jungle babbler chicks, are raised by many helpers.)
- Science (settled): Countries leave unpaid home work and care out of their income figures. They measure it separately, with time use surveys.
- Science (debated): Human mothers have always raised children with help from others, such as grandmothers, fathers and older children. Scientists still argue about which helpers mattered most.
- Other view, Gary Becker, an American economist, in A Treatise on the Family (1981): It can make sense for one person in a family to do most of the home work and another to earn money, because each gets good at their part. On this view, an unequal split of minutes is not always unfair, if the family chose it and shares what it earns.
- Practice, Count the hands in your meal (3 min): Before we eat, let's sit still for a moment. Look at the food on your plate. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly. Now, without speaking, count the people whose work is in this meal. Who bought the rice? Who cooked it? Who washed the plate? Who grew it? Breathe out. Now tell me your number.
- Ask: If nobody counts a piece of work, is it still there?
- Ask: Can you ever pay back someone who looked after you before you could say thank you?
- Ask: Is a family one thing, or many people? Could it be both?

## Story: Ajji's Minutes
*The story is made up. What it shows is true: home work takes many hours, it is often unpaid, and many girls of earlier generations left school to do it.*

Kavya was eight, and she lived in a flat in Mysuru with her Amma, her Appa, her Ajji and her little brother Chintu, who was three.

Amma was a nurse. That week she worked at night. Appa worked in a bank. Ajji was Appa's mother, and she had lived with them since Kavya was born.

One Saturday Kavya had a science job. She had to write down every home job done that day, who did it, and how many minutes it took.

She took a notebook and sat where she could see the kitchen clock.

Ajji was already awake. At half past five she swept the doorstep and drew a rangoli with white powder. Then she boiled the milk and made coffee for everyone. Kavya wrote it all down.

Gowramma came at seven to sweep and mop the floors. She worked for an hour, and Appa paid her at the end of every month. Kavya wrote, "Gowramma, 60 minutes, paid."

Amma came home from the hospital at eight. She was tired, but she bathed Chintu and fed him idli before she went to sleep. Kavya wrote, "Amma, 40 minutes."

Appa read the newspaper. Then he washed his own tiffin box. Kavya wrote, "Appa, 5 minutes."

All day the list grew. Ajji ground the batter, cut vegetables, cooked lunch, washed the vessels, folded the clothes and took Chintu to the park. When Chintu cried, Ajji picked him up. When the gas cylinder ran out, Ajji called the gas agency.

At night Kavya added up the minutes. She added them again, because she could not believe it.

"Ajji did six hours and ten minutes," she said. "Appa did thirty-five minutes. I did twenty."

Appa put down his phone. "Six hours?"

"And Gowramma did one hour, and she was paid," said Kavya. "Ajji, who pays you?"

Ajji laughed. "Nobody pays me, kanda. Nobody ever has."

"But it's the same work," said Kavya. "Gowramma sweeps, and you sweep."

Ajji sat down on the mat next to her. "When I was your age, I lived in a village near Mandya," she said. "I went to school until Class 4. Then my mother had my fourth brother, and she needed help at home. So I stayed home and looked after the babies. My brothers went to school."

"Did you want to go?" asked Kavya.

"I loved sums," said Ajji. "I was the fastest in my class. I still add the vegetable bill before the shopkeeper does."

"That's not fair," said Kavya.

"No," said Ajji. "It was not fair. Things are better now. Your Amma studied nursing. You will study whatever you like."

Kavya looked at her notebook. Every line was a job that had to be done, or nobody would have eaten, and nobody would have had clean clothes. Ajji's minutes had held up the whole day, and nobody had counted them before.

"Can I draw a picture of the minutes?" she asked. "One clock for every half hour?"

She drew it. Ajji's row had twelve clocks and a bit. Appa's row had one clock and a bit.

Appa looked at the picture for a long time. "I don't know how to make ragi mudde," he said at last.

"I will teach you," said Ajji. "Tomorrow."

The next evening Ajji stood next to Appa at the stove. She showed him how to stir the ragi flour into the boiling water with a wooden stick, fast, so no lumps formed. His first mudde was lumpy. His second was better. Chintu ate both.

Kavya stuck the picture on the fridge. A week later she did the count again. Appa's row had four clocks. Kavya's had two. Ajji's had nine.

That evening Ajji had an hour with nothing to do. She sat with Kavya at the table, and they did sums together. Ajji won.

**For the parent:** Work is work even when nobody pays for it. Ajji's minutes kept the whole family going, and they only became visible when Kavya counted them.

- Ask: Why did Kavya add up the minutes twice?
- Ask: Why did Ajji leave school when she was a girl? Was that fair to her?
- Ask: What changed in the family after Kavya drew the picture of the minutes?
- Ask: Who taught Appa to make ragi mudde, and why do you think he had never learned?

  *Parent says:* Before you turn it over, tell us why you chose that carer.

  - Note: Many children put every animal under 'Mother'. The penguin, seahorse, babbler and koel cards show that this is not always so.

  - Think: The crows raised a koel chick. Did they know the chick was not theirs?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Home Work Count: who keeps our home running in one evening? (45 min)
- **question:** How many minutes of home work does our home need in one evening, and who does it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Who will do the most minutes of home work this evening? How many minutes will all the jobs add up to? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Count every person in the same way: the job, the start time and the end time, all from the same clock. Count everyone, including yourself, any house help or cook, and anyone looking after a baby or an elder. Ask everyone to have a normal evening, so the count shows a usual day. Count from 5 o'clock until bedtime.
- **safety:** Nothing in this experiment is unsafe. The child watches jobs and writes. They do not need to go near the stove.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a pictograph with one clock for every 15 minutes of work, one row for each person.
- **results table:** Job | Who did it | Start time | End time | Minutes | Paid or unpaid?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit together before 5 o'clock. 2. Ask your child to name the home jobs that happen in an evening. 3. Write their guesses in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* Tonight we will count every home job and time it. First, guess. Who will do the most minutes of work? How many minutes will it be in all? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Tonight you count every home job. Guess first. Who will work the most? How many minutes in all? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that person will do the most? (good: Because Ajji does the cooking, and cooking takes long.; Because Amma comes home and starts the dinner and the washing.)
  - Note: Write down the guess with the reason. It is the child's prediction, and it matters more when it turns out wrong.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table in the notebook. 2. Tell the family about the count. 3. Ask everyone to have a normal evening.  
  *Parent says:* Please do everything as you always do. Kavya is only counting, not checking anyone.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the table. Ask everyone to do what they always do.
  - Note: This count is not about blaming anyone. Nobody should be told off for doing few minutes. If people change what they do because they are being watched, the count will not show a usual evening. Remember to count jobs such as feeding a baby, helping with homework and giving medicine. These are care work.
- **s3 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. From 5 o'clock, each time someone starts a home job, your child writes the job, the person and the start time. 2. When the job ends, they write the end time. 3. Write whether the person is paid for it.  
  *Parent says:* Someone is starting the cooking. What time is it now? Write it down. Tell me when they finish.  
  *Child mode:* Someone starts a job. Look at the clock. Write the time. Write it again when they stop.
  - Note: Spread this over the evening. If reading the clock is hard, you can read out the times and your child writes the job and the person. If two jobs happen at once, write both. Count your child's own jobs too, such as setting the plates.
  - Check: Ajji starts cooking at 6:10 and stops at 7:00. How long did she cook? → **50 minutes**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After the evening, work out the minutes for each job. 2. Add up the minutes for each person. 3. Write each person's total in a list.  
  *Parent says:* Now add up each person's minutes. Who did the most? Who did the least? Was any of it paid?  
  *Child mode:* Add up each person's minutes. Who did the most?
  - Note: Help with the adding if the numbers are big. In most Indian homes, one or two people, very often women, do most of the minutes. Your result may be different. Whatever you found is a real result.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a pictograph. 2. Draw one clock for every 15 minutes, in one row for each person. 3. Draw part of a clock for a part of 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw the minutes as clocks. One clock is 15 minutes. How many clocks does each person get?  
  *Child mode:* Draw one clock for every 15 minutes. One row for each person.
  - Note: If one person's total is 140 minutes, that is 9 clocks and a bit more, because 9 times 15 is 135.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and a reason. Compare with the guess from the start.

### e2 · Many Hands: do two people fold clothes twice as fast? (25 min)
- **question:** If two people fold a pile of clothes together, do they finish in half the time?
- **predict:** Guess how long it will take you to fold 20 clothes alone. Then guess how long it will take with two people, and with three. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same 20 clothes each time, and shake them out flat before each try. Fold them the same way each time. Time each try with the same clock. Fold alone at the start and again at the end, because practice can make you faster.
- **safety:** Nothing in this experiment is unsafe.
- **record:** Fill in the table, and draw a bar for each try.
- **results table:** Try | How many people | Time in minutes and seconds
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 20 dry clothes in a heap on a mat or a bed. 2. Agree on one way to fold each kind. 3. Ask for guesses.  
  *Parent says:* How long will 20 clothes take you alone? With two people? With three? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* How long will 20 clothes take you alone? With two? Why?
  - Note: Write down the guesses. Most children guess that two people take exactly half the time.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start the stopwatch. 2. Your child folds all 20 alone. 3. Stop it and write the time.  
  *Parent says:* Go! Fold as you always do, not in a rush.  
  *Child mode:* Fold all 20 alone. Write the time.
  - Note: If your child is very slow, use 10 clothes for every try instead. Keep the number the same for every try.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shake out all the clothes. 2. Two people fold them together. Time it. 3. Shake them out again. 4. Three people fold them. Time it.  
  *Parent says:* Now we shake them out and fold together. Let's see if two of us take half the time.  
  *Child mode:* Now fold with two people. Then with three. Time each one.
  - Check: Alone, Divya took 8 minutes to fold the clothes. With her mother, they took 5 minutes. Was that exactly half the time? → **No, half of 8 is 4, so it took a little longer than half**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Shake out the clothes again. Your child folds all 20 alone a second time. Time it.  
  *Parent says:* One more alone. Were you faster this time? Why might that be?  
  *Child mode:* Fold alone once more. Were you faster?
  - Note: If the second try alone is much faster, practice helped. Compare the helper tries with the average of the two alone tries.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results together. Ask for a claim, the evidence and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Did two people take exactly half the time? Did three take a third? Why not?  
  *Child mode:* Did two of you take half the time? Why or why not?
  - Ask: Why might two people not take exactly half the time? (good: Because we bumped into each other.; Because one of us was faster than the other.; Because we had to decide who folds what.)
  - Think: Is the work of a home done faster when it is shared? Is it done more fairly?

### c2 · A Steady Tray for Thatha (30 min)

### Who Does the Work at Home? (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Lakshmi times her Amma washing the vessels. Amma starts at 8:15 and finishes at 8:40. How long did Amma wash the vessels?  
   Options: 15 minutes / 25 minutes / 40 minutes  
   **Answer:** 25 minutes. *Why:* Count on from 8:15: to 8:30 is 15 minutes, and to 8:40 is 10 more. 15 and 10 make 25. A strong answer counts on from the start time.
2. (truefalse) Cooking for your own family is work, even when nobody pays for it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It takes time and effort, and the family cannot eat without it. Paid or unpaid, it is work.
3. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Generation → people in a family born at about the same time; Guardian → a grown-up who takes the place of a parent; Care work → looking after people who need help; Relative → someone who belongs to your family. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit.
4. (fill) A family with grandparents, parents, uncles, aunts and cousins under one roof is called a ____ family.  
   **Answer:** ['joint']. *Why:* A joint family has several generations and branches living together.
5. (sort) Sort these jobs. Is each one paid or unpaid?  
   **Answer:** a house help sweeping the floor for a salary → Paid; Ajji cooking lunch for the family → Unpaid; Amma bathing her own baby → Unpaid; a cook making dinner in someone else's home → Paid; Appa folding the family's clothes → Unpaid; a nurse giving medicine in a hospital → Paid. *Why:* The same kind of job can be paid or unpaid. It is paid when someone outside the family is given money to do it. A strong sorter looks at who pays, not at the job.
6. (write) In your Home Work Count, who did the most minutes of work? Write the number of minutes, and one reason why that person did the most.  
   **Answer:** For example: Ajji did 150 minutes, because she cooks dinner and puts Chintu to sleep.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the real number from the count and a reason that comes from what the child saw.
7. (draw) Draw your family tree with three generations: grandparents at the top, parents in the middle, and you at the bottom. Write what you call each person.  
   **Answer:** A tree with grandparents at the top, parents in the middle and the child at the bottom, with relation words.. *Why:* A strong tree joins couples with a line across and children with a line down, and shows both sides of the family.
8. (mcq) In Hindi, Bua is your father's sister and Mausi is your mother's sister. What does English call both of them?  
   Options: Aunt / Cousin / Grandmother  
   **Answer:** Aunt. *Why:* English uses aunt for both. Hindi has two words, so the word tells you which side of the family she is on.
9. (truefalse) A baby monkey in Harlow's study loved the wire mother most, because she gave milk.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The babies drank from the wire mother but clung to the soft cloth mother, and ran to her when scared.
10. (fill) Treating someone unfairly because of who they are, for example because she is a girl, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['discrimination']. *Why:* Discrimination means unfair treatment because of who a person is.

   **Answer:** For example: a parent is the one who feeds, protects and cares for a child every day, whoever gave birth.. *Why:* A strong answer uses both examples and says what parents do, not only who gave birth. There is more than one good answer.
4. (mcq) In the 2011 Census, about 82 in every 100 men in India could read and write, and about 65 in every 100 women. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Women are not as good at learning to read / For many years, fewer girls were sent to school / Women did not want to read  
   **Answer:** For many years, fewer girls were sent to school. *Why:* For many years, many families kept daughters at home to work, so fewer women learned to read. It is about who got the chance, not about ability.
5. (count) A family's Home Work Count showed 240 minutes in one evening. If the family does this every evening, how many minutes is that in a week?  
   **Answer:** 1680. *Why:* 240 times 7 is 1680 minutes, which is 28 hours. A strong answer also says that this is more than a whole day and night of work each week, in one evening slot alone.
6. (write) If every person in your home stopped all unpaid home work for one week, what would happen? Name three things.  
   **Answer:** For example: no cooked food, dirty plates and clothes piling up, a baby or grandparent not looked after, and nobody able to go out to a paid job.. *Why:* A strong answer shows that paid work and school depend on unpaid home work being done.
7. (draw) Draw a bar chart of the Many Hands test: one person, two people and three people. Use your own times, or these: 10 minutes, 6 minutes, 5 minutes.  
   **Answer:** Three bars of 10, 6 and 5, getting shorter, but not halving each time.. *Why:* A strong chart has labels on both axes and shows that adding helpers saves time, but less and less with each extra person.
8. (mcq) Selvi folded 20 clothes alone in 12 minutes, then with her aunt in 7 minutes, then alone again in 9 minutes. Why was her second try alone faster than her first?  
   Options: Practice made her faster / The clothes got smaller / Her aunt was still helping  
   **Answer:** Practice made her faster. *Why:* She had folded the same clothes twice before, so she had practised. This is why the test folds alone at the start and the end.
9. (truefalse) India's Constitution, from 1950, gives women and men equal rights.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Constitution says nobody can be treated unfairly by the government because of their sex. But laws alone do not change every home.
10. (write) Savitribai Phule kept teaching though people threw mud at her. Why do you think some people were so against girls going to school?  
   **Answer:** For example: they thought girls should only do housework, they were afraid of change, or they wanted girls to depend on others.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a real reason people held then, and does not make it seem like the reason was right.
11. (label) Here is a picture of the results table from the Home Work Count. Label the column that shows how you know who was paid.  
   **Answer:** ['Paid or unpaid?']. *Why:* The last column records whether the person is paid for the job. A strong answer also says that the 'Minutes' column comes from the start and end times.
12. (match) Match each person to what they found or did.  
   **Answer:** Savitribai Phule → opened a school for girls in Pune in 1848; Harry Harlow → found baby monkeys clung to a soft cloth mother; Mary Ainsworth → showed babies use a trusted grown-up as a safe base; Kristen Hawkes → studied how Hadza grandmothers feed grandchildren. *Why:* Each of these people changed how we understand families.
13. (sort) Sort these animals. Does the father help to look after the young, or not?  
   **Answer:** emperor penguin → Father helps; house crow → Father helps; cow → Father does not help; seahorse → Father helps; olive ridley turtle → Father does not help. *Why:* In penguins, crows and seahorses the father cares for the eggs or young. A bull does not look after the calf, and turtle hatchlings get no care at all.
14. (fill) A survey that asks people how they spent every part of their day is called a time ____ survey.  
   **Answer:** ['use']. *Why:* A time use survey shows how many minutes people spend on paid work, home work, care, rest and sleep.
15. (mcq) A scientist wants to know if fathers in a city do more home work than fathers did 20 years ago. What is the best way to find out?  
   Options: Ask one father what he thinks / Compare time use surveys from 20 years ago and today / Look at old family photos  
   **Answer:** Compare time use surveys from 20 years ago and today. *Why:* Time use surveys measure the minutes for many people in the same way, so you can compare them fairly across years. One person's opinion is not enough.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Kavya counts the home work in her flat one evening. Her Ajji cooks for 70 minutes and nobody pays her. Their house help sweeps for 60 minutes and is paid. Who did work?  
   Options: Only the house help, because she was paid / Both of them / Only Ajji, because she worked longer  
   **Answer:** Both of them. *Why:* Both did work. Cooking and sweeping both need time and effort, and the family needs both. Being paid or not does not change whether it is work. Choosing 'only the house help' means thinking only paid jobs count.
2. (mcq) Farhan lives with his grandparents, his parents, his uncle, his aunt and two cousins in one home. What kind of family is this?  
   Options: A nuclear family / A joint family / Not a family, because there are too many people  
   **Answer:** A joint family. *Why:* A joint family has grandparents, parents, uncles, aunts and cousins under one roof. A nuclear family is only parents and their children.
3. (mcq) Divya lives in Kochi with only her mother. Are they a family?  
   Options: Yes, because they live together and care for each other / No, a family needs a mother and a father / Only if grandparents live with them  
   **Answer:** Yes, because they live together and care for each other. *Why:* Care is what makes a family, not how many people there are. A mother and a daughter are a family of two. Choosing 'No' means thinking there is only one shape of family.
4. (mcq) In Tamil, Arjun calls his mother's brother Mama. What does English call this person?  
   Options: Cousin / Uncle / Brother  
   **Answer:** Uncle. *Why:* English uses uncle for a mother's brother, a father's brother and the husband of an aunt. Tamil has a separate word for each.
5. (mcq) Each person has 2 parents and 4 grandparents. How many great-grandparents does each person have?  
   Options: 6 / 8 / 12  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Each of the 4 grandparents had 2 parents. 4 times 2 is 8. The number doubles each generation back. Choosing 6 means adding 2 instead of doubling.
6. (mcq) India's Time Use Survey in 2019 found that women who did unpaid home work spent about 299 minutes a day on it. What did it find for men who did any?  
   Options: About 300 minutes, the same / About 97 minutes / About 600 minutes  
   **Answer:** About 97 minutes. *Why:* Men who did any unpaid home work spent about 97 minutes a day. That is about one third of the women's 299 minutes. Women in India do most of this work.
7. (mcq) Which of these must parents or guardians in India do for a child of 8?  
   Options: Send the child out to work for money / Send the child to school / Keep the child at home to do housework  
   **Answer:** Send the child to school. *Why:* The Right to Education Act of 2009 says every child from 6 to 14 has the right to free schooling. Parents must send daughters and sons alike. Sending a child under 14 to work for money is against the law.
8. (mcq) In 1848 in Pune, Savitribai Phule and Jyotirao Phule did something new. What was it?  
   Options: They opened a school for girls / They built a hospital / They started a newspaper  
   **Answer:** They opened a school for girls. *Why:* They opened a school for girls, at a time when most girls were not sent to school. People threw mud at Savitribai on her way to teach, but she kept going.
9. (mcq) Harry Harlow gave baby monkeys two model mothers. One was wire and gave milk. One was soft cloth and gave no milk. Which did the babies cling to?  
   Options: The wire one, because it gave milk / The cloth one / Neither of them  
   **Answer:** The cloth one. *Why:* The babies drank from the wire mother, then went back to cling to the cloth one, and ran to her when scared. Comfort mattered to them as much as food. Choosing the wire one is the old idea that babies love whoever feeds them.
10. (mcq) An Asian koel lays her egg in a house crow's nest. Who feeds the koel chick?  
   Options: The koel mother / The house crows / Nobody  
   **Answer:** The house crows. *Why:* The crows raise the koel chick along with their own chicks. The koel mother does not come back to feed the chick.
11. (mcq) Who keeps an emperor penguin egg warm through the cold winter?  
   Options: The mother / The father / The egg is left on the ice alone  
   **Answer:** The father. *Why:* The father holds the egg on his feet under a fold of skin for about two months. The mother is at sea, feeding. Choosing 'the mother' is the usual guess, because in many animals she does it.
12. (mcq) Meena and her brother fold 20 clothes together. Alone, Meena took 10 minutes. What is the most likely time for the two of them?  
   Options: Exactly 5 minutes / A bit more than 5 minutes / 20 minutes  
   **Answer:** A bit more than 5 minutes. *Why:* Two people often take a little more than half the time, because they wait for each other or get in each other's way. Exactly 5 minutes would happen only if both worked at the same speed with no waiting.

## Applications
- Governments use time use surveys to plan for childcare, water taps near homes and cooking gas, which save women hours of unpaid work every day.
- Doctors and hospitals now let parents stay with young children in hospital, because studies showed children suffer when kept apart from their carers.
- Schemes that pay for girls' schooling and bicycles for girls going to school are based on studies of why girls drop out.
- Family trees help doctors understand illnesses that run in families.
- Adoption laws protect children who need a family, and make adoptive parents full parents.

## Spotted around us
- A grandmother drawing a kolam or a rangoli at the door before sunrise: an hour of home work that many people never see being done.
- A house crow carrying twigs to a nest in a tree or on a building ledge, with the other parent nearby.
- Jungle babblers, the brown 'seven sisters', hopping in a noisy group on the ground in a park or a campus.
- A school van in the morning with as many girls as boys inside.

## Open questions
- **Why do human children take so many years to grow up, when most animals grow up in months?** Scientists have several ideas, such as big brains that need time to learn, and grandmothers who help. Nobody knows which matters most.
- **What is the fairest way to share home work in a family?** Families, economists and thinkers disagree. Some say half each, some say by skill, and some say by how much time each person has.
- **Do crows know that a koel chick in their nest is not theirs?** Some birds throw out eggs that look different. House crows often raise koel chicks. How much they notice, and what they feel about it, is not known.

## Connected maths
- In Kavya's Home Work Count, Ajji did 135 minutes, Amma did 60 minutes, Appa did 30 minutes and Kavya did 15 minutes. In a pictograph, one clock is 15 minutes. How many clocks does each person get? **Ajji 9 clocks, Amma 4, Appa 2, Kavya 1.**. 135 divided by 15 is 9, because 9 times 15 is 135. 60 divided by 15 is 4. 30 divided by 15 is 2. 15 divided by 15 is 1. (queues `math.data.pictographs.L3`)
- Ajji starts making dinner at 6:20 and finishes at 7:35. How long did she cook? **1 hour and 15 minutes, which is 75 minutes.**. From 6:20 to 7:20 is 1 hour. From 7:20 to 7:35 is 15 more minutes. 60 and 15 make 75 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In the Many Hands test, Meena folded 20 clothes alone in 10 minutes. With her sister they took 6 minutes. With three people they took 5 minutes. How much time did the second helper save? How much did the third helper save? **The second helper saved 4 minutes. The third helper saved only 1 more minute.**. 10 minus 6 is 4 minutes saved. 6 minus 5 is only 1 more minute. Each extra helper saved less time than the one before. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- You have 2 parents, 4 grandparents and 8 great-grandparents. How many great-great-grandparents do you have? How many generations back do you go before you have more than 100 ancestors in one generation? **16 great-great-grandparents. At 7 generations back you have 128, which is more than 100.**. The number doubles each generation: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. The seventh number, 128, is the first one over 100. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
