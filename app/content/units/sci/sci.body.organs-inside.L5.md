# Organ systems: how the organs inside you share parts and work together
`sci.body.organs-inside.L5`

**Summary.** Sitting still, a child can count aloud for about 20 seconds on one breath. Straight after a minute of step-ups, the same child manages about 6 seconds. The lungs did not shrink. The voice uses the air that the lungs breathe out. After exercise your muscles use up oxygen fast, so your body makes you breathe in again much sooner. That cuts your voice short. Your organs are grouped into organ systems, such as the digestive, respiratory and circulatory systems. Some organs work in two systems, and the systems hand things to each other all day. Your child times their own voice, shares out 100 beans as blood, and measures their skin with one hand.

**Intro.** *Say 'aaaa' in one long, steady sound. In the middle of it, swallow. Your voice stops, every time. Why can you not make a sound and swallow at the same moment?* Your throat is one passage with two jobs. Air passes through it to the lungs, and food passes through it to the stomach. Your voice box sits at the top of the windpipe, so your voice needs the same air as your breathing. In this unit you learn the organ systems of the body and the organs in each one. You also find the places where two systems share one part. You time how long you can count on one breath, at rest and after exercise. You share out 100 beans to see where the blood goes when you run.

## Terms
- **Organ**: A body part that does a special job. An organ is built from several kinds of tissue. *e.g.* The heart, the liver, a kidney, the skin and an eye are all organs.
- **Tissue**: A group of cells of the same kind that work together. Organs are made of tissues. *e.g.* Muscle tissue can shorten. Nerve tissue carries signals. The heart contains both.
- **Organ system**: A group of organs that work together on one big job for the whole body. *e.g.* The mouth, food pipe, stomach, intestines, liver and pancreas make up the digestive system.
- **Joint**: A place where two bones meet. A movable joint lets the bones move. A fixed joint does not. *e.g.* Your knee and elbow are movable joints. The joints between the bones of your skull are fixed.
- **Voluntary muscle**: A muscle that you move when you choose to. Voluntary muscles are joined to bones. *e.g.* The biceps in your upper arm is a voluntary muscle. You decide when to lift a tumbler.
- **Involuntary muscle**: A muscle that works without your choosing. It keeps working while you sleep. *e.g.* The muscles in the walls of your stomach and intestines are involuntary. So is the muscle of your heart.
- **Windpipe**: The tube that carries air from the throat down to the lungs. Doctors call it the trachea. *e.g.* You can feel the hard rings of your windpipe at the front of your neck, just above your chest.
- **Alveoli**: Tiny air bags at the ends of the air tubes in the lungs. Oxygen passes from them into the blood. *e.g.* A grown-up's lungs hold hundreds of millions of alveoli. One alone is called an alveolus.
- **Atrium and ventricle**: The chambers of the heart. The two atria at the top receive blood. The two ventricles below pump it out. *e.g.* The left ventricle pumps blood to the whole body, so its wall is the thickest.
- **Artery, vein and capillary**: Three kinds of blood vessel. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, and veins carry it back. Capillaries are the finest vessels, and they join the arteries to the veins. *e.g.* The pulse in your wrist is an artery. The blue lines on the back of your hand are veins.
- **Kidney**: One of two bean-shaped organs at the back, just under the ribs. Kidneys filter the blood and make urine. *e.g.* Each of your kidneys is about the size of your own fist.
- **Urea**: A waste that the liver makes when the body breaks down protein. The kidneys remove urea from the blood. *e.g.* Urea leaves the body dissolved in urine.
- **Cerebrum**: The largest part of the brain, at the top. The cerebrum does thinking, remembering, speaking, sensing and chosen movement. *e.g.* When you work out 100 take away 3, your cerebrum is working.
- **Cerebellum**: A smaller part of the brain, at the back and below the cerebrum. The cerebellum keeps movement smooth and keeps your balance. *e.g.* When you ride a bicycle without thinking about each push, your cerebellum is helping.
- **Brainstem**: The stalk at the base of the brain that joins it to the spinal cord. The brainstem runs breathing, heartbeat and swallowing. *e.g.* Your brainstem keeps you breathing all night while you sleep.
- **Hormone**: A chemical message made by a gland and carried in the blood. A hormone changes what another organ does. *e.g.* Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas. Insulin tells cells to take sugar out of the blood.
- **Throat**: The passage behind the nose and mouth. Air and food both pass through the throat. Doctors call it the pharynx. *e.g.* The air you breathe and the idli you swallow both cross your throat.
- **Voice box**: The top of the windpipe, where two folds called the vocal cords sit. Air passing out makes the folds shake, and that makes your voice. Doctors call it the larynx. *e.g.* Put your fingers on the front of your throat and hum. The buzzing you feel comes from your voice box.
- **Epiglottis**: A flap behind the tongue. When you swallow, the epiglottis folds down over the opening of the windpipe. *e.g.* The epiglottis is why a mouthful of rasam goes to your stomach and not into your lungs.
- **Bone marrow**: The soft tissue inside many bones. Red bone marrow makes new blood cells. *e.g.* The marrow in your hip bones, ribs and backbone makes millions of red blood cells every second.
- **Glycogen**: The form in which the liver and the muscles store sugar. The liver turns glycogen back into sugar when the blood needs more. *e.g.* Between dinner and breakfast, your liver keeps your blood sugar steady from its glycogen.
- **Reproductive organs**: The organs that make it possible to have a baby. In a male they are the testes and the penis. In a female they are the ovaries, the fallopian tubes, the uterus and the vagina. *e.g.* An ovary releases an egg. A testis makes sperm.

## Explanations
### Cells, tissues, organs and organ systems · *defined*
**Definition:** An organ system is a group of organs that work together on one big job for the whole body.

Your body is built in levels. The smallest living unit is the cell, and a grown-up has about 30 trillion of them. Cells of one kind form a tissue, such as muscle tissue or nerve tissue. Several tissues together form an organ, such as the heart. Several organs together form an organ system. Books usually list about ten systems. This unit uses nine: skeletal, muscular, digestive, respiratory, circulatory, excretory, nervous, hormone and reproductive. Doctors call the hormone system the endocrine system. The skin, hair and nails are often counted as one more system. No organ system can keep the body alive without the others.
- e.g. Level 1 is a cell. One example is a single heart muscle cell.
- e.g. Level 2 is a tissue. One example is heart muscle tissue.
- e.g. Level 3 is an organ. One example is the heart.
- e.g. Level 4 is an organ system. The heart, the blood vessels and the blood together form the circulatory system.
### The skeletal and muscular systems: frame and movement · *defined*
**Definition:** The skeletal system is the bones and joints. The muscular system is the muscles that move the bones and the muscles inside organs.

A grown-up has 206 bones. A newborn baby has about 300 separate pieces, and many of them join as the child grows. The skeleton does five jobs. It gives the body its shape. It protects soft organs: the skull guards the brain, and the ribs guard the heart and lungs. It gives the muscles something to pull on. Its marrow makes blood cells. It also stores calcium. Bones meet at joints. The knee and the elbow bend like a door hinge. The shoulder and the hip turn in a circle. The joints of the skull are fixed and do not move. Strong bands called ligaments tie bone to bone, and cords called tendons tie muscle to bone. A muscle can only pull, never push. So muscles work in pairs. The biceps bends the elbow, and the triceps at the back straightens it. Muscles joined to bones are voluntary: you choose when to move them. The muscles in the walls of the stomach, the intestines and the blood vessels are involuntary. Heart muscle is a third kind. Heart muscle is involuntary and never rests.
- e.g. Voluntary muscles include the muscles of your arms, legs, fingers, tongue and face.
- e.g. Involuntary muscles include the ones that squeeze food along your intestines.
- e.g. Some muscles can work both ways. You can blink on purpose, but your eyelids also blink without you choosing.
- e.g. A fixed joint is one that does not move. The wavy lines where the bones of your skull meet are fixed joints.
### The digestive system: from food to blood · *defined*
**Definition:** The digestive system breaks food into pieces small enough to pass into the blood, and pushes out what is left.

Food travels along one tube. The tube runs from the mouth down the food pipe to the stomach. It goes on through the small intestine and the large intestine, and ends at the anus. Teeth break the food, and saliva wets it and starts to digest starch. The stomach churns the food with acid. In the small intestine, which is several metres long in a grown-up, juices finish the digesting. The digested food passes through the wall into the blood. The large intestine takes back water. What is left leaves as faeces. Two organs help without food ever passing through them. The liver makes bile, which breaks fat into tiny droplets. The pancreas makes a juice that digests starch, protein and fat. The liver is the heaviest organ inside the body. A grown-up's liver weighs about one and a half kilograms. It also stores sugar as glycogen, and it changes many poisons into safer substances.
- e.g. Rice is starch. Digestion cuts starch into sugar, and the sugar enters the blood.
- e.g. Dal and egg hold protein. Digestion cuts protein into small units called amino acids.
- e.g. The skin of a sweetcorn kernel is not digested. It leaves the body as it came in.
### The respiratory system: air in, air out · *defined*
**Definition:** The respiratory system brings oxygen from the air into the blood. It also takes carbon dioxide out of the blood.

Air enters through the nose, where hairs and sticky mucus catch dust. The air passes through the throat and the voice box, and down the windpipe. The windpipe splits into two tubes called bronchi, one for each lung. The tubes branch again and again and end in tiny air bags called alveoli. Each alveolus is wrapped in capillaries. Oxygen passes from the air in the alveolus into the blood. Carbon dioxide passes from the blood into the air. This swap is called gas exchange. The lungs have no muscle of their own. A sheet of muscle under them, the diaphragm, pulls down, and the rib muscles lift the ribs. The chest gets bigger and air flows in. When these muscles relax, air flows out. The brainstem sets the pace. It measures carbon dioxide in the blood, and when there is more, it makes you breathe faster and deeper.
- e.g. The path of air: nose, throat, voice box, windpipe, bronchi, alveoli.
- e.g. Out of every 100 parts of the air you breathe in, about 21 are oxygen. In the air you breathe out, about 16 are oxygen.
- e.g. The urge to breathe after a run comes mainly from extra carbon dioxide in the blood.
### The circulatory system: one pump, two loops · *defined*
**Definition:** The circulatory system is the heart, the blood vessels and the blood. It carries oxygen, food, wastes, heat and hormones between all the organs.

The heart is a muscle with four chambers. The right side and the left side are two pumps joined together. Blood from the body, low in oxygen, enters the right atrium. It passes down to the right ventricle, which pumps it to the lungs. In the lungs the blood picks up oxygen. It returns to the left atrium and passes down to the left ventricle. The left ventricle pumps it out to the whole body. So blood passes through the heart twice on each trip: once on the way to the lungs, and once on the way to the body. Valves in the heart open one way only, so blood cannot flow back. Arteries carry blood away from the heart. They branch into capillaries, vessels so fine that red blood cells pass in single file. Through the thin capillary walls, the blood gives oxygen and food to the cells and takes their wastes. Veins carry the blood back. A child of ten has about 2 litres of blood, and a grown-up about 5 litres.
- e.g. The loop to the lungs: right ventricle, lungs, left atrium.
- e.g. The loop to the body: left ventricle, body, right atrium.
- e.g. The 'lub-dub' a doctor hears is the sound of heart valves closing.
### The excretory system: taking wastes out of the blood · *defined*
**Definition:** The excretory system removes the wastes that cells make. It also keeps the right amount of water and salt in the body.

Cells make wastes as they work. When the body breaks down protein, the liver turns the leftover part into urea. Blood carries the urea to the two kidneys. Each kidney holds about a million tiny filters. The filters let water, salt, sugar and urea out of the blood. Then the kidney takes back almost all the water and everything else the body needs. What is left is urine. Urine runs down two tubes called ureters into the bladder, a bag of muscle that stores it. Urine leaves through a tube called the urethra. A grown-up's kidneys filter about 180 litres of liquid a day. From this they make about one and a half litres of urine. Other organs remove wastes too. The lungs breathe out carbon dioxide. The skin loses water and salt as sweat. Faeces are different. Most of what is in faeces was never inside the body's cells. So scientists do not call it excretion.
- e.g. The path of urine: kidney, ureter, bladder, urethra.
- e.g. On a hot day you sweat more, so your kidneys make less urine, and it is darker.
- e.g. A person can live well with one healthy kidney.
### The nervous system and the hormone system: two ways to send messages · *defined*
**Definition:** The nervous system sends fast signals along nerves. The hormone system sends slower chemical messages through the blood.

The nervous system is the brain, the spinal cord and the nerves. The brain has three main parts. The cerebrum is the large wrinkled part at the top. It thinks, remembers, speaks, makes sense of what the eyes and ears send, and starts movements you choose. The cerebellum sits at the back, under the cerebrum. It keeps your balance and makes practised movements smooth. The brainstem joins the brain to the spinal cord. It runs breathing, heartbeat and swallowing, and you do not have to think about any of them. The spinal cord runs down inside the backbone. Nerves branch from it to every part of the body. The hormone system is a set of glands. A gland makes a hormone and releases it into the blood. The hormone travels everywhere, but only organs with the right receptors answer. A nerve signal arrives in a fraction of a second. A hormone takes seconds, minutes or hours, and its effect lasts longer.
- e.g. Your cerebrum is working when you read this sentence and decide to turn the page.
- e.g. Your cerebellum is working when you catch a ball, or walk without watching your feet.
- e.g. Your brainstem is working when you breathe while you sleep.
- e.g. Adrenaline is a hormone made by the adrenal glands. It makes your heart beat faster when you get a fright.
### The reproductive system: how a new person begins · *defined*
**Definition:** The reproductive system is the set of organs that makes it possible to have a baby.

This is the one system that differs between male and female bodies. In a female body, two ovaries hold tiny eggs. Each ovary is joined to the uterus by a fallopian tube. The uterus, also called the womb, is a bag of muscle where a baby grows. The vagina is the passage that leads from the uterus to the outside. In a male body, two testes make sperm. The sperm leave the body through the penis. A baby begins when one sperm from the father joins one egg from the mother. The joined cell divides again and again and settles in the wall of the uterus. There the baby grows for about nine months. These organs are present from birth, but they start their full work only in the teenage years. Hormones from the brain, the ovaries and the testes start that change. For the parent: use the correct names calmly, as you would say 'elbow' or 'kidney'. Children who know the right words can ask clear questions and can tell a trusted adult if something is wrong.
- e.g. Female organs: ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, vagina.
- e.g. Male organs: testes, penis.
- e.g. The ovaries and the testes also make hormones, so they belong to the hormone system as well.
### One organ, two systems: parts that are shared · *defined*
**Definition:** Many organs do work for more than one organ system.

The lists of systems look tidy in a book, but several organs appear on two lists. The throat carries air for the respiratory system and food for the digestive system. When you swallow, the voice box lifts, the epiglottis folds over the windpipe, and breathing stops for about one second. The voice box makes your voice from breathed-out air, so talking and breathing share one stream of air. The pancreas makes digestive juice, and it also makes the hormones insulin and glucagon. Bones belong to the skeleton, and their marrow makes the cells of the blood. The diaphragm is a muscle, and it does the work of breathing. The liver makes bile for digestion, makes urea for the kidneys to remove, and stores sugar for every organ. The kidneys make urine. They also make a hormone that tells the bone marrow to make more red blood cells. When two systems share one part, they cannot both use it fully at the same moment. In the One Breath Count you measure this. After exercise, your body needs fresh air for gas exchange, so less of each breath is left for your voice.
- e.g. The throat works for the digestive system and for the respiratory system.
- e.g. The pancreas works for the digestive system and for the hormone system.
- e.g. The thigh bone works for the skeletal system and for the circulatory system, because the marrow inside it makes blood cells.
- e.g. The ovaries and the testes work for the reproductive system and for the hormone system.
### Systems working together: handing things over · *defined*
**Definition:** Every job the body does needs several organ systems, each passing something to the next.

Take climbing three floors of stairs. The cerebrum decides to climb, and nerves carry signals to the leg muscles. The muscles pull on the bones. Working muscle cells need more oxygen and more sugar. The lungs pass oxygen to the blood. The small intestine passed sugar to the blood after breakfast, and the liver adds more from its glycogen. The heart pumps the blood to the muscles. The muscles hand back carbon dioxide and heat. The blood carries the carbon dioxide to the lungs and the heat to the skin. The brainstem senses the extra carbon dioxide and speeds up the breathing and the heart. The blood is also shared out in a new way, compared with when the body is resting. At rest, a grown-up's heart pumps about 5 litres a minute. The gut, the liver and the kidneys take almost half of it. In hard exercise the heart can pump about 20 litres a minute, and roughly four fifths goes to the muscles. The gut and the kidneys get less than half of what they had at rest, so digestion slows. The brain gets about the same amount as before. You model this with beans in the Hundred Beans.
- e.g. When you eat a dosa, your muscles chew it and the digestive system digests it. The blood carries the sugar, and the pancreas sends insulin.
- e.g. On a hot afternoon, your skin sweats, your kidneys save water, and your brain makes you feel thirsty.
- e.g. When you get a fright, the adrenal glands send adrenaline, your heart speeds up, and your muscles get ready to move.
### Organs send each other chemical messages · *idea*
For a long time, people pictured the brain as the one organ that gives orders. We now know that many organs send messages of their own through the blood. In 1981 Adolfo de Bold and his team in Canada found that the heart makes a hormone. When too much blood stretches the heart, this hormone tells the kidneys to let out more salt and water. In 1994 Jeffrey Friedman's team in New York found leptin. Leptin is a hormone made by body fat that tells the brain how much fat is stored. The stomach and the intestines make hormones that tell the brain about hunger and fullness. Working muscles release chemicals too. Scientists are still finding out what those do. In 2007 Gerard Karsenty's team reported that bone makes a hormone that changes how mice handle sugar. Other teams have not found the same effect, so this one is still argued.
- e.g. This is settled: the heart, body fat, the gut and the kidneys all make hormones.
- e.g. This is still argued: whether a hormone from bone changes how the body handles sugar.
- e.g. This is not yet known: how many such messages there are, and what they all do.
- **Many scientists who study hormones:** Almost every organ releases chemical messages. The body is run by many organs signalling each other, and the brain is one of them.
- **More careful scientists:** Finding a chemical in the blood does not show that it carries a message. Each one must be tested, and some early claims have not held up.
### How many organs are there? · *debated*
Many websites say the human body has 78 organs. No group of scientists has agreed on that list. The answer depends on what counts as one organ. Are the 206 bones one organ each, or parts of one skeleton? Is each tooth an organ? In 2016 the Irish surgeon J. Calvin Coffey argued that the mesentery should be counted as an organ. The mesentery is the sheet that holds the intestines in place. Earlier books had described it as several separate pieces. He showed that it is one continuous sheet. In 2018 a team in New York described fluid-filled spaces in the wrappings of organs. They called these spaces a new organ, the interstitium. Many anatomists replied that these spaces were already known and are a tissue, not an organ.
- e.g. Counted as an organ by everyone: heart, liver, kidney, lung, brain, skin.
- e.g. Argued: the mesentery, the interstitium, each bone, each tooth.
- **Anatomists who want a fixed list:** An organ is a body part with its own shape, its own tissues and its own job. With that rule we can count organs, even if the number is argued at the edges.
- **Anatomists who doubt the count:** The body does not come divided into pieces. 'Organ' is a useful word that people apply in different ways, so there is no one true number.
### Other animals arrange the same jobs differently · *defined*
**Definition:** Every animal must take in food and oxygen, move them around and remove wastes. Different animals use different organs and plans to do it.

Humans are one kind of animal, and our plan is one among many. In whales and dolphins, the windpipe leads to the blowhole on top of the head. Their air and their food take separate paths, so they do not share a throat the way we do. A fish has a heart with two chambers, and the blood makes one loop: heart, gills, body, heart. A frog's heart has three chambers. Birds and mammals have four chambers and two loops. An insect has no lungs. Fine air tubes run from holes along the insect's sides to every part of the body. The insect's blood carries food but almost no oxygen, so breathing and blood are separate there. A bird's lungs are joined to air bags, and air flows through the lungs in one direction. An earthworm breathes through their damp skin, so the skin is their breathing organ.
- e.g. In a whale, air and food take separate paths.
- e.g. An insect has air tubes that bring oxygen to the body without using blood.
- e.g. A fish has a heart with two chambers, and the blood makes one loop.
- e.g. An earthworm breathes through their skin, so the skin does the work of lungs.

## Mostly agreed
- The body is built in levels: cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs form organ systems. *How we know:* Microscopes show the cells and tissues of every organ. Anatomists have traced how the organs connect for more than 400 years.
- Blood passes through the heart twice on each trip: once towards the lungs and once towards the body. *How we know:* William Harvey showed in 1628 that blood goes round in a circle. Today doctors watch the two loops with ultrasound scans.
- The throat is shared by air and food, and breathing stops for about a second during every swallow. *How we know:* X-ray films of people swallowing show the voice box lifting and the epiglottis folding over the windpipe. Breathing sensors record the pause.
- In hard exercise, the body sends most of the blood to the muscles. The gut and the kidneys get less. *How we know:* Blood flow to each organ has been measured in people at rest and in exercise since the 1940s.
- The liver makes sugar and releases it into the blood, as well as making bile. *How we know:* Claude Bernard found sugar in the blood leaving the livers of dogs fed only meat, in 1848. He separated glycogen from liver in 1857.
- Organs other than glands also make hormones. *How we know:* The heart's hormone was found in 1981 and the hormone leptin from body fat in 1994. Doctors now measure both in blood tests.

## How we found out
**Claude Bernard, a French scientist, and his wife Fanny Martin, who opposed his tests on animals, 1848 (sugar found in blood leaving the liver), 1855 (the washed liver) and 1857 (glycogen).** In the 1840s, most scientists held two ideas. The first was that each organ had one job, and the liver's job was to make bile. The second was that only plants could make sugar, and animals could only use it up. In 1848 the French scientist Claude Bernard fed dogs on meat alone, with no sugar or starch. He still found sugar in the blood leaving their livers. Was the sugar left over from older meals? In 1855 he washed water through the vessels of a liver until no more sugar came out. He left the liver until the next day. By then it was full of sugar again. The liver was making sugar from a store of its own. In 1857 he separated that store in pure form and named it glycogen. So the liver had at least two jobs, and through the blood it fed every other organ. Bernard tested living dogs, and many people thought this cruel. His wife, Fanny Martin, opposed his experiments on animals.  
*What people believed before:* That each organ has one job, and that animals cannot make sugar. Bernard showed that a liver washed clean of sugar makes sugar again, so the liver does more than make bile.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Each organ has one job.** (A common belief, and the view of most scientists before the 1850s; *rejected*)  
  For: It is the simplest picture, and each organ does have a best-known job: the heart pumps, the lungs breathe, the liver makes bile.  
  Against: Claude Bernard showed that the liver also makes sugar. Since then, more jobs have been found for the pancreas, the kidneys, the bones and the heart.
- **The human body has exactly 78 organs.** (Many websites and quiz books; *debated*)  
  For: A fixed number is easy to remember, and lists with 78 names do exist.  
  Against: No scientific body has agreed on a list. In 2016 a surgeon argued that the mesentery is one more organ. The count depends on how you define an organ.
- **The brain gives the orders and the other organs only obey.** (A common picture of the body; *rejected*)  
  For: The brain does control breathing, movement and many glands, and damage to the brainstem stops the body working.  
  Against: The heart, the gut, the fat and the kidneys send hormones that change what the brain does. A heart keeps its own beat, and the gut has hundreds of millions of nerve cells of its own.
- **Bone sends a hormone that changes how the body handles sugar.** (Gerard Karsenty and his team at Columbia University, from 2007; *debated*)  
  For: In their mice, removing the bone protein osteocalcin changed blood sugar and body fat.  
  Against: In 2020 two other teams made mice without osteocalcin and found normal blood sugar. Nobody has shown the effect clearly in people.
- **The interstitium is a newly found organ.** (Neil Theise and a team in New York, in 2018; *debated*)  
  For: With a new kind of microscope, they saw fluid-filled spaces inside the living wrappings of organs. Older methods had squashed these spaces flat.  
  Against: Many anatomists say the fluid between cells was already well known. They add that a set of spaces is a tissue and cannot be called an organ.

## Roots
In this unit you learned the organ systems, and you found organs that work in two systems. Here we ask what those shared parts tell us about a body.

**Deep truth.** In the One Breath Count, you could count for far less time straight after the step-ups. Your voice and your breathing use the same stream of air, so your voice had to wait. Your throat is shared too, by air and by food. So one body part can work in two systems, and the two must take turns.  
*Habit it breaks:* We learn the organ systems as separate lists, as if each organ belonged to one list only. People made the lists, to make the body easier to study. The body uses one throat for air and food, and one stream of air for breathing and speaking.  
*Evidence:* In the One Breath Count, the counting time falls from about 20 seconds at rest to about 6 seconds straight after step-ups. It returns within about 3 minutes. In the Swallow Pause, breathing stops at every swallow. In the card game Which System?, 10 of the 24 organ cards belong on two mats.

- *who am i:* You speak with your breath, and you breathe through the passage you eat with. Talking, eating and breathing are done by one body, using the same few parts in turn.
- *what is life:* A living body does many jobs with only a few parts. So the parts must share and take turns every second. Most of this happens without you deciding anything.
- *what is this world:* A whale, a grasshopper and a child all need food and oxygen, and each body is arranged differently. The names of the systems come from the people who study bodies.

You began as one cell. That cell divided, and its daughter cells became bone, muscle, nerve, blood and gut. So all your organs are the descendants of one cell, doing different work. They never became fully separate. They share one supply of blood, one throat and one stream of air. When we name nine systems, we are describing one body, and each system is a different way of looking at it.

Each one has the shape of a person and fills the one before. (In the Layer Book you draw one body on several see-through pages, one page for each group of systems. Every page fills the same outline. The Upanishad uses its layers to ask what the self is, which science does not test. What the two share is the idea that one body can be described fully in more than one way.)
- **Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book 2, section 1:** “γεγόναμεν γὰρ πρὸς συνεργίαν ὡς πόδες, ὡς χεῖρες, ὡς βλέφαρα, ὡς οἱ στοῖχοι τῶν ἄνω καὶ κάτω ὀδόντων.” We were born to work together, like the feet, the hands, the eyelids, and the rows of upper and lower teeth. (He was writing about how people should treat each other. He chose the body as his example of parts that need one another. This unit shows that his example holds. No organ system can keep a body alive without the others.)
- Science (settled): Every organ in your body grew from one fertilised egg cell.
- Science (settled): Many organs do work for two or more organ systems, and many organs make hormones that act on other organs.
- Science (debated): Are the organ systems real divisions in the body, or useful ways of describing one body? Anatomists and philosophers still argue about this.
- Other view, Many anatomists and doctors: The organ systems are real divisions. The organs of one system are joined by their own tubes, grow together before birth and often fall ill together. A kidney doctor must know the excretory system as one thing. Shared parts such as the throat are the exceptions. They do not show that the divisions were only made by people.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your throat belongs to the breathing system and to the digestive system. So where does one system end and the next begin? (hint: Think about who drew the lists of systems, and why.)
- *big:* If a surgeon gives you another person's kidney, and it works in you for 20 years, whose kidney is it? (hint: Think about what the kidney does all day, and for whom.)
- *deep:* Your heart, lungs and liver work without asking you. Are they doing it, or are you doing it? (hint: Try saying 'I digest my food' and then 'my stomach digests my food'. Which sounds more true?)

## Story: Sadugudu on One Breath
*The people in this story are made up. The rule is real. Many people in Tamil Nadu call kabaddi sadugudu. A raider must chant the word aloud on one breath for the whole raid. If the chant stops, the raider is out. In today's professional matches a raid is also limited to 30 seconds by a clock. The science is real too: the voice uses breathed-out air, and after hard running after hard running the body needs fresh air much sooner, so the chant is cut short.*

In Yazhini's village near Thanjavur, the game was called sadugudu. The school called it kabaddi. Under both names, the rule that mattered was the same.

A raider crosses the middle line alone. She must touch one of the seven girls on the other side and get home again. From the moment she crosses the line until she is back, she must chant the word aloud: sadugudu, sadugudu, sadugudu. She must do it all on a single breath. If the chant stops, or if she takes a second breath, she is out.

Yazhini was ten, and she was the fastest runner in Class 5. She could beat everyone in her lane to the tea shop and back. So when Selvi Akka, who had played for the district, started a girls' team behind the ration shop, Yazhini expected to be its best raider.

She was out in four seconds.

"Sadugudu-sadugudu-sadu..." Then came a gasp, loud enough for both teams to hear. Selvi Akka blew her whistle. Out.

It happened again the next evening, and the evening after that. Yazhini warmed up the way runners do. She sprinted up and down the side of the court until her legs felt quick and light. Then she charged across the line. Her legs were ready every time. Her chant died almost at once.

"My lungs are too small," she told her grandmother that night.

"Then stretch them," said Paati, who believed that most things could be stretched.

So Yazhini practised. She sat under the wall clock and held her breath while the seconds hand went round. Forty seconds. Fifty. By the end of the week she could hold her breath for a full minute.

On the court she still lasted four seconds. It made no sense. A minute at home, and four seconds behind the ration shop.

The best raider on the team turned out to be Kaviya. Kaviya was small, and she came last in every race. Yet Kaviya raided for twenty seconds at a time. Her chant ran on as evenly as a sewing machine. She touched an ankle, turned, and was home before anyone caught her.

On Thursday, Yazhini stubbed her toe on the water pot, and Selvi Akka made her sit out. She sat on the low wall with her foot up and watched Kaviya properly for the first time.

Before each raid, Kaviya stood quite still at the back of the court. She breathed in and out slowly, four or five times. Then she took one more breath, a deep one, and walked to the line. She walked. Kaviya did not run one step until she had to.

Yazhini had been sitting on the wall for ten minutes. Without quite meaning to, she took a breath and began under her voice. Sadugudu, sadugudu, sadugudu. She counted on her fingers. She reached forty-one before she needed air.

Forty-one. On the court she had never passed nine.

She had the same lungs on the wall as on the court. So what was different? On the wall, her legs were doing nothing. On the court, she had always sprinted first. And after a sprint she panted. Everybody did. Straight after a sprint she could not even answer Selvi Akka properly. Her words came out in pieces: "Yes... Akka... I will."

Panting was her body pulling air in and pushing it out, fast, because her legs had used so much. The chant needed the air to go out slowly, a little at a time. The legs and the chant wanted the same breath. And her legs had been spending it before she even reached the line.

On Saturday the girls from Thiruvaiyaru came for the Pongal match. With the score level, Selvi Akka pointed at Yazhini.

Yazhini did not sprint. She stood at the back of the court. "Come on, runner!" a girl on the other side called. Yazhini breathed in and out slowly, five times, until her chest was quiet. She took one deep breath and walked to the line.

"Sadugudu, sadugudu, sadugudu." She kept it soft. She had worked out that a shout spends air faster than a murmur. She took small steps. She let the seven girls shuffle and crouch and wait for the famous charge that did not come.

Somewhere past the fifteenth sadugudu she felt it. It was the first tightness, her chest beginning to ask for air. She knew now what was asking, and she knew it would win in the end. So she did not argue with it. She used her fast legs for one second only. One lunge, one touch on the corner girl's shoulder, three strides, and she was over the line.

"...gudu." And then air, a great deal of it.

The whistle blew. A point.

Kaviya thumped her on the back. "You walked!"

"I ran," said Yazhini, between breaths. "At the end. When I could pay for it."

Selvi Akka only said, "Now you know what the chant is for."

Yazhini thought she did. Whoever made the rule, long ago, had not needed a stopwatch. Every raider carries her own clock in her chest, and running makes it tick faster.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Yazhini thinks her problem is the size of her lungs. Holding her breath for a minute at home does not help. Something else sets the limit on the court. The voice is made by air leaving the lungs through the voice box. So a chant can last only as long as one breath out. After a sprint, the muscles have used oxygen and made carbon dioxide. The brainstem then forces the next breath within seconds. Breathing and speaking share one stream of air, and when the body needs gas exchange urgently, the voice loses. Yazhini wins by starting her raid with quiet breathing, chanting softly and saving her running for the last second.

- Ask: Why could Yazhini chant forty-one times on the wall, but only nine times on the court?
- Ask: Why did holding her breath for a minute at home not help her on the court?
- Ask: Why did she keep her chant soft instead of shouting it?
- Ask: Selvi Akka said, 'Now you know what the chant is for.' What do you think the chant is for?
- Ask: What else do you do that is harder to do straight after running? Why?

## Experiments
### e1 · One Breath Count: how long can you count aloud on one breath? (40 min)
- **question:** How long can you count aloud on one breath at rest? Does the time change straight after exercise? If it changes, how quickly does it come back?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will count aloud on one breath at rest, and again straight after one minute of step-ups. Will the second time be longer, shorter or the same? How many minutes will it take to come back? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** The same person counts every time, sitting down, in a normal talking voice, at the same speed. Start every count with one full breath in. Do the same step-ups for the same time, one minute, in both runs. Only one thing changes: how long ago the exercise ended. Do three tries at rest and two whole runs, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Use a low, firm step with nothing slippery on it, and keep a grown-up beside the child. Stop counting as soon as you need air, and never push on until you feel dizzy. Stop the step-ups at once if there is chest pain or wheezing. A child with asthma or a heart condition should do gentle marching on the spot and ask their doctor first.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a line graph. Put the time since the step-ups along the bottom and the seconds of counting up the side. Mark your resting average as a line across the graph. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** When you counted | Run 1 (seconds) | Run 2 (seconds) | Average (seconds)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the test. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write both in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* We will time how long you can count aloud on one breath. First you sit still. Then you do step-ups for a minute and count again. Will the time be the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will count aloud on one breath. First at rest, then after step-ups. Will the times be the same? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think the time will change, or stay the same? (good: After exercise I am out of breath, so I will need air sooner.; My lungs are the same size, so the time should be the same.; I breathe faster after running, so each breath is shorter.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. 'My lungs are the same size' is a sensible reason, and the test will answer it.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits quietly for 2 minutes. 2. They take one full breath in and count aloud at their usual talking speed. 3. Start the stopwatch on 'one'. Stop it when they need to breathe in. 4. Write the seconds. 5. Do this 3 times, with 1 minute of quiet breathing between tries.  
  *Parent says:* Take one full breath. Count aloud in your normal voice until you need air. Stop as soon as it stops being easy.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still for two minutes. Take one big breath. Count aloud until you need air. Do this three times.
  - Note: Most children of ten manage 12 to 25 seconds. The three tries often differ by 2 or 3 seconds. Keep the voice at talking loudness, because a loud voice uses air faster.
  - Check: In the One Breath Count, why do you time three counts at rest and not just one? → **One try can be a fluke**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add the three resting times. 2. Divide by 3. 3. Write the answer in the table as the resting average.  
  *Parent says:* Add your three times and divide by 3. That average is the number we compare everything else with.  
  *Child mode:* Add your three times. Divide by 3. Write the average in the table.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child does step-ups on the bottom stair for 1 minute, as fast as is safe. 2. They sit down at once, take one full breath and count aloud. Time it. 3. Note the time on the wall clock when the step-ups ended. 4. They sit and breathe quietly. 5. They count again when 1 minute, 2 minutes and 3 minutes have passed. Time each count.  
  *Parent says:* Step up and down for one minute. Then sit, take one full breath and count. After that, breathe quietly. We will count again each minute.  
  *Child mode:* Do step-ups for one minute. Sit down. Take one breath and count. Count again after 1, 2 and 3 minutes.
  - Note: The first count after the step-ups is often only 4 to 8 seconds. Your child may be surprised and want to try again. Write down the first try, because that is the honest result.
- **s5 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rests in the chair for 5 minutes. 2. Repeat the whole run: 1 minute of step-ups, then a count straight away and at 1, 2 and 3 minutes. 3. Write these times in the Run 2 column.  
  *Parent says:* Rest for five minutes. Then we do the whole run once more. Two runs tell us more than one.  
  *Child mode:* Rest for five minutes. Then do the whole run again. Write the new times.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each row, add Run 1 and Run 2 and halve the answer. 2. Draw a line graph of the averages. 3. Draw a straight line across the graph at the resting average.  
  *Parent says:* Find the average for each row. Then plot the points and join them. Where is the line lowest? When does it get back to your resting line?  
  *Child mode:* Find the average for each row. Plot the points. Join them with a line.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the graph together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your graph. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Your lungs stayed the same size. So what changed?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Your lungs were the same size in every count. So why was the count so short straight after the step-ups? (good: My body needed fresh air sooner.; My muscles had used a lot of oxygen.; I could not hold the air in for long, because I had to breathe.)
  - Ask: Where does the air for your voice come from? (good: From my lungs, when I breathe out.; It is the same air I breathe.)
  - Note: Straight after the step-ups, most children count for a third of their resting time or less. They are nearly back after 3 minutes. Write what you really found. The reason: the voice is made in the voice box, at the top of the windpipe. Air leaving the lungs shakes two folds there. So you can speak only while breathing out, and no fresh air comes in while you speak. During the step-ups the muscles used oxygen and made carbon dioxide. The brainstem measures carbon dioxide in the blood. When there is more, it forces the next breath sooner. So breathing and speaking share one stream of air, and after exercise the breathing takes it first.
  - Think: A kabaddi raider must chant 'kabaddi' on one breath for the whole raid. Why does the rule make running harder?
### e2 · Beans and Threes: can your brain do two hard jobs at once? (25 min)
- **question:** You move beans and count backwards in threes at the same time. Do you do each job as well as when you do it alone? Is it different if the second job is singing a song you know by heart?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will move beans alone, count backwards alone, and then do both together. Will you move as many beans when you also count? Will singing a song slow the beans as much as counting does? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every round lasts 30 seconds. Use the same hand, the same two bowls and the same gap of about 20 centimetres between them. Move one bean at a time. Rest for half a minute between rounds. Do every kind of round twice. The second time, start counting from 101 and not 100, so that the answers are new. Only one thing changes: what else the brain is doing.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows: beans alone, threes alone, beans with threes, and beans with a song. Write the beans moved and the right answers for both tries.
- **results table:** The round | Beans moved, try 1 | Beans moved, try 2 | Right answers, try 1 | Right answers, try 2
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the bowls and the beans. 2. Explain the four kinds of round. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* You will move beans, count backwards in threes, and then do both at once. Will you still move as many beans? Will singing slow you as much as counting? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will move beans and count backwards. First alone, then together. Will the beans slow down? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: My hands and my mouth are different parts, so both can work.; I can only think about one thing at a time.; Singing is easy, so it will not slow me.)
  - Note: Write the list of right answers in the notebook before you start: 100, 97, 94, 91, 88, 85, 82, 79, 76, 73, 70, 67. For the second try: 101, 98, 95, 92, 89, 86, 83, 80, 77, 74, 71, 68.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Beans alone: your child moves beans one at a time for 30 seconds. Count the beans moved. 2. Rest for half a minute. 3. Threes alone: your child counts backwards from 100 in threes, aloud, for 30 seconds. Tick each right answer on your list.  
  *Parent says:* Move the beans one at a time, as fast as you can. Stop when I say stop. Next, count backwards from 100 in threes, aloud.  
  *Child mode:* Move beans for 30 seconds. Count them. Rest. Then count backwards from 100 in threes.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Beans with threes: your child moves beans and counts backwards from 100 in threes at the same time, for 30 seconds. Count the beans and tick the right answers. 2. Rest for half a minute. 3. Beans with a song: your child moves beans while singing a song they know by heart, for 30 seconds. Count the beans.  
  *Parent says:* Now do both at once. Keep moving beans and keep counting backwards. After a rest, move beans again while you sing a song you know well.  
  *Child mode:* Move beans and count backwards together. Rest. Then move beans while you sing.
  - Note: Watch the hand. Many children stop the hand for a moment each time they work out a number. That pause is worth pointing out afterwards.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Repeat all four rounds. 2. This time start the counting from 101. 3. Write every number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We do all four rounds once more, starting from 101 this time. Then we can see whether the first result was a fluke.  
  *Child mode:* Do all four rounds again. Start counting from 101 this time. Write every number.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the numbers. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Which second job slowed the beans more? Did your breathing stop in any round?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Why did counting backwards slow the beans more than singing did? (good: Counting backwards needs thinking, and so does picking up each bean.; I know the song by heart, so I do not have to think about it.)
  - Ask: Which jobs did your body keep doing in every round, without slowing? (good: Breathing.; My heart kept beating.; I kept sitting upright.)
  - Note: In most children, both numbers fall when the two jobs are done together. The beans often fall by a quarter or more. A song known by heart hardly slows the beans. Write what you really found. The reason: picking up a small bean and taking away three both need attention from the cerebrum. The cerebrum switches between two hard jobs, and each switch costs time. A song known by heart needs little attention. All the while, the brainstem ran the breathing and the heartbeat without a pause. Scientists still argue about exactly what limits attention.
  - Think: Why is it dangerous for a driver to read a phone message, even on an empty road?
### e3 · The Swallow Pause: what does your breathing do when you swallow? (15 min)
- **question:** Does your breathing stop when you swallow? Straight after a swallow, is your first breath in or out?
- **predict:** Guess first. When you swallow a sip of water, does your breathing carry on or stop? Straight after the swallow, will you breathe in first or out first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Take ten small sips of the same size. Sit upright every time. Breathe through your nose in your normal way, and take two or three breaths between sips. Do not try to control your breathing. Count all ten swallows, even the ones that surprise you.
- **safety:** Sit upright and take small sips. Never try to breathe in while there is water in your mouth. In the last part, use only your own saliva.
- **record:** Make a tally of ten swallows: how many times the air stopped, and how many times the first air afterwards was out.
- **results table:** Swallow number | Did the air stop? (yes or no) | First air after the swallow (out or in)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a guess and a reason. 2. Write both down. 3. Your child wets one finger and holds it under their nostrils, with the mouth closed.  
  *Parent says:* When you swallow, does your breathing carry on or stop? After the swallow, do you breathe in or out first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Does your breath stop when you swallow? What comes next, in or out? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: I think it stops, or water would go into my lungs.; I think I breathe in after, because I missed a breath.; I think I breathe out, to push the water away.)
  - Note: On a wet finger, air breathed out feels warm and air breathed in feels cool. Let your child practise for a few breaths until they can tell the two apart.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child takes a small sip and swallows, with the wet finger under the nose. 2. They say whether the air stopped, and whether the first air afterwards was out or in. 3. Tally it. 4. They take two or three normal breaths. 5. Do this for ten sips.  
  *Parent says:* Take a small sip and swallow. Did the air stop? Was the first air afterwards warm, going out, or cool, coming in?  
  *Child mode:* Take a small sip. Swallow. Did the air stop? Was the next air out or in? Do ten sips.
  - Note: You can check with the cold spoon. Hold it just under your child's nose. Mist appears on the steel when they breathe out. If the feeling is hard to judge, write a question mark for that swallow.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the water away. 2. Your child tries three times to swallow their own saliva and breathe in at the same moment. 3. Ask what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now, with no water, try to swallow and breathe in at the same moment. Try three times. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Put the water away. Try to swallow and breathe in together. Try three times.
  - Check: In the Swallow Pause, why can you not breathe in while you swallow? → **The windpipe is covered during a swallow**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the tallies. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Count your tallies. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Count your tallies. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Ask: Why might breathing out be the safer first breath after a swallow? (good: It blows any last drops away from the windpipe.; Breathing in could pull drops into the lungs.)
  - Note: The air stops at every swallow, for about one second. In studies of grown-ups, the first breath afterwards is usually out. Children vary more, so write what you really found. The reason: the throat is shared by air and food. During a swallow, the brainstem stops the breathing, the voice box lifts and the epiglottis covers the windpipe. Scientists think breathing out afterwards helps to clear any drops left near the opening.

## Worksheets
### The organ systems and their organs (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which list names organs of one organ system?  
   Options: Mouth, stomach, intestines, liver / Heart, lungs, brain / Kidneys, eyes, skull / Bones, bladder, tongue  
   **Answer:** Mouth, stomach, intestines, liver. *Why:* The mouth, stomach, intestines and liver all work on digesting food. The other lists mix organs from different systems.
2. (order) Put these in the order that breathed-in air passes through them.  
   **Answer:** ['Nose', 'Throat', 'Windpipe', 'Bronchi', 'Alveoli']. *Why:* Air enters by the nose and crosses the throat. It goes down the windpipe and through the bronchi to the alveoli.
3. (match) Match each organ system to its job.  
   **Answer:** Circulatory → Carries things round the body; Excretory → Removes wastes from the blood; Skeletal → Holds the body up; Nervous → Sends fast signals. *Why:* Each system has one big job for the whole body. The blood carries, the kidneys remove wastes, the bones hold the body up and the nerves signal.
4. (fill) The two lower chambers of the heart, which pump blood out, are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ventricles']. *Why:* The atria at the top receive blood. The ventricles below pump it out to the lungs and to the body.
5. (truefalse) Blood passes through the heart twice on each full trip round the body.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The right side pumps blood to the lungs. The blood comes back, and the left side pumps it to the body.
6. (sort) Sort these muscles. Is each one voluntary or involuntary?  
   **Answer:** biceps → Voluntary; tongue → Voluntary; leg muscles → Voluntary; heart muscle → Involuntary; stomach wall → Involuntary; muscles of the intestines → Involuntary. *Why:* You choose when to move your arm, tongue and legs. The heart, the stomach and the intestines work without your choosing.
7. (mcq) Which part of the brain keeps your balance and makes movement smooth?  
   Options: The cerebellum / The cerebrum / The brainstem / The spinal cord  
   **Answer:** The cerebellum. *Why:* The cerebellum sits at the back, under the cerebrum. Choosing the cerebrum is a common slip, because the names sound alike. The cerebrum does the thinking.
8. (order) Put these in the order that urine passes through them.  
   **Answer:** ['Kidney', 'Ureter', 'Bladder', 'Urethra']. *Why:* The kidney makes urine. A ureter carries it to the bladder, which stores it. Urine leaves through the urethra.
9. (truefalse) The liver stores sugar as well as making bile.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The liver stores sugar as glycogen and releases it into the blood between meals. It has many jobs.
10. (fill) A baby begins when a sperm joins an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['egg']. *Why:* The egg comes from an ovary and the sperm from a testis. The joined cell divides and grows in the uterus.
11. (label) Label the four chambers of the heart on this drawing.  
   **Answer:** ['Right atrium: upper chamber, receives blood from the body', 'Right ventricle: lower chamber, pumps blood to the lungs', 'Left atrium: upper chamber, receives blood from the lungs', 'Left ventricle: lower chamber, pumps blood to the body']. *Why:* The atria are on top and the ventricles below. In a drawing, the heart's right side is on the left of the page, as if the person faces you.
12. (write) Name two organs that work in two organ systems. Give both jobs of each organ.  
   **Answer:** For example: the throat carries air to the lungs and food to the stomach. The pancreas makes digestive juice and also makes the hormone insulin.. *Why:* A strong answer names two real organs and gives two different jobs for each. The thigh bone, the diaphragm, the liver and the kidneys are other good choices.
13. (write) Straight after step-ups, you can count for far less time on one breath. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The voice uses air that is leaving the lungs. After step-ups the muscles have used oxygen and made carbon dioxide. The body must breathe in fresh air sooner, so the breath out is cut short.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the voice and the breathing share the same air. It also says that exercise makes the body need fresh air sooner.
14. (draw) Draw the throat dividing into the windpipe and the food pipe. Show the epiglottis.  
   **Answer:** One passage behind the mouth divides into two tubes. The windpipe is in front, with the voice box at its top. The food pipe is behind. A flap, the epiglottis, sits above the opening of the windpipe.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows one shared passage and two tubes below. The flap sits over the windpipe, and the food pipe stays open.
15. (perspective) Which organ is the most important?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. For example, the heart cannot work without oxygen from the lungs and sugar from the gut and the liver.. *Why:* Each view has something right. A strong answer follows at least one handover from one organ to another to make its point.
### Shared parts and handovers (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does your breathing stop for about a second when you swallow?  
   Options: The windpipe is covered / The lungs are full / The stomach pushes up / The nose closes  
   **Answer:** The windpipe is covered. *Why:* The voice box lifts and the epiglottis folds over the windpipe, so food cannot enter it. No air can pass until the swallow ends.
2. (mcq) In hard exercise, which organs get less blood than at rest?  
   Options: The gut and the kidneys / The leg muscles / The heart muscle / The skin  
   **Answer:** The gut and the kidneys. *Why:* In the Hundred Beans, the gut and liver fall from 6 beans to 2. The kidneys fall from 5 to 2. Muscles, heart muscle and skin all get more.
3. (mcq) Six hours after a meal, where does most of the sugar in your blood come from?  
   Options: Glycogen stored in the liver / The air in the lungs / The bones / The bladder  
   **Answer:** Glycogen stored in the liver. *Why:* The liver turns its stored glycogen back into sugar and releases it. The lungs, bones and bladder do not store sugar.
4. (mcq) Which organ makes urea, and which organ removes it from the blood?  
   Options: The liver makes it, the kidneys remove it / The kidneys make it, the liver removes it / The lungs make it, the skin removes it / The stomach makes it, the bladder removes it  
   **Answer:** The liver makes it, the kidneys remove it. *Why:* The liver makes urea from used protein. The blood carries urea to the kidneys, which pass it out in urine.
5. (truefalse) A muscle can push a bone as well as pull it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A muscle can only shorten, so it can only pull. That is why muscles work in pairs, one to bend a joint and one to straighten it.
6. (truefalse) A hormone reaches its target faster than a nerve signal does.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A nerve signal arrives in a fraction of a second. A hormone travels in the blood and takes seconds, minutes or hours.
7. (sort) Sort these organs. Does each one work in one system, or in two?  
   **Answer:** bladder → One system; windpipe → One system; stomach → One system; pancreas → Two systems; throat → Two systems; thigh bone → Two systems. *Why:* The pancreas digests and makes hormones. The throat carries air and food. The thigh bone holds you up and makes blood cells.
8. (odd) Three of these are parts of the brain. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Cerebrum / Cerebellum / Brainstem / Bronchus  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A bronchus is an air tube leading into a lung. The other three are the main parts of the brain.
9. (count) The kidneys get 5 beans of blood a minute. One bean is 200 millilitres. How many millilitres is that?  
   **Answer:** 1000. *Why:* 5 beans of 200 millilitres each is 5 times 200, which is 1,000 millilitres. That is 1 litre every minute.
10. (order) Blood returns from the body to the heart. Put its next stops in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Right atrium', 'Right ventricle', 'Lungs', 'Left atrium', 'Left ventricle']. *Why:* Blood enters the right atrium and passes to the right ventricle, which pumps it to the lungs. It returns to the left atrium and then the left ventricle.
11. (write) Running straight after a big lunch makes the stomach feel heavy. Use the Hundred Beans to explain why.  
   **Answer:** In hard exercise most of the blood goes to the muscles. The gut gets less than half of what it had at rest. So digestion slows, and the food stays in the stomach for longer.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the numbers: the gut and liver fall from 6 beans to 2 while the muscles rise from 5 to 80.
12. (write) On the Handover Map, cover the liver. Between meals, what would the leg muscles run short of, and why?  
   **Answer:** The muscles would run short of sugar. Between meals the small intestine has no more sugar to pass on. So the blood depends on the sugar that the liver releases from its glycogen.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the arrows: liver to blood, blood to muscles. It names sugar as the thing handed over.
13. (write) Humans use one throat for air and for food. Give one cost of this and one gain.  
   **Answer:** Cost: food can go down the windpipe and make us choke, so breathing must stop at every swallow. Gain: we can breathe through the mouth when the nose is blocked. We also shape breathed-out air into speech with the mouth.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a real cost and a real gain. It does not call the shared throat simply good or simply bad.
14. (perspective) Are the organ systems real divisions of the body, or lists made by people?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For example, in the card game Which System?, 10 of 24 organs belonged on two mats, which supports the philosopher. The joined tubes of the gut support the anatomist.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement with no single winner. A strong answer uses a named organ as evidence and gives the other side a fair point.
15. (label) Label the three main parts of the brain on this side view. Write one job beside each.  
   **Answer:** ['The cerebrum is the large top part. It does thinking, memory, speech and chosen movement.', 'The cerebellum is the small part at the lower back. It keeps your balance and makes movement smooth.', 'The brainstem is the stalk at the base. It runs breathing, heartbeat and swallowing.']. *Why:* The cerebrum is by far the largest part. The cerebellum sits under it at the back, and the brainstem leads down to the spinal cord.
### Testing ideas about organs (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A liver is washed until no sugar comes out. The next day it holds sugar again. What does this show?  
   Options: The liver makes sugar itself / Sugar came in from the air / The washing was not finished / The liver makes bile from sugar  
   **Answer:** The liver makes sugar itself. *Why:* No food could reach the washed liver, so the new sugar must have come from a store inside it. That store is glycogen.
2. (mcq) A child moves 24 beans alone, 17 while counting backwards and 23 while singing. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: Two jobs that need attention slow each other / Singing makes hands faster / The hands were tired by the second round / Counting backwards is impossible  
   **Answer:** Two jobs that need attention slow each other. *Why:* Counting backwards cost 7 beans and singing cost only 1. Tired hands would have slowed the singing round as well.
3. (mcq) An insect's blood carries almost no oxygen. How do the insect's cells get oxygen?  
   Options: Air tubes reach every part of the body / The insect does not need oxygen / Through the mouth and stomach / The wings fan it in  
   **Answer:** Air tubes reach every part of the body. *Why:* Fine air tubes run from holes along the insect's sides to every part of the body. So in an insect, breathing does not depend on blood.
4. (mcq) When a person's kidneys stop working, why does the heart come under strain?  
   Options: Spare water and salt stay in the blood / The kidneys stop pumping blood / The heart has to make the urine / The lungs stop working too  
   **Answer:** Spare water and salt stay in the blood. *Why:* The kidneys let out spare water and salt. If they stop, the amount of blood grows and the heart must pump harder. The kidneys never pumped the blood.
5. (truefalse) Scientists agree that the human body has exactly 78 organs.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. No group of scientists has agreed on a list. The number depends on what you count as one organ.
6. (truefalse) The urge to breathe comes mainly from carbon dioxide building up in the blood.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The brainstem measures carbon dioxide. When the level rises, it makes you breathe sooner and deeper.
7. (match) Match each organ to its second, less famous job.  
   **Answer:** Pancreas → Makes insulin; Thigh bone → Makes blood cells; Kidney → Sends a hormone to bone marrow; Liver → Stores sugar. *Why:* Each of these organs is known for one job and also does another. That is why each sits on two lists of systems.
8. (count) A one-breath count lasts 19 seconds at rest and 6 seconds after step-ups. How many seconds shorter is that?  
   **Answer:** 13. *Why:* 19 take away 6 is 13. The count after exercise is 13 seconds shorter, less than a third of the resting time.
9. (fill) The flap that covers the windpipe during a swallow is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['epiglottis']. *Why:* The epiglottis sits behind the tongue. It folds down over the windpipe as the voice box lifts.
10. (write) Many people think the brain gives all the orders. Give two pieces of evidence that other organs send messages too.  
   **Answer:** The heart makes a hormone that tells the kidneys to let out salt and water. Body fat makes leptin, which tells the brain how much fat is stored. The stomach and intestines make hormones about hunger.. *Why:* A strong answer names two organs and says what each message does. It does not just say 'organs talk to each other'.
11. (write) Plan a fair test of this question: does talking loudly use up a breath faster than talking softly?  
   **Answer:** Count aloud on one full breath, sitting at rest, once softly and once loudly. Time each count in seconds. Keep the person, the speed of counting and the rest between tries the same. Do three tries of each and compare the averages.. *Why:* A strong plan changes only the loudness, measures seconds, keeps everything else the same and repeats each try.
12. (write) After most swallows, the first breath is out, not in. Suggest why this could be safer for the lungs.  
   **Answer:** A breath out blows any drops left near the opening away from the windpipe. A breath in could pull them down into the lungs.. *Why:* A strong answer links the direction of the air to where leftover drops would go.
13. (write) A hospital machine can do the work of failed kidneys. What two things must the machine do to the blood?  
   **Answer:** It must take urea and other wastes out of the blood. It must also remove spare water and salt, and leave in the right amount of each.. *Why:* A strong answer gives both jobs of the kidneys: removing wastes, and keeping water and salt at the right level.
14. (perspective) How many organs does a body have?  
   **Answer:** Any choice with a reason, and one honest weakness. Counting each bone gives a huge number and treats a toe bone like a liver. Counting by job makes it hard to place an organ with two jobs.. *Why:* There is no agreed answer. A strong answer sees that the number depends on the definition of an organ.
15. (draw) Sketch a line graph of one-breath counting time: at rest, straight after exercise, and 1, 2 and 3 minutes later.  
   **Answer:** The line starts high at rest and drops to its lowest point straight after exercise. It climbs back towards the resting level over the next 3 minutes. The climb is steepest in the first minute.. *Why:* A strong sketch shows the sharp drop and the gradual recovery, and does not rise above the resting level.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is an organ system?  
   Options: A group of organs sharing one big job / The largest organ in the body / A group of cells of one kind / Any organ with two jobs  
   **Answer:** A group of organs sharing one big job. *Why:* Organs that work together on one big job, such as digesting food, form an organ system. A group of cells of one kind is a tissue.
2. (mcq) Which chamber of the heart pumps blood out to the whole body?  
   Options: The left ventricle / The right ventricle / The left atrium / The right atrium  
   **Answer:** The left ventricle. *Why:* The left ventricle has the thickest wall, because it pumps blood all round the body. The right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
3. (mcq) Where does oxygen pass from the air into the blood?  
   Options: In the alveoli / In the windpipe / In the heart / In the throat  
   **Answer:** In the alveoli. *Why:* The alveoli are tiny air bags wrapped in capillaries. The windpipe and throat only carry the air.
4. (mcq) Which part of the brain runs your breathing and heartbeat?  
   Options: The brainstem / The cerebrum / The cerebellum / The spinal cord  
   **Answer:** The brainstem. *Why:* The brainstem keeps breathing and heartbeat going without your thinking, even in sleep. The cerebrum does the thinking.
5. (mcq) Which of these is an involuntary muscle?  
   Options: The muscle of the stomach wall / The biceps / The tongue / The calf muscle  
   **Answer:** The muscle of the stomach wall. *Why:* The stomach wall churns food without your choosing. You move the biceps, the tongue and the calf when you decide to.
6. (mcq) In which organ does a baby grow before birth?  
   Options: The uterus / The stomach / The ovary / The bladder  
   **Answer:** The uterus. *Why:* The uterus, or womb, is a bag of muscle where the baby grows for about nine months. The ovary only releases the egg.
7. (mcq) Which organ works for both the digestive system and the hormone system?  
   Options: The pancreas / The bladder / The windpipe / The skull  
   **Answer:** The pancreas. *Why:* The pancreas makes digestive juice and also the hormones insulin and glucagon. The other three each work in one system.
8. (mcq) Why is a one-breath count shorter straight after exercise?  
   Options: The body must breathe in fresh air sooner / The lungs have become smaller / The voice box is tired / The room has less air  
   **Answer:** The body must breathe in fresh air sooner. *Why:* Working muscles add carbon dioxide to the blood, so the brainstem forces the next breath sooner. The lungs stay the same size.
9. (mcq) In hard exercise, where does most of the blood go?  
   Options: To the muscles / To the gut / To the kidneys / To the brain  
   **Answer:** To the muscles. *Why:* About four fifths of the blood goes to the working muscles. The gut and the kidneys get less, and the brain gets about the same as at rest.
10. (truefalse) Food and air take completely separate paths from the face down into the body.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both pass through the throat. The paths divide lower down, into the windpipe and the food pipe.
11. (mcq) Three resting counts were 18, 20 and 19 seconds. What is the average?  
   Options: 19 seconds / 57 seconds / 20 seconds / 18 seconds  
   **Answer:** 19 seconds. *Why:* 18 + 20 + 19 = 57, and 57 ÷ 3 = 19. Choosing 57 means adding without dividing by the number of tries.
12. (mcq) Which organ adds stored sugar to the blood between meals?  
   Options: The liver / The stomach / The lungs / The heart  
   **Answer:** The liver. *Why:* The liver stores sugar as glycogen and releases it when the blood needs more. The stomach only holds food that has just been eaten.

## Applications
- Surgeons can move a healthy kidney from one person into another. The first lasting kidney transplant was done in Boston in 1954, between identical twin brothers. Surgeons in many Indian cities now transplant kidneys, livers and hearts.
- A living person can give away part of their liver. The part that is left and the part that was given both grow. Each reaches nearly full size within a few months.
- A dialysis machine does the work of failed kidneys. The Dutch doctor Willem Kolff built the first working one in the 1940s. It takes urea, spare water and spare salt out of the blood.
- A small clip on a fingertip, called a pulse oximeter, measures how much oxygen the blood is carrying. Many families first saw one during the covid years.
- Athletes use the 'talk test'. If you can still speak in full sentences while you run, the effort is moderate. If you can manage only a word or two, it is hard.
- Singers and players of the nadaswaram, the flute and the shehnai train their breathing. They breathe in quickly and let the air out slowly. They are managing the one stream of air that breath and sound share.
- People are taught not to talk or laugh with food in the mouth. Talking opens the windpipe, and food can then enter it.

## Open questions
- **How many organs does the human body have?** Scientists have no agreed rule for what counts as one organ. In 2016 a surgeon argued that the mesentery, the sheet that holds the intestines, is an organ. Others still disagree.
- **Does bone send a hormone that changes how people handle sugar?** One team found this in mice in 2007. In 2020 two other teams could not find it. Nobody has shown it clearly in people.
- **How does a liver know when to stop growing back?** After part of a liver is removed, the rest grows until it is about the right size for the body. Then it stops. The signals that tell it to stop are only partly known.
- **Can an organ from a pig work in a person for many years?** In 2022 surgeons in the United States gave a man a pig's heart. The pig's genes had been changed to suit a human body. He lived for about two months. Later tries have lasted longer, but nobody yet knows the limit.
- **Why can the brain not do two hard jobs at once without slowing down?** The slowing is easy to measure, as in Beans and Threes. Scientists still argue about what exactly is in short supply.

## Connected maths
- In the One Breath Count, three counts at rest lasted 18, 20 and 19 seconds. What is the average? **19 seconds.**. Add the three tries: 18 + 20 + 19 = 57. Divide by the number of tries: 57 ÷ 3 = 19. So the average is 19 seconds. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the One Breath Count, the resting average was 19 seconds. Straight after step-ups the count lasted 6 seconds. About what fraction of the resting time is that? **About one third.**. 3 times 6 is 18, which is close to 19. So 6 seconds is about one third of 19 seconds. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- After step-ups, the count was 6 seconds straight away. At 1, 2 and 3 minutes it was 11, 15 and 18 seconds. The resting average was 19. What do you expect at 4 minutes? Explain. **About 19 seconds, and no more than that.**. The gains are 5, then 4, then 3 seconds, so they are getting smaller. The next gain would be about 2, which gives 20. But the count is returning to the resting level of 19, so it should level off there. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the Hundred Beans, the kidneys get 5 of the 25 beans at rest. What fraction of the blood is that? How many litres a minute is it, if one bean is 200 millilitres? **One fifth of the blood, which is 1 litre a minute.**. 5 out of 25 is the same as 1 out of 5. 5 beans of 200 millilitres make 1,000 millilitres, which is 1 litre. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- In the Hundred Beans, the brain gets 4 beans out of 25 at rest. In hard exercise the brain gets 4 beans out of 100. Did the brain get less blood in exercise? Explain. **No. The brain got the same amount, 800 millilitres a minute.**. 4 beans is 4 times 200, which is 800 millilitres, in both rounds. The share fell from 16 in every 100 to 4 in every 100. That happened because the total grew four times, not because the brain lost any blood. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Hundred Beans, the muscles get 5 beans at rest and 80 in hard exercise. The gut and liver get 6 at rest and 2 in exercise. How many times as much do the muscles get? What fraction of its resting supply does the gut keep? **The muscles get 16 times as much. The gut and liver keep one third.**. 80 ÷ 5 = 16. For the gut and liver, 2 out of 6 is the same as 1 out of 3. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Your Largest Organ, a traced hand covers 96 squares of 1 square centimetre. One hand is about one hundredth of the skin. What is the area of the skin? Is it more or less than 1 square metre? **About 9,600 square centimetres, a little less than 1 square metre.**. 96 × 100 = 9,600 square centimetres. One square metre is 100 centimetres by 100 centimetres, which is 10,000 square centimetres. 9,600 is 400 less than that. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L5`)
