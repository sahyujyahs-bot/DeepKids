# Rainwater harvesting: catching the rain
`sci.water.rainwater-harvesting.L3`

**Summary.** In December 2015 Chennai was under flood water, and in June 2019 the same city's taps ran dry. How can one place have too much water and too little? You work out how much rain lands on a roof, then build a model that catches it and lets it sink into different kinds of ground. You find out why rain that sinks in refills wells and why rain that runs off is lost by evening. You also meet the stepwells, johads, eris and desert kunds with which people across India have caught the rain for thousands of years.

**Intro.** *The next time it rains, stand at a window and pick one raindrop that lands on the road. Where will that drop be in one hour? In one month?* A drop that lands on a hard road is in a drain within minutes and on the way to the sea within hours. A drop that lands on soft soil may sink in. It may stay under the ground for months or years, until a root or a well finds it. The rain is the same. What it lands on decides the rest. Today you will make it rain on a model roof and on three kinds of ground, and measure what you catch. Then you will see how this one idea has kept towns alive in deserts.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what rainwater harvesting is and give two reasons for doing it.
1. I can work out how many litres of rain fall on a roof from its size and the rainfall.
2. I can name the parts of a rooftop system and say what each part does.
3. I can explain how rain that sinks into the ground raises the water in wells, and why some ground lets water in faster than other ground.
4. I can describe a stepwell, a johad, an eri and a kund, and say which kind of place each one suits.
5. I can build a model that catches rain and compare how fast water passes through different kinds of ground.
6. I can give two views on a question people still argue about, such as whether catching rain upstream takes water from people downstream.

## Terms
- **rainwater harvesting**: Catching rain where it falls and either keeping it in a tank or letting it sink into the ground. *e.g.* A steel bucket under the corner of a roof is the smallest rainwater harvester.
- **rainfall**: How much rain falls on a place, told as the depth of water in millimetres. *e.g.* Chennai gets about 1,400 millimetres of rainfall in a year.
- **millimetre**: A small length. Ten millimetres make one centimetre, and a thousand make one metre. *e.g.* A one-rupee coin is about 1.5 millimetres thick.
- **litre**: An amount of liquid. A standard plastic water bottle for a school bag holds about one litre. *e.g.* A bucket from a hand pump holds about 10 litres.
- **square metre**: The area of a square that is one metre long on each side. *e.g.* A doormat of one metre by one metre covers one square metre of floor.
- **catchment**: The surface that collects rain and sends it to one place. *e.g.* A roof is the catchment for the water that reaches the gutter.
- **rain gauge**: A straight-sided jar with a scale that shows how deep the rain is, in millimetres. *e.g.* After a shower, a gauge on a balcony shows 12 millimetres.
- **monsoon**: A wind that changes direction with the season and brings most of India's rain. *e.g.* The south-west monsoon reaches Kerala in early June.
- **run-off**: Rain that flows away over the surface instead of soaking in. *e.g.* After a shower, water rushes along the tarred road to the drain. That is run-off.
- **infiltration**: Rain soaking down into the ground. *e.g.* Water poured on garden soil disappears in a few seconds because of infiltration.
- **gutter**: A channel along the edge of a roof that carries rain to a pipe. *e.g.* A split bamboo pole or a bent tin sheet can be a gutter.
- **down-pipe**: A pipe that carries roof water down to the ground or to a tank. *e.g.* Rain from the gutter runs down the down-pipe at the corner of the house.
- **first-flush**: A device that throws away the first, dirtiest rain from a roof before the cleaner water goes on. *e.g.* The first shower of the monsoon washes off a summer's dust, and the first-flush pipe lets it go.
- **filter**: A layer of cloth, sand or gravel that lets water through and holds back dirt. *e.g.* A cloth tied over a bottle mouth is a simple filter.
- **tank**: A closed container that stores water for use. *e.g.* A covered tank under a courtyard in Jaisalmer holds the water of one storm for a year.
- **storage**: Keeping water in a tank or a pond to use later. *e.g.* Rain stored in a tank in the monsoon is used in summer.
- **recharge**: Putting water back into the ground so that the water under it rises. *e.g.* A pit that leads roof water down into the soil is a recharge pit.
- **recharge well**: A ring-lined pit, about a metre wide and several metres deep, that leads rain down to the water under the ground. *e.g.* In Bengaluru, families dig recharge wells beside their houses to take in the roof's rain.
- **groundwater**: Water held in the gaps between grains of soil and in cracks in rock under the ground. *e.g.* The water you pump from a borewell is groundwater.
- **water table**: The level under the ground below which the gaps are full of water. *e.g.* When the water table rises, the water in a well rises with it.
- **aquifer**: A layer of sand, gravel or rock that holds groundwater and lets it move. *e.g.* The sandy plains of the Ganga have a very large aquifer.
- **permeable**: Letting water pass through. *e.g.* Sand is permeable. A cup of water vanishes into it in seconds.
- **impermeable**: Not letting water pass through, or letting almost none through. *e.g.* Wet clay is impermeable, so water sits on top of it.
- **sand**: Ground that is made of large grains with wide gaps that join up. *e.g.* Sand at a beach lets rain through almost at once.
- **clay**: Ground made of tiny flat flakes that pack tight, so water can barely pass. *e.g.* A potter uses clay because it holds water without leaking.
- **borewell**: A narrow, deep hole drilled by machine to reach groundwater, with a pump to lift it. *e.g.* A borewell can go down 300 metres, much deeper than a dug well.
- **stepwell**: A well with long flights of steps leading down to the water. *e.g.* Rani ki Vav in Patan, Gujarat, is a stepwell that goes down seven levels.
- **johad**: A curved earth wall built across a slope to hold monsoon run-off so that it soaks into the ground. *e.g.* The villagers of Gopalpura rebuilt their johad in 1985.
- **eri**: A tank of South India: a wide, shallow lake made by an earth wall across a gentle slope. It is called a kere in Kannada and a cheruvu in Telugu. *e.g.* Tamil Nadu has about 39,000 eris.
- **sluice**: A gate in a tank wall that lets water out to the fields below. *e.g.* The farmer opens the sluice, and water flows to the paddy.
- **spillway**: A stone channel that lets extra water pass safely over or round a tank wall. *e.g.* In a very big storm, the extra water leaves by the spillway, so the wall does not break.
- **silt**: Fine mud that water carries and leaves behind in a tank or a river bed. *e.g.* Each summer, people dig the silt out of a tank bed and spread it on fields.
- **kund**: A covered underground tank in the Thar desert, with a plastered, saucer-shaped catchment around it. *e.g.* One good storm fills a kund, and the cover keeps the water clean for a year.
- **kuin**: A very narrow well in the Thar desert that gathers damp held in the sand above a layer that water cannot pass. *e.g.* A kuin gives a few pots of sweet water a day, though the water below is salty.
- **gypsum**: A soft white mineral that sometimes forms a hard layer under desert sand. *e.g.* In parts of the Thar, a gypsum layer stops rain from sinking deeper.
- **zabo**: A system from Nagaland in which forest on the hilltop and ponds below it catch rain and carry it to the terraces. *e.g.* In Kikruma, the Chakhesang people use the zabo to water their rice terraces.
- **ice stupa**: A tall cone of ice, made in winter in Ladakh, that melts slowly in spring so fields have water. *e.g.* An ice stupa is built by spraying stream water into the freezing air.
- **reservoir**: A large store of water made by people. *e.g.* Dholavira had at least 16 reservoirs, some cut into rock.
- **inscription**: Writing cut into stone or metal so that it lasts. *e.g.* An inscription at Girnar in Gujarat tells how a lake was built and repaired.
- **commons**: Something that a whole community shares, such as a tank, a well or a grazing ground. *e.g.* The village tank is a commons. Everyone needs a rule about who looks after it.
- **river basin**: All the land whose rain drains to one river. *e.g.* Every field and hill whose rain runs into the Cauvery is in the Cauvery basin.
- **upstream**: Nearer the source of a river, where the water comes from. *e.g.* A village high on the river is upstream of a village near the sea.
- **downstream**: Nearer the mouth of a river, where the water goes to. *e.g.* A town at the river's mouth is downstream of the villages above it.
- **dam**: A wall built across a river or a stream to hold water back. *e.g.* The Sardar Sarovar dam holds back the Narmada.
- **sponge landscape**: Land whose plants, soil and wet ground soak up rain and let it out slowly. *e.g.* The shola forests on the Western Ghats soak up the monsoon and release it into rivers.
- **ecosystem**: A place with all the living beings in it, and the water, soil and air they share. *e.g.* A pond with its fish, frogs, plants, mud and water is one ecosystem.
- **sponge city**: A city designed so that most rain soaks in where it falls, through parks, ponds and paving that lets water through. *e.g.* China began building sponge cities from about 2013.
- **evaporation**: Water turning into a gas and rising into the air. *e.g.* A bowl of water left open in the sun gets lower each day because of evaporation.
- **mulch**: A cover of dry leaves or straw laid on top of soil. *e.g.* A layer of dry leaves on a pot keeps the soil damp for longer.

## Explanations
### Rainwater harvesting is catching rain where it falls, before it runs away · *defined*
**Definition:** Rainwater harvesting means collecting rain close to where it lands, and either storing it for use or helping it sink into the ground.

A farmer harvests a crop by gathering it before it spoils. Rain can be gathered too. Left alone, most rain that lands on roofs and roads runs off to a drain, a river and the sea within hours. Rainwater harvesting means stopping it on the way. There are two things to do with caught rain. You can keep it in a tank and use it. Or you can lead it into the ground, where it tops up the water that wells draw on. Both have the same aim. The rain that falls here should serve the living beings who live here.
- e.g. A bucket under the corner of a roof is the smallest rainwater harvester.
- e.g. A village pond that fills in the monsoon is harvesting rain from all the land that slopes towards it.
- e.g. A pit filled with gravel under a roof pipe sends rain into the ground.
- e.g. Soil kept covered with plants catches rain on every leaf and lets it soak in slowly.
### One millimetre of rain on one square metre is one litre · *defined*
**Definition:** Rainfall is measured as the depth of water the rain would leave on flat ground if none ran off or soaked in, in millimetres. The area that collects rain is called the catchment.

How much water lands on a roof? There is an easy sum. Picture a flat square, one metre on each side. If one millimetre of rain falls on it, the square holds exactly one litre. So litres of rain equal the area in square metres times the rain in millimetres. A roof of 100 square metres is about the size of a small flat. Chennai gets about 1,400 millimetres of rain in a year. So 100 times 1,400 is 1,40,000 litres falling on that one roof each year. Some splashes away or dries off. About four-fifths can be caught, which is more than a lakh litres.
- e.g. Jaisalmer, in the Thar desert, gets only about 200 millimetres a year. The same roof there still receives about 20,000 litres. At 20 litres a day for each of four people, that is drinking and cooking water for about eight months.
- e.g. Mawsynram, in Meghalaya, gets about 11,800 millimetres a year, among the highest in the world.
- e.g. A heavy shower of 20 millimetres puts 2,000 litres on a 100 square metre roof in an afternoon.
- e.g. A rain gauge is a straight-sided jar with a scale. It measures the depth of rain in millimetres.
### Most of India's rain falls in a few months, so it must be kept for the rest · *defined*
**Definition:** The monsoon is a wind that changes direction with the season and brings most of India's rain. Most of India receives it from June to September.

In much of India it rains for about four months and hardly at all for eight. About three-quarters of the country's yearly rain arrives with the south-west monsoon, from June to September. Tamil Nadu is different. It gets most of its rain from October to December, when the winds turn round. Even inside the rainy months, the rain comes in bursts. In many places, most of a year's rain falls in about a hundred hours of heavy showers. A year has 8,760 hours. So the puzzle of water in India is a puzzle of timing. The rain of a hundred hours has to last the other 8,660.
- e.g. A dry river bed in May and the same river in flood in August are one river at two times of the year.
- e.g. Farmers sow by the monsoon. A delay of two weeks can change a whole harvest.
- e.g. The Thar desert may get its entire year's rain in a handful of storms.
- e.g. Mountains store winter snow and release it as melt-water in summer. Snow is nature's way of keeping water for later.
- **Weather scientists at the India Meteorological Department:** The monsoon is driven by the land heating faster than the sea in summer. It is measured by thousands of rain gauges and by satellites, and it differs a great deal from year to year.
- **Thiruvalluvar, in Thirukkural, chapter 2, 'The glory of rain', verse 20:** Without water, life in the world cannot go on for anyone. And without rain, water does not flow. He places rain second only to the opening chapter, as what everything else rests on.
- **Anupam Mishra, an Indian writer on water, in the 1990s:** He wrote that the people of the Thar never thought of their land as short of water. They counted the rain as it came, drop by drop, and built their lives around keeping every one. Scarcity, he argued, lies partly in how a society treats its rain.
### The same city can flood and run dry, because hard ground makes rain run fast · *defined*
**Definition:** Run-off is rain that flows away over the surface. Infiltration is rain soaking down into the ground.

Rain that lands has two roads. It can run off over the surface, or it can soak in. On soil with plants, most of a gentle rain soaks in. On concrete, tar and tiles, almost all of it runs off, fast. A city is mostly hard surface. So in a big storm the water rushes to the low places all at once and floods them. Two days later it has reached the sea. Very little has gone into the ground. When summer comes, the wells are empty. In December 2015 Chennai was flooded. In June 2019 its four main lakes were nearly dry. The yearly rain had not changed much. The ground had.
- e.g. Chennai's Pallikaranai marsh, which once soaked up flood water like a sponge, has shrunk from about 5,000 hectares to roughly 600 as buildings spread over it. A hectare is a square with sides of 100 metres.
- e.g. A paved courtyard sheds a shower in minutes. A garden of the same size is still damp the next day.
- e.g. A sloping field with bare soil loses rain and soil together. Low ridges built across the slope slow both.
- e.g. A lake bed built over with houses is still the lowest ground, and the water still goes there.
### A rooftop system has five parts: roof, gutter, first-flush, filter and tank · *defined*
**Definition:** A rooftop rainwater system is a set of parts that leads rain from a roof to a store. The gutter is the channel along the roof's edge. The first-flush is a device that throws away the first, dirtiest water.

Follow one raindrop through a house that harvests rain. It lands on the roof, which is the catchment. It runs down to the gutter, a channel along the edge, and into a down-pipe. Then comes a clever part. The first rain after a dry spell washes dust, leaves and bird droppings off the roof. A side pipe called a first-flush catches that first dirty water and lets it go. The cleaner water that follows passes through a filter of gravel, sand or mesh. Then it goes to a covered tank, or to a pit that leads it into the ground. A cover keeps out sunlight, mosquitoes and dirt.
- e.g. A tank kept dark grows no green algae, because algae need light.
- e.g. A mesh over the top of the down-pipe stops leaves from blocking it.
- e.g. The roof is swept before the monsoon, the way a plate is washed before food.
- e.g. A sloping tin roof and a flat concrete terrace both work. A thatched roof gives water that is coloured by the straw.
### Is rainwater safe to drink? It depends, and scientists disagree on how careful to be · *debated*
**Definition:** Safe drinking water is water with nothing in it, living or non-living, at a level that harms health.

A raindrop begins very clean. It forms from water that rose into the air as a gas and left salt and dirt behind. On the way down it picks things up. It dissolves a little gas from the air, which makes all rain very slightly sour. Over cities it gathers smoke and dust. On the roof it meets bird droppings. So stored rain is usually filtered and boiled before drinking. Many families in the Thar and in the hill towns of Mizoram have drunk stored rain for generations. In 2022 scientists reported that rain everywhere now carries tiny traces of long-lasting factory chemicals. How much that matters is argued over.
- e.g. Rainwater has very little dissolved salt, so soap lathers in it at once and clothes wash well.
- e.g. Rain caught from the open sky in a clean vessel, without touching a roof, is cleaner than roof water.
- e.g. Underground tanks in Rajasthan are cleaned and lime-washed before the rains.
- e.g. Rainwater used for washing, flushing and gardens needs no boiling, and that is most of what a home uses.
- **The World Health Organization, in its drinking-water guidelines:** Rainwater from a clean roof, with a first-flush and a covered tank, is generally of good quality. The main risk is germs from droppings, and boiling or simple treatment deals with that.
- **Ian Cousins and colleagues at Stockholm University, in 2022:** They gathered measurements of a group of factory chemicals called PFAS in rain around the world, even in Antarctica and Tibet. The levels were above the strictest new health limits set in the United States. They argued that by those limits, rain is no longer safe to drink untreated anywhere.
- **Other water scientists who answered them:** The strictest limits are set far below any level known to cause illness, and river and well water carry the same chemicals. For a family with salty or dirty well water, well-kept rainwater is still the safer drink.
- **Families of the Thar desert, as recorded by Anupam Mishra:** They call rain 'palar pani' and prize it above all other water for drinking. The catchment around a tank is kept so clean that nobody walks on it with shoes.
### Two choices for caught rain: store it in a tank, or sink it into the ground · *defined*
**Definition:** Storage means keeping harvested rain in a tank for direct use. Recharge means leading it into the ground to add to the water there.

Once rain is caught, there is a choice. A tank gives water you can count and use at once. But a tank big enough for eight dry months is costly and takes space. The ground is a far bigger tank, and it is free. Rain led into a pit sinks down and joins the water that wells and hand pumps draw on. It loses nothing to the sun on the way. But it does not stay under your house. It spreads to your neighbours too, and it cannot be had back without a well. Dry places with little rain mostly store. Wetter places with good soil mostly recharge. Many homes do both.
- e.g. In Jaisalmer, where every drop is needed for drinking, rain goes into covered underground tanks.
- e.g. In Chennai, where wells are common, most roof water is led into recharge wells.
- e.g. An open tank in a hot, dry place can lose more than a metre of depth in a year to the sun and wind. A covered or underground store loses almost nothing.
- e.g. A house can fill a small tank first and send the overflow into the ground.
- **Engineers who design for single homes:** Store first. Water in your own tank is certain. Water sent into the ground may be pumped out by someone else or flow away below.
- **Groundwater scientists and groups such as the Rain Centre in Chennai, founded by Sekhar Raghavan in 2002:** Recharge first. A whole street recharging together lifts the water under everyone. No family could afford a tank as large as the ground beneath the street.
### Why sinking rain refills wells: the water table rises · *defined*
**Definition:** Groundwater is water held in the gaps between grains of soil and in cracks in rock. The water table is the level below which the gaps are full of water. A layer that holds and passes water is called an aquifer.

Under the ground there is no lake. There is soil and rock with small gaps, and below a certain depth the gaps are full of water. The top of that wet zone is called the water table. A well is a hole dug down past the water table, and water seeps in from the sides until it stands at that level. Now pour rain into the ground. It trickles down and fills more gaps, so the water table rises. The water in every well nearby rises with it. Pump out more than the rain puts in, and the water table falls and wells go dry. A well shows you the level of something you cannot see.
- e.g. In 2003 Tamil Nadu made rainwater harvesting compulsory for every building. Surveys in Chennai in the years after found the water in many wells standing several metres higher.
- e.g. In Bengaluru, families of the Mannu Vaddar community, traditional well-diggers, have dug thousands of recharge wells with the group Biome since about 2015.
- e.g. Water in the ground moves slowly: perhaps a metre a day through coarse sand, and only a few centimetres a year through clay.
- e.g. The Qur'an, chapter 23, verse 18, speaks of water sent down from the sky in measure and lodged in the earth.
- **Groundwater scientists:** An aquifer is a slow, shared store. Water added in one place spreads sideways, so recharge helps a neighbourhood, and pumping harms a neighbourhood.
- **Indian law, as it has stood since British rule:** Whoever owns the land may pump as much as they like from under it. Many water experts, such as those who wrote India's 2016 draft water law, say this treats a shared store as private and should change. The question is not settled.
### Some ground drinks rain quickly and some hardly at all: sand, clay and hard rock · *defined*
**Definition:** Permeable ground has connected gaps that let water pass through. Impermeable ground, such as clay or solid rock, lets almost none through.

Pour a cup of water on sand and it is gone in seconds. Pour it on wet clay and it sits there. Sand grains are large, with wide gaps between them that join up. Clay grains are tiny flat flakes that pack tight, and water can barely squeeze past. Solid rock, such as the granite under Bengaluru and Hyderabad, has no gaps except its cracks. So recharge works well in sandy ground and river plains, and poorly where clay or solid rock lies near the top. A good recharge pit is dug down through any clay to a layer that lets water in. The same clay that blocks recharge is used to line ponds, to hold water in.
- e.g. The plains of the Ganga are deep sand and silt, and hold very large amounts of groundwater.
- e.g. In the hard rock of the Deccan, a borewell finds water only if it happens to cross a crack.
- e.g. A village pond often has a clay bed, which is why it holds water for months.
- e.g. Potters choose clay because it holds water. Builders choose sand and gravel for drains because they do not.
### India pumps more groundwater than any other country, faster than rain puts it back · *defined*
**Definition:** A borewell is a narrow, deep hole drilled by machine to reach groundwater, with a pump to lift it.

A dug well reaches down perhaps 10 or 20 metres. A borewell can go down 300. Since the 1960s, India has drilled crores of them. Today India pumps roughly 250 cubic kilometres of groundwater a year. One cubic kilometre is a block of water a kilometre long, a kilometre wide and a kilometre deep, which is a thousand billion litres. That is about a quarter of all the groundwater pumped in the world. It is more than China and the United States together. Most of it waters crops. In many districts, more is pumped out each year than the rain puts back. So the water table falls, wells are drilled deeper, and shallow wells and village ponds go dry. Rainwater harvesting puts water back. It works only if less is taken out than goes in.
- e.g. In 2009 scientists used two satellites that weigh the Earth from space. They found that Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan and Delhi had lost more than 100 cubic kilometres of groundwater in six years.
- e.g. Growing one kilogram of rice with pumped water can use about 3,000 to 5,000 litres.
- e.g. In parts of Punjab the water table has been falling by about half a metre or more each year.
- e.g. Deep water is often very old. Some of it fell as rain thousands of years ago and will not come back in our lifetimes.
- **Farmers who depend on borewells:** A borewell lets a small farmer water crops whenever needed, without waiting for a canal or the rain. It is one reason India grows enough grain to feed itself.
- **Groundwater scientists such as Tushaar Shah and Himanshu Kulkarni:** Free or cheap electricity for pumps encourages over-pumping. They ask for villages to measure their own rain and groundwater and agree together which crops the water can support.
### Stepwells: stairs that go down to meet the water · *defined*
**Definition:** A stepwell is a well with long flights of steps leading down to the water, so that people can reach it whatever its level. It is called a vav in Gujarat and a baoli or bawdi in North India.

In a dry land, the water table sinks far down in summer and rises after the rains. How do you reach water whose level keeps changing? Builders in Gujarat and Rajasthan answered with the stepwell. They dug a deep trench down to the groundwater and lined it with stone steps and pillared halls, level below level. In the monsoon the water rises and covers the lower steps. In summer people walk down to it. Deep in the earth the air is cool, so a stepwell was also a place to rest. Rani ki Vav, in Patan, Gujarat, was built about 1063 CE by Queen Udayamati. It goes down seven levels.
- e.g. Chand Baori, at Abhaneri in Rajasthan, built about the 9th century CE, has about 3,500 narrow steps on 13 levels.
- e.g. The Adalaj stepwell near Ahmedabad was completed in 1499 CE under Queen Rudabai.
- e.g. Rani ki Vav lay buried in river silt for centuries. It was dug out in the 20th century and is shown on the 100 rupee note.
- e.g. Agrasen ki Baoli stands among the office towers of central Delhi.
- **Historians of architecture, such as Morna Livingston and Jutta Jain-Neubauer:** Stepwells were water works, meeting halls and places of worship in one. Many were paid for by queens, traders' wives and other women, as public gifts.
- **Water engineers today:** A stepwell stores little rain itself. It gives access to groundwater. It worked only while the water table stayed within reach. Many stepwells are dry now because borewells nearby have pulled the water table far below the lowest step.
### Johads: earth walls that brought a river back · *defined*
**Definition:** A johad is a curved wall of earth built across a slope or a small stream. It holds monsoon run-off long enough for it to soak into the ground.

In the 1980s the Alwar district of Rajasthan was drying. Wells were empty, and men had left the villages to find work. In 1985 a young man, Rajendra Singh, came to the village of Gopalpura wanting to help. An old farmer, Mangu Lal Meena, told him to stop talking and dig. He showed him where the village's old johads had stood. A johad is a curved earth wall across the path of the rain. It makes a pond in the monsoon, and the pond slowly soaks away into the ground. The villagers rebuilt theirs by hand. Wells filled. Over fifteen years, villages across Alwar built thousands. The Arvari, a river dry for decades, began to flow all year.
- e.g. Many women joined in the digging and gained most. They no longer walked hours each day for water.
- e.g. In 1999 about 70 villages formed the Arvari River Parliament to agree on rules, such as growing no sugarcane, which is a thirsty crop.
- e.g. Rajendra Singh's group, Tarun Bharat Sangh, received the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 2001 and the Stockholm Water Prize in 2015.
- e.g. A johad's site is chosen by reading the land: where the rain runs, and where the rock below has cracks.
- **The villagers of Alwar and Tarun Bharat Sangh:** The knowledge came from village elders, the labour from villagers and the rules from their own assembly. They say water works that people build and own themselves are the ones that last.
- **Rajasthan's irrigation engineers, in a dispute in 2001:** They declared one village-built dam in Alwar illegal and unsafe, because it was built without approved designs on a stream the state controlled. The dam was allowed to stay after public protest. The question of who has the right to hold back a stream remained.
### Eris and keres: chains of tanks across South India · *defined*
**Definition:** A tank is a wide, shallow lake made by building a long earth wall across a gentle slope to hold monsoon run-off. It is called an eri in Tamil, a kere in Kannada and a cheruvu in Telugu.

Much of South India has hard rock below and rain in one short season. People there built tanks. A long, low earth wall across a gentle slope holds the run-off as a broad lake. A stone sluice lets water out to the paddy fields below. A stone spillway lets extra water pass safely on. The tanks were built in chains. The overflow of the highest tank fills the next one down, and then the next. So the water goes all down the slope, until hardly a drop reaches the sea unused. Tamil Nadu alone has about 39,000 tanks. Stone inscriptions at Uttaramerur, from about 920 CE, record a village committee chosen each year to look after the tank.
- e.g. The silt dug out of a tank bed each summer was spread on fields as manure, which also kept the tank deep.
- e.g. Ramappa lake in Telangana was built under the Kakatiya rulers in the 13th century CE and still waters fields.
- e.g. Bengaluru once had hundreds of linked lakes. Many now lie under bus stands, stadiums and housing.
- e.g. Tank water also soaks down and keeps nearby wells full.
- **Writers at the Centre for Science and Environment, in the 1997 book 'Dying Wisdom' edited by Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain:** Villages managed tanks themselves for a thousand years. When the British government took over the tanks and taxed the land, village care collapsed. Reviving community control is the way forward.
- **David Mosse, a British anthropologist, in his 2003 book 'The Rule of Water':** From records and years of fieldwork in Tamil Nadu, he found that tanks were mostly controlled by local chiefs and dominant castes. Dalits often did the digging and were kept away from the water. He warns against remembering the old system as more equal than it was.
### Desert ways: the kund that catches a storm and the kuin that gathers hidden damp · *defined*
**Definition:** A kund, or tanka, is a covered underground tank with a clean, sloping catchment around it. A kuin is a very narrow well that collects moisture trapped in sand above a layer that water cannot pass.

The Thar desert gets very little rain, and the groundwater is often deep and salty. People there found two answers. The first is the kund. A circle of ground is cleaned and plastered so that it slopes gently inward, like a saucer. At the centre is a covered tank under the ground. One good storm fills it, and the cover keeps the water cool, dark and clean for a year. The second is stranger. In some places a hard layer of gypsum lies under the sand. Rain that sinks in cannot pass it. The damp stays in the sand above, sweet and separate from the salty water below. A narrow well, the kuin, gathers this damp, a few pots a day.
- e.g. A kuin is so narrow that the digger works with a short tool, in a space barely wider than their shoulders.
- e.g. Desert families name three kinds of water. Palar pani is rain, patali pani is deep groundwater, and rejani pani is the damp held in the sand between.
- e.g. Gadisar lake at Jaisalmer was built in 1367 CE and supplied the town for hundreds of years.
- e.g. A khadin is a desert field with an earth wall at its lower end. Run-off collects there, soaks in, and a crop is grown on the damp soil.
- **Anupam Mishra, in his 1995 book 'Rajasthan ki Rajat Boondein':** He recorded these methods from the people who build them and named their communities. He argued that this is exact science, worked out without laboratories. He said engineers had ignored it because it was not written in their language.
- **Government water engineers:** A kund or kuin gives a family drinking water and no more. For farming and towns, they say, the desert needs canals and pipelines. The Indira Gandhi Canal now brings Himalayan river water into the Thar. Critics answer that the canal has also brought water-logging and salty soil.
### Hill and mountain ways: bamboo pipes, the zabo, and towers of ice · *defined*
**Definition:** In hills, harvesting means slowing water as it runs downhill. In cold deserts, it means keeping winter water frozen until it is needed in spring.

On a steep hillside, rain is gone in minutes. In Meghalaya, Khasi and Jaintia farmers split bamboo into channels and lead spring water for hundreds of metres along the slope. At each fork the flow divides, until it reaches every plant as a slow drip. In Kikruma, a village in Nagaland, the Chakhesang people built the zabo. Forest is kept on the hilltop. Ponds below it catch the run-off. The water then passes through cattle yards and reaches the rice terraces carrying manure. In Ladakh, where it hardly rains, the engineer Chewang Norphel began in the late 1980s to freeze stream water on shaded slopes in winter. In 2013 Sonam Wangchuk began building cones of ice called ice stupas.
- e.g. A bamboo system can take about 20 litres a minute at the top and deliver it as a few dozen drops a minute at each plant.
- e.g. An ice stupa is a tall cone, so little of its surface faces the sun, and it melts slowly through spring when fields need water.
- e.g. In the Apatani valley of Arunachal Pradesh, rice and fish are raised together in fields fed by one shared set of channels.
- e.g. The word 'zabo' means holding back water.
- **The hill communities who built these systems:** Each system is a set of shared rules as much as a set of channels. The rules say who keeps the forest, who cleans the ponds and whose field gets water first.
- **Scientists who study glaciers, such as Marcus Nüsser's group in 2019:** They measured Ladakh's artificial ice stores and found them useful for a village in spring. But the stores are too small to replace the shrinking natural glaciers that feed them. They warned against calling ice stupas a cure for climate change.
### The oldest records: Dholavira's reservoirs, a lake repaired for 800 years, and a rain gauge · *defined*
**Definition:** A reservoir is a large store of water made by people. An inscription is writing cut into stone or metal to last.

Catching rain is among India's oldest sciences. Dholavira, a Harappan city in the salt desert of Kutch, flourished from about 3000 BCE to 1500 BCE. Its people built dams across two seasonal streams and led the water into at least 16 reservoirs, some cut into solid rock. At Girnar in Gujarat, a rock inscription of the ruler Rudradaman, from about 150 CE, tells the story of a lake called Sudarshana. It was built about 450 years earlier under Chandragupta Maurya. It was improved under Ashoka, broken by a storm and repaired by Rudradaman, who records that he did not tax the people for it. The lake was repaired again about 300 years later. People looked after it for some 800 years.
- e.g. Kautilya's Arthashastra, book 2, chapter 5, tells the keeper of the storehouse to set out a bowl of fixed width as a rain gauge. Chapter 24 lists how much rain different regions receive.
- e.g. Varahamihira's Brihat Samhita, written in the 6th century CE, has a chapter on finding underground water from signs such as termite mounds and certain trees.
- e.g. The Rig Veda, book 7, hymn 49, names waters that come from the sky, waters that are dug for and waters that rise by themselves.
- e.g. Dholavira was added to the United Nations' list of world heritage sites in 2021.
- **Archaeologists such as R. S. Bisht, who excavated Dholavira from 1990:** The reservoirs take up about a tenth of the city's area. A city in a salt desert put that much of its land and labour into water, which shows how central harvesting was.
- **Scientists who tested Varahamihira's signs, such as E. A. V. Prasad in the 1980s:** Some signs hold up. Termites need damp soil and their mounds are often found above shallow groundwater or along cracks in rock. Other signs have not been confirmed. The old text is a set of field observations worth testing, and not a guarantee.
### Why were old systems abandoned, and who was kept away from them? · *debated*
**Definition:** A commons is something shared by a community, such as a tank, a well or a grazing ground. Who may use a commons, and who must maintain it, are decided by that society's rules.

India has lakhs of old tanks, stepwells and ponds. Many are silted, built over or full of rubbish. Why? Piped water and borewells arrived, and they seemed to need no shared work. Under British rule, the government took over many tanks, and villages stopped repairing what was no longer theirs. Cities grew over lake beds. There is a harder truth too. The old systems were often not open to all. In many places Dalits were forbidden to draw water from the common tank. On 20 March 1927, at Mahad in Maharashtra, B. R. Ambedkar led thousands of people to drink from the Chavdar tank, to claim that right.
- e.g. In Telangana, a programme begun in 2015 has dug the silt out of thousands of old tanks.
- e.g. Jodhpur's Toorji ka Jhalra stepwell was cleared of decades of rubbish by volunteers and is in use as a public place again.
- e.g. A tank with houses built on its bed floods those houses in every heavy monsoon.
- e.g. The Constitution of India, in Article 15, forbids keeping any person from wells, tanks and bathing ghats on grounds of caste.
- **Anupam Mishra, and Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain of the Centre for Science and Environment:** The old systems fitted each landscape exactly and were kept up by the people who used them. Their neglect is a loss of knowledge. They can be revived to work beside modern supply.
- **B. R. Ambedkar, and scholars such as David Mosse:** Water in the old village was a tool of caste. A revival that brings back the tank must not bring back the rules about who may touch it. Piped water to every home, whatever its other faults, does not ask a person's caste.
- **City water engineers:** A city of a crore people cannot live on tanks and wells alone. It needs large reservoirs and pipelines too. Old water bodies are most useful for recharge and flood control.
### Does catching rain upstream take water from people downstream? Small works and big dams · *debated*
**Definition:** A river basin is all the land whose rain drains to one river. Upstream means nearer the river's source. Downstream means nearer its mouth.

Rain caught in one village is rain that does not run on to the next. So is harvesting only moving water from one group of people to another? Scientists argue about this. In a wet region, most rain rushes to the sea in floods that nobody can use, and catching some of it harms no one. In a dry river basin where every drop is already used, a thousand new ponds upstream may mean an emptier dam downstream. There is a second argument. Should a country store water in a few giant dams or in lakhs of small works? Big dams hold water through years of drought. They also drown forests and villages.
- e.g. The Arvari flowed again after johads were built upstream, which suggests that slowed water can feed a river in the dry months.
- e.g. The Sardar Sarovar dam on the Narmada supplies water to dry parts of Gujarat. Its lake displaced lakhs of people, many of them Adivasis.
- e.g. A shallow pond loses a larger share of its water to the sun than a deep reservoir does.
- e.g. India plans to join rivers with long canals, to move water from wet basins to dry ones. Scientists disagree on whether any basin truly has water to spare.
- **M. Dinesh Kumar and colleagues, Indian water researchers, in 2008:** In naturally dry basins, they found that harvesting a lot upstream often cut the flow into reservoirs downstream. They called the hope of 'new' water there a mirage, like a lake in the desert that is not really there. They asked that harvesting be planned for a whole basin.
- **Rajendra Singh, and researchers such as Tushaar Shah:** Small works put water into the ground, where it does not evaporate and where millions of small farmers can reach it. In Saurashtra and Alwar, wells and dry-season flows improved after people built thousands of check dams.
- **Medha Patkar and the Narmada Bachao Andolan, since 1985:** The costs of big dams fall on those who are moved, and the benefits go elsewhere. They ask that the people who lose their land be asked first and settled fairly, and that smaller alternatives be tried.
- **Engineers at India's Central Water Commission:** Only large reservoirs can carry water from a good year into a bad one and supply big cities. Small tanks often dry up in exactly the drought years when they are needed most.
### Other living beings harvest rain too: beetles, lizards, elephants, beavers and forests · *defined*
**Definition:** A sponge landscape is land whose plants, soil and wet ground soak up rain and release it slowly.

People did not invent rainwater harvesting. In the Namib desert of Africa, a beetle climbs a dune at dawn and stands head-down in the fog. Drops gather on the beetle's back and roll to the mouth. The thorny devil, a lizard of the Australian desert, has skin grooved with tiny channels. Water that touches any part of the body is drawn along the grooves to the mouth. Elephants dig wells in dry river beds with their feet and trunks, and many other animals drink there after them. Beavers build dams of sticks that raise the water table across a whole valley. A forest does it on the largest scale. Leaves, roots and deep leaf litter slow the rain and feed streams all year.
- e.g. The shola forests and grasslands on top of the Western Ghats soak up the monsoon. They release it slowly into the rivers of Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka.
- e.g. On a large tree, rain runs along the branches and down the trunk, and reaches the soil right at the roots.
- e.g. A desert rhubarb plant, studied in 2009, has grooved leaves that channel light rain to its root. It collects many times the water its neighbours get.
- e.g. Engineers have copied the beetle and the lizard to design fog-catching nets and surfaces that move water without pumps.
- **Ecologists:** Beavers, elephants and forests are 'ecosystem engineers'. An ecosystem is a place with all the living beings in it, and the water, soil and air they share. They reshape how water moves, and many other beings depend on what they build. Protecting them is water management.
- **Robert Jackson and colleagues, in 2005:** Trees also drink. Their study of tree plantations around the world found that planting fast-growing trees on grassland usually reduced the flow in nearby streams. A natural forest and a plantation are not the same, and 'more trees' does not always mean more water.
### Harvesting today: laws, recharge wells and 'sponge cities' · *defined*
**Definition:** A sponge city is a city designed to soak up most rain where it falls. It uses parks, ponds, rooftop gardens and paving that lets water through.

The old idea is being built into new cities. Tamil Nadu has required every building to harvest rain since 2003. Bengaluru requires it for larger plots. Singapore, an island with no large river, collects rain from about two-thirds of its land. In China, the landscape designer Yu Kongjian argued that cities should stop fighting water with concrete drains. From about 2013 China began building 'sponge cities', with wetland parks, ponds and paving that lets water through. The aim is to soak up most of a storm where it lands. Engineers still test how well this works in the very heaviest rain. The idea is the one that built the johad and the eri: slow the water down.
- e.g. Paving blocks with gaps between them let rain through to the soil. A car park laid this way floods less.
- e.g. A rooftop garden holds part of a shower in its soil and releases it over hours.
- e.g. A school with a large roof and playground can harvest lakhs of litres in a year.
- e.g. A recharge well is a ring-lined pit, a metre wide and several metres deep, that takes a roof's rain down past the topsoil.
- **Yu Kongjian and supporters of sponge cities:** He grew up in a farming village in China and saw how ponds and fields absorbed the monsoon. He argued that giving water room is cheaper and safer than ever larger drains.
- **Flood engineers who studied the 2021 flood in Zhengzhou, China:** Zhengzhou had spent heavily on sponge features, and a year's worth of rain fell in three days. The city flooded badly. They conclude that sponge design handles ordinary storms well and that extreme storms still need large drains and flood channels.

## Mostly agreed
- Nearly all the liquid fresh water on Earth is under the ground. *How we know:* Surveys of the world's water, gathered by Igor Shiklomanov in 1993, find this. Groundwater holds about 99 in every 100 parts of all liquid fresh water. Rivers and lakes together hold less than one part.
- India receives a very large amount of rain and snow, and can use only about a quarter of it. *How we know:* India's Central Water Commission estimates about 4,000 cubic kilometres of rain and snow each year. About 1,100 cubic kilometres can be put to use. The rest evaporates, soaks in beyond reach or runs to the sea in floods.
- Water under the ground can be dated, and some of it is thousands of years old. *How we know:* Scientists measure rare substances in water that slowly break down at a known rate, like a slow clock. Shallow well water is often a few months to a few decades old. Water from deep borewells in north-west India has been dated to thousands of years.
- Covering soil with plants or mulch greatly increases how much rain soaks in. *How we know:* Farmers and scientists have tested this in field trials in India and elsewhere. They compare a bare plot with a covered plot on the same slope. The covered plot loses far less rain as run-off, and far less soil is washed away.

## How we found out
**Henry Darcy, Matthew Rodell, Isabella Velicogna, James Famiglietti, V. M. Tiwari, John Wahr, Sean Swenson, 1856 to 2009.** For most of history, nobody could say how water moves under the ground. Many people pictured underground rivers and lakes that would never run out. It was a fair guess. A well dug deeper nearly always found more water. In 1856, in the French town of Dijon, the engineer Henry Darcy filled a tall pipe with sand and measured how fast water passed through it. He found a rule. The flow depends on how hard the water is pushed and on the size of the gaps between the grains. Groundwater was a slow seep through sand and rock, and it could be measured. Then came the question of how much there is. In 2002 two satellites were launched that follow each other round the Earth and sense tiny changes in its pull. Where water is lost from the ground, the pull weakens very slightly. In 2009 Matthew Rodell, Isabella Velicogna and James Famiglietti reported that north-west India had lost more than 100 cubic kilometres of groundwater in six years. In the same year V. M. Tiwari of Hyderabad, with John Wahr and Sean Swenson, found the same across northern India.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that groundwater lay in endless underground rivers and lakes. It was believable because nobody could see it, and deeper wells kept finding more. Darcy's sand pipe showed that it seeps slowly through gaps, and the satellites showed that it is being used up faster than rain returns it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Rainwater harvesting creates new water for a dry region.** (Tarun Bharat Sangh in Alwar, and the Government of India's 'Catch the Rain' campaign; *debated*)  
  For: In Alwar and Saurashtra, wells filled and dry-season river flow returned after thousands of small structures were built. Water in the ground is saved from evaporation.  
  Against: M. Dinesh Kumar and colleagues found in 2008 that in basins where all the water is already used, harvesting upstream reduced flow to reservoirs downstream. The total rain is fixed. Harvesting changes who gets it and when.
- **India's traditional water systems were run fairly by village communities.** (Many writers on traditional water wisdom, including the authors of 'Dying Wisdom' in 1997; *debated*)  
  For: Inscriptions such as those at Uttaramerur record elected tank committees, and many systems worked for centuries on shared labour and shared rules.  
  Against: David Mosse studied the tanks of Tamil Nadu. He found that dominant castes and local chiefs often controlled them, and that Dalits were kept away from the water. The struggle at Mahad in 1927 shows the same.
- **Planting more trees always brings more water.** (A common belief, and many tree-planting drives; *minority*)  
  For: Natural forests on hills slow the rain, hold soil and feed streams through the dry season. Clearing them causes floods and dry springs.  
  Against: Robert Jackson and colleagues found in 2005 that plantations of fast-growing trees on former grassland usually reduced stream flow. The trees used more water than the grass had.
- **India should join its rivers with canals to move water from wet regions to dry ones, and stop relying on small works.** (India's National Water Development Agency and supporters of river-linking; *debated*)  
  For: Some basins flood while others suffer drought in the same year. Canals could even this out and water crores of hectares.  
  Against: Scientists such as Subimal Ghosh showed in 2016 that rainfall in India's 'surplus' basins has been falling, so the spare water may not exist. The canals would drown forests and move many people.

## Roots
You have caught model rain and watched it sink through different kinds of ground. Now we ask what fast water and slow water tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You poured one cup of water onto a steel plate, and it ran off in two seconds. You poured the same cup onto soil, and it dripped out for minutes. The amount was the same each time. So a flood and a dry well can come from one and the same rain. What we call 'too much' and 'too little' water is often the same water, moving at different speeds.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of flood and drought as opposites, one meaning too much water and the other too little. That makes us fight them as two separate enemies, with drains for one and tankers for the other. The model shows a single cause behind both: water leaving faster than the land can take it in.  
*Evidence:* In the model, a cup of water left the hard plate at once and left the soil slowly over several minutes. Chennai flooded in December 2015 and ran dry in June 2019 with little change in its yearly rain.

- *who am i:* More than half of your body is water. Almost all of it fell as rain somewhere, and you will hand it on within weeks. You are one of the places where the rain is slowed down for a while. Seeing this, you can ask whether the water is passing through you, or is you.
- *what is life:* A forest, a beaver's pond, a root and a cell all do the same thing: they hold water back from running straight to the sea. Wherever water lingers, living beings gather. Life can be seen as water taking the long way round.
- *what is this world:* The Earth has had nearly the same water for billions of years. The rain on your roof has been ocean, glacier, river and the sap of ancient trees. The world does not make new water for each of us. It lends the same water to everyone in turn.

The model made one thing plain: speed decides whether water is a gift or a loss. The johad, the eri, the kund and the forest are all ways of asking water to stay a little longer. Your own body is another. Under the ground there are no fences, so water that one family slows becomes water that a whole street can draw. The science of aquifers says this in numbers. Some traditions go one step further. They say that consciousness is the ground from which the rain, the land and the drinker all arise. They say that the water moving between them is one thing passing through many hands. Science does not say this, and many people do not take the last step. Their view is given below.

- **Rig Veda, book 7, hymn 49, verse 2:** The poet calls on the waters that come from the sky. The poet calls on the waters that flow in dug channels and the waters that rise by themselves. All of them are bright and pure, and their goal is the sea. May those waters protect me here. (More than three thousand years ago the poet sorted water as this lesson does: rain, water that people dig for, and springs. The verse sees them as one family of waters on one journey to the sea, and asks them to stay and protect. Harvesting is the practical form of that request.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 8, by Laozi:** The highest good is like water. Water benefits all things and does not compete. It settles in the low places that people look down on, and so it is close to the Way. (The lesson shows water doing exactly this: seeking the lowest ground and sinking out of sight, where it becomes the store that every well depends on. The text turns that into a model for how to live. The science confirms the behaviour of water and is silent on how we should live.)
- **Thirukkural, chapter 2, verse 20, by Thiruvalluvar:** Without water, life in this world cannot go on for anyone, whoever they are. And without rain, there is no flow of water. (The words 'whoever they are' matter. The lesson's science shows that groundwater is shared under every wall, and its history shows that people have often refused to share it. The verse states the dependence as equal for all.)
- Science (settled): The total amount of water on Earth has stayed nearly the same for billions of years. It is the same water, cycling through sea, air, ground and living bodies.
- Science (debated): Where Earth's water first came from is not known. Some scientists think icy rocks from space brought it, and others think it was in the rocks that formed the Earth.
- Other view, Many water engineers and economists: Water is a substance with a formula, H2O, and a supply that can be measured. On this view, talk of sharing and oneness fills no pots. What prevents shortage is counting the water, charging a fair price for it, building good pipes and reservoirs, and enforcing rules on who may pump. Slowness is not always good either. In a cloudburst, getting water away fast saves lives.
- Practice, One slow glass (3 min): Pour your child a glass of water. Read these lines slowly, one at a time. 'Before you drink, look at the water. It was a cloud once. It fell as rain, perhaps on a hill far away. It ran, or it sank, or it waited in the ground. Take one small sip and hold it in your mouth. Breathe out slowly. Now swallow. By tomorrow, some of this water will be in your blood, your tears and your breath.' Drink the rest in silence. Take it one slow sip at a time.
- Ask: The plate and the soil got the same cup of water. Which one had 'more' water an hour later, and why?
- Ask: Where does the water in your body go next, and who or what might hold it after you?
- Ask: If water under the ground has no fences, what should the rules for using it be?
- Ask: Is a river the same river if its water is always new?

- *deep:* The water under your house flows on to your neighbour's well. Whose water is it? (hint: Ask where the rain fell, where it is now, and who could pump it.)

## Story: The Well That Eleven Roofs Fill
*A made-up story set in Chennai. The street and the people are imagined. What is real: Chennai was flooded in December 2015 and its main lakes ran nearly dry in June 2019, tanker lorries brought water from farm wells outside the city, Tamil Nadu has required rainwater harvesting since 2003, and water led into sandy ground does raise the level in wells nearby. How old the water in any one well is can only be found by laboratory tests.*

In June 2019 the taps on Kamarajar Street gave nothing but a cough of air.

Tamizhini, who was nine, stood in the tanker queue with two plastic pots. Behind her stood Imran, who was also nine and lived next door. The lorry was late. When it came, each house got six pots, and the driver wanted to be gone.

'Where does this water come from?' Tamizhini asked him.

'Farm wells near Tiruvallur,' said the driver, coiling his hose. 'The farmers sell water now. It pays better than paddy.' He shrugged. 'Their wells are going down too.'

Six pots did not last the day. That evening Tamizhini's mother sent her to the end of the street, to old Rukmani paati's house. Paati had an open well in her back yard, ringed with stone. The whole street knew it had not gone dry.

Tamizhini looked over the edge. Far down, a disc of sky looked back. 'Paati, why does your well have water when the borewells are empty?'

'Count the pipes,' said Paati.

Tamizhini looked up. A pipe came down from Paati's roof into a round pit covered with a slab. Another pipe came over the wall from the house on the left. Another from the house behind. 'Eleven roofs,' said Paati. 'In 2003 the government said every building must catch its rain. Most people stuck a pipe in the ground and forgot it. Our end of the street did it properly. For sixteen monsoons, eleven roofs have poured into this ground.'

She drew up a bucket and filled Tamizhini's pots. Then she filled Imran's, who had followed.

'How much do we pay?' asked Imran. His family had paid the tanker man.

'Which house is yours?'

'Number fourteen.'

'Is your roof one of the eleven?'

'No,' said Imran. 'Our pipe is broken.'

'Then you pay me by mending it,' said Paati. 'This is my well. I dug nothing of it. My husband's mother did. But the water in it is not mine. It fell on eleven roofs and it walks under every wall on this street. Shall I sell Tamizhini her own rain?'

Tamizhini thought of the farmer near Tiruvallur. 'The tanker water was somebody's rain too,' she said. 'Now it is here, and their well is going down.'

'Yes,' said Paati. 'A city's thirst does not vanish. It moves to someone else's village.'

Imran leaned over the well. 'How old is this water? Is it last year's rain?'

'I do not know,' said Paati. 'Nobody on this street knows. Scientists can test such things. In some wells the water fell last monsoon. In some deep bores it fell before there was a Chennai.' She tapped the stone ring. 'I only know that it stays because it went in slowly.'

She pointed at a brown line on her wall, above Tamizhini's head. 'You were small. Do you remember December 2015? The flood stood this high in my kitchen. The whole city was a lake. Four years later, the same city has not one pot of water. It is the same sky and nearly the same rain. All that flood water ran to the sea in a week.'

'So there was enough,' said Tamizhini slowly. 'It just did not stay.'

'When I was a girl in my village,' said Paati, 'there was a fine old tank. People call such things the wisdom of our elders. My friend Pechi was not allowed to fill her pot there, because of the family she was born into. So I do not say the old days were good. I say the ground still remembers how to drink, if we let it. And this time the water must be for every house.'

In October the monsoon came. Imran's father mended the pipe at number fourteen and dug a pit beneath it. That made twelve roofs. One wet evening Tamizhini and Imran lay on their stomachs at Paati's well and dropped a pebble. They counted until the splash.

The count was shorter than in June.

**For the parent:** The flood of 2015 and the dry taps of 2019 came from nearly the same rain. Water that leaves fast is lost, and water that sinks in slowly is still there months later, under every wall on the street.

- Ask: Paati said the well was hers and the water was not. What did she mean?
- Ask: The tanker water came from a farmer's well that was also going down. Was the city's water problem solved, or moved?
- Ask: Paati remembered that her friend was kept away from the old village tank. Why did she tell the children this?
- Ask: Why was the count to the splash shorter in October than in June?

### e1 · Fast water, slow water: a model roof, a tank and three kinds of ground (46 min)
- **question:** When one cup of rain lands on a roof, how much reaches a tank? When one cup lands on sand, soil and clay, how much goes down through each, and how fast?
- **predict:** Before you pour, guess. Out of one cup of rain on the plate, how much will reach the tank? Then guess which of the three grounds, sand, soil or flat dough, will let the most water through in five minutes. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** In Part 1 the plate, the slope, the gutter and the cup stay the same. The test is done three times, because one try can be a fluke. In Part 2 the bottle, the cloth, the depth of ground (4 centimetres), the cup of water and the way it is poured stay the same. Only the kind of ground changes. Each ground is tested twice with fresh ground, and the middle result of the two is kept. The 'rain' comes from a bottle with a holed cap, so it falls like rain and not like a jug of water.
- **safety:** Cutting the bottles and poking the cap holes is for the grown-up. Cover any cut edge with tape. Do the pouring over a tray, and wipe up spills so nobody slips. After the test, pour the used water on a plant.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For the roof, write the three results and circle the middle one. For each ground, write the two results and the time the water took to start dripping. Draw the three grounds side by side, with a blue bar for how much came through each. Then write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.
- **results table:** Test | How much reached the tank or came through (tenths of a cup) | Time it took
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Make the rain-maker. An adult pokes about 8 holes in a plastic bottle cap with a pin or a nail. Then screw the cap on a bottle. Fill the small cup with water and pour it into the clear tumbler. Mark the water line on the outside with tape and write 'one cup'. Divide the height of the water into ten equal parts with a ruler, and make a small tape mark for each tenth. Empty the tumbler.  
  *Parent says:* We will make our own rain. This tumbler will tell us how much water we catch. Guess first: if one cup of rain falls on a tilted plate, how much will end up in the tumbler?  
  *Child mode:* Poke holes in the cap. Pour one cup into the tumbler and mark the water line. Make ten marks up the tumbler. Then guess how much rain the plate will catch.
  - Ask: How much of the cup do you think will reach the tumbler? Why? (good: Most of it, because the plate is smooth and slopes.; Some of it, because a few drops will stick or bounce away.)
  - Note: Any small cup works, because everything is measured in tenths of that cup. If the family has a measuring jug, one cup is 200 millilitres and each tenth is 20 millilitres. Do not tell the child the answer. Write the guess down.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Make the model roof. Prop one side of the plate on a book, so that it slopes by about 10 centimetres at its high edge. An adult cuts a 1 or 2 litre bottle in half lengthways, and tapes the sharp edges. Put the half-bottle under the low edge of the plate, so water that runs off the edge falls into it. Tilt the gutter so it drains into the tumbler. Work over the big tray.  
  *Parent says:* This plate is our roof. The half-bottle is the gutter. The tumbler is the tank. Where will the water go once it leaves the plate?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt the plate on a book. Put the half-bottle under the low edge. Put the tumbler under the end of the half-bottle.
  - Note: The plate must slope clearly. If water pools in the middle, raise the book. The gutter must touch or sit just below the plate's edge, or drops will miss it.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Run the roof test three times. Fill the small cup. Empty it over the plate through the holed cap, taking about 10 seconds, so it falls like rain. Wait one minute. Hold the ruler against the tumbler and read the water height in tenths of a cup. Write it down. Empty the tumbler, wipe the plate dry, and repeat twice more.  
  *Parent says:* Read the tumbler. How many tenths of the cup did we catch? Did the three tries give the same answer? If not, why not?  
  *Child mode:* Sprinkle one cup over the plate. Wait one minute. Read the tumbler. Do it three times and write each answer.
  - Ask: Where did the missing water go? (good: Some drops stayed stuck on the plate.; Some bounced or splashed past the gutter.)
  - Note: A good result is 7 to 9 tenths each time. A few drops always stay on the plate and some bounce off the gutter. The three results should be close. If one is very low, the gutter was probably misplaced. Wet the plate before the first test if it is greasy.
- **s4 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Make three ground funnels. An adult cuts three bottles in half and keeps the top halves. Turn each top half upside down, so the neck points down, and stand it in a tumbler or a cut bottle bottom. Cover each neck with a square of cloth held on with a rubber band, so ground cannot fall out. Put 4 centimetres of ground into each. Use sand or rava in the first, damp soil in the second, and soft dough in the third. Press the dough flat and push it right to the edges, so no water can slip past the sides. Pour a little water on each first, so it is damp all through, then let it drip out.

  - Ask: Which ground will let water through fastest, and which slowest? Why? (good: Sand will be fastest, because its grains are big with wide gaps.; Dough will be slowest, because it has no gaps.)
  - Note: Wetting the ground first matters. Dry soil can soak up the whole cup before any water drips out, which would hide the real difference. The cloth only holds the ground in. Wash the cloths afterwards.
- **s5 (16 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test all three grounds in one round. Put a clean tumbler under each funnel. Start the clock. Sprinkle one cup of rain over each funnel in turn, taking about 10 seconds for each.   *Parent says:* Watch the clock. When do the first drops come through from each funnel? How much has come through after one minute? After five?  
  *Child mode:* Sprinkle one cup on each funnel, one after another. Watch the clock. Read each tumbler after one minute and after five minutes. Do a second round with fresh ground.
  - Ask: What is the difference between the three results? (good: The more gaps that join up, the more water gets through.; Water stands on top of the dough because it cannot get down.)
  - Note: Rough results for one cup: sand lets 5 to 8 tenths through, mostly in the first minute. Damp soil lets 1 to 5 tenths through, slowly over the five minutes. Pressed dough or clay lets 0 to 1 tenth through, and water stands on top. The numbers change with the soil, but the order, sand then soil then dough, is nearly always the same. If the soil lets everything through, press it down harder. If the dough leaks at the sides, press it to the bottle wall. Keep a spare bowl of each ground ready for the second round.

  *Parent does:* Look at everything in the table. Finish with the three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why. Then ask what this test cannot tell us. Pour the used water on a plant. Wash the cloths and the funnels.  
  *Parent says:* On the plate, most water ran off in seconds. On the sand, water sank in at once. On the dough, it sat on top. Why does the same cup of water do three different things? What does this test not tell us about a real roof or a real field?  
  *Child mode:* Write: what I think, what I saw, why. Which ground would you dig a recharge pit in?
  - Note: The test cannot tell us how fast water moves through ground that is deep, packed or full of roots. A real field has layers, and the water under it moves much more slowly than in our funnel. Scientists use the same idea, measured in a lab, to describe real ground. Do not give the child the 'right' sentence. Ask what they would test next.
  - Think: If rain lands on clay or solid rock, can it still be caught? How?

  - Note: If the hole collapses, make it a bit wider, or press the sand beside the glass firm first. The water in the well should stand within a few millimetres of the water table.
- **w3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add rain. Fill the small cup, and sprinkle it slowly over the top of the sand through the holed cap, taking about 20 seconds. Wait two minutes. Measure the water height in the well again. Mark the new level with tape. Work out how many millimetres it rose. Then do the same again with a second cup and write the new height.  
  *Parent says:* Where is the water in the well now? How much higher is it? What do you think the water did between the top and the well?  
  *Child mode:* Sprinkle one cup on the sand. Wait two minutes. Measure the well again. Do it twice.
  - Ask: Where did the extra water in the well come from? (good: From the rain that sank down through the sand.; It joined the water below and pushed the water table up.)
  - Note: A good result is a rise of 3 to 15 millimetres for each cup, depending on the jar and the cup. The water sinks through the sand and joins the water below, so the table rises. If the rise is tiny, use a slightly larger cup, or sprinkle more slowly. The rise is bigger in a narrow jar.
- **w4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take water out. Scoop water from the well with the spoon, five spoonfuls, then wait two minutes. Measure the water height in the well. Do five more spoonfuls and wait two minutes again, then measure. Write both heights.  
  *Parent says:* Where is the water now? How many spoonfuls of rain did we add, and how many did we take out? What happens to a real well if more is taken out than rain puts in?  
  *Child mode:* Scoop five spoonfuls from the well. Wait. Measure. Do it again. Write what happens to the level.
  - Ask: What happened when more water came out than went in? (good: The water table fell.; The well got lower, and in a real place it would go dry.)
  - Note: The level falls after each scoop, though it creeps back a little as water seeps in from the sides. If you take out more than the rain added, the level ends lower than it began. That is what happens in a real district when pumping beats the rain.
- **w5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Finish with the three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why. Then ask what the test cannot tell us. Pour the sand into a pot or a bin.  
  *Parent says:* Rain went in, and the water in the well rose. Water came out, and it fell. What does this show about why people recharge wells? What does this jar not show about real ground?  
  *Child mode:* Write: what I think, what I saw, why. What would you change in the jar to make it more like real ground?
  - Note: A real water table lies under layers of soil and rock, moves slowly, and can be far below. The jar shows the idea, not the speed. Ask the child how they could test a layer of clay in the jar.
  - Think: Who else uses the water under your house?

### a1 · Make a rain gauge and test it with a watering can (25 min)
- **record:** Write the depth in the jar after 5 litres. Then write how many litres would fall on a roof of 100 square metres in 5 millimetres of rain.
- **a1s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark a square on the floor, 1 metre on each side, with chalk or tape. Stand the clear jar in the middle. Ask your child to guess the depth of water in the jar. Say that 5 litres of rain will fall evenly on the whole square.  
  *Parent says:* This floor square is one square metre. I will sprinkle 5 litres on all of it, as evenly as I can. How deep do you think the water in the jar will be?  
  *Child mode:* Mark a square, 1 metre on each side. Put the jar in the middle. Guess how deep the water will be after 5 litres.
  - Note: Do not give the answer. Most children guess either a few centimetres or almost nothing. Both are interesting. Write the guess down.
- **a1s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the 1 litre bottle with water. Sprinkle it from the watering can or holed cap, in slow rows across the square. Cover all of the square about equally. Do this five times, so 5 litres fall. Stand back and let the water settle for a minute.  
  *Parent says:* Try to give every part of the square the same rain. Real rain falls evenly on a small square. Why do you think it matters that we sprinkle evenly?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bottle. Sprinkle it evenly over the square. Do this five times.
  - Ask: What do you think will happen to the depth if the sprinkling is uneven? (good: The jar might get more or less than the square's share.; The reading would be wrong for the whole square.)
  - Note: Real rain is even over a small square. Sprinkling is only close to even. If a lot of water splashes out of the square, the depth will be low. A result of 3 to 7 millimetres is good. Work on a floor that can get wet, and wipe up spills at once so that nobody slips.
- **a1s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the depth of water in the jar with the ruler, to the nearest millimetre. Then pour the water out, put a wider, shallower tin in the middle instead, and read its depth. Compare the two readings.  
  *Parent says:* How deep is it? Is the shallow tin the same depth as the tall jar? Which one holds more water? Which one tells you the rain?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the water in the jar. Then measure it in a wide tin. Are the depths close?
  - Ask: Why can a narrow jar tell you how much rain fell on a whole roof? (good: Depth is the same everywhere in an even shower.; The roof gets the same depth as the jar.)
  - Note: The depths should be close, within 2 millimetres, though the wide tin holds more water. This is the idea of a rain gauge. Depth is the same whatever the width of the container, as long as the sides are straight. If the readings are very different, the sprinkling was uneven. Repeat once.
- **a1s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Use the reading to work out the roof sum. The square got 5 litres and the depth is about 5 millimetres, so one millimetre on one square metre is one litre. Ask your child how many litres 5 millimetres of rain would put on a roof of 100 square metres.  
  *Parent says:* 5 litres on one square metre was about 5 millimetres. So how many litres on 100 square metres in 5 millimetres of rain?  
  *Child mode:* One millimetre on one square metre is one litre. How many litres fall on 100 square metres in 5 millimetres?
  - Note: The answer is 500 litres. 100 times 5 equals 500. Say that a light shower on one small roof fills a big drum.
  - Think: If a heavy shower brings 20 millimetres, how many buckets of 10 litres fall on that roof?
### a2 · Where does one cup of water go? A walk with a cup (20 min)
- **record:** Make a table of the five surfaces. Write whether the water soaked in at once, soaked in slowly or ran away.
- **a2s1 (4 min)**

  *Parent says:* Here are five places for rain to land. For each one, guess: will the water soak in or run away?  
  *Child mode:* Choose five surfaces. Write them in a table. Guess for each: soaks in or runs away?
  - Note: Pick surfaces that are safe to pour on. Do not pour on electrics. Do this in the same hour of the day so the surfaces have the same wetness. If you have no soil, use a pot of soil.
- **a2s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At each place, pour one small cup of water slowly, from about 20 centimetres above, in the same way every time. Watch for 30 seconds. Write what you saw: the water soaked in at once, soaked in slowly, or ran away and where it went. Note how long a puddle stays.

  - Ask: Which surface drank the water best, and why? (good: Soil or sand, because they have gaps.; Hard, smooth surfaces have no gaps, so water runs off.)
  - Note: A tiled floor or tar road usually gives run-off or a puddle. Soil usually drinks the cup in seconds. A doormat soaks a little, then holds the rest. Sand drinks fast. If a surface does something odd, ask why. Pour any left-over water on a plant.
- **a2s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the five in order from the one that drinks the most to the one that drinks the least. Ask your child which of these five their street or school ground is mostly made of.  
  *Parent says:* If it rained here for an hour, where would the water end up? What does your street look like from the sky?  
  *Child mode:* Put the five in order. Which is most like your street?
  - Note: A city street is mostly hard surface. That is why run-off is so fast, and why wells run dry in cities where very little soaks in.
  - Think: What would you change in your street so more rain soaked in?
### a3 · Dusty roof, first flush and a bottle filter (30 min)
- **record:** Draw the two tumblers side by side with their colours. Draw the filter bottle and label the layers.
- **a3s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sprinkle a spoon of fine dust over the plate, as if the roof had gone dry for months. Prop the plate up and put a tumbler at the low edge. Sprinkle one cup of water over it. Swap the tumbler for a clean one and sprinkle a second cup. Put the two tumblers side by side.  
  *Parent says:* A real roof is dusty after a dry spell. The first rain washes it. What colour do you think the first cup will be? And the second?  
  *Child mode:* Dust the plate. Sprinkle one cup. Catch it in a tumbler. Then sprinkle a second cup. Catch it in a new tumbler.
  - Ask: Which tumbler is dirtier and why? (good: The first, because it washed the dust off.; The second was cleaner because the dust was gone.)
  - Note: The first cup is brown and the second much clearer, because the first washes most of the dust away. A first-flush device throws the first dirty water away in the same way. If the second cup is as dirty as the first, the dust was too thick. Use less.
- **a3s2 (14 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* An adult cuts a plastic bottle in half and takes the top. Turn it upside down. Tie a cloth over the neck. Put in a layer of gravel, then a layer of sand, then another cloth on top. Pour the dirty water through slowly, over a clear tumbler. Look at the water that comes out.  
  *Parent says:* A filter is layers that let water through and hold dirt back. Which layer do you think will do the most work? What do you think the water will look like?  
  *Child mode:* Make the filter: cloth, gravel, sand, cloth. Pour dirty water through it. Look at what comes out.
  - Ask: Is the filtered water safe to drink? (good: No. It looks clearer, but germs and chemicals pass through.; It needs boiling or proper filtering first.)
  - Note: The water will be clearer but not always clear. It is still not safe to drink. A filter holds back dirt but does not remove germs. Stored rainwater is boiled or filtered properly before drinking. The first drops may be cloudy. Pour the water through twice if you like.
- **a3s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at all the tumblers. Pour the used water on a plant. Wash the cloths. Ask your child why a house with a rain tank has both a first-flush and a filter.  
  *Parent says:* The first-flush throws away the dirtiest water. The filter cleans what is left. Why do you think we need both?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the tumblers. Why do a roof tank and a filter need a first-flush too?
  - Note: The first-flush removes the worst of the dirt before it can clog the filter. Say that no filter on a dirty roof gives perfect water. Many families use the water for washing and gardens, which needs no boiling.
  - Think: What would you do to keep your roof cleaner?
### a4 · A stepwell in a tub: reaching water at any level (25 min)

  *Child mode:* Build five steps with blocks. Put the peg on the top step. Mark three water levels with tape.
  - Note: Blocks must be about the same height. The water table rises in the monsoon and falls in summer, so the water is at a different depth each season. That is the problem the steps solve.
- **a4s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour water up to the low mark. Move the peg down the steps to the one that touches the water. Say 'summer'. Add water up to the middle mark. Move the peg up. Say 'after the first rains'. Add water up to the high mark and move the peg up again. Say 'monsoon'. Try to reach the water from the top step with the cup on a string, at each level.

  - Note: The walker always finds a step at the water's edge. In a straight well, the rope must be long enough for the lowest level, and the water cannot be reached by walking. The steps also let people wash and rest, and the cool air deep in a real stepwell makes it a pleasant place. The model cannot show the cool air.
- **a4s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to draw the stepwell at the three levels. Pour the water on a plant. Ask who would have used a stepwell and who would have kept it in repair.  
  *Parent says:* Who built and cleaned a stepwell? Who could use it? Does anyone in your family remember a well like this?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the stepwell three times. Mark the walker each time.
  - Note: Many stepwells were built by royal families, merchants and queens, and looked after by communities. Some wells and tanks in India were closed to some people because of caste. The history is mixed, and both parts are true.
  - Think: What would you do to open a shut-out well to everyone?
### a5 · A year of hours on a paper strip: the long wait between rains (20 min)
- **record:** Keep the strip. Write the sum: how many hours of dry time for each hour of heavy rain?
- **a5s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* A year has 8,760 hours. Work out how long a strip must be if each centimetre stands for 100 hours. Divide 8,760 by a hundred and check that it is 87.6 centimetres. Tape paper together to make a strip of about 88 centimetres. Mark the start and the end with a pencil.  
  *Parent says:* A year has 8,760 hours. If each centimetre of the strip is 100 hours, how long is the strip for the whole year?  
  *Child mode:* Make a strip that is 88 centimetres long. Each centimetre is 100 hours of the year.
  - Note: A year has 365 times 24 hours, and 365 times 24 is 8,760. We chose 100 hours for each centimetre so that the strip fits on a table. A strip with 10 hours for each centimetre would be nearly 9 metres long. Say this to your child as a choice that we make.
- **a5s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say that in many places, most of a year's rain falls in about 100 hours of heavy showers. Colour 1 centimetre of the strip blue. That is the rain time. Colour the rest black. Ask your child how many centimetres are black.  
  *Parent says:* Most of the rain falls in about a hundred hours. Colour that much blue. How much of the strip is still black, with no rain?  
  *Child mode:* Colour 1 centimetre blue. Colour the rest black. How long is the black part?
  - Note: The black part is about 86.6 centimetres, which is about 8,660 hours. There are about 87 dry hours for every hour of heavy rain. The figure of about a hundred hours is an estimate that changes from place to place, so say 'in many places'. The point is how short the rainy time is.
- **a5s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to draw a tank under the strip. Draw an arrow from the blue part into the tank, and an arrow from the tank to the black part. Ask what the tank must do.  
  *Parent says:* The rain time is short. The dry time is long. What does a tank do for the long dry time?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a tank. Draw an arrow from the blue into the tank. Draw another from the tank into the black. What does the tank do?
  - Note: A tank moves water through time, from the rain days to the dry days. A recharge pit does the same in the ground. This is why water in India is a puzzle of timing as much as of amount.
  - Think: If the rain came evenly all year, would we need tanks?

### a7 · How much rain could your home catch? (25 min)
- **record:** Write your area and the three yearly amounts of rain on the roof.
- **a7s1 (8 min)**

  *Child mode:* Measure the length and the width of a floor or a roof. Multiply them to find the area in square metres.

  *Parent does:* Ask your child how long the Jaisalmer roof's water would last a family of four. Each person uses 20 litres a day for drinking and cooking. Work it out together.  
  *Parent says:* A family of four needs about 80 litres a day for drinking and cooking. How many days would 1,920 litres last?  
  *Child mode:* Divide 1,920 by 80. How many days will it last?
  - Note: The answer is 24 days for this small floor. A real roof of 100 square metres in Jaisalmer would catch about 16,000 litres, enough for 200 days. The sum shows why people there store water in tanks.
  - Think: Why is catching rain worth doing in both wet and dry places?

## Challenges
### ch1 · Catch the most: a model roof that gives nine tenths of the rain to the tank (35 min)
- **problem:** Make a model roof and gutter that sends at least 9 tenths of one cup of rain into a tank. There is more than one good answer.

### ch2 · A yard that does not flood: make a model courtyard soak up a storm (35 min)
- **problem:** Design a model yard on a tilted tray so that little of two cups of storm rain runs off the low edge. A hard path must still cross the yard so people can walk. There is more than one good answer.
- **your turn:** Design the yard you would like at your school. Where would the water go in a heavy storm? Who would look after it?
- **constraints:** Use the same tray, the same slope and the same two cups poured over 20 seconds for every design.; Keep a hard path of foil or card about 5 centimetres wide across the yard.; Measure the run-off in tenths of a cup with the gutter and tumbler.; Test each design at least twice.
- **general:** Dig a shallow pond at the lowest corner, so run-off collects there and sinks in.; Cover bare soil with leaves, straw or small plants.; Make the path from gravel, or slope it onto the soil.
- **creative:** Raise the edge of the yard with a low earth wall, like a small johad, so the whole yard is a pond for an hour.; Make the path from separate stones with gaps, so water drains between them.; Lay a sponge or a rolled cloth along the low edge to soak up run-off and let it go slowly.
- **ch2s1 (8 min)**

  *Parent says:* A plain yard with a hard path. How much water runs out of the low edge? Where does it go first?  
  *Child mode:* Make a plain yard with a foil path. Pour two cups. Read the run-off.
  - Note: A result of 4 to 8 tenths of the two cups is usual, mostly from the path and the firm soil. If less runs off, press the soil harder or use more water.
- **ch2s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try the usual fixes one at a time. Dig a shallow pond at the low edge of the soil and line it with gravel. Cover the soil with torn leaves. Slope the path so it drains into the soil. Put a strip of gravel beside the path. Test each fix twice and write the results.  
  *Parent says:* Which fix gave the biggest drop in run-off? Did any make things worse?  
  *Child mode:* Try a pond, then leaves, then a sloped path. Test each twice. Write the results.
  - Ask: Which fix worked best, and why? (good: A pond held water at the low edge so it could soak in.; Leaves and gravel slowed the water.)
  - Note: A pond at the low edge holds the water long enough to sink. Leaves slow the flow. Gravel is permeable. All are real ideas used in cities and fields.
- **ch2s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now combine your best fixes and add one idea of your own. Test the whole design twice. Write the best result and compare it with the plain yard.  
  *Parent says:* How much less ran off? What does your design do that the plain yard does not?  
  *Child mode:* Combine your best fixes. Test twice. Compare with the plain yard.
  - Note: A model cannot show a very big storm, or what happens when the soil is already full. Ask what the child thinks would happen in the second storm of the same day.
### ch3 · Keep the tank full: lose the least water in four days (30 min)
- **problem:** Store one cup of water for four days so that it loses as little as possible. There is more than one good answer.
- **your turn:** Design your own store for four days. What would you add? Do you think any store loses nothing at all?
- **constraints:** Every store starts with the same amount of water and a pencil line at the start level.; All stores sit in the same warm spot, near a sunny window or a fan.; Do not move or touch them for four days except to read the level.; Measure the level each day with a ruler in millimetres.
- **general:** Cover the container with a lid or a plate.; Use a tall, narrow container, so less surface is open to the air.; Keep the store in shade, away from sun and wind.
- **creative:** Float a thin layer of cooking oil on the water, to seal the surface. Do not drink it afterwards.; Cover with a plate and put a stone on it, so no air moves under the edge.; Put a small container inside a bigger one with a lid, so the air between is damp and still.
- **ch3s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 3 centimetres of water into each container. Mark the water line on the outside with tape, and write the start height. Cover some containers in different ways and leave one open. Put them all side by side in the same warm spot. Ask your child to guess which will lose most and which least.  
  *Parent says:* Which will lose the most water in four days? Which will lose the least? Where does the lost water go?  
  *Child mode:* Pour water to a mark in each container. Cover some. Leave one open. Put them side by side.
  - Ask: Which will lose the most, and why? (good: The open wide one, because more water touches the air.; A covered one will lose less.)
  - Note: Evaporation is the water turning into a gas in the air. In humid weather the loss is small, so put the stores near a fan or a sunny window, and measure to the nearest millimetre. If nothing changes in four days, give it two more days.
- **ch3s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each day at the same time, read the water level in each container against the ruler. Write it in a table. Do not move the containers. Note the day on which the open container is lowest.  
  *Parent says:* What has changed since yesterday? Which is changing fastest? Which is not changing?  
  *Child mode:* Read each level each day. Write it down. Do not move the containers.
  - Note: The lowest levels are usually in the open and widest stores. A cloth cover that is wet pulls the water out by wicking, so it may lose more than an open container. That is a result worth noting.
  - Wait: Come back each day for four days
- **ch3s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day four, draw a line chart of the water level in each container against the days. Say which design lost the least, and why. Try to explain any surprising result.  
  *Parent says:* Which design kept the most water? What do you think made the difference? What would you change next time?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line chart. Which container kept the most water? Why?
  - Note: In a hot dry place, a wide, shallow, open water store can lose more than a metre of depth in a year. Real tanks in Rajasthan are covered and deep. The model shows that a smaller surface and a cover reduce the loss.

## Worksheets
### Where rain goes and how to catch it (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Rain lands on a tarred road and on a patch of garden soil. Which is most likely?  
   Options: Both soak in at the same speed / Water runs off the road and soaks into the soil / Water soaks into the road and runs off the soil  
   **Answer:** Water runs off the road and soaks into the soil. *Why:* A tarred road has no gaps, so water runs off. Soil has gaps and plants, so most of a gentle rain soaks in. Choosing the first option treats all ground as the same.
2. (fill) One millimetre of rain on one square metre of roof is ____ litre.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* A flat square one metre on each side, with water one millimetre deep, holds exactly one litre.
3. (truefalse) If people catch rain, more rain falls in the monsoon.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Catching rain changes where the rain goes after it falls. It does not change how much falls.
4. (mcq) Which part of a rooftop system is the channel along the roof's edge?  
   Options: The gutter / The filter / The first-flush  
   **Answer:** The gutter. *Why:* The gutter leads water from the roof to the pipe. The first-flush throws away the dirtiest water and the filter holds back dirt.
5. (sort) Sort each part by its main job.  
   **Answer:** gutter → Leads water along; down-pipe → Leads water along; first-flush → Cleans the water; filter → Cleans the water; covered tank → Keeps the water. *Why:* The gutter and pipe carry water, the first-flush and the filter clean it, and the tank stores it for later.
6. (match) Match each kind of ground to what happens to a cup of water poured on it.  
   **Answer:** sand → water passes through fast; flat wet clay → water sits on top; soil under plants → water soaks in slowly; solid rock → water enters only through cracks. *Why:* Water moves through gaps that join up. Big grains leave wide gaps, tiny flakes leave almost none, and solid rock has only its cracks.
7. (order) Put the steps in order for rain that refills a well.  
   **Answer:** ['Rain is led into a pit', 'The water sinks down through the soil', 'It joins the water below', 'The water table rises', 'The water in the well rises']. *Why:* Rain sinks and joins the groundwater. The water table is the top of that water, so it rises, and a well is a hole down to it.
8. (mcq) A roof of 20 square metres gets 10 millimetres of rain. How many litres land on it?  
   Options: 200 litres / 30 litres / 2 litres  
   **Answer:** 200 litres. *Why:* Litres equal the area in square metres times the rain in millimetres, so 20 times 10 is 200. Adding the two numbers is a common mix-up.
9. (fill) A roof of 50 square metres gets 20 millimetres of rain. It receives ____ litres.  
   **Answer:** ['1,000', '1000']. *Why:* 50 times 20 is 1,000 litres.
10. (write) Give two reasons for catching rain.  
   **Answer:** A good answer gives two of these. Rain comes in a few months and the dry months are long. Rain on hard ground runs off in hours. Caught rain refills wells. Caught rain gives water close to home.. *Why:* A strong answer says why the water is worth keeping, with a reason from how rain falls or where it goes.
11. (truefalse) A well is a hole dug down to the water table.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Water seeps in from the sides and stands at the level of the water table.
12. (mcq) Where is it best to dig a recharge pit?  
   Options: On solid rock near the surface / On flat wet clay / In sand or coarse soil  
   **Answer:** In sand or coarse soil. *Why:* Water sinks quickly through sand. Clay and solid rock block it, so a pit must go down through clay to a layer that lets water in.
13. (draw) Draw a rooftop system with a roof, gutter, first-flush, filter and tank in order. Label each part.  
   **Answer:** A sloping roof with a gutter along its low edge. A pipe leads down to a first-flush, then a filter, then a covered tank. Arrows show the water, and each part is labelled in that order.. *Why:* The order matters. The first-flush must come before the filter, so the worst dirt does not clog it.
14. (write) A hillside farm loses soil and water in every shower. Which of India's old methods would you try, and why?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A good answer picks one method. For example, earth walls across the slope like a johad, or covering the soil with leaves and trees. It says the method slows the water so it can soak in.. *Why:* A strong answer ties the method to slowing water on a slope and does not just name it.
15. (perspective) Store the rain or sink it into the ground?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer uses the rain, the ground and the water that is already there. A tank suits dry places and a recharge pit suits places with good soil and wells. Many homes do both.. *Why:* Both families make sense. A tank is countable but costly. The ground is large but shared with neighbours.
### Roof sums, ground and old methods (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A roof of 8 by 6 metres gets 1,000 millimetres of rain a year. Four-fifths is caught. How many litres?  
   Options: 48,000 litres / 3,840 litres / 38,400 litres  
   **Answer:** 38,400 litres. *Why:* The area is 48 square metres, so 48,000 litres fall. Four-fifths of that is 38,400. 48,000 is what lands, not what can be caught.
2. (fill) A roof of 100 square metres gets a 25 millimetre shower. It receives ____ litres.  
   **Answer:** ['2,500', '2500']. *Why:* 100 times 25 is 2,500 litres.
3. (truefalse) A narrow jar and a wide tin stand in the same shower. They show different depths of rain.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In an even shower, the depth is the same whatever the width, as long as the sides are straight. The wide tin holds more water but is not deeper.
4. (mcq) Water moves through sand about a metre a day, and through clay a few centimetres a year. What follows for recharge pits?  
   Options: Clay is the best place for a pit / Sand cannot hold any water / Dig the pit down through the clay  
   **Answer:** Dig the pit down through the clay. *Why:* Water cannot easily pass clay, so a pit must go below it. Sand lets water in and holds it in its gaps.
5. (sort) Sort each method by what it mostly does with the rain.  
   **Answer:** a kund → Stores rain in a tank; a roof tank → Stores rain in a tank; a recharge well → Lets rain sink into the ground; a johad → Lets rain sink into the ground; leaves and trees on soil → Lets rain sink into the ground. *Why:* A kund and a roof tank keep rain in a store. A recharge well, a johad and a covering of leaves hold or slow rain so that it can soak in.
6. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** upstream → nearer where a river starts; downstream → nearer where a river ends; river basin → all the land that drains to one river; commons → something a whole community shares. *Why:* A river basin is the land the river drains. Upstream and downstream are places along it. A commons is shared by a community, such as a tank.
7. (order) Put these in order of how fast water passes through, fastest first.  
   **Answer:** ['sand', 'soil under plants', 'wet clay', 'solid rock']. *Why:* Big grains leave wide gaps. Soil has smaller gaps. Clay has almost none. Solid rock lets water through only in cracks.
8. (count) A tank holds 6,000 litres. Five people use 20 litres each every day. For how many days does the tank last?  
   **Answer:** 60. *Why:* Each day the family uses 5 times 20, which is 100 litres. The tank holds 60 lots of 100 litres, so it lasts 60 days.
9. (count) A 40 square metre roof catches 80 of every 100 litres. A 25 millimetre shower falls. How many litres does it catch?  
   **Answer:** 800. *Why:* 40 times 25 is 1,000 litres on the roof. Four-fifths of 1,000 is 800.
10. (mcq) Each year, pumping takes out 5 units of groundwater and rain adds 3. What has happened after 4 years?  
   Options: It has risen by 8 units / It has fallen by 8 units / It is the same as before  
   **Answer:** It has fallen by 8 units. *Why:* Each year the ground loses 5 and gains 3, a loss of 2. Over 4 years the loss is 8 units. Rain helps only if it puts back as much as is taken.
11. (write) In December 2015 Chennai flooded, and in June 2019 its lakes were nearly dry. Explain how one city can have both.  
   **Answer:** Hard ground makes rain run off in hours to the sea, so heavy rain floods the low places. Very little soaks in, so the wells and lakes are not refilled and run dry in summer. The yearly rain did not change much.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the speed of the water, and not only the amount, matters.
12. (write) A roof test gives results of 6, 8 and 7 tenths of a cup. Which result would you report, and why?  
   **Answer:** The middle value, 7, or all three together, because one try can be a fluke. Repeating a test shows whether the result is steady. A strong answer gives a reason for repeating.. *Why:* Three tries show the spread. Reporting only the best would hide how much the results change.
13. (label) Label the drawing of a roof system: roof, gutter, down-pipe, first-flush, filter, tank.  
   **Answer:** The roof is the sloping top. The gutter runs along its low edge. The down-pipe goes down from the gutter. The first-flush is a side pipe. The filter is a box of sand and gravel. The tank is the covered store at the end.. *Why:* Each part has one job, and the water passes them in that order.
14. (mcq) Why does a wide, shallow pond lose a bigger share of its water to the sun than a deep one?  
   Options: Deep water is colder / A wide pond has more surface in the air for its size / Shallow water is heavier  
   **Answer:** A wide pond has more surface in the air for its size. *Why:* Evaporation happens at the surface, where water meets the air. A wide, shallow pond has a large surface for its volume.
15. (perspective) Big dams or lakhs of small works?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer weighs what each protects and what each costs. It names something to check, such as how dry the basin is or who would be moved.. *Why:* Both views have evidence behind them, and scientists and engineers still argue about where each suits best.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Most of a place's yearly rain falls in a few months. What is a good reason to catch it?  
   Options: Catching rain makes more rain fall / It can be used in the dry months that follow / Rain left alone is always dirty  
   **Answer:** It can be used in the dry months that follow. *Why:* Rain comes in a short season and the dry months are long. Catching it moves water through time. Rain does not fall more because we catch it, and rain is not always dirty.
2. (mcq) A flat roof is 10 metres by 5 metres. A storm brings 40 millimetres of rain. How many litres land on it?  
   Options: 2,000 litres / 90 litres / 400 litres  
   **Answer:** 2,000 litres. *Why:* The area is 10 times 5, which is 50 square metres. One millimetre on one square metre is one litre, so 50 times 40 is 2,000 litres. Adding 50 and 40 mixes up the two numbers.
3. (mcq) Two equal roofs get 200 and 1,400 millimetres of rain a year. The second gets how many times more water?  
   Options: 2 times / 14 times / 1,200 times / 7 times  
   **Answer:** 7 times. *Why:* The number 200 goes into 1,400 seven times. The litres on a roof grow in step with the millimetres, so the second roof gets 7 times as much. Subtracting 1,200 gives an amount, not a comparison.
4. (mcq) After a long dry spell, a rain tank is built so that the first rain is thrown away. Why?  
   Options: The first rain is the heaviest / It is saltier than later rain / It washes dust and droppings off the roof  
   **Answer:** It washes dust and droppings off the roof. *Why:* A dry roof collects dust, leaves and bird droppings. The first rain carries most of it. The first-flush lets that dirty water go before the cleaner water reaches the filter.
5. (mcq) A cloth, sand and gravel filter is fitted to a roof tank. What can it not do on its own?  
   Options: Hold back leaves and dust / Make the water surely safe to drink / Make the water look clearer  
   **Answer:** Make the water surely safe to drink. *Why:* A filter holds back dirt but germs and some chemicals pass through. Stored rain is boiled or filtered further before drinking. Holding back dirt and clearing the water are things a filter does.
6. (mcq) A cup of water vanishes into pot soil in seconds, but sits on flat wet clay. Why?  
   Options: Clay is warmer than soil / Soil has gaps that join up, but clay packs tight / Clay is heavier than water  
   **Answer:** Soil has gaps that join up, but clay packs tight. *Why:* Water moves through the gaps between grains. Soil and sand have gaps that join up. Clay is made of tiny flat flakes that pack tight and leave almost no path.
7. (mcq) A village digs a recharge pit and the open well beside it fills. What changed underground?  
   Options: A pipe carried the water straight to the well / The well grew wider / The water table rose, so the well's water rose too  
   **Answer:** The water table rose, so the well's water rose too. *Why:* Rain led into the ground fills more gaps and the water table rises. A well is a hole down to the water table, so its water rises with it. Nothing needs a pipe between the pit and the well.
8. (mcq) In many districts, more groundwater is pumped each year than rain puts back. What follows?  
   Options: Wells fill to the top / The water table falls and wells go dry or must go deeper / The rain falls more heavily / The water table stays the same  
   **Answer:** The water table falls and wells go dry or must go deeper. *Why:* Water leaves the ground faster than it comes in, so the water table falls. Shallow wells go dry and new ones are drilled deeper. Harvesting only helps if less is taken out than goes in.
9. (mcq) Why were stepwells built with long stairs and not just a straight shaft?  
   Options: Stairs made the water cooler / People liked climbing / Steps reach the water as its level changes  
   **Answer:** Steps reach the water as its level changes. *Why:* In a dry land the water table sinks in summer and rises in the monsoon. Stairs let people walk down to the water at any level. The cool air was a gift of the depth, not the reason for the steps.
10. (mcq) A Thar village gets 200 millimetres of rain a year in a few storms. Which method suits it best?  
   Options: A covered underground tank with a sloping catchment / A chain of tanks feeding paddy fields / Bamboo pipes along a hillside  
   **Answer:** A covered underground tank with a sloping catchment. *Why:* Where rain is rare, every storm must be kept, and a covered tank loses almost nothing to the sun. A chain of tanks suits a longer rainy season. Bamboo pipes suit steep, wet hills.
11. (mcq) A curved earth wall across a slope makes a pond in the monsoon. How does it help the wells nearby?  
   Options: The pond soaks slowly into the ground and raises the water table / It stops the rain from falling / It makes the wells wider  
   **Answer:** The pond soaks slowly into the ground and raises the water table. *Why:* A johad slows run-off so it has time to sink. The water joins the groundwater and raises the water table, which fills the wells around it.
12. (mcq) Sand, soil and flat dough are tested in funnels with one cup each. What makes the test fair?  
   Options: Use less water on the dough / Same depth, cloth and cup each time, repeated / Pour the dough's water faster  
   **Answer:** Same depth, cloth and cup each time, repeated. *Why:* A fair test changes only the ground. Different amounts or speeds would change two things at once. Repeating shows that one try was not a fluke.
13. (mcq) A model roof catches 7 tenths of a cup. You want to know if a wider gutter helps. What do you do?  
   Options: Widen the gutter and tilt the roof more / Change only the gutter's width and test three times / Try the wide gutter once with more water  
   **Answer:** Change only the gutter's width and test three times. *Why:* Change one thing at a time. If two things change, you cannot tell which one helped. Three tries show whether the result is steady.
14. (mcq) A village upstream catches all its rain. A scientist says that downstream may get less water. Which statement is fair?  
   Options: It depends on how dry the river basin is / Downstream always gets more / Catching rain upstream never changes the river  
   **Answer:** It depends on how dry the river basin is. *Why:* Scientists disagree about how much, and it depends on the place. In a wet region the extra rain would run to the sea. In a dry basin, the water caught upstream is water that no longer reaches the river.
15. (mcq) A village tank is shared by many households. Which is true of any shared tank?  
   Options: It needs rules for use and for care / It needs no rules if the water is plentiful / Only the largest household needs rules  
   **Answer:** It needs rules for use and for care. *Why:* A shared store is a commons. The old tanks lasted because a village committee agreed on who looked after them, and some tanks were kept from some people. Rules about use and care decide whether a commons lasts and who it serves.

## Applications
- Tamil Nadu has required buildings to harvest rain since 2003. Look at a school, flat or office building in your town and find the pit or pipe with the harvesting mark or cover on the ground. Notice where the roof's water goes.
- Farmers build low earth walls across a slope, as in a johad or a khadin. Look at a field near a road or a rail line. Notice any low earth wall, or any line of stones, that holds the run-off.
- Potters, brick makers and builders choose clay for ponds and sand for drains. Look at a pond bed and a drain. Notice which one is made with clay and which with sand or gravel, and why.
- A bucket under a pipe or a tarpaulin spread to catch rain is rainwater harvesting at its smallest. Ask your family if they catch rain in a drum or bucket for washing or plants.
- City planners now lay paving with gaps in car parks and pavements, and plant rain gardens. Find a place in your town where paving has gaps or a dug bed of plants beside a road, and notice if puddles stay there.
- Borewell drillers find water only if they cross a crack in hard rock. Ask a neighbour with a borewell how deep it is, and whether it has gone deeper over the years.
- A rain gauge at a weather station or an agriculture office measures rain in millimetres. Ask at a school, a farm office or a weather report how many millimetres fell in the last monsoon.
- Hill towns and desert towns store rain in tanks. If you visit one, look for a covered tank under a courtyard or a roof, and ask who cleans it and when.

## Spotted around us
- After the next shower, look at a tarred road and a patch of soil side by side. Notice that the road shines with running water while the soil looks dark and quiet. The same rain, two roads.
- Look at the corner of your building's roof or balcony. Find the pipe or the spout where rain leaves. Notice where it ends: a drain, a pit, a plant or a tank.
- On the way to school, count the places where the ground is soil, grass or paving with gaps. Then count the places where it is tar, concrete or tiles. Notice which kind there is more of.
- Find a drain cover after a shower. Notice how fast the water goes in, and what it carries: leaves, plastic, mud. All of it goes to a river or the sea.
- Visit a hand pump, a well or a water tank in your neighbourhood in the dry months and again after the monsoon. Notice how the water level or the pump's effort has changed.
- Water a potted plant and look at the soil. Notice how fast the water vanishes into it, and how a layer of dry leaves or stones on top keeps the soil damp for longer.
- Look for an old tank, pond, stepwell or temple pool in your town. Notice what it is used for now, and whether it holds water, is dry, is full of rubbish, or has been built over.
- On a rainy day, stand under a tree and under a plain roof. Notice that rain from a tree reaches you more slowly, and that the soil under the tree is softer than the paving.

## Open questions
- **How much groundwater does India have left?** Satellites measure how fast it is changing, and wells show the level in one spot. Nobody can see the whole store, and the rocks differ from place to place, so estimates of the total are rough.
- **Does harvesting rain upstream reduce the water for people downstream?** It depends on the basin. Measuring it needs rain, river flow and groundwater records over many years in the same place, and few Indian basins have all three.
- **Is the monsoon changing?** Records suggest fewer gentle rainy days and a greater number of very heavy bursts. Scientists disagree on how much is due to a warming climate, to dust and smoke in the air, or to natural swings.
- **How harmful are the tiny traces of factory chemicals now found in rain?** The chemicals can be measured at extremely low levels. The health limits keep being revised as studies come in, and scientists do not agree on what level is safe.
- **Where did Earth's water come from in the first place?** Water in some meteorites matches Earth's water closely, and so does water locked in Earth's deep rocks. The evidence fits more than one story, and no test has yet decided between them.

## Questions to ask kids
- When it rains, where does the water from your roof go? Can you follow it with your eyes from the roof to the drain?
- Think of a place near you where the ground is hard, and a place where it is soft. If rain fell on both, what would be different an hour later?
- If you could keep all the rain that falls on your school roof, what would you do with it?
- Who looks after the water in your street, and who looks after it in your family's village or hometown?
- Water under the ground is shared with everyone near it. Is that fair? Why or why not?
- A tank gives water you can count. The ground gives water you cannot see. Which would you trust more, and why?

## Connected maths
- A house has a flat roof 12 metres by 7 metres. A family of four uses 20 litres each a day for drinking and cooking. The roof can catch 80 of every 100 litres that fall. How many days of drinking and cooking water would the roof's rain give in Chennai, with 1,400 millimetres of rain a year? How many in Jaisalmer, with 200 millimetres? Then say what a tank must do in each place. **Chennai about 1,176 days, more than 3 years. Jaisalmer 168 days, about 5 and a half months.**. Area: 12 times 7 is 84 square metres. Chennai: 84 times 1,400 is 1,17,600 litres. Four-fifths of that is 94,080 litres. The family uses 4 times 20, which is 80 litres a day. 94,080 divided by 80 is 1,176 days. Jaisalmer: 84 times 200 is 16,800 litres. Four-fifths is 13,440 litres. 13,440 divided by 80 is 168 days. In Jaisalmer, the tank must keep water through the whole dry year and every storm must be saved. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L3`)

## Warm-up
- Rain falls on a road and on a garden. On which does more water run away? → On the road. Hard ground has no gaps, so most water runs off.
- Where does the water in a well come from? → From the groundwater, the water held in the gaps of soil and rock below.
- Which holds water longer, a sandy pond bed or a clay pond bed? → Clay, because water cannot pass through it easily.
- How many litres are in a bucket that holds 10 litres, filled twice? → 20 litres. 10 + 10 = 20.
- Why do we measure rain in millimetres? → Because rain is told as the depth of water it would leave on flat ground.
- Why do farmers pray for rain, or look at the sky, in June? → Because in much of India the monsoon brings most of the year's rain from June to September.
- Name one place where a person keeps water for later. → A pot, a bucket, a tank, a pond or a well. Any answer is right.
- What happens to a puddle in the sun? → It dries, because water turns into a gas in the air. This is called evaporation.
