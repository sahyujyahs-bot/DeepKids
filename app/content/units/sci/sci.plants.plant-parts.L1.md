# Parts of a plant, and the job each part does
`sci.plants.plant-parts.L1`

**Summary.** A tall coconut palm stands through a monsoon storm and does not fall over. Under the ground, thousands of thin roots hold on to the soil. Every part of a plant has a job. Roots hold on and drink water, the stem holds the plant up, and the leaves make food from sunlight. Flowers grow into fruits, and fruits keep the seeds safe until they can grow. In this unit you grow moong sprouts on a cloth and find out how roots hold on.

**Intro.** *Here is a bunch of coriander from the market, with the roots still on. Shake it over a newspaper. Some soil falls off, but some soil stays stuck to the roots. Why does that soil hold on?* The part of a plant we usually see is the green part above the soil. But a plant also has parts under the ground, and every part has a job to do. In this unit you look at a whole plant, from the roots to the tip. You find the roots, the stem, the leaves, the flowers, the fruits and the seeds. Then you grow moong sprouts and test how roots hold on.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name the six parts of a plant, roots, stem, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds, and point to them on a real plant.
1. By the end I can say one job that each part does for the plant.
2. By the end I can say how roots hold a plant in the ground, using what I saw in my sprout test.
3. By the end I can say why the parts need each other: the roots bring water and the leaves make food.
4. By the end I can make a guess, say why, look carefully, and say what I saw.

## Terms
- **Roots**: Roots are the parts of a plant that grow down into the soil. They hold the plant in place and drink water for the whole plant. *e.g.* Pull a coriander plant from a pot and you see thin white roots, with soil stuck to them.
- **Stem**: The stem holds a plant up. Water travels up the stem from the roots to the leaves. *e.g.* A coconut palm's tall trunk is a stem. So is a stick of sugarcane.
- **Leaf**: A leaf is the flat, usually green part of a plant. Leaves catch sunlight and use it to make food for the whole plant. *e.g.* A banana leaf, a curry leaf and a palak leaf are all leaves.
- **Flower**: A flower is the part of a plant that makes seeds. Part of the flower grows into a fruit. *e.g.* A brinjal plant has purple flowers. Each flower can grow into a brinjal.
- **Fruit**: A fruit grows from a flower. The fruit holds the seeds and keeps them safe. *e.g.* A mango, a tomato and a bhindi are all fruits. Each one has seeds inside.
- **Seed**: A seed holds a tiny new plant and a little packed food. In wet soil, a seed can grow into a new plant. *e.g.* Moong, rajma and rice are all seeds.

## Explanations
### Roots hold on and drink · *defined*
**Definition:** Roots grow down into the soil. They hold the plant in place and take in water for the whole plant.

A root grows down from the bottom of the plant and pushes into the tiny gaps between bits of soil. It branches into many thin roots, with tiny hairs near the tips. The roots and hairs wrap round the bits of soil and hold on, and they drink water from the soil. A plant has far more root than we ever see.
- e.g. In 1937, Howard Dittmer, an American scientist, measured all the roots of one rye plant grown in a box of soil. Laid end to end, the roots would stretch more than 600 kilometres. That is farther than the road from Chennai to Bengaluru.
- e.g. A coconut palm has thousands of thin roots. They spread through the soil and hold the tall palm up in storms.
- e.g. A small peepal plant can grow in a crack in an old wall. The roots grow into the crack and hold on.
### The stem holds the plant up and carries water · *defined*
**Definition:** The stem holds the leaves, flowers and fruits up off the ground. Water travels up the stem from the roots, and food travels down from the leaves.

A stem is like a pole with thin pipes inside. It holds the leaves up into the light. Water goes up the pipes from the roots to the leaves. Food made in the leaves goes down other pipes to every part of the plant. Some stems are soft and green, like coriander. Some are hard and woody, like a tree trunk.
- e.g. A coconut palm's trunk is a stem that can grow taller than a four-storey building.
- e.g. Sugarcane is a stem. The plant stores sweet juice in the stem.
- e.g. A money plant has a long thin stem that climbs up a wall or a stick.
### Leaves make food from sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** Green leaves catch sunlight. They use the light to make food for the plant, from air and water.

A plant does not eat the way humans and other animals do. A plant makes their own food in the leaves. The leaf takes in air through tiny holes, and water comes up from the roots. Using sunlight, the leaf makes sugar from the air and the water. That sugar is the plant's food. Most leaves are flat and thin, to catch lots of light. The lines on a leaf are veins, and they carry water in and food out.
- e.g. Hold a palak leaf up to a window. You can see the veins as a pattern of branching lines.
- e.g. A lotus leaf is big and round. The lotus leaf floats flat on a pond, facing the sky.
- e.g. A plant left in a dark cupboard for a few weeks goes pale and weak, because the leaves cannot get light.
### Flowers, fruits and seeds make new plants · *defined*
**Definition:** A flower makes seeds. Part of the flower grows into a fruit, which holds the seeds. Each seed can grow into a new plant.

Bees and other insects visit flowers for a sweet juice. They carry a yellow dust called pollen from flower to flower, and pollen helps the flower make seeds. Then the petals fall off, and part of the flower swells into a fruit. The fruit holds the seeds and keeps them safe. Inside each seed is a tiny new plant and a little packed food. When the seed gets water, the tiny plant wakes up and grows.
- e.g. A purple brinjal flower grows into a brinjal full of tiny seeds.
- e.g. Cut a bhindi across. You see a star of seed rows inside.
- e.g. In the 1990s, Jane Shen-Miller, a plant scientist in the United States, and her team grew a lotus seed from a dry lake bed in China. The seed was about 1,300 years old, and still alive.
### The parts work for the whole plant · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant is one living body. Each part does a job for the whole plant, and the parts need each other.

The roots are in the dark, so they cannot make food. They get food from the leaves. The leaves get most of their water from the roots. The stem joins them, and carries water up and food down. If a plant loses many roots, the leaves droop. If a plant loses all the leaves, the roots run short of food.
- e.g. A plant whose pot falls over in a storm may droop for a few days. Some roots break, so the leaves get less water. New roots grow, and the leaves stand up again.
- e.g. The food stored in a carrot was made in the carrot plant's leaves.
- e.g. A banyan tree sends roots down from the branches into the ground. The roots then hold up the branches like pillars.
### Do roots feel and choose? · *debated*
Roots feel and choose where to grow. A root feels which way is down, and grows down. A root feels where the soil is wet, and grows that way. A root that touches a stone turns and grows round the stone. In 1880, Charles Darwin and his son Francis wrote that the tip of a root guides the whole root, a bit like a brain. Other scientists say these are automatic responses, and do not use words like 'choose'.
- e.g. A moong sprout's root grows down, whichever way the seed lies.
- e.g. Roots grow towards wet soil and spread out more where they find water.
- e.g. Roots grow round stones and pipes they meet under the ground.
- **Many scientists who study how plants behave:** Roots feel and choose. A root senses gravity, water, touch and food in the soil all at once, and decides which way to grow.
- **Many other plant scientists:** Roots respond automatically to gravity, water and touch. They describe this with words like 'response', not 'choice'.

## Mostly agreed
- Roots hold a plant in the ground and take in water. *How we know:* Pull up a small weed and soil comes up stuck to the roots. A plant whose roots are cut droops, because the leaves stop getting water.
- Green leaves make the plant's food, using sunlight, air and water. *How we know:* Plants kept in the dark go pale and weak. In the 1770s, Jan Ingenhousz showed that green leaves in sunlight give out a gas, now called oxygen, as they make food.
- A fruit grows from a flower, and holds the seeds. *How we know:* Watch one flower on a brinjal or tomato plant. After the petals fall, a small fruit grows in the same place, with seeds inside.

## How we found out
**Suzanne Simard, a forest scientist from Canada, and her team, The 1990s; published in 1997.** In the forests of Canada, people who planted trees used to cut down the birch trees that grew among young fir trees. They thought the birch trees took water and light from the firs. They believed each tree lived alone and only fought for their own share. Suzanne Simard grew up in those forests, in a family who had cut trees there for years. She became a forest scientist. She had seen that young firs often did worse after the birch trees were cut. So she tested what happens under the ground. She covered young birch and fir trees with plastic bags. Into the bags she put air with a specially marked kind of carbon, which the trees used to make their food. Later, she tested the other trees for the marked carbon. Some food made by the birch trees had reached the firs. It had travelled through their roots, and through thin threads of fungi that join the roots of many trees. In 1997 she published her results in the science journal Nature. Other scientists still argue about how much food trees really share this way.  
*What people believed before:* That each tree lives alone under the ground, so cutting down the birch trees would always help the firs grow. Simard's marked carbon showed that some food passes between trees through roots and fungi. How much, and how much it matters, is still argued about.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Every plant has roots, a stem, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds.** (A common idea, from many school books; *rejected*)  
  For: Most plants we see around us, like mango trees, brinjal plants and roses, do have all six parts.  
  Against: Ferns and mosses never have flowers, fruits or seeds. They make new plants from tiny specks called spores. Mosses do not even have true roots, only thin threads that hold on.
- **Grass and rice have no flowers.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: We rarely see a bright flower on grass or on a rice plant.  
  Against: Grass and rice do have flowers. They are tiny and green, with no bright petals, because they do not need to call bees. The wind, or the flower itself, moves the pollen. Every grain of rice grew from one tiny rice flower.
- **Plants eat soil through their roots.** (An old idea that the Greek thinker Aristotle wrote about, and many people still think; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants grow in soil, and they die if the soil dries out.  
  Against: Roots drink water and take in tiny amounts of minerals from the soil. But most of a plant's body is made from food the leaves make, from air and water. A big tree in a pot uses up very little of the soil.

## Roots
We have found the six parts of a plant and the job each one does. Now we ask what the sprouts on the cloth tell us about how a plant and the ground belong together.

**Deep truth.** Our moong sprouts held on to the cloth when we turned it over. Their roots had grown down into the gaps between the threads. In a pot, roots grow into the gaps between bits of soil in the same way. So a plant holds on by growing into the ground, and the ground holds the plant.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a plant standing in the soil like a stick pushed into sand, which pulls straight out. But a plant grows into the soil, and the roots and the soil end up wrapped together. The plant and the ground hold each other.  
*Evidence:* In the sprout test, most sprouts on the cloth held on upside down, and root tips poked through the cloth. Most sprouts on the smooth plate fell off. In the moong cup project, the soil with roots came out in one lump, but bare soil crumbled.

- *who am i:* You have parts that work together too. Your feet hold you up, and your mouth takes in food and water for your whole body.
- *what is life:* A plant's life is all the parts working for the whole. The roots drink for the leaves, and the leaves make food for the roots.
- *what is this world:* Under every city street and park, roots are holding the soil together and holding the trees up.

A plant begins as one small seed. From that one seed grow roots, a stem, leaves, flowers, fruits and new seeds. Each part looks different and does a different job, yet they are all one plant. The roots feel the soil and choose where to grow, and the leaves feel the light. Together they keep one life going.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.1:** Speech, sight, hearing, mind and breath once argued about which of them was the most important. Each one left the body for a year, and the body lived on without them. But when breath began to leave, all the others were pulled away too. So they agreed that breath mattered most, because the others depend on breath. (The story asks which part matters most. In a plant the answer is harder. The roots depend on the leaves for food, and the leaves depend on the roots for water.)
- **Tao Te Ching 16:** All things grow and flourish, and then each one returns to their root. Returning to the root is called stillness. (The Tao Te Ching uses the root as a picture of the quiet place that things grow from. A plant's roots are out of sight and quiet, yet they hold the plant and drink for them every day.)
- Science (settled): One rye plant grown in a box for four months had about 622 kilometres of roots. Most of a plant's roots are too small or too deep for us to see.
- Science (settled): Root tips sense gravity, water and touch, and the root grows in the direction those senses point to.
- Other view, Many plant scientists: They describe roots growing into gaps as an automatic response to gravity and touch. They prefer words like 'response' and 'growth' to words like 'choose' and 'feel'.
- Practice, Stand Like a Tree (3 min): Take off your chappals. Stand with your feet a little apart. Close your eyes. Press your toes gently into the floor, as if they are roots growing down. Now sway slowly, like a tree in the wind. Feel how your feet hold you up. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out slowly, three times. Now open your eyes.
- Ask: If roots could tell us what they feel under the ground, what do you think they would say?
- Ask: Your sprouts held on to the cloth. What else do you think roots might hold on to?
- Ask: Is there one part of a plant that matters most, or do they all matter?

## Story: The Night the Brinjal Fell
*Anu, her Ammamma (her grandmother) and her Appa (her father) are made up. The plant facts are true: roots hold a pot's soil together, a plant can droop for a few days after some roots break, brinjal flowers are purple and grow into brinjals, and a ripe brinjal is full of seeds.*

It was the middle of the monsoon in Kochi. All night the wind blew hard, and the rain beat on the windows.

In the middle of the night there was a loud crash on the balcony. Anu sat up in bed. She was six, and she was not scared of storms. But she was scared for her brinjal plant.

In the morning she ran outside. The big brinjal pot lay on its side. The plant lay on the wet floor next to the pot.

Appa came out with his tea. 'Oh no,' he said. 'The plant is broken. We will have to throw the plant away.'

Ammamma came out behind him, holding on to the door. 'Wait,' she said. 'Look first. Then decide.'

Anu bent down. The soil had not spilled all over the floor. It had come out of the pot in one big lump, the same shape as the pot.

All round the lump were thin white roots. They went round and round, and in and out, like the threads of a net.

'The roots are holding the soil together!' said Anu.

'Yes,' said Ammamma. 'Roots grow into every tiny gap between the bits of soil. They wrap round the bits and hold on. That is how a plant stands up, even on a windy night like last night.'

'Then why did the pot fall over?' asked Appa.

Ammamma laughed. 'The roots held the soil. But nobody held the pot. The pot is light, the plant is tall, and the wind was strong.'

Ammamma showed Appa how to lift the plant gently, by holding the lump and not the stem. Anu put some fresh soil in the bottom of the pot. Together they slid the lump back in and pressed soil down round the edges. Then Anu gave the plant a big drink of water.

They moved the pot into a corner, where the wind could not reach.

By the afternoon, the leaves were hanging down, soft and floppy.

'Is my brinjal plant dying?' asked Anu.

'Some roots broke when the pot fell,' said Ammamma. 'The roots drink water for the whole plant. With fewer roots, the leaves get less water. Keep the plant out of the hot sun for a few days. New roots will grow.'

Every morning Anu checked. On the fourth morning, the leaves stood up again, wide and flat.

'The roots are drinking again,' she told Appa. 'The water goes up the stem to the leaves. The stem is like a road from the bottom of the plant to the top.'

Appa touched a big leaf. 'Why are the leaves so big and flat?' he asked.

Anu knew this one, because Ammamma had told her last month. 'Leaves catch the sunlight,' she said. 'They use the light to make food for the plant, out of air and water. A big flat leaf catches lots of light.'

A week later, Anu found a flower. The flower was purple, with a yellow middle, and hung down under a leaf. A small bee came and buzzed inside the flower.

'Why does a plant make flowers?' asked Anu.

'Watch this flower every day,' said Ammamma. 'You will see.'

A few days later, the purple petals had dried up and fallen. In their place was a tiny green brinjal, wearing the flower's green cap on top.

'The flower turned into a brinjal!' said Anu.

'Part of the flower grew into the brinjal,' said Ammamma. 'A brinjal is the plant's fruit. And the fruit holds the seeds.'

That month the family ate many brinjals, in sambar and fried in coconut oil. But Anu left one brinjal on the plant. It grew big, then turned yellow, then brown and wrinkly.

Ammamma cut the old brinjal open on a steel plate. Inside were lots of tiny flat seeds, pale brown, packed close together.

'Each seed has a tiny brinjal plant inside, fast asleep,' said Ammamma. 'Each seed also has a little packed food, for when the seed wakes up in wet soil.'

Anu dried the seeds on a newspaper in the sun. She put them in a paper packet and drew a brinjal on the front.

That night she told Appa all about the plant, from the bottom to the top. 'The roots hold on and drink. The stem holds the plant up and carries the water. The leaves make the food. The flower grows into the fruit. The fruit holds the seeds. And the seeds grow into new plants.'

'So which part is the most important?' asked Appa.

Anu thought about the night of the storm. 'All of them,' she said. 'The leaves need the roots, and the roots need the leaves. Every part works for the whole plant.'

Outside, in the corner of the balcony, the brinjal plant stood tall, holding on to the soil with every root.

**For the parent:** Anu learns each part's job by watching one plant. The roots held the soil in a lump, the leaves drooped when roots broke, the flower became a fruit, and the fruit held seeds. In the end she sees that the parts need each other.

- Ask: When the pot fell over, why did the soil come out in one lump?
- Ask: Why did the leaves droop after the storm, and why did they stand up again?
- Ask: Where did the brinjal come from?

## Experiments
### e1 · Sprouts Hold On: can roots grip? (25 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Moong sprouts grow on a cloth and on a steel plate. When we turn them upside down, which sprouts will hold on?
- **predict:** Guess first. We will turn the cloth and the plate upside down. Will the sprouts on the cloth hold on, or fall off? What about the sprouts on the plate? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both get the same moong, soaked the same night, the same water and the same place in the kitchen. Only the thing under the seeds is different: a cotton cloth or a bare steel plate.
- **record:** Draw the cloth and the plate after you turned them over. Draw the sprouts that held on. Write or say the two numbers.
- **results table:** Where the sprouts grew | How many sprouts | How many held on
- **schedule:** day 1: At night, soak 20 moong seeds in a bowl of water. (3 min); day 2: Wet the cloth and lay it on a plate. Put 10 soaked seeds on the cloth and 10 on a bare wet plate. Guess what will happen. Cover both. (10 min); day 3: Sprinkle a few drops of water on both, morning and evening. Peep at the roots. (1 min); day 4: Turn both upside down over a bowl. Count the sprouts that hold on. Look under the cloth. Say what you saw. (11 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At night, count 20 whole moong seeds with your child. 2. Put them in a small bowl. 3. Cover them with water.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count 20 moong seeds. They will drink water all night.  
  *Child mode:* Count 20 moong seeds. Put them in water.
  - Note: Use whole green moong, not split dal. Split dal has lost the tiny plant inside and cannot sprout.

### The Parts of a Plant (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (label) Here is a drawing of a plant. Write or say the name of each part in its box: roots, stem, leaf, flower, fruit.  
   **Answer:** ['the thin lines under the soil: roots', 'the long part in the middle: stem', 'the flat green part: leaf', 'the coloured part at the top: flower', 'the round part with seeds inside: fruit']. *Why:* Roots are under the soil. The stem holds the plant up. Leaves are flat and green. The flower is often coloured. The fruit grows from the flower and holds seeds.
2. (match) Draw a line from each part to the job it does.  
   **Answer:** Roots → drink water and hold on; Stem → holds the plant up; Leaves → make food from sunlight; Fruit → keeps the seeds safe. *Why:* Each part has its own job for the whole plant.
3. (mcq) A coriander plant came from the market with soil stuck to the roots. Why did the soil stay on?  
   Options: The soil was wet glue / The roots had grown into the soil and held on / Someone pressed the soil on  
   **Answer:** The roots had grown into the soil and held on. *Why:* Roots grow into the tiny gaps between bits of soil and wrap round them. That is why some soil stays stuck to the roots.
4. (truefalse) The leaves make food for the whole plant, even for the roots.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Leaves use sunlight to make food from air and water. The food goes down the stem to the roots, which live in the dark and cannot make food.
5. (draw) Draw the cloth from your sprout test after you turned the cloth upside down. Draw the sprouts that held on.  
   **Answer:** A cloth with most of the sprouts still hanging on it, and roots going into the cloth.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the sprouts hanging under the cloth, and roots going into it.
6. (sort) Which jobs belong to the roots, and which to the leaves? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** drink water from the soil → Roots; catch sunlight → Leaves; hold on to the soil → Roots; make food → Leaves. *Why:* Roots are under the soil, so they hold on and drink. Leaves are in the light, so they catch sunlight and make food.
7. (count) Count the seeds in this bhindi slice.  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* There are 5 rows with 2 seeds in each row. 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. So there are 10 seeds.
8. (odd) Three of these are parts of a plant. Which one is not?  
   Options: Roots / A leaf / A flower pot / A flower  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Roots, leaves and flowers are parts of the plant's body. A flower pot is a container that the plant grows in.
9. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: a banyan tree sends roots down from the branches. What do you think those roots are for?  
   **Answer:** They go into the ground and hold the branches up like pillars, and they drink water too.. *Why:* A strong answer says the new roots hold up the heavy branches, like pillars, and can drink like other roots.
10. (perspective) Lakshmi and her brother Hari look at the tulsi plant on their balcony in Madurai.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then sees that the other is right too. The roots need food from the leaves, and the leaves need water from the roots. A plant needs both.. *Why:* Each part does a job the others cannot do. The parts need each other.
11. (order) Put these in order, from the bottom of a plant to the top.  
   **Answer:** ['roots', 'stem', 'leaves', 'flower']. *Why:* Roots are under the soil. The stem grows up from the roots. Leaves grow from the stem. Flowers often grow at the top, where insects can find them.
12. (draw) Draw a plant with all six parts: roots, stem, leaves, a flower, a fruit and seeds. Ask a grown-up to help you write the names.  
   **Answer:** A plant with roots under a soil line, a stem, leaves, a flower, a fruit, and seeds inside the fruit.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the roots under the soil and the seeds inside the fruit.
### How the Parts Help Each Other (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) In the sprout test, why did the sprouts on the bare steel plate fall off?

## Applications
- Farmers and road builders plant grasses like vetiver on slopes. The deep roots hold the soil, so rain does not wash it away.
- People plant trees along river banks, so the roots hold the banks together in floods.
- Gardeners give a plant a bigger pot when the roots fill the old one, so the roots have room to grow.
- We eat every part of some plant: roots like carrots, stems like sugarcane, leaves like palak, flowers like banana flower, fruits like mangoes, and seeds like rice and dal.

## Open questions
- **How does a root tip know which way is down?** Scientists know that cells in the root tip feel gravity, using tiny heavy grains inside them. But exactly how those cells pass the message on, and how the root decides how fast to bend, is still being worked out.
- **Do the roots of different plants talk to each other under the ground?** Roots give out chemicals into the soil, and roots are joined by thin threads of fungi. Some scientists think plants share food and warnings this way. Others say the evidence is still weak.
- **Why do leaves come in so many different shapes?** Some leaf shapes help in hot sun or in strong wind. But for many shapes, nobody is sure why the plant grows them that way.

## Connected maths
- Shreya did the sprout test. On the cloth, 9 sprouts held on. On the bare plate, 2 sprouts held on. How many more sprouts held on to the cloth? **7 more sprouts held on to the cloth.**. Start at 2 and count on to 9: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. That is 7 more. The cloth held more sprouts because the roots could grow into the cloth. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- In the Six Parts, One Plant game, Kavya, a girl in Class 1, has 4 lotus cards. A whole plant needs 6 cards. How many more lotus cards does she need? **2 more cards.**. 4 and 2 make 6. So she needs 2 more lotus cards to have the whole lotus plant. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L1`)
- On day 8 of the moong cup project, Ritu, a girl in Class 1, measured three moong plants with her finger widths. One was 3 fingers tall, one was 5 fingers tall and one was 4 fingers tall. Put them in order, from the shortest to the tallest. **3 fingers, 4 fingers, 5 fingers.**. 3 is less than 4, and 4 is less than 5. So the order is 3, 4, 5. The plants came from the same packet of moong, but they did not all grow the same. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
