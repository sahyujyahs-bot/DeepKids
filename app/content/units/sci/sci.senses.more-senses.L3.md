# More than five senses: balance, body position, warmth, cold and pain
`sci.senses.more-senses.L3`

**Summary.** A steel plate feels colder than a wooden board, even when both have sat in the same room all morning. Put an ice cube on each, and the ice on the steel melts first. Your skin does not read temperature like a thermometer. It feels how fast heat leaves it or comes into it. This is one of many senses that are not on the list of five. Your inner ears sense turning and tilting, your muscles tell you where your hands are, and pain warns you before you get badly hurt. Other animals have senses people lack: a fish feels the water move along their sides, and a prawn keeps their balance with grains of sand.

**Intro.** *Spin round slowly five times, then sit down. The room seems to keep turning, though you and the room are both still. What is still spinning?* The answer is inside your ears. Deep in each ear are three tiny loops full of liquid. When you spin, the liquid starts to swirl. When you stop, it keeps swirling for a few seconds, so your brain thinks you are still turning. In this unit you find this sense and four more that are not on the list of five: the sense of where your body parts are, the sense of warmth and cold, the sense of pain, and the senses inside you. You test them with ice, steel plates, mint leaves and a bowl of water. Then you meet animals with senses people do not have.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name at least four senses that are not on the list of five, and say what each one tells me.
1. I can explain why I feel dizzy after spinning, using the liquid in the loops of my inner ear.
2. I can explain why a steel plate feels colder than a wooden board at the same temperature, using my ice test as evidence.
3. I can say how my muscles and joints tell my brain where my hands and feet are, without my eyes.
4. I can explain how pain protects the body, and what happens to people who cannot feel it.
5. I can describe a sense that people lack but another animal has. I can say what problem the animal faces, and how the sense helps them live.

## Terms
- **Sense**: A way your body detects something and tells your brain about it. *e.g.* Your balance sense tells you when you are tipping over, even with your eyes shut.
- **Receptor**: A special cell, or the end of a nerve, that detects one kind of thing, such as cold, stretch or a turn of the head. *e.g.* Cold receptors in your skin send a signal when your hand touches an ice cube.
- **Nerve**: A long, thin bundle that carries signals between the brain and every part of the body. *e.g.* A nerve carries the signal from your fingertip to your brain when you touch a hot tumbler.
- **Balance sense**: The sense that tells you which way is down, and when your head turns, tilts or speeds up. Its organs are deep inside each inner ear. Scientists call it the vestibular sense. *e.g.* Your balance sense tells you the lift has started to go up, even when the doors are shut.
- **Semicircular canals**: Three tiny loops of tube in each inner ear, filled with liquid. When your head turns, the liquid lags behind and bends tiny hairs, and the hairs send signals to your brain. *e.g.* When you shake your head to say no, the liquid in one loop moves and tells your brain your head is turning.
- **Ear crystals**: Tiny crystals, made of the same stuff as chalk and seashells, that sit on a jelly over hair cells in your inner ear. Gravity pulls on them, so they tell you which way is down. Scientists call them otoliths. *e.g.* When you tilt your head to one side, the ear crystals slide a little and your brain knows you are tilted.
- **Proprioception**: The sense of where your body parts are and how they are moving. Stretch sensors in your muscles, tendons and joints tell the brain. *e.g.* You can scratch your ear in the dark because proprioception tells you where your hand is.
- **Temperature sense**: The sense of warmth and cold. Separate receptors in the skin feel warming and cooling. *e.g.* When you step from a hot balcony into a room with the AC on, your skin feels the cold at once.
- **Conductor of heat**: A material that lets heat pass through it quickly. Metals are good conductors of heat. Wood, cloth and plastic are poor conductors. *e.g.* A steel spoon left in hot payasam gets hot all the way up its handle. A wooden ladle does not.
- **Pain**: The sense that warns you something is damaging, or about to damage, your body. Pain receptors detect strong heat, cuts, crushing and harmful chemicals. *e.g.* A pinch from a tight chappal strap hurts, so you loosen it before it makes a blister.
- **Reflex**: A quick movement your body makes without you choosing it, to protect you. The signal goes to your spinal cord and straight back to the muscle. *e.g.* Your hand jerks away from a hot vessel before you have time to think.
- **Interoception**: The senses inside your body. They tell you when you are hungry, thirsty, full, short of breath, or need the toilet. *e.g.* Your tummy rumbling and the empty feeling before lunch are interoception at work.
- **Lateral line**: A line of tiny sensors along each side of a fish's body. They feel the water moving around the fish. *e.g.* A rohu in a muddy pond feels a fish swimming close by through their lateral line, even when they cannot see it.
- **Statocyst**: A small balance pit in animals such as prawns, crabs and octopuses. Tiny grains or crystals rest on sensing hairs inside it, and show which way is down. *e.g.* A prawn has a statocyst at the base of each of their short feelers, holding grains of sand.

## Explanations
### Many more senses than five · *defined*
**Definition:** A sense is a way the body detects something and tells the brain. Besides sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch, people have a balance sense, a sense of body position, a temperature sense, a pain sense, and senses inside the body.

Each sense has its own receptors. Some sit where you can see them, like the eyes. Many are hidden. The balance organs are inside your head. The body-position sensors are inside your muscles and joints. Warm, cold and pain receptors are nerve endings in your skin and inside your body. Each kind of receptor turns what it detects into nerve signals. Your brain puts all the signals together. That is why you can walk down a dark staircase, feel that the bath water is too hot, and know when you are hungry.
- e.g. Walking down stairs in a power cut uses your balance sense and your body-position sense, because your eyes help very little.
- e.g. Feeling that the tea in your tumbler is too hot to drink uses your temperature sense and your pain sense.
- e.g. Knowing you need the toilet uses a sense inside your body.
### How your balance sense feels turning · *defined*
**Definition:** Each inner ear has three liquid-filled loops called semicircular canals. When the head turns, the liquid lags behind and bends tiny hairs, which send signals to the brain.

The three loops sit at right angles to each other, like the floor and two walls in the corner of a room. So one loop feels nodding, one feels shaking your head, and one feels tipping your head towards a shoulder. When you start to spin, the loops turn with your head, but the liquid inside is slow to start moving. It pushes against the hairs, and the brain knows you are turning. If you keep spinning, the liquid catches up and turns along with you. When you stop, the liquid keeps swirling for a few seconds. It bends the hairs the other way, and the brain thinks you are spinning in the opposite direction. That is dizziness. You saw the same thing with the bowl of water: the water kept turning after the bowl stopped.
- e.g. After a ride on a giant wheel at a temple festival, you may feel the ground sway for a moment.
- e.g. Dancers who spin, such as Kathak dancers, fix their eyes on one spot and whip their head round, so they get less dizzy.
- e.g. After spinning, a grown-up can see your eyes flick from side to side for a few seconds. The brain moves the eyes because it thinks the head is still turning.
### Ear crystals: which way is down · *defined*
**Definition:** Two small pouches in each inner ear hold tiny crystals of calcium carbonate on a layer of jelly. Gravity and speeding up make the crystals press or slide, and the hair cells below tell the brain.

Calcium carbonate is the same stuff as chalk and seashells. The crystals are heavier than the jelly around them. When you tilt your head, gravity pulls the crystals down a little, and they bend the hairs under them. When a lift starts to go up, the crystals lag behind for a moment and press down harder. So you feel the lift start, even with the doors shut. When it stops, you feel a moment of lightness. Your balance sense works only for changes. In a smooth lift moving at a steady speed, or a plane flying smoothly, you feel nothing at all.
- e.g. You feel the lift start and stop, but not the steady part of the ride.
- e.g. Lying on your side, you still know which way is up with your eyes shut.
- e.g. In space, there is no feeling of down, and many astronauts feel sick for the first few days.
### Knowing where your hands are · *defined*
**Definition:** Proprioception is the sense of where your body parts are and how they move. Stretch sensors in muscles, and sensors in tendons and joints, send signals to the brain all the time.

Every muscle has tiny stretch sensors inside it. When your elbow bends, some muscles stretch and others get shorter, and the sensors report it. Sensors in your tendons feel how hard a muscle is pulling. Your brain puts these signals together into a picture of where each part of you is. That is how you can eat idli without looking at your hand, or type on a phone while looking at the screen. In the dot test, your pencil landed close to the dot with your eyes shut, but not exactly on it. Without your eyes to check, the body-position sense is good but not perfect.
- e.g. You can put a spoon of rice into your mouth while looking at the TV.
- e.g. When your foot 'falls asleep' after sitting cross-legged, you cannot feel where it is, so it is hard to stand on.
- e.g. In 2021, Ardem Patapoutian shared the Nobel Prize for finding Piezo2, a tiny gate in nerve endings that opens when they are stretched. People born without working Piezo2 have very poor body-position sense.
### Why steel feels colder than wood · *defined*
**Definition:** Your skin feels warmth and cold by how fast heat flows out of it or into it. Metals are good conductors of heat, so they draw heat from your skin quickly and feel colder.

Heat always flows from a warmer thing to a cooler thing. The skin of your hand is warmer than most rooms. Touch a steel plate, and the steel carries heat away from the skin very fast, so the skin cools quickly. Your cold receptors fire strongly, and you feel cold. Touch a wooden board at the same temperature, and the wood carries heat away slowly. The skin stays almost as warm, so the wood feels only a little cool. The ice test shows that it is not the plate itself that is colder. The ice was colder than both. Steel carried heat into the ice fast, so that cube melted first. Wood carried heat in slowly, so that cube lasted longer.
- e.g. The steel railing on a balcony feels colder than the wooden door in the morning, though both are at the temperature of the air.
- e.g. A steel tumbler of tea feels hotter in your hand than a clay cup with the same tea, because steel carries heat into your skin fast.
- e.g. A tiled floor feels colder to bare feet than a cotton mat on the same floor.
### Warm spots and cold spots · *defined*
**Definition:** The skin has separate receptors for cold and for warmth. They are not spread evenly, and there are many more cold spots than warm spots.

In 1882, a Swedish scientist, Magnus Blix, touched the skin with a thin metal tip, point by point. Some points felt cold, some felt warm, and some felt only a touch. In 1884, Alfred Goldscheider in Germany found the same on his own. So warmth and cold are sensed by different receptors in different places. Cold receptors are the more common kind. That is why the cold-spot map on the back of your hand had cold squares and squares that felt only a touch. The same receptors can be fooled by chemicals. Menthol, in mint, switches on cold receptors, so your breath feels icy after a mint leaf. Capsaicin, in chilli, switches on heat receptors, so chilli feels hot. David Julius and Ardem Patapoutian shared the 2021 Nobel Prize for finding these receptors.
- e.g. After chewing pudina, a sip of room-temperature water feels cold.
- e.g. Green chilli makes your mouth feel hot, though the chilli is no warmer than the rest of the food.
- e.g. Some balms for aches contain menthol, and they feel cool on the skin.
### Pain is a guard · *defined*
**Definition:** Pain receptors detect things that damage the body: strong heat, deep cold, cuts, crushing and harmful chemicals. Pain makes you stop, pull away and look after the hurt part.

Pain is unpleasant on purpose. It makes you take your hand off the hot tawa, loosen a tight strap and rest a sprained ankle. Some protection is faster than feeling. When you touch something very hot, the signal goes to your spinal cord and straight back to your arm muscles. Your hand pulls away, often before you feel the pain. This is a reflex. A very small number of children are born unable to feel pain. They often get hurt without noticing: they bite their tongues, burn their hands and walk on broken bones. In the 1940s and 1950s, the surgeon Paul Brand found the same in people with leprosy in Vellore, as the how-we-found-out card tells. Pain is a gift that keeps the body whole.
- e.g. A small pebble in your shoe hurts, so you stop and shake it out before it makes a wound.
- e.g. A sore throat makes you rest your voice.
- e.g. Your hand jerks away from a hot vessel before you have even said 'ouch'.
### Animals with senses people lack · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals have senses that people do not have. Each one helps that animal with a problem in their own life.

A fish in a muddy river cannot see far. They need to know where other fish and hunters are. Their lateral line, a row of sensors along each side, feels the water moving. So a shoal of sardines can turn together without bumping, even at night. A prawn lives in dark, moving water and must know which way is up. They keep grains of sand in a small pit at the base of each short feeler. When a prawn moults their shell, they lose the grains, and pick up new sand. A female mosquito needs blood to make her eggs, and must find someone in the dark. She senses the carbon dioxide in your breath from metres away, then your warmth and smell. A python hunts warm-bodied animals at night. Pits along their lips sense the heat from a rat's body. A spider's eyes see little, but the hairs and slits on their legs feel every shake of their web, so they know where an insect is caught.
- e.g. A shoal of mathi, sardines, off the Kerala coast turns all at once, using their lateral lines and eyes.
- e.g. A mosquito finds you in a dark room by your breath and warmth.
- e.g. A garden spider rushes to the exact spot where a fly hit the web.
### Why some people feel sick in a car or a boat · *debated*
Many children feel sick reading in a moving car, or on a ferry. Nobody is completely sure why. The best-known idea is that the senses disagree. The ears feel the car turning and bumping. The eyes, looking at a book inside the car, see no movement at all. Another idea says the sickness comes from the body struggling to keep steady when the ground moves in an unfamiliar way. Both ideas agree on what helps: look out at the horizon, sit where the ride is smoothest, and get fresh air.
- e.g. Reading a book in a car on the ghat road to Ooty makes many children feel sick.
- e.g. Looking out of the front window at the road often helps.
- e.g. Sailors often stop feeling seasick after a few days at sea.
- **Sensory conflict idea (James Reason and others, 1970s):** The balance sense and the eyes send signals that do not match. The brain is confused by the mismatch, and the confusion makes you feel sick.
- **Body-steadiness idea (Gary Riccio and Thomas Stoffregen, 1991):** Sickness comes when the body cannot keep itself steady in a new kind of moving place. People who sway more before the trip are more likely to feel sick.
### How many senses are there? · *debated*
Scientists do not agree on one number. It depends on how you count. If you count by kind of receptor, you find many: warmth, cold, pain, stretch, the three loops of the ear, the ear crystals, light, sound, the five basic tastes, many kinds of smell, and sensors that check your blood and your breath. If you count by experience, you get fewer. Some scientists say about 10, some say over 30.
- e.g. Is touch one sense, or several, since light touch, vibration, warmth, cold and pain each have their own receptors?
- e.g. Is balance one sense, or two, since the loops feel turning and the crystals feel tilting?
- **Count by receptor:** Each kind of receptor is a separate sense, because each detects something different. This gives more than 20.
- **Count by organ or experience:** Senses that feel alike, such as warmth and cold, are one sense. This gives about 10.

## Mostly agreed
- People have many more than five senses, including balance, body position, warmth, cold, pain and senses inside the body. *How we know:* Scientists have found a separate kind of receptor for each, and people who lose one of them, through illness or a rare gene, lose that sense only.
- The semicircular canals in the inner ear sense the head turning, and the ear crystals sense tilting and speeding up. *How we know:* In the 1820s, Pierre Flourens found that damaging the canals in pigeons upset their balance. Today doctors test the canals by watching the eyes after spinning or after warm or cool water is put in the ear.
- The skin feels how fast heat flows in or out of it, so good conductors of heat feel colder or hotter than poor conductors at the same temperature. *How we know:* A thermometer shows a steel plate and a wooden board in the same room are at the same temperature. Yet ice melts faster on the steel, and the steel feels colder.
- Pain protects the body, and people who cannot feel pain get injured far more often. *How we know:* Doctors have followed children born unable to feel pain, and people with leprosy whose nerves were damaged. Both get burns, cuts and broken bones without noticing.
- Fish feel water movement through their lateral line, and prawns and crabs sense which way is down using grains in a balance pit. *How we know:* Fish with a blocked lateral line swim less well in a shoal. In 1893, Alois Kreidl gave freshly moulted shrimps iron filings instead of sand, and the shrimps tilted when he held a magnet near them.

## How we found out
**Paul Brand, a surgeon, and Margaret Brand, an eye doctor, at the Christian Medical College, Vellore, The late 1940s to the 1960s.** For thousands of years, people believed leprosy made fingers and toes rot and fall off by itself. People with leprosy lost parts of their hands and feet, and many were pushed out of their towns. In the late 1940s, Paul Brand, a British surgeon at the Christian Medical College in Vellore, began to look closely. He noticed that his patients often hurt their hands while working, and did not seem to notice. He tested the hands of many patients. Their flesh was healthy, but the nerves had stopped carrying pain. So their hands were being hurt in daily life. A patient could hold a hot cooking pot, walk all day with a sharp stone in a shoe, or keep using a cut hand, and never feel it. The wounds got worse because nobody rested them. His wife, Margaret Brand, an eye doctor, found that the same thing harmed the eyes. Patients who could not feel their eyes did not blink enough to protect them. With soft shoes, careful habits and checking hands and feet every day, people with leprosy kept their fingers and toes. Leprosy can now be cured with medicines that are free in India.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the disease itself ate away fingers and toes. Paul Brand showed that leprosy damages the nerves, so people stop feeling pain, and the injuries come from everyday use that nobody feels.

## Challenging perspectives
- **People have exactly five senses.** (Aristotle, about 2,350 years ago, and many school books since; *rejected*)  
  For: Five senses each have an organ you can point to, so the list is easy to teach.  
  Against: Balance, body position, warmth, cold and pain each have their own receptors. Anyone can test them, as you did with the bowl, the dot and the ice.
- **Metal is colder than wood.** (A very common idea, because metal feels colder; *rejected*)  
  For: When you touch them, the steel really does feel colder than the wood.  
  Against: Left in the same room, both reach the same temperature, as a thermometer shows. Ice melts faster on the steel, which could not happen if the steel were colder.
- **Pain is bad, and life would be better without it.** (Many people, when they are hurting; *rejected*)  
  For: Pain is unpleasant, and long-lasting pain from an illness can make life very hard.  
  Against: People who cannot feel pain get badly hurt without knowing. Short, sharp pain is a warning that protects the body. Long-lasting pain is different and needs a doctor's help.
- **Feeling dizzy means something is wrong with you.** (Some children, the first time they get dizzy; *rejected*)  
  For: Dizziness feels strange and can make you fall.  
  Against: Dizziness after spinning is your balance sense working correctly: the liquid in the loops keeps moving after you stop. Dizziness that comes for no reason, again and again, should be checked by a doctor.

## Roots
We have tested our balance, our body sense, warmth, cold and pain. Now we ask what these senses tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The steel plate felt colder than the wooden board, though both sat in one room all morning. Yet the ice melted faster on the steel. Steel carries heat fast, out of your hand and into the ice. So your skin feels heat moving in or out, not how hot a thing is.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think our hand reads temperature like a thermometer, so whatever feels colder must be colder. But the skin reports the flow of heat through it. Two things at the same temperature can feel very different.  
*Evidence:* In the Ice Race, the steel plate felt colder to the touch. The ice cube on the steel melted much faster than the one on the wooden board. Both had been in the same room for at least an hour, so both were at room temperature.

- *who am i:* You are always trading heat with everything you touch, and your skin keeps track of every trade.
- *what is life:* A prawn uses sand from the sea floor to find down, and you use crystals your body made. Every living being builds senses for the life they live.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of things that mosquitoes, fish and spiders sense and we cannot. What we feel is only part of what is there.

Heat flows from warmer things to cooler things everywhere: from the Sun to the Earth, from hot tea to the air, from your hand to a plate. Your skin does not stand outside that flow. It is one of the places the flow passes through, and the feeling of cold is that flow passing. The same flow melted the ice. So your feeling and the melting ice were two signs of one movement of heat.

- **Bhagavad Gita 2.14:** Krishna tells Arjuna that the touch of the senses with things brings cold and heat, pleasure and pain. These come and go, and do not last. He asks Arjuna to bear them steadily. (The Gita calls cold and heat a meeting of the senses with things. Your test found the same. The cold was in the meeting, as heat moved between your hand and the steel, and it changed when the meeting changed.)
- **Satipatthana Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 10:** The Buddha teaches that when walking, one knows 'I am walking'. When standing, sitting or lying down, one knows that too. One stays aware of the body as it moves. (Knowing how your body is placed, without looking, is the sense of body position. The Buddha asked people to notice this sense, which works quietly all day.)
- Science (settled): Heat always flows from a warmer thing to a cooler thing, and it flows faster through metals than through wood, cloth or air.
- Science (settled): Separate receptors in the skin detect cooling and warming. Some of them were found only in the last 30 years, and the work won the 2021 Nobel Prize.
- Science (open): How signals from cold receptors become the feeling of cold is not known.
- Other view, Many physiologists: Cold is just the name we give to signals from cold receptors. Saying the feeling is a meeting with the world adds nothing to the science of how heat flows and how nerves fire.
- Practice, Feel the heat moving (3 min): Close your eyes. Rest one palm on a steel tumbler and the other on a wooden spoon or a cloth. Keep them still for one minute. Notice how the cold under the steel fades. Your skin is giving its warmth to the steel, and the steel is getting warmer. Now lift both hands, and breathe slowly three times. Can you still feel where the steel was?
- Ask: If your skin feels heat moving, not temperature, what else might your senses be telling you that is different from what you think?
- Ask: The steel took your warmth until it was nearly as warm as you. Where does your warmth go when you touch things all day?
- Ask: A prawn uses sand from outside their body to sense. Can something from outside you become part of how you sense?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your skin said the steel was colder, but the ice said it was not. When a sense and a test disagree, which one tells you the truth? (hint: Think about what each one really measured: the heat leaving your hand, or the temperature of the plate.)
- *deep:* When you stop spinning, the room seems to turn. Where is that turning: in the room, in your ears, or in your mind? (hint: Think about the liquid that keeps swirling in the loops, and what your brain does with the signal.)
- *big:* A prawn feels which way is down using sand grains they picked up. Is the sand part of the prawn? (hint: The sand came from the sea floor, and it does a job inside the prawn's body.)
- *big:* A fish feels the water move, and a mosquito senses your breath. What might be all around us that no person can sense? (hint: Think of all the senses people lack, and what each one picks up.)
- *big:* Pain hurts, but it keeps us safe. Can something unpleasant also be a kind of care? (hint: Think about the children and adults who could not feel pain, and what happened to their hands and feet.)
- *deep:* You feel your hand even with your eyes shut. How do you know a hand is yours? (hint: Think about the stretch sensors in your muscles and the signals they send all the time.)

## Story: The Prawn Who Picked Up Sand
*The story is made up, and the people in it are not real. The science is true: prawns and shrimps keep sand grains in a balance pit at the base of their short feelers, lose them when they moult and pick up new ones. Alois Kreidl's iron filings test was done in Austria in 1893. Looking at the horizon helps many people with seasickness, though not everyone.*

The ferry from Ernakulam to Vypin was crowded and loud. Arjun was eight, and he had been looking forward to this trip all week. His aunt, Reshma Chechi, studied prawns at a laboratory on the island. Today she was taking him to see them.

But the ferry rocked. Arjun had brought his comic, and he was reading it on the bench. After a few pages his tummy began to feel strange. His mouth filled with spit. His forehead was cold and damp.

"Chechi," he said, "I think I'm going to be sick."

Reshma Chechi took the comic gently out of his hands. "Look out there," she said, and pointed at the line where the grey water met the sky. "Keep your eyes on that line. Breathe slowly."

Arjun looked. The ferry went up and down, and the far line stayed still. Slowly, his tummy settled.

"Why did that help?" he asked.

"Inside your ears you have balance organs," his aunt said. "They felt the boat rocking. But your eyes were on the comic, which was rocking with you. So your eyes said 'nothing is moving', and your ears said 'everything is moving'. Many scientists think that kind of disagreement is what makes people feel sick. When you looked at the horizon, your eyes saw the rocking too. Your eyes and ears agreed again."

At the lab there were long rows of glass tanks, bubbling softly. Reshma Chechi led him to one with sand at the bottom. Small, see-through prawns hung in the water, their long feelers waving.

"Look at that one," she said. In the corner of the tank lay something that looked like a whole prawn, but empty and thin as paper.

"Is it dead?" Arjun whispered.

"That is a shell," she said. "A prawn's shell does not grow. So when a prawn gets too big, they wriggle out of the old shell and grow a new one. This is called moulting. The prawn who left that shell is over there, soft and new."

She pointed at a prawn near the sand. The prawn was tilting oddly, leaning to one side, then the other.

"Why is she wobbling?" asked Arjun.

"Because she has lost her balance stones," said his aunt. "At the base of a prawn's short feelers there is a tiny pit. Inside it are hairs, and on the hairs sit a few grains of sand. When the prawn tips, the grains roll and press on the hairs. That tells her which way is down. But the pit is part of the shell. When she moulted, the old pit went away with the old shell, sand and all."

"So how does she get new ones?"

"Watch."

The prawn dipped her head to the sand. Her tiny front legs moved quickly, picking at the grains. A few minutes later she was swimming straight and steady.

Arjun's mouth fell open. "She put sand in her own head!"

Reshma Chechi laughed. "More or less. In 1893, a scientist in Austria called Alois Kreidl had a clever idea. He put freshly moulted shrimps in a tank with no sand at all, only tiny bits of iron, called iron filings. The shrimps picked up the iron and put it in their balance pits. Then Kreidl held a magnet above them."

"What happened?"

"The magnet attracted the iron towards it. So the shrimps felt as if down had moved. They tipped over to one side, as if the sea floor had moved."

Arjun laughed out loud. Then he stopped. "Chechi, I don't have sand in my ears. Do I?"

"Not sand," said his aunt. "But very close. In each of your inner ears there are two little pouches. They hold thousands of tiny crystals, made of the same stuff as chalk and seashells. They rest on a jelly, on top of hairs. When you tilt your head, the crystals slide a little, and the hairs tell your brain. Your body made those crystals itself. The prawn borrows hers from the sea floor."

"And the rocking on the ferry?"

"That is a different part. You also have three tiny loops full of liquid in each ear. They feel your head turning, and the boat kept turning you this way and that."

On the ferry home, the sun was low and orange. The ferry rocked just as much as before. Arjun did not open his comic. He sat at the rail and kept his eyes on the line where the water met the sky.

He tilted his head slowly to one side, and then to the other. He imagined thousands of tiny crystals sliding on their jelly, and the hairs underneath sending their signals.

"Chechi," he said, "the prawn and I are doing the same thing."

"Yes," said Reshma Chechi. "With different stones."

**For the parent:** For the parent: balance comes from something heavy resting on sensing hairs. When the head tips, gravity pulls the heavy bits, they press on the hairs, and the brain knows which way is down. A prawn uses grains of sand. People use tiny crystals of calcium carbonate made by the body. Seasickness is not fully understood, but the best-known idea is that the eyes and the balance sense disagree, and looking at the horizon helps them agree.

- Ask: Why did Arjun feel sick while reading his comic on the ferry?
- Ask: Why did the prawn wobble after she moulted?

### e1 · The Ice Race: does ice melt slower on the colder-feeling plate? (30 min)
- **question:** A steel plate feels colder than a wooden board. If we put an ice cube on each, which one melts first?
- **predict:** Touch the steel plate and the wooden board. Which feels colder? Now guess: on which one will the ice cube melt first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Leave both surfaces in the same room for at least an hour, out of the sun and away from the fan, so they reach the same temperature. Use two ice cubes of the same size from the same tray. Put them down at the same moment. Only the surface changes.
- **safety:** No heat is used. Wipe up melted water so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the two cubes as they looked after 10 minutes, with their puddles.
- **results table:** Surface | How it felt to my hand | My guess: melts first or last? | After 5 minutes | After 10 minutes | After 15 minutes | Which melted first?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. An hour before, put the steel plate and the wooden board side by side on a table. 2. Keep them out of the sun and away from the fan. 3. Now your child presses one palm flat on each for 5 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* These two have been sitting in the same room for an hour. Put one hand flat on each. Which one feels colder?  
  *Child mode:* Put one hand on the steel. Put the other hand on the wood. Which feels colder?
  - Ask: Which one feels colder? Is it really colder, do you think? (good: The steel feels colder.; The steel is colder.; I am not sure, they have been in the same room.)
  - Note: Almost everyone says the steel feels colder. Accept whatever your child says about why. Do not explain yet.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now we will put an ice cube on each. On which one will the ice melt first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* An ice cube goes on each. Which one melts first? Why?
  - Note: Many children say the ice will last longer on the steel, because the steel feels colder. That is a good guess with a good reason. Let the test answer it.
- **s3 (16 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take two ice cubes from the same tray. 2. Put one in the middle of the plate and one in the middle of the board, at the same moment. 3. Look every 5 minutes. 4. Write what you see each time.  
  *Parent says:* Both cubes go down together. Now we watch. Every 5 minutes, tell me how big each cube is and how big its puddle is.  
  *Child mode:* Put both cubes down at the same time. Look every 5 minutes. Which cube is getting smaller faster?
  - Note: The cube on the steel melts much faster. In a warm room it is often a puddle in 10 to 15 minutes, while the cube on the wood is still mostly whole. Steel thalis are thin, so the difference is smaller than with a thick metal. If the difference is small, add a third cube on an aluminium vessel or lid. Aluminium carries heat even faster than steel. While you wait, you can play a round of Which Sense Told You?, but do not explain the result yet.
  - Check: The ice on the steel plate melted first. The steel had felt colder. What does this tell you? → **The steel was not really colder than the wood**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Touch the steel plate where the ice was, and the wooden board where its ice was.  
  *Parent says:* Touch the steel under the puddle. Now touch the wood under its cube. What do you feel now?  
  *Child mode:* Touch the steel under the puddle. Touch the wood under its cube. What do you feel?
  - Note: The steel feels icy all around the puddle, because it gave its heat to the ice. The wood is cold only right under the cube. Heat moves through steel quickly, and through wood slowly.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A strong answer: What I think: the steel was not colder than the wood, it only felt colder. What I saw: the ice melted faster on the steel. Why: steel carries heat fast. It takes heat out of my hand fast, so it feels cold. It gives heat to the ice fast, so the ice melts. My skin feels heat leaving, not the temperature.
  - Check: Why did the steel feel colder to your hand? → **It carried heat away from your hand faster**

  *Child mode:* Spin slowly 5 times. Sit down. Look at a grown-up's face. Say stop when the room is still.

- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* Which time was longer? What do you think is still moving after you stop?  
  *Child mode:* Which dizziness lasted longer? What keeps moving after you stop?
  - Note: A strong answer: after 10 turns, the dizziness lasted longer. The liquid in the loops of my inner ear was swirling more, so it took longer to stop. While it was still swirling, my brain thought I was turning.
  - Check: Why does the room seem to spin after you stop? → **The liquid in your ear loops keeps swirling for a few seconds**

## Applications
- Doctors test the balance sense by watching a patient's eyes after they turn their head, or after warm or cool water is put gently in the ear.
- Pilots train in spinning chairs to learn that their balance sense can fool them in cloud, so they trust their instruments.
- Cooking vessels have wooden or plastic handles, because these carry heat slowly and do not burn the hand.
- Mint and menthol are used in toothpaste, sweets and balms to make the mouth or skin feel cool.
- People with diabetes or leprosy check their feet every day, because they may not feel a wound.
- Engineers have built robot boats and underwater robots with flow sensors that copy a fish's lateral line.
- Physiotherapists help people recover their body-position sense after an injury, with balance boards and eyes-shut exercises.
- Mosquito traps use carbon dioxide and warmth to attract mosquitoes, copying the signals they follow to find people.

## Open questions
- **How do signals from cold receptors become the feeling of cold?** Scientists can follow the signals into the brain, but nobody knows how signals become a feeling.
- **Exactly why do some people get car sick or seasick, and others never do?** There are several ideas, such as the senses disagreeing or the body struggling to stay steady. None of them explains every case.
- **How many senses do people really have?** It depends on how you count, by receptor or by experience. Scientists have not agreed on one way.
- **Do elephants hear through their feet?** Elephants make very low rumbles that travel through the ground. Some studies, such as Caitlin O'Connell-Rodwell's, found that elephants react to these ground rumbles. How far and how well they use them is still being studied.

Each extra 5 turns added 8 seconds, so 14 + 8 = 22. It is only a guess, because the liquid in the ear may stop speeding up after a while, and we have not tested 15 turns. - In Find the Dot, Arjun's misses with his writing hand were 2, 1, 3, 1 and 2 cm. With his other hand they were 4, 3, 2, 3 and 3 cm. Which hand did better? By how much in total? **His writing hand did better, by 6 cm in total.**. Writing hand: 2 + 1 + 3 + 1 + 2 = 9 cm. Other hand: 4 + 3 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 15 cm. 15 - 9 = 6. A smaller total miss means the hand landed closer. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)

These are made-up numbers, and your own times will depend on the room. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
