# Day and night: why the Sun reaches Guwahati before Mumbai
`sci.day-night-seasons.day-and-night.L4`

**Summary.** One Sun, one country and one clock, yet the Sun rises over Guwahati about an hour before it rises over Mumbai. You check this with sunrise and sunset times from five cities, and you find a neat pattern hidden in the untidy numbers: the Sun is highest about 4 minutes later for every degree you go west. You measure the spinning Earth on an orange and find that a person in Chennai is carried much faster than a person near the Arctic, though both make one turn a day. Then you follow the spin out to the length of a day on other planets, to a day that is slowly getting longer, and to the animals who live in the dark.

**Intro.** *Open a weather app and look at today's sunrise in Guwahati and in Mumbai. Both times are on the same clock, the clock of one country. Yet they are about an hour apart. Did the Sun visit Guwahati first?* The Sun did not visit anyone. The Earth is turning, and the Sun's light reaches each place in its turn. In this unit you collect real sunrise and sunset times from five cities and find the pattern in them. You measure how fast the spin carries different places, using an orange. You make a model of why a day is a little longer than one spin. You also find out how the length of a day changes on other planets and over millions of years, and who is awake while we sleep.

## Terms
- **Rotate**: To spin around a line through the middle. *e.g.* The Earth rotates once in about a day.
- **Axis**: The line that something rotates around. The Earth's axis runs through the North Pole and the South Pole. *e.g.* A skewer pushed through an orange is its axis.
- **Anticlockwise**: Turning the opposite way to the hands of a clock. Seen from above the North Pole, the Earth turns anticlockwise, which is from west to east. *e.g.* A person turning slowly to their left is turning anticlockwise.
- **Day-night line**: The line between the half of the Earth that is lit by the Sun and the half that is dark. *e.g.* At dawn the line is passing over you.
- **Latitude**: How far north or south of the equator a place is, measured in degrees. *e.g.* Chennai is about 13 degrees north.
- **Longitude**: How far east or west of a line through Greenwich in London a place is, measured in degrees. *e.g.* Chennai is about 80 degrees east. Mumbai is about 73 degrees east.
- **Equator**: The imaginary circle around the middle of the Earth, halfway between the poles. *e.g.* The equator passes through Kenya, Indonesia and Brazil.
- **Solar noon**: The moment when the Sun is at its highest point in the sky at a place. Shadows are shortest then. *e.g.* At solar noon, a stick's shadow is at its shortest.
- **Indian Standard Time**: The one clock time used all over India. It is set to match the Sun on the line of longitude 82.5 degrees east. *e.g.* A train timetable from Kolkata to Mumbai uses Indian Standard Time at both ends.
- **Star day**: The time the Earth takes to spin once compared with the faraway stars. It is 23 hours 56 minutes. Scientists also call it a sidereal day. *e.g.* A bright star comes back to the same place in the sky after one star day.
- **Equatorial bulge**: The Earth is a little wider around the equator than from pole to pole, because it spins. *e.g.* The Earth is about 43 kilometres wider across the equator than from pole to pole.
- **Nocturnal**: Active at night and resting in the day. *e.g.* A bat is nocturnal.
- **Light pollution**: Too much artificial light at night. It hides the stars and confuses animals. *e.g.* Streetlights near a beach can lead baby turtles away from the sea.

## Explanations
### The Earth spins west to east, so the Sun reaches the east first · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth spins once around its axis in about a day, from west to east. So places in the east turn into the sunlight before places in the west.

Seen from above the North Pole, the Earth turns anticlockwise. A place in the east is carried into the sunlit half before a place in the west. That is why the Sun rises in the east, and why Arunachal Pradesh sees sunrise before Gujarat does. Half of the Earth is always lit. The day-night line runs from the North Pole to the South Pole. Relative to the ground, it sweeps westward all day and all night. When the dawn part of the line is over Guwahati, Dong in the east is already in daylight, and Mumbai in the west is still in the dark. The line never stops, so at every moment, somewhere on Earth, it is sunrise.
- e.g. The Sun rises in the east because the ground under us turns towards the east.
- e.g. Sunrise in Arunachal Pradesh comes more than an hour before sunrise in Gujarat.
- e.g. At any moment, it is sunrise somewhere on Earth.
### The Sun is highest about 4 minutes later for every degree you go west · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth turns 1 degree of longitude in 4 minutes. So solar noon comes about 4 minutes later for each degree of longitude we move west.

The Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours. 24 hours is 1,440 minutes. 1,440 divided by 360 is 4. So the Earth turns 1 degree every 4 minutes. Two places that are 1 degree apart in longitude have their solar noon 4 minutes apart. India is about 29 degrees wide, from about 68 degrees east to about 97 degrees east. That is about 116 minutes, nearly two hours. Sunrise gaps are untidy, because sunrise also depends on how far north you are and on the season. In winter, a northern city has a later sunrise and an earlier sunset than a southern one. But the middle of the day, solar noon, depends only on longitude. You can find it by adding the sunrise and sunset times and taking the middle. That is why the noon gaps come out neat.
- e.g. Chennai is about 4 degrees east of Kochi, so noon comes about 16 minutes earlier in Chennai.
- e.g. Delhi and Bengaluru are almost on the same line of longitude, so their noons are only about 2 minutes apart, though they are far apart north to south.
- e.g. Guwahati and Mumbai are about 19 degrees apart, so noon in Mumbai is about 75 minutes later.
### Clock time and Sun time: Indian Standard Time · *defined*
**Definition:** Indian Standard Time is the one clock time used all over India. It matches the Sun on the line of longitude 82.5 degrees east, which passes near Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh. It is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of the world's reference time, UTC.

Long ago, every town set its clocks by its own Sun. That worked until trains and the telegraph needed timetables that made sense in many towns. In 1906 British India introduced one standard time, Indian Standard Time. Calcutta and Bombay kept their own city clocks for many more years. Today one Indian Standard Time is used for the whole country. On this clock, the Sun is highest at 12 o'clock only on the line 82.5 degrees east. Chennai is about 2 degrees west of that line, so its noon comes about 9 minutes after 12. Guwahati is about 9 degrees east of it, so its noon comes about 37 minutes before 12. Dong, in Arunachal Pradesh, is about 14 degrees east, so its noon comes close to an hour before 12. These noon times are yearly averages. During the year the Sun's noon moves a few minutes either way, but the gaps between cities stay the same. The clock is an agreement among people. The Sun's time belongs to each place. Some tea gardens in Assam have kept their own garden time, one hour ahead of Indian Standard Time, so that work starts at sunrise.
- e.g. Noon by the Sun in Mumbai is about 12:38, not 12:00.
- e.g. Noon by the Sun in Guwahati is about 11:23, not 12:00.
- e.g. Many Assam tea gardens run on garden time, one hour ahead of Indian Standard Time.
### How fast you are carried: the same turn, a different circle · *defined*
**Definition:** Every place on Earth makes one full turn in a day. A place far from the equator travels round a smaller circle, so it is carried more slowly.

The equator is about 40,075 kilometres around. A person on the equator is carried once round it in 24 hours. 40,075 divided by 24 is about 1,670 kilometres an hour. Move north and the circle that your place makes gets smaller, like the rings on a spinning top. The circle at 60 degrees north, where Oslo and Helsinki lie, is only half as long as the equator. A person there is carried at about 835 kilometres an hour. At the North Pole itself, a person only turns on the spot. Speed depends on latitude, and the nearer you are to the equator, the faster you go. Chennai, at 13 degrees north, is carried at about 1,625 kilometres an hour. Delhi, at 29 degrees north, is carried at about 1,465.
- e.g. Kochi is carried at about 1,645 kilometres an hour.
- e.g. Delhi is carried at about 1,465 kilometres an hour.
- e.g. A person at the North Pole is not carried anywhere. They turn once on the spot.
### Why we feel nothing, and the two small things we can feel · *defined*
**Definition:** We feel no spin because it is smooth and steady, and everything around us is carried along too. But the spin does change the Earth's shape and our weight a tiny bit.

In a smooth train, you do not feel the speed. You only feel bumps, starts and stops. The Earth turns smoothly, so there are no bumps. Yet the spin does leave two small marks. First, the spin flings things outward a little, so the Earth is slightly wider across the equator than from pole to pole. The difference is about 43 kilometres. Second, the spin and the bulge together make gravity a tiny bit weaker at the equator. A child who weighs 30 kilograms at the poles weighs about 150 grams less at the equator. That is about the weight of an apple. Scales can show it, but your body cannot feel it.
- e.g. The Earth is about 12,756 kilometres wide across the equator and about 12,714 kilometres from pole to pole.
- e.g. A 30 kilogram child weighs about 150 grams less on the equator than at the North Pole.
- e.g. Spinning a ball of soft clay fast makes it bulge in the middle.
### A day is 24 hours, but the Earth spins once in 23 hours 56 minutes · *defined*
**Definition:** One full spin of the Earth takes 23 hours 56 minutes. A day from one noon to the next takes 24 hours. The extra 4 minutes are there because the Earth also moves round the Sun.

While the Earth spins, it also travels round the Sun, once in a year. In one day it moves a little along its path, about 1 degree. After one full spin, the Sun is not yet back overhead, because the Earth has moved. The Earth has to turn about 1 degree more to face the Sun again. At 4 minutes for each degree, that is about 4 minutes more. So one spin takes 23 hours 56 minutes, and the Sun takes 24 hours to come back to the same place in the sky. The faraway stars are so far that the Earth's small move round the Sun does not change where they are. So a star comes back to the same place after exactly one spin. This is why a star rises about 4 minutes earlier each night.
- e.g. A bright star sets about 4 minutes earlier each night.
- e.g. After a month, a star sets about 2 hours earlier than at the start.
- e.g. The same 4 minutes per degree gives both the longitude rule and the star-day rule.
### Days on other planets · *defined*
**Definition:** A planet's day is the time it takes to spin once. It is different on every planet.

Mars spins once in about 24 hours 37 minutes, so a day there is almost like ours. Jupiter is the biggest planet, but it spins once in only about 9 hours 56 minutes. Its edge is carried at more than 40,000 kilometres an hour. Venus spins once in 243 Earth days, which is longer than its year of 225 Earth days. Venus also spins backwards, so the Sun would rise in the west. Why Venus spins this way is not known. Ideas include a giant crash long ago, and the pull of the Sun on its thick air.
- e.g. On Jupiter, you would have about 2 and a half sunrises in one Earth day.
- e.g. A day on Mars is about 37 minutes longer than a day on Earth.
- e.g. On Venus, one spin takes longer than one trip round the Sun.
### The Earth's day is slowly getting longer · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's spin is slowing down. A day gets about 2 thousandths of a second longer every hundred years.

The Moon pulls on the oceans and makes the tides. As the tides move over the sea floor, they rub, like a gentle brake on the spinning Earth. The spin loses energy. The Moon gains it and drifts about 3.8 centimetres further away each year, which scientists measure by bouncing laser light off mirrors that Apollo astronauts left on the Moon. A few thousandths of a second a century sounds like nothing. Over hundreds of millions of years it adds up. In 1963, John Wells counted daily growth lines on fossil corals that lived about 400 million years ago. He found about 400 lines in a year. So the year had about 400 days, and each day was about 22 hours long. Records of ancient eclipses also show the slowing. Big events can change a day too. The 2011 earthquake in Japan moved so much rock that it shortened the day by about 2 millionths of a second.
- e.g. Corals from 400 million years ago show a year with about 400 days.
- e.g. The Moon is moving away from the Earth by about 3.8 centimetres a year.
- e.g. The 2011 Japan earthquake shortened the day by about 2 millionths of a second.
### Why moths circle a lamp · *debated*
Moths and many other insects fly in circles round lamps at night. Scientists agree that the light changes how the insect steers. They do not all agree on how.
- e.g. Moths circling a tube light on a balcony.
- e.g. Insects gathering under a streetlight.
- e.g. Moths flying straight when the Moon is the only light.
- **The Moon-compass idea (an older explanation):** Insects fly straight by keeping the Moon at the same angle. A lamp is much nearer than the Moon, so as the insect flies past the lamp, the angle keeps changing. The insect turns to fix the angle and ends up spiralling in.
- **The back-to-the-light idea (Samuel Fabian and colleagues, 2024):** High-speed cameras showed insects tilting so that their backs face the brightest light. This works well under a bright sky. Near a lamp, it makes them tilt, circle, and sometimes fly upside down.
### Night is full of life, and light can harm it · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals and plants are active at night. Artificial light at night, called light pollution, can confuse them and hide the stars from us.

Night animals solve the problem of the dark in different ways. A barn owl hears the tiny rustle of a mouse with ears set at different heights on her head. A bat shouts and listens to the echoes. A slender loris has huge eyes that gather every bit of light. An Indian rock python feels the warmth of prey with pits on her lips. Fireflies make their own light. Night-blooming flowers such as raat ki rani send out a scent that moths follow. Artificial light disturbs this. Baby olive ridley turtles walk towards the brightest horizon, which should be the sea. Streetlights on a beach can lead them the wrong way. Migrating birds can crash into lit towers. A study in 2016 found that more than 80 percent of people on Earth live under a sky made brighter by artificial light.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtle hatchlings on Chennai and Odisha beaches can be led away from the sea by lights.
- e.g. A barn owl can catch a mouse in total darkness by sound alone.
- e.g. Fireflies flash to find each other, and bright lights make this harder.

## Mostly agreed
- The Earth spins once on its axis in about 24 hours, from west to east. *How we know:* A Foucault pendulum, star trails in long photographs, and pictures taken from space all show the spin.
- Solar noon comes about 4 minutes later for every degree of longitude we go west. *How we know:* Sunrise and sunset tables for cities around the world, and world time zones, all follow this rule. 360 degrees in 24 hours is 4 minutes a degree.
- Every place makes one turn a day, so places far from the equator are carried more slowly than places near it. *How we know:* The Earth's size has been measured by surveyors and satellites. A smaller circle covered in the same time means a slower speed.
- The Earth's spin is slowing by about 2 thousandths of a second each century, mainly because of the tides raised by the Moon. *How we know:* Records of eclipses made over 2,700 years, growth lines in fossil corals and shells, and atomic clocks all agree.
- Artificial light at night changes the behaviour of many animals, including turtles, birds and insects. *How we know:* Studies on beaches, on migration routes and under streetlights have measured the changes.

## How we found out
**Jean Richer, Isaac Newton, and the French Academy expeditions led by Pierre Louis Maupertuis and Charles Marie de La Condamine, 1672 to 1740.** For a long time most people pictured the Earth as a perfect ball. In 1672, French astronomer Jean Richer took a pendulum clock to Cayenne, near the equator. It lost about two and a half minutes a day compared with Paris, as if gravity were weaker there. In 1687, Isaac Newton explained why. A spinning ball of rock and water must bulge at its middle, as soft clay does. Yet the Cassini family in France measured the ground and believed the Earth was longer from pole to pole, like a lemon. In 1735 and 1736, the French Academy sent two expeditions to settle it. One went to Peru near the equator. The other went to Lapland, far in the north. Both measured the ground length of one degree of latitude. The degree in Lapland was longer, which means the ground curves less near the pole. The Earth is flatter at its poles, as Newton said. The French writer and scientist Émilie du Châtelet, who later translated Newton's book into French, had argued for his side.  
*What people believed before:* Many French scientists, including the Cassini family, believed the Earth was longer from pole to pole than across the equator, like a lemon. The two expeditions showed that it is flattened at the poles, and wider at the equator, because it spins.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Sun is highest at 12 o'clock everywhere in India.** (Many people, because every clock in the country says 12.; *rejected*)  
  For: All of India uses one clock time, so it feels like the same moment everywhere.  
  Against: Noon by the Sun is about 11:23 in Guwahati and about 12:38 in Mumbai. The clock is an agreement. The Sun's time belongs to each place.
- **India should have two time zones.** (Some people in the north-east of India, and some scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: In the east, the Sun rises very early and sets early. In winter it is dark soon after 4 in the afternoon, so people lose daylight in the evening, and children may walk home from school in the dark.  
  Against: Two clocks inside one country could confuse trains, flights, banks and phone systems. Many people prefer the simplicity of one time.
- **Everything on Earth moves at the same speed as the Earth spins.** (A common first guess.; *rejected*)  
  For: Everything makes one turn in the same 24 hours.  
  Against: The circle you travel is longer near the equator and shorter near the poles, so the speed is different. A person on the equator is carried about twice as fast as a person at 60 degrees north.
- **A day has always been 24 hours long.** (Almost everyone, because clocks have always said 24.; *rejected*)  
  For: Within a human life, the length of the day looks fixed.  
  Against: Fossil corals, eclipse records and the Moon's slow drift show that days were shorter long ago. About 400 million years ago a day was about 22 hours.
- **Moths fly round lamps because they are confused about the Moon.** (A popular explanation for many years.; *debated*)  
  For: It is neat, and some experiments seem to fit it.  
  Against: High-speed cameras in 2024 suggested that the light makes insects tilt their backs towards it, which makes them circle. Scientists are still testing this.

## Roots
We have followed the Sun's light round the spinning Earth, from Guwahati to Mumbai. Now we ask what this tells us about morning, about time, and about our own place in it.

**Deep truth.** Sunrise is not an event that happens to a whole country at once. It is a line that crosses the Earth and never stops. The dawn that reached Guwahati arrived in Mumbai about an hour later. So morning belongs to a place, and no one moment is morning for everyone.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the Sun rises at 6' as if morning were one thing that happens to everyone together. One clock for a whole country hides the fact that each place has its own morning, and that the Sun's time and the clock's time are different things.  
*Evidence:* In the noon table, the Sun was highest about 4 minutes later for every degree west. Five cities under one Sun and one clock had five different noons. And because the Earth keeps turning, a dawn is always crossing somewhere.

- *who am i:* You are not waiting for the morning to come to you. You are being carried through it, at about 1,500 kilometres an hour, on a turning Earth.
- *what is life:* Birds, flowers and people wake as the dawn reaches them. Many birds start singing as the light arrives, so a wave of birdsong travels with the line of dawn round the world.
- *what is this world:* There is one Sun and one turning Earth. So a line of dawn and a line of dusk are always crossing the planet, and somewhere it is always morning.

From one Sun comes light, and from one turning Earth comes a dawn that passes from place to place. No place owns the morning. Each place receives it a few minutes after its eastern neighbour, and passes it on to the west. The many mornings are one morning, handed on. This is the one becoming the many in time. A single dawn line, circling the world, becomes a different time of day for every place it crosses.

- **Rig Veda 1.113:** This hymn to Night and Dawn describes them as two sisters who follow each other across the sky. They look different, but they work together and are never apart for long. (Night and dawn are made by one turning Earth. The dawn line is the border where one sister meets the other.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 6 (about 500 BCE):** The Greek thinker Heraclitus wrote that the Sun is new every day. (The sunrise that reaches you today is a new meeting between you and the light. Yet it is one dawn line that has been circling the Earth for billions of years.)
- Science (settled): The Earth turns 1 degree in 4 minutes, so the Sun reaches the places along a line of latitude in turn. Time zones and Indian Standard Time are human agreements laid over this.
- Science (settled): Einstein showed in 1905 that for places far apart, 'at the same time' depends on how the observers are moving. On Earth the effect is far too small to notice.
- Other view, Many physicists: For everyday life, one shared time works perfectly well. Atomic clocks keep a single world time called UTC, and every place can tell its Sun time from it. They would say the line of dawn is only geometry, and that there is nothing deeper to find in it.
- Practice, Facing the dawn line (3 min): Stand or sit at a window that faces east, early in the morning. If it is dark, close your eyes. Breathe in slowly and breathe out slowly. Feel the ground under you being carried towards the east. Say quietly in your mind: the light is coming to me from the east. Somewhere to my east, someone is seeing it now. Breathe three more times and open your eyes.
- Ask: If it is always sunrise somewhere, is there a first morning of the day?
- Ask: Is 6 o'clock a fact about the Sun, or an agreement between people? How would you tell?
- Ask: Which clock should a country keep: the one that matches the Sun at its middle, or one for each place?

## Story: The Morning That Was on Its Way
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Port Blair is about 12 degrees of longitude east of Chennai. The line of dawn sweeps west at about 4 minutes for each degree, so it reaches Chennai about fifty minutes after Port Blair. Chennai faces the Bay of Bengal on its east side, so the Sun there rises over the sea. Kavya, Patti and Rajam are imagined.*

When Patti came to live with us in Chennai, she brought two suitcases, a brass lamp and a habit of waking before five o'clock.

For fifty years, Patti had lived in Port Blair, on the Andaman Islands. There the sky turns gold early, long before most of India has woken. Her little sister, Rajam, still lives there, in a house above the harbour.

On her first morning in our flat, Patti sat at the east window with her tea. It was grey and dark outside. The sea was only a darker darkness beyond the rooftops. She waited, and nothing happened.

'This Sun is lazy,' she said. 'At home it would be up by now.'

She said it the next morning too, and the morning after. Slowly her tea went cold in her hands. I was nine, and I did not know what to say.

Every morning, Patti rang Rajam. And every day Rajam sent a photograph from her window: the sea, the sky, a ribbon of orange. Patti looked at it for a long time.

'My sister's morning is already over,' she said once. 'Here it is only starting. I am always late in this city.'

That week at school, our teacher, Ms Hema, gave us a puzzle. She turned the classroom globe slowly. 'The Earth turns all the way round, three hundred and sixty degrees, in twenty-four hours,' she said. 'How many minutes is that for one degree?'

We worked it out on our slates. Twenty-four hours is one thousand four hundred and forty minutes. Divided by three hundred and sixty, it is four. The Earth turns one degree every four minutes.

That night, at home, I took the globe from my shelf. I put one thread on Port Blair and one on Chennai. I counted the degrees of longitude between them. There were about twelve. Twelve times four is forty-eight.

I looked at the number for a long time. Forty-eight minutes. Not lazy. Not late. Just on its way.

The next morning I woke Patti at ten to five. 'Patti, come to the window. Please.'

She came, grumbling, in her nightdress. Her phone buzzed on the sill. It was Rajam's photograph. The sky over the harbour was bright gold.

'Look at the photograph,' I said. 'That is the dawn at Rajam's window. It is moving west, over the sea, at the speed of the turning Earth. It is coming here. In about forty-eight minutes, it will be at our window.'

Patti looked at me. She looked at the phone. Then she looked out at the grey. 'The same dawn?'

'The same line of dawn,' I said. 'It never stops. It is leaving her window now, and it is coming to ours.'

We sat together and waited. Patti held her cold tea. The crows began first. Then a thin pale line appeared on the sea, far out. It grew. It turned pink and then orange, and then a bright gold light came over the water and across the rooftops and into our window. It touched Patti's hands.

'There,' said Patti, very quietly. 'Rajam's morning has come.'

She did not say anything for a while. Then she laughed. 'So I am not late. I am only downstream.'

A week later, it rained. The sky over Chennai was thick with cloud. Patti stood at the window and her face fell. 'It has not come. The cloud has stopped it.'

I thought about this. 'The cloud hides it,' I said. 'It cannot stop it. The Earth does not wait for the sky to be clear. The dawn is already here, behind the cloud. We just cannot see it.'

Patti rang Rajam. 'Is it clear there?' she asked. 'It was? Good. Then my morning is here, in the rain. I can feel it.'

Patti pointed at the globe, which I had brought to the window. 'And after us?'

I turned it with my finger. 'After us, it goes on to Kochi. Then Mumbai, then over the Arabian Sea. Then it reaches Africa, and it keeps going all the way round.'

'So every morning I have is passed along to someone else,' said Patti.

'Yes,' I said. 'And every morning someone passes one to us.'

From then on, she woke at five every day, and we sat at the window and waited for Rajam's morning. Sometimes we saw it. Sometimes the cloud hid it. But Patti never said that the Sun was lazy again.

**For the parent:** A sunrise is not an event that happens to a whole country at once. It is a line of dawn that crosses the Earth and never stops. It reaches each place about 4 minutes later for every degree of longitude to the west. So Rajam's sunrise in Port Blair reached Chennai about fifty minutes later.

- Ask: The line of dawn reaches a city at 5:30 by the clock. About when does it reach a city 10 degrees of longitude to the west?
- Ask: If thick cloud hides the sunrise, has the line of dawn stopped? Why or why not?
- Ask: Where on Earth is the line of dawn right now, and which way is it moving?

### e2 · Five Noons: is the Sun highest at the same moment in every city? (30 min)
- **question:** The clocks in Guwahati, Kolkata, Chennai, Kochi and Mumbai all show the same time. Is the Sun at its highest in all five cities at the same clock time?
- **predict:** Before you look at any data, guess. Will the Sun be at its highest at the same clock time in all five cities? If not, which city comes first, which comes last, and how far apart are they? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same date for all five cities and the same source for the times, such as one weather app. Read each time to the minute. Work out noon in the same way for every city. Only the city changes, so any difference in noon comes from where the city is.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all five cities. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each city. The bar shows how many minutes later than Guwahati the Sun is highest there.
- **results table:** City | Longitude (degrees east) | Sunrise | Sunset | Noon (the middle of the day) | Minutes later than Guwahati
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the five cities on a map. Tell them the clocks in all five show the same time. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* All five cities use the same clock. Will the Sun be at its highest at the same time in all of them? If not, which city is first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Five cities, one clock. Is the Sun highest at the same time in all? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the noons will be the same, or different? (good: The clock says so.; The Sun gets to the east first.; Guwahati is nearer the Sun's rising place.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say the noons are the same, because the clock is the same. That is a good guess to test.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look up today's sunrise and sunset in each of the five cities. Write each time to the minute. Use the 24-hour clock for sunset, so 5:58 in the evening is 17:58.  
  *Parent says:* Look up the sunrise and the sunset for each city. Use the same day and the same app for all five. Write the times to the minute.  
  *Child mode:* Find sunrise and sunset for each city. Write them in the table.
  - Note: No phone? Use the weather box in a newspaper, or ring a relative in another city and ask when the Sun rose and set. If a city is missing, use any city whose longitude you can look up. Working together: with 2 to 4 children, give each child one or two cities to look up and write on the shared table, then check each other's times. One child on their own does all five cities.
  - Check: Why do we use the same date and the same app for all five cities? → **So that only the city changes**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out noon for the first city. Count the time from sunrise to sunset. Halve it. Add the half to the sunrise. The answer is when the Sun is highest.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a way to find the middle of the day. Count how long it is from sunrise to sunset. Cut that time in half. Add the half to the sunrise time.  
  *Child mode:* Find the middle of the day. Halve the daytime, then add it to sunrise.
  - Note: Worked example with made-up numbers: sunrise 6:10 and sunset 17:50. From 6:10 to 17:50 is 11 hours 40 minutes. Half of that is 5 hours 50 minutes. 6:10 plus 5:50 is 12:00. So noon is at 12:00. The Sun is highest in the middle of the daytime, so the middle is noon.
  - Check: Sunrise is at 6:00 and sunset is at 18:30. When is noon by the Sun? → **12:15**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out noon for the other four cities. Then, for each city, write how many minutes later than Guwahati its noon is.  
  *Parent says:* Do the same for the other four cities. Then count how many minutes later each noon is than the noon in Guwahati.  
  *Child mode:* Find noon for every city. Count the minutes after Guwahati.
  - Note: Expect Guwahati to be first and Mumbai last. Your gaps should be roughly 13 minutes to Kolkata, 46 to Chennai, 62 to Kochi and 75 to Mumbai, though your numbers may be a minute or two different. A quick check: timeanddate.com also shows the solar noon of each city. If your noon is more than 3 minutes away from that, look at your sum again.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Divide each gap in minutes by the gap in degrees of longitude from Guwahati. For Mumbai, that is the gap in minutes divided by 19 degrees, which is about 91.7 minus 72.9. What number do you keep getting?  
  *Parent says:* Look at the longitudes. Divide each gap in minutes by the gap in degrees from Guwahati. What number do you keep getting?  
  *Child mode:* Divide minutes by degrees for each city. What number do you get?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the minutes for each degree? (good: It is about 4 each time.; It stays the same.; The further west, the later the noon.)
  - Check: Noon in Guwahati is at 11:30. Mumbai is 19 degrees of longitude to the west. About when is noon in Mumbai? → **About 12:45**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw in the data and why it happened. Then look at the sunrise gaps.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you find? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you found, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that noon came later for every city to the west, by about 4 minutes for each degree. The Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, which is 4 minutes for each degree. ### e1 · The Orange Earth: does every place on Earth move at the same speed? (35 min)
- **question:** Every place on Earth goes round once in 24 hours. Does a place on the equator move at the same speed as a place far to the north?
- **predict:** Before you measure, guess. Will a person far to the north be carried faster than, slower than or at the same speed as a person on the equator? And how much slower, if you think so? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three circles are drawn on the same orange and measured with the same thread and ruler. Every spot on the orange turns once in the same time, so a longer circle means a faster spot. Only the latitude of the circle changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up should help with the scissors if your child is young. Draw on the peel only, and do not eat the orange afterwards. Wash your hands after using the pen, and after handling the orange if your child has sensitive skin or a citrus allergy.
- **record:** Fill in the table with the three lengths. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each circle.
- **results table:** Circle | Latitude (degrees north) | Length of the circle (centimetres) | Compared with the equator (fraction)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the orange and say it stands in for the Earth. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Every place on Earth goes round once in 24 hours. A person on the equator and a person far to the north go round once each day. Do they move at the same speed? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Everyone on Earth goes round once a day. Is everyone moving at the same speed? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think they move at the same speed, or at different speeds? (good: They all take 24 hours.; The circle near the equator is bigger.; The north is closer to the middle.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say everybody moves at the same speed because everybody takes a day. That is a good guess to test. One child with a parent: the child does the drawing and measuring, and the parent holds the orange. With 2 to 4 children, give each pair or child their own orange (they are cheap) and share the three lengths on one chart, which also shows how much the oranges differ. Or split the roles: one holds, one draws, one threads and measures.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The dimple where the stem was is the North Pole. Mark a dot on the opposite end for the South Pole. Slip a rubber band round the fattest part and move it until it is the same distance from both poles. Draw a line along it.  
  *Parent says:* The small dimple is the North Pole. Mark the other end as the South Pole. Put a rubber band round the middle. Draw a line along it. This line is the equator.  
  *Child mode:* Mark the two poles. Draw the equator with a rubber band.
  - Note: To check the band is in the middle, lay a thread from the North Pole dot to the band, and compare it with a thread from the South Pole dot to the band. They should be the same length. An orange is never perfectly round, so be happy with close.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay a thread along the skin from the equator line up to the North Pole dot, and cut it. Measure it with the ruler. Work out one third and two thirds of its length. Mark both points on four sides of the orange, with a dot each.  
  *Parent says:* Lay a thread from the equator up to the North Pole. Cut it and measure it. Mark one third and two thirds of the way up, on four sides.  
  *Child mode:* Measure from the equator to the pole. Mark one third and two thirds.
  - Note: The way from the equator to the pole is one quarter of the way round the Earth, which is 90 degrees. One third of it is 30 degrees of latitude. Two thirds of it is 60 degrees. For a 6 centimetre thread, mark 2 and 4 centimetres from the equator. Delhi is close to the 30 degree circle. Oslo and Helsinki are close to the 60 degree circle.
  - Check: The thread from the equator to the pole is 6 centimetres long. Where is the mark for two thirds of the way? → **4 centimetres from the equator**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark dots at one third of the way up, on eight sides, all the same height above the equator. Join the dots with a pen line. Do the same at two thirds. You now have three circles.

  *Child mode:* Join the dots into a circle. Do this for both new circles.
  - Note: A rubber band slides towards the pole on a small circle, so do not use it here. Instead measure the same distance up from the equator line at eight places and join the dots by eye, with a thread laid along them as a guide. A wavy line is fine. If it wanders, measure a few more dots.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wrap a thread round the equator, snip it where it meets itself, and lay it straight along the ruler. Write its length. Do the same for the other two circles.  
  *Parent says:* Wrap a thread once round the equator. Snip it, straighten it and measure it. Do the same for the other circles.  
  *Child mode:* Wrap, snip and measure the thread for each circle. Write the lengths.
  - Note: Typical numbers: an orange about 24 to 26 centimetres round the equator, about 21 to 23 round the 30 degree circle, and about 12 to 13 round the 60 degree circle. Your numbers will not be exact: anything from 40 to 60 out of 100 for the 60 degree circle is a fair result. If the thread stretches or slips off the circle, measure again.
  - Check: Each spot on the orange turns once in the same time. Which spot is carried fastest? → **The one on the longest circle**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they measured and why. Then ask how long the 60 degree circle is compared with the equator.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you find? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you found, and why.

Real fruit is not a perfect sphere, so do not worry if your answer is a little off half.
  - Think: If the equator is carried at 1,670 kilometres an hour, how fast is a person at the 60 degree circle?

  *Parent says:* That extra bit is why a day is longer than one spin. For the real Earth the extra turn is about 1 degree. The Earth turns 1 degree in 4 minutes. So a day is 4 minutes longer than a spin.  
  *Child mode:* The real extra turn is one degree. That takes four minutes.
  - Note: Try it once more, walking the other way (clockwise). The lamp is now on the other side, so the child must turn back the other way to face it, which means less than a full day, not more. That is why the Earth's spin and its orbit going the same way makes a day longer.
  - Check: One full spin of the Earth takes 23 hours 56 minutes. The Sun takes 24 hours to come back overhead. Why is there a gap? → **The Earth also moves round the Sun, so it must turn a little more**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child why a star rises 4 minutes earlier each night.  
  *Parent says:* A star always lies in the same direction. After one spin, the star is back in the same place. But the Sun needs 4 minutes more. So when does the star come up, compared with yesterday?  
  *Child mode:* Stars come back after one spin. The Sun takes four minutes more. What does that mean?
  - Note: A star comes back after one spin, which is 4 minutes before the Sun comes back. So a star rises 4 minutes earlier each night by the clock. After 30 nights it is 2 hours earlier.
  - Think: After a whole month, how much earlier does a star rise?

  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children guess that the biggest planet turns the slowest. Jupiter is the biggest and turns the fastest.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to stand with arms out and turn slowly to the left. Start the stopwatch and ask them to take 24 seconds for one full turn. Write down the time they really took and how far off it was. This is the Earth. Then do Mars at about 25 seconds.

  *Child mode:* Turn to your left. Take 24 seconds for one turn. That is Earth.

Working together: one child turns, one runs the stopwatch and counts aloud, one writes down the real time. A good test: try the turn again with the same time, and see if you get better. Do not spin faster than the target time, because the aim is slow and steady.

  *Parent does:* Now ask your child to turn once in only 10 seconds. This is Jupiter. Ask how it feels different from the Earth turn.  
  *Parent says:* Now turn once in 10 seconds. That is Jupiter. It spins more than twice in one Earth day. How does it feel?  
  *Child mode:* Turn once in ten seconds. That is Jupiter. How does it feel?
  - Ask: Jupiter is much bigger than the Earth. How can it turn faster? (good: It spins like a top.; It was spun fast when it formed.; It formed from a fast-spinning cloud.)
  - Note: Jupiter is mostly gas and is bigger than 1,000 Earths in volume. It spins once in about 9 hours 56 minutes. Its middle is carried at more than 40,000 kilometres an hour. Scientists think the planets got their spin from the swirling cloud they formed from.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to work out Venus. Venus takes 243 Earth days to turn once. One Earth day is 24 seconds in the game. Multiply 243 by 24 seconds. Change the answer to minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Venus takes 243 Earth days to turn once. One Earth day is 24 seconds in our game. How many seconds would Venus take to turn once? How many minutes is that?  
  *Child mode:* Venus takes 243 days to turn. Each day is 24 seconds. Work it out.
  - Note: 243 times 24 is 5,832 seconds. 5,832 divided by 60 is about 97 minutes. So Venus would take about an hour and a half to turn once. Venus spins the opposite way to the Earth, so the Sun would rise in the west.
  - Think: Venus turns backwards. If you turned the other way, what would the Sun do?

## Worksheets
### Day and Night, Noon and Speed (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) The Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours. How long does it take to turn 1 degree?  
   Options: 4 minutes / 15 minutes / 1 minute  
   **Answer:** 4 minutes. *Why:* 24 hours is 1,440 minutes. 1,440 divided by 360 is 4. So the Earth turns 1 degree every 4 minutes.
2. (mcq) Seen from above the North Pole, which way does the Earth spin?  
   Options: Anticlockwise, from west to east / Clockwise, from east to west / It does not spin, and the Sun goes round it  
   **Answer:** Anticlockwise, from west to east. *Why:* The Earth turns from west to east. That is why the Sun seems to rise in the east and why places in the east see the Sun first.
3. (mcq) City A is 5 degrees of longitude west of city B. When is the Sun at its highest in A, compared with B?  
   Options: About 20 minutes later / About 20 minutes earlier / At the same time  
   **Answer:** About 20 minutes later. *Why:* 5 degrees times 4 minutes is 20 minutes. A is to the west, so the Sun reaches it later.
4. (truefalse) At 12 o'clock on the clock, the Sun is at its highest in every city in India.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Everyone uses one clock, but the Sun is highest about 4 minutes later for every degree you go west. In Guwahati it is about 37 minutes before 12 and in Mumbai about 38 minutes after.
5. (fill) The Sun is at its highest about ____ minutes later for every degree of longitude we go west.  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* The Earth turns 360 degrees in 1,440 minutes, which is 4 minutes for each degree.
6. (count) A town has sunrise at 6:20 in the morning and sunset at 18:00. At what time is the Sun at its highest? Write it as hours and minutes, like 12:10.  
   **Answer:** 12:10. *Why:* From 6:20 to 18:00 is 11 hours 40 minutes. Half is 5 hours 50 minutes. 6:20 plus 5:50 is 12:10.
7. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Latitude → how far north or south of the equator a place is; Longitude → how far east or west of Greenwich a place is; Equator → the circle around the middle of the Earth, halfway between the poles; Axis → the line the Earth spins around. *Why:* Latitude tells you how far north or south you are. Longitude tells you how far east or west. The equator is halfway between the poles. The axis is the line that the Earth turns about.
8. (order) Put these cities in the order in which the Sun rises over them, first to last: Mumbai, Dong, Chennai, Guwahati.  
   **Answer:** ['Dong', 'Guwahati', 'Chennai', 'Mumbai']. *Why:* The Earth spins from west to east, so the Sun reaches the places in the east first. Dong is furthest east, then Guwahati, then Chennai, and Mumbai is furthest west.
9. (mcq) A circle drawn on a spinning ball is shorter near the pole than at the equator. Why does that make a spot near the pole move more slowly?  
   Options: It goes round a shorter path in the same time / It is further from the Sun / It is colder there  
   **Answer:** It goes round a shorter path in the same time. *Why:* Every spot goes round once in 24 hours. A shorter path in the same time means a slower speed.
10. (truefalse) A person standing at the North Pole is carried round at about 1,670 kilometres an hour.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A person at the pole only turns on the spot. The speed of about 1,670 kilometres an hour belongs to the equator.
11. (mcq) Why can we not feel the Earth spinning?

## Applications
- Railway and flight timetables, which all use a single agreed clock time across places where the Sun's time differs.
- Rockets launched towards the east, as ISRO does from Sriharikota, which get a free speed boost of about 0.45 kilometres a second from the Earth's spin.
- Finding longitude at sea by comparing local noon with the time at a home port, with 4 minutes for every degree.
- Jet lag, which is the body clock catching up with a change of 4 minutes per degree of longitude.
- Designing street lighting that points down and is dim and warm, to protect the night sky and wildlife.
- Planning solar power and farm work by knowing exactly when the Sun is highest in each place.

## Open questions
- **Why does Venus spin backwards, so slowly that its day is longer than its year?** Ideas include a giant crash long ago, and the pull of the Sun on its thick air. Nobody has shown which is right.
- **How fast was the Earth spinning when it was young, and how long was the first day?** Scientists agree it was much faster. The numbers depend on how the Moon formed, and that is still being worked out.
- **Why do moths and other insects circle lights?** Studies in 2024 gave a new explanation, that insects tilt their backs towards the light. More experiments are needed to test it.
- **Is there a 'now' that is the same everywhere in the universe?** Einstein's relativity says that 'at the same time' depends on how observers move. What this means for the universe as a whole is still discussed.
- **What exactly made the Earth spin in the first place?** The planets formed from a swirling cloud, but the details of how the Earth got its spin, and how a giant crash changed it, are still argued.

- [Red cellophane or a red gel sheet](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=red+cellophane+sheet) (material): For the red torch in the Night Watch and the lamp challenge. Red cellophane from a stationery or gift-wrap shop costs a few rupees. A red sweet wrapper, red polythene or a thick red cloth over a torch also works, but use two layers if the light still looks white. Used in a2, c2. 
- [A small pair of binoculars](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=binoculars+for+kids) (tool): Optional. For the Star Gate project and the star count. Stars are easy to see without them, but binoculars show many more. Used in p1, a3. 

## Warm-up
- How many minutes does the Earth take to turn 1 degree? → 4 minutes.
- Which way does the Earth spin, seen from above the North Pole? → Anticlockwise, from west to east.
- Where is the Sun highest first: in the east or the west? → In the east.
- Why do all cities in India show the same clock time? → Indian Standard Time is an agreement, set by the Sun at 82.5 degrees east.
- Who is carried faster by the spin: a person at the equator, or a person at 60 degrees north? → The person at the equator, about twice as fast.
- How long does one spin of the Earth take, compared with the stars? → 23 hours 56 minutes.
- What is slowly making the Earth's day longer? → The tides raised by the Moon.
- Name one animal that is awake at night and how she finds her way. → A barn owl, with her ears. A bat, with echoes. A python, with heat.
